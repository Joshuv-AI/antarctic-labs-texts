Neoseiulus californicus (Acari: Phytoseiidae) chemical impaired functional response type assessment preying on Panonychus citri (Acari: Tetranychidae)
Authors: Muhammad Asif Qayyoum, Syed Husne Mobarak, Zi-Wei Song, Dun-Song Li, Bilal Saeed Khan, Muhammad Irfan Akram, Daochao Jin, Tian-Ci Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated sublethal effects of the insecticides SYP-9625, abamectin, vegetable oil, and EnSpray 99 on the functional responses of the predatory mite Neoseiulus californicus, highlighting their potential disruption of biological control functions. When predators fed on all stages (eggs, immature, and adults) of Panonychus citri without insecticides, they exhibited a type II functional response. Sublethal exposure to SYP-9625 and vegetable oil altered the predator’s functional response curves without changing their response type compared to controls. Exposure to abamectin resulted in distinct functional response shifts, causing type I, type II, and type III responses when N. californicus fed on egg, immature, and adult stages of the pest, respectively. Exposure to EnSpray 99 led to a type I functional response in predators consuming immature and adult prey stages, characterized by non-significant linear parameters. The prey consumption rate was significantly impaired by abamectin, followed by EnSpray 99 and SYP-9625, compared to the control, while vegetable oil treatments increased predation rates by attracting predators. The fertility rate of N. californicus showed significant differences between vegetable oil and SYP-9625 treatments across impaired pest life stages. Abamectin and EnSpray 99 demonstrated the lowest compatibility with biological control, while vegetable oil increased predator attack rates and improved predator efficiency against P. citri. This study suggests that sublethal pesticide exposure can significantly alter predator behavior and efficiency. Integrated chemical-biological control strategies utilizing selective pesticides like vegetable oil show promise for citrus pest management but require further field validation.

Introduction

Pesticides are widely used globally to control arthropod pests1 in agricultural systems as part of integrated pest management (IPM) strategies. Additionally, naturally occurring predatory insects play a critical role in maintaining plant health and productivity1. Pesticides pose significant risks to biological control agents, necessitating acute toxicity assessments to evaluate their safety towards natural enemies2. IPM practitioners emphasize pesticide compatibility with biocontrol agents to maintain resilience within agroecosystems. Recent research highlights sublethal pesticide effects on predators, including longevity, fecundity, developmental rate, sex ratio, and behavioral modifications3,4. However, a comprehensive understanding of how pesticides affect predator efficacy in pest suppression remains limited5.

Predators suppress pests through numerical and functional responses6. Holling (1959b) described three functional response types: linear (type I), curvilinear (type II), and sigmoid (type III). These functional responses help identify predators imposing density-dependent mortality, stabilizing prey populations, estimating predator foraging parameters, and understanding predator–prey relationships7. The commonly studied functional responses, types II and III, are influenced by factors such as host plant interactions8, alternative prey9, predator–prey size10, and pesticide exposure11.

Citrus plantations are severely infested by the citrus red mite, Panonychus citri, across various citrus-growing regions globally12–20, including China21–25. Southern China provides optimal climatic conditions for P. citri, resulting in two peak infestation periods annually (June–July and October–November)26. All developmental stages of P. citri reduce fruit production by feeding on leaves, twigs, and fruits. During severe infestations, chemical applications are the preferred control method by Chinese farmers27. Predatory mites from Phytoseiidae have been successfully utilized for biological control28. Among commercially available predatory mites, Neoseiulus californicus (Acari: Phytoseiidae) is commonly associated with tetranychid mites, including P. citri28,29. However, the functional responses of N. californicus to eggs, immature stages, and adults of P. citri have not yet been compared, nor has information regarding pesticide effects on predator functional response parameters been documented.

This study aimed to develop functional response curves for N. californicus preying on eggs, immature stages, and adults of P. citri. We further investigated the effects of commonly used pesticides in citrus orchards—SYP-9625, abamectin, vegetable oil (soybean oil), and mineral oil (EnSpray 99)—on the functional response parameters of N. californicus. Synthetic and non-synthetic chemicals were selected based on their history of use in citrus orchards. More than 60% of chemicals applied in orchards are abamectin-based30, while SYP-9625 represents new chemistry increasingly adopted by farmers due to its low toxicity31,32. Vegetable oil (soybean oil)33,34 and EnSpray 9935–37 are frequently utilized to repel and kill pests in Guangdong citrus orchards, as reported previously38,39.

This research addresses critical knowledge gaps regarding how sublethal pesticide effects influence predator–prey interactions and biological control. We demonstrate that LC30 pesticide doses significantly impact the functional response of N. californicus, affecting both attack rates and handling time. Additionally, the discovery that vegetable oil mitigates these sublethal effects provides a promising strategy for pest control in pesticide-treated crops. Furthermore, we show that pesticide-induced changes in P. citri life-stage preference interfere with biological control efficiency. These findings offer specific insights into chemical exposure, ecological functions, and management strategies for IPM and risk assessment in citrus production systems.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

We tested the insecticides SYP-9625 (SYP-9625®, Shenyang Kechuang Chemicals Co. Ltd., China), abamectin (Abamectin®, North China Pharmaceutical Group Ainuo Co. Ltd., China), vegetable oil (Soybean oil®, Institute of Zoology, Guangdong Academy of Sciences, Guangzhou city, Guangdong, China), and mineral oil (EnSpray 99®, Korean Oil Pvt. Ltd., South Korea). These products are commonly used in orchard plantations to manage various pests. Experiments utilized sublethal concentrations (LC30) of two synthetic chemicals (SYP-9625 and abamectin) and two oils (vegetable oil and mineral oil) previously evaluated38,39.

Mites culture

Neoseiulus californicus and P. citri were collected from papaya orchards (2011) and citrus orchards (2019), respectively. Following taxonomic confirmation, both mite species were reared to maintain robust populations for laboratory and field experiments. Rearing of both mites occurred under controlled conditions at 26 ± 1 ºC, with a photoperiod of 16h:8h (Light: Dark), and 75 ± 1% relative humidity28. Predator–prey synchrony was achieved by rearing N. californicus on P. citri. Eggs of N. californicus were isolated onto new leaf discs containing sufficient prey mites to obtain adult females of the same age.

Experimental methodology

Prior to evaluating chemically impaired functional responses of N. californicus, we tested whether predators would consume dead P. citri (killed by a needle). After 24 h, consumption data showed that N. californicus did consume dead prey, indicating that sublethal toxic effects could kill prey before predation.

The IOBC Working Group “Pesticides and Beneficial Organisms” recommends LC30 concentrations for evaluating sublethal effects, as they balance observable biological responses with organism survival, essential for ecological risk assessment, chronic toxicity investigations, and mechanistic studies. We used previously established LC30 values (0.065%, 0.049%, 0.024%, and 0.08% for SYP, abamectin, vegetable oil, and EnSpray 99, respectively, against P. citri)38,39. For controls, leaf dipping with distilled water was used. Citrus reticulata leaves were dipped into insecticide solutions for 5 s, air-dried for 10–15 min, then placed onto a water-saturated sponge with a diameter of 3.5 cm. Leaf disc edges were covered with moist tissue paper to prevent mite escape. Adults P. citri were transferred onto leaf discs at densities of 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, and 100 mites per leaf disc, based on preliminary tests demonstrating predator consumption capability.

For egg-stage experiments, 30 adult females and 10 males were placed onto citrus leaves for 24–48 h to lay eggs. Excess eggs were removed, leaving the required number per leaf disc for testing. Each egg-containing leaf disc was dipped into insecticide solutions as described above. Fertilized N. californicus females were introduced, totaling 80 replicates per density. After 24 h, prey consumption was calculated by subtracting remaining dead or alive prey. We used 2–3-day-old adult females for both predator and prey, and 1-day-old protonymphs for immature prey stages. All experimental conditions were maintained as previously described (26 ± 1 ºC, 16h:8h (L:D), 75 ± 1% RH)28.

To assess numerical responses, we recorded egg production by N. californicus after measuring prey consumption over 48 h. Consumption was assessed at 24 h, after which remaining intact prey eggs were removed to prevent further feeding. Predators were maintained under controlled conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 60 ± 5% RH, 16h:8h (L:D)) for an additional 24 h before recording predator egg production.

Results

Estimating functional response

The chemically impaired functional response of N. californicus to various densities (5, 10, 20, 30, 60, and 100) and life stages (eggs, immatures, and adults) of P. citri treated with different chemicals (SYP-9625, abamectin, vegetable oil, and EnSpray 99) is presented in Fig. 1. Functional response curves demonstrated considerable differences among treatments. Estimating predation rate

Predator daily consumption was higher at a prey density of 60 across all treatments and lowest at the lowest density of 5. Vegetable oil attracted predators and resulted in the highest predation rate, leaving 12.5%, 32.5%, and 22.5% of eggs, immatures, and adults uneaten, respectively, with decreased uneaten prey as prey density increased. Consumption rates were significantly reduced by abamectin-impaired eggs (3.5% to 18.125%) and adults (4.5% to 19.375%), followed by EnSpray 99, SYP-9625, and control treatments. Immatures impaired by EnSpray 99 and abamectin significantly reduced predator consumption rates compared to other treatments (Fig. 1).

Daily prey consumption showed significant differences between prey densities and chemical treatments except for vegetable oil-impaired eggs and immatures, and SYP-9625-impaired immatures across densities. Non-significant differences occurred at prey densities of 10 (F = 5.18, P = 0.002) and 30 (F = 3.66, P = 0.014) (Table S1). Detailed analyses confirmed three distinct functional responses depending on prey stage and chemical impairment (Fig. 1, Table S1). ANOVA indicated significant effects of prey density, prey stage, and chemical treatment on predator consumption rates, including significant interactions (Table 3). Regression analyses (Table 4, Figure S1) demonstrated a strong relationship between N. californicus consumption and prey densities at all life stages and chemical treatments (P < 0.001). Predation rates were highest for immatures in controls (slope b = 0.069) and lowest in abamectin-impaired treatments. SYP-9625 and EnSpray 99 treatments enhanced egg predation (r2 = 0.58 for EnSpray 99), suggesting improved biological control efficacy. Discussion

Our results demonstrate that sublethal pesticide exposure can markedly alter the functional response of the predatory mite N. californicus to its prey P. citri, with significant implications for predator efficiency and pest suppression3,46. Consistent with previous studies, N. californicus generally exhibited a Type II functional response under control conditions, characterized by a decelerating predation rate as prey density increased47,48. A Type II response is commonly observed in phytoseiid mites preying on tetranychid pests, indicating that the proportion of prey consumed declines at high prey densities due to predator satiation or handling time constraints29,47,49. However, under certain sublethal chemical treatments in our study, the predator’s functional response shifted away from the typical Type II pattern. For instance, when feeding on abamectin-impaired adult P. citri, N. californicus showed a sigmoidal Type III response, whereas EnSpray 99 oil treatments on mobile prey stages yielded a near-linear Type I response. Such shifts in functional response type highlight the predator’s behavioral plasticity and the influence of prey condition and stage on predation dynamics50,51. Environmental and physiological factors are known to induce changes in functional response curves; for example, low-quality or defended prey can elicit a Type III response in N. californicus, whereas favorable conditions typically produce a Type II response. Our findings align with this theoretical context, as the suboptimal prey conditions created by certain pesticides likely induced the predator to adopt different foraging strategies7,52,53. Notably, Type III responses are generally considered more stabilizing for predator–prey dynamics because predation intensity increases with prey density, which could improve pest suppression at high infestations, whereas Type II responses may lead to proportionally lower prey mortality at high densities and thus risk pest outbreaks54. The observation of a Type III functional response in some treatments suggests that N. californicus might better regulate P. citri populations under those specific conditions, though this benefit would depend on the predator maintaining its efficacy in field environments53. Conversely, a Type I response (linear prey consumption) observed with certain oil-impacted prey stages indicates predator consumption did not decelerate with density, potentially due to uniformly easy capture of incapacitated prey, but such a response could also result in inadequate control if the predator’s intake is capped by early satiation or time constraints40. These shifts underscore the need for context-specific evaluation of predator–prey interactions when integrating chemical and biological control – a point reinforced by prior research showing that factors like prey species, predator acclimation, and environmental conditions can all modulate functional response outcomes29,47,50,55.

Importantly, our study reveals clear stage-specific predator–prey dynamics that affect predation rates. Neoseiulus californicus did not prey equally on all life stages of P. citri, instead displaying higher attack rates and shorter handling times on the immature stages than on eggs or adults of the pest mite47,56. This is consistent with earlier findings that N. californicus and related phytoseiids are more efficient at consuming mobile stages (larvae and nymphs) of spider mites, while eggs (and especially adult prey) tend to be less readily exploited47,48. For example, Xiao et al.48 reported that N. californicus consumed nearly an order of magnitude more citrus red mite nymphs than eggs when given a choice, highlighting a strong prey-stage preference. In our trials, predator performance was likewise stage-dependent: the predator’s maximum predation rate (T/Th) was highest on P. citri larvae and nymphs, intermediate on eggs, and lowest on adult prey, indicating that larger or better-defended stages (adult mites) escaped predation more often. These stage-specific differences have important ecological implications. Predation concentrated on immature stages can effectively suppress the pest’s reproductive potential by preventing those individuals from maturing47,57, whereas lower predation on adults might allow some egg-laying to continue if adults survive. Nonetheless, removing a high proportion of eggs and juveniles can significantly reduce future pest population growth, and N. californicus is known to be an effective biocontrol agent largely because of its capacity to curtail these developing stages of pest mites29,47. Our findings reinforce that any assessment of predator efficacy should consider prey stage composition in the field, as the predator–prey encounter rate and outcome will vary with the life stage structure of the prey population48. In practical terms, augmentative releases of N. californicus might be most effective when timed to target eruptions of mite eggs and juveniles, as the predator’s functional response to those stages is strongest, whereas additional measures may be needed to manage residual adult populations.

Sublethal exposure to pesticides had nuanced effects on N. californicus performance and functional response parameters58. We observed that certain conventional acaricides significantly impaired the predator’s predation capacity, evidenced by reduced attack rates and lower overall prey consumption in those treatments. For instance, abamectin – a widely used acaricide – caused a pronounced decrease in N. californicus’s searching efficiency and predation on P. citri, congruent with reports that abamectin is highly toxic to phytoseiid mites even at low concentrations59–61. In our experiments, abamectin-treated prey led to the predator exhibiting longer handling times and a lower maximum attack rate, indicating that the chemical either directly hampered the predator’s ability to capture and consume prey or indirectly affected the interaction (e.g., through prey condition or predator intoxication via prey). This result is in line with similar studies on other predatory mites; for example, sublethal doses of pyridaben were found to significantly diminish the predation rate and searching behavior of N. womersleyi61. Such sublethal impairments can stem from behavioral changes (reduced mobility or aggressiveness of the predator) or physiological stress (detoxification costs, impaired sensory abilities), ultimately translating to lower biocontrol efficacy46,62. On the other hand, our study found that a botanical oil-based treatment (vegetable oil, including the product EnSpray 99) had minimal negative impact on the predator’s performance; intriguingly, this treatment actually enhanced certain aspects of predation. Neoseiulus californicus exposed to prey treated with the vegetable oil showed an elevated attack rate and consumed a higher proportion of available prey compared to the control, leaving fewer prey uneaten especially at higher densities. One explanation is that the oil may have physically debilitated the P. citri (e.g., by partially immobilizing or weakening them), making them easier for the predator to catch, thus effectively boosting the predator’s feeding efficiency in those trials51. Another possibility is that the vegetable oil could act as a mild attractant or phagostimulant for the predator, as suggested by the observation that N. californicus initiated attacks more readily on oil-treated prey (evidenced by the increased attack coefficient). This finding corroborates reports that certain plant-derived oils can be compatible with or even beneficial to biological control agents – for example, degummed plant oils were found to be highly selective (low toxicity) to predatory mites like N. baraki, although at high doses they might still alter predator behavior51. Overall, our study underscores that not all “reduced-risk” or biorational pesticides are equal in their effects on natural enemies: some, like horticultural oils, may be largely innocuous or supportive of predator activity, whereas others, including certain new-generation chemicals (e.g., SYP-9625) or bio-insecticides, should be vetted carefully for sublethal impacts on predators46,59,60. By integrating such selective compounds, growers can mitigate harm to N. californicus and preserve its predatory function in mite management programs.

From an applied perspective, the compatibility of N. californicus with selective pesticides offers valuable opportunities for Integrated Pest Management (IPM) in citrus orchards. Neoseiulus californicus is already a widely used biocontrol agent against spider mites in various crops47, and our findings provide guidance on how to integrate this predator with chemical controls. The enhanced predation observed under vegetable oil treatment suggests that incorporating horticultural oils or other soft pesticides could be a viable strategy to suppress P. citri while conserving predator activity51. In practice, this means growers could apply a product like EnSpray 99 to knock back pest populations or disrupt their behavior, and simultaneously rely on N. californicus to clean up the surviving mites, with minimal risk of predator mortality or functional impairment. This approach aligns with the principles of IPM that emphasize combining biological and chemical tactics in a complementary way3,51. Conversely, our results also caution against the indiscriminate use of harsher acaricides in IPM systems. Sublethal negative effects on N. californicus (as seen with abamectin and potentially SYP-9625) could undermine biological control, leading to pest resurgence once the chemical effect wanes46,51. Therefore, it is crucial to choose pesticides that are selective – killing or disabling the pest while sparing the predator – and to apply them at timings or concentrations (such as LC30 levels) that minimize impacts on predatory mites46,59. Our study specifically used LC30 doses to explore sublethal effects, and this approach reflects realistic exposure scenarios where predators in the field encounter pesticide residues not high enough to kill them outright but potentially sufficient to alter their behavior. In an IPM context, understanding these sublethal effects helps in scheduling chemical applications (e.g., using oils or reduced-risk acaricides when predator presence is high, and reserving more toxic compounds for periods or areas where predators are absent or can be reintroduced later). We also acknowledge that field conditions present additional complexities: factors such as temperature extremes, prey refuges, and alternative food sources can all influence functional responses and predator efficiency50,53,63. For instance, structural refuges in the crop canopy might allow some P. citri individuals to escape predation at low densities, potentially inducing a Type III-like dynamic in the field even if laboratory tests showed a Type II response53. Likewise, repeated or chronic exposure of predator populations to sublethal pesticide doses over multiple generations could impair their population growth and resilience, an aspect not captured in our short-term laboratory assays62,64. Future studies should investigate the long-term and transgenerational effects of sublethal pesticide exposure on N. californicus (e.g., impacts on fecundity, longevity, and offspring performance) and how environmental stressors (like heat or drought) might interact with chemical stress to affect predator–prey dynamics. Addressing these gaps will enhance our ability to translate laboratory findings to field applications.

In summary, this work highlights that the predatory mite N. californicus maintains a high capacity to regulate P. citri populations, but its functional response and efficacy are significantly influenced by both prey life stage and sublethal chemical exposures. Type II functional responses predominated, aligning with most classical predator–prey models for this species, yet shifts to Type I or III under certain treatments illustrate a context-dependent flexibility in feeding behavior29,47. From a practical standpoint, our findings support the integration of N. californicus with selective controls – notably horticultural oils – in citrus IPM programs, as these can enhance predator performance or at least avoid impairing it, leading to more effective and sustainable suppression of citrus red mites. By contrast, care must be taken with the use of chemicals like abamectin that, even at sublethal levels, can reduce the predator’s impact on the pest. Ultimately, optimizing predator–prey interactions through informed pesticide choice and timing can improve biological control outcomes, reducing pest damage while minimizing pesticide inputs and preserving ecosystem health3,46,59. This study contributes to a better understanding of how sublethal pesticide effects translate into functional response changes and offers actionable insights for enhancing the compatibility of chemical and biological control tactics in managing P. citri and similar agricultural pests. Each element – from prey stage targeting to pesticide selectivity – plays a role in determining the success of IPM strategies, and our results underscore the importance of a holistic approach to pest management that accounts for these intricate ecological interactions.
