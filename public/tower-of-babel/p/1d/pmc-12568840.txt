Reducing surgical site infection in emergency colorectal surgery: the case for topical tranexamic acid
Authors: Rathin Gosavi, Geraldine Ooi, Thang Chien Nguyen, William Teoh, Vignesh Narasimhan
Published: 2025

Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a major challenge in emergency colorectal surgery, with rates cited as high as 80%. This is in stark contrast to elective procedures, with rates as low as 5% through implementation of SSI prevention bundles [1]. The consequences of SSI are significant; slower recovery, persistent pain, prolonged hospital stays, and higher rates of incisional hernia [2]. From a health economics perspective, SSIs impose a heavy burden: each case is estimated to cost an additional USD $18,000 in direct healthcare expenditure [3], and in Australia, affected patients face out-of-pocket expenses 2.5 times higher (AUD $1,518 vs $587) than those without infection [4].

While strategies such as wound protectors, glove changes, wound lavage and negative pressure dressings are commonly employed, their efficacy in this high-risk population is limited [5]. A scalable, low-cost adjunct that is simple to implement is needed. One such candidate may be tranexamic acid (TXA), an antifibrinolytic agent widely used in trauma, cardiac, and orthopaedic surgery. TXA inhibits plasminogen activation, reducing fibrinolysis and stabilising fibrin clots. When applied topically, usually instilled into the wound bed after fascial closure, it achieves high local concentrations with negligible systemic absorption [6]. Beyond its role in haemostasis, laboratory studies suggest TXA improves the wound milieu by dampening neutrophil activation and modulating the local inflammatory response [7].

Emerging evidence suggests that fibrinolysis is upregulated in inflamed or septic tissues, leading to rapid degradation of fibrin clots. This breakdown promotes exudate formation, wound edge separation, and accumulation of fluid that favours bacterial colonisation. By preserving fibrin integrity, TXA may reduce capillary oozing and fluid accumulation while reinforcing wound barrier function. A stable fibrin matrix acts not only as a scaffold for healing but also as a physical barrier against microbial invasion [8]. This dual benefit, haemostatic and immunological, makes TXA a particularly good candidate in contaminated wounds, where traditional antiseptic measures fall short.

Metanalysis of multiple trials in orthopaedic and breast surgery has demonstrated that topical TXA reduces wound haematoma, seroma, and infection, without increasing thromboembolic events [9]. Notably, the cost-effectiveness of TXA is extremely favourable. In Australian public hospital formularies, injectable TXA costs $0.79 per gram [10], dramatically lower than advanced wound therapies such as negative pressure devices or silver-impregnated dressings. Its long shelf life, ease of administration, and minimal training requirements further support its use in emergency settings.

Despite compelling evidence from other specialties, data on topical TXA in gastrointestinal or contaminated abdominal surgery are scarce. Emergency colorectal operations, with their high SSI rates, logistical constraints, and biologically vulnerable wounds, offer a unique opportunity to test this intervention. A pragmatic randomised trial evaluating topical TXA at skin closure could determine its role in reducing SSIs and broader complications. Even a negative result would clarify the limits of TXA’s utility and contribute meaningfully to infection prevention strategies in abdominal surgery.

In summary, topical TXA is biologically plausible, economically viable, and operationally simple. Its potential to reduce infection and improve outcomes in emergency colorectal surgery warrants formal investigation. Given the scale of the problem and the relative ease of the solution, the case for topical TXA is appealing.
