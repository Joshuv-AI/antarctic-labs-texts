Tissue-resident neutrophils serve homeostatic and immunological functions in embryos
Authors: Julian Hofmann, Laura Lintukorpi, Emmi Lokka, Venla Ojasalo, Venla Korpela, Sheyla Cisneros Montalvo, Damien Kaukonen, Lin Ma, Noora Kotaja, Heidi Gerke, Marko Salmi, Pia Rantakari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Development of neutrophils in the bone marrow and their crucial role in first-line defense are well understood in adults, but remarkably little is known about fetal neutrophils. Here, we analyze the production, distribution, and functions of neutrophils during embryonic development in the mouse. We discover that multiple non-hematopoietic steady-state organs harbor substantial numbers of immature and mature neutrophils, many of which are localized in the tissue parenchyma outside the blood vasculature. Using single-cell transcriptomic analyses, we reveal the presence of neutrophil progenitors and precursor cells in the blood and even in non-hematopoietic tissues in fetal and newborn mice. Embryonic tissue-resident neutrophils are transcriptionally different from embryonic blood-borne neutrophils and adult neutrophils. We demonstrate, through functional analyses, that embryonic neutrophils proliferate actively, have a high glycolytic capacity, and undergo partial diurnal rhythmicity. Embryonic neutrophils display lineage-specific innate immune effector functions and are responsive to maternal immunostimulation and immunosuppression. Using a genetic embryonic neutrophil depletion model, we discover that neutrophils may impact homeostatic processes in the testis. Collectively, our data provides a cross-tissue atlas of the fetal neutrophil landscape and assesses their responses in steady-state.

Introduction

Bone marrow-derived neutrophils are short-lived immune cells that migrate from the blood into sites of injury to engage in various antimicrobial functions, including the secretion of effector molecules and phagocytosis of pathogens1–4. In addition to their well-established role as the first line of innate immune defense in acute infections, studies in postnatal mice have revealed that neutrophils can infiltrate non-infected, steady-state tissues5. A heavy neutrophil infiltrate was observed in the bone marrow and spleen parenchyma, and within capillaries in the lungs, whereas only a few cells were present, typically located within blood vessels, in tissues such as the liver, adipose tissue, skin, and muscle. Interestingly, no neutrophils were observed in the brain, ovaries, and testis under steady-state conditions. The prolonged presence of neutrophils in many healthy tissues suggests that these myeloid cells may perform regulatory or supportive functions, possibly contributing to tissue homeostasis and immune surveillance6.

In mice, neutrophils are first detected in the blood on embryonic day 11.5 (E11.5)7,8. These early embryonic neutrophils differentiate from erythro-myeloid progenitor cells (EMPs) that colonize the fetal liver at E9.5, initiating the production of fetal erythrocytes, monocytes, and neutrophilic granulocytes. As the embryo develops, granulopoiesis gradually shifts to the bone marrow, where mature neutrophils are produced from hematopoietic stem cells via multipotential progenitors, granulocyte-macrophage progenitors, pro-neutrophils, pre-neutrophils, and immature neutrophils. However, the presence of neutrophils in fetal tissues (apart from tissues with hematopoietic activity) and their potential functions in an essentially sterile milieu in utero remain unexplored.

Here, we demonstrate that neutrophils are present at early stages in most steady-state embryonic tissues, not only within the vasculature but also as extravascular resident cells. Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), we generated a cross-tissue atlas of embryonic neutrophils, revealing that although embryonic neutrophils follow a developmental trajectory similar to that of adult neutrophils, they display distinct transcriptional programs. Functional assays further demonstrate that embryonic neutrophils are already competent to execute canonical neutrophil functions, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and phagocytosis. Notably, we also show that embryonic neutrophils respond to maternal cues, indicating early environmental imprinting during development.

Results

Immature and mature neutrophil populations are present in non-hematopoietic tissues in embryos

To characterize steady-state neutrophils during development, we performed flow cytometric analyses of multiple tissues from wild-type (WT) mice housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. In newborn (NB) mice, we observed neutrophils, identified as CD11b+Ly-6G+ cells, not only in hematopoietic organs (liver and spleen), but also in the lungs, pancreas, kidneys, and testes (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). On the other hand, in the thymus and brain, no or only very occasional CD11b+Ly-6G+ cells were found (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). To determine the kinetics of neutrophil infiltration in detail, we collected blood and tissues from steady-state mice at embryonic day (E) 17.5, at birth, and postnatally at 1, 2, 5, and 8 weeks of age and identified the frequency and absolute numbers of immature and mature neutrophils using flow cytometry (Fig. 1a, b). In the liver, we found that 70–90% of the CD45+CD11b+SiglecFNegative/LowLy-6C+Ly-6G+ cells (hereafter assigned as neutrophils) had an immature CD101LowCXCR2Low phenotype in the prenatal, NB, and 1-week-old mice (Supplementary Fig. 1d–f). In 2-week-old and older mice, in contrast, less than 30% of the liver neutrophils were immature (Supplementary Fig. 1f). This transition coincided with a decrease in the frequency and absolute numbers of neutrophils in the liver at 2 weeks of age (Supplementary Fig. 1e). In the bone marrow, neutrophils were present from E17.5 to 8-week-old mice (Supplementary Fig. 1g–i), their absolute number increased exponentially with time, and the immature neutrophils dominated at each time point without any declining trend (Supplementary Fig. 1h). In the blood, where the frequency of neutrophils peaked at birth, the proportions of immature and mature neutrophils were roughly equal from E17.5 to 1-week-old mice whereas in older mice, most of the cells displayed the mature phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 1j, k). We notably observed substantial numbers of neutrophils in non-hematopoietic tissues during early development (Fig. 1c–h and Supplementary Fig. 1c). In the kidney, 5–10% of leukocytes were immature neutrophils and 3–15% mature neutrophils at E17.5 and in NB. The number of mature neutrophils was at the highest level (1000–2000 cells/kidney) in NB mice but remained detectable until the end of the 8-week analysis period (Fig. 1d). A similar pattern of a predominantly pre- and perinatal neutrophil infiltrate (20–30% of all leukocytes) consisting of both immature and mature cells was observed in the testis (Fig. 1f, g). Analyses of the cellular morphology of sorted Ly-6G+ cells revealed a characteristic ring-shaped and multilobular nuclear morphology of immature and mature neutrophils, respectively, already in E17.5 and NB mice tissues (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 1l). Embryonic neutrophils are extravascular in the tissue parenchyma

To define the distribution of neutrophils within embryonic tissues, we used a neutrophil-specific Ly6G Cre-tdT/Cre-tdT9 reporter mouse and crossed it with R26tdT/tdT mice for improved tracking of neutrophils via tdTomato (tdT) expression (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Our flow cytometry analyses revealed 25–35% labeling efficiency of Ly-6G+ neutrophils in different tissues of E17.5 neutrophil-specific reporter embryos (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Using 3D confocal imaging of optically cleared wholemount tissues, the tdT+ positive neutrophils were detected in all hematopoietic tissues studied (liver, bone marrow, spleen; Supplementary Fig. 2c–e). In the E17.5 kidney, the tdT+ positive neutrophils were scattered throughout the tissue parenchyma (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2f). Although a few tdT+ positive neutrophils were found close to blood vessel walls, the majority of tdT+ positive neutrophils appeared to reside outside the vessels (Fig. 2a, b, and Supplementary Video 1). Occasionally, we found neutrophils in small clusters both in the cortex and outer medulla of the kidney (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2f). Additionally, embryonic neutrophils were observed sometimes lodged between tubule epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 2g). In the E17.5 testis, neutrophils were concentrated around the highly vascular connective tissue in the mediastinum and the rete testis area that is involved in the transport of sperm cells from the testicle to the epididymis10 (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 2h, Supplementary Videos 2 and 3). To better discriminate between tissue-resident and intravascular neutrophils, we performed intravascular in situ administration of a fluorescently labeled Ly-6G antibody via the umbilical vein of E17.5 embryos. One minute after injection, tissues were collected and processed into a single-cell suspension, after which cells were ex vivo stained with the same Ly-6G antibody conjugated to a different fluorochrome (Supplementary Fig. 2i). The in situ labeling step marks circulating neutrophils, whereas the subsequent ex vivo staining labels all neutrophils, enabling differentiation of tissue neutrophils from blood-borne neutrophils. To ensure the effectiveness of in situ labeling, we used blood neutrophils as an internal control and only included in the analysis the embryos in which 95–100% of blood neutrophils were labeled. In these embryos, flow cytometric analysis of the kidney and testis revealed both in situ-labeled Ly-6G⁺ neutrophils (representing intravascular cells) and only ex vivo-labeled neutrophils (representing extravascular, tissue-resident cells) in varying proportions (Supplementary Fig. 2i), consistent with our imaging data. Overall, our observations revealed a significant extravascular population of neutrophils in the parenchyma of non-hematopoietic tissues in normal embryos.

Embryonic neutrophils show a linear developmental trajectory

Next, we used scRNA-seq to generate a comprehensive cross-tissue transcriptional map and to characterize embryonic neutrophils during steady-state tissue development (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). We sorted and sequenced CD45+ cells from the bone marrow, liver, blood, kidney, and testis of E17.5 and NB WT mice. To compare neutrophils from embryos and adults, we further integrated our embryonic data with published scRNA-seq data of sorted leukocytes from blood and bone marrow of normal 8–15-week-old mice2,11,12 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To focus on neutrophils, we performed fine-resolution clustering and annotated seven clusters using unbiased analysis of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Data 1). We identified a common hematopoietic progenitor cluster expressing genes, such as Flt3, Runx1, and Kit, and a cluster of granulocyte-monocyte progenitor-like cells (GMP-like) marked by the expression of Cebpa (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). Two clusters of pro-neutrophils (proNeu1 and proNeu2) and a pre-neutrophil (preNeu) cluster were also detectable. Finally, the biggest neutrophil cluster expressed markers of immature neutrophils (e.g., Mmp8 and Mmp9), and the seventh neutrophil cluster represented mature neutrophils expressing, e.g., Cxcr2 and Il1b (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). Correlation and pseudotime trajectory analysis projected the clusters onto a single continuum developmental trajectory (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). In vivo validation using 5-bromo-20-deoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling, which marks proliferating cells and enables tracking of their differentiation3,13 revealed that only immature neutrophils in the fetal liver had incorporated BrdU between the 2- and 24-h time points (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Immature neutrophils differentiated into mature forms by the 48-h time point and had largely disappeared after 72 h. Concurrently, the number of mature neutrophils increased, suggesting conserved developmental dynamics between embryonic and adult neutrophils.

Similar to adult bone marrow, distinct neutrophil populations were also observed at the E17.5 and newborn tissues (Fig. 3d). However, E17.5 mice had the highest frequency of early progenitors, while the proportion of mature neutrophils was the lowest at E17.5 when compared to newborn and adult mice (Fig. 3f). We found distinct cluster-to-tissue distribution patterns among neutrophil sub-clusters (Fig. 3e). In the fetal liver and adult bone marrow, approximately 1/3 of the neutrophils were early precursors (progenitors, preNeus, and proNeus), and approximately 2/3 were immature neutrophils. In blood, a few precursors were found in E17.5 and NB mice, but not in adults, and the proportion of mature neutrophils increased progressively with age, culminating in the majority of neutrophils in adult blood being fully mature (Fig. 3e). In the embryonic and NB kidney and testis, both immature and mature neutrophils and, interestingly, also a few progenitor cells were observed (Fig. 3e).

We next evaluated whether neutrophil transcriptional profiles were similar across embryonic tissues or more aligned with blood neutrophil profiles. Our results showed that at E17.5, neutrophils in the kidney and testis typically expressed fewer than 50 tissue-specific DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 1). Immature neutrophils in the bone marrow and liver displayed a higher number of DEGs (366 and 564, respectively), of which the vast majority were downregulated, compared to blood neutrophils (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Data 1). Gene ontology analysis revealed that both upregulated and downregulated genes in embryonic tissue-resident neutrophils were predominantly linked to general biological processes, such as the regulation of aerobic respiration and other cellular metabolic processes (Fig. 3g, h, and Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). By the NB stage, these gene expression differences were vastly diminished (Supplementary Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 1). This aligned with our observation that no cluster-to-tissue distribution patterns emerged in our scRNA-seq analyses. Overall, our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that the neutrophil development trajectory in utero is linear, neutrophils are present in steady-state non-hematopoietic tissues, and that a few progenitor and early precursor cells are also identifiable among tissue-resident neutrophils, which are dominated by immature neutrophils.

Developmental-stage-dependent factors uniquely shape neutrophil transcriptomic profiles

Although embryonic and adult neutrophils follow broadly conserved differentiation trajectories, they arise in distinct anatomical niches, with embryonic progenitors developing in the fetal liver and adult progenitors in the bone marrow. To assess whether this spatial difference is associated with differences in transcriptional programs, we performed comparative pathway analyses on matched neutrophil populations, excluding the low-abundance GMP-like and proNeu1 clusters, from E17.5 fetal liver and adult bone marrow. Comparative analysis revealed transcriptional differences between embryonic and adult neutrophils across all stages of neutrophil differentiation. Both proNeu2 and preNeu cells isolated from E17.5 fetal liver exhibited a shared set of differentially expressed genes relative to adult bone marrow progenitors (Fig. 4a, b, and Supplementary Data 1). In addition to these shared changes, proNeu2 and preNeu populations also displayed some distinct subset-specific pathway signatures (Fig. 4c, d, and Supplementary Data 2), suggesting heterogeneity within the embryonic neutrophil progenitor compartment. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed clear transcriptional differences between embryonic and adult neutrophil progenitors. E17.5 fetal liver preNeu and proNeu2 populations were enriched for pathways associated with regulation of molecular and catalytic activity, leukocyte activation, and myeloid cell development (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Data 2), consistent with active differentiation programs. In addition to progenitor-stage differences, we observed transcriptional divergence between immature and mature neutrophils when comparing E17.5 and adult cells in hematopoietic organs and blood (Fig. 4f, g, and Supplementary Data 1). Immature neutrophils from E17.5 embryos exhibited differential gene expression compared with adult immature neutrophils (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Data 1), although to a lower extent than observed in mature cells (Fig. 4g). Embryonic immature neutrophils showed enrichment of pathways related to metabolic remodeling, biosynthetic activity, and cellular growth (Supplementary Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 2), consistent with a developmental state biased toward expansion and differentiation. Conversely, immune-associated pathways were globally downregulated in embryonic immature neutrophils (Supplementary Data 2), suggesting restrained immune priming during fetal life.

Mature neutrophils from embryonic hematopoietic organs and blood exhibited more differentially expressed genes than immature neutrophils when compared with adult cells (Fig. 4g). We observed that mature embryonic neutrophils highly expressed stefin family (type 1 cystatins) genes, such as Stfa1, Sfta2, and Stfa3 (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 5c, and Supplementary Data 1), which encode cytoplasmic inhibitors of cysteine proteases and play a crucial role in the cellular antiviral response14. Similarly, Olfactomedin 4 (Olfm4), another protease inhibitor, was expressed consistently in embryonic neutrophils from the pro-neutrophil stage to mature neutrophils. In contrast, in adults, Olfm4 expression was limited to a small, specific neutrophil subpopulation (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Data 1), as previously reported15. To validate these findings at the protein level, we analyzed neutrophils from multiple tissues by flow cytometry. OLFM4 was expressed by the majority of Ly-6G⁺ neutrophils in E17.5 embryos, both in circulation (84.2% ± 4.8) and liver (76.3% ± 4.8) (Fig. 4i), whereas only a small fraction of neutrophils in adult blood (6.7% ± 2.3) and bone marrow (7.6% ± 2.0) were OLFM4⁺ (Fig. 4j). Mature embryonic neutrophils in the blood highly expressed signaling molecules like Lyn, Syk, and Vav3, which were only weakly expressed in adult blood neutrophils (Fig. 4h). Genes down-regulated in mature embryonic neutrophils were often linked to antigen presentation on MHC-I, such as B2m, and cytoplasmic translation (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 5e). We found that the genes upregulated in mature embryonic neutrophils often differed both in the fraction of positive cells and in mean expression level, whereas the downregulated genes differed only in mean expression level. Pathways upregulated in embryonic mature neutrophils were enriched for metabolic and cellular maintenance programs (Supplementary Fig. 5f and Supplementary Data 2), consistent with adaptation to the fetal environment despite terminal differentiation. In contrast, pathways associated with cytoplasmic translation, ribosome assembly, and oxidative phosphorylation were reduced compared with adult bone marrow neutrophils (Fig. 4k and Supplementary Data 2), indicating lower biosynthetic and mitochondrial metabolic activity. Notably, many genes upregulated in E17.5 immature and mature neutrophils remained elevated in newborn neutrophils relative to adults (Supplementary Fig. 5g and Supplementary Data 1). Collectively, these data indicate that while core features of neutrophil development are conserved, embryonic and adult neutrophils engage distinct molecular programs that are evident from the progenitor stage onward. Moreover, our analyses identify transcriptomic and protein markers that selectively define embryonic-type neutrophils.

Embryonic neutrophils have the capacity for lineage-specific functions

To assess the functional capacity of embryonic neutrophils, we conducted in vitro experiments to measure phagocytosis, ROS production, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. To study phagocytosis, we first incubated a single-cell suspension prepared from embryonic bone marrow, liver, and kidneys, and from adult bone marrow as a control, with 0.5-µm fluorescent polystyrene beads (Fig. 5a). Flow cytometric analyses revealed that a lower percentage of embryonic bone marrow-derived mature neutrophils were loaded with beads compared to adult bone marrow-derived cells. Moreover, mature fetal neutrophils isolated from liver, bone marrow, and kidney had comparable phagocytic capacity (Fig. 5b). Immature neutrophils in both embryos and adults were poorly phagocytic. ROS production is one canonical outcome of phagolysosome activation in neutrophils16. We therefore analyzed spontaneous and phorbol myristate acetate-induced (PMA) ROS production capacity in immature and mature embryonic neutrophils using the CellROX green-based flow cytometric assay (Fig. 5c). We found comparable basal ROS production in immature and mature neutrophils derived from embryonic bone marrow, liver, and kidney (Fig. 5d). Similar to adult bone marrow neutrophils, a significantly higher CellROX green signal was observed after PMA induction in immature and mature embryonic bone marrow, liver, and kidney neutrophils (Fig. 5d). We also evaluated the potential of embryonic neutrophils to form NETs. Using PMA-stimulated release of DNA as a read-out, we observed comparable NET-like structures in single-cell suspensions of cells from E17.5 embryonic liver and adult bone marrow (Fig. 5e). Together, these functional analyses show that embryonic neutrophils are already endowed with effector functions like phagocytosis, ROS production, and NETosis.

Embryonic neutrophils exhibit enhanced proliferation and metabolic flexibility

In adults, mature neutrophils are thought to be non-proliferative, short-lived, metabolically active cells that are mainly cleared by apoptosis4. Therefore, we next determined the proliferative capacity of embryonic neutrophils by analyzing more closely the 2-h time point after BrdU pulse in various embryonic tissues (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 3g). We found the biggest fraction of BrdU+ S-phase neutrophils in E17.5 liver, in which, on average, 10.3% (±3.2) of immature neutrophils proliferated (Fig. 6a). This was almost two times more than in adult bone marrow (5.5% ± 0.9). In embryonic liver, but not in the embryonic or adult bone marrow, BrdU+ mature neutrophils were also observed. Also, embryonic testis and kidney harbored a small number of proliferating immature neutrophils (Fig. 6a). To assess the viability of embryonic neutrophils, we conducted flow cytometry analyses of Annexin V staining and fixable viability dye (FVD) binding (Fig. 6b). We found that 60–80% of neutrophils isolated from E17.5 liver, bone marrow, kidney, and testis were FVD–Annexin V– (viable), which was comparable to the frequency of viable adult bone marrow neutrophils. E17.5 tissue-resident neutrophils showed oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial ATP synthesis as the top upregulated gene pathways compared to blood neutrophils (Fig. 3g). To functionally assess the metabolic activity of embryonic neutrophils in the E17.5 liver and kidney, we took advantage of the flow cytometry-based single-cell method SCENITH17. We found that embryonic neutrophils relied primarily on glycolysis when glucose was readily available (Fig. 6c). No significant difference was observed between embryonic and adult neutrophils in their capacity to utilize fatty acids and amino acids as alternative energy sources when glucose oxidation was blocked (Fig. 6c). Consistent with previous findings18, adult bone marrow-derived immature neutrophils exhibited a greater reliance on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) compared to mature neutrophils. However, this dependency was not observed in embryonic neutrophils (Fig. 6c). Notably, the embryonic immature cells had a better glycolytic capacity than adult immature cells to sustain protein synthesis when mitochondrial OXPHOS was inhibited (Fig. 6c). Collectively, our analyses indicate that embryonic neutrophils exhibit enhanced glycolytic capacity, different kinetics in progression of apoptosis compared to adult cells, and a higher proliferative capacity. Moreover, proliferating tissue-resident neutrophils also existed in embryos.

Embryonic neutrophils respond to maternal stimulation

We then studied whether maternal cues impact embryonic neutrophils. Glucocorticoids are routinely used to enhance lung maturation in preterm infants19. Because glucocorticoids are also powerful immunosuppressants, we investigated their effects on embryonic neutrophils. To detect the acute effect of dexamethasone (DEX) on embryonic neutrophils, we administered DEX (5 mg/kg) or PBS intraperitoneally (i.p.) to the pregnant dam at E17.5 (Supplementary Fig. 6a), and collected the embryonic blood and kidneys after 2 h for scRNA-seq analyses (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c and Supplementary Data 3). DEGs analyses showed that in both blood and kidney, immature and mature neutrophils exhibited reduced expression of genes associated with inflammatory processes. The down-regulated genes included those regulating neutrophil differentiation (Lrrc25, Hist1h1e, and Alas1)20–22, recruitment (Eno1, Orai1, and Gadd45a)23–25, and NET formation (Il1b, Clec5a)26,27, whereas the expression of several genes with anti-inflammatory functions (Arrb2, Gpr141, Cited2, Nfkbia, Dusp1, Il1r2, and Tsc22d3)28–31 was markedly elevated (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e).

Both low-grade chronic inflammation (e.g., maternal obesity, gestational diabetes) and acute inflammatory conditions (e.g., bacterial infections) of the mother can affect the fetus32,33. We used lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced gestational inflammation to study the inflammatory responses of embryonic neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 6f–h). To induce mild maternal inflammation, pregnant dams were administered i.p. 100 µg/kg of LPS at E17.5, and embryonic tissues were collected 4 h post-treatment for scRNA-seq analyses (Supplementary Fig. 6f). LPS treatment resulted in an apparent increase in the proportion of mature versus immature neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h) compared to both steady-state conditions (Fig. 3e) and DEX-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). However, LPS stimulation did not significantly alter gene expression in immature or mature neutrophils in the blood or the kidney (Supplementary Fig. 6i, j, and Supplementary Data 3). Overall, the results reveal that embryonic neutrophils are responsive to external stimuli, such as glucocorticoids, suggesting that embryonic neutrophils may serve as a cellular reservoir ready to provide innate immune protection to the fetus in utero and at birth.

In adults, the number of circulating neutrophils follows circadian oscillations34,35. To explore whether embryonic neutrophils exhibit circadian properties, we collected tissues from E17.5 embryos at multiple times of day for transcriptional analysis (Fig. 6d). In adults, neutrophils are released from the bone marrow with high surface expression of CD62L, which gradually declines over the course of the day36. Therefore, neutrophils can be broadly divided into CD62LHigh “fresh” recently released neutrophils and CD62LLow “aged” neutrophils returning to the bone marrow. Our analysis showed that in fetal bone marrow neutrophils, Sell (encoding CD62L) expression showed a circadian oscillation (Fig. 6e), peaking during the active phase and declining after “lights on” (Zeitgeber time 0, ZT0). In contrast, expression of genes that were shown by Adrover et al.34 to be circadian sensitive did not show clear circadian variation in the fetal liver (Supplementary Fig. 6k) or in the kidney (Supplementary Fig. 6l).

When studying L-selectin protein expression at the times ZT5 and ZT13, which represent the highest reported difference in frequency of aged neutrophils in the adult blood35 (Fig. 6d), we observed that both embryonic and adult neutrophils in the bone marrow (Fig. 6f) and blood (Supplementary Fig. 6m) showed fluctuations in the frequency of aged CD62LLow neutrophils in accordance with the light-dark rhythm. Notably, the total leukocyte frequency (among live cells) remained stable from ZT5 to ZT13 in embryonic blood, unlike in adults (Supplementary Fig. 6n). The frequency of aged CD62LLow neutrophils between the ZT5 and ZT13 time points also changed in the fetal liver, but not in the kidney and testis (Supplementary Fig. 6o). Together, these findings indicate that embryonic bone marrow neutrophils already exhibit features of circadian regulation; however, tissue-specific neutrophil circadian regulation likely becomes fully coordinated only after birth.

Gfi1 genetic model reveals more efficient fetal neutrophil depletion compared to antibody and Cre-based strategies

To examine the effects of neutropenia in embryonic tissues, we initially applied an anti-Ly-6G antibody (clone 1A8), commonly used in adults to specifically deplete neutrophils37. We administered 1 mg of anti-Ly-6G antibody i.p. to pregnant females at E12.5, E14.5, and E16.5. After ex vivo extracellular Ly-6G staining using a fluorochrome-conjugated anti-Ly-6G antibody (clone 1A8), we found a near complete absence of Ly-6G⁺ cells in the liver of E17.5 embryos (Supplementary Fig. 7a). However, when we used a secondary antibody ex vivo to detect the in vivo injected anti-Ly-6G antibody, we observed that Ly-6G+ neutrophils were not depleted but instead became undetectable by conventional direct Ly-6G surface staining due to antigen masking (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Ly-6G depletion has been reported to be improved by an “isotype switch” strategy, in which the Ly6-G antibody 1A8 (rat IgG2a; depleting by Fc-dependent macrophage opsonization) is followed by a complement-activating mouse IgG2a anti-rat antibody38,39. We therefore injected pregnant mice daily from E12.5 to E16.5 with anti-Ly-6G antibody (250 µg on the first day, followed by 100 µg on subsequent days) and administrated an anti-rat IgG2a antibody (100 µg) every other day starting at E12.5 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). However, we still found numerous Ly-6G+ cells in the livers of the E17.5 embryos (Supplementary Fig. 7b), which led us to conclude that the anti-Ly-6G antibody is an inefficient tool for neutrophil depletion in embryos. Next, we crossed neutrophil-specific Ly6gCre-tdT/Cre-tdT mice with DTAlox/lox (Ly6gCre-DTA) mice to enable the conditional expression of diphtheria toxin in neutrophils. However, only a ~50% reduction in the frequency of Ly-6G+ blood leukocytes was observed in Ly6gCre-DTA mice, and the depletion efficacy in tissue neutrophils was even lower (Supplementary Fig. 7c). This partial ablation may be attributed to the presence of Ly-6GLow neutrophil or Ly-6GNegative pre-neutrophil populations in the tissues that escape Cre-mediated recombination because of the low efficiency of Ly6g+/Cre-tdT mice (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

To achieve more extensive neutrophil depletion, we took advantage of a previously generated Gfi1R412X/R412X (hereafter called Gfi1mut/mut) mouse model40. Growth factor independence 1 (Gfi1) is a transcription factor regulating the differentiation of neutrophils from their progenitors41,42, directing pre-T-cell differentiation43,44, formation of inner ear hair cells and pancreatic acinar/centroacinar unit45–47, and the development of secretory cell types in the intestine48. Complete knockout of Gfi-1 affects multiple leukocyte lineages and disrupts development in various cell types. However, the Gfi1R412X mutation, which introduces a premature stop codon, selectively prevents only the formation of mature neutrophils in postnatal mice40,49. We observed that 4-week-old Gfi1mut/mut mice had 6.6% Ly-6G+ cells in bone marrow and 3.7% in the blood, whereas WT controls had 21.4% and 8.6%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Reduction of neutrophils was accompanied by monocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 7e). We then used heterozygous Gfi1+/mut mice for breeding to assess embryonic neutrophils. At E17.5, Gfi1mut/mut embryos showed a more pronounced reduction in neutrophil numbers than 4-week-old mutants, with a significant decrease observed both in hematopoietic and peripheral tissues (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 7f, g). A mild monocytosis was also detected in the E17.5 peripheral blood (Supplementary Fig. 7h). Notably, heterozygous Gfi1+/mut embryos, but not 4-week-old (Supplementary Fig. 7d), also displayed a modest reduction in neutrophils, without accompanying changes in monocyte numbers (Supplementary Fig. 7f–h). Neutrophil reduction alters the fetal testis transcriptome

We finally used the Gfi1mut/mut mice to analyze the functional consequences of diminished neutrophil counts during development. Histological analysis of E17.5 liver, kidney, and testis revealed no detectable differences in overall organ architecture or morphology between Gfi1mut/mut and the control (Supplementary Fig. 7i). To assess whether the absence of neutrophils in Gfi1mut/mut embryos affects the establishment of tissue-resident macrophage populations, many of which are established before birth from fetal liver-derived monocytes50–55, we performed flow cytometry analysis on 4-week-old Gfi1mut/mut mice. However, no difference in macrophage frequencies in liver, kidney, or testis was observed between Gfi1mut/mut and control mice at 4-week-old (Fig. 7b), apart from a minor increase of F4/80Intermediate macrophages in the 4-week-old kidney of Gfi1mut/mut mice (Fig. 7b).

Neutrophils have been extensively studied in hematopoietic tissues, such as bone marrow and blood, but their roles in non-hematopoietic tissues are less well-known. To investigate whether neutrophil reduction affects the function of non-hematopoietic organs, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of E17.5 testes and kidneys, which undergo critical developmental processes at this stage and contain resident neutrophils. The kidney exhibited only minor transcriptional changes (Supplementary Fig. 8a and Supplementary Data 4), whereas the testis showed pronounced alterations, with 63 upregulated and 474 downregulated genes in Gfi1mut/mut embryos (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Data 4). Gene enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated genes in the testis were associated with cholesterol metabolism and steroid biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 8b), whereas downregulated genes were associated with piRNA metabolic processes and DNA methylation (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 8c). Our observation that monocyte or macrophage numbers in the E17.5 testis were not altered in Gfi1mut/mut mice (Supplementary Fig. 8d) is consistent with the possibility that alterations in gene pathways in the testis are associated with neutrophil loss.

piRNAs are essential for silencing retrotransposons, including long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), one of the most active autonomous transposable elements in mammalian genomes56. However, LINE-1 expression was not altered in Gfi1mut/mut E17.5 testes compared to the control (Supplementary Fig. 8e), and surprisingly, global analysis demonstrated a predominant reduction in many LINEs, SINEs, and LTR elements targeted by piRNAs in the Gfi1mut/mut testes (Supplementary Fig. 8f and Supplementary Data 5). Furthermore, despite the established role of gonadal piRNAs in spermatogenesis57–59, no morphological abnormalities were observed in Gfi1mut/mut testes at 7 weeks of age (Supplementary Fig. 8g). In females, the occurrence of vaginal plugs was similar between Gfi1+/mut and control in fertility assays (Supplementary Fig. 8g). However, the rate of successful pregnancies following plug detection (WT 85.6% vs. Gfi1+/mut 66.0%) and the overall pregnancy rate (WT 73.0% vs Gfi1+/mut 52.1%) was compromised in the Gfi1+/mut group compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 8h). Together, our findings suggest a potential role for embryonic neutrophils in shaping germline-associated transcriptional programs, although the functional consequences of these changes remain to be determined and will require further mechanistic investigation.

Discussion

Neutrophil biology in non-hematopoietic organs under steady-state conditions and especially in fetuses has remained virtually unexplored. Here, we demonstrate kinetically shifting neutrophil infiltrates in multiple non-hematopoietic tissues during embryogenesis under homeostatic conditions. Notably, fetal neutrophils predominantly resided within the parenchyma of tissues rather than within the blood vasculature. The tissue-resident embryonic neutrophils were transcriptionally different from circulating embryonic neutrophils and from adult neutrophils. We also showed that the embryonic neutrophils responded to maternal stimulation. The embryonic neutrophils were competent at triggering canonical innate immune responses, and analyses of neutrophil-deficient mice suggested that embryonic tissue-resident neutrophils may also perform homeostatic functions.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that embryonic neutrophils follow a linear developmental trajectory from progenitor cells through pro- and pre-neutrophils and immature neutrophils to mature neutrophils. Consistent with ongoing developmental hematopoiesis, fetal tissues contained a higher proportion of immature neutrophils, many of which were actively proliferating, than adult tissues. Although neutrophil development occurs in distinct hematopoietic niches during development and adulthood, these differences are not fully resolved during neutrophil maturation. Mature embryonic neutrophils remained transcriptionally distinct from adult bone marrow neutrophils, indicating that terminal differentiation does not erase embryonic identity but preserves aspects of fetal programming. This persistence of a fetal transcriptional signature in mature neutrophils may suggest that embryonic neutrophils fulfill functions adapted to the developmental context, potentially contributing not only to early immune surveillance but also to tissue remodeling and organogenesis in the sterile intrauterine environment.

We found fetal neutrophils within normal, uninflamed non-hematopoietic organs. Notably, intravascular labeling combined with whole-mount imaging revealed that many tissue-resident neutrophils occupied extravascular locations in non-hematopoietic organs. This spatial positioning, together with their distinct transcriptional profile, suggests that embryonic tissue-resident neutrophils constitute a distinct population from circulating blood neutrophils. Therefore, neutrophil distribution under steady-state conditions in embryos differs from the adult equivalent, where neutrophils are normally largely intravascular and primarily infiltrate tissues in response to inflammation.

We observed that OLFM4 is expressed much more broadly in embryonic neutrophils compared to adult neutrophils, at both the mRNA and protein levels. OLFM4 has been shown to directly limit neutrophil effector functions by inhibiting the activity of antimicrobial and inflammatory proteins, such as cathepsin C60,61, and dampening NF-κB–mediated inflammatory responses62. Hence, selective expression of OLFM4 in embryonic but not adult neutrophils may suggest that fetal neutrophils are intrinsically tuned toward a more restrained antimicrobial and inflammatory phenotype. Expression of OLFM4 is also shown to be elevated in neonatal neutrophils and during neonatal sepsis, and its absence protects juvenile mice from sepsis63. These observations suggest that OLFM4 may act as a developmental regulator that tempers neutrophil antimicrobial activity during early life. Consistent with a regulatory rather than defective state, our functional assays show that embryonic neutrophils retain intact effector machinery, as evidenced by robust phagocytosis, ROS production, and NET formation upon activation.

By residing within the parenchyma, embryonic neutrophils are well-positioned to interact with developing stromal and parenchymal cells, potentially influencing organogenesis, tissue remodeling, and local homeostasis. Recent research has begun to reveal that neutrophils can also perform important homeostatic functions in adults. In the skin, for example, matrix-producing neutrophils contribute to the composition and structure of the extracellular matrix, promoting the barrier function of the skin64. Using the genetic Gfi1mut/mut mouse model, we observed differences in the embryonic testis transcriptome, including alterations in pathways related to steroid synthesis and piRNA regulation, in the presence and partial absence of neutrophils. Importantly, macrophage numbers in the testes were not affected in Gfi1mut/mut mice, suggesting that the observed transcriptional changes are unlikely to result from altered macrophage abundance. Although these data are correlative and should be interpreted cautiously, they highlight the intriguing presence of neutrophils across embryonic non-hematopoietic tissues and suggest that their functions may be tissue-specific during development.

Our study has some limitations. We showed that several established neutrophil depletion strategies used in adult mice are not suitable for embryonic applications. It is known that in the embryonic setting, diphtheria toxin does not cross the placenta efficiently, and intra-amniotic injection do not achieve sufficient systemic exposure in embryos65. Therefore, identifying models that induce neutropenia during development or establishing more targeted strategies for embryonic neutrophil depletion will be essential to directly test their functional roles in vivo. The extravascular localization and kinetics of tissue-resident neutrophils would be best analyzed using real-time imaging (e.g., two-photon microscopy), for which no intrauterine protocols have been developed. Finally, low quantities of tissue-resident neutrophils in minute embryonic tissues also make it difficult to study the functions of isolated cells in vitro.

In summary, our findings support a model in which embryonic neutrophils are not merely immature precursors of adult blood neutrophils but instead represent a developmentally specialized and functionally competent population. Their extravascular localization under normal health conditions suggests that embryonic tissue-resident neutrophils constitute a distinct population from circulating blood neutrophils. Our data provides a comprehensive single-cell atlas of neutrophils in hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic organs under steady-state and perturbed conditions in embryonic and newborn mice. This resource provides a foundation for future studies aimed at dissecting the roles of neutrophils in organogenesis, tissue homeostasis, and immune surveillance during development. Moreover, it provides a framework for investigating how embryonic neutrophil function may be altered in pathological contexts, including congenital disorders, inflammatory conditions, and perinatal infections. Our data lays the groundwork for mechanistic studies that can uncover tissue- and developmental stage-specific neutrophil functions and guide strategies to modulate their activity in disease or therapeutic interventions.

Methods

Mice

C57BL/6N WT mice were acquired from Janvier Labs. The C57BL/6-Ly6gtm2621 (Cre-tdTomato) Arte reporter mice were kindly provided by Prof. Matthias Gunzer9. These mice were crossed with B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J for enhanced tdTomato expression. B6.129P2-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(DTA)Lky/J diphtheria toxin-producing mice were kindly provided by Prof. Pipsa Saharinen. Neutropenic Gfi1R412X/ R412X mice49 acquired from Jax lab (Gfi1em1Hlg/J, 035156). We used male embryos in the majority of experiments to maintain consistency across analyses. In selected experiments, female embryos were included as controls to confirm that the results were comparable between sexes. All mice were kept in specific pathogen-free conditions under controlled environmental conditions (temperature 21 ± 3 °C, 12 h light cycle) at the Central Animal Laboratory of the University of Turku (Turku, Finland). Mice had access to food and water ad libitum. In timed matings, the day when the vaginal plug was observed was recorded as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). Animal experiments were conducted under the revision and approval of the Regional Animal Experiment Board in Finland (license numbers 14685/2020 and 23546/2023) according to the 3R principles and the Finnish Act on Animal Experimentation (497/2013).

Cell isolation

According to the recommendations of the Animal Experiment Board, adult mice were euthanized with carbon dioxide asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, newborn mice by decapitation, and 1-week and 2-week-old mice were terminally anesthetized with isoflurane before decapitation. Embryos were dissected from the uterus and euthanized by hypothermia followed by decapitation. Tissues were harvested around 8:00–10:00, except for the circadian rhythm studies, in which tissues were collected at several Zeitgeber times as shown in Fig. 6d.

Blood was collected by cardiac puncture from euthanized adult mice, and by bleeding onto heparin-phosphate buffered saline (PBS; D8537, Sigma-Aldrich; 50 µl of 100 IU/ml heparin (585661, LEO Pharma) in 500 µl of PBS) from euthanized E17.5–2-week-old mice. Erythrocytes were lysed from blood as described66. Testes, kidneys, liver, thymus, lungs, pancreas, brain and spleen were gently minced with scissors and digested with 50 µg/ml DNase 1 (10104159001, Roche) and 1 mg/ml Collagenase D (11088866001, Roche) in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; RNBF2378, Sigma-Aldrich) solution at 37 °C for 15 to 45 min. After digestion, the brain cells were resuspended in isotonic Percoll (17-0891-01, GE Healthcare), and the microglia were isolated as described67. The kidneys and liver from 5- to 8-week-old mice were homogenized in RPMI-1640 (R5886, Sigma-Aldrich) medium in a gentleMACS C-tube (130-093-237, Miltenyi Biotech) with a Miltenyi Biotech gentleMACS Dissociator, and leukocytes were isolated by OptiPrepTM (D1556, Sigma-Aldrich) gradient centrifugation. Bone marrow cells were collected by carefully crushing and rinsing the femora (and from embryos also tibiae) with HBSS, followed by sequential filtering through silk cloth (pore size 77 µm, 77-48Y115, Marabu Scandinavia) until no visible aggregates were detected. Finally, all the isolated cells were washed with 500 µl of PBS and suspended in PBS and filtered through a silk cloth before cytospin or labeling for cell sorting or flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Prior to antibody staining, cells were stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor® 780 or 506 (65-0865-14 or 65-0866-14, respectively, Thermo Fisher) and Fc-receptor binding was blocked with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody (clone 2.4G2, BE0307, Bio X Cell). The cells were then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies diluted in flow cytometry buffer (PBS with 2% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS, S181B-500, Biowest) and 0.05% (v/v) sodium azide (S2002, Sigma-Aldrich)) at 4 °C for 30 min. Alternatively, unconjugated primary antibodies followed by appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were used. To label intracellular antigens, either Foxp3 Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00-5523-00, Thermo Fisher) or BrdU Flow Kit (559619, BD Pharmingen) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After staining, the samples were fixed with 1% (v/v) formaldehyde (1040021000, Merck) in PBS unless otherwise indicated. In apoptosis experiments, the cells were washed with Annexin V binding buffer (422201, BioLegend) and incubated with BV421-conjugated Annexin V (640924, BioLegend) for 10 min to label apoptotic cells before fixation. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Samples were acquired on a BD Biosciences LSRFortessa flow cytometer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Single-cell cytology

Tissues were harvested and processed into a single cell suspension as described above. CD45+ Ly-6G+CD101-positive and -negative cells were sorted from the single-cell suspensions using a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter. The purified cells were cytospinned (Thermo Shandon Cytospin 3 Centrifuge) onto SuperFrost glass slides, air-fixed, and stained with modified Wright-Giemsa staining (9990701, Thermo Scientific). The slides were imaged with an Olympus BX60 fluorescence microscope equipped with an Olympus U-Plan Apochromat 100×/1.35 objective using Cargille type B immersion oil. White-balance adjustments were done using Fiji/ImageJ68.

Wholemount tissue clearing and volumetric imaging

Sample preparation and tissue clearing were performed as described previously69. Briefly, intact tissues were harvested from embryos at E17.5, initially fixed for 1 h at room temperature in IC Fixation Buffer (00-8222-49, Thermo Fisher), followed by incubation in 25% (v/v) fixative in PBS overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, tissues were blocked by incubation in blocking buffer (PBS with 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma-Aldrich), and 1% (v/v) each of FCS, bovine serum albumin (BSA; P6154, Biowest), and other normal sera according to the secondary antibody species (all Jackson ImmunoResearch)) overnight and stained with an appropriate mix of primary antibodies diluted in Blocking buffer for 72–96 h. Samples requiring secondary antibody staining were rinsed with PBS, washed overnight in blocking buffer, and stained with the appropriate secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 32–36 h. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Finally, samples were washed (PBS with 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 0.5% (v/v) 1-thioglycerol (M6145, Sigma-Aldrich)) and then dehydrated using ascending dilutions of 30, 50, 70, 100, and 100% (v/v) isopropanol (I9516, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30–60 min each, and cleared using undiluted ethyl cinnamate (W243000, Sigma-Aldrich).

Imaging (for Fig. 2a, d–f, and Supplementary Fig. 2c–f) was performed with a 3i CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal microscope (50-µm pinholes) equipped with 405/488/561/610 nm solid state lasers combined with an illumination field uniformizer (except Supplementary Fig. 2c), corresponding band-pass filters, a Teledyne Photometrics Prime BSI or Hamamatsu Orca-Flash4.0 sCMOS camera, Zeiss Plan-Apochromat 10×/0.45 Ph1, Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8, or LD Plan-Neofluar 40×/0.6 Corr Ph2 objectives, and using 3i SlideBook microscope control software (ver. 6.0+). Alternatively, images (for Fig. 2b, c, g, and Supplementary Fig. 2g, h) were acquired using a Leica STELLARIS 8 FALCON FLIM confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) equipped with a tunable white light laser and opto-acoustic filtering, Leica HC FLUOTAR L VISIR 16×/0.60 IMM incl BABB or HC PL APO 20×/0.75 IMM CORR CS2 objectives using Leica F type immersion oil, and Power HyD X, HyD S, and HyD R detectors controlled by Leica LAS X software.

CLSM imaging was performed by 600 Hz bidirectional scanning of 2048 × 2048 points with 4× line averaging, 0.75-1x zoom at 1 A.U. (at 580 nm) pinhole size, system optimized z-step size, and frame sequential scans. CSLM images were processed using Leica LIGHTNING deconvolution (RIMedium = 1.558, strategy = adaptive). In some images (Fig. 2b, c, g, and Supplementary Fig. 2h), minor antibody-mediated image artifacts in tdTomato staining were masked to improve image clarity using surface objects (Fig. 2b: smooth = true (0.284 µm), intensity threshold = 5900, voxels = below 600; Fig. 2c: smooth = false, intensity threshold = 2780.88, voxels = below 500; Supplementary Fig. 2h: smooth = false, intensity threshold = 8000, voxels = below 300) using Oxford Instruments IMARIS (ver. 10.0.2). Additionally, evaluation of tdT+ neutrophil localization in the testis (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2h) was performed using surface object tracing of tdT+ cells (smooth = true (0.755 µm), background elimination = true (15 µm), intensity threshold = 4900, morphological split = true (2.50 µm), quality = above 500, sphericity = above 0.750) and vasculature (smooth = true (1.8 µm), intensity threshold = 2100, voxels = above 500, intensity mean = 2800–5600, intensity StdDev = above 4000) using the same software tool. Pseudo coloring is based on overlapping volume ratio and classifies objects into extravascular (0% overlap), proximal (<25% overlap), and intravascular (>25% overlap).

All images were linearly adjusted for brightness and contrast and rendered in Oxford Instruments IMARIS Viewer (ver. 10.0+). Videos were rendered in IMARIS and edited with Blackmagic Design DaVinci Resolve (ver. 20.0+).

Blood neutrophil labeling (umbilical cord injections)

To ensure embryo viability during antibody injection, the uterus of the pregnant dam was collected at E17.5 into pre-warmed PBS. Embryos were carefully dissected into individual 3 mL dishes containing warm PBS, and the amniotic sacs were opened. Each embryo was injected via the umbilical vein with 10 µl of a solution containing 0.5 µg Ly-6G-AF647 antibody (127610, BioLegend) and 0.25% (w/v) Evans blue (E2129, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. Evans blue was included to allow visualization of the vasculature during injection. The solution was allowed to circulate for approximately one minute, after which embryos were euthanized, and blood was collected by bleeding into heparin-PBS. Kidneys, liver, testes, femora, and tibiae were harvested and processed as described in cell isolation. Cells were then further labeled ex vivo with the same Ly-6G antibody conjugated to a different fluorochrome to distinguish between circulating (in situ- and ex vivo-labeled) and tissue-resident (ex vivo-labeled only) cells and analyzed by FACS.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing

Single-cell suspensions were generated as described above from livers (only E17.5), bone marrow, blood, kidneys (both), testes (both) from WT E17.5 embryos (pooled samples: liver n = 30, bone marrow n = 4, blood n = 12, kidney n = 9, and testis n = 9) and NB animals (pooled samples: bone marrow n = 10, blood n = 9, kidney n = 9, and testis n = 9) in steady-state and after maternal dexamethasone treatment (pooled samples: blood n = 3 and kidney n = 3) or LPS exposure (pooled samples: blood n = 3 and kidney n = 5), [n, number of animals]. E17.5 liver and NB bone marrow samples were collected from both male and female embryos/animals. All other samples were collected only from male embryos/animals. Tissues for the “newborn testis” sample originate from animals between E18.5 (c-sectioned) and postnatal day 1.

Cells were stained for flow cytometry without fixation as described above. The cells were sorted with a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter (100-µm nozzle, purity mode). Single cells were gated with FSC-H vs. FSC-W and SSC-H vs. SSC-W to avoid doublets, and dead cells were excluded. Live single CD45+ cells (kidney, testis) or CD45+ CD3− CD45R− cells (liver, BM, blood) were sorted into the RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% (v/v) FCS. Freshly sorted CD45+ cells were processed immediately according to 10× Genomics guidelines (CG000126_Guidelines for Optimal Sample Prep Flow Chart RevA). Single-cell RNA-sequencing libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (CG00052 RevB) using 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3 Library and Gel Bead Kit v3 and Chromium Single Cell A Chip Kit. The libraries were sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq6000 Sequencing System (RRID: SCR_016387). The Cell Ranger (ver. 7.1.0 or 7.0.1, 10× Genomics) “count” function was used to align samples to the mm10-2020-A reference genome, quantify reads, and filter reads and barcodes. Data analysis and displays were generated using the scanpy (ver. 1.9.8, SCR_01813999), scvi-tools (ver. 1.0.4, SCR_026673), and other common Python (ver. 3.10+, SCR_008394) toolboxes.

The count matrices of our newly generated datasets were concatenated with selected, publicly archived, steady-state datasets generated using 10X Genomics technology of adult bone marrow and blood from Ballesteros et al.2 (GSE142754; “blood_ZT5,” “BM1,” “BM2”), Tabula Muris12 (GSE109774; “Marrow-10X_P7_2,” “Marrow-10X_P7_3”), and Xie et al.11 (GSE137539; “wt_ctl_bm1,” “wt_ctl_bm2,” “wt_ctl_pb2”) (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

Low-quality observations, filtered individually per sample based on observed distribution by number of expressed unique genes (minimum: 400–800, maximum: 2500–7000), total read counts (maximum: 10,000–52,500), and percentage of mitochondrial genes (maximum: 12–20%) and genes expressed by fewer than three cells were removed (Supplementary Data 5). Counts per cell were normalized as total counts over all genes (target sum: 104) and were log1p transformed. Next, principal components (PCA) were calculated and a single-cell variational inference (scVI)70 model (layer: “counts,” categorical covariates: “source,” “batch,” n_latent: 30, n_layers: 2) was generated. For the complete dataset, the neighborhood graph (nearest neighbors: 50) based on the scVI model’s latent space representation, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; SCR_018217103; min. distance: 0.5, neg. edge sample rate: 15), and initial Leiden clustering (ver. 0.10.1; resolution: 1.0) were computed with the indicated settings and initial cluster annotation was performed.

Under-resolved clusters containing hematopoietic and neutrophil-committed progenitors were selected and processed again using Leiden clustering (resolution: 0.75), resulting in the final overall cluster annotation (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Markov affinity-based graph imputation of cells71 as implemented in the Palantir72 package (ver. 1.3.2) was used for improved visualization of gene expression, where indicated. Pseudotime analysis was performed with scFates73 (ver. 1.0.6) by generating a principal tree (method: “ppt,” ppt_metric: “cosine,” Nodes: 300, ppt_sigma: 0.1, ppt_lambda: 50) and computing pseudotime values (n_map: 600) based on the distance of the projected cell position on the tree from the root node.

Differential expression genes were computed with sci-tools’s “differential_expression” function (mode: “change,” delta: 0.25, fdr_ target: 0.05)74 and restricted to genes either expressed in at least 20% of cells in both or in more than 50% of cells in one of the comparison groups. Functional pathway enrichment analyses and visualizations were performed using the STRING database and its “multiple proteins by names/identifiers” search tool (ver. 12.0)75.

Cell proliferation assay

Pregnant dams at E17.5 were injected i.p. with 120 µl of 10 mg/ml 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU, 51-2420KC, BD Biosciences) and euthanized 2, 24, 48, or 72 h later. Tissues were processed and stained for flow cytometry with additional intracellular staining with BrdU Flow Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. BrdU incorporation in neutrophils was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Single-cell energy metabolism measurements

Single-cell energy metabolism was determined by quantifying protein synthesis in freshly isolated cells subjected to various short metabolic interventions using the flow cytometry-based SCENITH method17. Briefly, single-cell suspensions were prepared without enzymatic digestion by trituration with a pipette from kidney and liver, and by mincing with scissors or crushing with a pestle and mortar from embryonic and adult bone marrow, respectively. 4 × 105 cells suspended in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) FCS and 2 mM L-glutamine (25030-024, Gibco) were seeded into a 96-well plate and treated sequentially with 90 µl of 100 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (D6134, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min to inhibit glycolysis and/or with 10 µl of 20 µM oligomycin A (75351, Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min to suppress oxidative phosphorylation. Thereafter, 10 µl of puromycin (0.2 mg/ml, P7255-25MG, Sigma), which incorporates into proteins and terminates protein synthesis, was added to the samples for 30 min at 37 C/5% CO2. Finally, the samples were placed on ice to stop metabolic processes, washed with ice-cold PBS, and stained for flow cytometry. After staining of surface antigens, the cells were permeabilized and stained with a mouse anti-puromycin antibody (clone 12D10, MABE343, Sigma-Aldrich) followed by AlexaFluor Plus 647-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (A32728, Thermo Fisher).

NETosis assay

Single-cell suspensions of adult bone marrow and E17.5 livers were prepared as described above. To yield a leukocyte-enriched cell suspension, cells were purified through a 33% (v/v) Percoll gradient (25 min, 800 × g, min. acceleration/deceleration), collected from the bottom of the tube, and washed in cold RPMI-1640 medium. The cell suspension was then plated on 12-well diagnostic slides, cleaned with acetone (1.00014.2500, Merck), isopropanol, and distilled H2O, and rested for 20 min at 37 °C. Next, cells were treated with either 25 nM PMA (P1585, Sigma-Aldrich) or an equal amount of DMSO (D4540, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 µM SYTOX Green (S34860, Thermo Fisher) in medium and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Samples were fixed with 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA, SC-281692, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 5 min at RT, washed with PBS, and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (P36930, Thermo Fisher) mounting medium and 1.5H cover glass. Imaging was performed with a spinning disk confocal microscope (described above) using a Zeiss Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 Oil DIC objective and Immersol 518F (444960-0000-000, Zeiss) immersion oil.

Phagocytosis assay

Adult bones (femora) were crushed using a pestle and mortar, embryonic bones (femora and tibiae), liver, and kidney were cut into pieces with scissors. Tissues were homogenized into RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) FCS and 2 mM L-glutamine by trituration with a pipette. Five percent of recovered cells from an embryonic liver, all cells from two embryonic kidneys, all cells from four embryonic bone marrows (two femora and two tibiae), and 5% of cells from adult bone marrow (one femur) were seeded into a 96-well plate. Cells were plated without leukocyte separation or cell counting to minimize cell losses from minute embryonic tissues. After a 30 min recovery period, 0.5-µm FluoSpheres carboxylate-modified microspheres (1:1000, F8813, Thermo Fisher) were added to the wells, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C or on ice (negative controls) for 1 h. Thereafter, the cells were collected, washed with ice-cold PBS, and stained for flow cytometry.

ROS production assay

Single-cell suspensions were prepared by trituration with a pipette in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) FCS and 2 mM L-glutamine. Thereafter, 3 × 105 (embryonic liver and adult bone marrow) or 5 × 105 (embryonic kidney) cells were plated on a 96-well plate. Cells were allowed to rest for one hour at 37 °C or on ice (negative control). CellROX Green reagent (500 nM, C10492, Thermo Fisher) was then added to the samples with or without 50 nM PMA for 20 min at 37 °C or on ice (control). The cells were then collected using a 3 min centrifugation at 500 × g at 4 °C, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and stained for flow cytometry.

Dexamethasone treatment and LPS exposure

For embryonic dexamethasone treatment or LPS exposure experiments, pregnant dams at E17.5 were injected i.p. with either 5 mg/kg of dexamethasone (D2915, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS or 100 µg/kg LPS (from E. coli O55:B5; L4524, Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution or the equivalent volume of the respective solvent. Animals were euthanized 2 or 4 h after dexamethasone or LPS treatment, respectively, as described above.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Single-cell suspensions were stained and sorted (single, live CD45+/CD11b+ Ly-6G+ cell) using a BD Biosciences Discover S8 (100-µm nozzle, purity mode). When necessary, sorted neutrophils were pelleted (5 min, 500 × g, +4 °C) and mixed with 50–100 µl of lysis buffer (740955.250 Macherey-Nagel). Cells were snap frozen in liquid N2 or on dry ice and stored in −80 °C. RNA was extracted with the NucleoSpin RNA Mini kit (740955.250, Macherey-Nagel) and translated into cDNA with the SensiFAST cDNA Synthesis Kit (BIO-65054, Meridian Bioscience). The qPCR was carried out using SensiFAST SYBR Lo-ROX Kit (BIO-94020, Meridian Bioscience) for Sell, Cxcr2, Per234, Per1 (forward 5′-CGGATTGTCTATATTTCGGAGCA-3′, reverse 5′-TGGGCAGTCGAGATGGTGTA-3′), and GAPDH (forward 5′-AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG-3′, reverse 5′-TGTAGACCATGTAGTTGAGGTCA-3′; housekeeping gene). The reactions were run using the Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System, and raw data were analyzed using the Thermo Fisher Diomni Design and Analysis software (ver. 3.0.1). Relative expression levels were determined using the Pfaffl method76.

Antibody treatment experiments

For depletion of embryonic neutrophils, pregnant dams at E12.5, E14.5, and E16.5 were injected i.p. with either 1 mg of anti-Ly-6G (BP0075-1, Bio X Cell) or IgG2a control antibody (BE0089, Bio X Cell). Animals were euthanized at E17.5, and livers were collected and processed for FACS analysis as described before. A secondary anti-rat IgG-AF488 antibody (A11006, Thermo Fisher) staining against the injected anti-Ly-6G antibody was performed before staining with conjugated antibodies for flow cytometry.

For the isotype-switch experiment, pregnant dams were injected i.p. at E12.5 with either 250 µg of anti-Ly-6G antibody or IgG2a control antibody, after which they were injected 100 µg of anti-Ly-6G or control antibody daily until E16.5. Alongside the daily injections, the dams were administered 100 µg of anti-rat kappa IgG light chain isotype (BE0122, Bio X Cell) i.p. every other day at E12.5, E14.5, and E16.5. At E17.5, the animals were euthanized, and the livers were processed for FACS analysis.

Gfi1 bulk RNA-sequencing

Kidneys (both) and testes (both) from male WT and Gfi1mut/mut E17.5 embryos (N = 3 from pooled samples n = 1–2) were collected in RNAlater RNA Stabilization Reagent and stored frozen until processing. RNA was isolated with either the NucleoSpin RNA Mini kit for RNA purification (kidneys) or the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (testes, 74034, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sample quality was assessed using an Agilent Advanced Analytical Fragment Analyzer and Stranded mRNA Library Preparation (1000000124518, Illumina), followed by next-generation sequencing performed by the Finnish Functional Genome Centre (Turku Bioscience Centre, Finland) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 SP v1.5 (650–800 M reads/run, 2 lanes).

Fastq files, after automatic adapter trimming, were processed in line with recommendations outlined by HiSAT2 and Stringtie77,78. In brief, the quality of the reads was assessed using FASTQC (ver. 0.12.1), reads were trimmed using BBMap (ver. 39.06) and aligned to the GRCm38.84 genome using HiSAT2 (ver. 2.2.1). Reads were then sorted by coordinate and filtered by alignment score using a cutoff of greater than 30 using samtools (ver. 1.19.2)79. Transcript assembly was done using stringtie (ver. 2.2.1), and the read count matrix was prepared using the prepDE.py3 script provided with HiSAT2.

To perform the differential expression analysis, the read count matrix was processed using pyDESeq2 (ver. 0.5.0) with the recommended parameters80 and logarithmic fold change shrinkage81 was applied. Genes with an adjusted p value of less than 0.05 and showing an absolute fold change of over 1.5 were considered significant.

For analysis of transposable elements, low-quality nucleotides and reads were removed from raw data by Trimmomatic (ver. 0.39)82, and the clean data was mapped to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38.101) using STAR (ver. 2.7)83. The transposable elements were analyzed by TEtranscripts (ver. 2.2.3)84.

Immunofluorescence and conventional histology

For Gfi1mut/mut testis section histology, tissues from E17.5 and 7-week-old animals were fixed with 10% formalin (HT501128-4L, Sigma-Aldrich) or 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. After the fixation, the tissues were rinsed with PBS, dehydrated in an ascending ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and 5-µm sections were cut.

For immunofluorescence staining, slides were deparaffinized, antigens were retrieved with 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 6 at 95 °C for 2.5 h, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 2% (v/v) normal donkey serum (017-000-121, Jackson ImmunoResearch) and 1% (w/v) BSA in PBS (blocking buffer).

Next, samples were stained with a primary antibody against LINE-1 ORF1p (Supplementary Table 1) overnight at 4 °C, followed by a secondary antibody diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at RT, and counterstained with 1 µg/ml DAPI (D1306, Thermo Fisher) in PBS for 10 min at RT. Finally, slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant mounting medium and 1.5H cover glass. Images were acquired using a Leica THUNDER Imager Live Cell equipped with a Leica LED8 light source, DFT51010 filter cube, 20×/0.70 IMM UV objective, and K8 camera operated using LAS X.

For hematoxylin-eosin staining, slides were deparaffinized and stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (MHS16-500ML, Sigma-Aldrich) and eosin (180072, Reagena), or with H&E staining kit (ab245880, Abcam). The sections were imaged using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic 1000 digital scanner with a 40×/0.95 objective, and images were rendered with 3DHISTECH CaseViewer (ver. 2.3).

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (ver. 10.0+). All data are presented as mean or geometric mean values ± SD, geometric SD, or SEM as indicated in the figure legend. Statistical significances between groups were determined using the two-tailed Mann–Whitney test and one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s, Dunnett’s, or Šídák’s multiple comparisons test, depending on the data, and are indicated in the figure legend. Analysis of evaluating diurnal gene expression has been adapted from Adrover et al.34. Briefly, nonlinear least squares regression using the COSINOR function (outlier exclusion at Q = 1%) was performed as described and assessed using the extra sum-of-squares F test comparing the best-fit amplitude value with a simpler model using a hypothetical amplitude of zero. P < 0.05 are considered to be statistically significant.
