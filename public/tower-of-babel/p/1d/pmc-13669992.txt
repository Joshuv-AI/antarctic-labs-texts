Vocal Outcomes After Cell‐Based Outer Vocal Fold Implant in Porcine Bilateral Vocal Fold Resection
Authors: Keer Zhang, Georgia Crane, Mateo Ruiz Alvarez, Alexandra Klomhaus, Yazeed Alhiyari, Bhavani Gowda, Michael Döllinger, Jennifer Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCell‐based outer vocal fold replacement (COVR) consists of human adipose‐derived stem cells and is a potential treatment for severe vocal fold injury. Prior rabbit studies demonstrated enhanced wound healing and COVR's safety in pigs. Building on prior work defining pig‐specific acoustic parameters, this study evaluates phonatory recovery and establishes quantitative voice assessment frameworks following bilateral cordectomy and COVR implantation in a large animal model.MethodsCOVR implantation was performed in eight Yucatan mini‐pigs after bilateral vocal fold resection. Functional voice recovery was tracked, with spontaneous vocalizations recorded pre‐ and post‐surgery for up to 6 months. Pig squeals were extracted using Python, manually verified, and nonrepresentative sounds excluded. Acoustic features including Q50 (median frequency of spectral energy), flux (rate of spectral energy change), F0 (fundamental frequency), jitter (period perturbation), and shimmer (amplitude perturbation) were analyzed using linear mixed‐effect models and Wilcoxon rank sum test with Benjamini–Yekutieli correction. After 6 months, larynges were harvested and human cells were sought via fluorescent in situ hybridization.ResultsQ50 significantly decreased immediately postoperatively (p < 0.001) and returned to preoperative range by 6 months, while F0 and flux changed modestly over time. Human cells persisted in 7 of the 8 subjects.ConclusionsCOVR implantation improved key nonperiodic acoustic parameters 6 months after extensive laryngeal injury in pigs. Implanted cells were detected after 6 months. Future work includes one‐year monitoring to assess long‐term voice recovery and extracellular matrix remodeling, artificial intelligence integration for expanded acoustic analysis, and a unilateral injury model comparing control cordectomy alone versus COVR reconstruction.Level of EvidenceN/A.

Introduction

Structural vocal fold injury, such as cancer resection or severe scarring, can result in permanent voice disability [1]. Tissue loss and the replacement of the specialized, vibratory lamina propria with disorganized collagen impairs the normal vocal fold vibration [1, 2, 3]. Traditional surgical interventions frequently fail to restore the complex biomechanics of the vocal fold cover, prompting the development of regenerative strategies [4, 5]. Cell‐based outer vocal fold replacement (COVR), a tissue‐engineered construct composed of human adipose‐derived stem cells (hASCs) within a three‐dimensional fibrin scaffold, has emerged as a promising solution [6, 7, 8]. In previous leporine studies, COVR enhanced wound healing, restored tissue biomechanics, and increased hyaluronic acid content in both acute and chronically scarred models [8].

Translation of these findings to human clinical practice requires validation in a large animal model that approximates human laryngeal anatomy and physiology. The Yucatan minipig is a superior model for laryngeal research, as its vocal fold mucosa thickness (~0.9 mm) and distribution of elastic and collagen fibers closely mirror human parameters [9, 10, 11]. Furthermore, porcine vocalizations provide a functional measure of vocal fold integrity [12, 13]. High‐pitched squeals are of particular interest as they are produced by true glottic oscillation and oral emission, making them representative of vocal fold health [14, 15, 16]. In contrast, low‐frequency grunts are partial‐glottic or nasal in origin, involving supraglottic or oropharyngeal vibrations that do not accurately reflect vocal fold vibratory dynamics [15, 17].

In a pilot pig study, a COVR‐treated minipig demonstrated reconstitution of lamina propria architecture with both collagen and elastin fibers at 6 months (n = 1), without clinical evidence of xenograft rejection, whereas an injured control (vocal fold resection alone) exhibited marked lamina propria thinning and prominent collagenous fibrosis [13]. A recent study evaluated bilateral COVR implants in eight swine assessing safety and toxicity of the human cell‐based implants at the maximal expected cell load for 6 months [18]. Adverse events were self‐limited, including skin rash and gastroenteritis in one subject each, wound dehiscence in two subjects, and minor inconsistent serologic abnormalities. However, longitudinal functional data remain sparse. This study evaluates the longitudinal vocal recovery over 6 months of those eight swine previously reported.

A previous study established a framework for porcine vocal assessment by identifying five sensitive acoustic parameters, including median spectral energy (Q50) and spectral flux, capable of distinguishing preoperative voices from postoperative impairment [13, 14]. However, that study included only three animals (n = 1 unilateral COVR‐treated, n = 2 unilateral injured controls), limiting statistical power and allowing the unoperated contralateral fold to potentially mask the extent of vocal impairment. The present study addresses these limitations by increasing sample size and employing a more severe injury paradigm: bilateral vocal fold resection. By removing the vibratory mucosa from both folds, contralateral compensation is eliminated, providing a more rigorous test of COVR‐mediated regeneration. This investigation represents the first effort to monitor functional voice recovery for 6 months in a bilateral resection model, tracking the longitudinal trajectory of acoustic changes to evaluate the efficacy of COVR in restoring vocal fold function following extensive laryngeal injury.

Materials and Methods

This study was UCLA IACUC‐approved and conducted in accordance with USDA and AALAC guidelines. Eight Yucatan mini‐pigs (four male, four female) underwent bilateral laryngeal cordectomy and vocal fold replacement surgery with cell‐based outer vocal fold replacement (COVR) implants as described below. Animals were pair‐housed by sex as required by our veterinary facility in an AAALAC‐accredited vivarium with ad libitum access to food and water. Each pair was obtained in the same shipment with similar age and weight, underwent identical surgical procedures within 1–2 weeks of each other, and was maintained together throughout the study; individual acoustic outputs within each pair could not be distinguished. Following a two‐week acclimation period, all animals underwent bilateral European Laryngological Society type II cordectomy and immediate COVR implantation. Preoperative ages were 6–7 months, with weights of 28–32 kg; final postoperative weights after 6 months ranged from 44 to 73 kg. Acoustic recordings were obtained before surgery and longitudinally for 6 months postoperatively. Spontaneous high‐pitched phonations were analyzed and are herein referred to as “squeals.”

Surgical Procedure

Yucatan miniature pigs were sedated with intramuscular Telazol (tiletamine) to permit intravenous access, followed by induction with inhaled isoflurane and intravenous propofol. Endotracheal intubation was performed using a size 6.0 endotracheal tube. Through a vertical midline cervical incision, the endolarynx was accessed via superior pharyngotomy through the thyrohyoid membrane. The epithelium and lamina propria of both vocal folds were excised with curved Metzenbaum scissors from just anterior to the arytenoid cartilage to immediately posterior to the anterior commissure, preserving 1–2 mm of native tissue at each margin, consistent with European Laryngological Society type II cordectomy. Defects were reconstructed with COVR implants (human adipose‐derived stem cells embedded in a fibrin scaffold) secured with anterior and posterior 5–0 plain gut sutures. Implant fabrication was previously described [19]. All animals tolerated the procedure, regained ambulation after anesthesia, and resumed oral intake the same day.

Acoustic Recordings

Porcine vocalizations were collected using methods adapted from Schlegel et al. [14] Briefly, spontaneous vocalizations were continuously recorded during 4–5‐day sessions preoperatively and at serial postoperative intervals while the same pair of unrestrained animals remained in a hard‐walled animal enclosure room isolated from other pigs. Recordings were obtained using a digital audio recorder (H6 Handy Recorder, XYH‐6/BLK Zoom model, 16‐bit, 44,100 Hz sampling rate, stereo) mounted on the enclosure wall. Audio files were processed in Python (v3.12.2). Squeals were automatically extracted using predefined criteria: duration ≥ 0.75–1 s; root mean square amplitude ≥ 0.08–0.1 (normalized units); 0.8 s padding before and after detected events and fundamental frequency 50–500 Hz. The extraction algorithm was adapted from previously published MATLAB code [14] and reimplemented in Python to avoid version‐dependent incompatibilities and improve reproducibility. All extracted squeals were manually reviewed to confirm the true glottic vocalizations and adherence to selection criteria. Samples with excessive amplitude, artifacts, background noise, or waveform distortion were excluded following independent quality assessment. A total of 291 preoperative and 574 postoperative squeals were retained for analysis (Table 1). Representative pre‐, early and late postoperative vocalizations are provided in Data S1.

Acoustic Parameter Analysis

For acoustic analysis, each recording was normalized to [−1;1] as absolute sound pressure levels could not be reliably established in the recording environment. Five acoustic parameters were selected based on prior validation in distinguishing porcine squeals from other call types and in characterizing aperiodic human phonation. Parameter names, units, and computational details are summarized in Table 2 and were consistent with the methodology previously described [13, 14]. Detailed descriptions of parameter computation have been reported previously and briefly described in Table 2 [14]. A sample calculation of spectral energy and flux is visualized in Figure 1. Jitter and shimmer were computed using a custom Python script based on peak detection (SciPy) [20], applied across the full duration of each squeal. Jitter and shimmer were included for comparison with human acoustic parameters although they are dependent on periodic vibration, which is absent in pigs. Python scripts used for squeal extraction and feature computation are provided in Data S2.

Statistical Analysis

Acoustic parameters were calculated for each squeal. Statistical analyses were performed in Python and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Time was modeled as a categorical variable: Preop (weeks −1 and 0), Early Postop (weeks 1–8), Mid Postop (weeks 9–16), and Late Postop (weeks 17–27). Longitudinal recovery trends were evaluated using mixed‐effects linear regression with time as a fixed effect and subject pair as a random intercept to account for within‐pair correlation. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals were calculated and visualized using forest plots.

To assess individual variability, Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Benjamini–Yekutieli correction were used to compare preoperative values to mid and late postoperative values within each pig pair for each parameter. Individual pigs within a pair could not be acoustically distinguished, thus analyses were conducted at the pair level (n = 4). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Fluorescent In Situ Hybridization (FISH)

To identify transplanted human stem cells present 6 months after COVR implant, we generated a FISH probe targeting GUCY2F, an X‐linked gene functional in humans but disabled in situ and non‐coding in pigs [21]. DNA isolation, amplification, and Alexa Fluor 594‐labeling were performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, a 466‐bp human‐specific GUCY2F amplicon (GenBank: NM_001322.3) was generated (Forward: 5′‐CCCTTGGGCTTGTGATTCGC‐3′; Reverse: 5′‐TAAGCCGTGGCTCCGAAGAG‐3′) using a 65°C annealing temperature to prevent porcine cross‐reactivity.

FISH was conducted on 5‐μm FFPE pig larynx sections according to Goel et al. [22], with minor modifications. The 80°C sodium citrate step was omitted; instead, immediately prior to dehydration, slides were sequentially incubated in 100 mM glycine, 1× PBS (2 × 5 min), and 70% formamide/2× SSC (pH 7.0) at 72°C for 2 min. Imaging was performed using a 594 nm fluorescence filter to identify cells with functioning human isoform.

Results

A total of 865 vocalizations meeting inclusion criteria were analyzed (Table 1). Squeal counts varied across timepoints due to the variability in subject talkativeness and availability of the recording enclosure; individual pig pairs contributed between 125 and 383 total squeals. Linear mixed‐effects modeling with pig pair as a random intercept was employed to appropriately account for within‐pair correlation and unequal contribution across subjects.

Population‐Level Acoustic Changes

Linear mixed‐effects modeling with pig pair as a random intercept revealed significant changes in all five acoustic parameters across the postoperative period (Figure 4). Q50 declined significantly from the preoperative to early postoperative period (p < 0.001) but showed no significant difference between preoperative and late postoperative timepoints, similar trend was found in flux but with modest significant (p = 0.036). Late postoperative recordings demonstrated significantly higher fundamental frequency (p < 0.0001) compared with preoperative recordings. Between preoperative and early postoperative periods, significant differences were observed for jitter (p < 0.0001), shimmer (p < 0.001), and Q50 (p < 0.001). Fluorescent In Situ Hybridization (FISH)

Six months following bilateral cordectomy and COVR implantation, FISH confirmed the persistence of human‐specific GUCY2F DNA within the excised porcine vocal folds (VFs) in seven out of eight pigs (Data S4). Degree of cell persistence varied across subjects, ranging from sparse positive cells to large numbers positive. Most cells were found in the lamina propria, with some animals also demonstrating labeling in the epithelium. Discussion

Restoration of phonatory function following extensive vocal fold mucosal injury remains a central challenge in the development of regenerative laryngeal therapies. This study presents a longitudinal characterization of acoustic parameters in a porcine model up to 6 months following bilateral cordectomy and cell‐based outer vocal fold replacement (COVR) implantation. By employing linear mixed‐effects models (LMM) to capture population‐level trends alongside Wilcoxon rank sum tests, we identified distinct patterns providing insight into phonatory recovery following severe vocal fold injury. Although this study included four subject pairs and both sexes to account for biological variability, the effective analytic sample size at the pair level remained limited, a constraint common to large‐animal vocal fold models that may reduce statistical power for detecting subtle individual‐level effects.

Q50 as an Indicator of Vocal Recovery

Q50, the median frequency of spectral energy, reflects the contribution of higher‐frequency harmonics to the overall acoustic signal. In this study, Q50 demonstrated a significant decline immediately postoperatively followed by restoration toward preoperative levels by 6 months. As a nonperiodic acoustic measure that has been explored in prior animal vocalization studies as a potential indicator of vocal fold condition [14], this trajectory may suggest progressive recovery of phonatory function over time. The absence of significant difference between preoperative and late postoperative Q50 values suggests that, at the population level, spectral energy distribution approximated baseline by 6 months following COVR implantation. Figure 5 illustrates the distribution of individual squeals across time together with the LMM estimated marginal means and 95% confidence intervals, demonstrating an early postoperative decline followed by gradual recovery toward baseline values. Because spontaneous vocalizations could not be elicited on demand, the number of recorded squeals varied across weekly sessions. Nevertheless, the LMM framework used here accommodates unbalanced observations and missing data across timepoints. These findings align with prior work [14], demonstrating Q50 sensitivity to surgical injury, while the extended observation period and longitudinal modeling in the present study provide additional insight that recovery continues beyond the immediate postoperative period.

Fundamental Frequency: Late Elevation

Postoperative F0 demonstrated a statistically significant rise during the late recovery phase. Prior studies characterizing the immediate post‐surgical period (0–14 days) reported a general decrease in median frequency values and narrowed F0 range [13, 14]. In contrast, the significant elevation of F0 observed in this late postoperative period could indicate a stiffer neo‐glottis, although the effect size estimate is small (0.65). Other causes for elevated F0 must also be considered, including behavioral, size, or aging changes. For example, preoperative recordings were obtained shortly after animal transport to the vivarium, when the stress of acclimatization may have altered vocal output. Also note that tissue stiffening in the vocal tract following the thyrohyoid pharyngotomy approach could influence F0. Given that extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, particularly the stabilization of collagen and elastin, can take up to 12 months [23], additional attention to the fundamental frequency is warranted in further studies including control groups and longer monitoring.

Spectral Flux: Transitions in Vibratory Stability and Airflow Turbulence

Spectral flux also decreased significantly during both the mid and late postoperative period. In the context of laryngeal reconstruction, this reduced flux indicates a vocal signal that is more stable and consistent compared to baseline. Previous pilot study identified spectral flux as sensitive to acute vocal injury [13]. The current larger and longer‐term study instead found significant change from baseline in the mid‐postoperative phase (p < 0.001, effect size = 1.1). Flux subsequently trended back toward baseline in the late‐postoperative phase. Note that although the statistical significance remains due to large numbers of measurements, the effect size of 0.23 is small due to small numeric change. As with F0, the persistent fluctuations in flux may indicate that ongoing ECM reorganization continues to modulate the aerodynamic‐myoelastic properties of the neo‐glottis; behavioral or larger physiologic changes cannot be ruled out. Consequently, future longitudinal investigations should expand the observation period to at least 12 months to fully capture the completion of the ECM remodeling timeline and determine if F0 and flux eventually achieve a stable functional baseline.

Jitter and Shimmer: Translational Considerations

Jitter (period perturbation) and shimmer (amplitude perturbation) exhibited a biphasic pattern. While included to facilitate translational comparison with standard human clinical assessments [24, 25], these perturbation metrics should be interpreted cautiously, as porcine vocalizations inherently exhibit non‐linear and aperiodic qualities [14, 15, 26, 27]. The early postoperative elevation reflects acute vibratory instability following the loss of the native lamina propria. The subsequent reduction in perturbation likely indicates neo‐glottic maturation via ECM remodeling, potentially yielding a more regular vibratory pattern than normal porcine tissue.

Individual Subject‐Pair Variability

Subject‐pair analysis revealed notable variation. Strict IACUC and AAALAC welfare mandates require social species to be pair‐housed and recorded simultaneously, precluding the isolation of individual acoustic data. To mitigate this limitation and ensure experimental consistency, paired animals received identical surgical and postoperative care, with procedures staggered by only one to two weeks. However, no consistent significant differences emerged across acoustic parameters between pairs or sexes.

The heterogeneous response observed likely reflects biological variation in natural voice, different wound healing rates, or individual sensitivity to the COVR implant [14, 28]. This inter‐subject variability, consistent with previous reports in porcine vocal studies [13, 14], underscores the importance of employing both population‐level longitudinal models and individual animal‐level comparisons to comprehensively characterize recovery trajectories.

Cellular Persistence

Importantly, the persistence of human‐specific DNA in seven out of eight pigs at 6 months post‐COVR implantation suggests that some human cells engrafted as xenografts in the pig. The ultimate neo‐glottis became a hybrid host‐donor tissue in those animals. There was wide variability in the numbers of human cells present; sources of that variation, and correlation with ECM remodeling and voice outcomes, will be a topic of ongoing study.

Future Directions

This study represents a longitudinal assessment of porcine voice recovery extending to 6 months postoperatively. While Q50 demonstrated recovery to preoperative levels by 6 months, the remaining four acoustic parameters did not reach baseline values within this timeframe. Future studies should extend the monitoring period to 1 year to align with established extracellular matrix remodeling timelines and determine whether F0, flux, jitter, and shimmer demonstrate continued recovery beyond the observation window of the current study.

The application of artificial intelligence may expand the scope of acoustic analysis beyond the five parameters examined here and may better assess the voice pattern over time [29]. In human voice research, recent studies have employed machine learning models to estimate dysphonia and severity from recorded acoustic signals [30], demonstrating potential for quantitative, objective voice assessment. Analogous models could be adapted for animal studies to characterize vocal health across a broader parameter space and identify novel biomarkers of voice recovery.

This study employed a bilateral injury model to characterize acoustic changes following the most severe form of vocal fold injury. However, given that unilateral injuries are more prevalent in clinical practice, future investigations should examine unilateral cordectomy models, both with and without COVR reconstruction. Such comparative studies would establish the efficacy of COVR in a more representative setting and enable direct attribution of observed recovery patterns to the implant rather than other aging or recovery processes.

Conclusion

COVR implantation following bilateral cordectomy in a porcine model resulted in recovery of Q50 (median frequency of spectral energy) to preoperative levels by 6 months, alongside increased F0, decreased flux, and fluctuating jitter and shimmer relative to baseline. Fluorescent in situ hybridization showed the persistence of COVR cells at 6 months. These findings suggest partial phonatory recovery with evidence of ongoing tissue remodeling. One‐year monitoring and comparative unilateral injury models are warranted to fully characterize COVR's therapeutic potential.
