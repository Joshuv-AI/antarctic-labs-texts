The association between monocyte to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and clinical prognosis of acute ischemic stroke after intravenous thrombolysis therapy
Authors: Ning Yang, Liqun Hu, Yulong Han
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) ratio (MHR) is accepted as a novel marker for demonstrating inflammation. We investigated whether the monocyte-to-HDL ratio is related to the 90-day clinical prognosis of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) after intravenous thrombolysis (IVT). Patients with AIS treated with alteplase IVT were included consecutively, and clinical information and laboratory data were collected. The 90-day prognosis of all patients was determined with a clinical assessment using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The optimal cutoff values for patients were evaluated by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve method. Then, a multivariate logistic regression model was used to evaluate the risk factors for poor prognosis of IVT in AIS. We retrospectively enrolled 192 patients who were diagnosed with AIS and received IVT between February 2020 and July 2022, with final follow-up on September 30, 2022. The patients in the poor prognosis group had significantly higher monocyte counts, lower HDL levels, and higher MHR values than the good prognosis group. The optimal cutoff value of the MHR for predicting the 3-month outcome of acute pontine infarction was 0.621. Multivariate logistic regression revealed that the MHR (OR = 4.626, 95% CI: 1.156–18.512, P = .030) was strongly associated with poor prognosis in AIS. The MHR is an independent risk factor for the clinical prognosis of AIS patients receiving IVT therapy and shows a certain predictive value.

1. Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is the most common type of stroke and is characterized by high rates of disability, death and recurrence, and the cost of treatment is increasing worldwide every year.[1–4] Intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) is one of the most effective measures to target revascularization in patients with AIS, and the neuroprotective effect of alteplase involving the promotion of revascularization can significantly improve the clinical prognosis of some AIS patients.[5–7] However, there is still a significant proportion of patients with unsatisfactory clinical prognosis after rt-PA IVT therapy, and some of them have further aggravation of symptoms.[8,9] Therefore, it is important to explore the risk factors and predictors of neurological deterioration after thrombolysis in patients with AIS. Numerous studies have shown that the inflammatory response plays an important role in the occurrence, development and clinical prognosis of AIS,[10–12] and the inflammatory response and dyslipidemia can accelerate the development of atherosclerosis.[13–15] Monocytes, a subtype of leukocytes, participate in the inflammatory damage response by binding to adhesion molecules on activated endothelial cells and infiltrating into the central nervous system during the acute phase of AIS.[16] High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), a common lipid marker, has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant antithrombotic effects and may reverse cholesterol transport to prevent endothelial dysfunction,[17,18] inhibit low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) oxidation and protect endothelial cells, which may reduce the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events.[19] Since inflammation and dyslipidemia play an important role in the process of atherosclerosis, the monocyte to HDL ratio (MHR), as a convergence of inflammatory pathways and dyslipidemia, is an emerging inflammatory response indicator that has received much attention in recent years and is closely associated with a variety of chronic inflammatory diseases. MHR is closely associated with the development and progression of atherosclerosis and may serve as a serum marker for the prognosis of ischemic cardiovascular diseases.[20–22] A few studies have shown the predictive value of the MHR for early clinical prognosis in patients with AIS.[23,24]

There are no studies on the relationship between MHR and prognosis after IVT, and the aim of this study was to retrospectively analyze the predictive value of MHR on clinical prognosis 90 days after the onset of AIS in patients who received IVT.

2. Methods

2.1. Study subjects

This retrospective, observational study was conducted from February 2020 to July 2022. Patients with AIS who received IVT with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) in the Department of Neurology, Hefei Hospital Affiliated to Anhui Medical University were enrolled continuously during the study period. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hefei Hospital of Anhui Medical University, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their guardians before inclusion in the study.

We then retrospectively analyzed data from all subjects receiving IVT. All patients were treated in the stroke unit and received standard treatments, such as antiplatelet therapy and statin therapy. Patients eligible to participate were enrolled in the final analysis if they met the following criteria.

Inclusion criteria were as follows:

•Older than 18 years; ischemic stroke onset <4.5 hours; first onset or previous history of ischemic stroke without sequelae before the current onset; and ischemic stroke confirmed by cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [magnetic resonance diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)] or cranial CT.

Older than 18 years; ischemic stroke onset <4.5 hours; first onset or previous history of ischemic stroke without sequelae before the current onset; and ischemic stroke confirmed by cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [magnetic resonance diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)] or cranial CT.

The exclusion criteria were as follows:

•Symptomatic hemorrhage conversion within 24 hours, bridging endovascular treatment; previous history of AIS and mRS score > 2; previous history of major trauma, history of surgery, history of brain tumor, malignant tumor or known underlying malignancy; severe liver, kidney or other organ damage; chronic inflammatory disease or chronic infection or pretreatment of active infection, autoimmune disease (e.g., rheumatic autoimmune disease and lupus), hematologic disease and anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drug use; and missing laboratory data or incomplete clinical or imaging data.

Symptomatic hemorrhage conversion within 24 hours, bridging endovascular treatment; previous history of AIS and mRS score > 2; previous history of major trauma, history of surgery, history of brain tumor, malignant tumor or known underlying malignancy; severe liver, kidney or other organ damage; chronic inflammatory disease or chronic infection or pretreatment of active infection, autoimmune disease (e.g., rheumatic autoimmune disease and lupus), hematologic disease and anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drug use; and missing laboratory data or incomplete clinical or imaging data.

2.2. Intravenous thrombolysis with rt-PA

All patients with AIS were first admitted to the hospital for cranial CT examination to exclude cerebral hemorrhage and were given a standard dose (0.9 mg/kg) of rt-PA (manufactured by Boehringer Ingelheim, Germany, size 20 and 50 mg) intravenous thrombolytic therapy within 4.5 hours of onset, of which 10% of the total dose was injected intravenously within 1 minute, and the remaining agent was pumped intravenously by continuous micropump within 60 minutes. The maximum applied dose of rt-PA is <90 mg.

2.3. Clinical data

General baseline data were collected from all patients, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), systolic/diastolic blood pressure at admission, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), hyperlipidemia, smoking, alcohol consumption), smoking history defined as continuous or cumulative for more than 6 months, smoking >1 cigarette/d, and alcohol consumption >50 g/d for >1 year. Preadmission medication status (antiplatelet, anticoagulation, statin, antihypertensive) and baseline severity of neurological damage within 24 hours of admission in patients with AIS were assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS).[25] All patients included in the analysis were reexamined on cranial CT 24 hours after IVT to evaluate the presence of hemorrhagic transformation and whether they had symptomatic bleeding.

2.4. Laboratory test data

Routine laboratory tests were performed within 24 hours after admission, including leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets, and hemoglobin, with an automatic blood cell analyzer (Beckman DXH800) for routine blood tests. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), HDL, LDL, and C-reactive protein (CRP) were collected in a fasted state on the following morning and analyzed with an automatic biochemistry analyzer (Beckman AU5800).

2.5. Calculation of the MHR

We calculated the MHR by dividing the absolute value of the monocyte count by that of HDL-C.

2.6. Prognosis assessment

The clinical outcomes of all enrolled patients were determined by a qualified trained neurologist who was unaware of the patient.

MRI data via telephone or by appointment in the outpatient department. All patients with AIS underwent a clinical prognosis assessment after IVT using the modified Rankin scale (mRS)[26] at 90 days and were divided into a good prognosis group (mRS score ≤ 2) and a poor prognosis group (mRS score > 2) according to the magnitude of the mRS score.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Data obtained from this study were evaluated by using the SPSS 20.0 program. Measures that conformed to a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and an independent samples t test was used for comparison between 2 groups; measures that did not conform to a normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile spacing, and independent samples Mann–Whitney U tests were used for comparisons between the 2 groups. A logistic regression model was used for multivariate analysis, and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. The predictive value of adverse prognosis of patients with AIS after IVT was analyzed by applying the ROC curve, and the AUC value of the MHR was calculated by the ROC curve.

3. Results

3.1. The clinical and demographic data of the study

During this study, 231 patients with AIS received intravenous thrombolytic therapy, 15 patients received bridging endovascular therapy, 11 patients had incomplete clinical data, 8 patients had mRS scores >2 before onset, 3 patients did not undergo cranial MRI examination, and 2 patients were lost to follow-up. A total of 192 patients were ultimately included in the statistical analysis, as shown in Figure 1.

The diagram shows the selection of patients with acute ischemic stroke after intravenous thrombolysis therapy.

Among them, 114 patients (59.4%) were male, and 78 patients (40.6%) were female, with an average age of 64.1 ± 11.7 years. The median NIHSS score at baseline was 5 (interquartile range 2–12). The prognosis at 90 days after onset was good in 126 patients (65.6%) and poor in 66 patients (34.4%), and there were no fatalities. There were 9 patients with hemorrhagic transformation and no symptomatic cerebral hemorrhage. The clinical and demographic data of this study are presented in Table 1.

3.2. Factors influencing the prognosis of AIS patients after receiving intravenous thrombolysis

The baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. Univariate analysis showed that the admission NIHSS scores were higher in the poor prognosis group than in the good prognosis group ([4.33 ± 3.99] vs [12.30 ± 5.09], P < .001), and the poor prognosis group had higher white blood cell (WBC) count (P = .015), neutrophil (NEU) count (P = .004), monocyte (MON) count (P < .001), FBG level (P < .001), CRP level (P = .036), and MHR (P < .001) than the good prognosis group. However, the differences in age, sex, BMI, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, stroke or TIA, atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia), antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, statins, antihypertensive applications, history of alcohol consumption, history of smoking and other laboratory tests were not statistically significant between the 2 groups.

Using MHR 0.621 as the critical value, the patients were divided into 2 groups: 128 cases in the low MHR group (≤0.621) and 64 cases in the high MHR group (>0.621). In the 2 groups, we found statistically significant differences in many of their factors: NIHSS, mean ± SD (5.46 ± 5.20) vs (10.30 ± 5.63), P < .001; WBC, (6.55 ± 1.79) vs (8.42 ± 3.35), P < .001; NEU, (4.33 ± 1.81) vs (5.94 ± 3.32), P < .001; MONO, (0.39 ± 0.13) vs (0.78 ± 0.35), P < .001; TC, (4.40 ± 1.19) vs (4.04 ± 0.85), P = .033; HDL, (1.20 ± 0.27) vs (1.04 ± 0.28), P < .001; CRP, (4.78 ± 9.01) vs (10.95 ± 28.20), P = .025; MHR, (0.35 ± 0.12) vs (0.94 ± 0.47), P < .001. However, there were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, BMI, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, stroke or TIA, atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, smoking, alcohol consumption), preadmission medication status (antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, statins, antihypertensives), lymphocytes, platelets, hemoglobin, FBG, TGs, or LDL (Table 2).

3.4. Patients with a high MHR had a higher rate of poor prognosis

To assess the trend of adverse prognosis rates with increasing MHR, the study population was divided into 3 groups of 64 patients each according to MHR values. In the first tertile (MHR ≤ 0.34), the adverse outcome rate was 24.2%; in the second tertile (0.34 < MHR ≤ 0.62), the adverse outcome rate was 14.5%; and in the third tertile (MHR > 0.62), the adverse outcome rate was 64.2%; the differences were statistically significant (P < .001) (Fig. 2).

3.6. Predictive value of the MHR for poor prognosis after intravenous thrombolysis

The ROC curve showed that the area under the curve of MHR predicting poor prognosis at 90 days of onset in patients with AIS after IVT was 0.706 (95% CI 0.622–0.789; P < .01), the Jorden index was 0.438, the best cutoff value was 0.621, and the sensitivity and specificity of predicting poor prognosis were 62.1% and 81.7%, respectively, affecting the risk factors for AIS patients after IVT. The AUCs for monocytes and HDL to predict poor prognosis in patients with IVT were 0.652 (95% CI 0.570–0.734; P = .01) and 0.509 (95% CI 0.421–0.598; P = .832), respectively (Fig. 3).

4. Discussion

In this study, we found that a high MHR at admission was highly associated with poor prognosis at 90 days; patients with a high MHR (>0.621) had a high risk for poor prognosis at 90 days after AIS when receiving IVT. Consequently, the MHR value is a readily available prognostic predictor for AIS patients receiving IVT.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the relationship of MHR with short-term prognosis in AIS patients receiving IVT therapy.

Previous studies have shown that immune inflammatory responses and oxidative stress play an important role in stroke and that ischemic stroke has a differential regulation of monocytes. As immune cells, different subpopulations of monocytes play a complex and important role in the pathobiology of ischemic stroke, depending on the severity of the injury and the time window, and may have a direct impact on the prognosis of ischemic stroke.[27,28]

First, monocytes secrete proinflammatory and pro-oxidant factors during the immune response, leading to atherosclerotic plaque formation, and patients with high monocyte counts are more prone to atherosclerosis than patients with low monocyte counts in coronary artery disease.[29]

Second, after ischemic stroke, cytokine-mediated poststroke inflammation and leukocyte in-flow exacerbate the development of ischemic brain injury, and brain ischemia and hypoxia stimulate monocytes to produce inflammatory mediators, such as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), interleukin-6 (interleukin, IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor (NF), and granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-SCF), which further exacerbates cellular ischemia and hypoxia and aggravates brain tissue damage; overexpression of MCP-1 also aggravates brain damage and increases the migration of inflammatory cells.[30]

In addition, monocytes, which bind to adhesion molecules expressed on damaged vascular endothelial cells,[31] infiltrate into the subendothelial space and differentiate into macrophages, which take up oxidized LDL via scavenger receptor A (SR-A) and CD36 and transform into foam cells,[32] which release inflammatory cytokines and cause more monocytes to accumulate in the focal area, thus contributing to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques.

A correlation was found between the increased number of CD14++CD16 + monocytes in the blood of poststroke patients and the severity of brain injury, such as increased adverse outcomes and mortality after stroke and deterioration of early clinical function.[33,34]

Impaired lipid metabolism is also an important cause of atherosclerosis, and the formation of unstable plaques is an important risk factor for ischemic stroke. HDL exerts antiatherogenic effects by controlling the activation of monocytes and the proliferation of monocyte precursor cells, as well as by inhibiting the metastasis of macrophages and the oxidation of LDL molecules and protecting endothelial cells from inflammation and oxidative stress.[14,35,36]

Murphy et al demonstrated that HDL and its major protein component apolipoprotein A-1 showed anti-inflammatory effects on human monocytes by inhibiting the activation of CD11b and effectively inhibited the migration and adhesion of monocytes, both of which had a significant negative correlation in the regulation of atherosclerosis.[37–40]

The correlation between monocytes and HDL-C is closely related to the onset, progression and severity of atherosclerotic disease. The proinflammatory effect of monocytes and the anti-inflammatory effect of HDL-C make MHR a new inflammatory marker. Studies have shown that the MHR is a useful new marker for prognosis[22,41,42] in cardiovascular diseases and rheumatic immune diseases.

Vural G. et al suggested that diabetic patients with higher MHR are more likely to develop polyneuropathy.[43] Studies have shown that the MHR is not only indicative of a systemic inflammatory response but also a predictor of the development of atherosclerosis.[22] As a new inflammatory index proposed in recent years, the MHR is valuable for the early detection of atherosclerosis and improvement of prognosis.[44]

Kanbay M. et al first reported in a study of 340 dialysis CKD patients with a mean follow-up of 33 months that increased MHR was associated with worsening cardiovascular status and became an independent predictor of major cardiovascular events during follow-up.[45]

The association of the MHR with AIS compared with cardiovascular disease has not been fully elucidated. In a prospective study including 1090 patients with AIS within 24 hours of onset, Liu et al applied the mRS score for prognostic assessment of patients and divided the prognostic group into mRS ≤ 2 and mRS > 2 according to the mRS value. After the 3-month follow-up, the MHR was significantly higher in the poor prognosis group than in the good prognosis group [0.53 (95% CI: 0.37–0.69) 0.48 (95% CI: 0.33–0.60) 0.69) 0.48 (95% CI: 0.33–0.60), P = .007], revealing that the MHR may be an important and independent predictor of poor functional prognosis in AIS patients.[24]

In a retrospective study of 316 patients with acute cerebral hemorrhage, You et al showed that a higher MHR was associated with an increased risk of disability or death at discharge and 3 months after cerebral hemorrhage.[46] Bolayir et al retrospectively analyzed 466 patients diagnosed with AIS within 24 hours of symptom onset and divided the patients into 2 groups based on 30-day mortality (patients in group 1 survived >30 days and patients in group 2 died 30 days after AIS), and the MHR values in group 2 were significantly higher than those in group 1 (P < .001), which was 17.52, with a sensitivity of 94.4% and specificity of 84.8%, suggesting that high MHR values can be used as an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in AIS patients.[47] Our study also found that the MHR was associated with a poor clinical prognosis at 90 days of IVT in AIS and further confirmed that patients with a high MHR had a worse prognosis.

5. Limitations of the study

The present study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective and single-center study that included a small sample size. A single-center regression study is prone to bias, and subsequent multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed. In addition, the present study did not monitor the changes in the MHR index dynamically. Therefore, to determine whether the changes in the MHR over time can provide the value of clinical prognosis after IVT, a large sample size study is still needed in the future.

6. Conclusion

MHR at admission was a predictor of 90-day prognosis in patients with AIS receiving IVT. An MHR value >0.621 at admission indicated a poor prognosis at 90 days. Targeting the MHR may be an important new therapeutic strategy for prospective trials of AIS patients receiving IVT.
