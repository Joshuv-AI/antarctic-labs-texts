Disease mechanisms revealed in the P23H opsin knock-in mouse model of retinitis pigmentosa
Authors: Jin Zhao, Diego Montenegro, Hye Jin Kim, Keiko Ueda, Janet R Sparrow, Andrey Abramov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe molecular mechanisms driving disease progression in autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) due to a proline-to-histidine substitution (P23H) in opsin are not completely understood. Thus, we undertook to unlock features of the P23H/+ knock-in mouse model. Bisretinoids, the source of short-wavelength fundus autofluorescence (SW-AF), were elevated in albino P23H rhodopsin transgenic rats and in black and albino P23H/+ mice relative to wild type. Conversely, bisretinoid levels were lower in albino P23H/+ relative to black P23H/+ mice. Fluorescence microscopy revealed an inherent and aberrant autofluorescence in photoreceptor inner segments of albino P23H/+ mice. Outer nuclear layer (ONL) thinning indicative of photoreceptor degeneration was more pronounced in albino versus black P23H/+ mice and was accentuated in inferior versus superior hemiretina. Dark-rearing alleviated ONL thinning in albino P23H/+. The greatly diminished levels of ocular retinoid were not fully accounted for by loss of photoreceptor cells. P23H/+ mice treated with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) exhibited higher intensities of SW-AF, improved photoreceptor viability, and diminished 4-hydroxynonenal (HNE) immunoreactivity; the latter included 4-HNE adducts deposited in association with photoreceptor cells. The lower levels of bisretinoid in association with higher retinal illuminance are indicative of toxic bisretinoid photooxidation/degradation while the higher bisretinoid levels conferred by NAC are consistent with inhibition of these photodegradative processes. Collectively, these findings reveal that anomalous bisretinoid production can account for fundus hyperautofluorescence, and the photoreactive properties of bisretinoids can explain the propensity for environmental light to exacerbate photoreceptor cell degeneration. The findings support the benefits of antioxidant intervention in human adRP.Keywords: retina, photoreceptor cell, retinitis pigmentosa, retinoids, bisretinoid

This study addresses characteristics of the natural history of retinitis pigmentosa (RP), a group of inherited retinal disorders that culminate in blindness. Utilizing mouse and rat models of autosomal dominant RP, a proline-to-histidine substitution in rod opsin, elevations in photoreactive vitamin A–aldehyde adducts were revealed. Since these deleterious visual cycle products initiate photooxidative processes and are also the source of fundus autofluorescence, links were established between disease processes underlying anomalous hyperautofluorescence in fundus images and light-mediated disease exacerbation. Moreover, the findings support efforts to restrain disease processes by antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine.

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is the term given to a group of blinding inherited retinal diseases. Worldwide RP affects at least 1.5 million people, and in the United States, it is estimated that the incidence is 1:4,000 (1–4). RP is genetically heterogeneous. Many of the genes responsible for RP exhibit rod-specific expression. For instance, mutations in the rod opsin protein, which together with the 11-cis-retinal (11cisRAL) chromophore forms photopigment, are the most common cause of autosomal dominant RP (adRP). Indeed, 30% of individuals in the United States presenting with adRP have mutations in opsin (5). Moreover, the proline-to-histidine substitution (P23H) opsin mutation is situated in the N-terminal intradiscal loop of rhodopsin and is the most common cause of adRP, accounting for ∼10% of cases (3, 6). Several mechanisms have been considered as the cause of photoreceptor cell degeneration in the presence of P23H opsin (7). Studies of the P23H knock-in mouse (P23H/+) model (8) have revealed that P23H opsin does not accumulate in the endoplasmic reticulum of photoreceptor cells. Instead, it is degraded and thus is present in outer segments of P23H/+ knock-in mice at low levels. P23H opsin can activate phototransduction but does not activate proapoptotic signaling by the unfolded protein response. Additionally, outer segments are structurally defective (8–11).

In RP, photoreceptor cell degeneration typically begins in the mid-peripheral retina and progresses toward the macula. In some cases of RP, including in individuals expressing the P23H mutation (Figure 1), disease progression is exemplified in short-wavelength fundus autofluorescence (SW-AF) images (488 nm) by a paracentral ring or arc of elevated autofluorescence (12–15). These rings are not genotype specific and, in at least some cohorts, are visible in a majority of RP patients (16). Hyperautofluorescent rings in RP are considered to reflect a transition between abnormal (outside) and normal (internal to) retinal function, and SD-OCT and functional studies have shown that photoreceptor cells within the ring are degenerating (14, 17–25).

C) SD-OCT scan acquired from the patient. The horizontal axis of the SD-OCT scan is indicated by a horizontal line in the infrared reflectance image on the left.

The source of SW-AF is the family of bisretinoid fluorophores (26, 27) that form in photoreceptor cells due to nonenzymatic reactions of vitamin A aldehyde (retinaldehyde). Bisretinoids are deposited in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells as components of phagocytosed outer segment membranes and accumulate as lipofuscin (28). The toxicity of bisretinoids is attributable, at least in part, to their propensity to photogenerate reactive oxygen species and to undergo photolysis, releasing dicarbonyl- (glyoxal, GO; methylglyoxal, MG) and aldehyde-bearing fragments (29, 30). Thus, we sought to probe features of the P23H/+ mouse model so as to gain molecular insights into the underlying basis for hyperautofluorescence patterning and the adverse effects of light in adRP.

Results

Outer nuclear layer thinning

Photoreceptor cell viability was analyzed by measuring the thickness of the outer nuclear layer (ONL). To test the light sensitivity of P23H/+ mice housed under conditions of cyclic light, we compared ONL thicknesses in black versus albino P23H/+ mice with black C57BL/6J and albino C57BL/6Jc2j mice as wild-type controls. Ocular melanin deficiency is associated with substantially increased intraocular light intensity (31, 32).

Thinning of the ONL in both black and albino P23H/+ was readily visible in histological sections and OCT scans at 4 and 8 weeks of age (Figure 2A–C). Histometric analysis of ONL thicknesses plotted as a function of distances superior and inferior to the optic nerve head (ONH) at age 4 weeks revealed ONL thinning that was more pronounced in the albino P23H/+ mice than black P23H/+ mice (Figure 2D). The thinning was further accentuated at age 8 weeks (Figure 2D). For additional quantitation, ONL area was determined from the sum of the ONL thicknesses together with the measurement interval (33, 34). At age 4 weeks, ONL area was lower in both albino and pigmented P23H/+ mice relative to wild type (Figure 2E). In albino P23H/+ mice at age 4 weeks, ONL area in the inferior retina was 45% lower relative to the superior hemiretina (P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparison test; Figure 2F). In the inferior retina, the reduction in ONL area was also more pronounced in albino P23H/+ mice (62% reduction) than in black P23H/+ mice (P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparison test; Figure 2F). Moreover, in albino P23H/+ mice that were dark reared, ONL thinning was reduced in both superior and inferior retina (Figure 2G and H; P < 0.001, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test).

Retinoid levels in P23H mice

At 2 weeks of age in black P23H/+ heterozygous mice, 11cisRAL was 70% lower (P < 0.01, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test) than in wild-type mice at the same age (Figure 3A). All-trans-retinal (all-transRAL) was also lower (33%; P < 0.05, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test). However, since 11cisRAL was lower in the wild-type mice relative to the levels observed 2 weeks later at age 4 weeks, these lower levels in the wild-type mice may reflect an early stage of development when the visual cycle was not fully functional.

At age 4 weeks, ocular retinoids 11cisRAL and all-transRAL in the dark-adapted retinae were reduced in the black and albino P23H/+ mice relative to black and albino wild-type mice (C57BL/6J and C57BL/6Jc2j; P < 0.01, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test; Figure 3B and E). The greatest decline was observed in 11cisRAL. Specifically, in the black mice at age 4 weeks, the reduction in 11cisRAL relative to the wild-type mice was 72% (P < 0.01, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test; Figure 3B), while the decrease in 11cisRAL in the albino P23H + mice was 85% (P < 0.01, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test; Figure 3E). The reductions in 11cisROL and 11-cis-retinyl ester were also statistically significant (P < 0.01, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test) in both the albino and black mice (Figure 3B and E). All-trans-retinal ester (all-transRE) was reduced in the albino P23H/+ mice (73% decrease) and black P23H/+ mice (63%; age 4 weeks; Figure 3B and E) relative to the corresponding wild-type mice, but the reduction in the black mice did not reach significance (P > 0.05, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test). The albino and black mice (P23H/+ mice) also exhibited similar reductions in all-transRAL (68%) (P < 0.01, two-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test; Figure 3B and E) but ocular all-transROL was not different in wild-type versus P23H/+ mice, in neither the albino nor the black strain (Figure 3B and E). At age 4 weeks, summed retinoid (all-transROL, all-transRE, all-transRAL, and 11cisRAL) in the black P23H/+ mice was reduced by 67% (Figure 3C), while in the albino P23H/+ mice at 4 weeks, the reduction was 77% relative to the wild-type mice (wild type: 853.7 pmole/eye; P23H/+ 192 pmole/eye; P < 0.05, two-tailed paired t test). Given the difference in ocular retinoids due to the P23H knock-in mutation, we measured retinoids in plasma (Figure 3D). In P23H and C57BL/6J mice at age 4 weeks, plasma levels of retinol were not statistically different (P > 0.05, two-tailed t test).

Analysis by qRT-PCR of expression levels of retinol dehydrogenase enzymes

Retinol dehydrogenase (Rdh) activity is critical to the detoxification of retinaldehydes and to the retention of retinoids within the visual cycle as opposed to the formation of bisretinoids. Thus, we examined for changes in expression of retinol dehydrogenase 8 and 12 (Rdh8 and Rdh12) by RT-qPCR with the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as endogenous control. At age 2 weeks, expression of Rdh8 and Rdh12 in the mutant mice was comparable to wild-type mice (Figure 3F, left). In albino P23H/+ mice at age 6 weeks, Rdh8 and Rdh12 were both down-regulated with the expression of Rdh12 being <50% of the control wild-type expression (Rdh12: P < 0.01, Rdh8: P > 0.05, unpaired two-tailed t test; Figure 3F, right).

Bisretinoid detected in P23H heterozygous mouse retina

SW-AF originates from the bisretinoid lipofuscin fluorophores that form in photoreceptor cells (28); thus, we also analyzed chloroform/methanol extracts of mouse eyes by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Representative HPLC and ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) chromatograms in Figure 4A revealed the detection of bisretinoids in chloroform/methanol extracts of whole eyecups from albino P23H/+ and wild-type littermates. With monitoring at 430 nm, absorbance peaks analyzed by UPLC were assigned to the pyridinium–bisretinoid fluorophores A2-glycerophosphoethanolamine (A2-GPE; λmax 340 and 440 nm) (35). In HPLC chromatograms, A2E (λmax 330 and 430 nm) and iso-A2E (λmax 330 and 440 nm) (36) were identified in albino and black heterozygous P23H mice.

By integrating peak areas and converting to picomolar quantities using calibration curves, we quantified A2-GPE, together with A2E and iso-A2E (summed as A2E) in black and albino P23H/+ and in wild-type mice at age 4 weeks. A2E and A2-GPE were both more abundant in black P23H/+ and albino P23H/+ mice relative to the corresponding wild-type mice (C57BL/6J and C57BL/6Jc2j; P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test; Figure 4B). For further analysis, we summed A2E and A2-GPE levels at age 4 weeks and found that together (summed bisretinoids) there was 1.8-fold greater summed bisretinoid in black P23H/+ mice than in black C57BL/6J mice (P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparison test) and a 2.9-fold difference in summed bisretinoid between albino P23H/+ mice and albino C57BL/6J−c2j mice (P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparison test; Figure 4C). Summed bisretinoid was also 28% lower in the albino P23H/+ mice than in the black P23H/+ mice (P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test), indicating that bisretinoid levels varied with ocular pigmentation (Figure 4C). In the wild-type mice, the 50% reduction in summed bisretinoid in albino versus black mice (Figure 4C; P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparison test) was also consistent with our previous studies (37).

To probe for the presence of autofluorescence in outer retina, we examined cryostat sections of black P23H/+ and wild-type mice (age 24 weeks) that had been stained with DAPI. Using fluorescence parameters (ex: 450–490 nm; em: 500–550; ∼1 s exposure time) that yielded no visible autofluorescence in the wild-type mouse, orange autofluorescence was recorded in the P23H/+ mouse posterior to the ONL, where it was aberrantly located within inner and possibly remaining outer segments (Figure 4E). In the ONL of the black P23H/+ mouse, DAPI-stained photoreceptor cell nuclei were considerably fewer than in the wild-type mouse.

Plots of retinoid quantity and ONL area versus bisretinoid amounts (the sum of A2E, iso-A2E, and A2-GPE) are presented in Figure 4D. Bisretinoids form due to nonenzymatic indiscriminate reactions of retinaldehyde. Thus, it is also notable that even as total retinoid levels (all-transROL, all-transRAL, all-transRE, and 11cisRAL) in the mutant mice were only 33% of normal as early as age 4 weeks in the P23H opsin knock-in mice, ONL area (reflecting photoreceptor cell numbers) was diminishing, and bisretinoid levels were increasing (Figure 4D).

In the SD-OCT scan, in the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained histological sections (Figure 2A–C) and in the fluorescence micrograph (Figure 4E), outer segments in P23H/+ mice appear to be shorter than in wild type. Nevertheless, our analysis indicates that reduced bisretinoid in the albino mutant is not attributable to outer segment shortening. Since 11cisRAL levels are positively correlated with outer segment length (8), we compared the ratio of bisretinoid/11cisRAL in P23H/+ mice and wild-type mice (age 4 weeks). This ratio is greater in P23H/+ mice (albino P23H/+, 1: 0.012; pigmented P23H/+, 1: 0.013) versus wild type (albino wild type, 1: 0.003; pigmented wild type, 1: 0.002) consistent with higher levels of bisretinoid in the mutant mice (Figure 2C). Conversely, the bisretinoid/11cisRAL ratio was similar in albino and pigmented P23H/+ mice. Photoreceptor cell degeneration is accelerated in the albino mutants (Figure 2A and D). If diminished bisretinoid in the albino relative to the pigmented mutant (age 4 weeks) was due to shorter outer segments (instead of photooxidation), one would expect the bisretinoid/11cisRAL ratio to be different in the pigmented versus albino mutants. This is not observed.

Bisretinoid analysis in P23H-1 rats

We conducted additional bisretinoid analyses of eyes obtained from albino P23H rhodopsin transgenic rats generated on a Sprague–Dawley background (SD-Tg(P23H)1Lav; P23H-1) (38). These mutant rats carry one insertion of the transgene along with two copies of the wild-type rhodopsin gene. The P23H-1 line exhibits the earliest age of onset and fastest rate of degeneration of the three P23H lines. As was the case for the P23H/+ mouse, 11cisRAL was greatly diminished in the albino P23H-1 rats at age 10 weeks (Figure 4F), and at this age, the bisretinoids A2E and A2-GPE were increased relative to wild-type albino Sprague–Dawley rats (Figure 4G).

N-Acetyl-L-cysteine

Antioxidants have been shown to provide protection to photoreceptor cells in rd1/rd1 mice, rd10/rd10 mice and Q344ter mice (39–41). The effectiveness of N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) (42), in particular, has been tested in these same RP mouse models (rd1/rd1 and rd10/rd10) and in a human clinical trial (43). Thus, we also set out to examine NAC efficacy in the P23H/+ knock-in mouse model. We treated mice with NAC from birth first by providing NAC to lactating mothers (7 mg/mL in drinking water) and upon weaning, mice received NAC (1 mg/mL) in their drinking water.

In the black P23H/+ mice at age 12 weeks, NAC was associated with amelioration of ONL thinning that was manifest as a 78% increase in ONL area (P < 0.01, two-tailed unpaired t test; Figure 5A and B). To further study the black P23H/+ mice treated with NAC, we recorded full-field electroretinographic responses. In NAC-treated mice (age 6 weeks), a-wave scotopic amplitudes reflecting rod photoreceptor cell activity were increased (P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test) when responding to flash stimuli of 10 cd-s/m2 and b-wave scotopic amplitudes were elevated with stimuli of 0.01 and 0.1 cd·s/m2 (P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test; Figure 5C left, right). At irradiances in the photopic range, NAC-treated black P23H mice exhibited significantly increased a- and b-wave responses to 10 and 0.1 cd-s/m2, respectively (Figure 5C left, right; P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test). In NAC-treated albino P23H/+ mice, the observed improvement in ONL width was not statistically different, but SW-AF intensity was greater in the NAC-treated mice (Figure 5F, left) in both the superior and inferior hemiretina (Figure 5F, right). In an additional mechanistic study, we tested NAC in a cell-free assay employing the bisretinoid A2E and 430 nm irradiation. The consumption of A2E associated with photooxidation was diminished by the addition of NAC (Figure 5G).

As an additional line of evidence corroborating NAC efficacy, we assessed the distribution of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)-modified proteins in NAC-treated P23H/+ mice. Generated as a product of lipid peroxidation, 4-HNE fragments are derived from ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (linoleic and arachidonic acid) when their conjugated double bonds undergo hydrogen atom extraction (by OH•, HO2∙ or addition (by 1O2) by reactive oxygen species (44). Bisretinoids are photosensitizers that generate reactive oxygen species such as singlet oxygen and superoxide anion (45–50); we previously detected 4-HNE as a downstream product of bisretinoid photoreactivity (51). In the P23H/+ mice (NAC-treated and untreated mice), positive 4-HNE immunoreactivity was most pronounced in the photoreceptor cell-attributable laminae of outer retina (Figure 5H). In untreated P23H/+ mice, this included bright immunofluorescence posterior and anterior to the ONL; the posterior band of labeling coincided with the positions of the photoreceptor cell inner segments. Immunolabeling was also visible in the outer plexiform layer. The signal was visibly lower in NAC-treated mice. Labeling was not present when primary antibody was omitted (Figure 5H).

The 4-HNE immunocytochemical labeling (Figure 5H) can be readily distinguished from the inherent autofluorescent signal recorded and presented in Figure 4E. For the immunocytochemical experiment (Figure 5H), cryostat sections from young P23H/+ mice (age 4 weeks) were used to reduce the contribution from autofluorescence, as the latter is expected to increase with age. The red immunocytochemical signal obtained in black P23H/+ mice was consistent with the Cy3 emission and was imaged with an exposure of ∼200 ms. Conversely, the autofluorescence exhibited an orange emission as expected for bisretinoid emission and was recorded in much older (24 weeks) albino P23H/+ (Figure 4E). The 4-HNE immunoreactivity included a faint reaction product in the inner retina (i.e. inner plexiform layer) that was absent in the negative control and diminished in the NAC-treated mouse. An autofluorescent signal was not observed in the inner retina of the P23H/+ mouse (Figure 4E).

Discussion

Sometime ago, it was shown that P23H opsin has limited ability to bind 11cisRAL so as to form photopigment (52). In both the black and albino mutants, inadequate incorporation of chromophore and a decline in P23H opsin protein (8, 11) occur as a result of the gene mutation. It is plausible that with the resulting excess in unbound 11cisRAL, the latter chromophore would leave the visual cycle by reacting with PE to form bisretinoid (2 retinaldehyde per molecule of bisretinoid). Ultimately, these anomalous processes would manifest as decreased 11cisRAL and increased bisretinoid, factors ancillary to the gene mutation. Indeed, we found that in albino P23H/+ mice 11cisRAL was only ∼14% of wild type at age 4 weeks (Figure 3E).

P23H is also one of a group of opsin mutations in the N-terminus of rhodopsin that confers sectoral RP in humans (53–56). The asymmetry that characterizes these RP cases presents as greater retinal dysfunction in the inferior retina (superior visual field) than in the superior retina along with more aggressive abnormal ONL thinning in the inferior hemisphere; the superior temporal quadrant is the last to degenerate completely (8, 53–56). The superior–inferior difference associated with P23H opsin is attributed to greater retinal illuminance in the inferior hemiretina (57–59). Although opsin is rod-specific, cones eventually succumb (60–62).

As in the human presentation of P23H disease, disease mechanisms in the P23H/+ mice were aggravated by light. Specifically, light entering the eye is substantially increased in the albino (31, 32, 63). Thus, not only was ONL thinning accelerated in albino versus black P23H/+ mice, photoreceptor cells in albino P23H/+ mice were protected by dark rearing. ONL thinning was also more rapid in the inferior hemiretina versus superior retina in both black and albino P23H/+ mice (Figure 2F) (8) and in the comparison of black versus albino, ONL thinning indicative of photoreceptor cell loss was also more advanced in the inferior retina of albino P23H/+ versus black P23H/+ mice. These findings are meaningful since inferior retina is exposed to greater environmental light than superior hemiretina (53–56, 64–72).

Using single-cell transcriptomics a significant decrease in expression of genes associated with phototransduction and metabolic pathways in both rods and cones was previously observed at the age of 7 weeks in P23H/+ mice (73). Here, we observed that Rdh12 mRNA expression in albino P23H/+ mice was <50% of control wild-type expression at age 6 weeks. Bisretinoids form due to unchecked reactivity of retinaldehyde with phosphatidylethanolamine. NADPH-dependent chemical reduction of retinaldehyde (to retinol) is energy consuming and given the metabolic imbalance that exists in RP (74) the impaired photoreceptor cells may be overwhelmed. Thus, the reduced Rdh12 expression and impaired ability to handle retinaldehyde may explain why in both the black P23H/+ mice and albino P23H/+ mutants bisretinoid levels were elevated when compared with the corresponding wild-type mice (C57BL/6J and C57BL/6J-c2j).

Bisretinoid levels were 28% lower in 4-week-old albino P23H/+ mice than in the black P23H/+ mice. We do not attribute the declining levels in albino mice to a difference in photoreceptor cell survival only, since the black versus albino P23H/+ difference in ONL area at age 4 weeks was only 10% (Figure 2). Moreover, as was observed previously (37) and in the current work (Figure 4C), this black versus albino difference is also observed in wild-type mice without any evidence of photoreceptor degeneration. Additionally, when albino C57BL/6Jc2j mice are dark reared, bisretinoid levels are similar to black C57BL/6J mice (37). Instead, we note that bisretinoids are photoreactive and undergo toxic photooxidative degradation (29, 75–80). The latter photodegradation process also accounts for the lower bisretinoid levels in the albino versus black P23H/+ knock-in mouse, the more advanced ONL thinning in the albino versus black P23H/+ mouse and the more aggressive ONL thinning in the inferior versus superior hemiretina. The sensitivity to light conferred by bisretinoid has also been observed in other settings including in Abca4−/− mice having elevated levels of bisretinoid. Specifically, light-damage-associated ONL thinning was more pronounced in Abca4−/− mice than in age-matched wild-type mice (81). The ONL thinning was also greater in 5-month versus 2-month-old mice. In Rpe65rd12 mice, bisretinoids are not detected chromatographically, and light damage is not observed (81). Support for involvement of photooxidative processes was illustrated by our finding that vitamin E treatment of Abca4−/− mice reduced disease-associated ONL thinning (37).

Mechanisms linking light, bisretinoid photooxidation and photoreceptor cell degeneration were also revealed in these studies. Greater photoreceptor cell nonviability together with greater bisretinoid photodegradation was observed in albino P23H/+ versus black P23H/+ mice. Moreover, not only was it demonstrated in an in vitro assay that the antioxidant NAC protected against photooxidative degradation of bisretinoids, but NAC-treated mice also exhibited preserved intensities of SW-AF (that originates in bisretinoid lipofuscin), defense against ONL thinning and preservation of ERG amplitudes when compared with untreated P23H/+ mice. These findings are supportive of toxic mechanisms involving light together with oxidative processes that act on bisretinoid and lead to a worsening of photoreceptor cell viability.

We detected an anomalous autofluorescence in the inner segments of photoreceptor cells. The inherent emission generated by the relatively long-wavelength excitation (450–490 nm) is indicative of an origin from bisretinoid. While the autofluorescence is inappropriately located, low levels of opsin are reported to be expressed in the inner segments of healthy photoreceptor cells (82). Mislocalized rhodopsin has been described in P23H transgenic rats (83) and in a mouse knock-in that carries a human P23H rhodopsin-GFP fusion gene on one genomic rhodopsin locus (84). Several years ago lipofuscin-like inclusions were reported to form in photoreceptor inner segments in association with RP (85). In a healthy retina, bisretinoid fluorophores form in outer segments and are transferred secondarily to RPE, where they constitute the lipofuscin. Conversely, the formation of bisretinoid in the inner segments is consequential: the inner segment membrane is not renewed as are outer segments. Photoreactive bisretinoid fluorophores forming in the inner segments would be retained in the membrane and could serve as photosensitizers generating reactive forms of oxygen that damage photoreceptors (29).

That photooxidative mechanism is a key component of and contributor to RP disease progression, which is consistent with evidence that disease progression in human and mouse models of RP, including in the current study, can be slowed by antioxidants that reduce carbonyl content (43, 86). The antioxidant NAC (39, 40, 42, 60, 86, 87), when employed in RP models, protects both rods and cones (60). NAC has been considered to mediate antioxidant effects through its ability to promote glutathione (GSH) synthesis (88). It is thus noteworthy that the photooxidation and photolysis of bisretinoid release the dicarbonyls MG and GO (29, 30), and GSH is able to donate hydrogen atoms to and form conjugates with these photoproducts (89). Recent evidence also indicates that NAC triggers intracellular formation of enzymatically generated reduced sulfur (H2S and hydrogen sulfide) and small molecule intracellular hydropersulfides (such as cysteine hydropersulfide and glutathione hydropersulfide) that are efficient scavengers of radicals, peroxides, and electrophiles (90, 91). As opposed to the benefit afforded by NAC treatment, the AMPK activator metformin impaired photoreceptor cell viability in P23H/+ mice despite reports that it improved rhodopsin folding and trafficking (92).

Toxic nonenzymatic products of lipid peroxidation in the form of 4-HNE are well-known biomarkers of oxidative insult. The 4-HNE protein adducts form when the aldehyde moiety of 4-HNE reacts with an amino group on cysteine, lysine, or histidine residues in a protein. Proteins modified by 4-HNE are reported to form in retinas following exposures to damaging light intensities (93). The investigators not only established that these protein adducts preceded evidence of photoreceptor cell degeneration, they also demonstrated that retinal areas with the highest 4-HNE staining coincided with areas exhibiting decreased ONL thickness (93). We have shown previously that 4-HNE forms as a downstream product of bisretinoid photoreactivity (51). In the current study, the region of retina having the highest HNE immunoreactivity was outer retina in the laminae occupied by photoreceptor cells. Of particular interest was 4-HNE-associated immunoreactivity in the inner segments. Also found in this region was autofluorescence reasonably attributed to aberrant formation of bisretinoid in the inner segments. NAC visibly reduced the 4-HNE immunoreactivity detected in the inner segments.

A limitation of this study is that given the difference between human versus mouse retina topography, the P23H/+ mouse does not present with a hyperautofluorescent ring as in human RP. Nevertheless, the P23H/+ mouse faithfully models human RP. ONL thinning in the inferior retina occurs in advance of that in the superior retina. Furthermore, black P23H/+ mice have an increase in bisretinoid fluorophores (the source of SW-AF) that corresponds spatially and temporally with ONL thinning. The elevated SW-AF (relative to healthy eyes) that is associated with fundus AF rings or arcs in RP patients (15) together with the increased bisretinoid observed in the P23H/+ mice may indicate that the process of rod photoreceptor cell dysfunctioning in the presence of a gene mutation leads to increased bisretinoid formation and photodegradation as secondary factors in disease progression.

In summary, we have shown here that photoreceptor cell degeneration in at least some forms of RP is aggravated by a secondary disease-associated increase in the formation and toxic photodegradation of bisretinoid. The presence of elevated bisretinoid in association with the P23H/+ mutation relative to wild type is significant since heightened bisretinoid formation in photoreceptor cells is expected to confer light-exacerbated photoreceptor cell degeneration. Perhaps the increase in bisretinoid occurs because rod photoreceptor cells disabled by gene mutation are unable to provide sufficient NADPH for the detoxification of retinaldehyde by retinol dehydrogenase activity. These findings reveal that increased bisretinoid lipofuscin is likely a factor in the formation of SW-AF rings in RP (Figure 1). Importantly, these results corroborate the employment of oral NAC as an agnostic approach to attenuating photoreceptor cell loss in RP. In phase 1 and 2 clinical trials, oral NAC was shown to be safe and effective when administered to RP patients (43).

Materials and methods

Animals

Black homozygous RhoP23H mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, United States) and bred in house. Heterozygous F1 were generated by mating homozygous with wild-type C57BL/6J mice or albino C57BL/6Jc2j mice. All mice carried the Rpe65-Met450 variant and were either male or female. Control wild-type mice were either litter mates of the albino P23H/+ or were C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratories). In P23H/+ mice, the endogenous mouse gene is replaced by a mouse opsin gene carrying the P23H mutation on one allele (8). mRNA levels of the P23H opsin are comparable to that of the wild-type opsin (8). Mice were screened for Rd1, Rd2, Rd3, Rd6, Rd8, Rd10, and Rd7 by a genotyping service (Transnetyx, Cordova, TN, United States). Albino P23H rhodopsin transgenic rats generated on a Sprague–Dawley background (SD-Tg(P23H)1Lav; P23H-1) were obtained from Rat Resource & Research Center (University of Missouri, Columbia, MO, United States). Mice were treated with NAC from birth by providing NAC to lactating mothers (7 mg/mL in drinking water) (42). Upon weaning, mice received NAC (1 mg/mL) in their drinking water (42).

Included in the study was one patient. Procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was obtained. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Columbia University. Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Columbia University and complied with guidelines set forth by the ARVO Animal Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

In vivo fundus imaging

SW-AF images (55° wide-field lens; 0.98 mm detection pupil; 488 nm excitation) were obtained from anesthetized mice with a Spectralis HRA (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) with laser power set at ∼280 μW and sensitivity at 100 (34). Nine successive frames were acquired at 488 nm excitation with the high-speed mode, and SW-AF intensity (qAF) was calculated by normalization to the GL of the reference after subtraction of zero light (GL0) and inclusion of a reference calibration factor. Mean gray levels (GLs) were calculated from eight predefined segments around the optic disc; blood vessels were excluded by histogram analysis.

Horizontal and radial B scans were also acquired by spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT, Bioptigen, Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, United States) with 1.8 mm radial and rectangle scans.

Histological analysis

Mouse eyes of various ages were marked at the superior limbus with an animal tattoo ink paste (Ketchum Manufacturing Inc., Ottawa, Canada) and fixed in Alcoholic Z-fix (4% paraformaldehyde, 20% isopropanol, 2% trichloroacetic acid, and 2% zinc chloride) (94). Five micron H&E-stained paraffin sections were prepared and digitized with Leica AT2 by Histowiz (Brooklyn, NY, United States). The three sections most centrally located within the ONH were selected. ONL thickness was measured at 200 micron intervals superior and inferior to the edge of the ONH along the vertical meridian (33). ONL area was calculated as the sum of the ONL thicknesses in the superior and inferior retina multiplied by the measurement interval of 200 microns (95).

Electroretinography

A Celeris rodent ERG system (Diagnosys) was used to acquire full-field ERG recordings; corneal electrodes were positioned with GenTeal Tears gel (Alcon Laboratories). In dark-adapted mice, scotopic ERG responses were generated by single flashes (4 ms) having intensities from 0.001 to 10 cd.s/m2. Photopic responses were elicited using 0.1 to 32 cd.s/m2 intensities and were recorded in mice adapted to background light of 30 cd.s/m2 for 10 min. Ten responses were averaged.

Analysis of Rdh8 and Rdh12 transcript abundance by qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from freshly dissected neuroretinas using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and integrity were assessed as the OD260/OD280 nm absorption ratio using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States). Isolated RNA (0.5 μg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems). The synthesized cDNA was used for Q-PCR amplification in a 96-well optical plate using SYBR Green I Master mix (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) with 25 μM of each primer. Serial dilutions of the cDNA were initially used for Q-PCR amplification of the reference gene GAPDH to evaluate the quality of each cDNA library using the reference gene standard curve. The reliability of the reference gene and the quality of the cDNA libraries were determined by linear correlation of the threshold crossing point values (Ct) with the logarithmic value of the DNA amount (96). All reactions were performed as technical triplicates and were carried out in a 7900 HT Fast Real-Time RT-PCR System (Applied Biosystems, United States) with an initial activation step at 95 °C for 5 s followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and extension at 60 °C for 25 s. RDH8 and RDH12 primers based on previous designs (97) were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, United States). Reference gene GAPDH primer sequences were previously published (98). All data were normalized to GAPDH expression levels, and the fold changes were calculated for each gene using the comparative 2-(ΔΔCt) method. Fold change for each gene of interest was calculated with the control as 1.

Quantitative HPLC and UPLC analysis of bisretinoid

Mouse eyecups (4–10 eyes/sample) were homogenized, extracted in chloroform/methanol and concentrated, as previously described (37). For UPLC quantitation, murine eyecup samples were re-dissolved in ethanol after extraction and injected (10 μL) into a Waters Acquity UPLC-MS system (Waters, Milford, MA, United States) using an Acquity BEH Phenyl Column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters). Eluents were water–acetonitrile (1:1) with 0.1% formic acid (A) and isopropanol–acetonitrile (9:1) with 0.1% formic acid (B). Separation was achieved using the following elution gradients: 0–50 min, 100–55%; 50–110 min, 55–35%. Flow rate was 0.2 mL/min. UV absorbance peaks were identified by comparison with external standards of synthesized and purified A2E and A2-GPE. Identification was confirmed by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Peak areas were determined using Waters Empower Software. Molar quantities per eye were calculated based on standard solutions with concentrations determined spectrophotometrically (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, United States). For analysis by HPLC (Alliance System, Waters Corp.), samples were re-dissolved in CHCl3/MeOH = 1:1 after extraction as above and injected (30 μL) as previously described (37). Absorbance peaks were identified using synthesized authentic compounds, and molar quantities per eye were calculated by comparison with synthesized standards. All-trans-A2E (A2E) and iso-A2E were measured separately and summed.

Tissue extraction and quantitative UPLC analysis of retinoids

Frozen mouse eyecups (one eye/sample) were homogenized and derivatized with 1 mL of O-ethylhydroxylamine (100 mM) in DPBS (Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline, pH 6.5, without CaCl2 and MgCl2) on ice using glass homogenizers (94). After vortexing, samples were allowed to stand for 15 min at 4 °C. All-trans-retinyl acetate as an internal standard was added to each sample. Methanol (1 mL) was added, and the samples were extracted with hexane (3 mL, three times). After centrifugation (1,500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C), the hexane phase was dried under argon gas and then resuspended in 20 µL of acetonitrile for UPLC analysis. For separation (94), a Waters Acquity UPLC-PDA system (Waters) was used with a CSH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) and gradients of water (A) and acetonitrile (B) with 0.1% formic acid as follows: beginning at 60% (B), holding for 5 min; a linear increase to 70% (B) over 55 min; a linear increase to 100% (B) over 10 min with holding for 20 min (flow rate of 0.3–0.5 mL/min). RAL (O-ethyl)oximes were monitored at 360 nm and 11cisROL, all-trans-retinyl palmitate, 11-cis-retinyl palmitate were monitored at 320 nm. UV absorbance peaks were identified by comparison with external standards of synthesized 11cisRAL (O-ethyl)oxime, all-transRAL (O-ethyl)oxime, all-transROL, and all-trans-retinyl palmitate. Molar quantities per eye were calculated based on standard solutions with concentrations determined spectrophotometrically. Peak areas were calculated using Waters Empower Software and results were analyzed in Excel (Microsoft). O-ethylhydroxylamine was used to convert retinaldehyde into a stable oxime product for accurate quantitation.

Immunocytochemistry

Cryostat sections of 4% paraformaldehyde-fixed eyes (12-micron thickness) acquired from NAC-treated and untreated P23H heterozygous mice (age 4 weeks) were stained with rabbit polyclonal antibody (ThermoFisher, cat. # BS-6313R) to 4-HNE protein adducts (1:200 dilution, 5 μG/mL) overnight at 4 °C followed by Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Fab),1:400 dilution at room temperature for 1 h (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) for indirect detection. A negative control (omission of primary antibody) was performed concurrently. The nuclei were stained with DAPI. The immunostained sections were imaged using a Leica DM6B upright LED fluorescence microscope with a TXR filter set (excitation 540–580 nm, emission 593–667 nm).

In vitro assay

Synthesized A2E (36) (200 μM in DPBS; 1% dimethyl sulfoxide) was exposed to 430 nm light for 1 min in the absence and presence of 400 μM NAC. A2E was extracted and analyzed by HPLC as described above.
