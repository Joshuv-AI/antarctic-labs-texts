Sarcopenia and dynapenia are associated with depression rather than anxiety in Chinese hemodialysis patients
Authors: Xiaoyu Chen, Peipei Han, Zhenwen Liang, Liou Cao, Jing Gao, Ke Ding, Wenzhen Li, Siyi Zhu, Yifan Xue, Qi Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAffective disorders contribute to poorer outcomes in hemodialysis patients. We aimed to investigate the association of depression and anxiety with sarcopenia and dynapenia among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.MethodsA total of 1074 patients (676 men, mean aged 61.3 ± 12.6 years) undergoing hemodialysis from 7 facilities in Shanghai of China from 2020 to 2023. Depression was evaluated by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and anxiety was assessed by the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7). The cut-off values of muscle mass and muscle function to define sarcopenia, dynapenia, and presarcopenia were according to the consensus by the Asia Working Group of Sarcopenia in 2019.ResultsAmong 1074 participants, 160 (14.9%) had depression, 85(7.9%) had anxiety. Patients were divided into four groups: sarcopenia (n = 211), dynapenia(n = 267), presarcopenia(n = 100), and the robust (n = 496) group. The proportion of depression in the group sarcopenia, dynapenia, presarcopenia and robust were 19.4%, 22.8%, 14.0%, and 8.9% (sarcopenia vs. robust P < 0.001; dynapenia vs. robust P < 0.001; overall p value < 0.001). The proportion of anxiety in the four group were 10.4%, 10.5%, 6.0%, and 5.8%, respectively (overall p value > 0.05). Multivariate analysis revealed that sarcopenia, dynapenia, Malnutrition Inflammation Score (MIS), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and Fractional clearance index for urea (Kt/V) were significant factors linked to the presence of depression.ConclusionOur findings suggest that sarcopenia and dynapenia are associated with depression rather than anxiety in hemodialysis patients. Interventions or programs aiming to alleviate sarcopenia or dynapenia can be effective strategies to prevent depression in the hemodialysis population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07365-3.Keywords: Depression, Anxiety, Sarcopenia, Dynapenia, Hemodialysis

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are the most common psychological symptoms observed in patients receiving hemodialysis therapy, with higher prevalence in this population than the general population [1, 2]. These psychiatric comorbid conditions have elevated risk of adverse impact on hemodialysis patients, such as poorer dialysis treatment outcomes, reduced quality of life, and higher rates of hospitalization and mortality [3, 4]. However, both depressive and anxiety symptoms in hemodialysis patients are frequently underdiagnosed and untreated [5]. Thus, it is essential to early recognize of modifiable risk factors and appropriate management of affective disorders in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis.

Previous research indicates that mental health is associated with multiple factors, including genetics, serious illness, drugs, social factors and lifestyle [6]. More recently, studies have focused on examining the relationship between mental health and muscle function. Evidence suggests that muscle secretion, for example irisin, is thought to be involved in the pathogenesis of depression and anxiety [7]. Sarcopenia- characterized by reduced muscle mass and impaired muscle function - is a common condition among hemodialysis individuals [8]. Since the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older Adults (EWGSOP) established diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia in 2010, research has increasingly integrated assessments of muscle mass and strength. Notably, the co-occurrence of low muscle strength with preserved muscle mass defines dynapenia, a significant mortality risk factor in dialysis populations [9]. Consequently, it makes sense to correctly distinguish between loss of muscle mass and strength. In fact, the discrepancy in how muscle mass relates to muscle strength and its subsequent impact on adverse clinical outcomes has become a focus of ongoing research within the international scientific community [10, 11].

Since sarcopenia and psychiatric disorders have similar consequences and risk factors, sarcopenia is correlated with depression was reported by a recent systematic review involving community and outpatient settings [12, 13]. In addition to depression, sarcopenia is also linked to anxiety in the general population [14]. In particular, multiple studies involving hemodialysis patients showed a strong relationship between sarcopenia and depression as well as anxiety [15–17]. However, incomplete diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and small sample size are limitations of the above studies. Regarding dynapenia, preliminary research indicated that it is related to mental health in older adults [18, 19] and obese patients [20]. However, no studies to data have examined potential associations between dynapenia and depression/anxiety in hemodialysis patients. Clarifying the association of sarcopenia and dynapenia with affective disorders is clinically important as it may serve as a basis for the establishment of interventions to improve muscle mass and muscle function and effectively prevent psychiatric impairment.

Therefore, we performed a multi-center cross-sectional study in seven hospital setting to investigate the association between sarcopenia, dynapenia with depression and anxiety among patients undergoing hemodialysis.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Shanghai Hemodialysis Cohort Study (SHCS) is a multicenter prospective dynamic cohort study focusing on the relationships between physical performance and health status of a population receiving maintenance hemodialysis living in Shanghai, China (Clinical trials. gov UMIN000056404. Registered Dec 9, 2024). Our research population included maintenance hemodialysis patients from seven hospital in Shanghai of China who participated in and completed the questionnaire survey during 2020 and 2023. Participants were eligible to participate if they were over 18 years of age, receiving maintenance hemodialysis treatment for at least three months, and able to provide informed consent. Patients were excluded as following criteria: (1) missing data on diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia or dynapenia, (2) did not complete date on the depression and anxiety assessments, (3) patients with visual impairment difficulties or hearing impairment; (4) clinical instability (presented with an infection, heart failure, or malignancy), and (5) unable to communicate with the researchers or refusal to participate in this study. After excluding 62 subjects, the final analytic sample consisted of 1074 patients (men 676, women 398). 36 patients refused to participant in depression or anxiety assessment due to personal reasons; 4 were unable to finish the handgrip strength test because of hand disability;22 unable to measure the body composition. The Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Medicine and Health Sciences approved the study and these methods were implement in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (number 2019-A4-2621-19-201001-03-12010419771113601X). All of the participants provided the informed consent prior to enrollment in the study.

Covariates

All patients needed to accept a face-to-face interview to finish a standardized questionnaire. Covariates consisted of sociodemographic characteristics, chronic disease condition, health behaviors. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, post-dialysis weight, dialysis vintage and education level. Comorbidity was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index(CCI), which describes multiple comorbidities and forms a summation score based on the presence of 19 comorbidities [21]. Physical activity was evaluated according to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [22]. In addition, all the blood sample were drawn before dialysis. Fractional clearance index for urea (Kt/V) was used to assess the dialysis quality, which was monitored in every dialysis treatment using the OCM® (On-line Clearance Monitor; Fresenius Medical Care, Bad Homburg, Germany). Nutritional status was assessed according to the Malnutrition Inflammation Score (MIS) [23], which is a comprehensive scoring system with significant relationships with prospective hospitalization and mortality in hemodialysis patients.

Definition of sarcopenia, dynapenia, and presarcopenia

A direct segmental multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis was used to measure muscle mass (BIA; InBody S10; Biospace, Seoul, Korea) [24]. Low muscle mass was defined as skeletal muscle index (SMI) < 7.0 kg/m2 in male and < 5.7 kg/m2 in female, respectively. Muscle strength was evaluated using the dynamometer (GRIP-D; Takei Ltd, Niigata, Japan) on the non-fistula hand prior to the dialysis. Low muscle strength was defined as handgrip strength less than 28 kg and 18 kg in men and women, respectively. Poor physical performance was assessed using the usual gait speed, which was less than 1.0 m/s in both men and women. All participants underwent these tests on dialysis days before dialysis treatment by a physical therapist at each facility using the same measurement method.

2019 new version of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria was used to diagnose sarcopenia [8]. The criteria included both low muscle mass and low muscle strength and/or poor physical performance. Dynapenia was diagnosed as patients with decreased muscle strength and normal muscle mass [25]. Presarcopenia.

was defined as low SMI with normal handgrip strength and normal gait speed [26].

Assessment of depression

Depressive symptoms over the past two weeks were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [27]. The total score ranges from 0 to 27, with an overall score of ≥ 10 representing clinically significant depression [28]. Details of measurement methods can be found in our previous study [23].

Assessment of anxiety

Anxiety was measured by the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7) [29].Patients reported the frequency with which they had experienced each of the seven symptoms during the last two weeks on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half of the days; and 3 = nearly every day). A score of 8 or higher was defined as anxiety [30, 31], and the scale was proved to be reliable and valid in dialysis patients.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups with Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as dictated by distribution. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In terms of categorical variables, difference between groups were estimated using Fisher’s exact tests or Pearson χ2 test, as appropriate. First, soci-dempgraphic and health-related characteristics of hemodialysis patients were stratified by depressed or anxious status. Next, Participants were classified into mutually exclusive groups based on sarcopenia status and dynapenia: sarcopenia, dynapenia, presarcopenia and robust groups. Independent factors were determined by incorporating the p < 0.05 factors associated with depression in univariate analysis into multivariate logistic regression analysis (Age, education, MIS, IPAQ, number of medications, CCI, and Kt/v). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), and P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association of sarcopenia, dynapenia and presarcopenia with depression and anxiety in a multi-center hemodialysis population. We found that both sarcopenia and dynapenia were associated with depression but not anxiety among patients undergoing hemodialysis. These findings highlight the importance of integrating of muscle mass and muscle function into clinical practice throughout the dialysis treatment to prevent depression disorders.

This study shows that the prevalence of depression and anxiety are 14.9% and 7.9%. Dano et al. and Mapes et al. reported that the prevalence of depression in hemodialysis patients was 19% and 20%, respectively. Similarly, Mohamed et al. showed the prevalence of anxiety was 7%. However, it is important to note that the prevalence of depression and anxiety was notably lower than the estimated prevalence of 22.8%−39.3% for depression and 12%−52% for anxiety reported in comprehensive systematic reviews [32, 33]. The possible reasons are the differences in the scale selection and the different cut-off values chosen. Another possible reason is that people in Shanghai of China have a better economic status, and the universal health insurance system allows all dialysis patients to get better treatment, which also reduces their psychological pressure to a certain extent.

Our findings regarding the association between sarcopenia and depression align well with those of previous studies conducted among the general population [12] and patients undergoing the hemodialysis [15–17]. However, compared with the three previous studies on dialysis, our study differs in several aspects. First, the diagnosis of sarcopenia in Kim et al. ‘s study was incomplete, using only muscle mass and muscle strength to diagnose sarcopenia [16]. In addition, the sample size of Kim et al.’s study [16] included 95 hemodialysis patients, while that of Khoury et al.’s study [17] only contained 42 patients on hemodialysis, both of which had certain limitations. Our study used the 2019 new version of the AWGS criteria to diagnose sarcopenia and included 7 hemodialysis centers with a sample size of more than 1000 individuals. Second, although Kurita et al. found that depression was associated with sarcopenia in dialysis patients, the study mixed peritoneal dialysis as well as non-dialysis chronic kidney disease patients. Similarly, the diagnosis of sarcopenia in this study was not rigorous, using only a questionnaire, and some of the potential confounders of sarcopenia were not collected in the study, such as nutritional measures [15]. Our study addresses these limitations well, but further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm our results. Studies on the association between anxiety and sarcopenia are very rare, with only one report to date, and the conclusion is inconsistent with the finding of our study [17]. Part of the reason for the difference is that the sample size of that study was only 42 hemodialysis patients, and other difference may be in the setting of the pandemic, inflation, and the Beirut blast. Further studies are needed to confirm the relationship.

Some previous studies have demonstrated that dynapenia is associated with mental health in older adults [18, 19] and obese patients [20]. Presently, no study has explored the link between dynapenia and depression and anxiety in patients on hemodialysis. This study was the first to demonstrate that dynapenia is associated with depression in hemodialysis patients. Although there are no studies of dynapenia and depression in dialysis populations, there is still a link between a certain element such as muscle strength and depression, and the finding is consistent with ours. Zhang et al. [6] suggested that lower muscle strength is associated with a higher prevalence of depression in Chinese patients undergoing hemodialysis. Our study also found no evidence that dynapenia was associated with anxiety. This finding is consistent with the only study conducted to date which found no associations between dynapenia and anxiety in older adults [19]. Further research is needed to determine whether the selected GAD-7 recognizes anxiety states in hemodialysis populations that are associated with dynapenia, which would also aid in development of interventions.

The mechanism of the associations between sarcopenia and dynapenia with depression are unclear, but appear to be complex and multifactorial. Studies have shown that skeletal muscle, as an endocrine organ, can secrete myokines. Muscle contraction leads to changes in the skeletal muscle environment, which can affect the physiological state of myokines [34]. It is worth noting that myokines can improve brain function. Several studies have indicated that numerous myokines, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor [35], irisin [36], etc., can act on the nervous system through gene regulation and many endocrine signaling pathways. Moreover, depression has been consistently related to a low-grade inflammatory state [37]. The decrease of inflammation and oxidative free radicals may reduce the risk of depression by improving muscle function [38, 39]. Psychosocial mechanisms may also at least partly explain the associations between sarcopenia, dynapenia and depression in the current study. Depressed hemodialysis patients typically experienced a decrease in physical activity, thereby declining the likelihood of social contact with others and increasing the likelihood of developing depression through a vicious cycle.

We believe that the present findings suggest that strategies to increase muscle mass and function may provide suitable adjunctive treatments for depression among hemodialysis population. Thus, this highlights a need for nephrology physicians and co-medical staff to pay attention to these patients. Emerging evidence indicates that resistance training and nutritional supplements may improve depression among older adults [40, 41]. Early screening and management of musculoskeletal disorders, and encouraging patients to engage in effective exercise and nutritional programs may help reduce the likelihood of developing depression.

Our study also confirmed the association of nutritional status, comorbidity and dialysis efficiency with depression. More details of the relationship between malnutrition and depression can be found in our previous study [23]. In fact, patients who receive hemodialysis treatment are usually unstable, in a very poor state, and have numerous complications. Our study showed that patients undergoing hemodialysis with more comorbidities were at elevated risk of depression. In addition, effective hemodialysis efficiency is associated with a lower risk of depression.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between sarcopenia, dynapenia, and depression and anxiety in a multi-center hemodialysis population. Understanding this association is essential for the prevention and treatment of mental disorders in patients on hemodialysis. Second, we used new version criteria to diagnose sarcopenia and dynapenia, as opposed to other studies that used only one element. However, several limitations should be considered. This study is based on a cross-sectional design. Thus, it is not possible to elucidate clear causal associations among sarcopenia, dynapenia and depression in the patients receiving hemodialysis. Secondly, this study only conducted a general assessment of the patients’ nutritional status, and did not conduct a detailed evaluation of their protein and calorie intake. In future research, a more in-depth assessment of nutrition should be conducted. Thirdly, although the results of this study are statistically significant, only patients from seven hospital in Shanghai of China were selected for the study, and the results may not be extended to other populations (such as mental disorders, and people from other countries). More prospective cohort studies are needed for further investigation.

In conclusion, our study showed that sarcopenia as well as dynapenia were associated with a higher risk of depression rather than anxiety in patients undergoing hemodialysis, therefore suggesting that improving muscle mass and muscle function may have potentially beneficial effects on depression. Furthermore, the association between sarcopenia, dynapenia and depression in patients on hemodialysis should be investigated in a large cohort study to clarify this finding.
