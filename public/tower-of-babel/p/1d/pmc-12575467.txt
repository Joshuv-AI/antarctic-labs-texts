Role of IL-17 cytokines in tubal fibrosis and infertility due to salpingitis
Authors: Jiao Zhao, Li Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review systematically examines the etiology and pathophysiology mechanisms of infectious diseases affecting the female reproductive tract, with a specific focus on the intricate relationship between inflammatory lesions in the fallopian tubes and reproductive dysfunction. The interleukin-17 cytokine family, as pivotal inflammatory regulators, plays a central role in pathological processes of fallopian tube inflammatory responses, tissue fibrosis, and fertility impairment through a multi-faceted regulatory network. IL-17 C and IL-17 A, key members of the IL-17 family, are pivotal drivers of fibrosis. IL-17 A activates Th17 cells and T cells, promoting the release of inflammatory factors. Furthermore, IL-17 C boosts the expression of IL-17 A, establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies both inflammatory and fibrotic responses. This exacerbates tissue damage, ultimately culminating in luminal obstruction and dysfunction that hinders gamete transport and fertilization. Although the precise pathogenic mechanisms of tubal inflammatory infertility are not fully elucidated, the detrimental impact of tubal inflammation on fertility is widely recognized. This is particularly relevant given the increasing trend of delayed childbearing. While perioperative interventions can partially mitigate infertility risk, persistent inflammatory stimulation remains the primary pathological factor driving infertility. By systematically investigating the epidemiological characteristics and pathogenesis of this condition, this study aims to enhance clinical treatment strategies.Keywords: Salpingitis, Infertility, Fallopian tube epithelial cell damage, Fibrosis, Mechanism

Introduction

Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) is a common infection of the female pelvic organs, characterized by the ascent of microorganisms from the vagina and cervix to the endometrium, fallopian tubes, and adjacent structures [1]. Reproductive tract infections can be categorized by their origin: those caused by exogenous microorganisms and those arising from endogenous flora of the lower genital tract. Exogenous pathogens are predominantly sexually transmitted, such as; Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis [2]. These pathogens induce uterine contractions that facilitate the ascent of cervical contents, triggering an inflammatory response in the fallopian tube epithelium and consequently leading to tissue damage in the fallopian tubes.

Infertility is a major global health issue with profound personal and social consequences [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines infertility as the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected intercourse [4]. Globally, indicate that infertility affects approximately 15% to 20% of women of reproductive age. Tubal factors are a leading cause of female infertility [5], responsible for about 25% to 35% of cases. The functional integrity of the fallopian tubes is essential for key reproductive events, including sperm-egg interaction, embryo transport, and implantation [6]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of tubal inflammation is crucial for advancing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for infertility.

Salpingitis is a well-established cause of infertility, most commonly resulting from pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), and it increases the risk of ectopic pregnancy(EP) by 7 to 10 fold [6]. EP can directly damage tubal epithelial cells, leading to fibrosis and the scar tissue formation, which further exacerbates tubal factor infertility (TFI). The clinical management of these conditions remains challenging, underscoring the need for more effective treatments. A key question is whether salpingitis directly causes infertility through its pathogenesis. This review aims to elucidate the underlying pathogenic mechanisms linking salpingitis to infertility, with a specific focus on alterations in the local microenvironment and their role in lesion progression.

Epidemiology

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is an infectious and inflammatory condition of the upper female genital tract, encompassing the endometrium, fallopian tubes, ovaries, or pelvic peritoneum. The spread of inflammation from the vagina or cervix to the upper genital tract marks an intermediate stage in the progression of PID. Clinical diagnosis often relies on findings such as pelvic tenderness; however, many women with upper genital tract infection experience minimal or no symptoms, a presentation termed subclinical PID. PID is a major clinical concern primarily because of its long-term sequelae, including infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain [7].

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is a leading cause of tubal factor infertility and a significant risk factor for ectopic pregnancy, primarily due to inflammation and adhesions in the fallopian tubes [8]. In high-income countries, PID accounts for 94% of female morbidity attributable to sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Although PID is rarely life-threatening, its long-term sequelae, such as chronic pelvic pain and infertility, represent a substantial health burden [9]. In resource-limited regions, the incidence of tubal infertility is influenced by complex socioeconomic factors. A primary driver is the higher-than-global-average prevalence of PID in these areas, which is partly attributable to inadequate healthcare infrastructure and a high burden of sexually transmitted infections [10]. For instance, in 2019, countries such as Niger, Burkina Faso, and Gambia reported the highest global PID rates, despite notable declines since 1990 [11]. Consequently, the strong link between tubal infertility and ectopic pregnancy is particularly pronounced in these geographical areas. Studies confirm that PID-induced tubal damage is a major risk factor for ectopic pregnancy, with incidence rates substantially higher in low-income nations than in high-income countries [12]. Persistent poverty, limited healthcare accessibility, insufficient sexual education outreach, and inadequate prevention measures for STIs collectively exacerbate the reproductive health vulnerabilities faced by women in these regions [13].

Timely diagnosis of PID remains challenging, as many women with upper genital tract infections exhibit minimal or no symptoms, a condition termed subclinical PID. Consequently, some individuals only become aware of their PID status upon a subsequent diagnosis of tubal factor infertility. The etiology of PID is involves several factors: infection with sexually transmitted pathogens such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis infections; infections of the female reproductive tract by respiratory or intestinal pathogens; and the ascent of bacteria and mycoplasma from the vaginal microbiome which triggers inflammation [2]. Epidemiologically, the annual incidence of PID is estimated at 10–13 cases per 1,000 women aged 15–39, peaking at 20 cases per 1,000 among those aged 20–24. PID increases the risk of ectopic pregnancy by tenfold, with PID accounting for approximately 25% of this increased risk. Infertility rates following PID range from 5.6% to 60%, depending on the infection’s severity, frequency, and the patient’s age [6].

Infertility carries profound implications for women, families, and society. A laparoscopic study on Swedish women with suspected PID revealed that 74% of them had salpingitis, with 16% subsequently experiencing infertility and 9% developing an ectopic pregnancy [14, 15]. Inflammatory damage from such infections promotes scar tissue and adhesion formation within the fallopian tubes, potentially causing partial or complete blockage. The loss of ciliated epithelial cells can further disrupt ovum transport, leading to tubal infertility or ectopic pregnancy [16]. Despite advancements in assisted reproductive technologies, the crucial role of fallopian tubes in natural conception has been underappreciated. However, its normal physiological function is indispensable for successful conception, as they facilitate gamete gathering, embryo transit to the uterus, and provide an optimal environment for fertilization and early embryonic development [17]. The parasympathetic innervation of the fallopian tubes stems from the pelvic nerve plexus, increasing the risk of tube infection in cases of PID [18]. This review aims to investigate how fallopian tube inflammation impacts fertility, explore potential pathogenic mechanisms, assess the relationship between tube inflammation and infertility, and delve into associated factors.

IL-17 cytokines play a crucial role as inflammatory mediators in various diseases, including autoimmunity, allergies, and infections [19]. Among them, IL-17 A is the most extensively studied. Initially identified as a product of helper Th17 cells in adaptive immune reactions, IL-17 A exerts its effects through the IL-17RA/IL-17RC receptor complex [20]. IL-17 C, an epithelial-derived cytokine, functions as a homodimer through IL-17RA/IL-17RE. There is functional redundancy between IL-17 A and IL-17 C as both require IL-17RA. These cytokines induce the expression of cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides in target cells, such as lung epithelial cells, by activating transcription factors NF-κB and MAPK, and stabilizing mRNA. They also synergize with inflammatory mediators like TNF-α or microbial stimuli. IL-17 A signaling contributes to fibrosis via the TGF-β1 pathway [21]. Research has demonstrated that kidney diseases trigger an inflammatory response through intricate interactions between renal tubular epithelial cells and the immune system, instigating and propelling pathogenic inflammatory processes. In mice, heightened IL-17 C expression exacerbates crescentic glomerulonephritis, leading to Th17 cell activation, elevated IL-17 A levels, and increased neutrophil infiltration [22]. Moreover, activation of the Th17/IL-17 A pathway is evident in acute kidney injury (AKI) and db/db mice, with inhibition of IL-17 C significantly reducing IL-17 A expression and Th17 cell infiltration [23]. Mechanistic investigations reveal IL-17 A’s pivotal role in Th17-mediated asthma, with its suppression effectively mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress, potentially ameliorating steroid-resistant asthma. Additionally, IL-17 A can directly modulate epithelial cells, fostering tissue remodeling [24].Therefore, IL-17 C, on the other hand, enhances inflammation in an autocrine manner by amplifying Th17 cell responses through IL-17RE, leading to excessive IL-17 A production that worsens fibrosis. Subsequent sections will delve into the impact of IL-17 cytokines on the onset and progression of salpingitis-induced infertility.

Mechanisms of salpingitis

The pathogenesis of inflammatory infertility in the fallopian tubes involves several key mechanisms, which can be categorized into three primary processes: inflammatory damage to the epithelium, loss of ciliated cells, and fibrosis [25]. Histopathologically, the inflammatory response involves the infiltration of neutrophils and lymphocytes. In contrast, chronic or recurrent infections are characterized by the mononuclear cell infiltration [26], epithelial cell necrosis, and ciliated cell loss [27]. Furthermore, ulcerated epithelium is replaced by fibrous tissue, leading to connective tissue atrophy, chronic scar formation, and ultimately, tubal blockage [28]. Inadequate clearance of pathogenic microorganisms can lead to persistent inflammatory stimulation, with tubal fibrosis representing a severe consequence of such stimulation [29].

In mammals, the skin and mucosal epithelial cells constitute the primary barrier against pathogen invasion and play a crucial role in the host immune response to infections. The crosstalk between the immune system and epithelial cells is vital not only for effective host defense against infections but also for preventing autoimmunity [30, 31]. Among immune cells, the TH17 subset of helper T cells is essential for defending against extracellular bacterial and yeast infections. TH17-derived cytokines, particularly interleukin 17 C (IL-17 C) and IL-17 A, enhance the innate immune responses of epithelial cells and fibroblasts [32]. Research indicates that IL-17 C is produced by a variety of cell types in both mice and humans, including colonic and pulmonary epithelial cells [33, 34], keratinocytes [35], and smooth muscle cells [36]. Furthermore, IL-17 C promotes IL-17 A production from Th17 cells through the induction of TNF-α generation, thereby contributing to the progression of Th17 cell-mediated autoimmune encephalomyelitis [37].

In prior research, Vladimir Ramirez-Carrozzi and colleagues identified IL-17 C and IL-17 A as members of the IL-17 family. IL-17 C is predominantly expressed in epithelial cells and signals through a unique heterodimeric receptor complex composed of IL-17RA and IL-17RE subunits. Specifically, IL-17RE partners with IL-17RA to function as a proficient signaling receptor for IL-17 C, which mediates inflammatory responses [38]. This mechanism can be selectively triggered in epithelial cells by bacterial infiltration and inflammatory stimuli. In contrast, IL-17 A, upon binding to its receptors IL-17RA and IL-17RC, induces the production of pro-inflammatory chemokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-8), chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL2), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), promoting fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, and hastening tissue fibrosis. Synergistically, IL-17 A and IL-17 C exacerbate epithelial damage and contribute to microenvironmental remodeling in the fallopian tubes [39]. Furthermore, the ability of IL-17 C to enhance IL-17 A production by Th17 cells may provide a mechanistic basis for inflammatory infertility in the fallopian tubes.

Epithelial inflammatory injury, ciliary cell death or shedding

T cells play a critical role in regulating host defense responses initiated by epithelial cells during infection [40]. Among helper T cell subsets, TH17 cells are particularly important for defending against extracellular bacterial and fungal infections [41]. Notably, IL-17 C, a member of the IL-17 cytokine family, is predominantly produced by epithelial cells and contributes to Th17 cell development [42]. Elevated levels of IL-17 C are detected in lung and skin tissues during infections with Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus, as well as in psoriatic skin lesions [35, 39], This suggests a potential role for IL-17 C in both host defense and inflammatory processes within these tissues. Functionally, studies have demonstrated that IL-17 C stimulates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β in monocyte cell lines [43]. Delivery of IL-17 C to the lungs via adenovirus induces neutrophil aggregation [41]. Moreover, CD4 + T cells overexpressing IL-17 C have been shown to exacerbate collagen-induced arthritis in recipient mice [44]. Collectively, these findings collectively indicate a pro-inflammatory function for IL-17 C, which is further supported by observations that its ectopic expression in vivo induces inflammation by stimulating TNF-α production [44, 45]. Mechanistically, IL-17 C signaling is dependent on Act1, a juxtamembrane adaptor molecule, which is crucial for its downstream effects. Specifically, IL-17 C activates the IL-17RA-RE and Act1 complex, leading to the expression of IkBz, a nuclear IkappaB family member that enhances the Th17 cell response. Salpingitis is primarily caused by infections, resulting in inflammatory damage to the fallopian tube epithelium. The ciliated cells within the fallopian tube play a crucial role in facilitating pregnancy; however, the death or shedding of these ciliated cells is a consequence of inflammation and impairs oocyte transport to the uterus [46]. The pathogenic process begins with bacterial infiltration and adherence to the mucosal epithelium. This leads to fallopian tube swelling and redness, epithelial destruction, and luminal accumulation of an exudate containing lymphocytes and shed epithelial cells. Furthermore, extensive infiltration of lymphocytes and polymorphonuclear leukocytes into all layers of the tube wall occurs, ultimately resulting in ciliary stasis and shedding [27, 47]. Importantly, the human fallopian tubes exhibit IL-17 C receptor expression on their epithelial surface. Treatment with purified IL-17 C not only leads to epithelial exfoliation and systemic tissue damage but also the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines. These findings indicate that IL-17 C plays a crucial, albeit previously underrecognized, role in the inflammatory response induced by gonococci in the human PID explant model [48].

Evidence indicates that IL-17 C contributes to inflammation by recruiting neutrophils to local sites and promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and hepoxilin A3 (HXA3); These factors collectively drive tissue damage [49, 50]. Furthermore, such cytokines modulate the inflammatory process by activating and inducing leukocyte chemotaxis. Notably, TNF-α levels are closely associated with the loss of ciliary activity, and TNF-α alone is sufficient to induce epithelial damage [51].

Significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the pathophysiology of fallopian tube inflammation. To address this, Erin M. Garcia and colleagues utilized the human fallopian tube organ culture (FTOC) method to study mechanisms of inflammation-induced ciliated cell damage. Through ex vivo culturing of human fallopian tubes and paired RNA sequencing, they identified transcripts associated with cytokines, chemokines, cell adhesion, and apoptosis [38, 52]. The IL-17 C, a member of the IL-17 cytokine family, is uniquely expressed in epithelial cells and is among the most significantly upregulated genes that stimulate the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines.Treatment with IL-17 C triggers inflammatory cell death responses in both ciliated and non-ciliated cells, upregulating anti-apoptotic factors, including BCL2A1 (Bfl-1), BIRC3 (cIAP-2), and PTGS2 (COX-2), following infection-induced stimulation of the ciliated cell epithelial [53, 54]. The role of IL-17 C in inflammatory diseases of the reproductive tract remains unclear. The specific receptor chain of IL-17 C, IL-17RE, is expressed on fallopian tube epithelial cells and can mediate microbial infection. During inflammation, IL-17 C activity increases, exacerbating inflammatory responses and causing epithelial tissue damage in the fallopian tube, which aligns with its proposed autocrine function [26].

In various mucosal infection models, IL-17 C signaling is pivotal for enhancing innate epithelial host defense by inducing antimicrobial peptides and cytokines, maintaining barrier integrity, and facilitating tissue repair [55, 56]. IL-17 C also modulates the adaptive immune response, since Th17 cells express the IL-17 C-specific receptor and are activated by IL-17 C stimulation [37]. However, excessive IL-17 C production can exacerbate inflammation and contributes significantly to various T cell-dependent and independent inflammatory pathologies [57, 58]. Beyond synergizing with other pro-inflammatory ctokines, IL-17 C intensifies its inflammatory responses through a positive feedback loop of its own secretion signaling. Specifically, IL-17 C augments the impact of TNF-α through a pro-inflammatory feedback loop, enhancing the expression of both cytokines, thereby cooperatively upregulating the expression of diverse pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and innate immune factors [57, 59]. This synergistic interplay between IL-17 C and TNF-α has been validated in human FTOC, where IL-17 C treatment induced TNF-α production, and TNF-α was shown to be involved in tissue destruction. The IL-17 C/TNF-α synergy is therefore believed to be a key driver of inflammation in PID [60, 61].

Fibrosis

Chronic or recurrent infections in the female reproductive tract can cause infection-induced tubal fibrosis, which may result in ectopic pregnancy or infertility [62]. The invasion and damage of fallopian tubes by pathogenic microorganisms trigger pro-inflammatory and fibrinogenic signaling pathways in epithelial cells, promoting the synthesis of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins that act as a provisional scaffold [63]. This inflammatory response is amplified by the infiltration of immune cells. The effective suppression of inflammation is crucial for initiating the repair process, which involves the recruitment of myofibroblasts. These myofibroblasts are central to wound healing and fibrosis, as they synthesize and remodel collagen, fibronectin, and other ECM components [64]. Furthermore, they facilitate wound closure through contractile forces generated by cytoskeletal stress fibers rich in smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). Epithelial cells can also contribute to this pool via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a reversible process of transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts [65]. Under normal conditions, upon wound resolution, myofibroblasts undergo programmed cell death or dedifferentiation, coinciding with the cessation of proliferation and fibrinogen signaling [66, 67]. However, in the context of recurrent injury, persistent inflammation, or aberrant growth factor signaling, myofibroblasts may persist, leading to excessive collagen deposition, sustained growth factor production, and abnormal epithelial cell proliferation, ultimately culminating in scar tissue formation.

Fibrosis is not a distinct disease but rather a consequence of dysregulated tissue repair following various forms of injury, particularly in the setting of chronic inflammatory conditions. This pathological progression can impact any organ, and is currently associated with approximately 45% of all deaths worldwide. Although fibrosis has conventionally been considered irreversible, recent investigations in animal models and clinical trials across various organ systems have revealed its dynamic and potentially reversible nature [68].When tissue damage occurs, the initial stage involves acute inflammation and repair. In this stage, inflammatory mediators are released due to tissue damage, leading to the activation of the NLRP3-caspase-1 platform. This activation triggers the rapid release of IL-1β and IL-8, which in turn attract neutrophils and monocytes to the site of injury. Additionally, TGF-β1 is primarily sourced from platelets and epithelial cells during this stage. Fibroblasts undergo a transient transformation into α-SMA activated myofibroblasts, and there is a preferential deposition of type I collagen (COL1A1). In the subsequent stage, if the injury persists or recurs, macrophages transition from the M1 to M2 phenotype. The polarization of CD4 + T cells is primarily driven by Th17/IL-17 A. Additionally, IL-4/IL-13 upregulates the TGF-β1 autocrine loop through STAT6, enhancing stability of collagen type III (COL3A1). This process ultimately leads to chronic scars formation, underscoring the central role of inflammation in fibrosis [69, 70]. Thus, fibrosis can be viewed as he endpoint of persistent injury and/or dysregulated wound healing processes [71].

IL-17 C amplifies TNF-αeffects through a pro-inflammatory feedback loop that induces the expression of both IL-17 C and TNF-α, thereby synergistically upregulating various other pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and innate immune factors [72]. Furthermore, IL-17 C enhances IL-17 A production by Th17 cells [37]. IL-17 A signals via a receptor complex comprising IL-17RA and IL-17RC, present on cell types such as keratinocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts [38]. TNF-α utilizes TNFR-1 (p55) and TNFR-2 (p75) on dermal fibroblasts to promotes DNA synthesis and the secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 [73]. TNF-α also upregulates IL-17RA expression, leading to elevated mRNA levels of the (IL-17RA-IL-17RB) complex in human primary lung fibroblasts [74]. In contrast, TGF-β1 downregulates (IL-17RA-IL-17RB) mRNA expression in these fibroblasts. Studies by Nakashima et al. have shown that TGF-β1 stimulation reduces IL-17RA expression in normal fibroblasts via a Smad3-dependent manner [75]. Consistently, subsequent studies have demonstrated that TGF-β1-stimulated human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) exhibit increased Smad3 mRNA expression alongside decreased IL-17RA and IL-17RC expression, indicating that the TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway regulates both IL-17RA and IL-17RC [76].

IL-17 A and TNF-α are central mediators in the inflammatory network, driving immune activation while concurrently perpetuating epithelial damage. IL-17 A, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, can induce fibrosis in multiple organ systems, including the lungs, liver, kidneys, heart, and skin [77–80]. Neutrophils and mast cells represent important cellular sources of IL-17 A [81]. Studies using IL-17 A-deficient mice, IL-17RA-deficient mice, and therapeutic interventions with IL-17 A neutralizing antibodies have confirmed the critical role of IL-17 A signaling in the fibrotic processes across different tissues [82–84]. Mechanistically, IL-17 A not only stimulates the TGFβ production but also upregulates and stabilizes TGFβRII expression on fibroblasts, thereby enhancing their responsiveness to TGF-β [76, 85]. Furthermore, the combined action of IL-17 A and TNF-α induces TGF-β expression [86, 87], thereby establishing a feed-forward loop among these acute-phase cytokines that promotes fibrosis following injury [88]. The TGF-βligand superfamily is widely recognized as a key driver of fibrosis [89]. Caspase-1, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and NF-κB have been identified as critical upstream regulators of the IL-17 A-TGF-βaxis. Studies have shown that sustained activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is closely linked to increased chemokine expression, recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages, and continuous production of IL-17Aand TNF-α [77]. Thus, IL-17 A plays a key role in the activation of the profibrotic TGF-β signaling pathway.

IL-17C drives fallopian tube fibrosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome through the TH17 response

Conclusion

This study explores the mechanisms of tubal inflammatory infertility and proposes the IL-17 family member hypothesis to elaborate on the role of IL-17 C in augmenting IL-17 A production by Th17 cells, elucidating the pathogenesis of tubal inflammatory infertility. This proposed mechanism primarily centers on fibrosis triggered by persistent inflammatory stimuli. By providing these novel research insights, this hypothesis offers insights a framework for understanding and addressing tubal inflammatory infertility, thereby contributing to advancements in prevention, treatment, and clinical diagnosis. As a common gynecological condition, tubal inflammatory infertility warrants focused research. Future studies should prioritize strategies to modulate pathological inflammatory responses within the context of fibrosis, aiming to restore female fertility.

The pathogenesis of salpingitis begins with pathogen invasion or tissue damage, which triggers oviductal epithelial cells to express IL-17 C. IL-17 C subsequently stimulates Th17 cells to enhance their proliferation and effector capabilities, leading to increased IL-17 A production. IL-17 A, in turn, acts on epithelial, establishing an “IL-17 C-Th17-IL-17A” positive feedback loop that amplifies inflammation. The persistence of this loop drives chronic fallopian tube inflammation, tissue fibrosis, functional impairment, and ultimately, infertility. As a central upstream regulator, IL-17 offers a promising therapeutic target. The development of neutralizing monoclonal antibodies against IL-17 C or its receptor IL-17RE could precisely disrupt the inflammatory cascade at its origin, potentially minimizing broader immune system implications and improving treatment safety. Future efforts should prioritize the effective translation of these findings into clinical practice.①In the future, employing cell co-culture systems, conditional gene knockout mice (e.g., specific IL-17 C receptor knockout in T cells), and inhibitors is essential to elucidate whether IL-17 C directly promotes Th17 differentiation in T cells or indirectly influences Th17 polarization by modulating cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, and TGF-β released by antigen-presenting cells (e.g., epithelial cells). Furthermore, it is critical to investigate how factors, such as IL-17 A from Th17 cells, reciprocally regulate IL-17 C expression in epithelial cells to fully delineate the drivers of this feedback loop. ②To observe the dynamic interaction of this regulatory axis in the oviduct microenvironment, the application of multiphoton intravital imaging is needed. This technique will allow for the real-time visualization of the localization and interplay between IL-17 C-producing cells and Th17 cells within fallopian tube tissue in disease models. By utilizing mouse genetics and single-cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology, all cell subsets participating in this regulatory axis and their distinct gene expression profiles can be precisely identified under inflammatory conditions. This approach will unveil the cellular foundation and operational mechanisms of this network in vivo.③The therapeutic potential of targeting key nodes within this regulatory axis requires systematic evaluation. Targeting upstream or intermediate components of IL-17 C may offer a more precise intervention strategy than broad IL-17 A inhibition, which could increase infection risk. Future studies should systematically compare the efficacy and safety of anti-IL-17 C neutralizing antibody, anti-IL-17 A antibody, and interventions targeting Th17 cell differentiation in animal models. Additionally, investigating the synergistic effects of combined targeted therapies is also warranted. These studies can provide essential preclinical evidence for the development of precise immune intervention strategies.④It is imperative to validate this regulatory axis in clinical samples and assess its association with disease progression. Tissue samples from individuals with salpingitis at various stages (acute, chronic, fibrotic) should be collected to spatially and quantitatively assess the infiltration of IL-17 C, Th17 cells, and IL-17 A using techniques such as multiplex immunofluorescence and flow cytometry. Subsequent analysis should explore their correlation with specific pathogen infections, tissue damage severity, and the long-term fertility outcomes of patients. This approach will help to confirm the clinical significance of this regulatory axis.

This review aims to advance the development of experimental models for studying the inflammation-fibrosis transition in human tissues. Enhanced comprehension of the underlying mechanisms, signaling pathways, and associated targets of inflammation holds promise for novel therapeutic approaches in addressing infertility. Translating these insights into clinical practice is critical for enhancing the precision and efficacy of interventions for tubal infertility. For instance, current perioperative interventions such as hydrotubation, hysterosalpingography (HSG), ultrasound-guided hysterosalpingography (HYCosy), and laparoscopic hydrotubation may mitigate infertility risk. However, many aspects of the disease pathogenesis remain unexplored. Therefore, future research should elucidate the molecular mechanisms implicated in tubal inflammatory infertility, along with the upstream signals or predisposing factors contributing to tubal epithelial impairment. Presently, promising avenues for investigation are emerging. Ultimately, insights into the mechanisms of inflammation-induced tubal damage are poised to advance our comprehension of tubal inflammatory infertility pathogenesis, thereby paving the way for restoring female fertility.
