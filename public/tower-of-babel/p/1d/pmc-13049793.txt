Enhancing expertise in management of gestational diabetes mellitus: protocol for development of structured curriculum
Authors: Yashdeep Gupta, Simran Kaur, Ravneet Kaur, Arun Pulikkottil Jose, Anshika Sharma, Rashmi Ramachandran, Ambuj Roy, Nikhil Tandon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is high prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), which is associated with short and long-term adverse outcomes for the mother and her offspring. It is crucial to improve management of GDM. However, currently, there is no or limited structured program(s) incorporating the education principles delineated here for training of the physicians, which could help in further improving management of GDM.​ObjectiveWe intend to design such a curriculum for healthcare professionals following principles of educational learning, which could help in improving the learning of medical students and expertise of physicians regarding better management of GDM.MethodsWe will develop a curriculum for management of GDM through a systematic process, which will involve problem identification, needs assessment, goal setting, educational strategies, implementation, and evaluation. ​ We will adopt a blended learning approach, which will combine online sessions for theoretical preparation and in-person workshops for hands-on practice. ​ For learning strategies we will use pre-recorded lectures, assessments, interactive sessions, case discussions, and supplementary materials. ​ Focus will to impart knowledge regarding diagnosis of GDM, antenatal and peripartum management, postpartum care, and planning of subsequent pregnancies. ​The proposed course will likely be of around 10 modules, which could span over 3 to 4 h. The proposed course will incorporate Miller’s pyramid and Merrill’s principles to help the learners in skill-based mastery and practical application of the knowledge. ​ We will conduct pilot testing in two batches over six months to understand the lacunae and refine the curriculum.​ConclusionThis course will likely help the healthcare professionals to deliver timely and effective care for women with GDM. Better management can help in improving patient outcomes and could reduce the burden on healthcare systems.

Introduction

The global burden of diabetes is huge and is further rising at an alarming rate [1]. ​ As per current estimates, 588.7 million individuals (20–79 years) had diabetes in 2024. The number is further projected to increase to 852.5 million in 2050 [2]. Alongside, the prevalence of hyperglycemia in pregnancy is also increasing. In 2024, 23.0 million (19.7% of all pregnancies) pregnancies were affected with hyperglycemia, and 79.2% had GDM [2].

Hyperglycemia in pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes for both the mother and her offspring [3–5]. ​ Effective management is essential to decrease the risk for adverse outcomes [6, 7]. ​ For achieving goal of effective management, it requires both adequate healthcare system resources and the specialized expertise of medical professionals [8].

Context of the training and building the expertise of medical professionals

In India rural areas accounts for 65% of the total population [9, 10]. ​ In rural areas, the primary point of contact with a physician is usually the Primary Health Centre (PHC) [10]. Patients can be referred to Community Health Centres (CHCs), which has doctors specialized in medicine, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, and surgery [11]. ​ This structure helps in delivering the care to women with GDM at the grassroot level. ​ However, the public health system is overburdened and unable to meet the needs of all population, which leads to a significant dependence on and role of private-sector doctors for the care of pregnant women [12].

With rapidly evolving knowledge, it is challenging for doctors to stay updated with the latest clinical skills. Further, even mastery over existing guidelines and evidence-based practice is lacking at many places. ​ This gap create an urgent need for a structured curriculum, which could enhance the care provided to women with GDM. ​ Such a curriculum would benefit physicians, whether in public or private sector, and those pursuing postgraduate degrees in various related specialities like medicine or obstetrics and gynecology.

Description of intended course

This course is tentatively divided into 10 modules, each module of around 20 to 30 min, and with total time duration of 3 to 4 h. The curriculum will allow participants to progress at their own pace, but within a stipulated 6-month window. ​ Every module will have a pre-module assessment, which will consist of 10 multiple-choice questions (MCQs). Conceptual problems will be created to gain insights into the participants’ understanding of the module’s content. After a pre-module assessment, participants will go through pre-recorded lectures and will be required to complete a post-module assessment (same set of MCQs and conceptual problems) to evaluate their learning. ​ Additionally, links to relevant reading materials, including review and original articles, will be provided for further study. ​ Participants will be able to post their queries, and based on common/recurring doubts, a supplementary recorded video lecture will be created to address these concerns. ​ Furthermore, an open interactive session, conducted either online or face-to-face, will be held at the end of the course to resolve any remaining doubts, and facilitate further interactive discussion. Interactive session will be optional, as it may not be possible for all, if the number of trainees surpass the numbers feasible to accommodate for interaction with the facilitator. Alternatively, efforts will be made to run synchronous discussion forum to clear doubts during the learning phase and subsequently arising from clinical practice. The course will be designed to be completed within three to six months.

Six elements of learning

The courses will emphasize six key elements of learning: (a) acquisition, (b) practice, (c) collaboration, (d) discussion, (e) investigation, and (f) production [35]. ​ These elements will guide the delivery of content by trainers and the process of knowledge acquisition and consolidation by learners. ​ Details on the modes of content delivery and learning strategies for each of these elements are illustrated in Fig. 1. All learning strategies listed in Fig. 1 may not be feasible for all participants, or for facilitators to implement, but where possible, maximum of them will be used to enhance the effectiveness of this course. Courses will also be piloted in two batches enrolled over 6 months each, before scaling it further.

Problem identification and general needs assessment

To achieve this, we will conduct a general needs assessment through a comprehensive literature review. ​ This review will focus on guidelines from professional organizations, expert reviews and commentaries, and research articles, particularly qualitative studies that explore the learning needs of physicians. Additionally, we will hold structured and open-ended discussions with key opinion leaders across the country to gather their perspectives on the curriculum. ​.

Targeted needs assessment

To make courses useful and interesting for the target audience, we will review and separate the essential and optional parts of the curriculum for doctors from different specialties and healthcare levels. ​ We will conduct two Delphi surveys, one for the learners and another for trainers.​ We will talk to people crucial for implementation like department heads to check if the course can be added to the training program for medical residents. ​ Additionally, we will seek advice from experts in blended learning to make sure the course can be expanded to reach a large number of participants.

Goals and objectives

We will use Miller’s pyramid to create a thorough learning experience [38, 39]. ​ We will start by giving basic knowledge (“knows”) and then use examples to show how to apply this knowledge in patient care (“knows how”). ​ Participants will practice applying what they learn through simulations or real-life cases (“shows how”). ​ We will then try to find ways to check for some of the participants if they are using their knowledge effectively in their actual medical practice (“does”).

Education strategies

We will use a blended learning approach, which means it will include both online and in-person sessions [40, 41]. ​ This method is designed to make curriculum flexible and accessible for widespread participation. ​ The online part of the course will follow a four step training process to help learners prepare thoroughly before attending the in-person or online interactive classes (Fig. 3).

Face to face session or interactive online session

The face-to-face sessions will include interactive activities where participants will work on real or simulated case scenarios. ​ They will collaborate in small groups to create solutions and share their ideas during class discussions. ​ Role-playing exercises will help improve communication skills between doctors and patients. ​ Participants will be able to discuss and present complex cases including the ones they have experienced in their clinical practice. We will create an online discussion forum to help participants address any questions or challenges they face in their clinical practice.

We will follow Merrill’s five principles of effective learning [42], for the teaching methods in this curriculum. They involve.

Implementation

We will have team of experts comprising of subject matter specialists, instructional designers, e-learning professionals, and technical support staff. The team will work together to create a well-rounded and effective curriculum. Since, developing of the curriculum takes time and resources, we will evaluate how motivated the trainees are for the curriculum, how much time they can dedicate to the courses, and whether the program is useful in improving the knowledge and skills of the participants.

Evaluation and feedback

We will evaluate the program’s success using Kirkpatrick’s four-level model [43]:

Level 1: We will check how satisfied learners are with the curriculum.​Level 2: Assess how well the program improved learners’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes.​Level 3: Look at whether learners are applying the knowledge and skills in their work.​Level 4: Evaluate if the knowledge and skills gained have led to better clinical practices, improved patient outcomes, and more efficient healthcare management.​

For this curriculum, only the first two levels will be used initially because it is difficult to measure the last two levels without more resources and funding [44]. ​ Feedback from participants will be collected to improve the courses. ​ The program will be shared through a network of institutions to reach more people. ​ Structured questionnaires will be created to help with the evaluation, and research will be done to see if the program is making a real difference. ​In the future, additional funding will be sought to assess the last two levels of the model.

Discussion

We have developed and described here a curriculum prototype based on the expertise of subject specialists (YG and NT), input from stakeholder colleagues involved in the care of pregnant women with diabetes, and insights from experts in educational research. ​ However, we recognize that there may be differences between an ideal curriculum and a practical one. ​ The needs of learners can vary significantly depending on factors such as their educational background (postgraduation in Medicine or Obstetrics and Gynaecology), whether they have completed their training or are in-training, their work setting (private or public sector), the level of healthcare facility they operate in, and their familiarity with the subject matter. ​ Therefore, it is essential to design a curriculum that prioritizes teaching tailored to the diverse needs of these groups. For this, we will be doing general and targeted needs assessment described ahead as part of Kern’s 6 step model for development of curriculum [36, 37].

The curriculum will be taught using a mix of online and face-to-face methods. ​ The online part is flexible, so learners can study at their own pace and save time by accessing pre-recorded videos whenever they want [45]. ​ Pre-recorded videos will help trainers in reducing the need to deliver the same lessons repetitively. ​ The online format will also make it easier to involve expert trainers. ​ At the end of the course, face-to-face sessions or online discussions will facilitate direct interaction with trainers, where participants will have an opportunity to clear their doubts. ​ This interaction can help learners to better understand the course.

The curriculum aim to help learners to develop practical skills rather than just passing tests. ​ We will be using both simulated and real-life cases to ensure participants gain the necessary expertise [46]. ​ The simulation exercises will include both common and uncommon scenarios to provide participants with a comprehensive learning experience. ​ The curriculum will also be designed keeping in mind specific needs of different learner groups. ​ The purpose of the curriculum is to create clear and measurable outcomes that will help in improving knowledge, attitudes, and practices of healthcare professionals, and clinical skills which can effectively translate into better patient care.

The experts creating this curriculum have significant experience in clinical practice and research in GDM. ​ AIIMS, New Delhi and CCDC (institutions involved) have a strong track record in improving medical education in India. ​ AIIMS also has a wide network of medical colleges, which can help in implementing this program successfully on a larger scale.

Summary

We will create a specialized training program to manage GDM., by reviewing existing evidence and gathering feedback from experts and stakeholders, and testing it in small groups to make improvements. ​ The program will use a mix of online and in-person learning to make it accessible to more people. ​ Experts will help in designing the program that can be used widely and effectively.
