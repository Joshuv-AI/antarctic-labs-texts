A disulfidptosis‐related gene signature predicts prognosis and immune‐metabolic landscape in multiple myeloma
Authors: Li Wang, Jinjin Wang, Yutong Wang, Ting Niu, Ailin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDisulfidptosis is a newly recognized cell death induced by disulphide stress under glucose deprivation. However, its clinical implications in multiple myeloma (MM) remain largely unexplored. We identified disulfidptosis‐related genes via co‐expression analysis and developed a risk signature through univariate Cox, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The model was internally and externally validated for overall survival prediction. Downstream analyses included functional enrichment, tumour mutation burden (TMB), tumour microenvironment (TME) characterization and immune‐metabolic profiling. Preliminary in vitro validation was conducted with PLEC‐knockout KMS‐11 cells under glucose deprivation. A nine‐gene signature was established and validated, showing robust prognostic performance across cohorts. High‐risk patients displayed proliferative profiles, higher TMB, an immunosuppressive TME with neutrophils, eosinophils and exhausted T cells and activation of glycolysis and cystine/glutathione metabolism. This signature defines an aggressive MM subtype likely unresponsive to immune checkpoint therapy. Functional assays confirmed that PLEC mediates actin disulphide cross‐linking during metabolic stress. We present a novel disulfidptosis‐related gene signature with independent prognostic value in MM, offering insights into the molecular and immune landscape of MM and serving as a useful tool for risk stratification and therapeutic suggestion.Keywords: disulfidptosis, multiple myeloma, prognostic biomarkers, tumour microenvironment (TME), tumour mutation burden (TMB)

INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma (MM) is the second most common haematological malignancy worldwide, characterized by clonal proliferation of plasma cells in the bone marrow. 1 Its global incidence is about 2.1 per 100 000 with mortality of 1.39 per 100 000. 2 Although therapies such as proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, monoclonal antibodies and Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR‐T) cells have advanced, MM remains incurable due to heterogeneity, relapse and drug resistance. 3 , 4 Prognostic systems like Durie–Salmon, the International Staging System (ISS), and the Revised International Staging System (R‐ISS) improve classification but provide limited guidance for individualized therapy. 5 , 6 , 7 Thus, precise prognostic models and robust biomarkers are needed to improve risk stratification and treatment.

Recently, attention has turned to disulfidptosis, a novel form of regulated cell death characterized by the abnormal accumulation of disulphide bonds, which leads to protein misfolding, cytoskeletal disruption and ultimately cell death. 8 This process is often triggered by glucose deprivation. 9 Normally, cells maintain redox balance, but tumour cells enhance antioxidant defences, mainly via the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11. 10 SLC7A11 promotes cystine uptake for glutathione synthesis, neutralizing reactive oxygen species, 11 and its overexpression can cause cystine overload, inducing disulfidptosis. 12 This mechanism is currently being explored as a potential therapeutic strategy, especially for refractory or drug‐resistant cancers. Previous studies have identified disulfidptosis‐related genes as promising prognostic markers and therapeutic targets. 11 , 13 , 14 Related bioinformatics and statistical strategies, including co‐expression analysis and LASSO–Cox regression, have been used to construct prognostic signatures in other malignancies, providing methodological context for this study. 15 , 16 , 17

The Warburg effect in MM may cause bone marrow glucose deprivation, predisposing tumour cells to disulfidptosis, particularly in those with high SLC7A11 expression. 18 In this study, we identified disulfidptosis‐related genes and explored their roles in MM. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The MMRF_COMMPASS dataset was obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), comprising RNA‐seq data, clinical information and mutation data for 764 MM patients. Normal samples were sourced from two Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE94339, GSE120444), and an additional GEO dataset (GSE24080) was used as an external validation dataset. Gene symbols were annotated using platform‐specific files. To ensure comparability with the microarray‐based GSE24080, the R package TDM (v0.3) was applied to convert RNA‐seq counts to microarray‐like data. 19

Identification of disulfidptosis‐related genes differing between normal and tumour samples

A total of 11 disulfidptosis genes were identified through a review of previous literature: ACTB, ACTN4, CAPZB, CD2AP, DSTN, FLNA, IQGAP1, MYH9, MYL6, SLC7A11, TLN1. 9 , 20 Genes with SD >0.5 were screened for differential expression between normal and tumour samples (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.01). Genes correlated with disulfidptosis genes (Pearson |r| > 0.7, p < 0.001) were defined as disulfidptosis‐related genes.

Construction of a prognostic risk model based on disulfidptosis‐related genes

Patients were randomly divided into training and internal validation sets (6:4) using caret package. In the training set, univariate Cox regression identified prognosis‐related genes (p < 0.05), followed by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (glmnt package) and multivariate Cox regression to construct the risk model.

Validation and evaluation of the prognostic risk model

Risk scores were computed (survival package), and patients were classified as high‐risk or low‐risk groups by the median cut‐off. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves, log‐rank tests and time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (timeROC package) evaluated prognostic performance for 1‐, 3‐ and 4‐year overall survival (OS) in training, internal, combined and external cohorts. Subgroup analyses further tested the model across age, gender and ISS stage.

Construction of nomograms

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression identified independent prognostic factors (age, gender, ISS, risk score). A nomogram was developed to predict 1‐, 3‐ and 4‐year OS, with ROC and calibration curves assessing performance.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

Genes differentially expressed between risk groups were identified (limma, adj. p < 0.05, |log2FC| > 2) and visualized with a volcano plot. Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (clusterProfiler package) explored functional pathways.

Tumour mutation burden analysis

Simple nucleotide variation (SNV) data from MMRF_COMMPASS were used to calculate tumour mutation burden (TMB, mutations/Mb). 21 Mutation types included missense, nonsense, frameshift, in‐frame, splice site, start site and multi‐hit. TMB differences and prognostic relevance were analysed between risk groups using KM curves and log‐rank tests.

Tumour microenvironment and immune infiltration analysis

Tumour microenvironment (TME) analysis was performed with the ESTIMATE package to calculate stromal and immune cell scores. 22 The CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to estimate the relative proportions of 22 cell types with 1000 permutations. 23 Immune function was evaluated by single‐sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) using 29 gene signatures (Table S1). 24 ssGSEA quantified six curated metabolism‐related gene sets (tricarboxylic acid [TCA] cycle, glycolysis, oxidative stress, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate [NADPH], glutathione, cystine/cysteine; Table S2). The immune balance index was defined as activation minus inhibition score, with details in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R 4.3.3. Continuous variables were compared with the Wilcoxon test and categorical variables with χ 2 or Fisher's exact test. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by limma (|log2FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05). Survival was analysed with KM curves, log‐rank tests and Cox models. Prognostic accuracy was assessed by time‐dependent ROC and C‐index, and correlations by Pearson's coefficient. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cell culture

KMS‐11 cells (Procell, CL‐0794) were maintained in RPMI‐1640 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO2. For glucose deprivation, cells were cultured in glucose‐free RPMI‐1640 with dialysed FBS (Procell, PM150122) as previously described. 9 , 25 Cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed mycoplasma‐free.

CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated PLEC knockout

PLEC knockout was generated using lentiCRISPRv2 (Addgene #52961) carrying single guide RNA (sgRNA) (forward 5′‐CACCGGCCACGCAGACGTACCCTCA‐3′ and reverse 5′‐AAACTGAGGGTACGTCTGCGTGGCC‐3′). Lentiviruses were generated in HEK293T with psPAX2 and pMD2.G and used to transduce KMS‐11 cells. Stable clones were selected with puromycin (2.5 μg/mL for 3 days) and validated by western blotting.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described, 26 , 27 with modifications in protein preparation. Samples were mixed with non‐reducing loading buffer (Beyotime, P0016P); half was analysed directly, and half was reduced with 2% β‐mercaptoethanol. After heating at 70°C for 10 min, proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE. Primary antibodies: PLEC (1:2000, Proteintech, 29170‐1‐AP) and actin (1:3000, Abways, AB0035).

RESULTS

Identification of disulfidptosis‐related genes

We identified 11 disulfidptosis genes from the literature, including ACTB, ACTN4, CAPZB, CD2AP, DSTN, FLNA, IQGAP1, MYH9, MYL6, SLC7A11 and TLN1. 9 , 20 Co‐expression analysis revealed 3746 genes significantly associated with disulfidptosis (|R| > 0.7, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). Univariate Cox regression identified 336 survival‐related genes (Table S4), which were further narrowed by LASSO to a nine‐gene signature: BRIX1, DDHD2, LPCAT1, NSUN3, PIKFYVE, PLEC, PPARA, SIPA1L3 and USP37 (Figure 2B–D).

(C) LASSO coefficient profiles as a function of log(λ). (D) Correlation heat map between the nine signature genes and disulfidptosis genes. (E) Forest plot of multivariate Cox regression analysis for the nine retained genes. (F) Heat map of expression profiles of the nine signature genes in high‐ and low‐risk groups across training and internal validation sets.

Construction of the disulfidptosis‐related gene risk signature

A multivariate Cox analysis of the nine selected genes established a disulfidptosis‐related risk signature (Figure 2E), with risk scores calculated using the following formula: Risk Score=∑Gene Expression×CoxCoefficient. Patients were stratified by the median score into high‐ and low‐risk groups, showing distinct gene expression patterns (Figure 2F). KM survival curves confirmed significantly worse OS in the high‐risk group across training, validation and combined cohorts (Figure 3A–C). The area under the curve (AUC) of ROC curves for 1‐year, 3‐year and 4‐year survival rates, based on all patients, were 0.755, 0.752 and 0.714. This disulfidptosis‐related gene signature demonstrates strong predictive performance and efficiency in forecasting OS (Figure 3D). ROC curves for training and internal validation datasets were provided in Figure S1. External validation in GSE24080 further confirmed poorer OS (p < 0.001) and event‐free survival (EFS) (p = 0.004) in the high‐risk group (Figure 3E,F), supporting the robustness of the model.

Prognostic performance and validation of disulfidptosis‐related gene risk signature. (A, C) Kaplan–Meier curves of overall survival (OS) in the training, internal validation and overall cohorts. (D) Time‐dependent ROC curves for 1‐year, 3‐year and 4‐year OS prediction in the full dataset. (E, F) Kaplan–Meier curves of OS and event‐free survival in the external validation cohort.

Independent prognostic factors and nomogram for overall survival in multiple myeloma

Cox regression confirmed the risk group (HR = 3.34, 95% CI: 2.29–4.88) as an independent prognostic factor for OS (Figure 4A,B). ROC analysis showed that the risk score had the highest AUC at 0.754 (Figure 4C). A nomogram combining age, sex, ISS and risk score predicted 1‐, 3‐ and 4‐year OS with AUCs of 0.753, 0.758 and 0.750 (Figure 4D). ROC curves and calibration curves (Figure 4E,F) showed that the nomogram‐predicted OS closely matched the observed outcomes. Subgroup analyses by age, sex and ISS consistently showed worse prognosis in the high‐risk group (Figure S2).

Differential gene expression and function enrichment analysis of risk groups

We identified 1211 DEGs between risk groups (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1), including 295 upregulated and 24 downregulated genes (Figure 5A). GO and KEGG analyses showed enrichment in mitotic processes, such as chromosome segregation and organelle fission, as well as key signalling pathways related to cell cycle progression, neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and transcriptional dysregulation in cancer (Figure 5B,C). GSEA further indicated that high‐risk patients were enriched in mitotic cell cycle and secretory granule pathways, while low‐risk patients showed B‐cell‐mediated immune signalling (Figure 5D), highlighting distinct differences in proliferation and immune activity.

Correlation between TMB and disulfidptosis‐related gene signature

Somatic mutation profiling showed frequent mutations in IGHV2‐70, IGLV3‐1, KRAS, IGHV2‐70D and NRAS in both groups (Figure 5E,F), with overall mutation prevalence >85%. High‐risk patients had significantly higher TMB (Figure 5G, p < 0.001), which was associated with worse OS (Figure 5H, p < 0.001). Combining TMB with the risk model stratified patients into four groups—H‐TMB + high risk, H‐TMB + low risk, L‐TMB + high risk and L‐TMB + low risk. This stratification revealed distinctly different prognoses across groups. Notably, patients in the L‐TMB + low‐risk group exhibited the most favourable survival, while those in the H‐TMB + high‐risk group had the worst outcomes (Figure 5I, p < 0.001).

Association between disulfidptosis‐related gene signature and TME

TME analysis showed no overall differences in stromal or immune scores (Figure 6A), but detailed profiling revealed significant changes. The high‐risk group exhibited a significantly elevated abundance of eosinophils, neutrophils, T‐cell CD4 memory resting, macrophages M0 and less abundance of T‐cell CD4 naïve (Figure 6B). Immune activation pathways (e.g. CD8+ T cells, cytolytic activity, pDCs, type II IFN response) were elevated (Figure 6C), and the immune balance index was higher (Figure 66D, p < 0.001). Immune function scoring based on ssGSEA showed increased inhibitory receptors (PDCD1, CTLA4) and cytokines (IL10, IL6, IL27) in the high‐risk group, consistent with elevated T‐cell exhaustion (Figure 6E,F). Metabolically, high‐risk patients exhibited higher glycolysis, TCA cycle, cystine/cysteine and glutathione activity (Figure 6G). A radar plot summarized these immune–metabolic differences (Figure 6H).

Preliminary in vitro validation of PLEC in disulfidptosis

We verified disulphide bond formation in actin cytoskeletal proteins under glucose starvation by non‐reducing western blot analysis. After 4 h deprivation, actin showed slower migration with smearing, which disappeared under reducing conditions, confirming intermolecular disulphide bonds (Figure 7A). To assess the role of PLEC, we constructed a stable PLEC knockout KMS‐11 cell line (Figure 7B). In PLEC‐deficient cells, the actin mobility shift was markedly reduced under glucose deprivation (Figure 7C), indicating that PLEC contributes to disulphide bond‐mediated actin modifications during metabolic stress.

PLEC contributes to actin disulphide cross‐linking under glucose deprivation. (A) Non‐reducing and reducing western blotting analysis of indicated actin cytoskeleton proteins in KMS‐11 cells cultured in glucose‐free medium for 0 and 4 h. (B) Western blotting showing PLEC protein levels in wild‐type (WT) control and PLEC knockout (KO) KMS‐11 cells. (C) Non‐reducing and reducing western blotting analysis of indicated actin cytoskeleton proteins in WT, empty vector (EV) and PLEC‐KO KMS‐11 cells cultured in glucose‐containing or glucose‐free medium for 4 h.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed and validated a nine‐gene disulfidptosis‐related signature in MM that stratified patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups with distinct survival outcomes. The model showed strong predictive performance, and analyses of enrichment, mutation burden, immune infiltration and metabolism revealed divergent patterns, underscoring both prognostic and biological heterogeneity in MM.

While disulfidptosis has recently emerged as a novel form of regulated cell death, its clinical relevance in haematological malignancies remains largely unexplored. Only one study investigated a disulfidptosis‐based signature in MM using different datasets and analytical methods compared to this study. 28 Here, we show that a nine‐gene disulfidptosis signature has strong prognostic value, serving as an independent predictor superior to age, gender and ISS stage 6 , 29 , 30 , 31 and further improving accuracy when integrated with clinical factors (AUCs >0.75 for 1‐, 3‐ and 4‐year survival). High‐risk patients also exhibited significantly higher TMB, and the combination of high TMB with high‐risk scores predicted the poorest survival, indicating a cumulative adverse effect. 32 Moreover, patients with concurrently high TMB and high disulfidptosis risk scores showed significantly worse survival, indicating a cumulative negative impact on clinical outcome. These findings indicate that the disulfidptosis‐related gene signature provides independent and clinically meaningful prognostic information and may help refine risk stratification in MM.

Functional enrichment and TMB analyses revealed that high‐risk patients display a transcriptional profile suggestive of increased proliferation and genomic instability. GO, KEGG and GSEA confirmed activation of mitosis‐related pathways, while the high‐risk group was also enriched in NET formation, a process associated with an increased risk for metastasis and cancer relapse. 33 One study revealed the unfavourable impact of NETs on prognosis in patients with MM, consistent with those findings in this study. 34 In addition, high‐risk patients carried more mutations per sample, indicating increased genomic instability and worse outcomes. 35 , 36 Together, these findings define the high‐risk subtype by aggressive features including hyperproliferation, inflammatory signalling and transcriptional dysregulation.

Analysis of the immune microenvironment and metabolism indicated a complex immune‐metabolic state that may drive aggressiveness and resistance. Although global TME components showed no major differences, detailed profiling revealed significant enrichment of eosinophils and neutrophils in high‐risk patients. Previous studies have shown that eosinophils act synergistically to suppress anti‐tumour immune responses and accelerate disease progression. 37 , 38 , 39 Furthermore, neutrophils, particularly the subset with elevated CD64 expression, exhibited pronounced immunosuppressive activity, which strongly correlated with poorer clinical outcomes among MM patients. 40 , 41 , 42

Exhaustion marker profiling (Figure 6E) indicated that the high‐risk group exhibited upregulation of PDCD1 and CTLA4, accompanied by increased expression of effector molecules such as GZMB and PRF1, while the progenitor marker TCF7 remained detectable in both groups without significant difference. In contrast, terminal exhaustion markers such as LAG3, HAVCR2, TIGIT and TOX were not consistently increased. Taken together, these features align more closely with a progenitor‐like/intermediate exhausted phenotype (Texh‐int) rather than a terminally exhausted state, consistent with the recognized hierarchical differentiation of exhausted T cells. This pattern, characterized by increased PDCD1/CTLA4 together with preserved effector activity and TCF7 expression, is most consistent with the features of intermediated exhausted T cells (Texh‐int). 43 , 44 Prior studies indicate that Texh‐int cells are prone to terminal differentiation in the TME, with reduced proliferative capacity and limited responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. 45 , 46 These findings suggest that high‐risk patients are unlikely to benefit from ICB treatment.

Nutrient availability has been recognized as a set of signals that regulate T‐cell function and differentiation. 43 In this study, high‐risk samples showed elevated glycolysis and cystine/cysteine metabolism, both of which are typically associated with disulfidptosis. 9 Additionally, this was accompanied by a parallel upregulation of glutathione metabolism and NADPH generation, which are key components of the cellular antioxidant defence system. These findings suggest that the high‐risk group is defined by an immunosuppressive TME with eosinophil and neutrophil enrichment, Texh‐int T‐cell dysfunction and a redox‐adaptive metabolic profile, which may underlie aggressive behaviour and limited response to immunotherapy.

Our in vitro experiments support the computational findings. Under glucose deprivation, actin disulphide cross‐linking was observed in KMS‐11 cells but was reduced in PLEC‐deficient cells (Figure 7). These results provide functional evidence for the involvement of PLEC in disulfidptosis, although validation remains limited to a single gene and cell line.

Despite the strengths of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analyses relied on retrospective public datasets, which may carry biases in sample selection, preprocessing and annotation. Although external validation was performed using the GSE24080 dataset, this dataset was generated on a microarray platform and required TDM transformation for cross‐platform comparability with RNA‐seq data. We confirmed improved distributional consistency after TDM (Figure S3), but the possibility of residual artefacts cannot be excluded. Second, the model was based solely on transcriptomic data without integration of multi‐omics or single‐cell information. Third, functional validation was limited to PLEC, and broader experimental confirmation of disulfidptosis‐related genes in MM is needed. Fourth, while our model exhibited strong prognostic performance, we did not compare it with other published signatures such as ferroptosis‐, immune‐ or autophagy‐related models, which should be addressed in future studies to clarify its unique prognostic value.

In summary, we established and validated a robust disulfidptosis‐related gene signature that provides independent prognostic information in MM. It defines a biologically aggressive and immunosuppressive disulfidptosis‐related molecular phenotype, which suggests limited benefit from ICB treatment. This signature may be a valuable tool for risk stratification and therapeutic suggestion in MM.
