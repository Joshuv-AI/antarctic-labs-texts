Mobitz Type I Progressing to High-Grade AV Block

History of Presentation

A 78-year-old woman with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with chronic hypoxic respiratory failure requiring 3 L/min of home oxygen, probable left bundle branch block (LBBB)-associated cardiomyopathy, hypertension, and anxiety presented to the emergency department with 3 days of progressively worsening generalized weakness. She reported associated lightheadedness with ambulation and subjective gait unsteadiness, but denied chest pain, palpitations, and worsening dyspnea.

Physical examination on initial presentation revealed a well-appearing woman in no acute distress. Vital signs demonstrated a heart rate of 56 beats/min, blood pressure of 148/56 mm Hg, respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 95% on 3 L/min nasal cannula. Cardiovascular examination revealed a regular rhythm without murmurs, gallops, or jugular venous distention. Pulmonary examination was notable for mild expiratory wheezing. Neurological examination was normal.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was significant for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease requiring continuous 3 L/min supplemental oxygen, hypertension, and a probable LBBB-associated cardiomyopathy. She had a known baseline LBBB on prior electrocardiograms. Prior transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45%.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's advanced age, underlying conduction disease, and presenting symptoms of progressive weakness and lightheadedness, the differential diagnoses included symptomatic bradycardia, progression of atrioventricular (AV) conduction disease, and volume depletion.

Investigations

Initial laboratory studies demonstrated mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin values without a dynamic rise or fall: 57, 54, and 56 ng/L at presentation, 1 hour, and 6 hours, respectively. B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 3,000 pg/mL, consistent with history of underlying cardiomyopathy. Serum electrolytes revealed mild hyperkalemia (potassium 5.5 mmol/L) with normal magnesium levels (2.1 mg/dL). Renal function was impaired, with a creatinine level of 1.78 mg/dL, compared with a baseline value of 1.2 mg/dL. Thyroid function tests were within normal limits.

Electrocardiogram (ECG) on presentation demonstrated sinus rhythm with LBBB, PR interval prolongation, and intermittent nonconducted P waves with progressive PR lengthening, consistent with Mobitz type I second-degree AV block (Figure 1). ECG on hospital day 2 revealed progression to high-grade 2:1 AV block with a ventricular rate of 32 beats/min, maintaining the LBBB morphology (Figure 2).

Management

On hospital day 2, telemetry monitoring demonstrated sustained bradycardia with ventricular rates in the 30s. The patient demonstrated decreased alertness but remained arousable to stimuli. Repeat ECG confirmed progression to high-grade AV block. Intravenous atropine was administered without improvement in heart rate, raising concern for advanced conduction disease. The patient's telemetry showed a period of regular narrow complex escape rhythm at 39 beats/min (Figure 3).

Given the presence of symptomatic high-grade AV block, cardiology was consulted, and emergent temporary transvenous pacing was recommended.

A temporary pacing wire was placed via the right internal jugular vein. The patient was subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit for continuous monitoring overnight. The temporary pacemaker was set to a rate of 60 beats/min with adequate capture and sensing.

Following stabilization, the patient underwent permanent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation on hospital day 4. The procedure was performed without complications. Postprocedure device interrogation demonstrated appropriate sensing and capture thresholds.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient tolerated permanent pacemaker implantation without complication and experienced resolution of her presenting symptoms of generalized weakness and lightheadedness. Her postprocedural course was uneventful, with no evidence of pneumothorax, lead dislodgement, device malfunction, pericardial effusion, or cardiac tamponade. She was discharged home in stable condition following device implantation with appropriate outpatient cardiology follow-up confirmed.

Discussion

Mobitz type I second-degree AV block has traditionally been regarded as a benign rhythm, particularly when localized to the AV node and occurring in asymptomatic patients. However, distinguishing AV nodal from infranodal Wenckebach is critical, as the latter is associated with an increased risk of progression to high-grade or complete AV block.1 Localization of conduction disease is therefore central to triage and management decisions.

In this present case, the progression from LBBB to Mobitz type 1 AV block to 2:1 AV block with evidence of a narrow complex escape rhythm shows the advanced conduction disease in this patient. This case also demonstrates the anatomic ECG localization of the conduction system disease. The presence of a narrow complex escape rhythm suggests preserved His-Purkinje system activation and raises the possibility of either a proximal Hisian escape rhythm with rate-dependent recovery of left bundle conduction or conduction through longitudinally dissociated His fibers. This electrographic pattern may provide a noninvasive clue to localize conduction disease and identify patterns at risk for rapid deterioration.

Two possible mechanisms may explain the coexistence of baseline LBBB and a narrow complex escape rhythm. First, the LBBB morphology does not necessarily imply disease within the distal left bundle branch itself; rather, the abnormality may reside proximally within longitudinally dissociated His bundle fibers committed to the left bundle. Narula2 demonstrated that bundle branch block patterns may arise from asynchronous conduction within the His bundle itself rather than from distal bundle branch disease. In this setting, a focal lesion or region of altered refractoriness within the His bundle could produce LBBB during conducted beats, whereas a Hisian escape arising distal to the zone of functional delay could activate both bundle branches synchronously, resulting in a narrow QRS escape rhythm.

Second, the escape rhythm may represent a junctional or proximal His escape associated with rate-dependent, phase 3, LBBB that recovers at slower cycle lengths. In this mechanism, impaired conduction within diseased left-sided His-Purkinje fibers manifests as LBBB during faster supraventricular rates, whereas slower escape cycle lengths permit recovery of left bundle conduction and normalization of ventricular activation. Either of these mechanisms support an infranodal contribution to the conduction disturbance.

This patient also had an unclear response to atropine. Atropine may paradoxically worsen infranodal block by increasing atrial rate without improving His-Purkinje conduction, resulting in periods of nonconducted atrial activity and emergence of a junctional escape rhythm.3 If the disease was infranodal, given atropine's antivagal effect, an increase in heart rate would be expected.

In addition, although 2:1 AV block represents an indeterminate ECG pattern in which differentiation between type I and type II blocks is not definitive without electrophysiologic testing, clinical practice dictates managing such cases as high risk because of the possibility of progression to complete heart block. In this context, early pacing was warranted and ultimately definitive.

Reversible etiologies of AV block were excluded in this patient including ischemic disease, drug-related AV block, and infection.4 The patient's cardiomyopathy had been evaluated with coronary angiography, which demonstrated nonobstructive coronary artery disease, excluding ischemic heart disease as an underlying etiology. The patient was on carvedilol, with the last dose presumed 24 hours before the onset of the high-degree AV block. Given the patient's stage 1 acute kidney injury, carvedilol washout was extended to 72 hours before permanent pacemaker insertion. Carvedilol undergoes hepatic metabolism with a half-life of 6 to 7 hours; however, acute kidney injury impairs the renal clearance of active metabolites. The 72-hour interval between the last dose and procedure provided adequate time for drug clearance despite reduced renal function. Electrolyte abnormalities were ruled out with initial laboratory investigations.

Current 2018 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society guidelines provide clear recommendations applicable to this clinical scenario.5 Symptomatic high-degree second-degree or third-degree AV block constitutes a Class I indication for permanent pacemaker implantation. In addition, guidelines recognize that asymptomatic Mobitz type II or high-degree AV block, particularly when accompanied by LBBB, suggests advanced conduction disease and warrants permanent pacing because of the risk of unpredictable progression.5

Age-related degenerative changes of the cardiac conduction system, characterized histologically by fibrosis and collagen deposition, represent the most common etiology of progressive AV conduction disease in older patients.6 These pathologic processes preferentially involve the His-Purkinje system and provide a plausible explanation for the rapid progression of AV block observed in this patient.

Crucially, older patients with clinically significant conduction disease may present with nonspecific symptoms like weakness and lightheadedness, rather than classic cardiac complaints. Fatigue, generalized weakness, gait instability, or cognitive changes may be the only manifestations of advanced bradyarrhythmia. Failure to recognize these can delay diagnosis and definitive intervention. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for conduction abnormalities in older patients presenting with otherwise nonspecific symptoms like unexplained weakness.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that Mobitz type I AV block in the presence of LBBB may reflect advanced conduction disease rather than benign AV nodal Wenckebach. Such patients are at increased risk for rapid progression to high-grade or complete AV block, requiring prompt evaluation and close monitoring. Early cardiology involvement, serial ECG assessments, and early pacing should be considered. In older patients, nonspecific symptoms such as generalized weakness should prompt consideration of underlying conduction abnormalities, even in the absence of classic cardiac complaints.
