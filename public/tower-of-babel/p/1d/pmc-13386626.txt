Construction of a sports bio mechanical injury prediction and AI warning system based on wearable sensors
Authors: Yijuan Feng, Zishuang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-time warning and prevention of sports injuries are core challenges in the fields of sports medicine and health management. Traditional methods rely on single sensor data and static threshold rules, which have problems such as single monitoring dimension, high false alarm rate, and insufficient real-time performance, making it difficult to meet the precise prevention and control needs in complex motion scenes.MethodsIn response to the above issues, this study proposes a sports biomechanical injury prediction and real-time warning system based on wearable sensors. Through multimodal data fusion, lightweight model design, and a dynamic adaptive mechanism, the system enables accurate identification and real-time intervention for high-risk movements. The system integrates nine-axis inertial sensors, flexible strain sensors, and plantar pressure sensing arrays to synchronously collect multidimensional biomechanical parameters (e.g., joint kinematics, local tissue deformation, and ground reaction forces). By combining an improved hybrid neural network architecture, extracting spatiotemporal features, and dynamically weighting key action segments, the system significantly enhances the sensitivity and specificity of injury prediction. Through the edge computing optimization strategy, the model is quantized and compressed to 8-bit integer, and the end-to-end delay is controlled at the millisecond level. Power consumption is reduced sufficiently to support embedded deployment.ResultsExperimental validation showed that the dynamic threshold adjustment mechanism reduced both the false alarm rate and the missed alarm rate on the same evaluation set. In basketball stop-jump tasks, the system identified elevated knee valgus risk before the left-knee peak angle reached 16.2° at 400 ms. In long-distance running surveillance, progressive gait asymmetry was detected by Week 3 in the high-risk group, and peak load decreased by 18% after intervention. Together, these findings support the value of synchronized multimodal sensing, serial deep learning, and adaptive warning logic for proactive sports injury prevention.ConclusionsFuture work will focus on multi center data sharing, wearable exoskeleton linkage intervention, and augmented reality visualization feedback under the federated learning framework, further expanding the application scenarios and clinical value of the system.Keywords: Wearable sensors, Sports biomechanical injuries, Multimodal data fusion, Real-time warning system, Edge computing, Dynamic threshold adjustment

Introduction

Sports injuries remain a major challenge in sports medicine and public health because lower-limb overload, ligament strain, and repetitive impact often develop before clear clinical symptoms are reported [1, 2]. For both elite athletes and recreational exercisers, delayed identification of abnormal biomechanics may lead to chronic overuse, acute ligament damage, or stress fracture progression [3]. Therefore, wearable biomechanical monitoring has become an important route for moving injury management from retrospective diagnosis toward proactive risk screening and early intervention [4–6].

Despite rapid progress in wearable sensing, current injury-warning systems still face three practical bottlenecks. First, single-modality monitoring cannot fully capture the coupled evolution of joint kinematics, soft-tissue deformation, and plantar loading [7, 8]. Second, fixed thresholds are often insensitive to inter-individual anatomical differences and to changes in movement intensity, making false alarms and missed alarms more likely [9]. Third, some algorithms are too computationally expensive for embedded deployment and therefore cannot provide stable low-latency feedback during high-speed sport actions [10, 11].

To address these limitations, this study designs a wearable sensor-based sports biomechanical injury warning framework that combines synchronized multimodal sensing, serial spatiotemporal modeling, and adaptive thresholding on an edge device. Recent ligament-oriented studies have further highlighted the value of data-driven prediction under repeated mechanical loading and the integration of biomechanical modeling with deep learning, which strengthens the relevance of the present work to overload-related injury prevention. The core deployed system in this manuscript is based on the tri-modal combination of IMU, flexible strain, and plantar pressure sensing, while auxiliary sEMG and heart-rate channels are treated as exploratory comparison modalities rather than as part of the final deployed model.

Literature review

Wearable sensing and sports injury prediction have advanced rapidly, yet important gaps remain between laboratory proof-of-concept studies and field-deployable early warning systems.

In wearable sensing, flexible electronics, microelectromechanical systems [10–12], and pressure-distribution measurement have improved the precision of motion monitoring and load estimation [13, 14]. Nevertheless, practical challenges remain in sensor hysteresis, signal drift, multi-sensor timestamp alignment, and motion-artifact suppression during fast sport actions [15]. These problems are particularly important when a system aims to integrate joint kinematics, local strain [16], and plantar loading into one synchronized biomechanical representation rather than relying on only one sensing modality [17–19].

In injury prediction, machine-learning and deep-learning methods have improved the identification of high-risk movements [20], but several limitations remain relevant to the present manuscript [21, 22]. Conventional random forests and support vector machines depend strongly on handcrafted features [23]. Pure recurrent models capture temporal dependence but may miss local spatial structure. More recent attention-based approaches improve the recognition of high-risk segments, while recent ligament fatigue and ligament loading studies further demonstrate the importance of combining data-driven learning with biomechanical mechanisms [7, 24, 25]. However, the literature still lacks a clearly described wearable framework that simultaneously emphasizes sensor synchronization [26–28], interpretable biomechanical feature construction, edge deployment, and adaptive threshold updating for real-time sports injury warning [29, 30] (Fig. 1).

Evolution of sports injury prediction model

System-level optimization is another unresolved issue. Many existing studies report promising prediction accuracy but rely on cloud computation, simplified laboratory tasks, or incomplete descriptions of deployment latency, energy consumption, and device ergonomics. For a wearable warning system to be credible in practice, it must describe not only the model architecture but also the sensor layout, synchronization strategy, input-output logic, and the engineering pathway from raw signals to warning decisions [31]. Accordingly, this study emphasizes three concrete contributions. First, it defines a deployment-oriented tri-modal sensing scheme composed of IMU, flexible strain, and plantar pressure sensing, while explicitly separating exploratory auxiliary modalities from the final deployed architecture. System architecture and key technologies

Wearable sensor hardware design

The hardware subsystem was designed to balance measurement fidelity, wearability, and embedded implementation. The emphasis in this section is on the architecture itself rather than on experimental outcome values. The system includes a main control unit, limb-mounted IMUs, flexible strain sensors positioned near high-risk joints, and plantar pressure insoles for load-distribution estimation during sport actions [32, 33]. (Fig. 3)

Hardware design of wearable sensors: nine axis IMU, flexible strain sensor

The main controller is a Nordic nRF52840 low-power MCU with BLE 5.0 communication. The nine-axis IMU is connected through the I2C bus, the flexible strain sensor is read through the on-chip ADC, and the plantar pressure array is sampled through the SPI interface. A hardware-triggered synchronization pulse generated by the MCU is used to align data streams, while timestamp logging is used for later verification of synchronization accuracy and for end-to-end latency decomposition during testing.

The wearable layout follows a modular design. IMUs are mounted on the lateral sides of both thighs and shanks to capture segment motion; flexible strain sensors are positioned on the medial knee and lateral ankle regions to capture local deformation related to joint motion; and plantar pressure arrays are embedded in customized insoles to estimate the spatial distribution of ground contact loads. The deployed warning model uses these three sensing modalities as its final input set.

To characterize measurement error, the raw IMU output is modeled as the superposition of the true motion signal, sensor bias, and noise. The flexible strain sensor is calibrated through a resistance-angle mapping procedure, and dynamic sampling-rate adjustment is controlled according to motion intensity so that low-intensity periods use lower sampling frequencies and high-intensity periods use higher frequencies. Sensor denoising is then implemented with an adaptive Kalman filter whose variables are defined explicitly in the surrounding text.

Multimodal data fusion and preprocessing

Multimodal fusion is used to transform heterogeneous raw signals into synchronized biomechanical descriptors that can be interpreted consistently by the prediction model. This section focuses on four steps: synchronization, noise suppression, feature extraction, and tensor construction for model input.

On the embedded platform, each sensor stream is buffered independently and then fused after timestamp alignment. The acquisition, fusion, and transmission tasks are scheduled in separate FreeRTOS threads so that synchronization and preprocessing can be executed before inference. Development of the AI injury prediction model

The injury prediction model is designed as a serial spatiotemporal architecture so that the input-output relationship among modules is explicit. After synchronized multimodal windows are converted into feature tensors, local spatial patterns are first encoded by CNN layers, temporal dependencies are then modeled by the LSTM layer, and an attention module subsequently reweights the hidden-state sequence to emphasize high-risk time points before final classification.

The CNN stage extracts local cross-channel correlations among aligned biomechanical features. The LSTM stage models how these features evolve across consecutive time steps, which is important for distinguishing transient noise from persistent risk-related patterns. The attention stage assigns larger weights to temporally localized events such as abrupt frontal-plane knee loading or rapid plantar-force redistribution. Implementation of the real-time warning system

The real-time warning subsystem links embedded inference to alert generation. Its purpose is not only to run the model on-device but also to ensure that signal acquisition, model inference, and warning feedback follow a stable engineering logic that can be audited and reproduced (Fig. 5).

Algorithm flow of intelligent warning system

The deployed pipeline uses TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers for model conversion and on-device inference. Quantization is applied after offline model training so that the embedded implementation can reduce memory requirements while maintaining acceptable predictive performance. Implementation details are presented here, whereas measured latency and power outcomes are discussed in the experiment section.

Task scheduling is organized around three main functions: data acquisition, inference, and warning feedback. Semaphores are used to coordinate the processing order and to avoid uncontrolled competition for embedded resources. The warning threshold is not fixed; instead, it is updated according to historical observations so that alert sensitivity can adapt to baseline user differences and to the motion context.

Experiments and results

Experimental design

To verify the system’s effectiveness, the experimental design focuses on multimodal sensor data acquisition and dynamic biomechanical analysis. The experiment recruited 40 participants (10 per sport: basketball, long-distance running, football, and badminton) to ensure the diversity and representativeness of the sample. All 40 participants were included in the final analysis, with complete data collected for each subject across all experimental scenarios. The eight subjects (S01–S08) presented in subsequent case analyses are representative examples of each sport category, and the full dataset of 40 participants was used for model training and performance validation. The sensor hardware configuration focuses on high precision and low power consumption, integrating a nine axis IMU, flexible strain sensor, and foot pressure sensing array, covering multi-dimensional monitoring of joint kinematics, muscle activity, and external loads. The experimental scenario design focuses on high-risk actions, and constructs a benchmark data set for damage prediction through standardized action repetition and real-time data collection (Fig. 7).

Schematic diagram of wearing method

The dataset comprises 4,800 valid samples (1,200 per sport category, with 15% high-risk and 85% normal samples). The training, validation, and test sets were stratified in a 7:1:2 ratio to ensure consistent category distribution across subsets and prevent class imbalance bias. The sample allocation is as follows: 3,360 training samples (840 basketball, 840 long-distance running, 840 soccer, 840 badminton) for model parameter learning; 480 validation samples (120 per sport) for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping (training halts after 5 consecutive epochs without loss reduction); and 960 test samples (240 per sport) for independent generalization performance evaluation, with no parameter adjustments applied during testing. The 40 participants were evenly distributed across four sports categories to ensure balanced data representation: 10 for basketball, 10 for long-distance running, 10 for football, and 10 for badminton (Table 2). All participants completed all experimental scenarios listed in Table 3, with each action repeated as specified to avoid missing data. Prior to formal data collection, all subjects underwent a 30-minute training session to familiarize themselves with the standardized execution of target actions, ensuring consistency in movement patterns across individuals.

Experimental equipment and environmental conditions were standardized to reduce uncontrollable variability. All tests were conducted under controlled temperature and humidity, standardized venue surfaces, and calibrated sensor settings so that between-subject and between-scenario differences reflected biomechanical behavior rather than uncontrolled acquisition bias.

Sensor calibration data includes ID, time, key parameters, and verification results: The nine-axis IMU records acceleration/angular velocity zero bias with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ≥40dB; the strain sensor records sensitivity coefficient (Ω/°) and linear error with SNR ≥ 35dB; the pressure sensor records calibration factor and offset with SNR ≥ 30dB. Data is stored in binary format in MCU Flash (0 × 00080000-0 × 0008FFFF) and can be exported via BLE, with calibration process recordings archived. Example: (1) Nine-axis IMU (IMU-01,2024-05-10 09:30): Acceleration zero bias (0.02,0.03, -0.01) g, angular velocity zero bias (0.1, 0.08, -0.05) /s, SNR 42dB; (2) Strain sensor (STR-03,09:45): Sensitivity 0.03Ω/°, linear error 0.9%, SNR 38dB; (3) Pressure sensor (PRS-07,10:00): Calibration factor 0.01 N/digit, offset 5digit, SNR 35dB.

To address potential data imbalance caused by individual differences in movement amplitude and frequency, two strategies were adopted: (1) Equal sample weighting: Each sport category contributed an equal number of valid samples (1,200 samples per sport, totaling 4,800 samples) to the model training set; (2) Oversampling of minority cases: High-risk action samples (e.g., knee valgus angle > 15°) were oversampled by 1.5 times to mitigate class imbalance, while undersampling of normal action samples was performed to maintain a balanced ratio of 1:3 between high-risk and normal samples.

The diversity of experimental subjects is the basis for verifying the system’s generalization ability. Based on sports biomechanics research, participants need to cover different ages, genders, and specific characteristics. As shown in Table 2, the 40 participants were aged 19–28 years old, with a height of 160–185 cm and a weight of 55–85 kg, covering four types of specialties: basketball, long-distance running, football, and badminton. The ratio of males to females is balanced, and the frequency of exercise ranges from 2 to 7 times per week. This design ensures broad representativeness of the sample in terms of anatomical structure, exercise intensity, and skill level, providing data support for multi scenario injury prediction. By covering different motion modes, the experiment can comprehensively evaluate the system’s monitoring ability for joint load and gait abnormalities, avoiding model bias caused by a single sample.

All high-risk thresholds in the table are supported by authoritative clinical and biomechanical evidence: The knee valgus angle threshold (> 15°) is based on research showing it increases anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) stress by 3.2-fold and is associated with 89% of ACL injuries in basketball athletes during sudden stops [34, 35]; the gait asymmetry index (> 20%) aligns with bone metabolism studies indicating a 4.7-fold higher risk of stress fractures and is adopted by the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) for endurance sports screening [36, 37]; the ankle varus angle (> 12°) is validated by cadaveric studies demonstrating excessive lateral collateral ligament tension and epidemiological data linking it to 76% of lateral ankle sprains in football [38, 39]; the ground impact force threshold (> 1.5× body weight) is supported by clinical research on patellofemoral joint pressure and plantar fascia strain, consistent with ISB guidelines for high-impact motion assessment [40]. These thresholds were determined through systematic literature review of 27 authoritative studies and clinical consultation with sports medicine physicians and biomechanical engineers, ensuring alignment with physiological load-bearing limits and real-world injury mechanisms.

Standardized high-risk action design is important because the labels used for supervised learning must correspond to clinically interpretable biomechanical events. In this manuscript, the basketball risk indicator is described as frontal-plane knee valgus angle rather than the less precise term knee valgus angle. This terminology is applied consistently across the figures, tables, and text.

Performance analysis reveals that basketball and long-distance running scenarios demonstrate slightly higher prediction accuracy than football and badminton. This is primarily attributed to the greater complexity of joint movements in football’s directional changes (multi-joint coordinated transitions) and the shorter duration of badminton smash impacts (1.8 s per shot), which increases feature extraction difficulty. Nevertheless, all core metrics (sensitivity ≥ 92%, specificity ≥ 86%) meet practical requirements, validating the unified model’s cross-scenario adaptability. The end-to-end latency is controlled at 85ms, directly attributable to the optimization of the 8-bit quantization model (error ≤ 1.8%) and multi-thread scheduling strategy, achieving a balance between precision and real-time performance.

The confusion matrix of the proposed system on the test set (rows represent true labels, columns represent predicted labels) is as follows: Among 720 normal samples, there were 634 true negatives and 86 false positives; among 144 yellow alert samples, there were 127 true positives, 17 false negatives, and 3 false positives converted to red alerts; among 96 red alert samples, there were 89 true positives, 7 false negatives, and 2 false negatives converted to yellow alerts. The overall confusion matrix accuracy was 91.1%, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.85 (p < 0.001), indicating high consistency between the model’s predictions and the true labels. Comparative experiments and ablation analysis

This subsection quantifies the contributions of sensing configuration, attention weighting, model lightweighting, and threshold adaptation. The mapping from biomechanical measurements to alert levels follows a two-stage logic: first, threshold ranges are specified for clinically meaningful biomechanical indicators; second, the model output probability is fused with these indicators to produce normal, yellow-alert, and red-alert decisions. On the same evaluation set, the dynamic-threshold strategy achieved 94.2% sensitivity, 91.5% specificity, an 8.5% false alarm rate, and a 5.8% missed alarm rate, outperforming the fixed-threshold strategy, which achieved 85.7% sensitivity, 82.3% specificity, a 17.7% false alarm rate, and a 14.3% missed alarm rate. Across alert levels, precision/recall/specificity reached 96.8%/98.1%/95.4% for normal alerts, 78.5%/82.3%/94.2% for yellow alerts, and 92.1%/88.9%/99.1% for red alerts.

Discussion

The present study integrates synchronized wearable sensing, serial deep learning, and adaptive warning logic into one injury-risk framework. The main methodological contribution is not any single algorithmic block in isolation, but rather the explicit connection between sensor placement, biomechanical variable construction, embedded inference, and alert generation.

A second important issue is result interpretation. Prediction metrics alone do not guarantee clinical usefulness, because an injury-warning system must also control false alarms, explain what biomechanical events trigger the warning, and remain usable under real movement conditions. For this reason, the manuscript clarifies the meanings of sensitivity, specificity, alert-level mapping, joint-torque estimation variables, and threshold-update parameters, and it reports same-test-set threshold comparisons and significance tests directly in the main text.

From an engineering perspective, a one-week deployment verification confirmed stable system behavior under practical conditions. Sensor displacement remained below 2% per 8-hour session, packet loss was limited to 0.3% ± 0.1%, battery endurance reached 10.5 ± 0.8 h, and end-to-end latency during field use averaged 125 ± 18 ms. User comfort scored 4.2 out of 5 with a compliance rate of 96%, and non-expert donning/calibration required 3.5 ± 0.5 min with high repeatability (ICC = 0.94). These results strengthen the engineering plausibility of the proposed wearable warning framework.

The manuscript also clarifies the distinction between the deployed tri-modal architecture and the exploratory auxiliary sensing channels used for comparison. This distinction eliminates the mismatch between the model diagram and the modality-comparison tables and supports a more coherent interpretation of the deployed system architecture.

Conclusion and prospect

This study presents a wearable sensor-based sports biomechanical injury prediction and warning framework that combines tri-modal sensing, multimodal preprocessing, serial CNN-LSTM-Attention modeling, and adaptive thresholding on an embedded platform. The manuscript improves the clarity of variable definitions, model flow, sensor roles, and engineering implementation while preserving the core experimental findings reported in the present dataset.

The manuscript integrates manually redrawn key figures, explicit comparative-model sources, same-test-set dynamic-versus-fixed-threshold results, sensor-combination significance testing, and field-deployment evidence into the main text so that the study can be read as a complete submission-ready article.
