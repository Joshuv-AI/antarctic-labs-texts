Development and evaluation of a patient-centric approach for accurate medication capture
Authors: Larry Ma, Alan V Rincon, Joshua Ide, Katrina O’Hara, Rachel Weinstein, Sebastien Hannay, Lucie Keunen, Vincent Keunen, Ivelina Popova, Sherry Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and evaluate a patient-centric medication module within a personal health record (PHR) app for capturing medication use, focusing on accuracy, usability, and concordance.Materials and MethodsThe medication module offered 4 entry methods: picklist, National Drug Code (NDC), free-text, and portal import, with the first 2 leveraging RxNorm and openFDA APIs. Patients from an integrated delivery network (IDN) created medication lists and recorded daily use in the app’s diary. Pharmacists evaluated medication accuracy by reviewing patient-uploaded medication images. Usability was measured using the System Usability Scale (SUS). Concordance was assessed by comparing Electronic Health Records (EHR) with diary entries.ResultsOver a 14-day period, 85 patients entered 617 medications, with 533 logged in the diary representing current use. Picklist was the most used entry method. Overall medication entry accuracy was 92% (picklist 97%; NDC 87%; free-text 84%; and portal import 100%). The mean system usability score was 56.5 for the study app (patients) and 80.8 for the medication module (pharmacists). EHR concordance with diary entries was low (25% using the 14-day window; 53% using a 1-year window); most unmatched entries were over-the-counter (OTC) medications.DiscussionAccurate and complete medication records are essential for the safe and effective use of medications. This patient-centric medication module supported accurate capture of prescription and OTC medications. Gaps in EHR data highlight the need to improve medication record accuracy and reconciliation.ConclusionPatient-generated health data can have a central role in creating the “Best Possible Medication History” envisioned by the World Health Organization.

Introduction

Medications play a vital role in the treatment and management of conditions and symptoms. However, the safe and effective use of medications requires knowledge and consideration of all medications that a patient is currently taking or receiving, in order to avoid omissions, duplications, dosing errors, and potential drug-drug interactions.1,2

Medication discrepancies, which are differences between a patient’s actual medication use and their documented records, are common across all medication classes, care settings, and regions worldwide. Stewart and Lynch reported discrepancies among 74% of patients in a primary care clinic in the United States while Säfholm et al reported discrepancies among 84% of patients across 10 primary care centers in Sweden.3,4 Bedell et al reported medication discrepancies in 76% of patients in a practice of cardiologists and internists, where more than half the patients reported taking medications that were not recorded.5 The most common causes for these discrepancies included the omission of medications (in particular over-the-counter [OTC]), unfilled prescriptions, and incorrect dosage. Medication discrepancies can lead to medication errors and adverse outcomes, which cost an estimated $42 billion in global health expenditure annually.1,6

Medication reconciliation is the process of comparing a patient’s current medications with their medical records to identify and resolve medication discrepancies. It has been recognized as an important step for achieving better medication safety and optimizing transitions of care by several international patient safety organizations, including The Joint Commission (TJC), Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), and the World Health Organization (WHO).7,8

The WHO Collaborating Centre for Patient Safety Solutions introduced a concept called “Best Possible Medication History (BPMH)” which involves capturing all medications the patient is currently taking including prescription, OTC, and complementary.1,9 Similarly, the United Kingdom National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) defines a comprehensive medication list as one including prescribed, OTC, and complementary medicines.2

Medication reconciliation is resource intensive process and complicated by the fact that patients may be unable or unwilling to provide accurate information.10–13 Various mobile applications have been developed to help patients manage their medications, track usage, and improve adherence.14–18 However, many of these apps are disease- or product-specific, require tedious manual entry, and offer limited features beyond pill reminders and adherence tracking. Most importantly, there is a lack of standardization of medication capture given the large number of medications approved in the United States (>20 000 Rx, >300 000 OTC).19,20 While these applications may help patients track their personal use of medications, they often fail to capture accurate or complete drug information, such as ingredient(s), strength(s), and dosage form. Lastly, the medication information may be difficult to share with the healthcare providers, or to aggregate for research purposes, due to the lack of standardization.

Objective

The objective of the study was to evaluate the accuracy, usability, and concordance of a patient-centric medication module within a personal health record (PHR) app for capturing medication use. Through this evaluation, we sought to demonstrate the value of patient-generated health data (PGHD) in achieving the Best Possible Medication History (BPMH) proposed by the WHO.

Methods

MyMMR study

My Medications and Medical Records (MyMMR) was an observational study designed to evaluate the feasibility and scalability of a mobile app-based technology for collecting real-world medication usage data, with a focus on OTC analgesics. Patients with ongoing acute or chronic (non-cancer) pain conditions were recruited from an integrated delivery network (IDN). In addition to the medication data collected via the app, the study also included surveys, patient-reported outcomes, and medication image assessments. Medication order and reconciliation data were extracted from IDN’s EHR. The study was approved by the IDN’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), and the protocol was registered at The European Network of Centres for Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacovigilance (EUPAS50081).

The research was carried out within a large not-for-profit IDN in Northern California, which delivers a wide array of medical services, including primary and specialty care, through over 100 ambulatory clinics, 23 hospitals, and 33 ambulatory surgery centers.21 This IDN serves a highly diverse population of approximately 3.4 million individuals, including 1.1 million in primary care. The EHR data within the IDN are relatively comprehensive, as encounter records from outside the IDN are integrated through a health information exchange service.22 Gaps occur only when patients receive care from providers that do not use the same EHR system.

MyMMR study app

The MyMMR study app is a private label version of the Andaman7 PHR platform.23 It was customized to support the study with features such as an enhanced medication module, tailored study workflows, instructional materials, and notifications. Access was restricted to potential study subjects, who completed a screener upon download to confirm eligibility. Study data collected on the app were pseudonymized and then transferred to the sponsor for analysis.

Design of the medication module

The medication module consists of 2 interconnected components: a medication list for compiling an inventory of medications and a medication diary for logging daily usage (Figure 1). Patients build their list by adding entries, which can then be selected in the diary to record intake details such as date and time of intake, quantity and unit, either in real-time or retrospectively.

Medication list and medication diary.

Four methods for adding entries were implemented for the medication list:

Picklist: As patients begin typing a medication name, the app dynamically queries RxNorm APIs to generate a filtered list of matching options at the branded and clinical drug levels, eg “Tylenol Extra Strength 500 MG Oral Tablet” or “acetaminophen 500 MG Oral Tablet,” respectively (Figure 2).24 This method guides users toward standardized selections, to reduce errors from manual input.

Picklist: As patients begin typing a medication name, the app dynamically queries RxNorm APIs to generate a filtered list of matching options at the branded and clinical drug levels, eg “Tylenol Extra Strength 500 MG Oral Tablet” or “acetaminophen 500 MG Oral Tablet,” respectively (Figure 2).24 This method guides users toward standardized selections, to reduce errors from manual input.

NDC: Patients can manually enter the National Drug Code (NDC) or use the device’s camera to scan it from the packaging (Figure 2). The NDC is a unique numerical identifier assigned to every medication intended for human use in the United States.25 The NDC code is commonly printed on the original packaging for both prescription and OTC medications. When using the scan option, the app employs optical character recognition (OCR) to automatically detect the code. Once detected, the NDC is mapped to the corresponding RxNorm concept. The medication name is then displayed in a pop-up for the patient to confirm. The use of NDCs makes the entry of multi-ingredient products much easier and offers additional product details, such as manufacturer, brand, and the structured product label.26

Medication entry via picklist and NDC.

For each item in the medication list, patients were instructed to upload images of medication bottles or packaging (Figure 1). These images supported patients in identifying their medications during diary entries and were also used by the study team to assess the accuracy of medication list.

Patient enrollment and study procedure

Eligible patients aged 18 to 89 with at least one encounter more than a year prior to and another encounter within 6 months prior to outreach were identified using EHR and invited through the patient portal. Interested individuals underwent chart review and phone screening to confirm non-cancer pain and exclude pregnancy or breastfeeding. Informed consent was obtained electronically. Consented patients received an email to download the MyMMR study app and completed a screener confirming eligibility. The study aimed to recruit at least 20% of participants from each age group (18-44, 45-64, and 65-89) and at least 40% from each gender.

The study diagram is shown in Figure 3. After enrollment, participants completed a survey of demographic and baseline characteristics. Following the baseline survey, participants were instructed to connect to the IDN health portal through the app and create a list of their oral medications; however, the medication module also supported entry of non-oral medications. The date of the first successful IDN portal connection was designated as the index date, with the 14-day medication diary period starting the following day. To promote compliance, participants received a daily medication survey during the diary period. For participants who withdrew, no further data were collected after the date of withdrawal.

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

Medication entry accuracy

Pharmacist volunteers, primarily PharmDs with retail experience, were recruited through a companywide pharmacist network at the sponsor to assess the accuracy and characteristics of medication entries using patient-uploaded images. The medication entry accuracy was defined as a full match on ingredient(s) and strength(s). Training was provided by a lead pharmacist to ensure consistency across assessments. To protect privacy, any patient or pharmacy information on the images was redacted before they were sent to the sponsor.

Usability assessment

Usability was measured using the System Usability Scale (SUS), adapted for mobile applications.29,30 The SUS is a validated 10-item questionnaire evaluating users’ agreement with statements regarding the ease of use of a system or application. The SUS survey was administered on Day 15, to evaluate the full app experience at the end of the 14-day diary period, and on Day 45 as part of the exit survey. Prior to patient enrollment, pharmacist volunteers completed the SUS to assess the standalone medication module.

Medication concordance between EHR and diary data

RxNorm is a standardized drug terminology maintained by the U.S. National Library of Medicine, providing normalized names and Concept Unique Identifiers (RxCUIs) for clinical drug entities.31 It offers comprehensive coverage of medicinal concepts in the United States and is the federally mandated standard for medication terminology in EHRs.32–34

Medication records from the EHR were extracted for enrolled patients following the OMOP common data model, including RxCUIs and exposure start and stop dates.35,36 Two exposure windows were applied to the EHR data: a narrow window aligned with the 14-day diary period, and a broad window spanning 365 days before through 45 days after the index date, serving as a sensitivity analysis (Figure 3).

Concordance was assessed by comparing EHR and diary medications at 3 RxNorm levels:

Picklist, NDC, and portal import entries contained RxCUIs in their metadata. For free-text entries, the RxNorm GetApproximateMatch function was used to identify the best-matching RxCUI, followed by manual curation using entered drug names and available images.

Multi-ingredient products were represented by concatenating the corresponding RxCUIs at the SCDC and IN levels, eg, “acetaminophen 325 MG | hydrocodone bitartrate 5 MG” (315263 | 856902).

Results

Patient characteristics and engagement

Of 24 602 invited patients, 186 provided consent, and 100 passed the screening and were enrolled (59 females, 41 males; median age 59 years, range 20-86). Most patients (80%) identified as White (ST1); all reported pain within 30 days prior to screening. The most commonly reported conditions were low back pain (69%), arthritis (59%), and chronic knee or hip pain (54%). Of the 100 participants, 71 used iOS devices, and 29 used Android.

Thirteen (13) participants withdrew at various time points of the study. Of the 85 participants who created a medication list, 84 responded to the daily medication survey and/or recorded a medication diary entry on at least 1 day, with 55 participants completed entries every day throughout the 14-day diary period.

Summary of medication list and diary entries

During the study, 85 patients entered a total of 617 medications, averaging 7.26 medications per patient (Table 1). The picklist was the most popular entry method, used by 71 patients (83.5%) to enter 337 medications (54.6%). Free-text entry was the second most popular method, used by 57 patients (67.1%) for 196 medications (31.8%). The NDC method was utilized by 23 patients (27.1%) to enter 50 medications (8.1%). Additionally, 2 patients imported 34 medications (5.5%) from non-IDN health portals (FHIR MedicationStatement). The maximum number of medications entered by any patient was 38, with 20 via picklist, and 18 through free-text.

Pharmacist’s assessment of medication accuracy

Images were uploaded for 429 medications (69.5%), including 313 with 2 images and 116 with 1 image, for a total of 742 images (Table 2). Image quality was sufficient for most medications; however, 4 (0.9%) could not be evaluated and 15 provided only partial information. The majority of the medications (68.1%) were prescription drugs, followed by non-prescribed OTC drugs (24.2%) and supplements (5.1%) (Figure 4, Q5). After excluding 19 medications with incomplete or poor-quality images, 410 medications were evaluated for accuracy (Figure 5). The overall accuracy was 92.2% (378/410), with picklist achieving 97.0% (230/237), NDC 87.2% (34/39), free-text 83.9% (104/124), and portal import 100% (10/10).

System Usability Scale

The mean SUS score for the MyMMR app on day 15 was 56.5 across all patients, with a higher mean score of 65.2 among a subset of 31 patients who enrolled using the final version of the app (Table 3). Four iterations of the MyMMR app were released during the study, and these updates appeared effective in improving the app usability. When examining SUS scores by age group, the 18 to 44 years group reported a higher average score of 70.0 than groups 44 to 64 (48.2) and 65 to 89 (55.7). The assessment of usability of the app varied greatly across patients, as indicated by high standard deviations.

However, this improvement is less meaningful clinically, as strength is often a critical factor for safe medication use.

To evaluate overall completeness of EHR medication records beyond those actively used, the EHR window was expanded to include medications documented from 1 year before through 45 days after the index date. This broader timeframe accounts for delays or inconsistencies in updating medication status. Using this extended window, concordance rose to 52.7% at the SCDC level and 63.2% at the ingredient level (Table 4). Nevertheless, about half of diary-recorded medications remained unmatched in the EHR, underscoring persistent gaps in EHR capture.

Patterns of matched versus unmatched drugs revealed a systematic gap (ST2, ST3). At the SCDC level, OTC products dominated the list of non-matched medications under both narrow and broad exposure windows, whereas prescription drugs dominated the list of matched medications. These findings indicate that EHRs are less effective at capturing OTC medications, which are often self-managed and inconsistently documented in clinical settings. The results also suggest that medication records in EHRs may not be fully up to date, especially concerning current use.

Interestingly, some EHR medication records appeared to capture only ingredient information, but not the strength. For example, atorvastatin 20 and 40 mg were among the top 10 non-matched medications at the SCDC level, yet atorvastatin was not among the top 10 non-matched ingredients (ST3). This suggests that some mismatches may not reflect missing medications, but rather imprecise documentation in the EHR—where drugs are recorded without essential details like strength or dosage form—limiting their utility for accurate reconciliation.

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of a patient-centric medication module for medication capture, demonstrating an overall accuracy of 92.2%. Among the 3 main entry methods, picklist achieved the highest accuracy (97.0%), followed by NDC (87.2%) and free-text (83.9%). Although portal import was used by only 2 patients, it showed promise with 100% accuracy based on the 10 medications evaluated with images. The NDC method initially relied on an OpenFDA API function for mapping to RxNorm, which introduced a small number of inaccurate mappings; had the RxNorm API been used consistently, NDC entries would have achieved 100% accuracy. Importantly, live API calls to RxNorm and openFDA ensured interoperability by assigning RxCUIs for picklist and NDC entries.

A major strength of the study was to capture both prescription and OTC medications, which are often poorly recorded in EHRs. Of the medications entered, 89.4% were also recorded in the diary representing current use, supporting the diary’s validity as the source of truth. Concordance between EHR and diary entries was low (24.5% at the SCDC level for the 14-day window), largely reflecting the absence of OTCs, and prescriptions likely obtained outside the IDN. Most matched medications were prescription drugs, while unmatched entries were predominantly OTC products.

These findings have significant clinical implications. Incomplete medication lists can pose safety risks, such as when patients receive prescriptions from multiple providers or obtain additional medications outside a health system. International and national initiatives—including WHO’s call for a “Best Possible Medication History” and AHRQ’s vision of a single “One Source of Truth”—highlight the importance of comprehensive, unified medication records.1,9,37 While the importance of unified medication records is widely recognized, most reconciliation efforts are carried out by healthcare providers at points of care, with passive patient engagement.7,8,10,11 The MyMMR medication module addresses this longstanding challenge by enabling patients to actively contribute to reconciliation. By integrating patient-generated health data into clinical workflows, it provides a feasible path to closing documentation gaps, particularly in fragmented care environments.

While medication accuracy and concordance were the primary focus, the usability findings provide valuable insights from the patient’s perspective. The overall SUS score (56.5) suggested room for improvement, though this should be interpreted in light of the app’s complexity and healthcare context. Patients were asked to complete multiple tasks, including enrollment, portal connectivity, surveys, and tracking their medication use. In contrast, the standalone medication module received a higher scores of 80.8 tested by pharmacist volunteers before the study recruitment.

Several design features may have hindered usability. The app was designed to display the FHIR MedicationStatement resource to align with a patient-centric model, but the IDN portal provided only MedicationRequests, leaving patients with empty lists and requiring manual reconstruction of medication histories. The NDC scanning function was also underutilized (27.1% of patients for 8.1% of the medications), likely due to the lack of understanding and the NDC availability. Usability was further constrained by delays in implementing updates, which required IRB approval; nevertheless, the few upgrades released during the study improved patient experience. Overall, these findings are consistent with expectations for an application that integrates with healthcare data systems and requires substantial user input.30,38–42

The study population had a median age of 59 years and chronic pain conditions, a group often characterized by polypharmacy and frequent use of OTCs, making accurate medication tracking especially critical. Their strong engagement with the diary over 14 days demonstrates the feasibility of this approach, though long-term engagement and generalizability to other patient populations should be examined in future studies.

Looking ahead, tools like MyMMR have the potential to support evolving patterns of self-care and medication use. OTC products play an increasingly important role in patient self-management, and new regulatory initiatives such as the FDA’s Additional Condition for Nonprescription Use (ACNU) rule could expand access to OTC treatments for chronic conditions, such as hyperlipidemia and diabetes.43–46 By capturing both prescription and OTC medications with links to product labels, MyMMR demonstrates how such tools may contribute to patient education, medication safety, and reconciliation across care settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations:

Scope of medications: The focus was on orally administered medications; inhalers, injectables, and other formulations were not assessed, although the app can capture them. In addition, 5.1% of entries were classified as “Supplements” by pharmacists, most of which were entered as free-text without RxCUIs.

Conclusion

Maintaining accurate and comprehensive medication records remains a critical challenge in healthcare, affecting both patient safety and quality of care. The MyMMR study demonstrated that a patient-centric medication module can reliably capture both prescription and over-the-counter (OTC) medications. Gaps in EHR data highlight the need to improve medication record accuracy and reconciliation. PGHD can have a central role in creating the “Best Possible Medication History” envisioned by the World Health Organization.
