Introduction

Congenital broncho-esophageal fistula (BEF) is a rare developmental anomaly characterized by abnormal communication between the esophagus and the bronchial tree during embryonic development [1,2]. This condition, associated with esophageal atresia, and can lead to life-threatening complications in neonates and children, including recurrent aspiration pneumonia, respiratory distress, and failure to thrive [[3], [4], [5]]. The clinical presentation of BEF often includes symptoms such as coughing, dyspnea, recurrent pneumonia, hemoptysis, and gastrointestinal complaints like dysphagia or regurgitation [6]. Unlike tracheoesophageal fistulas (TEF) diagnosed in neonates, isolated BEF may remain undiagnosed until later childhood or adulthood, often presenting as recurrent respiratory infections or chronic cough triggered by feeding [7].

Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and surgical techniques, congenital BEF remains a challenging condition due to its variable presentation and potential for delayed diagnosis. Diagnosis typically involves a combination of imaging modalities such as chest X-ray, CT scans, and endoscopic procedures like bronchoscopy and esophagoscopy. Confirmation of the diagnosis is crucial for appropriate management, including endoscopic interventions, surgical repair, or treatment of underlying conditions such as malignancy [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]].

Thus, early recognition and prompt intervention are crucial to prevent severe pulmonary and nutritional sequelae [13].

The current manuscript was prepared following the revised surgical case report (SCARE) guideline [14].

Case presentation

A 10-year-old female was admitted with complaints of recurrent cough and choking after swallowing liquids. She had experienced repeated episodes of pneumonia since infancy, with a chronic cough occasionally productive and exacerbated by meals and drinks. She had been treated multiple times with antibiotics for the diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia at the nearby primary hospital without sustained improvement. There was no history of surgery, trauma, or exposure to smoking. Her vital signs were stable on examination, and systemic physical examination was unremarkable except for reputations localized to the right lower lung filled. Laboratory tests revealed a white blood cell count of 9000 cells/μL (68.2 % granulocytes), hemoglobin 12.8 g/dL, and platelet count of 457,000 cells/μL. Her blood type was B- positive.

A barium esophagogram demonstrated a fistulous tract connecting the mid-esophagus to the right main bronchus (Fig. 1).

A contrast-enhanced chest CT confirmed the esophagobronchial fistula (Fig. 2), and a chest radiograph showed bronchiectatic changes in the right lower lung (Fig. 3). Echocardiography revealed normal cardiac anatomy with situs solitus and levocardia. The patient underwent a right posterolateral thoracotomy. After a course of parental antibiotics, an elective right posterolateral thoracotomy was performed.

Intraoperatively, the fistula was identified between the middle intrathoracic esophagus and the right main bronchus (Fig. 4). The fistula was divided, and the esophageal and bronchial defects were closed primarily with 3-0 prolene sutures.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient recovered without recurrence of symptoms. Finally, the patient was discharged on the 8th postoperative day.

Discussion

Congenital BEF is a rare anomaly, resulting from incomplete separation of the foregut during embryogenesis, leading to a persistent communication between the esophagus and the bronchial tree [2]. This abnormal connection allows ingested materials such as food, saliva, and gastric contents to enter the airway, predisposing patients to chronic pulmonary inflammation, recurrent respiratory infections, and eventually bronchiectasis [2,[15], [16], [17]].

BEF is estimated to account for less than 0.1 % of congenital anomalies. The condition is classified into four types, according to the Braimbridge and Keith classification [2]:

Type I: Direct fistula between esophagus and bronchus.

Patients with BEF often present with recurrent pneumonia localized to the same pulmonary segment, chronic cough worsened by swallowing, choking episodes, and occasionally hemoptysis. In this case, the patient's recurrent pneumonia, chronic cough exacerbated by feeding, and choking on liquids were hallmark features suggestive of esophagorespiratory communication [2,5,6].

A barium esophagram remains the gold standard diagnostic tool for BEF, as it can demonstrate the fistulous tract [12,18,19]. Computed tomography further assists in delineating the anatomy and associated pulmonary complications. In some cases, endoscopic evaluation may be necessary for direct visualization of the fistula [15].

The definitive treatment for congenital BEF is surgical division and closure of the fistula, which prevents further pulmonary damage and resolves symptoms. Delayed diagnosis may lead to irreversible complications such as bronchiectasis, lung abscess, or chronic suppurative lung disease [2,8,9,11,13].

Our patient's presentation aligns with reported cases in the literature, emphasizing that BEF, though rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of recurrent localized pneumonia and feeding-related cough, particularly in older children where neonatal screening for tracheoesophageal anomalies was normal [13,20]. The authors declared the involvement of the multidisciplinary approaches in the early diagnosis of the case and promptly management as strength of the work and the study tried to review prior literature in the scenario.

Conclusion

Congenital BEF should be considered in children presenting with recurrent pneumonia, especially when symptoms are associated with feeding. Prompt diagnosis and surgical management can prevent long-term pulmonary complications.
