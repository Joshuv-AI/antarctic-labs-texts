Misdiagnosis of different types of aneuploidy in preimplantation genetic testing (PGT): a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Weiqun Jiang, Zhixin Hu, Youzhen Han, Jialin Shi, Kexin Chen, Yuxuan Lian, Weijia Li, Peiqi Hao, Zhaoyu Zhou, Ze Wu, Yunxiu Li, Xiaorong Wu, Mingyi Li, Lian Deng, Haishan Zheng, Zhaohong Bi, Lei Ding, Xinyi Pan, Rui Ma, Zouying Tang, Jianqiang Mu, Xiaoli Xie, Yonggang Li, Jiacong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRecent studies have reported that embryos diagnosed as aneuploid by preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) can still result in successful live births after transfer. This suggests that, in addition to mosaic embryos, fully aneuploid embryos may also carry a risk of diagnostic error, potentially reducing the overall accuracy of PGT. Therefore, thoroughly investigating the risk and characteristics of aneuploidy misdiagnosis is crucial for optimizing PGT strategies and improving clinical outcomes in assisted reproductive technology (ART).MethodsRelevant studies published from January 2000 to December 2024 were identified through PubMed and Web of Science. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). A diagnostic meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model to calculate pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), combining sensitivity and specificity. Results were visualized using forest plots.ResultsA total of 22 studies were included to assess the discordance between trophectoderm (TE) biopsy and inner cell mass (ICM) or whole blastocyst (WB) results. The discordance rate for euploid embryo diagnosis was low (2.6%), whereas it was significantly higher for aneuploid embryos (9.2%). Segmental aneuploidies showed the highest discordance rate (17.4%). In the PGT-A population, misdiagnosis of segmental aneuploid embryos was particularly prominent (OR = 10.04, 95% CI: 7.60–13.27, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe results indicate that embryos with segmental aneuploidies have a significantly higher risk of misdiagnosis, especially in the PGT-A population. This highlights the need for caution when interpreting trophectoderm (TE) biopsy results involving segmental aneuploidies, to avoid misdiagnosis and the inadvertent discard of potentially viable embryos.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-025-08283-1.

Introduction

Since its clinical introduction, preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) has contributed significantly to improved reproductive outcomes [1]; however, it has concurrently introduced new clinical and ethical concerns. The genetic diagnosis process necessitates an invasive trophectoderm (TE) biopsy, which may cause direct mechanical trauma to the embryo and has been hypothesized to induce iatrogenic aneuploidy [2–4]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that compared with natural conception, pregnancies following PGT are associated with increased risks of preterm birth and hypertensive disorders during pregnancy, and offspring may present with lower birth weight. However, robust evidence regarding the long-term safety for the offspring remains lacking [5–8].

Experimental studies comparing chromosomal profiles of TE biopsies, inner cell mass (ICM), and whole blastocysts (WB) have revealed substantial discrepancies, largely attributed to early embryonic lineage segregation. As such, the chromosomal constitution of the TE does not reliably reflect that of the ICM [9–11]. Supporting this, a recent diagnostic meta-analysis by Chen et al. reported an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of only 0.878 for TE biopsy—a value below the threshold typically considered indicative of high diagnostic accuracy—highlighting that a single TE biopsy may be insufficient to comprehensively represent the embryo’s genomic integrity [12].

Since PGT relies on the analysis of a limited number of trophectoderm (TE) cells obtained through biopsy to infer the genetic status of the entire embryo, the presence of embryonic mosaicism can lead to diagnostic misclassification. Recent evidence has identified mosaicism as a major factor compromising the diagnostic accuracy of PGT [13–17]. Moreover, numerous clinical reports have documented healthy live births following the transfer of mosaic embryos, suggesting that current PGT protocols may yield false-positive diagnoses in certain cases [18–20].

Notably, beyond the successful live births from mosaic embryos, Andria and colleagues reported the transfer of 25 non-mosaic aneuploid embryos. Among these, six embryos with segmental aneuploidies not only successfully implanted but were also confirmed as euploid by prenatal diagnosis, ultimately resulting in the birth of healthy infants [21]. Additionally, in a case reported by D. H. Barad involving the transfer of PGT-A abnormal embryos, a live birth was achieved from an embryo initially diagnosed as having a whole-chromosome aneuploidy, with subsequent chromosomal analysis confirming euploidy [22]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the risk of false-positive diagnoses in PGT extends beyond mosaic embryos and may also affect embryos classified as uniformly aneuploid [23, 24].

PGT is designed to improve pregnancy success rates and reduce the risk of genetic disorders [25, 26]; however, accumulating evidence indicates that the accuracy of PGT detection has not yet reached the ideal level [12, 27]. Although reports on misdiagnosis of aneuploid embryos are relatively limited, this issue remains significant. Currently, no studies have performed a classification analysis on the specific types of aneuploid embryos subject to misdiagnosis, resulting in potential bias regarding which types of aneuploid embryos are more prone to erroneous diagnosis [28]. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to thoroughly investigate the types of aneuploid embryos that may be misdiagnosed by PGT. Through this research, we hope to identify embryo types that are more susceptible to misdiagnosis under the current PGT framework, thereby providing clinicians with more accurate criteria for embryo selection and exploring ways to optimize detection protocols to enhance the accuracy of embryo assessment.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). Both quantitative and qualitative methods were employed to perform a comprehensive analysis. The study protocol was reviewed and registered in PROSPERO(registration number:CRD42024626656).

Literature search

This study searched the PubMed and Web of Science databases from January 2000 to December 2024. The search was independently conducted by two researchers (H.Y. and J.W.) to ensure reliability and accuracy. Quality assessment

Two researchers (H.Y. and J.W.) conducted quality assessments of the included studies. This research includes non-randomized controlled trials and observational studies. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was chosen to evaluate the quality of the included literature. The assessment method covers three major domains comprising a total of eight items: selection of study population, comparability, and outcome assessment. Each item has corresponding scoring criteria, with a maximum total score of 9 points. Studies scoring 7–9 points are considered high quality, those scoring 4–6 points are medium quality, and studies scoring less than 4 points are regarded as low quality.

Data extraction

The following information was extracted from each study: first author’s name, publication date, sequencing method, type of PGT, type of gold standard, discordance results, risk assessment, and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Data were independently collected by two researchers (H.Y. and J.W.). Any disagreements were discussed and resolved by a third researcher (Y.J.).

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0). The included variables were binary, and the odds ratio (OR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) was used as the measure of effect size. The inconsistency rate of embryo testing results between TE and ICM/WB was quantified by calculating the pooled OR. Because each study included in this meta-analysis was conducted at a single research center, potential bias due to laboratory-specific procedures or patient characteristics may exist. To address this heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied for data analysis. The overall significance of the effect was tested using the Z-test or t-test to evaluate statistical significance, with P < 0.05 generally considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding each study to determine the impact of individual studies on the overall prevalence estimate.

The model for the inconsistency rate between TE and ICM/WB accounts for heterogeneity among studies. When high heterogeneity exists between studies, a random-effects model is used to pool the inconsistency rates; for low heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model is applied to analyze the inconsistency rate. For the pooled estimates, tau2 is used to estimate the between-study variance, and the I2 statistic is used to quantify heterogeneity. Statistical significance is assessed with a threshold of P < 0.05.

Quality assessment included in the study

This study employed the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), a widely recognized tool for quality assessment. Using the NOS, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the included studies across three key domains. First, selection of the study population was assessed, focusing on the representativeness of the study subjects and the extent to which selection bias was minimized. Second, comparability was evaluated by examining the extent to which the studies controlled for confounding variables and ensured comparability between groups on key factors. Third, the outcome domain addressed the appropriateness of outcome measurement methods and the adequacy of follow-up duration to ensure the reliability of the findings. Following this rigorous evaluation process, the NOS scores of the included studies ranged from 5 to 6 (Supplementary Table 1), indicating that although certain methodological limitations were present, the studies were generally of moderate quality. These results provide a solid foundation for our subsequent analyses and discussions, while also underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of the findings.

Main outcomes

Aneuploid embryos and euploid embryos

Fifteen studies were included to compare the discrepancy rates between aneuploid and euploid embryos. The results demonstrated that the discrepancy rate for aneuploid embryos was significantly higher than that for euploid embryos (OR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.59–4.99, I2 = 24.2%, P = 0.0014, Fig. 2a). Subsequent analysis examined the differences in discrepancy rates between whole-chromosome aneuploidy, segmental aneuploidy, and euploidy. The findings indicated that both types of aneuploidy exhibited higher discrepancy rates compared to euploidy, with segmental aneuploidy showing particularly elevated rates. In analyzing the discordance rates between TE and ICM/WB results, we observed high heterogeneity among studies investigating aneuploid embryos and their subtypes. Therefore, a random-effects model was applied. In contrast, studies on euploid embryo discordance showed low heterogeneity, and thus a fixed-effects model was used to evaluate the discordance rate.

Discussion

This study conducted a comprehensive analysis of the discrepancies between trophectoderm (TE) biopsy and inner cell mass/whole blastocyst (ICM/WB) analysis in the detection of euploidy and aneuploidy. The findings revealed a low discordance rate for euploid detection (2.6%), whereas the rate increased substantially for aneuploid detection (9.2%). Notably, this discrepancy was even more pronounced in the detection of segmental aneuploidies (OR = 9.31, 95%CI: 6.20–13.98, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001, Fig. 2c), with a discordance rate reaching 17.4%.

At the blastocyst stage, embryonic cells differentiate into the ICM and TE, where the ICM develops into the fetus and the TE forms the placenta; both compartments exhibit inherent heterogeneity. Therefore, when there is chromosomal discordance between TE and ICM, the genetic status of the ICM more accurately reflects the true fetal genotype, particularly in cases where TE biopsy indicates aneuploidy while the ICM is euploid [51–53]. WB analysis, by evaluating the entire embryo—including all cells from both ICM and TE—provides a more comprehensive assessment of chromosomal status and can mitigate errors arising from the limited sampling of TE biopsy (typically only 5–10 cells) [10, 54]. In this study, ICM/WB was employed as the gold standard to systematically investigate the discrepancies between TE biopsy and ICM/WB in the detection of euploidy and aneuploidy.

Huang et al. demonstrated that TE biopsy can objectively reflect the genetic status of the ICM in aneuploid embryos [9]. However, existing studies comparing TE biopsy with ICM results have reported a relatively high discordance rate in aneuploid embryos [35, 39, 46]. Notably, multiple studies have reported cases where embryos diagnosed as aneuploid were transferred, yet subsequent prenatal diagnosis revealed euploidy and resulted in successful live births [21, 22, 24, 55]. Despite this, the current preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) system lacks a systematic analysis addressing the misdiagnosis of aneuploidy. Therefore, this study included 22 investigations to, for the first time, explore the discordance between TE biopsy and ICM/whole blastocyst (WB) results in aneuploid embryos. The analysis revealed a significant difference in discordance rates between euploid and aneuploid embryos (OR = 2.82, 95%CI: 1.59–4.99, Fig. 2a), with minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 24.2%). These findings indicate that, compared to euploid embryos, aneuploid embryos—whether exhibiting whole-chromosome or segmental abnormalities—are more prone to misclassification, potentially leading to the loss of viable embryos. The study by Nidhee M. Sachdev et al. further corroborated this observation, reporting a significant difference in chromosomal concordance between euploid embryos (99.5%) and aneuploid embryos (95.7%) (P < 0.001) [11].

Of particular concern is the significantly elevated discordance observed in the detection of segmental aneuploidies, with a pooled odds ratio of 9.31 (95% CI: 6.20–13.98; I2 = 0%; P < 0.001, Fig. 2c) and a discordance rate reaching 17.4%. Sensitivity analyses comparing discordance rates between segmental and whole-chromosome aneuploidies revealed greater consistency and robustness in the segmental aneuploidy estimates, further highlighting their higher susceptibility to diagnostic misclassification.

Consequently, subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate discordance across various types of aneuploidies and PGT protocols. The results demonstrated that segmental aneuploidies are associated with significantly higher discordance rates compared to other aneuploidy categories. This elevated discordance may stem from the intrinsic complexity of the chromosomal rearrangements involved in segmental aneuploidies, as well as from technical limitations in current detection methodologies, both of which increase the risk of false-negative results or misclassification in segmental aneuploidy diagnosis [56–58].

PGT-SR is specifically designed to screen for chromosomal abnormalities caused by structural rearrangements, such as translocations and inversions, enabling effective identification of unbalanced embryos through TE biopsy combined with high-resolution detection techniques [59–61]. However, during testing, de novo abnormalities unrelated to the primary rearrangement may be detected, potentially resulting from embryonic de novo mutations or technical limitations [62–64]. Consequently, such findings require cautious interpretation and should be evaluated comprehensively alongside technical validation and parental reanalysis.

Notably, among studies investigating segmental aneuploidy discordance within the PGT-SR population, only one reported discordant cases, whereas the majority of segmental aneuploidy discordances were observed in the PGT-A cohort. To further elucidate this phenomenon, we analyzed studies comparing segmental aneuploidy discordance between PGT-A and PGT-SR populations, as well as those exclusively focused on segmental aneuploidy discordance in PGT-A. The analysis revealed a considerable rate of false-positive segmental aneuploidy detections in the PGT-A group. In contrast, PGT-SR patients possess a lower likelihood of producing normal gametes, and embryos more frequently exhibit segmental abnormalities derived from parental translocations [65–68]. Accordingly, incorporation of parental karyotype analysis can effectively reduce the incidence of segmental false positives. Moreover, in cases involving complex rearrangements or when distinguishing between normal embryos and carrier embryos is necessary, PGT-SR may employ targeted amplification, long-read sequencing, or haplotype analysis for deep sequencing of specific regions, thereby helping to minimize both false-positive and false-negative results [69–71].

Overall, these findings indicate that aneuploid embryos exhibit higher rates of diagnostic discordance compared to euploid embryos, thereby increasing the risk of misdiagnosis. Notably, segmental aneuploidies demonstrate significantly elevated discordance rates, likely stemming from the inherent complexity of their underlying mechanisms combined with limitations of current detection technologies. This effect is particularly pronounced in the PGT-A population.

Clinical suggestion

Our study results indicate that the accuracy of PGT in detecting aneuploidies has yet to meet expected standards, with this issue being particularly pronounced in the detection of segmental aneuploidies. The observed higher discordance rates in segmental aneuploidy detection suggest that embryos classified as segmentally abnormal may still retain reproductive potential. This is corroborated by reports from Andria Besser et al., who documented cases in which embryos initially diagnosed as segmental aneuploid ultimately resulted in successful live births of healthy infants [21].

Therefore, clinicians should exercise greater caution when managing embryos exhibiting segmental aneuploidy and avoid their exclusion based solely on a single biopsy result. For embryos suspected of segmental aneuploidy, performing a repeat biopsy to reassess the chromosomal status should be considered. Supporting this approach, Danilo et al. conducted a retrospective multicenter analysis of blastocyst re-biopsies. Their findings demonstrated that re-biopsied embryos maintained good survival rates and yielded successful live births, indicating that repeat biopsy is an effective strategy to reduce misdiagnosis and missed diagnoses [72].

However, embryo re-biopsy, as a second invasive procedure, carries risks of additional embryo damage, and its long-term impact on offspring health remains unclear [6, 70–73]. Moreover, Letizia and colleagues [74] analyzed outcomes following second freeze–thaw cycles and re-biopsies, finding inferior clinical outcomes compared to single procedures. Thus, the decision to perform re-biopsy requires careful weighing of the benefits against potential risks, considering both the embryo’s inherent vulnerabilities and the diagnostic accuracy gained.

Moreover, a diagnostic meta-analysis has demonstrated that combining trophectoderm biopsy with spent culture medium (SCM) analysis enhances the diagnostic efficiency of PGT while reducing the need for repeat biopsy and its associated embryo impact [12]. With ongoing advances in assisted reproductive technologies, more advanced and safer diagnostic methods are anticipated to emerge, potentially replacing conventional embryo biopsy. This evolution promises further improvements in diagnostic accuracy and safety, minimizing the occurrence of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis.

Advantages and limitations of research

A growing body of evidence indicates that the accuracy of PGT has not yet reached an optimal level, which may lead to misdiagnosis of certain embryos and consequently reduce the number of embryos available for assisted reproductive technologies (ART). This study is the first to use the ICM/WB as the gold standard to conduct an evidence-based analysis of the diagnostic accuracy of PGT in detecting aneuploidy. By identifying embryo types that are prone to misdiagnosis, this study provides valuable insights to improve diagnostic precision and minimize unnecessary embryo loss. Furthermore, the study compares inconsistency rates between different types of aneuploid embryos and euploid embryos, thereby enhancing the clinical applicability of PGT. These findings offer a scientific foundation for optimizing embryo selection strategies and improving outcomes in assisted reproductive treatments.

Although this study provides important evidence regarding the limitations of PGT in diagnosing aneuploidy, several limitations should be carefully considered. First, the overall and subgroup sample sizes were relatively limited, particularly in analyses involving specific PGT types or embryo subcategories. This may have reduced the statistical power and increased the uncertainty of pooled estimates. Second, most of the included studies were conducted in single-center settings, which may introduce biases due to variations in embryology laboratories, embryo culture conditions, biopsy protocols, and operator techniques, potentially affecting the reliability of TE biopsy results [73–75]. Third, while the retrospective study design offers valuable data for analysis, it may also introduce selection bias, especially in embryo selection and biopsy processes, and the influence of confounding factors cannot be completely ruled out. Moreover, because most studies were conducted in specific clinical settings or among selected patient populations, the external validity and generalizability of the conclusions to broader PGT populations remain limited. Therefore, future research should aim to incorporate larger sample sizes and prospective study designs to enhance the robustness and clinical applicability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study revealed a higher discordance rate between trophectoderm (TE) biopsy results and gold-standard diagnostic outcomes in aneuploid embryos compared with euploid embryos. Subgroup analyses further confirmed misdiagnosis among aneuploid embryos. Notably, our findings indicate that within the PGT-A population, segmental aneuploid embryos are associated with an increased risk of discarding potentially viable embryos. These results underscore the necessity for greater caution in interpreting TE biopsy diagnoses of segmental aneuploidy to minimize both misdiagnosis risk and the loss of transferable embryos.
