Shoulder balance in Lenke type 2 adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: the role of upper instrumented vertebrae selection and risk factor analysis
Authors: Chenkai Li, Xinwei Wang, Bingtai Han, You Du, Yiwei Zhao, Xiaohan Ye, Jianxiong Shen, Shengru Wang, Terry Jianguo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to assess the incidence of postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI), evaluate the role of upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) selection on postoperative shoulder balance and identify the risk factors for PSI in Lenke type 2 adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS).MethodsA retrospective case-matched analysis of 70 patients with Lenke type 2 AIS was performed. Patients were matched at a 1:1 ratio (35 T2 and 35 T3/4). Radiological and clinical outcomes were compared between the two groups. Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic analysis were used to further identify the risk factors for PSI.ResultsIn Lenke type 2 AIS patients, the overall incidence of PSI was 32.9%. Compared with the T3/4 group, the T2 group achieved significantly better proximal thoracic curve (PTC) correction (0.63 ± 0.23 vs. 0.53 ± 0.16, P = 0.041). At the last follow-up, the T2 group had a significantly lower clavicle-rib cage intersection (CRCI) (0.84 ± 1.85 mm) and T1 tilt (4.40 ± 3.08°) than the T3/4 group (CRCI: 3.61 ± 3.76 mm, P < 0.001; T1 tilt: 6.07 ± 3.39°, P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that the ratio of the PTC correction rate to the main thoracic curve (MTC) correction rate (PTC/MTC) was an independent risk factor for the PSI (OR = 0.015, P = 0.025).ConclusionIn Lenke type 2 AIS patients, the overall incidence of PSI was 32.9%. Compared with fusion to T3/4, fusion to T2 provided superior PTC correction and medial shoulder balance. The coordination between the PTC and MTC correction played a significant role in the maintenance of lateral shoulder balance.Keywords: Shoulder balance, Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, Upper instrumented vertebra, Postoperative shoulder imbalance

Background

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a three-dimensional spinal deformity characterized by coronal curvature and axial rotation. The primary goal of treatment for AIS patients is to correct the deformity and halt its progression. Moreover, improving patients’ self-image, which is closely associated with patient satisfaction, is also important. For Lenke type 2 AIS patients, postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI) is a potential complication after correction and may lead to poor cosmetic results, low patient satisfaction, and, in some cases, unplanned reoperation [1].

Previous studies have reported that the incidence of PSI in AIS ranges from 6.7 to 55.4% [2]. It has been reported that the selection of the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV), correction rate of the proximal thoracic curve (PTC) or main thoracic curve (MTC), skeletal maturity, preoperative shoulder balance, anchor type of the UIV, and postoperative complications are associated with the PSI [3–7]. Among them, the selection of the UIV is considered a significant factor for postoperative shoulder balance in Lenke type 2 AIS patients. Several guidelines for UIV selection, such as the Rose-Lenke [8], Ilharreborde [9], and Trobisch guidelines [10], have been proposed for achieving satisfactory postoperative shoulder balance. However, it is still difficult to maintain postoperative shoulder balance in practice, even if the relevant guidelines are met [11].

Currently, there are no studies concerning shoulder balance in a highly homogeneous cohort of patients with Lenke type 2 AIS. Thus, the purpose of the present study was to: (1) assess the incidence of PSI; (2) evaluate the influence of UIV selection on postoperative shoulder balance; and (3) identify the risk factors for PSI in a highly homogeneous cohort of Lenke type 2 AIS patients who were followed up to skeletal maturity.

Materials and methods

Demographics

This study protocol was approved by our institutional ethics committee review board, and informed consent was obtained from every participant and their patients. Patients who were diagnosed with Lenke type 2 AIS from July 2006 to July 2019 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) 10–18 years old at the time of index surgery; (2) Lenke type 2 AIS with right MTC and left PTC; (3) underwent posterior correction using pedicle screw only system; (4) the UIV was T2, T3, or T4; (5) a minimum of 2 years of postoperative radiological follow-up; and (6) age ≥ 14 years and the Risser sign reached grade IV or V at the final follow-up. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-AIS diagnosis; (2) previous history of spine surgery; (3) complications other than PSI, such as alignment complications; and (4) incomplete clinical or radiological data.

Patients were divided into two groups according to the UIV: the T2 group and the T3/4 group. Patients in the T2 group and T3/4 group were matched at a 1:1 ratio. The matching criteria included the following: (1) same sex; (2) age difference within 2 years; (3) same curvature type, including that of the PTC and the MTC; (4) difference in the Cobb angle in the 2 curves < 10°; (5) same type of preoperative shoulder balance; (6) same distal fusion level; and (7) difference in postoperative follow-up time < 6 months.

Radiological measurements

Standing whole-spine anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs, and side-bending radiographs were taken preoperatively. The standing AP and lateral radiographs were taken 7 days after surgery and at the last follow-up. The radiological parameters included coronal, sagittal, and shoulder balance-related parameters, which were compared between the T2 group and the T3/4 group.

The coronal parameters included the Cobb angles of the PTC, MTC, thoracolumbar/lumbar curve (TL/LC), and UIV tilt, as well as the C7-central sacral vertical line (CSVL). In addition, the flexibility, correction rate of the curve, and the ratio of PTC correction rate to MTC correction rate (PTC/MTC) were calculated. The sagittal parameters consisted of T2-T5 kyphosis, T5-T12 kyphosis, L1-S1 lordosis, and the sagittal vertical axis (SVA). The shoulder balance-related parameters included lateral shoulder balance parameters, and medial shoulder balance parameters. The former included the radiographic shoulder height (RSH), clavicle angle (CA), and coracoid process height (CPH). The latter included the clavicle-rib cage intersection (CRCI), first rib–clavicle height (FRCH), and T1 tilt. The following methods were used to measure shoulder balance-related parameters, where a positive value indicates that the left side is higher. PSI was defined as a difference of |RSH|>10 mm.

Patient-reported outcomes

SRS-22 scores were recorded preoperatively and at the most recent follow-up, and were compared between the T2 group and the T3/4 group.

PSI analysis

The incidence of PSI was calculated, and compared between the T2 group and the T3/4 group. Furthermore, patients were divided into two groups according to postoperative shoulder balance: the PSI group and the non-PSI group. The demographic characteristics and radiological parameters were evaluated to identify the risk factors for PSI.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analysis. Continuous variables are expressed herein as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are described as percentages. Independent Student’s t-test was used to compare parametric continuous variables and Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison of nonparametric continuous variables. The χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables. Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic analysis were used to identify factors associated with the PSI. Predictive variables that achieved a p-value < 0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into backward stepwise multiple regression analysis. The level of significance was set at < 0.05.

Results

Demographics

A total of 70 patients were enrolled, with a mean follow-up duration of 42.59 ± 20.68 months (24–96 months). There were 4 males (5.7%) and 66 females (94.3%). The mean age of the patients was 13.89 ± 1.69 years (11–18 years). The mean preoperative Risser sign was 3.13 ± 1.23 (grade 0–5) and 4.53 ± 0.50 (grade 4–5) at the final follow-up. Preoperatively, 38 patients (54.3%) had shoulder balance, and 32 patients (45.7%) had shoulder imbalance (12 with the high left shoulder, 37.5%; and 20 with the high right shoulder, 62.5%). There were 35 patients in the T2 group and 35 patients in the T3/4 group. No significant differences were found in demographics between the T2 group and the T3/4 group (Table 1).

PSI analysis

The incidence of PSI was 61.4% immediately after surgery, which decreased to 32.9% at the last follow-up. The incidences of PSI in the T2 group and T3/4 group were 57.1% (20/35) and 71.4% (25/35), respectively, immediately after surgery, and 31.4% (11/35) and 34.3% (12/35), respectively, at the last follow-up. There was no significant difference in the incidence of PSI between the two groups immediately after surgery or at the last follow-up (P > 0.05).

Univariate analysis showed that the correction rate of PTC, the ratio of the PTC correction rate to the MTC correction rate (PTC/MTC), preoperative shoulder balance, RSH, CA, and CPH may be predictive variables of the PSI (P < 0.10) (Table 6). The results of multivariate logistic regression revealed that PTC/MTC was an independent risk factor for PSI (OR = 0.015, P = 0.025) (Table 7).

Discussion

Shoulder balance is closely related to postoperative self-image and satisfaction in AIS patients. The UIV is considered a significant factor for the PSI. However, currently, there is still no consensus on the influence of the UIV selection on postoperative shoulder balance in Lenke type 2 AIS patients. In addition, there are no studies concerning shoulder balance in a highly homogeneous cohort of patients with Lenke type 2 AIS who were followed up to skeletal maturity. Therefore, we conducted a case-matched study to evaluate the influence of the UIV on postoperative shoulder balance and identify the risk factors for PSI. All patients in the present study had the same preoperative shoulder balance, same curvature type, comparable curve magnitude, and same instrumentation, and all of them reached skeletal maturity at the last follow-up. The present study revealed that for Lenke type 2 AIS patients: (1) the overall incidence of PSI was 32.9%; (2) compared with fusion to T3/4, fusion to T2 achieved better PTC correction and medial shoulder balance; and (3) PTC/MTC was an independent risk factor for PSI (OR = 0.015, P = 0.025).

The adjustment of postoperative shoulder balance is a dynamic process. The original balance is disrupted by the correction. Thus, the incidence of PSI increases significantly immediately after surgery. During the subsequent follow-up, a new shoulder balance will be achieved, thus the incidence of PSI will gradually decrease. It had been reported that a new shoulder balance is gradually achieved by 7 months after surgery [14]. Namikawa et al. [15] reported that 29% of Lenke type 1 AIS patients experienced PSI after immediate surgery, but most of them (95%) achieved shoulder balance 2 years after surgery. A similar phenomenon was also found in the present study. In the present study, the incidence of PSI was 61.4% immediately, but it decreased to 32.9% at the last follow-up, which was similar to previous studies [2]. The main reason for this phenomenon was that the lateral shoulder included two major components: bony structure and soft tissue. The bony structure included the sternoclavicular and acromioclavicular joints, whereas the soft tissue was mainly the shoulder girdle. Both of them were indirectly connected to the spine, leading to the effect of correction manoeuvres being unable to be directly transferred to these adjacent structures. Therefore, the lateral shoulder balance is not only affected by the correction of the curve but also influenced by gravity and trunk orientation [16], which can explain why the lateral shoulder balance changes over time.

Previous studies have explored the relationship between the UIV and postoperative shoulder balance, but the results are inconsistent. Yang et al. [17] reported that selecting T2 as the UIV can improve postoperative medial shoulder balance only in Lenke type 2 patients. Another study reported that extension to T2 was recommended for patients with a flexible MTC and greater bone maturity to prevent PSI in Lenke type 2 AIS patients with left PTC and right MTC [18]. However, Brooks et al. [19] conducted a multicentre prospective study including 626 patients (189 patients in the T2 group, 205 in the T3 group, and 232 in the T4 group) with a minimum of 2 years of follow-up. The authors demonstrated that, regardless of preoperative shoulder balance, fusion to T4 can result in better shoulder balance than fusion to T2 or T3, and selecting T2 as the UIV did not guarantee postoperative shoulder balance. The present study revealed that, compared with fusion to T3/4, fusion to T2 achieved better PTC correction and medial shoulder balance at the last follow-up. However, no significant differences were found in the radiological parameters, lateral shoulder balance parameters, or SRS-22 scores between the two groups at the last follow-up. The main reason for the different outcomes between the lateral shoulder and the medial shoulder is the different anatomical structures. The medial shoulder is composed mainly of bony structures that are influenced mainly by curve correction. However, as mentioned above, owing to the special anatomy of the lateral shoulder, the correction had a minimal effect on the sternoclavicular and acromioclavicular joints and shoulder girdle. Therefore, the medial shoulder balance was determined mainly by curve correction, whereas the lateral shoulder balance was influenced by both correction and trunk balance.

Recently, several studies have emphasized the importance of the relative relationship between PTC and MTC correction for shoulder balance. Sielatycki et al. [3] reported that when the PTC was corrected > 52.0%, PSI was observed in only 20% of Lenke type 1 and type 2 AIS patients. In addition, the authors claimed that the correlation of the correction rate between PTC and MTC was more important than the UIV selection in Lenke type 1 and type 2 AIS patients: 59% of patients had PSI when the correction rate of the PTC was < 52.0% and the correction rate of the MTC was > 54.0%. Moorthy et al. [20] reported that relative deformity correction was a significant consideration for avoiding PSI after correction surgery in AIS patients. Similarly, the present study revealed that PTC/MTC was an independent risk factor for the PSI (OR = 0.015, P = 0.025). Unlike other risk factors reported in previous studies, such as skeletal maturity or preoperative shoulder balance, this index can be adjusted by surgeons intraoperatively through compression and distraction manoeuvres. With the development of modern techniques, curve correction can be achieved by 5–70% [16]. This provides surgeons with more flexibility for adjustment to achieve ideal shoulder balance. As mentioned earlier, the adjustment of lateral shoulder balance is a dynamic process, and both the initial correction and postoperative self-compensation could influence the lateral shoulder balance. However, the body’s self-compensation is often limited. Therefore, an appropriate PTC/MTC is a prerequisite for postoperative lateral shoulder balance. During surgery, an intraoperative radiological examination to evaluate the PTC/MTC is recommended.

In addition to UIV selection and the PTC/MTC, previous studies have reported that the anchor type of the UIV and postoperative complications are also associated with PSI [4–7]. Akazawa et al. [6] and Kuroya et al. [7] reported that hooks at the UIV can help adjust the shoulder balance over the long term in Lenke type 1 AIS. The authors demonstrated that the hooks could provide weaker biomechanical stiffness to the proximal anchor, which allows autoregulation of postoperative shoulder balance. Another study reported that postoperative distal adding-on can compensate for PSI, improving shoulder balance during follow-up [5]. However, distal adding-on is associated with low patient satisfaction and should be avoided postoperatively.

For the first time, the present study evaluated the PSI in a highly homogeneous cohort of patients with Lenke type 2 AIS. However, this study also has several limitations. First, the present study was limited by its retrospective design. Second, all patients in the present study underwent pedicle screw-only fixation, and the effects of the instrumentation type on PSI were not evaluated in the present study. Most importantly, owing to the strict inclusion and matching criteria, the sample size of the present study was relatively small, thereby limiting more detailed subgroup analysis based on UIV. It is necessary for subsequent studies to compare the impact of selecting UIV at T2, T3, and T4 on shoulder balance separately.

Conclusions

For Lenke type 2 AIS patients, the overall incidence of PSI after correction was 32.9%. Compared with fusion to T3/4, fusion to T2 achieved better PTC correction postoperatively and medial shoulder balance. The coordination between PTC and MTC correction played a significant role in the maintenance of lateral shoulder balance.
