Authors’ response to “Context, consent, and continuity: Strengthening dementia diagnosis communication”
Authors: Cristina Festari, Cristina Bonomini, Claudio Singh Solorzano, Sandra Rosini, Giovanni B Frisoni, Michela Pievani
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We appreciate the opportunity to respond to Professor Prazeres’ commentary on our study exploring the psychological impact of Alzheimer's disease (AD) diagnosis disclosure on informal caregivers. 1 We embrace his view that communicating a diagnosis of dementia should not be considered a single event but rather a process requiring careful preparation, empathic delivery, and ongoing continuity. His reflections complement our empirical findings and contribute to a broader dialogue on how diagnosis disclosure can be both person centered and supportive.

The observation regarding the influence of the disclosure context is highly relevant. The physical and relational setting in which a diagnosis is communicated is an integral component of the overall disclosure experience and may modulate both its immediate and long‐term emotional impact. Our study did not assess the physical or relational context of communication due to its retrospective design and the focus on caregivers’ emotional responses to the memory of diagnosis disclosure rather than on the communicative process itself. Nevertheless, existing evidence indicates that factors such as privacy, time availability, interpersonal dynamics, and modality of interaction substantially affect how life‐altering information, including a dementia diagnosis, is perceived. 2 , 3

The reflections on the Italian context add important ethical and cultural depth to the interpretation of our findings. In Italy, diagnostic disclosure is often directed primarily to caregivers rather than to the person with dementia, a practice that, although intended to protect patients, may inadvertently amplify caregivers’ emotional burden and sense of responsibility. Our results reinforce the need for more transparent, culturally sensitive, and dyad‐centered approaches to communication, in line with international recommendations advocating shared disclosure and respect for patient autonomy whenever feasible. 4

Empathic communication and continuity of care are crucial for effective dementia management. Clinical experience shows that the emotional impact of diagnosis disclosure often persists beyond the initial consultation, shaping how patients and families adapt to the condition. The persistence of stress reactions observed in our study highlights the importance of structured follow‐up and accessible psychological counseling for both patients and caregivers. Sustained psychological and medical support can help to process a dementia diagnosis, integrate it into everyday life, and maintain emotional balance. We particularly value the emphasis on shared responsibility across levels of care; only through a coordinated, integrated, and person‐centered approach can diagnostic disclosure become not an endpoint but the beginning of collaborative care. However, this vision of early and continuous support must also recognize the emotional, socioeconomic, and practical barriers that families often face. The literature has described this as the “paradox of need,” in which caregivers most affected by stress are often the least likely to acknowledge their own need for help or to seek assistance before exhaustion occurs. 5 Future efforts should therefore aim not only to refine research methodologies (i.e., combining quantitative measures of psychological distress with qualitative assessments of the communication process), but also to strengthen awareness among clinicians and families of the preventive value of early, sustained, and coordinated psychosocial care.

In conclusion, we are grateful to Professor Prazeres for the opportunity to engage in this constructive exchange, which underscores the interplay among context, consent, and continuity, and how effective diagnostic communication should not only convey information, but also foster resilience and partnership among clinicians, patients, and families.
