The Double-Double Row: A Technique for Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Wyatt H Buchalter, Ryan J Whalen, Natalie Cortes, Ajay C Kanakamedala, Madison Hauge, Nate J Dickinson, Ryan A DoPico, Matthew T Provencher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRotator cuff repair is one of the most common orthopaedic procedures. The double-row repair is commonly employed to repair the supraspinatus (SSP) because it effectively restores the tendon footprint, provides mechanical stability, and reduces gap formation required for effective healing. Despite this, retear rates remain high. Augmenting the double row with additional sutures and suture anchors at the robust anterior aspect of the SSP may improve construct strength and reduce the chance of repair failure. This Technical Note describes an SSP repair technique termed the double-double row to provide additional anterior footprint compression.

Rotator cuff tears are one of the most common musculotendinous injuries seen and treated.1 Surgical treatment may be indicated after failed conservative treatment, acute full-thickness traumatic tears in young patients, or in cases of sudden or severe weakness, indicating that a partial-thickness tear may have progressed to a full-thickness tear.2 Rotator cuff tears most commonly involve the supraspinatus (SSP) tendon, and the surgical technique to treat these tears depends on many factors, including tear geometry, soft tissue delamination, tissue quality, and rotator cuff retraction.3,4 The goal of rotator cuff repair surgery is to restore native anatomy to facilitate tendon-bone healing. The ideal repair construct has high fixation strength under maximal and cyclic loading with minimal gap formation.5

The double-row transosseous-equivalent repair technique is commonly employed and has been shown to have superior footprint restoration, increased construct strength, and decreased gap formation when compared with single-row repair.5 Despite these advantages, prior studies have reported retear rates of 21% between a 1- and 2-year follow-up with double-row transosseous-equivalent repair.6 The most common mechanism of repair failure in both biomechanical and clinical studies has been shown to be at the suture-tendon interface.7 Additional sutures and suture anchors in the repair may increase the surface area at the suture-tendon interface and may reduce the incidence of repair failure.8 Tendon-bone healing may also be improved with superior compression of the native footprint area, specifically the anterior aspect of the SSP tendon, which is more robust than the posterior half.9 In this report and associated video (Video 1), we describe a technique for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair with a double-double row pattern utilizing an additional anterior row of suture anchors to provide additional compression to the anterior aspect of the SSP tendon.

Surgical Technique

Patient Evaluation, Imaging, and Indications

Surgical repair of the SSP is indicated after failed conservative treatment for partial-thickness tears, acute traumatic tears, and chronic symptomatic tears if the tear pattern is amenable to repair. Typically, there is shoulder pain, weakness, and difficulty performing daily activities. Physical examination may reveal a positive empty can test, zero-degree abduction test, and supraspinatus stress test. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging may show a near-full-thickness to full-thickness tear of the SSP at the anterior edge with a significant span of around 10 mm with surrounding tendinosis (Fig 1). Patient Positioning

The patient may be placed in the typical beach-chair position with the head of the bed elevated to approximately 60°. An arm positioner can be utilized to maintain the arm in a neutral position.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy Portal and SSP Footprint Preparation

An initial diagnostic arthroscopy is performed utilizing a standard posterior portal and anterosuperior portal in the rotator interval with a 5-mm cannula. Any intra-articular pathology is addressed as necessary. A biceps tenotomy for future tenodesis is typically performed, given the frequency of concomitant biceps tendon pathology in this population, and this is also helpful to avoid incarceration of the biceps tendon in the anterior anchors, which will be placed in the bicipital groove.

The rotator cuff is identified and debrided or tagged as necessary to ease in identification of the tear from the subacromial space. The arthroscope is then introduced into the subacromial space. A posterolateral portal, which is just posterior to the midline of the acromion, is localized with a spinal needle and then made, and subacromial decompression, including bursectomy and partial acromioplasty, is performed as needed. Attention is turned to the SSP, the tear pattern is assessed, and any tendon retraction is noted. A second anterolateral portal is made up against the acromion after localizing with a spinal needle to confirm a satisfactory trajectory for anchor placement, and an 8-mm PassPort cannula (Arthrex) is inserted into this anterolateral portal. The greater tuberosity is prepared by a high-speed burr by lightly decorticating the tuberosity to achieve a good bed of bleeding bone. The SSP is then mobilized and reduced into a good anatomic position with a cuff grasper.

Double-Double Row Repair

First, two or three 2.6-mm knotless FiberTak RC anchors (Arthrex) are placed approximately 1 cm apart for the medial row, as shown in Figure 2. The decision to use 2 or 3 medial row anchors is based on the size and morphology of the tear. If 3 anchors are used, the SutureTape suture (Arthrex) from the posterior anchor is removed. A FastPass Scorpion SL Suture Passer (Arthrex) anterograde suture passing device is used to pass the preloaded FiberTape and SutureTape suture (Arthrex) through the SSP, as shown in Figure 3. The SutureTapes may be tied medially in a horizontal fashion to obtain anatomic reduction, bridging the medial anchors together. Next, the standard double-row suture bridge technique is completed by securing each FiberTape strand from each medial anchor into 2 lateral 4.75-mm SwiveLock anchors (Arthrex) (Fig 4) in the same fashion as a previously described technique.10 Then, to complete the double-double row construct, SutureTapes from each medial anchor are retrieved and passed through the eyelet of a 4.75-mm SwiveLock anchor. If 3 medial anchors are used and additional anterior footprint coverage is desired, SutureTapes from the middle and anterior medial row anchors may be placed through the eyelets of two 4.75-mm SwiveLock anchors. One or 2 sockets are subsequently created in the bicipital groove into which the anchors are placed to create anterior footprint coverage. The final double-double row construct with 3 medial anchors and 2 anterior anchors is shown in Figure 5. The repair is assessed for anatomic reduction and footprint compression intra-articularly and from the subacromial space. The arm is taken through passive range of motion to ensure range of motion and a solid repair. All instruments are removed, wounds are copiously irrigated and closed in a standard fashion, and the arm is placed in a padded abduction sling. Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

Postoperatively, the patient is kept nonweightbearing with the upper extremity in a padded abduction sling for 6 weeks. Physical therapy begins immediately with passive range-of-motion exercises for the first 4 weeks. At week 5, active range-of-motion exercises are begun along with open-chain proprioception and low-load static stretches. At week 9, strengthening and advanced proprioception, including initial progressive resistance strengthening, are begun. Patients are typically allowed to return to low-level recreational activities at approximately 4 months postoperatively and cleared for full return to activities with no restrictions at 6 months postoperatively.

Discussion

This Technical Note describes the double-double row technique for SSP repair. SSP tears commonly occur through age-dependent tissue degeneration or from acute traumatic shoulder injuries.1 Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair may be indicated after failed conservative treatment or in acute traumatic tears.2 While the double-row transosseous-equivalent technique is effective for repairing the SSP, construct failure rates remain high at 21% at both 3- to 6-month and 12- to 24-month follow-up due to suture-tendon pull-through and insufficient footprint compression.6 The anterior half of the SSP is more robust than the posterior half, and this provides the rationale for the double-double row technique, which adds sutures and suture anchors anteriorly to reduce stress on the suture-tendon interface and increase footprint compression, with the ultimate goal of decreasing the incidence of retear and gap formation and improving footprint compression and tendon-to-bone healing. Drawings of the standard double-row transosseous-equivalent technique and the double-double row technique are shown in Figure 3.

There is a scarcity of data on double-double row SSP repair. Clinical outcomes of the standard double-row transosseous-equivalent technique for SSP repair have been shown to be favorable. Johannsen et al.11 reported minimum 10-year outcomes and survivorship of arthroscopic double-row rotator cuff repair, showing statistically significant increases in American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons and 12-Item Short Form Health Survey physical component summary scores from the preoperative baseline scores. They also showed 94.4% survival. However, failure rates remained at 5.5%. A study by Carbonel et al.12 revealed that double-row repairs for large and massive rotator cuff tears significantly improved postoperative shoulder strength at a 2-year follow-up, albeit only to 72% to 78% compared to the unaffected contralateral shoulder. A recent study by Yau et al.,13 looking at 2-year clinical outcomes of intact rotator cuff repair using a double-row modified Mason-Allen stitch against a standard double-row suture bridge, found that the Mason-Allen group had a more substantial improvement across all clinical outcomes, consisting of the visual analog scale, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, and active shoulder forward flexion. Although these results are limited by only including intact repairs, they suggest the potential benefit from advanced repair methods over the traditional double-row repair. A similar study by Itoigawa et al.14 showed significantly lower retear rates of 7.7% in the combined suture bridge with Mason-Allen group versus 28.0% in the standard suture bridge group at a 2-year follow-up, suggesting more robust rotator cuff repair techniques may further reduce retear rates. Although these outcomes are overall favorable, there remains room for further improvement, which the double-double row technique may help achieve. Future studies that evaluate the clinical outcomes of the double-double row repair are warranted.

There are some key considerations when performing a double-double row rotator cuff repair. First, since this technique requires placing anchors into the bicipital groove, it may be advantageous to perform a biceps tenodesis, if indicated, to create space for anchor placement into the groove and to avoid biceps tendon incarceration in the repair. Additionally, it is important to apply the correct construct to the respective tear pattern. For example, certain tear patterns like an L-type tear may require additional anterior fixation, whereas in others, a more traditional repair may suffice. Thus, this technique should be used only in cases where SSP tears extend anteriorly where the anterior margin of the SSP is avulsed from the footprint.

Supplementary Data

Video 1

We are presenting our technique for a double-double row for an arthroscopic rotator cuff repair using 2 medial row anchors and 1 anterior row anchor.

This is a very active 68-year-old male patient who enjoys skiing and daily exercise. He has occasional shoulder pain that has been worsening. He describes the pain as sharp and achy. He had a left acromioclavicular joint separation at 25 years old, which was treated nonoperatively. He recently had a left subacromial injection, which gave him temporary relief for about a week, but the pain soon returned in a more severe manner. Additionally, he has tried physical therapy and medications, including naproxen, to no avail.

On a physical examination, he showed external rotation of 35°, with normal movement for internal rotation, flexion, and abduction. He had full muscular strength for forward flexion, external rotation, and internal rotation but slightly decreased abduction strength, where he experienced pain.

The magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography scans show a near-full-thickness bursal-sided supraspinatus tear at the anterior leading edge. The tear spans approximately 10 mm in width and is surrounded by moderated tendinosis.

The patient was placed in a beach-chair position with the head elevated to about 60°. A Trimano arm positioner was used to keep the arm in a neutral position. An initial diagnostic arthroscopy was performed utilizing a standard posterior portal and mid-glenoid portal with a 5-mm cannula in the rotator interval. A combination of a basket, shaver, and radiofrequency wand was used to debride the intra-articular portion of the shoulder joint.

Due to the instability, significant synovitis, and scarring of the long head of the biceps tendon, a biceps tenotomy was performed to prepare for a mini-open biceps tenodesis after the rotator cuff repair was completed.

Attention was then drawn to the supraspinatus, where a tear was noted at the greater tuberosity with 1.0 mm of retraction. A second anterolateral portal was made up against the acromion, and an 8-mm PassPort (Arthrex) cannula was inserted. To prepare the greater tuberosity, a high-speed burr was used to lightly decorticate the tuberosity to induce bleeding bone. The supraspinatus was then mobilized and reduced into a good anatomic position with a cuff grasper.

To start the double-double row repair, two 2.6-mm knotless FiberTak anchors were placed about 1 cm apart for the medial row. Then, a Scorpion (Arthrex) anterograde suture passing device was used to pass the FiberTak (Arthrex) suture through the supraspinatus. After passing 2 strands in a horizontal fashion to obtain anatomic reduction, the suture was then tied medially. The anchors must be daisy-chained to one another to create a bridge. Next, the traditional double-row suture bridge technique was performed by taking a strand from each medial anchor and securing it laterally with two 4.75-mm SwiveLock (Arthrex) anchors. To complete the double-double row, sutures from the medial anchors were daisy-chained to an anterior-based 4.75-mm SwiveLock anchor in the bicipital groove to create anterior footprint coverage. The repair was then assessed for anatomic reduction and footprint compression both intra-articularly and from the subacromial space. Proper range of motion was ensured by taking the repair through passive range-of-motion tests. Finally, all instruments were removed, and wounds were copiously irrigated and closed in a standard fashion. The arm was put in a padded abduction sling for 4 to 5 weeks. Due to the biceps tenodesis, resisted elbow flexion was restricted for the first 3 weeks postoperatively and limited to 10 lbs for weeks 3 to 6.
