Early blood pressure lowering and cerebral oedema in thrombolysis-treated stroke: secondary analysis of the ENCHANTED trial
Authors: Feifeng Liu, Xinwen Ren, Chen Chen, Menglu Ouyang, Qiang Li, Xia Wang, Guobin Zhang, Luyun Zhang, Leibo Liu, Shoujiang You, Richard I Lindley, Thompson Robinson, Yi Sui, Gang Li, Craig Anderson, Lili Song, on behalf of the ENCHANTED Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeControversy persists over the balance of benefits and harms of early intensive blood pressure (BP) lowering in thrombolysis-treated acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) patients. The BP-control arm of the Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke Study (ENCHANTED) (n=2196) showed that compared with guideline-recommended management (systolic BP (SBP)<180 mm Hg), intensive BP lowering did not improve functional outcome despite reducing intracranial haemorrhage. We aimed to evaluate the relationship between the BP parameters and cerebral oedema in ENCHANTED BP-control arm participants.MethodsENCHANTED was an international, multicentre, open-label, blinded outcome assessed, randomised controlled trial in thrombolysed AIS patients. All baseline and follow-up brain images were centrally analysed using standardised techniques and planimetric software by expert readers blind to clinical details. The severity of cerebral oedema was measured on a 7-point scale that ranged from 0 (no oedema) to 6 (most severe oedema); the primary outcome of ‘severe cerebral oedema’ defined by scores 4–6.Results1477 (67.3%) participants (mean age 67.7 years, 39.6% female) with available cerebral oedema data were included. Patients with a larger magnitude of SBP reduction in 1 hour had a lower odds of severe cerebral oedema (adjusted OR 0.72 per 10 mm Hg, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.98; p=0.04), whereas those with greater SBP variability between 1 hour and 24 hours had a shift towards worse cerebral oedema (adjusted OR 1.27 per 10 mm Hg, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.60; p=0.04).ConclusionAlthough the effect size is modest, achieving rapid lowering of SBP within 1 hour and then maintaining stable SBP over 24 hours appears to be associated with less cerebral oedema in thrombolysis-treated AIS patients.Clinical trial registrationThe trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01422616).

Introduction

Cerebral oedema is a complication of acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) that occurs in approximately one quarter of patients who receive thrombolysis treatment13 and is an independent predictor of poor outcome.2 4 Although elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP) is associated with cerebral oedema,5 6 there is uncertainty as to whether it is simply reactive to, or has causal effects on, the dysregulation of cerebral blood flow within the ischaemic penumbra.7 BP lowering treatment may therefore either attenuate the degree of reperfusion injury or exacerbate cerebral oedema and infarction.8 9 Current guidelines remain conservative in their longstanding recommendation to achieve a SBP <180 mm Hg in the management of thrombolysis-treated AIS patients.912 A recent secondary analysis of the international Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke Study (ENCHANTED) BP-control arm compared intensive versus guideline-recommended BP lowering and found no significant difference in overall cerebral swelling between treatment groups.13 However, it remains unclear whether specific SBP patterns—such as the magnitude and variability of SBP—are independently associated with cerebral oedema severity, irrespective of randomised treatment allocation. We therefore undertook this post-hoc analysis to determine these associations and to provide potential clues for future research and individualised BP management.

Methods

Study design

The design and main results of the ENCHANTED BP-control arm, an open-label, blinded-outcome assessed, randomised controlled trial, have been published.14 15 In brief, a total of 2196 thrombolysis-eligible AIS patients with elevated SBP (>150 mm Hg) within 6 hours after symptom onset participated at 110 hospitals in 15 countries between March 2012 and April 2018. Eligible patients were randomly assigned to intensive SBP lowering (target <140 mm Hg within 1 hour and maintained for 72 hours) or guideline-recommended BP management group (target <180 mm Hg). Locally available BP-lowering agents were used at the discretion of treating clinicians in both groups. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee at each participating hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their approved surrogates.

Procedures

BP was monitored frequently according to the ENCHANTED trial protocol: every 15 min during the first hour, hourly from 1 hour to 6 hours, every 6 hours from 6 hours to 24 hours and twice daily from 2 days to 7 days (or until hospital discharge or death, if earlier). For each participant, summary measures of SBP control were attained (mean between 1 hour and 24 hours); variability (SD of mean SBP between 1 hour and 24 hours) and magnitude reduction of SBP within 1 hour or 24 hours (difference between baseline and lowest levels within 1 hour and within 24 hours). These parameters were chosen based on the BP management protocol in the ENCHANTED BP-arm trial, which aimed to lower SBP to less than 140 mm Hg within 1 hour and then maintain this level. They reflect the magnitude, variability and stability of the SBP reduction. Severity of neurological impairment was defined according to scores on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Scale, recorded at baseline, 24 hours and 72 hours. Details of the characteristics of participants were collected at randomisation. Treatment details were collected at 24 hours and 7 days (or at hospital discharge or death if earlier).

CT and MRI scans performed within 24 hours post randomisation (as part of routine assessment of cerebral infarction and reperfusion injury), as well as those taken at other time points based on clinical necessity at each site, were uploaded in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format to a secure, purpose-built web-based system for central analysis at The George Institute for Global Health in Sydney, Australia.

Severity of cerebral oedema was graded according to a 7-point scale: 0=no swelling; 1=effacement of the sulci overlying the infarct; 2=1 + minor effacement of adjacent lateral ventricle; 3=1 + complete effacement of lateral ventricle; 4=1 + effacement of the lateral and third ventricle; 5=4 + shift of the midline away from the side of the ventricle; 6=5 + effacement of the basal cisterns.16 Four experts in radiology and neurology were trained in the use of MiStar software V.3.2 (Apollo Medical Imaging, Melbourne, VIC, Australia) and instruction materials used in the third International Stroke Trial (IST-3).17 All assessors were blind to each participant’s treatment group, outcomes and time of scan. During the reading process, 10% of all scans were double reviewed to assess inter-rater quality. For any disagreement, the adjudicated results of a senior reader with over 10 years of experience in brain imaging were considered as being final for inclusion in analysis.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was severe cerebral oedema, defined as scores 4–6 on the oedema severity scale. This threshold reflects compression of central ventricular structures (third ventricle, basal cisterns or midline shift), which has been shown to be more strongly associated with malignant cerebral oedema and poor outcome.18 19 The secondary outcomes were a shift in oedema scores towards greater cerebral oedema from baseline and any cerebral oedema assessed on a follow-up CT/MRI scan post randomisation. The scan closest to 72 hours post randomisation was used in the analyses in situations where there were multiple follow-up scans for a participant.

Statistical analysis

Univariate associations of the four summary measures of SBP control (continuous measures) and each cerebral oedema outcome are reported as an OR and 95% CI per 10 mm Hg SBP increase. The following variables were selected based on clinical relevance or prior literature for inclusion in the multivariable analysis: age, sex, severity of neurological impairment (baseline NIHSS score), a binary variable for any prior vascular risk factor (hypertension, stroke, coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus or atrial fibrillation), AIS pathological subtype, baseline SBP, randomised treatment, use of mannitol, any intracranial haemorrhage and infarct volume on the follow-up scan. For the ordinal outcome of oedema shift (graded 0–6, with higher scores indicating more severe oedema), we used ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds model) after confirming that the proportional odds assumption was satisfied. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed, including a simplified model adjusting only for key baseline variables (baseline NIHSS, baseline SBP and randomised treatment allocation) and analyses excluding patients with ICH; these were considered exploratory. Categorical SBP summary measures were also used for descriptive purposes and reported as comparisons between each category and the reference category. Missing data (~11%) were handled by complete case analysis; no multiple imputation was performed. All tests were two-sided using a nominal level of α of 5%. All analyses were performed with SAS V.9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) for Windows (IBM Corp) or R V.3.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Of 2196 randomised participants in the ENCHANTED BP-control arm, 628 were excluded due to no repeat scans, 81 were excluded for poor imaging quality and 10 were excluded due to unknown imaging scan time. Finally, a total of 1477 (67.3%) with available follow-up scans (CT used as follow-up scans 68.1%) were included in this study. The patient screening process is detailed in figure 1. Among the 1477 included patients (mean age 67.7 (12.1) years; female 39.6%; Asian ethnicity 64.7%), the median baseline NIHSS was 8 (IQR 5–13) at a median time from symptom onset to follow-up scan of 27.4 (IQR 24.6–31.9) hours (the distribution of time from onset to follow-up scan was shown in online supplemental Figure S1), the median final infarct volume was 1.7 (IQR 0.0–20.1) mL and 8.9% had any intracranial haemorrhage.

1006 patients (68.1%) underwent CT as the follow-up imaging modality. There were 326 (22.3%) participants with evidence of cerebral oedema, with a distribution of 176 (12.0%), 105 (7.2%), 10 (0.7%) and 35 (2.4%) for scores of 1, 2, 3 and 4–6, respectively. Mean attained and variability of SBP during 1–24 hours were 143.8 (SD 12.6) mm Hg and 12.4 (SD 6.3) mm Hg, respectively; and the magnitude of SBP reduction in the first hour and over 24-hours post randomisation was 22.4 (SD 15.5) and 37.5 (SD 15.8) mm Hg, respectively. Compared with patients without severe cerebral oedema, those with severe oedema had higher highest SBP over 24 hours, greater attained SBP (1–24 hours) and increased SBP variability (1–24 hours) (online supplemental Table S5). Mean SBP trajectories up to day 7 differed between the intensive and guideline-recommended BP lowering groups, as illustrated in online supplemental figure S2.

After adjusting for multiple covariates that included infarct volume and haemorrhagic transformation, the association between magnitude SBP reduction in 1 hour and severe cerebral oedema remained significant (adjusted OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.0.53 to 0.98; p=0.036), a greater magnitude SBP reduction over 1 hour was linked to a lower odds of severe cerebral oedema. While a larger SBP variability in 1–24 hours was associated with a higher risk of score shift towards worse cerebral oedema (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.60; p=0.040), there were no significant associations for other SBP parameters in relation to the severity of cerebral oedema. Sensitivity analyses showed consistent results. In the simplified model (online supplemental table S3), adjusted for baseline NIHSS, baseline SBP and treatment allocation, results were consistent with the primary model, with some associations appearing stronger. Results remained virtually unchanged after further adjustment for intravenous antihypertensive use or for baseline CT early ischaemic changes (online supplemental table S3). In analyses excluding patients with ICH (online supplemental table S4), SBP variability (1–24 hour) remained significantly associated with any oedema and with the ordinal oedema shift, while the association between 1-hour SBP reduction and severe oedema was attenuated.

Heterogeneity was observed in the relationship between the categorical classification of the SBP summary measures and cerebral oedema. Discussion

In this analysis of the ENCHANTED BP-control arm database, we have shown that a larger reduction in SBP over the first hour after thrombolysis treatment for AIS patients is associated with a lower likelihood of severe cerebral oedema. Moreover, a larger SBP variability in the post-thrombolysis period of 1 to 24 hours and a greater magnitude reduction of SBP in the first 24 hours were associated with a greater risk of worse shift in brain oedema. These findings may provide some indications for BP management in controlling cerebral oedema in this patient population.

There were no specific BP recommendations on the management of cerebral oedema guidelines for large variation and lack of data from randomised controlled trials.20 21 Two previous observational studies did not find any association between SBP, either prethrombolysis or post thrombolysis and cerebral oedema, but this might have been due to methodological limitations including insufficient SBP measurements.1 2 The current results show that a greater magnitude of SBP reduction in the first 24 hours and a larger SBP variability in the 1–24 hours are associated with a higher risk of worse shift in the degree of cerebral oedema, providing additional insight beyond a recent ENCHANTED secondary analysis,13 which reported no significant difference in cerebral swelling between intensive and guideline-recommended BP lowering and suggesting that the patterns of BP change—rather than treatment strategy alone—may be relevant to oedema risk. These results confirm the need to maintain a stable SBP over the 24 hours post-thrombolysis period, as observed after endovascular thrombectomy (EVT)22 and undifferentiated acute stroke.23 Although a post-hoc analysis of the IST-3 suggested that a larger decline in SBP in thrombolysis-treated AIS patients was non-significantly associated with a lower degree of symptomatic swelling of the cerebral infarct.24 It was likely that IST3 had few SBP measurements and only a small degree of SBP variability, with an average reduction of only 13–15 mm Hg in SBP from baseline to 24 hours. Thus, our data extend this finding through a more comprehensive evaluation of SBP parameters in a specific clinical trial of different intensities of BP lowering, where the mean reduction in SBP from a mean baseline of 165.8 mm Hg was 22.4 mmHg in the first hour and where the mean 24-hour decrease in SBP was 37.4 mm Hg.

In contrast to other studies,22 25 our results did not show any association between the level of attained BP and cerebral oedema. A recent randomised controlled trial in South Korea also suggested that the mean between-group difference in SBP over 24 hours was 9.6 mm Hg, resulting in a nearly six-fold increased risk of malignant cerebral oedema in patients who achieve successful reperfusion after EVT.26 One potential explanation for the differences in these results is that most of the participants in our study had a mild to moderate ischaemic stroke which was reflected in a mean infarct volume of 1.7 mL noted on CT scans, and only 2.2% (32 patients) in this population received EVT leading to a few incidences of cerebral oedema.27 Another explanation is that the mean level of attained SBP was only 143.8 mm Hg, and only 10% of participants had an attained SBP ≥160 mm Hg; this is a narrow range of SBP which is likely adequate to maintain cerebral autoregulation.28

Another new finding is that rapid control of SBP in the first hour may attenuate peri-ischaemia oedema in AIS patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis, which may reflect the active effect of intensive SBP lowering on preventing reperfusion injury. This was undertaken to extend the findings that intensive BP lowering reduces the odds of intracranial haemorrhage, which is another main format of reperfusion injury.14 One potential mechanism is that early BP lowering may reduce hydrostatic pressure gradients across the blood-brain barrier, thereby attenuating early vasogenic oedema.29 In sensitivity analyses excluding patients with ICH, this association was no longer significant, further suggesting that the protective signal of early BP reduction may largely relate to haemorrhage-associated oedema rather than ischaemia-driven swelling. The reverse correlation between the magnitude of SBP in the first hour and that in 24 hours with cerebral oedema suggests there might be a variability in the optimal level of SBP reduction and that excessive BP-lowering might be harmful. Dramatic BP changes may exacerbate the impairment of the altered cerebral dynamic autoregulation after ischaemic injury.7 30 BP variability might influence cerebral perfusion, compromise collateral flow, contribute to infarct growth and increase cytotoxic oedema post ischaemic stroke.3133 In addition, large BP fluctuations may directly injure the vascular endothelium, increase blood-brain barrier permeability and promote vasogenic oedema.33 34 Notably, in our study, SBP variability remained associated with oedema progression even after excluding ICH, and the effect size increased when infarct volume was not included in the model, supporting its closer link to ischaemia-related mechanisms.

Several strengths of our study include the relatively large population with brain imaging and a broad range of characteristics which allowed for the adjustment of multiple covariables in the regression models. Observer bias was reduced by having standardised imaging assessments without knowledge of clinical features. There are some limitations. First, the frequency of moderate to severe cerebral oedema was relatively low in this trial population with mainly mild degrees of clinical severity, few EVT-delivered patients, no systematic data on reperfusion status and a relatively high proportion of small vessel disease strokes that typically show minimal oedema and score 0 on the oedema scale. These factors likely reduced statistical power, contributed to wide CIs close to the null and may have diluted the observed associations. Second, the requirement for participants to have their baseline SBP ≤185 mm Hg as well as other inclusion criteria would have introduced selection bias in a clinical trial population. As the brain images were collected as part of routine clinical practice, there was variability in the quality, timing and modality. Most follow-up scans were performed at variable times (median, 27.4 hours from stroke onset) and often before the time of peak manifestation of cerebral oedema between days 3 and 5 post stroke,35 36 and serial imaging was not available for most patients. These factors precluded a detailed temporal analysis of oedema evolution in relation to BP changes. The 7-point cerebral oedema grading system may seem insensitive, particularly for oedema within the infarcts of the posterior circulation. In addition, we lacked detailed baseline imaging measures such as the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score or quantitative infarct size at admission and could only adjust for the presence of visible early ischaemic changes, and cerebral oedema related to infarct topography and pre-existing cerebral atrophy may have been overlooked. Finally, given the number of statistical comparisons performed, there is a potential risk of type I error. After Holm-Sidak correction, only the association between early SBP reduction within 1 hour and severe cerebral oedema remained significant, underlining the exploratory nature of our findings. Moreover, this was a secondary, post-hoc observational analysis of randomised trial data, and as such, the observed associations cannot be interpreted as causal but should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Taken together, our study should be interpreted as evaluating associations between BP profiles and cerebral oedema risk, and the findings require confirmation in future prospective or specifically designed randomised studies.

In summary, our study indicates that achieving rapid lowering of SBP within the first hour and maintaining stable SBP thereafter might attenuate cerebral oedema in AIS patients with mild to moderate neurological impairment who receive thrombolysis treatment. Further research is warranted to establish the optimal BP target following reperfusion therapy for AIS.
