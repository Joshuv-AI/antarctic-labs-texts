Attenuated Strains of Pseudomonas aeruginosa : A Promising Cell Factory for Rhamnolipid Production
Authors: Parvathy V Das, Meagan E Valentine, Timothy E Long, Hongwei D Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiodegradable compounds like rhamnolipids provide an environmentally friendly alternative to petroleum‐based surfactants. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most proficient producer of rhamnolipids. The genetic plasticity and metabolic versatility of P. aeruginosa can be harnessed to leverage the production of rhamnolipids. However, P. aeruginosa carries virulence factors and can cause debilitating diseases in immunocompromised individuals. Bacteria other than P. aeruginosa have been used as heterologous hosts for rhamnolipid production through genetic engineering. Our lab had previously developed genetically attenuated strains of P. aeruginosa to produce polysaccharide alginate. These strains can also be used for rhamnolipid production. This review examines the unique genetic, biochemical, and physiological characteristics of P. aeruginosa amenable to be exploited via bioengineering and the existing models for rhamnolipid production. Further, we propose the attenuated strains of P. aeruginosa as a safe cell‐factory for industrial production and biomedical use of rhamnolipids.Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa , rhamnolipids, safe strain generation

Introduction

Surfactants are compounds that reduce the surface and interfacial tension of liquids. Synthetic surfactants are derived from petroleum compounds and require chemical processing (Moldes et al. 2021). These chemicals, such as polysorbates, sorbitans, sulfates, sulfonates, and quaternary ammonium chlorides, are commercially used to manufacture detergents, foaming agents, emulsifiers, wetting agents, and dispersants. They are non‐biodegradable and damage the environment (Curstedt and Johansson 2005; Johnson et al. 2021). Vegetable or animal‐derived surfactants are non‐homogenous and require an extraction process that uses non‐renewable energy (Moldes et al. 2021). Microbial surfactants derived from bacteria and fungi are increasingly being explored to overcome the undesirable effects of synthetic surfactants. They are eco‐friendly options that can replace synthetic surfactants (Sharma et al. 2022).

Microbial surfactants can be broadly classified as glycolipids, phospholipids, lipopeptides, lipopolysaccharides, and fatty acids (Dini et al. 2024). Among these, rhamnolipids have been extensively explored for their industrial and research applications (Kashif et al. 2022; Mueller and Wenk 2021). Rhamnolipids are glycolipid biosurfactants produced mainly by Pseudomonas aeruginosa . They have commercial applications in agriculture and petroleum industries (Budzikiewicz 1993; Mueller and Wenk 2021; Soberón‐Chávez and Maier 2011). Specific applications include use in biodegradable detergents, as emulsifying agents, and as plant growth promoters (Pierre et al. 2025; Chong and li 2017; Mueller and Wenk 2021; Ali et al. 2024; Bettenhausen 2020). Rhamnolipids are also useful for the bioremediation of soil and water contaminated with crude oil and heavy metals (Thakur et al. 2021; Chong and li 2017). In a highly pure form – ‘biomedical grade’ – rhamnolipids can potentially become drug and vaccine delivery agents (Rodrigues 2015; Yi et al. 2019).

The concept of a microbial “cell‐factory” involves utilisation of a suitable microorganism as the seed‐organism in an industrial process that involves production or transformation of a commercially relevant product, which is generally a chemical substance (Becker and Wittmann 2015; Hussain et al. 2022; Chaudhary et al. 2024). Microbial processing is advantageous over chemical or physical processing as the former is eco‐friendly, sustainable and relatively cost‐effective. Biotechnology techniques exploit the genetics, metabolism and physiology of the host microbe to create an efficient and suitable strain that can convert raw materials to rhamnolipids in an industrial bioreactor. These strains may be genetically modified ones in which biosynthetic pathways for rhamnolipid production from P. aeruginosa are introduced to create ‘heterologous hosts’ (Soberón‐Chávez et al. 2021; Beuker, Steier, et al. 2016; Ochsner et al. 1995). The selected host organism must fulfil several criteria to be suitable for industrial use, which include genetic stability and the ability to utilise and convert regionally available substrates or raw materials to rhamnolipids. The organism must have a sustainable metabolic capacity that ensures robust growth in the bio‐fermenter. Most importantly, the organism must be safe for industrial use.

There is an increasing industrial demand for rhamnolipids from non‐pathogenic strains. A safe strain that is used for rhamnolipid production must not cause any disease in humans or animals and should not produce any toxic byproducts. P. aeruginosa , though the most efficient known producer, encodes a plethora of virulence factors that create a biosafety concern when the organism is used in large industrial fermentation tanks (Diggle and Whiteley 2020; Jurado‐Martín et al. 2021; Moellering Jr et al. 1995). The pathogenic potential of P. aeruginosa is well documented in various plant and animal models (Mahajan‐Miklos et al. 1999; Boucher et al. 1997; Cosson et al. 2002; Rahme et al. 1995). Individual virulence factor types have defined the strains involved in outbreaks, for instance the ExoU+ strain (Horna et al. 2019).

Previously, we used genetic techniques targeting the major pathogenic factors from P. aeruginosa . These novel strains are safe in a mouse model of infection and retain the ability to produce the polysaccharide alginate (Valentine, Kirby, Withers, et al. 2020). Our ongoing research is based on our experience that P. aeruginosa can be attenuated by deleting individual virulence genes. We describe here that these strains can also be used for rhamnolipid production. In this review, we present the intrinsic characteristics of P. aeruginosa that contribute toward making it a model cell‐factory for rhamnolipid production and advocate the use of genetically modified, attenuated strains for industrial use and as a chassis for further bioengineering.

Genome of P. aeruginosa

P. aeruginosa has certain distinctive genetic characteristics that make it an ideal candidate for bioengineering. These characteristics contribute to its pathogenicity, versatility, and adaptability, but can be exploited through genetic engineering techniques to produce valuable biomolecules like rhamnolipids. P. aeruginosa is considered to have a large genome of 5.5–7 million base pairs, with 4000–7000 ORFs (Winsor et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2006; Stover et al. 2000; Schmidt et al. 1996). The complete genome of more than 4000 strains of P. aeruginosa has been sequenced and is readily available (Stover et al. 2000; Grace et al. 2022; Choi et al. 2007). A notable fact is that in most bacteria with large genomes, the massive size of the genome is due to several gene duplications (Serres et al. 2009). It can be argued that the size of the genome of P. aeruginosa is due to a collection of distinct genes and gene families that code for products with diverse functions (Klockgether et al. 2011; Ozer et al. 2014). The large genome allows the organism to adapt to diverse environments and accommodates the immense metabolic capacity.

The genome of P. aeruginosa has a mosaic pattern. Whole genome sequencing reveals that a large core (approximately 5000 genes) of the P. aeruginosa genome is highly conserved across strains with an average nucleotide divergence of 0.5% (Spencer et al. 2003; Römling et al. 1995; Wolfgang et al. 2003). Within the core genome, several clusters of “accessory” genes occur which vary between strains. When many strains of P. aeruginosa are compared, this pattern of a highly conserved core interrupted by strain‐specific variable portions is observed. Together, the core and accessory genomes contribute to the genetic diversity and plasticity of P. aeruginosa . This genome arrangement also creates a structure malleable for genetic engineering techniques.

The core genome carries genes for housekeeping functions such as DNA replication, transcription and translation, protein synthesis, metabolism, biosynthesis and cell division (Poulsen et al. 2019; Moradali et al. 2017; Mathee et al. 2008). Metabolic enzymes and virulence factors common to the majority of strains of P. aeruginosa are encoded in the core genome (Wolfgang et al. 2003; Freschi et al. 2018). A sizeable part of the core genome also includes regulatory genes, mostly transcription factors.

The accessory genome of P. aeruginosa is interspersed as variable stretches of DNA within the conserved core genome (Stover et al. 2000). The accessory genome—approximately 11% of the total genome—is comprised mainly of mobile genetic elements and contributes to genomic diversity among strains (Klockgether et al. 2011; Kung et al. 2010; Ozer et al. 2014). These segments are of variable sizes and vary in sequence between strains. The accessory genome encodes factors that enable the individual strains to adapt to specific ecological niches where they are commonly seen, giving rise to specific phenotypes with a survival advantage in inhospitable environments (Mathee et al. 2008). The accessory genome may also include genes that allow metabolism of toxic substances such as heavy metals and pesticides, persistence in the host, and antibiotic resistance (Mesaros et al. 2007; He et al. 2004; Mathee et al. 2008). The accessory genome consists of integrons, transposons, genomic islands, phage elements, and plasmids often inserted into the chromosome at frequent intervals (Kung et al. 2010; Klockgether et al. 2011; Ozer et al. 2014).

The mosaic pattern of genetic material in P. aeruginosa creates a platform for gene manipulation without disturbing the essential physiology of the cell. The accessory gene segments of P. aeruginosa are often found close to sequences that facilitate gene mobility and differ from the core genome in their G + C content (Kung et al. 2010). The loci where the accessory genome of P. aeruginosa tends to cluster function as integration sites for foreign and phage DNA (Botelho et al. 2023). The presence of these integration sites opens up a wide possibility of genetic manipulation and rapid induction of mutations to transform native strains into commercially efficient cell‐factories (Schick et al. 2022; Choi and Schweizer 2006; Hoang et al. 2000; Hmelo et al. 2015). Segments of the genome can be inserted or deleted at these loci without altering the core genome. A stable core genome interspersed with segments of the accessory genome ensures that P. aeruginosa is sufficiently malleable for genetic engineering to create stable mutants that can endure the conditions within a bioreactor when used for rhamnolipid production.

Regulatory switches and drastic changes in the transcriptome are common in P. aeruginosa in response to the environment, nutrient or substrate availability, or growth phase (Waite et al. 2005; Caliskan et al. 2023). Rhamnolipid synthesis occurs only during the stationary phase as the repressor protein inhibits the expression of the biosynthesis genes during the growth phase and in the presence of nutrients (Schmidberger et al. 2013; Reis et al. 2011). When the bacterial growth reaches the stationary phase and nutrients are limited, the repression of the biosynthesis genes is removed and rhamnolipid secretion occurs. This regulatory switch occurs via the quorum‐sensing system that responds to the milieu (Keegan et al. 2023; Wittgens et al. 2017).

A classic example of a regulatory switch occurs when P. aeruginosa infects patients with cystic fibrosis. Isolates in the chronic stages of infection in these patients are phenotypically distinct from those in the acute stages of the infection. In chronic infections, clinical isolates are often mucoid and lack the virulence of those isolated in the early stages of infections (Iwańska et al. 2023; Sousa and Pereira 2014; Fothergill et al. 2012). These changes suggest that P. aeruginosa switches off the expression of some virulence factors and directs the transcriptional mechanism to produce a mucoid phenotype that offers a survival advantage in chronic infections (Hilliam et al. 2017; Qiu et al. 2007). The regulatory gene that prevents alginate production, mucA, is inactivated in mucoid isolates of P. aeruginosa from chronic respiratory infections in patients with cystic fibrosis (Govan and Deretic 1996). P. aeruginosa isolates in the early stages of cystic fibrosis often have a wild‐type mucA gene, the gene encoding an anti‐sigma factor (Qiu et al. 2007). This wild‐type can be mutated on exposure to stress and lead to a mucoid phenotype (Damron and Goldberg 2012). Mutated mucA causes activation of the sigma factor AlgU/T, which drives the overproduction of alginate (Yu and Head 2002).

Our previous work exploited strains of P. aeruginosa for overproduction of alginate at an industrial level (Withers et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2024). The stability of the core genome helped us delete selected virulence genes sequentially to create stable attenuated mutants (Valentine, Kirby, Withers, et al. 2020). These strains (PGN) efficiently expressed extraneously inserted gene segments, which helped us study alginate production pathways. Our current research focuses on exploring similar regulatory mechanisms, outside of known quorum‐sensing control, for the synthesis of rhamnolipids.

Metabolic Versatility of P. aeruginosa

The large genome of P. aeruginosa gives the organism enormous metabolic capabilities distinguished by biochemically efficient pathways that promote environmental adaptation and prolific production of several on‐demand virulence factors and secondary metabolites (Moellering Jr et al. 1995). Rhamnolipids are categorised as secondary metabolites, as they are produced from intermediates of core metabolic pathways. The organism's robustness, substrate utilisation, and metabolic flexibility contribute to a framework suitable for rhamnolipid production.

P. aeruginosa can maintain a core metabolism independent of the strain, growth phase, and carbon source. However, in the presence of stress, such as nutrient depletion, anaerobiosis, or hostile environments, substrate‐specific metabolic pathways are induced that precisely regulate the adaptive processes (Frimmersdorf et al. 2010). The highly branched electron transport chain of P. aeruginosa , with several dehydrogenases, terminal cytochrome oxidases, and denitrifying enzymes, further contributes to its metabolic flexibility (Liang et al. 2020; Arat et al. 2015).

P. aeruginosa is generally considered an obligate aerobic organism, but it has versatile oxygen requirements unlike the facultative anaerobes such as E. coli . Though the organism prefers an aerobic environment, P. aeruginosa can grow anaerobically in the presence of nitrates and inorganic electron acceptors (Wu et al. 2005; Glasser et al. 2014). In the presence of oxygen, the organism derives most of its energy by substrate oxidation. However, in an anaerobic environment, when nitrates are present, the regulatory networks of P. aeruginosa respond to oxygen tension and induce differential gene expression (Filiatrault et al. 2005; Trunk et al. 2010). Two regulons, Anr and Dnr, in the core genome have been described to sense low oxygen tension and activate the expression of genes involved in denitrification such as nitrate and nitrite reductase (Trunk et al. 2010; Galimand et al. 1991). Airways in cystic fibrosis lungs present a microaerobic or anaerobic environment due to the stagnant mucus. Isolates of P. aeruginosa from patients with cystic fibrosis have shown gene expression similar to that of anaerobic growth, suggesting a role of anaerobic growth in the pathogenesis of P. aeruginosa (Worlitzsch et al. 2002).

P. aeruginosa is a chemoorganotroph that can use various carbon sources for the industrial production of rhamnolipids (Fursova et al. 2004). Most substrate uptake in P. aeruginosa occurs via numerous (around 30) smaller, specific channels rather than via non‐specific porins as seen in E. coli (Eren et al. 2013; Kucharska et al. 2015). These channels, like the outer membrane OprG, OprD, TonB, OccD/K, and OprP/B proteins, help P. aeruginosa to uptake amino acids, succinate, acetate, and other TCA cycle intermediates (Eren et al. 2012; Benz and Hancock 1987; Hancock and Brinkman 2002). The presence of large nonspecific porins in E. coli may suggest a broader substrate uptake. On the contrary, the presence of specialised transporters in P. aeruginosa confers the ability to utilise specific substrates not readily taken up by E. coli . Furthermore, the lack of large, non‐specific porins like OmpF/C, as seen in E. coli , contributes to the intrinsic resistance to several antibiotics in P. aeruginosa (Eren et al. 2013; Nikaido 2003).

Microbes have a carbon catabolite repression (CCR) system that allows the organism to adapt differentially to various carbon sources. In the presence of a mixture of potential carbon sources, bacteria selectively utilise a preferred compound, which is determined by the genera (Valentini and Lapouge 2013). CCR is achieved by the lack of transcriptional activation of genes involved in a non‐preferred substrate, such as lactate, when a preferred substrate like glucose is present (Görke and Stülke 2008). Unlike Enterobacteriaceae, P. aeruginosa preferentially utilises organic acids, short‐chain fatty acids, amino acids, succinate, acetate, and other intermediates of the TCA cycle as sources of carbon rather than glucose, suggesting the difference in the natural niches of these families of bacteria (McGill et al. 2021; Rojo 2010). In media containing succinate and glucose, P. aeruginosa preferentially consumes succinate by repressing the expression of glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase and aldolase until succinate is exhausted (Collier et al. 1996). In a broad sense, P. aeruginosa can consume compounds like succinate and acetate, which require lower activation energy than glucose.

Isotope tracer studies have shown that the carbon flux in P. aeruginosa varies according to the substrate available (Dolan et al. 2020; Medić et al. 2022; Gil‐Gil et al. 2023). The organism undergoes a global remodelling of gene expression depending on the substrate it is grown on (Weir et al. 2008; McGill et al. 2021; Dolan et al. 2020). When grown on a carbon source such as glycerol, NADPH is mainly derived from a hybrid of the Entner‐Doudoroff pathway, the Embden‐Meyerhoff‐Parnas pathway, and the pentose phosphate pathway known as the EDEMP pathway (Dolan et al. 2020). This cyclic pathway generates the necessary NADPH required to endure oxidative stress and provide sufficient reductive power for the biosynthesis of fatty acids and secondary metabolites (Nikel et al. 2015). Then again, when grown on acetate, P. aeruginosa predominantly derives NADPH by the action of isocitrate dehydrogenase (Dolan et al. 2020). These findings suggest that the organism transitions smoothly between substrates by metabolic remodelling that involves switching metabolic pathways, modifying electron transport chains, and changing redox balance to maximise energy yield (Görke and Stülke 2008; Arat et al. 2015; Dolan et al. 2020; de Oliveira et al. 2021).

Metabolic flexibility enables P. aeruginosa to outgrow competitors when nutrients are limited. In glucose‐limiting conditions, P. aeruginosa rapidly consumes the available glucose by increasing the number of glucose transporters. Unlike E. coli , P. aeruginosa can oxidise glucose in its periplasm to gluconate (Lessie and Phibbs 1984). Oxidised glucose from the periplasm enters the EDEMP glycolytic pathway as 6‐phosphogluconate (She et al. 2019; Nikel et al. 2015). When an excess of glucose is present, the organism sequesters the available glucose in the periplasm via the extracellular oxidative pathway as gluconate and 2‐oxogluconate, which cannot be consumed by several other bacteria. In both situations, P. aeruginosa reduces the availability of glucose for its competitors in the environment (Whiting et al. 1976; Lessie and Phibbs 1984; Schleissner et al. 1997). The high metabolic flexibility and the wide range of substrates utilised make P. aeruginosa a hardy and profitable cell‐factory that can utilise cheap carbon sources in an industrial bio‐fermenter.

Rhamnolipid Biosynthesis in P. aeruginosa

Rhamnolipids are low‐molecular weight glycolipids, which contain rhamnose and are produced by most naturally occuring strains of P. aeruginosa (Burger et al. 1963). These molecules have a polar head section that has one or two rhamnose groups and a non‐polar tail section composed of fatty acids (Figure 1). Each strain produces a mixture of rhamnolipid congeners. The congeners are predicted by the number of rhamnose (Rha) and the length of the hydrocarbon (C) chain. In P. aeruginosa , the most common congeners are Rha–C10, Rha–C10–C10, Rha–Rha–C10, and Rha–Rha–C10–C10. The predominant congener produced is strain specific and depends on the phase of growth and substrates. The polar head (Rha or Rha‐Rha) and nonpolar tail (C10‐C10) within the molecule, an amphiphilic structure, give strong surface‐tension lowering abilities. This property makes rhamnolipids ideal emulsifying and wetting agents. The presence of hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts in the same molecule allows rhamnolipids to form micellar structures (Chong and li 2017; Jiang et al. 2020; Kłosowska‐Chomiczewska et al. 2017). The micelle‐forming property of rhamnolipids helps in bioremediation—the process of recovery of hydrocarbons and heavy metals from polluted soil and water using live microorganisms.

P. aeruginosa produces mono and dirhamnolipid congeners that are chiefly comprised of lipid residues derived from (R)‐3‐hydroxydecanoic acid (C10).

The biosynthesis of rhamnolipids involves several enzymes and occurs during the stationary phase of bacterial growth (Syldatk et al. 1985). The raw materials required are intermediates of fatty acid synthesis and glucose metabolism, namely β‐hydroxyacyl‐acyl carrier protein (ACP) and glucose‐6‐phosphate, respectively. Glucose‐6‐phosphate is required to form the charged sugar intermediate, dTDP‐rhamnose, which is added to the lipid segment formed from β‐hydroxyacyl‐ACP, an intermediate of fatty acid synthesis in bacteria (Figure 2). During the stationary phase, glucose metabolism follows a cyclical pathway in P. aeruginosa , so that two‐thirds of glucose‐3‐phosphate is recycled to form glucose‐6‐phosphate to meet the higher demand for rhamnolipid biosynthesis (de Oliveira et al. 2021). The AlgC enzyme (a phosphoglucomutase) converts glucose‐6‐phosphate to glucose‐1‐phosphate, which is further converted to dTDP‐L‐rhamnose by a series of enzymes encoded by the genes algC, rmlA, rmlB, rmlC, and rmlD. It is interesting to note that the algC gene product, which is crucial to generating dTDP‐rhamnose molecules, plays a significant role due to its position at the branching point for the production of two other polysaccharide virulence factors—namely alginate and lipopolysaccharide (Olvera et al. 1999).

Fatty acid synthesis (FAS) in both eukaryotes and bacteria is initiated by the condensation of acetyl‐CoA with malonyl‐acyl carrier protein (ACP). In most bacteria, this step is carried out by the FabH enzyme (β‐ketoacyl‐ACP synthase‐III). However, P. aeruginosa uses the β‐ketoacyl‐ACP synthase‐I/II enzyme, FabY, to condense acetyl‐CoA with malonyl‐ACP to produce β‐acetoacyl‐ACP (Figure 2) (Cronan and Thomas 2009; Yuan, Leeds, and Meredith 2012; Yuan, Sachdeva, et al. 2012; Zhang and Rock 2012). β‐acetoacyl‐ACP is further reduced to β‐hydroxyacyl‐ACP (Dusane et al. 2010).

The final steps in the synthesis of rhamnolipids begin with the condensation of two molecules of β‐hydroxy‐fatty acyl‐ACP to form β‐hydroxy‐alkanoyloxyalkanoic acid (HAA) by the rhlA gene product, an acyltransferase enzyme (Deziel et al. 2003). The RhlA enzyme in P. aeruginosa is thought to have a high affinity for β‐hydroxydecanoyl‐ACP (C10). Therefore, the predominant congeners in rhamnolipids produced by P. aeruginosa are of the C10‐C10 variety (Zhu and Rock 2008). Once HAA is formed, rhamnose molecules are added in succession by two specific rhamnosyltransferase reactions using dTDP‐rhamnose as the donor. The enzyme rhamnosyltransferase‐I, encoded by the rhlB gene, adds one molecule of rhamnose to the HAA molecule to produce a monorhamnolipid (Ochsner et al. 1994). Addition of another rhamnose unit to monorhamnolipid by the enzyme rhamnosyltransferase‐II, encoded by the rhlC gene, creates dirhamnolipid (González‐Valdez et al. 2024; Rahim et al. 2001).

Apart from rhamnolipid synthesis, β‐hydroxyacyl‐ACP, an intermediate of fatty acid synthesis, is shunted for the synthesis of signaling molecules for the quorum‐sensing system. Lipopolysaccharides, polyhydroxyalkanoates, phospholipids, lipoproteins, and pyoverdines also derive their lipid component from β‐hydroxyacyl‐ACP (Yuan, Leeds, and Meredith 2012; Yuan, Sachdeva, et al. 2012). Therefore, the formation of β‐hydroxyacyl‐ACP is another crucial step in the production of secondary metabolites with lipid components.

An interesting fact to consider is that the lipid portion of rhamnolipids may not be exclusively derived from de novo fatty acid synthesis. P. aeruginosa can utilise external fatty acids with variable‐length carbon chains. P. aeruginosa has several fatty acid degradation enzymes, FabD1‐6, which enable it to grow in the presence of short, medium, or long‐chain fatty acids as carbon sources (Zarzycki‐Siek et al. 2013). For example, its ability to use phosphatidyl choline as a carbon source is crucial to colonising the lungs of patients with cystic fibrosis. Curiously, P. aeruginosa has been shown to modify the composition of the rhamnolipids produced according to the carbon source. The carbon chain length and structure of the rhamnolipid are important determinants of physicochemical properties (Sałek et al. 2022). Several researchers have used various carbon sources such as glucose, a variety of cooking oils, motor oils, and synthetic hydrocarbons as carbon sources. One study used sunflower oil and obtained an optimal titre of 1.35 g/L (Rikalović et al. 2012). Another study identified olive oil as the optimal carbon source (Abouseoud et al. 2008; Govindammal 2014). When corn oil is used as the carbon source, P. aeruginosa produced predominantly monorhamnolipids; whereas with glycerol as the carbon source, the dirhamnolipid fraction was predominant (Hori et al. 2011; Ehinmitola et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2009; Mehdi et al. 2011). A high emulsifying index was reported with the use of heavy oils like crude black oil and lubricating oils which are rich in aromatic side chains and asphaltenes (Ehinmitola et al. 2018). Several studies have been published on the role of various carbon sources in rhamnolipid production (Dini et al. 2024; Abouseoud et al. 2008; Ehinmitola et al. 2018; Govindammal 2014; Koch et al. 1991). The reader may refer to published reviews on the effect of various carbon sources on rhamnolipid synthesis (Tan and Li 2018; Li 2017). These observations suggest an involvement of the fatty acid degradation pathway in rhamnolipid production.

Unlike eukaryotes, de novo fatty acid synthesis and beta‐oxidation of fatty acids can occur simultaneously in the presence of exogenous fatty acids in P. aeruginosa (Zhang et al. 2012). In a FabY defective mutant, Yuan et al. demonstrated a FabH‐like enzyme (ORF PA3286) that intercepts beta‐oxidation of fatty acids at the eight‐carbon stage. Then FabH condenses an intermediate of beta‐oxidation, octanoyl‐CoA (C8) with malonyl‐ACP to produce β‐ketodecanoyl‐ACP (C10) (Yuan, Leeds, and Meredith 2012). Through the activity of an unknown reductase, β‐keto‐acyl‐ACP is converted to β‐hyroxyacyl‐ACP, a precursor for HAA (Abdel‐Mawgoud et al. 2014). Furthermore, simultaneous inactivating mutations of FabY (PA5174) and PA3286 is lethal to the cell (Yuan, Leeds, and Meredith 2012; Yuan, Sachdeva, et al. 2012).

Though the lipid components of rhamnolipids are likely derived from both the fatty acid degradation pathway and/or the de novo synthesis pathway of fatty acids, the individual significance of each pathway in the regulation of rhamnolipid synthesis is not completely understood. The metabolic alterations that occur in the presence of external fatty acids such as absorption, carbon flow, energy production, and production of secondary metabolites are also less characterised. However, this property enables P. aeruginosa to produce rhamnolipids by utilising cheaper carbon sources like soybean oil.

Factors Affecting Rhamnolipid Biosynthesis

The most significant regulator of rhamnolipid synthesis is the quorum sensing (QS) system. This system involves chemical signals called autoinducers that influence the social behaviour of bacteria by controlling regulatory genes that respond to threshold levels of these autoinducers. The population density of the bacteria in an ecological niche determines the number of chemical signals or amount of autoinducers present. Once the threshold level is reached, the regulatory genes respond by coordinating population behaviour through collective responses of the bacterial community to ensure their survival. This mechanism is most evident in the formation of a biofilm (Ng and Bassler 2009; Lee and Zhang 2015). Three QS systems exist in P. aeruginosa , namely the Las, Rhl and PQS systems. The autoinducers for Las and Rhl systems are homoserine lactones and for the PQS system are alkyl quinolone (Lee and Zhang 2015; Miranda et al. 2022; Sánchez‐Jiménez et al. 2023). The regulatory pathways of quorum‐sensing system are complicated. The virulence factor regulator gene, vfr, controls the Las quorum sensing, which in turn regulates the Rhl quorum sensing for the expression of virulence factors (Albus et al. 1997; Suh et al. 2002; Rashedi et al. 2006; Fursova et al. 2004).

The biosynthetic genes, rhlA and rhlB, form an operon with the C4‐quorum sensing genes rhlR and rhlI (Dusane et al. 2010). RhlR protein is a transcription factor that controls the expression of numerous virulence factors (Keegan et al. 2023). RhlI is involved in the synthesis of an autoinducer, N‐butyryl‐L‐homoserine lactone that binds to the RhlR protein and prevents it from repressing the rhlABRI operon promoter (Ochsner and Reiser 1995). The Rhl‐QS is also known by an alternative name, the C4‐quorum‐sensing system, due to its autoinducer molecule. During the early stages of growth, RhlR protein represses the rhamnolipid synthesis genes, rhlA and rhlB. Once the bacterial population enters the logarithmic phase, the cell density is sufficient to elevate the autoinducer level high enough to remove the repression by RhlR protein. Once the repression is removed, rhamnolipid synthesis is activated.

All strains of P. aeruginosa produce rhamnolipids depending on growth conditions. Rhamnolipid production is determined by several environmental and nutritional factors. According to Guerra‐Santos et al., rhamnolipid production was highest using a medium with 7% glucose at pH 6.2 to 6.4 and temperature 32°C to 34°C (Guerra‐Santos et al. 1986). Presence of multivalent cations, nitrogen, phosphate, sodium chloride, and chelating agents also influence rhamnolipid production (Maťátková et al. 2022). The carbon to nitrogen ratio influences the biosynthesis of rhamnolipids. P. aeruginosa is the most efficient producer of rhamnolipids when nitrogen and phosphorus in the media are limited. In a phosphate depleted state, a metabolic shift occurs resulting in increased production of rhamnolipids (Mulligan et al. 1989). Iron limited environments can favour the production of rhamnolipids whereas increased concentration of glutamine and ammonium can repress the production (Glick et al. 2010; Invally and Ju 2020).

Other than P. aeruginosa, other species of Pseudomonas have also been found to produce rhamnolipids. Pseudomonas chlororaphis has been identified as a rhamnolipid producer. When grown in mineral salts medium with 2% glucose as a carbon source, P. chlororaphis produced 0.355 g/L to 1 g/L of monorhamnolipids (Gunther et al. 2005). P. fluorescence isolated from lubricant‐polluted water produced over 1 g/L of biosurfactants when 1.5% hexadecane was used as a sole carbon source (Vasileva‐Tonkova et al. 2006). A strain isolated from a water sample, Pseudomonas sp. DSM 2874 produced a maximum of 13.2 g/L of rhamnolipids in a 20 L bioreactor with n‐tetradecane as the carbon source using physiological saline as a medium (Syldatk et al. 1985). Few other rare isolates like P. stutzeri , P. indica , P. nitroreducens, and Pseudomonas mosselli, produce lesser amounts of rhamnolipids (Xia et al. 2023; Onwosi and Odibo 2012; Bhardwaj et al. 2015; Singh and Tripathi 2013). Non‐Pseudomonas producers of rhamnolipid have also been identified including Serratia rubidaea SNAU02 which utilised mahua oil cake as a substrate (Nalini and Parthasarathi 2014). Additionally, other Serratia spp. isolated from oil‐polluted soil were found to secrete rhamnolipids (Chafale et al. 2023). A rhamnolipid producing strain of Geobacillus stearothermophilus isolated in the mountainous regions of Saudi Arabia demonstrated the presence of rhlAB genes and could utilise waste sunflower frying oil and demonstrated rhamnolipid production at 572.4 mg/L after a period of five‐day incubation (Albasri et al. 2024).

Physiological Role of Rhamnolipids

The exact physiological role of rhamnolipids in P. aeruginosa is yet to be defined. Initial studies focused on the ability of rhamnolipids to enhance the uptake of hydrophobic substrates (Oberbremer et al. 1990; Chrzanowski et al. 2012; Arino et al. 1998). However, further research demonstrated other roles of rhamnolipids in swarming motility of P. aeruginosa, biofilm formation and maintenance, cell detachment, and the formation of nutrient transfer channels in a mature biofilm (Neu 1996; Deziel et al. 2003; Caiazza et al. 2005; Tremblay et al. 2007; Lequette and Greenberg 2005; Pamp and Tolker‐Nielsen 2007; Shanks et al. 2006). These findings suggest that rhamnolipids may aid in nutrient foraging during nutrient limitation (i.e., stationary phase) and in protecting the microbe from host defence (Chrzanowski et al. 2012; Caiazza et al. 2005). Rhamnolipids function as surface‐active agents and form micelles or vesicles around hydrocarbons and helping the bacterium in the uptake of these hydrophobic molecules as the carbon source.

Another physiological role of rhamnolipids is to maintain the architecture of biofilms. Optical sectioning of mature biofilms demonstrates that these are well‐hydrated, open structures with variable cell densities (Lawrence et al. 1991). Rhamnolipids help maintain open channels that facilitate hydration and removal of waste materials in biofilms (Pamp and Tolker‐Nielsen 2007). Ironically, rhamnolipids can be used to disrupt biofilms of other bacteria. Furthermore, rhamnolipids can interfere with biofilm formation of other bacteria by inhibiting acyl‐homoserine lactone production (Firdose et al. 2024). The ability of rhamnolipids to disperse biofilms of other bacteria has been explored for potential use against oral microbial pathogens, in food industry and for biofilms formed on drilling equipment used in marine oil wells and to prevent corrosion (Aleksic et al. 2017; Wood et al. 2018; e Silva et al. 2017).

Rhamnolipids are categorised as virulence factors that function as extracellular toxins and heat‐stable hemolysins (Smeal et al. 1987; McClure and Schiller 1992). Rhamnolipids disrupt cell membranes and sheaths of flagella via solubilisation of phospholipids and therefore have been used to study flagellar basal body structure (Hastie et al. 1987; Ferris et al. 1984). Tracheal cilia exposed to rhamnolipids lose dynein arms from axonemes, have altered ciliary membranes and have depleted ATPase activity (Hastie et al. 1986; Hingley et al. 1986). Prior studies revealed rhamnolipid priming increases oxidative burst in macrophages and monocytes which may enhance P. aeruginosa mediated respiratory tissue damage in chronic conditions like cystic fibrosis (Kharazmi et al. 1989).

Despite being a virulence factor, studies have examined the use of rhamnolipid micelles and nanoparticles for drug delivery. According to Yi et al. (2019), rhamnolipid molecules can self‐assemble as a nanoparticle core, which can be loaded with a photosensitiser. These nanoparticles when taken up by tumour cells in mice increase their susceptibility to laser irradiation. When loaded with hydrophobic drugs, rhamnolipid nanoparticle can enhance dermal drug delivery (Müller et al. 2017; Yi et al. 2019). The potential role of rhamnolipids as drug delivery agents require further research. The major hurdle for this purpose is the production of ultra‐pure rhamnolipids with consistent quality without batch‐to‐batch variability, which can modulate the innate immune response (Palanisamy and Raichur 2009; Yi et al. 2019; Cheow and Hadinoto 2012).

Genetic Engineering Models for Rhamnolipids Production

The unique characteristics of P. aeruginosa make it a model organism for studying bacterial phenomena such as quorum sensing, motility, cell signalling, biofilm formation, virulence, antibiotic resistance, and bioremediation. Due to its simple nutritional requirements, hardy characteristics, and defined lifestyle stages, P. aeruginosa can be easily grown in a lab and cryopreserved. As discussed, the genetic structure of P. aeruginosa is unique and distinct from E. coli . The sites were accessory genome interrupts the core genome act as loci for gene insertions and deletions (Wolfgang et al. 2003). Gene deletions can be done using allelic exchange by homologous recombination using suicide‐plasmid vectors via a conjugative E. coli or by direct electroporation (Hmelo et al. 2015; Schweizer 1992; Huang and Wilks 2017). Gene deletions can help understand the function of the gene by examining the phenotype of the deletion mutant.

Another tool for generating mutants and studying gene function is the use of transposon mutagenesis. Transposons are mobile genetic elements that can easily transpose or ‘jump’ from a plasmid to chromosomes. This process can disrupt genes randomly or insert promoters next to genes. Mutant libraries in P. aeruginosa created by transposon insertion can be screened for desirable phenotypes (Wong and Mekalanos 2000; Jacobs et al. 2003; Liberati et al. 2006). The role of rhlABRI operon in rhamnolipid synthesis was elucidated when a mutant P. aeruginosa produced by transposon mutagenesis could not utilise hydrocarbons (Koch et al. 1991; Ochsner et al. 1994). One study used atmospheric and room temperature mutagenesis to identify mutants derived from Pseudomonas sp. (Xu et al. 2023). Recent studies have relied on CRISPR systems for creating mutants (Chen et al. 2018).

Several natural and engineered strains that produce rhamnolipids have been reported in published literature. All P. aeruginosa strains produce rhamnolipids. The highest reported rhamnolipid production from P. aeruginosa PAO1 is 39 g/L after 90 h of cultivation using sunflower oil as the carbon source in a 30 L bioreactor (Müller et al. 2011). Other strains of P. aeruginosa that have been described as significant producers of rhamnolipids include P. aeruginosa BM02, NCIM 5514 and KT1115 (Cerqueira Dos Santos et al. 2024; Varjani and Upasani 2016; Liu et al. 2020). High amounts are produced by isolates from oil sludge waste (Li et al. 2024). An Indian study recycled waste engine oil using halotolerant P. aeruginosa KP163922 strain and reported production of a mixture of rhamnolipid congeners at 29.76 g/L in a fed batch bioreactor (Gaur et al. 2024).

The most common genetic engineering method to increase rhamnolipid yield involves increasing the number of copies of rhlAB genes within a host strain using expression vectors. In P. aeruginosa SG strain, overexpression of rhlAB genes using a plasmid along with a strong promoter increased the yield by 1.8 times that of the native strain (Zhao et al. 2015). Gutiérrez‐Gómez U et al. created a mutant variant of P. aeruginosa PA14 with mutations in phaC1 and phaC2 genes and deletion of the phaG gene. Over expression of rhlAB‐R genes increased the rhamnolipid yield only in the triple mutant for polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) synthesis by 59% (Gutiérrez‐Gómez et al. 2018). Genes apart from those involved in the terminal biosynthesis steps and regulatory ones outside the quorum‐sensing system can also influence rhamnolipid production. The deletion of the catabolite repression control gene (crc) in P. aeruginosa eliminates rhamnolipid production. However, when inhibitors of Rhl quorum sensing such as Lon protease are deleted, rhamnolipid production increases in the crc deficient mutant (Yang et al. 2015).

The autotransporter esterase EstA also plays a role in rhamnolipid secretion by P. aeruginosa . EstA is an outer‐membrane bound lipolytic enzyme that has an esterase domain on the cell surface. Overexpression of the estA gene in wild‐type strains of PAO1 increased the production of rhamnolipids whereas an estA deficient mutant did not produce rhamnolipids (Wilhelm et al. 2007; Dobler et al. 2017). The exact role of the EstA protein in rhamnolipid synthesis is however not completely understood.

Apart from quorum sensing, other transcription factors probably play a significant role in rhamnolipid synthesis. The transcription regulator gene irrE from Deinococcus spp. regulates DNA damage repair and stress‐resistance (Lu et al. 2009). Heterologous expression of this gene in P. aeruginosa YM4 using a pHERD plasmid in which the PBAD promoter is replaced with a constitutive promoter resulted in a 17.9% increase in production of monorhamnolipids (Wang et al. 2024).

Use of a heterologous host carrying rhamnolipid synthesis genes circumvents the complex regulatory framework of QS and mitigates the risks of opportunistic infections (Sánchez‐Jiménez et al. 2023). Heterologous rhamnolipid production in E. coli by controlled expression of biosynthetic genes in E. coli produced much less rhamnolipid than PAO1 (7%–28%) (Cabrera‐Valladares et al. 2006). Several studies have described the use of heterologous hosts such as Pseudomonas putida or Burkholderia spp. for industrial production of rhamnolipids. Earlier attempts using P. fluorescens as a heterologous host had very low yields (Ochsner et al. 1995).

P. putida is the most utilised heterologous host for rhamnolipid production due to its inherent low pathogenicity. However, the genome of P. putida lacks the rhlAB genes (Molina et al. 2013). Rhamnolipid biosynthetic genes were introduced into P. putida KT2440 using an expression vector with a synthetic promoter. The synthetic promoter was designed using consensus sequences of sigma 70 promoters and fused with rhamnosyltransferase genes (Beuker, Steier, et al. 2016; Jensen and Hammer 1998; Beuker, Barth, et al. 2016). Deletion of alginate and PHA operons increased the precursor availability for rhamnolipids. Deletion of the flagellar mechanism reduced energy expenditure (Pang et al. 2024; Tiso et al. 2016). Congener diversity was reduced by altering the combination of genes on the expression vector, i.e., using plasmids with rhlAB, rhlABC or rhlA, introduced into P. putida KT2440. This strain has been described to produce rhamnolipids at 19.77 g/L in a fermenter and 3 g/L in a shake flask (Pang et al. 2024).

Widberger et al. introduced pVLT31 plasmid vector carrying rhlAB genes with an inducible promoter into P. putida KT2440 and grew this strain in spent media used for an acetogenic bacterium. The engineered strain was able to produce monorhamnolipids at a titre of 360‐400 mg/L in acetate containing media (Widberger et al. 2024). P. putida does not utilise xylose. But introduction of heterologous xylose utilisation pathway genes from P. taiwanensis , C. crescentus and E.coli created an engineered strain that could utilise alternate carbon sources such as lignocellulose which yield xylose on degradation (Bator et al. 2019). Another study introduced rhlA, rhlB and rhlC genes along with the transcriptional regulator gene rhlR in Pseudomonas chlororaphis ATCC 9446 using plasmid vectors. The RhlR protein coupled with endogenously produced acylhomoserine lactones and activated the biosynthesis of rhamnolipids (González‐Valdez et al. 2024).

Though most studies used plasmid‐based expression systems for heterologous production of rhamnolipids, Tiso et al. developed an expression cassette that integrated biosynthetic genes to the genome with an inducible promoter (Tiso et al. 2020). This was done on an engineered strain of P. putida in which the energy and carbon flow were modified by eliminating flagellar genes and genes involved in PHA synthesis (Martínez‐García and de Lorenzo 2011). The rhamnolipid biosynthetic genes were then cloned onto a plasmid vector and transposed onto the genomic DNA to yield the engineered strain (Choi et al. 2005).

Bioengineering methods have also been applied to downstream processing of rhamnolipid production. Excessive foaming results in an overall reduction in industrial rhamnolipid production as foam fractionation can result in removal of bacteria accumulated at its interface. Genetically engineered strains with deletion of flagellar machinery reduce this loss of biomass (Blesken et al. 2020). Askitosari et al. describe boosting rhamnolipids production from KT2440 in a bioreactor by using the anode of a bioelectrochemical system as an electron acceptor to reduce oxygen requirement and thereby foaming (Askitosari et al. 2020). A so called “green synthesis” of HAA analogs from cellulose‐derived levoglucosenone has also been described (Petracco et al. 2024).

Congener diversity and carbon chain length of HAA are strain and substrate specific. P. aeruginosa produces C10‐C10 variety of rhamnolipid in abundance as the rhlA enzyme has the highest affinity for C10 variant of β‐hydroxyacyl‐ACP. However, other hosts such as P. chlororaphis and Burkholderia sp. can produce rhamnolipids with carbon chain lengths other than C10 (Gunther et al. 2005; González‐Valdez et al. 2024; Tavares et al. 2013; Funston et al. 2016; Zhu and Rock 2008). Burkholderia lata produces rhamnolipid methyl esters (Gauthier et al. 2024).

The Scope of Virulence Attenuated Strains of P. aeruginosa

For an efficient ‘cell‐factory’, the selected microorganism must be able to utilise cheap and sustainable raw materials or ‘substrates’ to generate desired products at high levels. Engineered strains, which are more efficient than naturally occurring ones, can reduce the cost of production and increase its efficiency. The functional capacity of a bacterial ‘cell‐factory’ in terms of metabolism and biosynthesis must match the available raw material and downstream processes to meet industrial demand for economic viability of its use. However, the safety of the organism is the most important criterion in deciding its suitability for industrial use. An organism used in an industrial process must be non‐pathogenic and genetically stable. It should not produce any harmful toxins and must not mutate rapidly.

A similar criterion also applies to a bacterial strain chosen for laboratory use. Bacteria used for genetic experiments must be easy to culture and maintain, readily identifiable and of appropriate biosafety level to be safely handled without risk for the laboratory personnel. The most extensively used organism for research in molecular biology is E. coli K12 and its derivatives. E. coli K12 was first isolated from the stool sample of a convalescent diphtheria patient in 1922. The strain was initially maintained in agar stabs and used at a later date by Lederberg and Tatum for their studies in recombination (Lederberg and Tatum 1946; Gray and Tatum 1944). Since its first use as a model organism a century ago, it has undergone considerable genetic changes giving rise to strains which are distinct from the parent strain (Bachmann 1972). E. coli K12 is a laboratory strain, which was chosen as a model organism at a time when cryopreservation was in its infancy and has since undergone drastic mutations (Sprouffske et al. 2016). Additionally, the K12 strains have been genetically modified and optimised for the stability required for recombinant protein production. Many currently used bio‐products such as human insulin are produced by E. coli K12 (Selas Castiñeiras et al. 2018; Kleiner‐Grote et al. 2018).

Most microbial molecular mechanisms are understood in the background of E. coli and may not translate similarly in P. aeruginosa . Using E. coli as a representative model for all bacterial cell mechanisms needs to be re‐examined. The cellular mechanisms in P. aeruginosa cater to a wider range of metabolic versatility and environmental adaptability than E. coli . The global regulatory control of gene expression in P. aeruginosa differs from that of E. coli (Suh et al. 2002). Theoretically, these differences justify the low levels of rhamnolipid production in E. coli models with extraneous insertion of biosynthetic genes.

P. aeruginosa is a sturdy and prolific producer of several secondary metabolites and is amenable to the various genetic engineering techniques and metabolic modifications. These techniques can be applied to harness biosynthetic rhamnolipid pathways to increase the efficiency of industrial production. The immense metabolic capacity, stability of the core genome, and the presence of mobile genetic elements due to the accessory genome make this organism an ideal candidate for genetic engineering (Hoang et al. 2000). On the downside, this organism encodes a plethora of virulence factors that make native strains a biosafety concern. Though often denoted as “opportunistic,” P. aeruginosa is a dreaded nosocomial pathogen in a healthcare setting due to its intrinsic resistance, ability to acquire resistance to multiple groups of antibiotics, and biofilm formation. Using P. aeruginosa for large‐scale industrial production is limited by these factors. In the post‐genomic era, rigorous gene‐based criteria must complement the Generally Regarded as Safe (GRAS) criteria for microorganisms used in research and industry. Our work has demonstrated that safe, engineered microorganisms generated by deletion of virulence genes can be used as a cell factory (Table 1). Alginate is a water‐soluble polysaccharide produced by P. aeruginosa that is used in medicine and industry. Previously, we discovered a signal encoded in the mucE gene of P. aeruginosa, which when overexpressed induced stable alginate production (Qiu et al. 2007; Yu et al. 2024). We also optimised bioreactor conditions and downstream processing to achieve a yield of 100 g/L wet weight and 30 g/L dry powder of alginate using the engineered strain, P. aeruginosa VE2 with overexpression of mucE (Qiu et al. 2007). But the parent strain, P. aeruginosa PAO1 expresses several virulence factors that hinder its industrial use.

Using pEX100TNot1, a suicide‐plasmid gene deletion tool, we sequentially deleted several virulence genes from PAO1 and generated antibiotic resistance‐free strains, PGN4 and PGN5 (Valentine, Kirby, and Yu 2020). Our stable, attenuated strain, PGN5, has five virulence factors deleted, namely exotoxin A, hemolytic phospholipase C, pyocyanin pigment, lipopolysaccharides O antigen, and a gene involved in the synthesis of aromatic amino acid (Valentine, Kirby, Withers, et al. 2020). We tested the pathogenicity of PGN5‐mucE, PGN5 with overexpression of mucE in a mouse model for systemic infections and found that when compared with PAO1 and VE2, PGN5‐mucE does not invade the bloodstream on intraperitoneal injections and fails to cause a systemic infection in mice. The genomes of both PGN4 and PGN5 were sequenced, and the respective deletions were confirmed (Liu et al. 2007; Valentine, Kirby, Withers, et al. 2020; Valentine, Kirby, and Yu 2020). PGN5 is comparable in its safety profile when compared to FDA‐approved E. coli K12 strain. Interestingly, a high infective dose of PGN5 caused zero death in mice whereas E.coli K12 killed 4 out of 20 mice (Valentine, Kirby, and Yu 2020). Due to the deletion of the aroA gene, PGN5 shows inhibited growth unless the aromatic amino acids are added to the growth medium. This ensures additional biosafety control in case of accidental environmental release. Targeted virulence gene deletions offer a viable approach to generate safe, designer strains for biomedical applications and for use as cell factories.

A strain selected for industrial use must have the most suitable metabolic framework to produce the target product. P. aeruginosa carries a complex metabolic core that supports the organism's sturdiness and versatility as a cell factory. Furthermore, it is a robust microorganism that can withstand extreme conditions and utilise a variety of substrates, which include waste materials and hydrocarbons. The genomic and metabolic plasticity of the organism provides ample opportunities for genetic engineering. Rhamnolipids are synthesised naturally as secondary metabolites in all strains of P. aeruginosa . When the rhamnolipid biosynthesis pathway, which is indigenous to P. aeruginosa , is nested in a heterologous host, the metabolic framework may not be supportive of production at full enzymatic efficiency.

However, PGN strains boast comparable efficiency to that of the parent PAO1 strain without a host of virulence factors. These attenuated strains can act as a reliable chassis for a biosafe cell factory. The phzM gene encodes the phenazine methylase enzyme that catalyses the production of the pigment pyocyanin, a potent cytotoxin. Elimination of this gene in the PGN strains removes the production of pyocyanin. Our lab intends to explore further genetic manipulation of these strains by eliminating endogenous pathways such as alginate that compete for the same raw materials as rhamnolipids. The PGN strains can also be used to produce rhamnolipids of higher purity by eliminating pathways that produce contaminants such as LPS and pigments. The wapR gene encodes a rhamnosyl transferase that is required for the formation of the O antigen in LPS. Elimination of the wapR gene produces LPS without the O antigen. In PGN strains, we assume that the contamination of the product with LPS shedding is reduced. We were able to extract and analyse rhamnolipids from PGN strains and compare them with those from PAO1. Our experiments show that the removal of these virulence factors did not affect rhamnolipid production (Table 2).

Concluding Remarks

Genetic engineering can increase the efficiency of rhamnolipid production from P. aeruginosa . Its inherent pathogenicity limits the potential use for industrial applications. The current FDA‐approved GRAS E. coli K12 offers excellent opportunities for biotechnology (Burdock and Carabin 2004). However, there are virulent genes such as hemolysins in the genome of E. coli K12 (Kehoe et al. 1983; Hunt et al. 2008; Del Castillo et al. 1997). These toxins, once expressed, can cause cytotoxic effects to the host cells (Maldonado et al. 2005). Coupled with biotechnology and metabolic engineering approaches, virulence‐attenuated PGN strains of P. aeruginosa can provide an alternative, safe and economical microbial cell factory. Bioproducts such as rhamnolipids, which are naturally produced by P. aeruginosa , can be efficiently produced in these strains.

The availability of many gene editing tools for P. aeruginosa can increase rhamnolipid production through metabolic engineering. However, there are still many hurdles ahead. For example, how the organism efficiently moves the bio‐ and petroleum‐based oils from the growth media to the site of synthesis and then transports rhamnolipids out of cells is still not clear. Regulatory mechanisms besides quorum sensing are not understood. Furthermore, the rate‐limiting steps in rhamnolipid production are not identified. Other challenges include downstream separation and purification procedures for rhamnolipids. Moreover, ideal fermentation methods for gene‐modified strains require further exploration of the metabolic pathways.
