Urinary Albumin‐to‐Creatinine Ratio, Cardiovascular Health, and All‐Cause Mortality in Hypertension: A Nationwide Cohort Analysis
Authors: Dingding Wang, Meng Zhang, Peichen Xie, Jianwen Yu, Jianbo Li, Lanping Jiang, Xunhua Zheng, Zhentian Wu, Suchun Li, Siyang Ye, Leigang Jin, Kam Wa Chan, Sydney C. W. Tang, Wei Chen, Bin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile both cardiovascular health (CVH) and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) are individually associated with mortality, their combined prognostic significance and potential mechanistic interplay in adults with hypertension remain unclear. This cohort study analyzed data from 9154 hypertensive adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2007–2018. CVH was assessed using the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 score, and UACR was measured from spot urine samples. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic spline analyses, joint exposure modeling, and causal mediation analysis were used to evaluate the independent, combined, and mediating effects of UACR and CVH on all‐cause mortality. Both lower CVH scores and higher UACR levels were independently associated with increased mortality. A nonlinear association was observed for each. Individuals with severely elevated UACR and poor CVH had the highest mortality risk (HR = 6.61; 95% CI, 3.72–11.74), while those with normal UACR (<10 mg/g) showed no significant mortality difference across CVH strata. Notably, even mildly elevated UACR (10–29.9 mg/g), considered within the conventional “normal” range, was associated with significantly increased mortality. Mediation analysis revealed that UACR explained 4.01% (95% CI, 2.83%–6.40%; p < 0.001) of the association between CVH and mortality. This study is the first to demonstrate that UACR not only modifies but also mediates the association between CVH and mortality in individuals with hypertension. These findings underscore the prognostic value of integrating renal and cardiovascular metrics and suggest that even low‐grade albuminuria has clinical relevance.

Introduction

Hypertension affects more than 31% of adults globally and remains the most prevalent modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and all‐cause mortality [1, 2]. Despite widespread implementation of evidence‐based antihypertensive therapies [3], substantial residual risk persists, particularly due to subclinical organ damage that often precedes overt disease [2, 4]. This underscores the need for improved risk stratification tools that capture early‐stage pathology and inform more personalized, integrated management strategies across the cardiovascular–renal continuum.

The urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) is a well‐established, noninvasive biomarker of glomerular injury and endothelial dysfunction [5]. It has been strongly associated with incident CVD events, kidney disease progression, and mortality, even in individuals without overt albuminuria [6, 7, 8]. Although a UACR threshold of ≥ 30 mg/g is conventionally used to define kidney damage in the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [9], accumulating evidence suggests that even low‐grade albuminuria within the so‐called “normal” range (< 30 mg/g) may portend adverse outcomes [8, 10, 11, 12]. Observational data show that risks of hypertension, CVD, and death rise continuously with UACR, without a clear inflection point, calling into question the traditional dichotomy of normal versus abnormal values [11, 12, 13]. Identifying individuals with mildly elevated UACR—particularly those with coexisting cardiometabolic risk—may enable earlier intervention before the onset of irreversible organ damage.

Simultaneously, the American Heart Association (AHA) has introduced the “Life's Essential 8” (LE8) as a comprehensive metric of cardiovascular health (CVH), encompassing four behavioral (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep) and four biological (body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose, blood lipids) components [14, 15, 16]. This refined CVH score has demonstrated predictive validity for cardiovascular events and mortality in both the general and hypertensive populations [17, 18]. However, whether the prognostic value of CVH is modified by coexisting renal injury remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, the potential mediating role of kidney injury—specifically, UACR—in the pathway linking poor CVH to increased mortality has not been well‐characterized. Given the interconnected nature of cardiovascular and renal systems and the emerging importance of integrating subclinical markers into global health assessment [7], there is a critical need to elucidate how UACR and CVH jointly affect mortality risk in hypertensive individuals. Such insights could guide the development of composite risk models that better reflect the pathophysiologic overlap between vascular and renal injury.

In this study, we used nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to assess the independent associations of UACR and CVH with all‐cause mortality in adults with hypertension, evaluate their joint effects, and determine whether UACR mediates the association between CVH and mortality. By quantifying both the synergistic and mechanistic relationships between CVH and renal injury, this study seeks to advance our understanding of the cardiorenal axis and inform early, integrated risk assessment strategies in clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

The data used in this study are openly available from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html.

Study Populations

We analyzed data from the NHANES, a nationally representative cross‐sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the US civilian non‐institutionalized population. NHANES uses a complex, multistage probability sampling design. All data are publicly available, de‐identified, and collected under protocols approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board; all participants provided written informed consent [19]. Since NHANES data are publicly available, further institutional review board approval is exempt. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. We included participants from NHANES cycles 2007–2018 (n = 59 842). After excluding individuals aged < 20 years (n = 25 072) and those without linked mortality, hypertension, CVH metrics, and UACR data (n = 24 459), 10 311 adults remained. Among these, individuals with missing data on our key covariates were excluded (n = 1157). The final analytic cohort comprised 9154 hypertensive adults. Definition of Hypertension

Hypertension was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 80 mmHg, in accordance with the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines [20], self‐reported physician diagnosis of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medication.

Assessment of UACR

Spot urine samples were collected during NHANES physical examinations, stored at –20°C, and analyzed in certified laboratories. Urine albumin was measured by solid‐phase fluorescent immunoassay. Urine creatinine was assessed using the kinetic Jaffé method prior to 2007 and the enzymatic method thereafter [21]. To ensure consistency across survey cycles, creatinine values from 1999–2006 were recalibrated using the recommended correction equation [22]. UACR was calculated as the ratio of urinary albumin (mg/dL) to creatinine (mg/dL) and expressed in mg/g. Based on clinical relevance, UACR was categorized as normal: < 10 mg/g; mildly increased: 10–29.9 mg/g; moderately increased: 30–299.9 mg/g; severely increased: ≥ 300 mg/g. UACR was also analyzed as a continuous variable.

CVH Metrics

CVH was assessed using the AHA's “Life's Essential 8” framework, which includes four behavioral (diet quality, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep duration) and four biological (body mass index, blood pressure, blood lipids, blood glucose) components [14]. Each metric was scored from 0 to 100. The overall CVH score was computed as the unweighted average of the eight components, resulting in a total score from 0 to 100. Participants were classified into three CVH categories: Poor: 0–49; Intermediate: 50–79; Ideal: 80–100 [14].

Covariates and Outcome

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance, including: Demographics: age (continuous), sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, other); Socioeconomic factors: education (categorized), marital status (married/unmarried), and poverty‐income ratio (PIR, continuous); Clinical parameters: body mass index (BMI), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the CKD‐EPI creatinine equation [23]), self‐reported history of heart disease, stroke, cancer, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and antihypertensive agent use; Lifestyle: smoking status and alcohol use (yes/no, self‐reported). All covariates were measured using standardized NHANES protocols, detailed at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality, ascertained via linkage to the National Death Index, with follow‐up through December 31, 2019. Time‐to‐event was defined as the duration from the NHANES exam date to death or censoring at the end of follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. Baseline characteristics were compared across UACR categories. Categorical variables were reported as weighted percentages; continuous variables as weighted means ± SD or medians (IQR), as appropriate. Chi‐square tests were used for categorical variables; weighted one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. Sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units were incorporated to account for NHANES's complex survey design. Multivariable Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between UACR/CVH (as continuous and categorical variables) and all‐cause mortality. Analyses were conducted in three hierarchical models: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR, and eGFR; Model 3: Further adjusted for history of heart disease, stroke, and cancer. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) models with three knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) were used to examine nonlinear relationships of continuous UACR and CVH with mortality. The likelihood ratio test compared RCS models with linear alternatives. Covariates from Model 3 were included in all spline models. To assess joint associations of UACR and CVH with mortality, a 12‐category composite variable was created by cross‐classifying the four UACR categories with the three CVH categories. This variable was entered into Cox models using the ideal CVH and normal UACR group as the reference. Linear trend testing and interaction terms were used to evaluate synergistic effects. Causal mediation analysis was conducted to assess whether UACR mediated the association between CVH and all‐cause mortality. In this study, the direct effect represented the association between CVH and all‐cause mortality in hypertension patients; the indirect effect was mediated by UACR; the proportion mediated indicated the percentage of the mediating effect. Model 3 covariates were used for adjustment. Nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 replications) estimated 95% CIs.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among 9154 adults with hypertension (median age 62 years; 49.6% men), participants were stratified into four UACR categories: normal (<10 mg/g, n = 4809), mildly increased (10–29.9 mg/g, n = 2510), moderately increased (30–299.9 mg/g, n = 1444), and severely increased (≥300 mg/g, n = 391). Individuals with higher UACR levels were more likely to be older, male, non‐Hispanic Black, unmarried, and have lower poverty‐income ratios (p < 0.001). They also had lower estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR), higher prevalence of heart disease, stroke, cancer, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and antihypertensive agent use history (p < 0.001), and were more likely to be current or former smokers (p = 0.013). BMI also varied significantly by UACR group (p < 0.05), while alcohol use did not differ across groups (p = 0.094). Full baseline characteristics by UACR category are shown in Table 1.

Association of CVH Score With All‐Cause Mortality

Crude and adjusted associations between CVH score and all‐cause mortality are presented in Table 2. When modeled continuously, each 10‐point increase in CVH score was associated with a 13% reduction in mortality risk (Model 3: HR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.83–0.92; p < 0.001). Categorical analysis revealed progressively higher mortality risk with lower CVH categories (p for trend < 0.01). Compared to participants with ideal CVH, those with poor CVH had significantly higher mortality risk (Model 3: HR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.31–2.45; p < 0.001), whereas the moderate CVH group showed no statistically significant difference. RCS analysis confirmed a nonlinear association between CVH and mortality, with an inflection point at a score of 60.2 (p = 0.003; Figure 2A), suggesting disproportionately increased risk below this threshold.

Association of UACR With All‐Cause Mortality

As shown in Table 3, UACR was positively associated with mortality both continuously and categorically. Each 100 mg/g increase in UACR was associated with a 3% higher risk of death (Model 3: HR = 1.03; 95% CI: 1.02–1.04; p < 0.001). Compared with individuals with normal UACR, the fully adjusted HRs for mortality were: mildly increased: HR = 1.51 (95% CI: 1.25–1.81); moderately increased: HR = 2.22 (95% CI: 1.85–2.67); severely increased: HR = 4.43 (95% CI: 3.51–5.60) (all p < 0.001). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear UACR–mortality association, with a threshold at 9.53 mg/g (p < 0.001; Figure 2B), suggesting that even mildly elevated UACR (10–29.9 mg/g) within the “normal” range was associated with increased mortality risk.

Joint Association of UACR and CVH with Mortality

The combined effects of UACR and CVH on mortality are summarized in Table 5 and visualized in Figure 3. In participants with ideal CVH, moderately elevated UACR was significantly associated with higher mortality risk (Model 3: HR = 2.93; 95% CI: 1.44–5.96; p = 0.003), while severe UACR was not significant, likely due to small sample size (n = 6; HR = 4.76; 95% CI: 0.92–24.59; p = 0.063) (Table 5). In moderate CVH, mortality risk increased stepwise with rising UACR levels (HRs: 1.37, 2.10, and 3.99 for mildly, moderately, and severely UACR categories, respectively; all p < 0.001) (Table 5). Similar patterns were observed in the poor CVH group, with the highest risk in the severely elevated UACR subgroup (HR = 4.63; 95% CI: 3.19–6.71; p < 0.001) (Table 5). The 12‐level joint exposure analysis revealed a significant interaction between UACR and CVH (p for trend < 0.001) (Figure 3). Participants with severely elevated UACR and poor CVH had the highest mortality risk (HR = 6.61; 95% CI: 3.72–11.74) (Figure 3). Interestingly, within the poor CVH group, individuals with normal UACR did not exhibit a significantly increased mortality risk (Figure 3).

Mediation Analysis

Causal mediation analysis demonstrated that UACR significantly mediated the relationship between CVH and mortality (Figure 4 and Table S1). We found that both direct and indirect effects played a significant role in the association of CVH and all‐cause mortality in adults with hypertension. Ultimately, UACR accounted for 4.01% of the total effect (95% CI: 2.83%–6.40%; p < 0.001) of the observational association of CVH with risk of all‐cause mortality, indicating substantial partial mediation.

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of U.S. adults with hypertension, we found that both elevated urinary UACR and diminished CVH, as defined by the AHA's LE8 score, were independently associated with increased risk of all‐cause mortality. Importantly, these factors demonstrated a synergistic effect, with the highest mortality observed among individuals with both poor CVH and severely elevated UACR. Mediation analysis further revealed that UACR accounted for over 4.01% of the association between CVH and mortality, suggesting a pathophysiologic role beyond mere correlation.

These findings build on and substantially advance the existing body of literature by addressing a critical gap in our understanding of how CVH and UACR interact to influence mortality risk in adults with hypertension. Although prior studies have independently linked low CVH scores and elevated UACR to adverse outcomes in general or mixed populations [6, 7, 17, 18], few have systematically investigated their combined prognostic implications in hypertensive individuals—a population uniquely predisposed to concurrent vascular and renal injury due to chronic hemodynamic stress and systemic inflammation. Our study not only confirms these independent associations in a large, nationally representative hypertensive cohort but also identifies a nonlinear threshold effect in the relationship between CVH and mortality, with markedly increased risk observed when CVH scores fall below approximately 60 [24, 25, 26, 27]. This inflection point, observed in the context of rigorous spline modeling, provides a clinically actionable benchmark for early intervention. It suggests that individuals with suboptimal but modifiable health behaviors may carry disproportionately higher mortality risk, even if they do not meet traditional criteria for high cardiovascular risk based on blood pressure or comorbidity profiles alone. Importantly, by focusing on a hypertensive population and using the AHA's updated LE8 framework—which integrates newer metrics such as sleep health—this study enhances the relevance, precision, and contemporary applicability of CVH scoring in clinical settings [28]. These findings support the utility of CVH as more than a conceptual health promotion tool; rather, they position it as a quantifiable, evidence‐based predictor of mortality that can guide risk stratification and personalized care planning in one of the largest and most at‐risk patient groups worldwide [29, 30].

Simultaneously, we identified a graded, nonlinear association between UACR and all‐cause mortality, with a risk inflection point emerging at a UACR as low as 9.53 mg/g. This is substantially below the conventional clinical threshold of 30 mg/g used to define microalbuminuria [9], and it reinforces mounting evidence that even low‐grade albuminuria—traditionally considered “normal”—may reflect underlying microvascular and endothelial dysfunction [8, 31, 32]. While previous studies have reported similar associations in general and diabetic populations [7, 33, 34], our findings are among the first to characterize this relationship specifically in adults with hypertension, a group uniquely susceptible to subtle renal injury and progressive vascular damage.

More importantly, our results suggest that CVH, as measured by the AHA's LE8, modifies the prognostic utility of UACR. In individuals with ideal CVH, elevated UACR was still associated with increased mortality, but the magnitude of risk was attenuated relative to those with poor CVH. Conversely, hypertensive individuals with poor CVH but normal UACR exhibited lower mortality than expected, suggesting a protective influence of preserved kidney function even in the presence of systemic cardiovascular stressors. This interaction between UACR and CVH is a novel and clinically relevant insight, indicating that the prognostic meaning of albuminuria is context‐dependent and that isolated evaluation of either parameter may underestimate true risk. Together, these findings highlight the potential for a paradigm shift in cardiorenal risk assessment, wherein UACR is no longer interpreted as a binary marker of kidney disease alone but as a dynamic risk indicator whose impact is shaped by an individual's broader cardiovascular health profile. This approach could enhance personalized risk stratification and support earlier, more targeted interventions in hypertensive patients before overt end‐organ damage occurs.

This study is among the first to rigorously quantify the mediating role of UACR in the association between CVH and all‐cause mortality, specifically, in adults with hypertension. The observed mediation proportion of 4.01% is consistent with prior estimates reported in general adult cohorts, where albuminuria typically explains 7% to 10% of the association between lifestyle risk factors and mortality [7]. This finding underscores the heightened susceptibility of hypertensive individuals to microvascular injury, glomerular barrier dysfunction, and systemic endothelial activation—processes that collectively amplify the adverse consequences of poor cardiovascular health [35, 36]. The findings offer empirical support for the evolving concept of the cardiorenal axis, in which vascular and renal pathophysiology operate in a bidirectional and synergistic manner [37, 38]. In this framework, even modest elevations in albuminuria may reflect early renal involvement in systemic disease, serving both as a marker of cumulative cardiovascular stress and as a mechanistic contributor to downstream organ damage. By demonstrating that UACR partially mediates the CVH–mortality relationship, our study positions albuminuria not simply as a parallel risk factor, but as an intermediate phenotype that may bridge lifestyle‐driven cardiovascular dysfunction and premature death.

Our study strengthens the recognition that albuminuria carries cardiovascular significance even when its contribution appears modest. The finding that UACR mediates 4.01% of the association between CVH and all‐cause mortality indicates that albuminuria represents only one of several biological pathways linking poor CVH to adverse outcomes, yet it remains clinically meaningful. Even a small proportion of mediated risk may translate into a substantial number of preventable deaths at the population level given the high prevalence of both hypertension and albuminuria. Importantly, UACR is a readily available, low‐cost, and noninvasive biomarker, making it a practical component of cardiovascular risk stratification. Furthermore, interventions that lower albuminuria—such as renin–angiotensin system inhibitors and sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors—have been shown to improve renal and cardiovascular outcomes, suggesting that part of their survival benefit may be mediated through this pathway [35, 39]. Consistent with our results, prior studies have shown that higher UACR correlates with elevated blood pressure, systemic inflammation (C‐reactive protein), and cardiac stress (B‐type natriuretic peptide), supporting its role as an integrative marker of vascular and hemodynamic burden [40]. These findings collectively emphasize the value of regular UACR surveillance in hypertensive patients and suggest that prevention or attenuation of albuminuria progression may offer incremental mortality risk reduction [41]. Although our findings do not prove causality, they provide a rationale for prospective studies to evaluate whether targeted albuminuria‐lowering interventions can translate into measurable survival benefit.

Our findings have several important clinical implications. First, they support the use of UACR screening—even at low levels—as a routine component of cardiovascular risk assessment in hypertensive patients. Current clinical guidelines often reserve UACR testing for patients with diabetes or established kidney disease [13, 42, 43]; however, our data suggest that early identification of low‐grade albuminuria could uncover hidden risk and inform preventative care. Second, the results reinforce the value of the LE8 CVH score as a comprehensive, modifiable risk construct that can be used not only to predict cardiovascular outcomes but also to identify patients at risk of renal dysfunction and mortality. Interventions targeting CVH—through diet, physical activity, smoking cessation, blood pressure control, glycemic optimization, and sleep hygiene—may confer dual cardiovascular and renal benefits. Third, the significant interaction and mediation effects observed between CVH and UACR suggest that an integrated, dual‐pathway approach to risk reduction may be particularly effective. Tailored strategies that address both behavioral risk factors and subclinical renal injury could yield synergistic benefits, particularly in the early stages of disease when interventions are more likely to alter long‐term trajectories.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a large, nationally representative cohort with standardized, high‐quality assessments of mortality, cardiovascular health, and kidney biomarkers. The use of multivariable Cox models, restricted cubic spline analyses, joint exposure modeling, and causal mediation techniques provides a robust and nuanced evaluation of the complex interplay between CVH, UACR, and mortality. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both CVH and UACR were measured at a single time point, limiting our ability to assess longitudinal changes or causal directionality. Second, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic confounders, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Third, subgroup analyses—particularly among participants with severely elevated UACR and ideal CVH—were limited by small sample sizes, potentially affecting statistical precision. Fourth, we acknowledge that measuring UACR from a single spot urine sample rather than a 24‐h urine collection—the reference standard—may introduce measurement variability. Spot UACR can be affected by diurnal fluctuations, hydration status, and short‐term physiological changes, potentially leading to misclassification of albuminuria severity. Moreover, any random measurement error introduced by using spot samples would likely bias results toward the null, suggesting that the true association between UACR and mortality might be even stronger than observed [44, 45, 46]. Fifth, considering our findings are derived from a US cohort, their generalizability to populations with different genetic backgrounds, lifestyles, or healthcare systems requires further investigation in external validation cohorts. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that both diminished CVH and elevated UACR are independently and synergistically associated with increased all‐cause mortality among adults with hypertension. Furthermore, UACR significantly mediates the association between CVH and mortality, highlighting its role as both a marker and a mechanistic link in the cardiorenal axis. These findings underscore the value of incorporating renal biomarkers into routine cardiovascular risk assessments and support early, dual‐targeted interventions to improve outcomes in this high‐risk population.
