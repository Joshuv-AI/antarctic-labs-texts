Characterising flavour differences in Mandarin fish muscle under live prey and formulated feed diets using flavouromics and metabolomics
Authors: Yang Cheng, Junjian Dong, Hetong Zhang, Yuan Zhang, Fengying Gao, Fubao Wang, Ziyang Wang, Xing Ye, Chengbin Wu, Chengfei Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubstituting formulated feed for trash fish in aquaculture has the potential to reduce farming costs and decrease dependence on natural resources. However, the impact of such feed substitution on muscle flavour remains unclear. This study is the first to utilise two-dimensional gas chromatography coupled with time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC × GC-TOF MS) and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to analyse differences in muscle flavour between mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) fed live prey and those fed formulated feed. The results identified 65 differential flavour compounds, with four key contributors: 2,3-butanedione, (E)-2-nonenal, furan, 2-pentyl-, and (E)-2-octenal. Metabolomic analysis revealed 139 differential metabolites, with significant upregulation of amino acid metabolites such as threonine and L-homoserine in the muscle of fish fed formulated feed. KEGG pathway enrichment highlighted glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis as critical pathways. Correlation analysis demonstrated significant associations between precursors like dihydroxyacetone phosphate and O-phosphoserine and key flavour compounds. The findings indicate that mandarin fish fed live prey exhibited a more pronounced nutty, buttery, ham-like aroma alongside a fresh, grassy scent, whereas those fed formulated feed showed a diminished earthy off-flavour. Dihydroxyacetone phosphate, L-homoserine, threonine, and linoleic acid were identified as important flavour precursors. Overall, these findings provide a theoretical basis for optimising the muscle flavour of mandarin fish in feed-based aquaculture.Keywords: Mandarin fish, Flavour, Flavouromics, Metabolomics, Live prey, Formulated feed

Introduction

Traditional feeding practices for carnivorous fish rely heavily on natural live prey resources, such as trash fish. Consequently, the rapid expansion of global aquaculture has resulted in dual pressure on natural resources. Overfishing of trash fish exacerbates the fragility of the marine ecological chain, while challenges such as pathogen transmission and nutritional imbalance associated with live prey limit aquaculture efficiency and product quality. As a result, the development and adoption of formulated feeds have become pivotal for industrial transformation. Compared with trash fish, formulated feed offers greater ecological and economic sustainability (Deng et al., 2019). Li et al. demonstrated that the survival rate and feed efficiency of Epinephelus akaara fed formulated feed were higher than those fed raw fish (Li et al., 2004). Similarly, Ye et al. showed that formulated feed more effectively enhances digestive capacity, immune function, and the structure of intestinal microbiota in juvenile hybrid groupers compared with trash fish (Ye et al., 2020). Additionally, the production process of formulated feed enables scalability and standardisation, aligning with the core requirements of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), specifically Goal 12: “Responsible consumption and production(United Nations General Assembly, 2015, Goal 12). Therefore, substituting formulated feed for trash fish in the cultivation of carnivorous species represents not only an inevitable path for industry advancement but also a strategic choice that promotes ecological and economic sustainability in aquaculture.

However, whether the substitution of formulated feed for trash fish affects flavour quality has become a focal point of interdisciplinary research in aquaculture and food science. Flavour refers to the comprehensive sensory perception of food, primarily involving human oral sensory organs, including the olfactory and gustatory senses. It is a critical attribute of aquatic products that directly influences consumer acceptance (Spence, 2022; Xu et al., 2021). In recent years, rapid advancements in flavouromics and metabolomics have driven technological innovation in food flavour research. Two-dimensional gas chromatography with time -of -flight mass spectrometry (GC × GC-TOF MS) is an advanced flavouromics technique used for the efficient detection of volatile flavour compounds (Sun et al., 2023) and is characterised by strong separation capability, high selectivity, and superior resolution (Schwanz et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) is a non-targeted metabolomics technique employed for the separation and identification of small-molecule metabolites (Ruan et al., 2023), and is characterised by high sensitivity and strong specificity (Liao et al., 2022). The development and widespread application of these technologies have facilitated the analysis of food flavour compounds and their formation mechanisms. Fu et al. utilised LC-MS and headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) to analyse the effects of a broad bean diet on the texture and flavour of grass carp. Their research showed that feeding broad beans significantly improved the texture of grass carp muscle but diminished the overall taste and flavour (Fu et al., 2024). Sensory evaluation, biochemical assays, and molecular analyses conducted by Xiong et al. revealed that Chinese mitten crabs fed formulated feed exhibited a reduced fishy odour compared to those fed frozen fish, while also demonstrating enhanced umami taste and a more intense and pleasant aroma (Xiong et al., 2023).

Mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi), endemic to East Asia, are naturally distributed across China, Russia, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. This high-quality freshwater species is renowned for its tender texture and exquisite flavour, making it highly valued commercially. In 2023, the total production of mandarin fish through aquaculture in China reached 478,000 metric tonnes (Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, Fisheries, and Fishery Administration Bureau, 2023). Mandarin fish exhibit a strong preference for live prey throughout their lifecycle. Although artificial domestication using formulated feeds is now feasible in aquaculture, quality differences between mandarin fish raised on live prey and those raised on formulated feed—particularly regarding flavour characteristics—remain unreported. Therefore, this study employed GC × GC-TOF MS and LC-MS technologies for the first time to analyse the muscle flavour of mandarin fish fed live prey versus those fed formulated feed. The aim was to elucidate the effects of different feeding regimes on the flavour components and metabolic pathways in mandarin fish muscle. Furthermore, by identifying key flavour compounds and their precursors, the metabolic mechanisms underlying flavour formation were explored. These findings highlight distinct flavour differences in mandarin fish muscle under different feeding conditions and offer a valuable reference for flavour improvement in other aquatic products.

Materials and methods

Preparation of mandarin fish muscle samples

The experimental fish were cultured at the Mingying Aquaculture Cooperative in the Dongcheng District of Sihui City, Guangdong Province. Progeny from the same batch of broodstock were initially reared on a complementary diet of mud carp baitfish until they reached a body length of 5 cm. At this stage, half of the progeny continued to be reared with mud carp baitfish and were designated as the live prey group (group B). The remaining half underwent feed domestication, following a progressive feeding protocol: live mud carp (3d) → fresh dead mud carp (5d) → dead mud carp combined with sticky powdered feed (7d) → formulated feed (15d). Upon completion of the domestication process, these fish were cultured exclusively on formulated feed and designated as the feed group (group F). The feed used for feeding mandarin fish in the feed group was the extruded compound feed for mandarin fish produced by Foshan Nanhai Jieda Feed Co., Ltd. Feed formula: fish meal, soybean meal, flour, calcium dihydrogen phosphate, sodium selenite, vitamin A, vitamin D3, vitamin E, vitamin K3, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, vitamin B6, vitamin C, niacin, folic acid, inositol, etc. Both groups were cultured under identical conditions using a recirculating aquaculture system, with each group comprising three parallel tanks, resulting in a total of six culture tanks (1 m3 each), with 30 fish per tank. The water temperature was maintained at 26 °C ± 1 °C; pH ranged from 7.2 to 7.8; dissolved oxygen was maintained at ≥5 mg/L; ammonia nitrogen concentration remained below 0.2 mg/L; and nitrite concentration was kept below 0.1 mg/L. Sampling was conducted after six months of culture, when the average body weight reached 600 g. Anaesthesia was administered using tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222), followed by the extraction of dorsal muscle tissue. For flavouromic analysis, muscle samples were collected in triplicate per group (one fish per tank). For metabolomic analysis, six muscle tissue samples were collected per group (two fish per tank). All samples were placed in 50 mL centrifuge tubes and stored at −20 °C.

Muscle quality index assessment

Muscle pH measurement

A 5 g sample of dorsal white muscle was minced and transferred to a homogenisation tube. Subsequently, 45 mL of distilled water was added to the mixture. The sample was homogenised on ice and then allowed to stand for 10 min. The pH was measured using a pH meter. Each sample was analysed in triplicate (Zhu et al., 2017).

Hydrodynamic assessment of muscle tissue

Dorsal white muscle was selected and cut into appropriately sized cubes, each weighing approximately 5 g. Three muscle cubes were taken from each fish. Surface moisture was blotted using filter paper, and the mass of each cube was measured accurately. Each cube was then placed in a separate resealable bag. One sample was stored at 4 °C for 24 h, then removed, blotted dry, and weighed. Another sample was stored at −20 °C for 24 h, thawed, blotted dry, and weighed. The third sample was boiled for 5 min, blotted dry, and weighed. The drip loss rate, flesh leaching loss rate, and liquid loss rate were calculated based on the changes in mass (Ma et al., 2015).

GC × GC-TOF MS flavouromics analysis

The internal standard solution was prepared as follows: deuterated n-hexanol-d13 and n-alkane standards (1000 mg/L) were diluted to 1 mg/L using a 50 % aqueous ethanol solution and n-hexane, respectively, and stored at 4 °C in the dark.

Next, 0.5 g of the sample was precisely weighed and placed in a 20 mL headspace vial. Then, 10 μL of the internal standard solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at 80 °C for 10 min. The SPME extraction head was preconditioned at 270 °C for 10 min, then inserted into the incubated headspace vial and exposed for adsorption at 80 °C for 25 min, followed by desorption in the GC injection port at 250 °C for 5 min. Additionally, 10 μL of an n-alkane solution was used as an external standard for retention index calculation.

Chromatographic conditions: Analyses were carried out using a LECO Pegasus®4D instrument (LECO, St. Joseph, MI, USA) comprising an Agilent 8890A GC system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a split/splitless injector and a dual-stage cryogenic modulator (LECO), coupled with a TOFMS detector (LECO). A DB-Heavy Wax column (30 m × 250 μm I.D., 0.5 μm) (Agilent, USA) was used as the first -dimension column (1D), and an Rxi-5Sil MS column (2.0 m × 150 μm I.D., 0.15 μm) (Restek, USA) was used as the second-dimension column (2D). The carrier gas was high-purity helium (>99.999 %) at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature programme was as follows: the initial temperature was held at 50 °C for 2 min, then increased to 230 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and held for 5 min. The secondary oven temperature was maintained 5 °C higher than the primary oven. The modulator temperature was kept 15 °C higher than the secondary oven. The modulation period was 6.0 s. The GC injector temperature was set to 250 °C. (Li et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2020).

Mass spectrometry conditions: Flavour compound analysis was performed using a LECO Pegasus BT 4D. The transfer line and TOF MS ion source temperatures were both set to 250 °C. The acquisition frequency was 200 spectra/s. The mass spectrometer operated in electron ionisation (EI) mode at 70 eV, with a mass scan range of m/z 35–550 and a detector voltage of 1960 V (Li et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2020).

The relative odds activity value (ROAV) was used to assess the contribution of each volatile compound to the overall flavour profile. The formula for ROAV was as follows:

The odour threshold of compound TA and the peak area of PeakA were assumed to be the lowest among all detected compounds. The ROAV of component A was set as the standard value of 100. The odour threshold and peak area of compound TB and PeakB were then measured. Compounds with a relative odour activity value (ROAV) ≥ 1 were considered key flavour compounds (Sun et al., 2023).

LC-MS untargeted metabolomics analysis

The sample was accurately weighed and placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, after which 1000 μL of tissue extraction solution containing steel beads (75 % (9:1 methanol: chloroform): 25 % H2O) was added. The tissue was ground twice at 50 Hz using a tissue homogeniser (Company name, City, State, Country) for 60 s each time, followed by sonication at room temperature for 30 min and subsequently in an ice bath for 30 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of 50 % acetonitrile solution containing 4 ppm 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (internal standard), filtered through a 0.22 μg membrane, and prepared for analysis (Warren et al., 2017).

Chromatographic conditions: LC analysis was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Chromatographic separation was conducted using an ACQUITY UPLC ® HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The column was maintained at 40 °C. The flow rate and injection volume were set at 0.3 mL/min and 2 μL, respectively. For LC-ESI (+)-MS analysis, the mobile phases consisted of (B2) 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (v/v) and (A2) 0.1 % formic acid in water (v/v). Separation was achieved using the following gradient: 0–1 min, 10 % B2; 1–5 min, 10 %–98 % B2; 5–6.5 min, 98 % B2; 6.5–6.6 min, 98 %–10 % B2; 6.6–8 min, 10 % B2. For LC-ESI (−)-MS analysis, separation was performed using (B3) acetonitrile and (A3) 5 mM ammonium formate. The gradient conditions were as follows: 0–1 min, 10 % B3; 1–5 min, 10 %–98 % B3; 5–6.5 min, 98 % B3; 6.5–6.6 min, 98 %–10 % B3; 6.6–8 min, 10 % B3 (Zelena et al., 2009).

Mass spectrometry conditions: Mass spectrometric detection of metabolites was performed using a Q Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ESI ion source. Simultaneous MS1 and MS/MS acquisition was conducted in Full MS-ddMS2 mode (data-dependent MS/MS). The parameters were as follows: sheath gas pressure, 40 arb; auxiliary gas flow, 10 arb; spray voltage, 3.50 kV and −2.50 kV for ESI(+) and ESI(−), respectively; capillary temperature, 325 °C; MS1 range, m/z 100–1000; MS1 resolving power, 70,000 FWHM; number of data-dependent scans per cycle, 10; MS/MS resolving power, 17,500 FWHM; normalised collision energy, 30 eV; dynamic exclusion time, automatic (Want et al., 2013).

Data analysis

The NIST2020 database and Chroma TOF software were used to annotate flavour compounds from the raw data. The PubChem database and ClassyFire software (Djoumbou Feunang et al., 2016) were employed for classification and annotation analysis of the detected flavour compounds, including the quantification of the number and relative content of each class. Sensory flavour comparisons were conducted using FlavorDB (Thévenot et al., 2015). Based on data from the FlavorDB database, a network graph was constructed using the iGraph package to elucidate the relationships between flavour compounds according to their unique sensory characteristics. Multivariate statistical analysis was carried out using SIMCA-P (v13.0) and the ropls package in R (Boulesteix & Strimmer, 2007). Overfitting of the model was evaluated via permutation testing. Differential compounds were selected based on the following criteria: p < 0.05 from a t-test or one-way ANOVA, and VIP > 1. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the hypergeometric distribution method (Xia & Wishart, 2011). Correlation analysis between differential flavour compounds and metabolites was conducted via the BioDeep (https://www.biodeep.cn) and GENE DENOVO (https://www.genedenovo.com/) platforms. Adobe Photoshop 2024 (version 24.0.0, Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) was used for the visualisation of the flavour formation mechanism.

Results and discussion

Identification of volatile flavour compounds

Volatile flavour compounds in the muscle of both groups were identified using flavouromics-based GC × GC-TOF MS analysis. A total of 743 shared flavour compounds were detected in both groups (group B and group F), including 2,3-butanedione and dimethyl phthalate. In addition to these shared compounds, 557 unique flavour compounds were identified in group B, such as 2,3-pentanedione and 2,4-octadienal, while 545 unique flavour compounds were identified in group F, including (−)-aristolene and (−)-myrtenol (Fig. 1B; Table S2). The major classes of flavour compounds identified in both groups included alcohols, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, esters, heterocyclic compounds, hydrocarbons, and ketones. Hydrocarbons were the most abundant class in both groups, followed by alcohols (Fig. 1C). Notably, a significant difference in relative alcohol content was observed between the two groups: group B exhibited a relative alcohol content of 16.22 %, whereas group F showed a significantly higher value of 27.39 % (Fig. 1D). Alcohols are typically formed via the enzymatic oxidative degradation of n-3 and n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids, as well as the reduction of carbonyl compounds (Zhang et al., 2018). Compared with group F, significantly higher levels of 1-octen-3-ol were detected in group B. This compound, known for its mushroom-like and earthy odour (Liu et al., 2012), is regarded as a key contributor to the earthy aroma commonly associated with fish meat (Zhang et al., 2019). In a previous study, Xiong et al. reported that the trimethylamine content in the muscle of Chinese mitten crabs was significantly lower in individuals fed formulated feed compared with those fed ice-fresh fish. As trimethylamine has a low sensory threshold and imparts a strong fishy odour, its reduction contributed to a milder, less fishy flavour in the feed group (Xiong et al., 2023). Collectively, previous research alongside our findings suggests that feeding formulated feed may reduce the fishy taste of mandarin fish muscle.

ROAV serves as an effective standard for flavour evaluation, enabling the identification of primary flavour-contributing substances detected by taste receptors. Compounds with an ROAV ≥1 are considered to contribute significantly to perceived flavour. In both groups, the key flavour compounds identified were 2,3-butanedione, 2-methylbutanal, heptanal, (E)-2-nonenal, furan, 2-pentyl-, and (E)-2-octenal (Fig. 1E).

To further understand the sensory differences, the flavour profiles of fish muscle from the two dietary groups were compared using FlavordB (Thévenot et al., 2015). Flavour was described using ten attributes: sweetness, oiliness, nuttiness, freshness, floral, fatty, waxy, fruity, grassy, and pungent. Both groups were primarily characterised by sweetness; however, group B exhibited more pronounced grassy and nutty notes compared with group F. Other flavour characteristics showed minimal differences between the groups (Fig. 1F). The enhanced grassy and nutty flavours observed in group B were likely attributable to the presence of distinct flavour compounds, including 2,3-pentanedione, trans-2-methyl-2-butenal, ethyl 2-methylbutyrate, and 2,4-octadienal. Among these, ethyl 2-methylbutyrate and 2,4-octadienal impart grassy notes, while 2,3-pentanedione and trans-2-methyl-2-butenal contribute nutty undertones and both grassy and nutty aromas, respectively. These compounds collectively account for the more pronounced grassy and nutty characteristics detected in group B. A study conducted by Xiong et al. found that the total aldehyde content in crab muscle increased following the use of a specific formulated feed, resulting in a more pronounced grassy flavour (Xiong et al., 2023). The differing flavour trends observed between Chinese mitten crabs and mandarin fish following compound feed administration may be attributed to the synergistic effects of differences in dietary nutrient composition and species-specific physiological traits.

Analysis of differential flavour compounds

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear distinction between groups F and B (Fig. 2A). A total of 65 differential flavour compounds were identified between the two groups (Table S3). To visualise these differences, differential heat maps and volcano plots were generated (Fig. 2B and C, Table S4). Among the differential compounds, 3-tert-butylphenol and ethylbenzene were significantly downregulated in group F compared to group B. Conversely, compounds such as 1,5-dimethyl-6-oxa-bicyclo[3.1.0]hexane, 3-phenyl-1,3-oxazolidin-2-one, and dimethyl phthalate were significantly upregulated. Further screening based on ROAV ≥1 identified four key differential flavour compounds: 2,3-butanedione, (E)-2-nonenal, (E)-2-octenal, and furan, 2-pentyl-. Violin plots were used to visualise the intergroup distribution of these four compounds (Fig. 2D). The results indicated that all four were significantly more abundant in group B. Each compound contributes uniquely to the overall sensory profile of the fish. For example, 2,3-butanedione is characterised by a creamy or buttery aroma, which may enhance the sensory appeal of mandarin fish (Liu et al., 2022). (E)-2-Nonenal, a metabolite of linoleic acid, imparts a fatty or waxy aroma. Owing to its low odour threshold, it exerts a strong sensory influence even at trace levels. (E)-2-Octenal, with a pronounced fatty odour, is typically derived from the metabolism of linoleic and arachidonic acids, and is considered a major contributor to fishy odours in aquatic species. Furan, 2-pentyl-, the most extensively studied volatile furan, is generated from linoleic acid metabolism and is known for its ham-like aroma and plant-like fragrance. With a low sensory threshold, it plays a substantial role in the flavour profile of fish meat (Fay & Brevard, 2005; Vichi et al., 2003). Collectively, these compounds contribute to the enhanced sensory complexity observed in group B. Similar findings have been reported in other species. For instance, Xiong et al. (2023) observed that the replacement of fresh fish with formulated feed significantly increased aldehyde concentrations in the muscle tissue of Chinese mitten crabs (Xiong et al., 2023). However, interspecies variations in metabolic regulation and feed composition may explain the differing trends observed in mandarin fish. In another study, Tan et al. reported a 2.25-fold increase in furan, 2-pentyl- content in the meat of pen-fed yaks compared to pasture-raised yaks (Tan et al., 2020), further illustrating the influence of dietary conditions on volatile compound profiles. Notably, in contrast to our findings, furan, 2-pentyl- content increased in yak meat, which may be attributed to fundamental differences in physiology, biochemistry, and metabolic regulation between species. Previous studies agree that distinct bait compositions exert considerable effects on flavour compounds. Research by Zhao et al. also demonstrated that (E)-2-nonenal, furan, 2-pentyl-, and (E)-2-octenal are critical contributors to the off-flavours characteristic of duck meat (Zhao et al., 2024). Overall, mandarin fish fed live prey appear to exhibit more pronounced buttery, ham-like aromas and grassy notes, whereas those fed formulated feed tend to develop a milder fishy odour.

To further investigate the relationship between flavour compounds and sensory perception, a network diagram was constructed using iGraph and FlavorDB. The network comprised the ten most common sensory flavour attributes and 21 associated differential flavour compounds (Fig. 2E). The results indicated that sweetness and grassy notes were the most prominent sensory features, aligning with the observations from the radar chart of flavour characteristics.

Differential metabolite analysis

The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score plot (Fig. 3A) demonstrated a clear separation between groups F and B, indicating significant differences in metabolite profiles under the two feeding regimes. A total of 139 differential metabolites were identified via MS2 analysis (Table S5). The differential metabolite heatmap and volcano plots (Fig. 3B, C, Table S6) revealed that 110 metabolites were upregulated, while 29 were downregulated in group F compared to group B. These findings suggest that the variation in muscle flavour between the two groups may be attributed to the upregulation of multiple metabolites associated with sensory attributes. The volcano plot (Fig. 3C) further highlighted the significant downregulation of O-phosphohomoserine and notable upregulation of indole and L-proline in group F. Additionally, several metabolites linked to flavour—such as O-phosphohomoserine, L-homoserine, L-threonine, and dihydroxyacetone phosphate—were identified. Notably, L-homoserine can be phosphorylated to produce O-phosphohomoserine, which serves as a metabolic precursor to L-threonine. The observed increases in O-phosphohomoserine, L-homoserine, and threonine levels in the muscles of mandarin fish from group F suggest potential synergistic regulation involving inhibition of phosphorylation and enhancement of hydrolysis. Zhou et al. (2014) similarly found that dietary variation significantly influenced amino acid content in fish muscle, further underscoring the impact of feed composition on muscle metabolite expression (Zhou et al., 2014). Although L-homoserine does not directly contribute to freshness, it can undergo transamination to form glutamic acid, which indirectly enhances freshness intensity. Therefore, the muscle of diet-fed mandarin fish may exhibit more pronounced sweetness and freshness characteristics. Threonine is a typical sweet-tasting amino acid and an important precursor for the production of pyrazines, which can be metabolically converted into ethyl amino acids, contributing to roasted, nutty, and other flavour characteristics (Zhu et al., 2023). Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) also plays a crucial role in muscle flavour development in mandarin fish. DHAP can be converted into glycerol-3-phosphate, a key intermediate in triglyceride synthesis. Triglycerides are subsequently broken down into fatty acids, which may be oxidised to form hexanal and nonanal—key contributors to grassy and fatty aroma notes (Sun et al., 2024). The significant increase in DHAP in the muscles of group F mandarin fish therefore suggests enhanced production of these volatile compounds, potentially intensifying the grassy and fatty sensory characteristics of the muscle tissue.

Analysis of differential metabolic pathways

To further analyse the potential metabolic pathways underlying the effects of different feeding regimes on muscle flavour in mandarin fish, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the differential metabolites. A total of 139 differential metabolites were significantly enriched across 51 metabolic pathways (Table S7). The top 20 pathways, ranked by p-values, were selected to generate bubble diagrams (Fig. 3D). These included aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis; pantothenic acid and coenzyme A biosynthesis; glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism; valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis; D-amino acid metabolism; tryptophan metabolism; and histidine metabolism. These pathways are primarily associated with amino acid metabolism, suggesting that diet-induced alterations in amino acid-related processes may play a major role in the distinct flavour profiles observed between the two groups. Among the enriched pathways, three exhibited the most significant differences: aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis; pantothenic acid and coenzyme A biosynthesis; and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism. Of these, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis showed the greatest level of significance. Aminoacyl-tRNA is a crucial intermediate in protein synthesis, enabling the attachment of amino acids to their corresponding tRNA molecules (Ling et al., 2023). In group F, several key flavour-related amino acid metabolites—including L-tryptophan, L-proline, L-valine, and L-threonine—were significantly downregulated within this pathway compared to group B. The downregulation of these amino acids may disrupt normal protein biosynthesis and associated downstream metabolic processes. Additionally, since these amino acids contribute directly or indirectly to flavour development, their reduced abundance may compromise muscle quality and diminish the flavour profile of S. chuatsi. The “glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism” pathway plays a crucial role in the formation of flavour compounds in the muscle tissue of mandarin fish. Differential metabolite analysis revealed significant alterations in L-homoserine, O-phosphohomoserine, and L-threonine within this pathway. Notably, L-homoserine functions as a key intermediate, which can be phosphorylated to form O-phosphohomoserine; this compound is subsequently metabolised into L-threonine. Importantly, L-homoserine serves as a central node in this pathway, capable of further catabolism to produce α-ketobutyrate. α-Ketobutyrate is a vital precursor in the biosynthesis of the key volatile flavour compound 2,3-butanedione. Therefore, fluctuations in the levels of L-threonine and its upstream metabolites (L-homoserine and O-phosphohomoserine) influence the production of α-ketobutyrate, which in turn significantly impacts the accumulation of 2,3-butanedione in mandarin fish muscle, thereby affecting flavour profile development. Supporting this interpretation, Feng et al. demonstrated in Cyprinus carpio var. Jian that dietary supplementation with moderate levels of threonine significantly increased crude protein content in muscle tissue (Feng et al., 2013). Increases in crude protein are closely linked to improved muscle texture (Bai et al., 2019), better retention of flavour compounds, and extended flavour perception. Furthermore, a review by Zhang et al. on the influence of amino acids on aquatic muscle quality confirmed that various amino acids exert important effects on the texture and flavour of fish (Zhang et al., 2023).

Joint analysis of differential volatile flavour compounds and differential metabolites

The formation of muscle flavours in mandarin fish involves multiple complex biochemical reactions, many of which are regulated by specific metabolites. To further investigate the potential metabolic contributors to flavour differences under distinct feeding regimes, a correlation analysis was conducted between 13 differential volatile compounds (with high ROAV values) and 28 differential metabolites (screened based on p-values). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to generate correlation heatmaps (Fig. 4A) and network diagrams (Fig. 4B). The results revealed strong associations between several metabolites—particularly DHAP, L-homoserine, and O-phosphohomoserine—and various volatile flavour compounds. Further analysis identified sugars (DHAP and galactose), amino acids (L-homoserine, O-phosphohomoserine, and threonine), and fatty acids (linoleic acid and arachidonic acid) as key flavour precursors.

(B) Correlation network diagram of differential flavour compounds with differential metabolites. (C) Diagram of the flavour formation mechanism.

Sugars are important precursors of volatile flavour compounds. Among the differential metabolites identified between the two groups, DHAP and galactose appear to influence the production of the key flavour compound 2,3-butanedione, which imparts creamy and buttery aromas. DHAP, an intermediate in glycolysis, is converted to 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde by the enzyme propylphosphate isomerase. Subsequent metabolism of 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde leads to the formation of pyruvate (Cui et al., 2010). Pyruvate is further metabolised in conjunction with threonine to form α-ketobutyric acid, which is then converted by pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC) into α-acetolactate. This intermediate is subsequently metabolised to generate 2,3-butanedione. Therefore, the observed differences in DHAP and galactose levels between the muscle tissues of the two groups may underlie the variation in 2,3-butanedione content.

Amino acids play a key role in multiple flavour-generating pathways, including the Maillard reaction, Strecker degradation, and transamination. Amino acid metabolites such as L-homoserine, O-phosphohomoserine, and L-tryptophan are significantly associated with various flavour compounds. For instance, Li et al. (2024) reported that groups with higher levels of free amino acids, such as tryptophan, exhibited superior flavour qualities (Li et al., 2024). L-Homoserine, an important intermediate in threonine biosynthesis (Zhang et al., 2021), is typically synthesised from aspartic acid through a series of enzymatic steps, forming aspartic semialdehyde and subsequently L-homoserine. Phosphorylation of L-homoserine produces O-phosphohomoserine, which is then hydrolysed to yield L-threonine. L-Threonine contributes to flavour development through two main routes: it can be converted into α-ketobutyric acid, which is subsequently metabolised to α-acetolactate and further reacted to produce 2,3-butanedione. Alternatively, L-threonine participates in the Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation, leading to the formation of a variety of volatile compounds, such as aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, esters, furans, and pyrazines (Diao et al., 2021). These metabolic pathways suggest that differences in amino acid metabolism, particularly involving L-threonine, are major contributors to the observed variations in muscle flavour between the two groups.

Several flavour-active volatiles, such as (E)-2-nonenal, furan, and 2-pentyl-, are primarily produced through the oxidative degradation of linoleic acid, while (E)-2-octenal is derived from the metabolism of linoleic acid and arachidonic acid (Zhao et al., 2024). Interestingly, although the metabolomic results showed no significant differences in the levels of linoleic and arachidonic acid between the two groups, their derivative volatile compounds exhibited significant variations. This discrepancy suggests that precursor abundance alone does not determine the levels of volatile compounds. Instead, factors such as enzyme activity, metabolic upregulation, and microbial interactions may play a crucial role in shaping the end-product profile. Therefore, the marked differences in (E)-2-nonenal, (E)-2-octenal, furan, and 2-pentyl- between the two groups of fish may reflect underlying differences in the efficiency of metabolic pathways, the regulation of oxidative enzymes, or gut microbiota composition. However, further research is required to clarify the exact mechanisms involved.

Conclusion

In this study, differences in muscle flavour between mandarin fish fed live prey and those fed compound diets were analysed using GC × GC-TOF MS and LC-MS techniques. Four key flavour compounds were identified: 2,3-butanedione, (E)-2-nonenal, (E)-2-octenal, and furan, 2-pentyl-. Fish fed live prey exhibited a more intense nutty, buttery, ham-like aroma, as well as grassy freshness, while those fed compound diets displayed a milder fishy flavour. Several metabolites, including DHAP, galactose, L-homoserine, O-phosphohomoserine, threonine, linoleic acid, and arachidonic acid, were identified as important flavour precursors that regulate the production of these key flavour compounds. This study provides insight into the flavour dynamics of mandarin fish muscle, paving the way for flavour enhancement in a variety of aquatic species. Future research should integrate metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics to systematically elucidate the full pathway from gene expression to enzyme activity, metabolic flux, and flavour compound production.
