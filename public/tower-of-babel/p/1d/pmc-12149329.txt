Where the road ends: emergency care sensitive conditions drive excess mortality in medevac-dependent rural Alaska
Authors: Brian Rice, Carla Britton, Stacy Rasmus, Tina Hernandez-Boussard
Published: 2024

Rural areas of the USA increasingly experience higher all-cause mortality than urban areas.1 Geographic variability in socioeconomic, racial/ethnic, lifestyle and metabolic characteristics accounts for some, but not all, of this disparity.2 The remaining excess mortality might be driven by disparities in emergency care access because rural residents often live in ‘ambulance deserts’ greater than 25 min from an ambulance station.3 Emergency care–sensitive conditions (ECSCs) are time-sensitive conditions (eg, stroke, myocardial infarction) for which emergency care can prevent mortality.4 We hypothesise that reduced access to emergency care in ambulance deserts contributes to excess ECSC mortality as compared with all other causes of death (non-ECSC).

Rural Alaska provides a unique opportunity to examine the effect of reduced access in its most extreme presentation. Over 80% of Alaska is not connected to a road system, and emergency care depends entirely on air medical ambulances (medevacs) to transport patients from remote villages to hospitals. Response times are measured in hours or days rather than the minutes typical for ground ambulances in the continental USA.5 Understanding how reliance on medevac services affects ECSC mortality will inform our group’s research on machine learning methods to support medevac decision-making in rural Alaska.

To investigate mortality differences associated with emergency care access, we used the CDC WONDER 1999–2020 mortality dataset (https://wonder.cdc.gov/). Alaska’s census tracts and boroughs were categorised into two groups by using publicly available administrative data to establish whether they contained a road system which connected patients to a hospital with surgical and medical capacity or whether a medevac was required for access (figure 1). These areas differed in health, lifestyle and socioeconomic factors previously associated with increased mortality.6 Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) with SEs for three groups (unintentional injuries, ECSC and non-ECSC) were exported from CDC WONDER into R to calculate rate ratios (RRs) and CIs.

The all-cause AAMR was 882.1 per 100 000 (95% CI 866.3 to 897.9) off-road and 731.7 (95% CI 725.7 to 737.7) on-road, producing RR=1.21 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.23). The AAMR for each group was higher in off-road regions during every 5-year period from 1999 to 2020 (figure 2). Both ECSC (51.4 (95% CI 42.8 to 60.0)) and unintentional injuries (38.8 (95% CI 34.2 to 43.3)) contributed more to the absolute rate difference between regions than did the more common non-ECSC causes (32.7 (95% CI 19.2 to 46.2)). Despite AAMR decreasing for ECSC and non-ECSC and increasing for unintentional injuries across the study period, the RR for cause-specific mortality outcomes between on-road and off-road regions remained unchanged over time.

While all-cause mortality was expectedly higher in off-road communities, it is striking that ECSC and unintentional injuries contributed disproportionately to that excess mortality. That this disparity has persisted for over 20 years, despite overall improvements in mortality, suggests a clear target for health system improvement. Policy discussions around rural health disparities in Alaska and nationwide often focus on structural issues like social determinants of health and the burdens of chronic disease, but emergency care is rarely part of the discussion. Our findings suggest that focusing on access to emergency care represents a direct and actionable opportunity for reducing excess mortality in rural communities. Notably, these findings may apply to rural regions worldwide, such as in Canada and Australia, which also depend largely on medevacs.7 Machine learning methods may be able to provide support for decision-making around medevac utilisation and target preventative interventions for patients at high risk for emergency care in rural Alaska. If successful, this work will pave the way for similar research throughout the rural USA and around the world.
