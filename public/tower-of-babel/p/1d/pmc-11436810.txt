Formation of acetonitrile (CH3CN) under cold interstellar, tropospheric and combustion mediums
Authors: Mohamad Akbar Ali, Saswathy R
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe knowledge of the formation of gas-phase carcinogenic acetonitrile (CH3CN) is limited in interstellar, troposphere, and combustion mediums; thus, its formation and fate are of great importance for all these gas-phase environments when accessing its toxicity and its wide range of applications. In this work, we propose a mechanism for the formation of CH3CN from the reaction of OH/O2 on ethanimine (CH3CH=NH) using ab initio/Density Functional Theory (DFT) potential energy surface in combination with microcanonical variational transition state theory (µVTST) and Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus (RRKM)/master equation (ME) simulation to predict the rate constants and branching fraction in the temperature range of 100 K to 1000 K and pressure range of 0.0001 bar to 100 bar. The reaction starts with cis (Z) and trans (E) CH3CH=NH isomer with OH radical followed by spontaneous formation via pre-reactive complex, forming the carbon and nitrogen-centered radicals. The O2 radical then attacks the carbon and nitrogen-centered radicals to form acetonitrile (CH3CN) and HO2 radicals. The results show that N–H and C–H dominate the H-atoms abstraction by OH radicals is similar to its isoelectronic analogous reaction system, i.e., CH3CHO + OH/O2 and CH3CHCH2 + OH/O2 and similar to methanimine (CH2NH) systems. The calculated rate constants for OH-initiated oxidation of CH3CH=NH are in the range of ~ 10–11 cm3 molecule−1 s−1 (at 300 K) and are in very good agreement with previous experimental values of its isoelectronic reaction system. The atmospheric lifetime due to the loss of CH3CHNH by OH radical (10 to 11 h) is in very good agreement with the similar pollutants in the troposphere temperature range between 200 and 320 K. The results indicate that its contribution to global warming is negligible. However, the formation of products such as CH3CN may interact with other atmospheric species, which could lead to the production of potentially hazardous compounds such as cyanogen (N2C2) and hydrogen cyanide (HCN).Keywords: Amines, Ethanimine, Photo-oxidation, Atmospheric, Interstellar, Combustion, Ab initio/DFT, Methyl cyanide, Microcanonical VTSTSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Atmospheric chemistry

Introduction

Amines (RNH2), especially alkyl-amines, are released into the atmosphere from a variety of sources such as treatment of sewage and waste, industries, automobiles, animal husbandry, and biogenic sources such as protein degradation, biomass burning, and ocean organisms1–6 and carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies7, which use amine-based aqueous solutions, usually monoethanol (MEA) amine, to absorb CO2, discharge significant amounts of amines during the processes2,4,6. As suggested7, the minimum amine concentrations do not appear to be very dangerous by themselves; however, when amines escape into the atmosphere, they undergo degradation and conversion reactions process. These processes occur in the atmosphere's presence of clouds, raindrops, and fog. Amines participate in atmospheric reactions with oxidizing agents such as •OH radical, nitrate (NO3) radical, and ozone (O3), resulting in organic nitrogen-containing molecules that can be condensed on or partitioned into the particle phase, thus enabling them to undergo heterogeneous reactions with acidic and carbonyl compounds. These reactions promote detrimental effects on air quality, leading to issues like acid rain, urban smog, tropospheric ozone growth, and the formation of atmospheric aerosols5. In addition, the products of the reaction of gaseous amines with •OH radical, O3 and NO3 may create the foundation of secondary organic aerosols (SOAs)5. Nielsen et al.7 provide complete details of the toxicity and fate of amines and their oxidation products under the atmospheric condition.

One of the amines, such as ethylamine (CH3CH2NH2), are emitted into the troposphere; they are expected to react with •OH radical, producing mostly carbon-centered (CH3•CH2NH2), along with fewer amounts of nitrogen-centered [CH3CH2N•H] as suggested in previous studies8–10. The carbon-centered radical is then attacked by the O2 radical to form ethanimine (CH3CH=NH) as a dominant product, as shown in Fig. 18–10. The atmospheric fate of CH3CH=NH is unknown because no experimental measurement and computational details have proceeded yet. This is due to the fact that the compound is highly reactive, soluble in water, and very sticky, thus posing many experimental challenges. Ethanimine is also an important prebiotic molecule since it is a precursor of amino acid α-alanine in Strecker synthesis11.

Photo-oxidation mechanism of Ethylamine8–10.

Imines such as ethanimine are among the molecular family important in astrobiology due to the presence of RCH=NH moiety12–15. It is an essential intermediate in forming the acetaldehyde ammonia trimer (AAT) in the presence of formic acid (HCOOH) at very low temperatures. In interstellar cloud medium (ISM), a great deal of chemistry could occur, where nearly 200 molecules have been detected. The astronomical analysis revealed that OH and O2 radicals are ubiquitous in the cold ISM medium where CH3CHNH, CH3CN, and HO2 are also identified16. Most of the identified molecules are organic in nature and can serve as precursors of many biological molecules like amino acids, nucleobases, and simple sugars16–19. The chemistry in the cold ISM region requires the knowledge of reliable rate constants from theoretical and experimental analysis to interpret the astronomical observations in terms of abundances relative to H nuclei, the most abundant species in the ISM. To the best of our knowledge, the formation of CH3CN from OH + O2 + CH3CHNH in the ISM medium is unknown and has not been published anywhere. Therefore, we have been motivated to understand the reaction mechanism and rate constants under low-temperature (<1000K) conditions.

Several theoretical and experimental analyses have been performed to understand the chemical behavior of CH3CHNH13–15. But the chemical kinetic detail of OH-initiated oxidation of CH3CHNH has yet to be discovered. Recently, atmospheric analysis of another higher imine i.e., methylmethanimine (CH3N=CH2) with OH radicals has been reported by Bunkun et al.20 The reported room temperature rate constant is 3.7 × 10–12 cm3 molecule−1 s−1 very similar to their earlier studies21 of OH + CH2NH reaction (~ 3. × 10–12 cm3 molecule−1 s−1). In our previous works22, we have computed the rate constant for hydroxyl radical reaction with the simplest imine, i.e., methanimine (CH2NH). We suggested that in the CH2NH + OH reaction, N–H abstraction is more favorable than C-H abstraction and OH addition under atmospheric conditions; however, at high temperatures, C-H abstraction plays a more important role. We reported the room temperature rate constants in the range ~ 10–11 cm3 molecule−1 s−122 using the ab initio//Density Functional Theory (DFT) method coupled with statistical rate theory.

It has also been suggested that biomass burning can release ethylamine, which can be further oxidized to form acetonitrile (CH3CN)23. The mechanism for forming acetonitrile, also known as cyanomethane, ethanenitrile, or methyl cyanide (CH3CN), is unknown under combustion conditions. Its low reactivity with atmospheric species makes it a long-lived pollutant that can reach the troposphere24. It has also been identified in other solar system objects, especially in the stratosphere and ionosphere of Titan, Saturn’s well-known satellite, and several comets. Because the reaction is important in all three mediums, we apply computational intuition to detail the understanding of the formation of CH3CN from the CH3CH=NH + OH + O2 reaction. The literature has already discussed the main sink for CH3CN with various radials24. It has been suggested that the main sink for CH3CN is due to photodissociation with N(2D), CH, C2H3, H, C2H and CN radicals24.

Due to the lack of experimental measurement, its importance in forming all three mediums, its toxicity and various applications, theoretical calculations provide the best alternative approach to investigate the reactions of CH3CHNH with OH/O2. Therefore, we explored the potential energy surface for both E and Z isomers of CH3CH=NH and discussed the important role of C–H, N–H abstraction reaction, and OH addition using the high-level ab initio//DFT method coupled with advanced statistical rate theories. The present study aims to report the temperature- and pressure-dependent rate constants and branching ratios of title reaction in all three different mediums. We aim to validate our results by comparing them with their counterpart's isoelectronic analogous reactions, i.e., OH/O2 + CH3CHO reaction and OH/O2 + CH3CHCH2. The ab initio//DFT method coupled with advanced statistical rate theory simulation reveals the formation of CH3CN and HO2 in all three mediums.

Computational methodology

Ab initio//DFT calculations

Geometries of all species involved in the reaction were optimized using the M06-2X/6–311+ + G(3df,3pd) level of theory25,26. To add corrections from the van der Waals interaction on M06-2X, the Grimme empirical dispersion “GD3” was used27. Rotational-vibrational analysis for each optimized species was carried out to obtain rotational energy and vibrational zero-point energy (ZPE). All the species show no imaginary frequency except the TSs, which show a single imaginary frequency. Intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations were carried out in both forward and reverse directions to confirm the identities of the pre-reactive and post-reaction complex for each TS28. Single-point energy calculations were carried out using the CCSD(T)/6–311 + + G(3df,3pd) level of theory29 at the M06-2X optimized geometries. The CCSD(T)//M06-2X combination has been tested by many research groups and provided reasonably accurate thermochemistry and chemical kinetics of many atmospheric reactions30–37. All the CCSD(T)/6–311 + + G(3df,3pd)//M06-2X with 6–311+ + G(3df,3pd) denoted as CCSD(T)//M06-2X calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 suite of programs38.

Statistical rate theory calculations

The pressure-dependent kinetics calculations were carried out using stochastic Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus (RRKM) theory39 as implemented in MultiWell ME code40–42. To account for the tunneling corrections, Eckart asymmetric tunneling correction was used. N2 bath gases were used with an approximate value of the energy transfer process < ΔE > down = 200 × (T/ 300)0.85 cm−143 for pressure and temperature-dependent branching ratios. The Lennard–Jones parameters for collider gases (N2) εkB, σ(N2) = 3.74 Å, ε/kB(N2) = 82 K were obtained from Hippler et al.44 The Lennard–Jones parameters of CH3CO2NH and CH3CO2HN were approximated based on previous study10 The pre-reactive complex plays a key role in the CH3CHNH + OH reaction systems, which is a barrierless reaction. The microcanonical variational state theory (µVTST) was used to calculate the rate constant for this reaction30. The theoretical details of µVTST for CH3CHNH + OH → CH3CH=NH….OH are given in Supporting Information.

Results and discussion

Atmospheric photo-oxidation of ethanimine

It is well known that the presence of C–H, N–H, and O–H bonds in the molecules is dominated by its reaction with OH radicals, which is the most dominant gas phase loss process for most tropospheric trace gases. The O2 radicals attack the generated carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen-centered radicals to lead to various toxic pollutants. The photo-oxidation process uses a reaction mechanism approach to propose plausible reaction pathways with the help of a potential energy surface diagram using ab initio//DFT methods and details in sections "Reaction Pathways for CH3CH=NH + OH" and "Reaction Pathways for CH3C⋅NH + O2".

Reaction pathways for CH3CH=NH + OH

The two isomers of cis-CH3CH=NH (Z) and trans-CH3CH=NH(E) are identified. The Z-CH3CH=NH isomer is more stable by ~ 0.6 kcal/mol than the E-CH3CH=NH. This value is in excellent agreement with the most accurate Active Thermochemical Tables (ATcT)45,46 database value (0.7 kcal/mol). The energy difference between cis and trans isomers is due to geometrical changes, is shown in Fig. 2.

The optimized structures of all other species are given in the supporting Information Table S1. The zero-point corrected energies of the pre-reactive complex, transition states, post-reactive complex and products are shown relative to the energy of reactants as shown in Fig. 4 (Fig. 4a: trans, Fig. 4b:cis). In the OH + CH3CH=NH reaction, a van der Waals complex formed, in which the H-atom of OH points towards the N-atom. The computed binding energies for forming Z and E isomers are almost equal, i.e., ~ 6.6 kcal/mol. This value agrees with the similar type of pre-reactive complex (N…H–O) observed in the previous studies30. The computed values are reasonably consistent with the value 5.5 kcal/mol computed by Bunkan et al.20 for OH + CH3N=CH2 reaction. Starting from the PRC, the OH molecule rotates in the plane until the O-atom comes close to the H-atom from N–H-atom, C–H, and –CH3 via the geometry of transition states, i.e., TS1, TS2, and TS3, respectively. The calculated energy barriers for abstraction reactions are 5.5 kcal/mol, 7.0 kcal/mol and 9 kcal/mol, respectively.

The calculated energy difference between these two isomer species is within ~ 1 kcal.

The enthalpies relative to the reactants for the E-Z isomer are shown in Fig. 4a and b and include some enthalpies from the ATcT45,46. The enthalpies of reaction for OH + CH3CHNH (E,Z) + OH → anti,syn-ethyldeneamidogen (anti,syn-CH3CHN⋅) + H2O are in excellent agreement with the ATcT thermochemical database within ~ 0.1–0.5 kcal/mol. The enthalpies of reaction for the OH + CH3CHNH (E, Z) → OH + ethyldeneamidogen CH3CHN• (syn,anti) + H2O reaction is in very good agreement with the ATcT thermochemical database (Fig. 4). These comparisons show that the CC//M06 combination is accurate within ~ 1 kcal/mol, providing more confidence in energy calculations. We have also compared the enthalpy of reaction value with isoelectronic reaction systems, i.e., CH3CHO + OH and CH3CHCH2 + OH reactions. The values are consistent with the current system's 2–3 kcal/mol difference. This difference is due to the different chemical environments of atoms in all three reaction systems are different.

Reaction pathways for CH3C•NH + O2

The 1-iminoethyl (CH3C•NH), ethyldeneamidogen (CH3CHN•) and 2-iminoethyl (•CH2CHNH) radicals are generated from the CH3CH=NH + OH. As suggested in the previous section, hydrogen abstraction from N–H and C-H is dominated over –CH3 and OH addition. Once CH3CHN⋅ and CH3C⋅NH radicals are generated, the O2 molecules can attack to these radicals and lead to the formation of CH3CN. The optimized geometries and structural change for the CH3C⋅NH + O2 are shown in Fig. 5.

The zero-point corrected potential energy surface (PES) for the CH3C⋅NH + O2 reaction is shown in Fig. 6 and PES for CH3CHN⋅ + O2 is shown in Supporting Information. The mechanism of CH3C⋅NH + O2 is very similar to the earlier mechanism for O2 addition to the carbon-centered radical forming intermediate CH3COONH (Int-c) and leading to the different products + HO234,35,37.

Zero-point corrected potential energy surface for O2 + CH3C⋅NH reaction.

The computed energy for Int-c is -41.7 kcal mol−1, which is in good agreement with the reported values by Bunkan et al.20 for a similar reaction system. The most favorable is the H-migration via a five-membered cyclic transition state (TS1c) leading to the form Int2 (QOOH). The calculated barrier height for TS1c is 14.6 concerning INT-c, similar to the H-transfer reaction. The Int2 further decomposes by eliminating the HO2 radical to form CH3CN via a TS2c. The calculated barrier for HO2 elimination is ~ 15 kcal/mol energy, similar to HO2 elimination of ⋅CH2NH2 + O2 and ⋅CH2OH + O2 reactions. The enthalpies of reaction (ΔHrxn (0 K) for CH3C⋅NH + O2 → CH3CN + ⋅HO2 (− 36.5 kcal mol−1) and CH3CHN⋅ + O2 → CH3CN + ⋅HO2 (− 27.9 kcal mol−1) is in excellent agreement with the ATcT (− 37.3 kcal mol−1) and (− 27.1 kcal/mol) database, respectively.

Rate constants and branching ratios

OH + CH3CH=NH reaction system

The rate constants for CH3CH=NH + OH were calculated using the combination of µVTST (for outer transition states) and RRKM theory (for tight transition states)30,47. As for our awareness, the chemical kinetics details for forming the pre-reactive complex (PRC) from OH + CH3CHNH in the temperature 100 to 1000 K have not been investigated. Similar complexes were formed in previous studies by Bunkun et al.20,21 for CH3N=NH2 + OH and CH2=NH + OH. They used a long-range transition state theory approach assuming a dipole–dipole potential (C = 4.0 × 10–10 cm3 molecule−1 s−1) with the Inverse Laplace Transform (ILT) method to account for the capture rate constants. In our previous calculation22, we used the VTST method to obtain the transition state for the reaction CH2=NH + OH → PRC system. In another reaction system, i.e., CH2=NH + HO2, we used the µVTST approach to calculate the capture rate constant for CH2=NH + HO2 → CH2=NH…O2H. We found that VTST and µVTST results were in perfect agreement with the previous calculation on similar reaction systems and experimental analysis. Therefore, in the current study, we used a combination of VTST and µVTST approaches to calculate the capture rate constant for the formation PRC, which is assumed to play a vital role under atmospheric conditions30,47.

To locate the transition state for each barrierless reaction, the potential energy (including zero point energy of the modes perpendicular to the newly-forming bond) was computed in a series of constrained optimizations as a function of the bond distance, as described in the Methods section. The potential energy profiles for the OH + CH3CHNH → PRC are shown in Fig. 7.

The rate constant value agrees with the previous calculation on a similar reaction system30,31,33. The rate constant for each channel i.e., hydrogen abstraction from N–H, C–H, –CH3 and OH addition were calculated based on the combination of µVTST and RRKM approach. This combination is considered a reasonably accurate kinetics calculation, which provides the rate constant with an accuracy of 50–70% when compared to the value of the experimental measurement30,31,33. As suggested in the previous work22 and to avoid the discrepancy from k = Ke × k2 in the rate constant calculation, we have used k = k1 × k2/(k-1 + k2) for the rate constant calculation. The rate constants for each channel with the effect of E and Z isomer and total rate constants are given in Table 1 and shown in Fig. 10. Under lower temperature (< 300 K) conditions, our results suggest that H-abstraction by OH radical from the N–H bond of CH3CH=NH is more favorable than H-abstraction from the C–H bonds. This is because the H-abstraction from the N–H bond has the lowest barrier (see Fig. 4) among all the other abstractions. The H-abstraction from C–H bonds of the CH3–C–H=NH bond plays an important role when the temperature rises to 300 K. A similar analysis was found in our previous study on the CH2=NH + OH reaction.22 The addition of OH and H-abstraction from the CH3 group is nearly two orders smaller than C–H and N–H under atmospheric conditions. Even though the barrier heights for C–H and OH are similar, the entropy factor of C-H abstraction by OH radical is dominated by the OH addition to CH3–C–H=NH. Our earlier studies suggested that the OH addition on C=N is less favorable than the H-abstraction reaction, which is consistent with the current analysis. The calculated rate constant shows negative temperature dependence under lower temperatures and positive temperature dependence at high temperatures. The U-shaped curve is also consistent with a reaction similar to that reported earlier34–36.

Rate constants for OH + CH3CHNH reaction.

The calculated rate constant at 300 K for CH3CHNH + OH (~2 × 10–11 cm3 molecule−1 s−1) is in good agreement with the estimated value of CH2=NH + OH reaction (~ 1 × 10–11 cm3 molecule−1 s−1). The factor 2 difference could be due to C–H abstraction in CH3CHNH playing 50% of the total rate constants; however, in the case of CH2NH, only N–H abstraction plays a vital role under tropospheric conditions. Another reason for understanding this discrepancy is the presence of the CH3 group on the terminal of CH, which lowers the barrier height for C-H abstraction, making it more facile. As expected, the higher branch chained compound on a similar reaction resulted in a higher rate constant; our analysis is consistent with each other. The results of our calculations are also compared with the recent report by Bunkan et al.20 for the CH3N=CH2 + OH system. The calculated rate constant by Bunkan et al.20 at room temperature ~ 4 × 10–12 cm3 molecule−1 s−1) is five times smaller than our predicted value for the CH3CH=NH + OH system. The result reported in their study is smaller than the present study due to the absence of the N–H bond and the absence of the E and Z isomer of CH3N=CH2. The other reason for this discrepancy is that they used the ILT method to compute the barrierless reaction. We have also noticed that their earlier reported rate constants for CH2NH + OH (3 × 10–12 cm3 molecule−1 s−1) are almost very similar to OH + CH3N=CH2. Their reported value is nearly a factor of 4–5 smaller than the experimentally reported value for isoelectronic analogous systems, i.e., CH2O + OH and CH2CH2 + OH reaction systems22.

To verify our calculation, we have compared our calculated value with an isoelectronic reaction system i.e., CH3CHO + OH and CH3CH=CH2 + OH reaction, whose experimental values are shown in Fig. 11. The rate constants between the 200–220 K temperature range are very good agreement with those reported in JPL for CH3CHO + OH48–50. The value is also in very good agreement with CH3CH=CH2 + OH between the temperature range of 300 to 375 K from the experimental value reported by the JPL database48–50. As discussed in the previous section, N–H abstraction is dominated at lower temperatures, shows the characteristics of CH3CHO, and as temperature increases, the C-H abstraction dominates, behaving like a CH3CH=CH3 molecule. Therefore, CH3CH=NH has similarities to both isoelectronic analogous systems. Similar results were also reported for CH2NH + OH, showing the similarities between CH2O and CH2CH2 systems. The rate constant for the CH3CH=NH + OH system at lower temperatures (100 K) and higher temperatures (1000 K) are very similar; our analysis suggests that under low temperatures, the enthalpy factor is dominated over the entropy factor as a result of the rate constant value is higher. At higher temperatures of 1000 K, the entropy factor dominates the enthalpy factor. As a result, the total rate constant increases.

Comparison of rate constants for three isoelectronic reaction systems.

Figure 12 shows the temperature-dependent (from 100 to 1000 K) branching fractions for both abstraction and addition reactions. At a lower temperature of 100 K, the calculation of BR shows equal contribution in all C–H, N–H, and –CH3 abstractions. As temperature increases up to 300 K, BRs for the formation of CH3C⋅NH and CH3CHN⋅ show almost equal contribution, and BR of OH-addition and hydrogen abstraction from CH3 group is almost negligible. As temperature increases from > 300 K, the formation of CH3C•NH is dominated over the formation of CH3CHN⋅. The corresponding BR for CH3C⋅NH (~ 65%), CH3CHN• (~ 20%), ⋅CH2CHNH (14%) and CH3CHOHNH (~ 1%) at temperatures > 300 K. Compared to the BR of a similar reaction system, i.e., CH2NH + OH reaction, the BR of the C–H abstraction is dominated over > 1000 K compared to the N–H abstraction. This is because the barrier height for C–H abstraction is higher than N–H by 1–2 kcal/mol in CH2NH + OH; however, in CH3CHNH + OH, the barrier height of C-H abstraction is higher than by 0.5 kcal/mol than of N–H abstraction. The entropy factor is also dominated in the case of CH3CHNH + OH compared to CH2NH + OH when the temperature is higher than 300 K.

Kinetics of CH3C⋅NH + O2 reaction

RRKM/ME simulation was used to obtain the rate constants for O2 + CH3C⋅NH in temperatures between 100 to 1000 K and pressures range of 0.0001 to 100 bar. The rate constants as a function of the temperature at 1 bar pressure are shown in Fig. 13. Conclusions

In this work, rate constants for OH + CH3CH=NH and O2 + CH3C⋅NH reaction have been analyzed using CCSD(T)//M06-2X levels in combination with µVTST_RRKM/ME simulation to understand the formation of CH3CN in three different environments. The most important reaction under lower temperature conditions (< 300 K) is hydrogen abstraction from the C–H and N–H bonds; under high-temperature conditions, abstraction from the C–H bonds becomes more dominant than N–H. The computed energies are in very good agreement with the most accurate thermochemical data and provide more confidence in our predicted rate constants. In the OH abstraction reaction, pressure dependence is negligible; however, for the O2 addition reaction, the pressure dependency is found to be strong. Pressure-dependence is observed. The computed rate constant for OH-initiated oxidation on CH3CHNH is in very good agreement with its isoelectronic species, i.e., OH/O2 + CH3CHO and OH/O2 + CH3CHCH2 have similarities to both reactions. The results show that CH3⋅CHNH + O2 leads to the form CH3CN in all three mediums and the reaction CH3CHNH + OH/O2 → CH3CN + HO2 via CH3⋅CHNH leading to is both kinetically and thermodynamically more favourable in all three gas phase mediums. Such kinetic analysis is the first step in providing a clue for the formation of carcinogenic compounds in all three mediums. The atmospheric lifetime of CH3CHNH ~ 10 to 11 h suggests that its contribution to global warming is negligible. However, the formation of products such as CH3CN, C2N2, and HCN may be responsible for acid rain, urban smog, tropospheric ozone growth, and atmospheric aerosols. We hope that our chemical kinetics data will be helpful in understanding amine screening studies for environmental risks.
