Genome‐wide association and haplotype analyses reveal genetic architecture of seed dormancy in grain sorghum with implications for preharvest sprouting tolerance
Authors: Yusa Ichinose, Dinesh Kumar Saini, Juliana M Espíndola Lima, Gunvant B Patil, Impa Somayanda, S V Krishna Jagadish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreharvest sprouting (PHS), triggered primarily by wet or humid conditions at maturity and further influenced by temperature, causes premature grain germination in sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench), reducing yield and grain quality. To elucidate the genetic architecture of seed dormancy and germination responses related to PHS tolerance, 172 genetically diverse Sorghum Association Panel accessions were evaluated under controlled conditions across two years. Germination index (GI) and arcsine‐transformed germination percentage (ArcG%) were used to characterize dormancy‐related variation. Genome‐wide association studies based on ∼4.8 million high‐quality single‐nucleotide polymorphisms identified 99 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for GI and ArcG%, several of which were stable across datasets. Notably, clusters of ArcG%‐associated QTL on chromosomes Sb4 and Sb5 represented major genetic hotspots enriched for dormancy‐related variation. Haplotype analysis refined these QTLs into 36 blocks, with 15 showing significant phenotypic differentiation. Favorable haplotypes consistently associated with lower GI or ArcG% indicated stronger dormancy and reduced germination under sprouting‐inducing conditions. Candidate‐gene mining within these regions identified 29 genes, of which 11 were prioritized for their functional similarity to known dormancy and sprouting regulators. Gene‐based haplotype analysis highlighted seven strong candidates, each showing a robust association with dormancy‐related traits. Several QTLs also colocalized with known sorghum dormancy genes (e.g., SbVP1, SbABI1, SbGA2ox1, SbPKABA1) and orthologs of cereal dormancy regulators (e.g., TaAFP, TaMYB10, TaPYL5, Sdr4, ABI5), suggesting conserved mechanisms across cereals. Collectively, these findings identify key genomic regions, favorable haplotypes, and high‐priority candidate genes associated with seed dormancy and germination response, providing valuable genomic resources for breeding sorghum cultivars with improved PHS tolerance.

INTRODUCTION

Preharvest sprouting (PHS) presents a significant agronomic challenge across various cereal crops, including sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench). This phenomenon primarily occurs in genotypes with reduced primary dormancy when grain at or just after physiological maturity is exposed to prolonged moisture (rain or persistent high humidity), accompanied with varying temperature (30°C–35°C), leading to germination while still attached to the mother plant (Benech‐Arnold & Rodríguez, 2018; Jia et al., 2024; Ju et al., 2025). The consequences of PHS are far‐reaching, resulting in considerable economic losses through mechanisms such as starch degradation, embryo elongation, increased vulnerability to fungal infections, reduced desiccation tolerance, and overall deterioration in grain quality (Matilla, 2024). Unlike cultivated cereals, wild relatives have retained robust dormancy mechanisms that delay germination under suboptimal conditions (Tai et al., 2021). However, modern breeding and agricultural systems have prioritized rapid and uniform germination to optimize crop establishment, inadvertently diminishing dormancy levels and increasing susceptibility to PHS (Tai et al., 2021). Consequently, the global cost of PHS in crops is substantial, with estimated losses approaching $1 billion annually (Matilla, 2024). These losses are being compounded further by changing climate patterns and modifications in cropping practices (J. Wang, 2021).

At the physiological level, PHS is fundamentally linked to reduced seed dormancy, an adaptive trait that ensures seeds remain quiescent until environmental conditions become favorable for germination. As a complex quantitative trait, PHS is influenced by a network of genes and environmental factors acting during seed development, which poses challenges for traditional breeding strategies (N. A. Gualano & Benech‐Arnold, 2009; Tai et al., 2021). Hormonal signaling, particularly the antagonistic interaction between abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellins (GA), plays a central role in regulating dormancy and germination (Rodríguez et al., 2015). While ABA promotes dormancy, GA facilitates germination, and their dynamic balance is critical for modulating seed behavior under varying conditions (Ali et al., 2022). Experimental comparisons between sorghum genotypes with contrasting dormancy levels, such as IS9530 (high dormancy) and RedlandB2 (low dormancy), have revealed differential hormone responsiveness (Benech‐Arnold & Rodríguez, 2018; Rodríguez et al., 2012). RedlandB2 grains exhibit lower sensitivity to ABA and higher GA accumulation, coupled with reduced expression of ABA signaling components, including SbABI3/SbVP1, SbABI4, SbABI5, and SbPKABA1 (N. Gualano et al., 2007; Rodríguez et al., 2012, 2009). Furthermore, SbABI4 and SbABI5 directly interact with the SbGA2ox promoter in the high‐dormancy genotype IS9530 but not in RedlandB2, illustrating the route through which ABA‐mediated repression of GA biosynthesis maintains dormancy (Cantoro et al., 2013). Comparative genomic studies across cereals reinforce the conserved nature of dormancy and PHS regulation. In rice (Oryza sativa L.), phs1‐4 encode enzymes in the carotenoid and ABA‐biosynthetic pathway whose mutations disrupt ABA accumulation and induce PHS (Fang et al., 2008). In maize (Zea mays L.), the viviparous (vp) mutants (vp5, vp7, vp9, vp14) similarly confer ABA deficiency and precocious germination (Y. Wang et al., 2021). In wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), conserved regulators including TaMFT, TaVp1 (ABI3 ortholog), TaDOG1, and TaMYB10 function within the ABA–GA signaling and transcriptional network controlling seed dormancy (Tai et al., 2021; Vetch et al., 2019). The recurrence of these homologous gene families across cereals highlights an evolutionarily conserved regulatory framework controlling dormancy and PHS.

Given the economic and agronomic importance of PHS, breeding tolerant varieties remains a priority, particularly in regions prone to wet harvest periods. Genomic tools now offer powerful means to identify loci underlying PHS tolerance, with next‐generation sequencing enabling high‐throughput single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) discovery and facilitating genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) that link genetic variants to phenotypic variation (Boatwright et al., 2024; Saini et al., 2022). Across cereals, GWAS has revealed key genomic regions associated with PHS tolerance. For instance, Min et al. (2024) identified 91 significant SNPs and 227 additional SNPs using linkage disequilibrium (LD) block‐based GWAS, resolving 32 distinct haplotypes and several promising genes in rice. In wheat, over 600 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) or significant SNPs associated with PHS tolerance had been reported up to 2022 (Singh et al., 2022), and the number of QTLs continues to increase with ongoing mapping studies. In sorghum, QTLs associated with dormancy and/or PHS susceptibility have been identified through linkage mapping (Cantoro et al., 2016; Rodríguez et al., 2025). Notably, Rodríguez et al. (2025) reported a major dormancy QTL (qDOR‐9) on chromosome 9 in a RedlandB2 × IS9530 RIL population, which overlapped with loci for plant height (dw1) and flowering time, revealing unintended trade‐offs from past breeding. However, only one GWAS on PHS tolerance in sorghum has been reported to date (Ju et al., 2025), which identified SbPP2C33 as a regulator of ABA signaling and carbohydrate mobilization during germination. While this pioneering study provided valuable mechanistic insights, its geographically narrow panel limited the capture of global allelic diversity and the discovery of novel loci. Thus, GWAS remains largely underutilized for exploring the full spectrum of natural variation and identifying new genomic regions conferring PHS tolerance in sorghum.

In contrast, our study leverages the Sorghum Association Panel (SAP), a globally relevant collection of tropical and temperate accessions derived from the Sorghum Conversion Program and widely used in genetic studies and in the United States and international breeding programs (Casa et al., 2008; Klein et al., 2008). The SAP, initially genotyped with SSRs and later re‐characterized with genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS), has now been sequenced at whole‐genome resolution, providing millions of high‐quality variants, including SNPs and structural variants (Boatwright et al., 2022; Boyles et al., 2016; Morris et al., 2013). These advances collectively provide a robust framework for dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits in SAP and accelerating gene discovery. Within this framework, the present study aimed to (i) evaluate a globally diverse panel of sorghum accessions for PHS responses under controlled conditions using germination percentage (GP) and germination index (GI) as quantitative indicators of dormancy and sprouting susceptibility; and (ii) dissect the genetic basis of PHS tolerance by integrating GWAS, candidate‐gene mining, and QTL‐ and gene‐based haplotype analyses to identify key genomic regions, functional genes, and superior haplotypes associated with dormancy regulation and sprouting tolerance. Collectively, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the genetic architecture and functional mechanisms underlying PHS tolerance, offering genomic resources for marker development, functional validation, and breeding of PHS‐resilient sorghum cultivars.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

A subset of 345 accessions from the SAP was obtained from the USDA‐ARS Cropping Systems Research Laboratory, Lubbock, TX, for field evaluation. The SAP includes converted (photoperiod‐insensitive) tropical accessions and temperate‐adapted breeding lines. Converted lines were selected for their geographic and genotypic diversity, and historical contributions to resistance against various biotic and abiotic stresses (Casa et al., 2008). Breeding lines include photoperiod‐insensitive inbreds, improved cultivars, and landraces widely used in sorghum improvement programs, selected primarily for their historical importance rather than racial classification (Casa et al., 2008). The selected accessions were planted at the New Deal Research Farm, Texas Tech University (33°44′13.76″ N, 101°43′58.04″ W) on May 9, 2023, and May 27, 2024. Each line was sown at a depth of 1.5 in., with 75 seeds per 17‐ft row (≈77,000 seeds/acre). Nitrogen fertilizer (UAN; 32:0:0) was applied at planting and during early growth according to standard farm management practices. Environmental data representing local field conditions were obtained from a nearby West Texas Mesonet station (www.mesonet.ttu.edu) and are presented in Figure S1. Approximately 50 days after flowering (defined as the date when 80% of plants in a row had reached anthesis), three to four panicles per line were harvested at physiological maturity, and seeds were obtained by threshing.

Controlled environment germination test and statistical analysis

To assess seed dormancy and germination response associated with PHS tolerance in sorghum, controlled germination assays were conducted on threshed seeds to eliminate variability arising from panicle structure, moisture gradients, and seed position, thereby ensuring uniform and reproducible conditions across genotypes (Rodríguez et al., 2021). This method enables accurate, high‐throughput phenotyping essential for genetic studies (Cantoro et al., 2016; Ju et al., 2025; Rodríguez et al., 2021, 2025). Germination response was quantified using two complementary metrics: (i) GP, which reflects overall sprouting susceptibility, and (ii) GI, which integrates both the rate and extent of germination to capture subtle differences in the level of dormancy.

For each genotype, four replicates of 25 seeds were placed between three layers of standard germination paper moistened with distilled water at 2.5 times the dry paper weight. The rolled papers were placed inside plastic zip bags (left partially unsealed) and incubated in an airflow growth chamber under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 25°C for 3 days (Cantoro et al., 2016; International Seed Testing Association, 2025; Ju et al., 2025). Germination was expressed as a percentage, calculated based on the number of seeds that had germinated by the third day after placement (International Seed Testing Association, 2025). GI was assessed by counting the number of germinated seeds at 24, 48, and 72 h after sowing. The GI was calculated following the method of Maguire (1962), which determines the speed of germination as the sum of the number of seeds germinated at each count divided by the number of days from sowing to that count. Since GP is a proportion‐based variable bounded between 0 and 100, it often violates the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance required for parametric statistical tests (Gianinetti, 2020). To address this issue, an arcsine square root transformation was applied to stabilize variances and improve the approximation of a normal distribution (Laurencelle & Cousineau, 2023). The transformation was performed in R using the asin() function in combination with the square root function sqrt(), after first converting percentage values to proportions by dividing by 100. The transformed data were subsequently used for downstream statistical analyses and association mapping.

Descriptive statistics and broad‐sense heritability (H 2) for both arcsine‐transformed germination percentage (ArcG%) and GI were estimated using the R package “variability” (R Core Team, 2016). Heritability was calculated separately for each year using the formula: H 2 = σ 2 g /(σ 2 g + σ 2 e /n Rep), where σ 2 g and σ 2 e are the estimated genotype and residual variance components, respectively, and n Rep is the number of replicates. SNP genotyping and population structure

A publicly available dataset comprising 43,983,694 variants, including ∼38 million single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), 5 million insertions/deletions (InDels), and 170,000 copy number variants (CNVs), derived from whole‐genome resequencing (WGS) of 400 diverse sorghum accessions (Boatwright et al., 2022), was utilized in this study. To ensure high‐quality genotypic data for downstream analyses, variant filtering was conducted using BCFtools v1.21 (Danecek et al., 2021). Variants were retained if they met the following criteria: (i) missing data rate below 10%, (ii) minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 5% (MAF > 0.05), and (iii) heterozygosity rate below 10%. This conservative filtering strategy was adopted to ensure robust genotype calls and reliable GWAS results given the large number of markers relative to the sample size (large‐p, small‐n; Diao & Vidyashankar, 2013; Saini et al., 2022). This filtering yielded a final set of 4,782,196 high‐quality variants, consisting of 4,147,418 SNPs (86.7%) and 634,778 InDels (13.3%); no CNVs remained after filtering. For additional details on how structural variants were initially characterized, see Boatwright et al. (2022).

To align with phenotypic data availability and quality, a subset of 172 accessions was selected from the original 345 for downstream genetic analyses. Accessions were retained only if they exhibited no fungal infection or abnormal germination in either year of the controlled germination tests, had complete phenotypic data across both years, and possessed corresponding high‐quality genotypic data. Population structure among the 172 selected accessions was investigated using principal component analysis (PCA) in R, implemented through the “GAPIT version 3.0” package (J. Wang & Zhang, 2021). To complement this analysis, Bayesian model‐based clustering was performed using STRUCTURE version 2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000), based on a subset of randomly selected markers from each chromosome and assuming an admixture model. STRUCTURE analysis tested K values ranging from 1 to 10, with five independent runs per K. Each run included a burn‐in period of 10,000 iterations followed by 10,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations. The optimal number of subpopulations (K) was determined using the DeltaK method (Evanno et al., 2005), which assesses the rate of change in log‐likelihood between successive K values, as implemented in STRUCTURE HARVESTER (Earl & VonHoldt, 2012).

Genome‐wide association analysis

Genome‐wide association analyses were conducted on a panel of 172 sorghum accessions to identify genetic variants significantly associated with GI and ArcG%. Six trait datasets were analyzed: GI_BLUP_2023, GI_BLUP_2024, GI_BLUP_Across_Years, ArcG%_BLUP_2023, ArcG%_BLUP_2024, and ArcG%_BLUP_Across_Years (Table S1). Two complementary models were used: the mixed linear model (MLM), which accounts for population structure and kinship, and the Fixed and Random Model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU), a multi‐locus method that reduces false positives by iteratively incorporating significant markers as fixed effects while modeling random effects (Kumar Saini et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2016; Saini et al., 2022). Both models have been validated in previous GWAS of the SAP using GBS and WGS data (Mural et al., 2021; Punnuri et al., 2022; Sahay et al., 2024). Analyses were performed in the R environment using the “GAPIT v3.0” package (J. Wang & Zhang, 2021). To control for population structure, the first three principal components (PCs) were included as covariates in both models, selected based on the scree plot, the DeltaK statistic from STRUCTURE analysis, and PCA.

For defining genome‐wide significance, a Bonferroni correction was initially calculated based on the total number of high‐quality variants retained (n = 4,782,196), yielding a stringent threshold of 1.05 × 10−8. However, due to its overly conservative nature in high‐dimensional genomic datasets, this threshold was considered too restrictive for capturing meaningful associations. Recent studies have recommended alternative approaches, including exploratory thresholds or corrections based on the number of effective independent tests (Getahun et al., 2025; Umar et al., 2025; J. Zhao et al., 2016). Accordingly, we adopted an exploratory significance threshold of −log10(p) = 4.00, which is commonly used in multi‐locus models like FarmCPU that inherently mitigate false positives without requiring overly stringent correction methods (Y.‐M. Zhang et al., 2019). Variants identified by both MLM and FarmCPU models were clustered into QTL regions based on physical proximity. Specifically, variants located within ±20 kb of each other were grouped into the same QTL, reflecting the average genome‐wide LD decay distance (Boatwright et al., 2022). This threshold was chosen based on previously reported LD decay estimates using a larger panel of 400 sorghum accessions (Boatwright et al., 2022), of which our 172‐accession panel represents a random subset. QTLs were named beginning with the letter q followed by the trait abbreviation (G for arcsine square‐root‐transformed germination percentage and GI for germination index), then the chromosome number on which the QTL is located, followed by a decimal point and the serial number of the QTL identified on that chromosome.

Haplotype analysis

Significant variants clustered within the identified QTL regions were further analyzed through haplotype analysis. To generate and visualize LD‐based haplotypes, we used Haploview v.4.2 (Barrett et al., 2005). For this analysis, high‐quality genotypic data were filtered to exclude InDels, and only biallelic SNP markers were retained. Based on these markers, the 172 sorghum accessions were grouped according to their respective haplotypes. To evaluate the phenotypic effects of haplotype groups, we performed nonparametric statistical analyses using the “ggstatsplot” package in R (Patil, 2021; R Core Team, 2016). Nonparametric methods were selected because the data showed deviations from normality and unequal sample sizes among haplotype classes, which violate the assumptions of parametric ANOVA. The Kruskal–Wallis test was first used to determine whether phenotypic distributions differed significantly among haplotypes. When significant differences were observed (p < 0.05), Dunn's post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons, with p‐values adjusted using Holm's method to correct for multiple testing. This analysis was performed separately for each trait using all three corresponding datasets.

Candidate gene mining, prioritization, and gene‐based haplotype analysis

Genes located within selected QTL regions were extracted using the BioMart tool in the Ensembl Plants database (http://plants.ensembl.org), based on the Sorghum bicolor v3.1.1 genome assembly. Candidate genes (CGs) were then prioritized based on their documented roles in seed dormancy and PHS tolerance in different crops. To further explore the developmental and physiological relevance of the prioritized genes, we examined their tissue‐specific expression profiles using publicly available transcriptomic datasets. Expression data were retrieved from the EBI Gene Expression Atlas (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa/home), which integrates data from multiple independent sorghum studies (Davidson et al., 2012; Makita et al., 2015; Turco et al., 2017; B. Wang et al., 2018). For each tissue, the maximum transcript abundance (transcripts per million) across datasets was used to represent expression intensity. The shortlisted genes were further subjected to CG‐based haplotype analysis. SNPs within each gene were used to define haplotypes based on allelic variation across genotypes. Sorghum accessions were grouped by haplotype, and trait data were analyzed using the “ggstatsplot” R package. Statistical significance was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's test, as described above. Gene structures for the targeted CGs were retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database.

RESULTS

Phenotypic responses of sorghum accessions and trait relationships

All sorghum accessions initiated germination within 48 h under favorable conditions, with no genotype exhibiting complete failure within the 72‐h assessment period (Table S1). Considerable variation was observed in both the rate and extent of germination, and both traits displayed continuous, near‐normal distributions across 2023, 2024, and combined environments (Figure 1A–F). Descriptive statistics for ArcG% and GI were calculated for the years 2023, 2024, and across environments (Table S2). For the ArcG%, values ranged from 0.63 to 1.57 across all datasets, with a mean of approximately 1.35 and a standard deviation (SD) of 0.18. The coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 13.48% to 13.73%. The broad‐sense heritability (H 2) for ArcG% across environments was 0.82, indicating a high genetic contribution. For GI, the values ranged from 5.50 to 25.00, with mean values between 16 and 17 across environments. The SD ranged from 3.95 to 4.05, while the CV varied between 23.44% and 24.83%, reflecting greater variability compared to ArcG% (Table S2). The heritability estimate for GI across environments was 0.92, suggesting strong genetic control. Combined ANOVA revealed highly significant genotype effects for both traits, along with significant year effects and genotype × year interactions (Table S3).

A significant correlation was also detected between GI values in 2023 and 2024 (r = 0.678, p < 0.001), indicating consistency in genotypic performance across years. For ArcG%, a significant positive correlation was observed between 2023 and across‐year data (r = 0.889, p < 0.001), as well as between 2024 and across‐year data (r = 0.884, p < 0.001). SNP genotyping and population structure analysis

After quality filtering, a total of 4,782,196 high‐quality SNPs were retained for downstream analysis. These variants spanned approximately 683.62 Mb across all 10 sorghum chromosomes, resulting in an average marker density of about seven markers per kilobase. Marker distribution varied across chromosomes, ranging from 346,460 variants on chromosome Sb07 to 587,271 on Sb01 (Table S4). PCA revealed considerable genetic diversity among the 172 sorghum accessions. The first three PCs (PC1, PC2, and PC3) explained 9.91%, 7.25%, and 4.68% of the total genetic variation, respectively (Figure S2A). Although no sharply defined clusters were evident, the three‐dimensional (3D) PCA plot indicated moderate population stratification, reflecting the diverse geographic and genetic background of the 172 accessions. To account for this structure and minimize false‐positive associations, the first three PCs were incorporated as covariates in the GWAS analysis. Further assessment using STRUCTURE analysis identified the highest ΔK at K = 3, suggesting that the population is optimally structured into three major genetic clusters (Figure S2B).

Identification and distribution of QTLs for ArcG% and GI traits

QTLs were defined based on SNPs located within ±20 kb and consistently identified by both GWAS models in at least two of the three datasets for each trait. For ArcG%, a total of 91 QTLs (designated as qG1.1 to qG10.1) were identified across all sorghum chromosomes except Sb06 (Table S5). The number of QTLs varied by chromosome: Sb01, Sb07, Sb09, and Sb10 each harbored only one QTL, whereas Sb04 contained the highest number, with 52 QTLs. Each QTL spanned between 1 and 34 SNPs (e.g., qG4.1), with additive effects ranging from 0.052 to 0.198 and −log10(p) values from 4.00 to 6.64. Notably, 14 QTLs for ArcG% were consistently detected across all three datasets. In some cases, ArcG%‐associated QTLs were tightly clustered within small genomic regions. For instance, on Sb04, 25 QTLs (qG4.11 to qG4.35) were located between 20,973,650 and 28,141,521 bp. A similar pattern was observed on Sb05, where 18 QTLs (qG5.9 to qG5.26) were clustered between 37,612,891 and 47,681,455 bp. In contrast, only eight QTLs (qGI1.1 to qGI10.1) were identified for GI, distributed across chromosomes Sb01, Sb03, Sb05, and Sb10. Chromosomes Sb05 and Sb10 each contained one QTL, while Sb01 and Sb03 had three QTLs each. These GI QTLs spanned 1–12 SNPs (e.g., qGI3.3), with effect sizes ranging from 1.244 to 3.203 and −log10(p) values between 4.00 and 5.63. Unlike ArcG%, none of the GI QTLs were consistently detected across all three datasets (Table S5). Manhattan and Q–Q plots for ArcG% are shown for FarmCPU (Figure S3A–F) and MLM (Figure S4A–F). These figures are representative of the overall GWAS calibration and signal profiles observed across traits and years.

Haplotype analysis of ArcG% and GI‐associated QTLs

Significant SNPs from selected QTLs were used for haplotype analysis. For ArcG%, 14 QTLs consistently identified across all three datasets were selected, while for GI, all eight identified QTLs were included. QTLs were excluded if their representative SNPs were not in LD with others, resulting in a total of 15 QTLs analyzed. In some cases, most SNPs within a QTL region were in strong LD, forming a single haplotype block. In contrast, some QTL regions contained SNPs that were not in LD with one another, resulting in the formation of two or more distinct haplotype blocks within the same QTL interval (Table S6). Overall, 36 haplotype blocks were identified, including 30 for ArcG% (365 SNPs) and six for GI (106 SNPs). Among the ArcG% blocks, nine showed significant haplotype differences across all three datasets, nine in two datasets, and 12 in one or none. All six GI blocks showed significant differences across all three datasets (Table S6).

Some of the key haplotype blocks include the following: (i) qG2.2_Block 1 (nine SNPs, Sb02:72,725,372–72,729,958 bp), associated with ArcG%, had four haplotypes (Hap1, Hap2, Hap3, and Hap4), occurring at frequencies of 48.5%, 38%, 5.3%, and 6.3%, respectively. Hap3 consistently exhibited the lowest ArcG%, while Hap2 had the highest across datasets (Figure 3A). (ii) qG5.4_Block 7 (13 SNPs, Sb05:28,076,274–28,078,077 bp), also associated with ArcG%, had four haplotypes with frequencies of 42%, 18.2%, 25.4%, and 5.4%. Hap4 consistently showed the lowest ArcG% across datasets (Figure 3B). (iii) qGI1.2_Block 1 (42 SNPs, Sb01:63,745,936–63,748,197 bp), associated with GI, had four haplotypes occurring at frequencies of 48.1%, 26.3%, 11%, and 4.6%, respectively. Hap2 consistently showed the lowest GI, while Hap4 had the highest across all three datasets (Figure 4A). (iv) qGI3.3_Block 1 (27 SNPs; Sb03:67,588,816–67,595,012 bp) also associated with GI had four haplotypes with frequencies of 52.7%, 16.8%, 13.2%, and 10.2%. Hap2 consistently showed the lowest GI in the 2024 and across‐year datasets, whereas in the 2023 dataset, Hap4 had the lowest GI, and Hap1 exhibited the highest across all three datasets (Figure 4B).

Prioritized candidate genes and haplotypes underlying trait variation

Only QTLs that contained haplotype blocks showing significant differences in median trait values between haplotypes, as determined in at least one dataset, were selected for CG mining. In total, 13 QTLs were considered for CG mining within ±20 kb regions around their peak positions. Among these, CGs were identified for six QTLs, including two associated with ArcG% and four associated with GI. Across these six QTLs, a total of 29 CGs were identified. Of these, 24 had functional annotations, while five remained uncharacterized (Table S7). Several genes with similar functional descriptions were repeatedly identified across different QTL regions, including two genes each encoding alpha/beta hydrolase fold‐1, glycoside hydrolase, large ribosomal subunit proteins, and multicopper oxidase. Out of the 29 CGs, 11 were prioritized based on their functional similarity to known genes associated with seed dormancy and PHS in other cereals. These prioritized genes encode proteins such as alpha/beta hydrolases, cytochrome P450s, GDP‐fucose protein O‐fucosyltransferases, glycoside hydrolase family members, and SANT/Myb domain‐containing transcription factors (Table S7). Expression analysis revealed that several genes (Sobic.002G367600, Sobic.001G348100, Sobic.003G357600, and Sobic.003G358000) exhibited relatively higher transcript levels in seed‐related tissues, including the embryo, endosperm, and pericarp. Other genes showed moderate expression in pistil, spikelet, or flag leaf tissues, whereas expression was low or undetectable in most vegetative tissues (Table S8).

Of the 11 genes analyzed for haplotypes, one was excluded due to having only a single SNP. The remaining 10 genes, each containing between 2 and 19 SNPs, resulted in the identification of 10 gene‐based haplotype blocks (Table S9). Haplotypes from seven of these blocks, based on genes Sobic.002G367400, Sobic.001G348000, Sobic.001G348100, Sobic.003G357600, Sobic.003G357700, Sobic.003G358000, and Sobic.003G358100, showed significant differences across all three datasets for the respective traits (p‐values ranging from <0.05 to <0.001). The remaining three blocks, based on Sobic.002G367600, Sobic.003G232400, and Sobic.003G232800, exhibited significant differences in only one dataset or none at all. For Sobic.001G348100, five haplotypes were identified, with frequencies of 47.67%, 27.33%, 9.88%, 4.65%, and 4.65%, respectively (Figure 5A). Hap4 exhibited the lowest GI values (11.59–11.74), while Hap5 showed the highest GI values (18.47–18.63) across all relevant datasets. Similarly, the Sobic.003G357600 block comprised three haplotypes, with frequencies of 48.26%, 23.26%, and 14.53%. Hap3 demonstrated the lowest GI (13.38–13.96), whereas Hap1 showed the highest GI (17.70–17.89) across all three GI datasets (Figure 5B).

DISCUSSION

Improving crop resilience to PHS is crucial for maintaining grain quality under increasingly erratic weather conditions. In sorghum, PHS arises from reduced seed dormancy when mature grains are exposed to humid conditions, often coupled with mild to warm temperatures, severely compromising grain value. While interval mapping studies have identified dormancy‐related QTL (Cantoro et al., 2016; Rodríguez et al., 2025), GWASs leveraging global diversity remain limited, with only one study reported to date (Ju et al., 2025). This study relied on a GWAS panel composed primarily of Chinese accessions, with limited representation from other regions, thereby restricting the capture of global allelic diversity and reducing the applicability of the findings. Moreover, the study lacked haplotype‐level resolution and focused solely on GP, a bounded trait (0–100) that may not always satisfy assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity unless appropriate data transformations are applied (Gianinetti, 2020). Physiologically relevant indices, such as the GI, which captures the rate of germination, a crucial phenomenon not reflected by final GP alone, were not considered. In contrast, our study utilized the diverse SAP, both GI and GP, and a high‐resolution multi‐locus GWAS approach, identifying 99 QTLs, some clustered around known dormancy regulators, while others were novel. These QTLs were further refined into haplotypes with consistent phenotypic effects across datasets. Notably, superior haplotypes were associated with stronger seed dormancy and delayed germination, effectively translating mechanistic insights into breeding‐ready markers. Thus, while Ju et al. (2025) advanced our understanding of hormonal regulation in a regional context, our work delivers globally relevant, haplotype‐level markers for genomics‐assisted improvement of PHS resilience.

Inherent germination response as a proxy for PHS tolerance in sorghum

Germination measured under uniform conditions reflects inherent genetic differences in primary dormancy, metabolic readiness, and seed‐coat‐mediated water uptake (Benech‐Arnold & Rodríguez, 2018). The high heritability of GI indicates strong genetic control over germination speed, consistent with roles for seed coat traits and ABA/GA signaling (Finch‐Savage & Leubner‐Metzger, 2006). ArcG% also showed high, though slightly lower heritability, suggesting a substantial genetic component with greater sensitivity to the maternal or developmental environment (Donohue et al., 2010). GI and ArcG% were positively correlated across years, implying partially shared control of germination rate and percentage (N. A. Gualano & Benech‐Arnold, 2009; Rodríguez et al., 2015).

Improvement status and sorghum type contributed to, but did not solely determine, the observed phenotypic variation. Many breeding (improved or temperate) lines showed low dormancy levels (high ArcG% and often higher GI), for instance BTx641, Early Hegari, RTx2536, and BTx2928; however, notable exceptions with higher dormancy levels were also present (e.g., Tx2891), with lower G% and ArcG%. In contrast, converted (landrace‐derived or tropical) lines encompassed a wide phenotypic range, including several high‐dormancy lines that consistently exhibited low GI and ArcG% (e.g., SC396, SC399, SC621) as well as low‐dormancy lines comparable to breeding lines (e.g., SC614, SC1074, SC1345), which may partly reflect their larger representation in the panel (n = 121) relative to improved lines (n = 51) (Table S1). At the race level, no uniform pattern was evident. For instance, the Caudatum group included both high‐dormancy accessions (e.g., SC396) and low‐dormancy accessions (e.g., SC322). Similar overlap was observed among the Durra, Guinea, and Bicolor backgrounds. Such intermixing likely reflects the prevalence of intermediate races and the historical genetic admixture that occurred during sorghum domestication and improvement (Paterson et al., 2009). This admixture, arising from recombination between tropical donor alleles and temperate elite backgrounds during the Sorghum Conversion Program (Klein et al., 2008), may have contributed to the distribution of dormancy‐related alleles across improvement classes. Overall, these results indicate that although modern improvement has generally reduced seed dormancy, the relationship is not uniform. Both tolerant and susceptible accessions occur among improved and converted lines as well as across racial groups, underscoring that dormancy‐ and PHS‐related alleles are broadly distributed throughout sorghum germplasm. This broad allelic distribution offers opportunities to breed PHS‐tolerant cultivars from both tropical and temperate backgrounds (Tai et al., 2021).

At the individual‐accession level, lines with lower germination rates (low GI) such as SC396, SC621, SC937, and SC424 consistently exhibited higher dormancy levels across years, while SC1319, JOCORO, BTxARG‐1, and SC1494 displayed high GI, indicating lower dormancy levels. Notably, SC396 and SC621 also had low final germination (low ArcG%), confirming consistent tolerance, while SC1494 showed high ArcG%, reinforcing its consistent susceptibility (Table S10). Together, these results indicate that integrating GI (rate) and ArcG% (capacity) across years enables reliable classification of germplasm into high‐ and low‐dormancy classes, minimizes misclassification due to seasonal effects, and highlights promising sources/donors for incorporating PHS tolerance. This dual‐trait, population‐structured approach provides a practical framework for early‐generation screening and guides the incorporation of dormancy‐associated alleles into elite breeding lines to enhance PHS tolerance.

Distinct QTL landscapes reflect divergent genetic control of germination traits

GWAS results highlight the polygenic basis of seed dormancy and PHS tolerance in sorghum. Although GI and ArcG% were positively correlated (r = 0.21–0.43), the absence of overlapping QTL under our stringent clustering criteria suggests largely distinct, yet potentially interconnected genetic regions that control PHS (Rodríguez et al., 2015). GI was associated with fewer loci of relatively large effect, indicating more centralized genetic regulation of early germination, whereas ArcG% displayed a broader architecture shaped by multiple moderate‐effect loci. Several ArcG%‐associated QTL clustered within small genomic intervals on chromosomes 4 and 5, representing potential dormancy hotspots that warrant fine‐mapping and functional validation. Although GI exhibited high broad‐sense heritability, the associated loci varied across years, likely reflecting environmental modulation of allelic effects and the conservative multi‐model, multi‐dataset criteria used for QTL declaration.

Some spatial proximity between QTLs for the two traits was observed (Figure 6). For instance, all GI QTLs were on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, and 10, which also carried ArcG% QTL. On chromosome 3, qG3.4 (ArcG%) at 57.17 Mb lay near qGI3.1 and qGI3.2 (GI) at 58.25 and 58.51 Mb, suggesting possible linkage or regulatory proximity despite no direct colocalization. Such clustering may reflect differences in seed coat permeability, hormonal sensitivity, or metabolic readiness (Finch‐Savage & Leubner‐Metzger, 2006; Li et al., 2004; Rodríguez et al., 2015). These results suggest that GI and ArcG% may respond to similar environmental cues, such as harvest‐time rainfall, through partially divergent genetic pathways. Stable QTLs for both traits across environments highlight genomic regions with resilient effects that are valuable for breeding PHS‐tolerant sorghum. Collectively, these findings support trait‐specific strategies by using major‐effect alleles to enhance GI and combining multiple loci to improve ArcG%, providing a foundation for marker‐assisted breeding and fine‐mapping to dissect and strengthen dormancy and PHS resilience.

Conserved and novel genetic determinants of dormancy and PHS tolerance

Hormonal regulation, particularly via ABA and GA pathways, plays a central role in controlling seed dormancy and PHS tolerance in sorghum. Key ABA‐ and GA‐related genes, such as SbABI1, SbVP1, and SbGA2ox1, along with loci like dw1 and QTLs including qGI‐1 and qGI‐3, have been linked to the GI (Cantoro et al., 2016; Carrari et al., 2001; Rodríguez et al., 2012, 2009, 2025) (Table S11). Functionally conserved regulators in other cereals, including vp1 and vp14 in maize, OsABI3 and Sdr4 in rice, and TaVP1, TaMFT, and TaPM19 in wheat, highlight a shared ABA–GA signaling network that underlies dormancy and PHS control across species (Giraudat et al., 1992; McCarty et al., 1991; Rikiishi & Maekawa, 2014; Y. Yang et al., 2007) (Table S12). Several QTLs identified in this study mapped in close proximity to genes known to regulate dormancy and PHS in sorghum. For instance, qG1.1 (ArcG%) on Sb01 lies near ABA‐signaling genes SbABI1, SbABI4, SbABI5, and SbPKABA1.2 (13.6–15.9 Mb), which are transiently upregulated in the high‐dormancy genotype IS9530 but not in low‐dormancy RedlandB2 (Rodríguez et al., 2009). qG9.1 on Sb09 is relatively close to the SbGA2ox1 and SbGA3ox2 genes (5.48–6.88 Mb); the former promotes dormancy by inactivating bioactive GAs, while the latter is involved in GA synthesis with a weaker dormancy link (Rodríguez et al., 2012).

Several QTLs also colocalized with sorghum orthologs of functionally characterized genes from other cereals. For instance, qGI1.1 and qGI1.2 on Sb01 were located near Sobic.001G335400 (ortholog of wheat TaAFP‐2A/2B/2D) and Sobic.001G340900 (TaMYB10‐3B/3D), within the 62.4–63.7 Mb region. TaAFP genes modulate ABA sensitivity through interaction with TaABI5, with higher expression associated with reduced dormancy (Feng et al., 2019). TaMYB10 contributes to the development of grain dormancy by modulating embryo sensitivity to ABA (Himi et al., 2011). qGI1.3 (67.2 Mb) was close to Sobic.001G403300 (68.8 Mb), orthologous to wheat TaPYL5‐2A/2B/2D, which encode ABA receptors. Higher TaPYL5 expression in dormant wheat embryos enhances ABA sensitivity (Izydorczyk et al., 2018). On Sb02, qG2.2 was near Sobic.002G356300 (ortholog of rice Sdr4 and wheat TaSdr‐2A/2B/2D), Sobic.002G375800 (TaQsd1‐5A/5B/5D), and Sobic.002G379400 (TaSnRK2/PKABA1‐2A/2B/2D), spanning 71.9–73.6 Mb. TaSdr enhances dormancy, TaQsd1 (an alanine aminotransferase) is associated with dormancy via ABA signaling, and PKABA1 promotes ABA responses while repressing GA signaling (Abe et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2002; Y. Zhang et al., 2017). qGI3.3 on Sb03 overlapped with several genes within 67.3–70.8 Mb: SbVP1 (ortholog of maize vp1/vp4, wheat TaABI3/TaVP1, rice OsABI3/OsVP1), Sobic.003G354000 (TaPP2C‐a10), Sobic.003G363400 (OsABI5, TaABI5/TaABF1), and Sobic.003G379500 (TaGA20ox2‐3A/3B/3D), along with QTL qGI‐3 (Cantoro et al., 2016) (Figure 6). SbVP1 promotes dormancy by activating ABA‐mediated transcription (Carrari et al., 2001); TaPP2C‐a10 reduces dormancy by repressing ABA signaling (Yu et al., 2019); TaABI5/TaABF1 reinforce dormancy via ABA‐responsive pathways (Ju et al., 2019); and TaGA20ox2 promotes GA synthesis, with higher expression linked to reduced dormancy and increased PHS susceptibility.

These results reinforce the conserved role of ABA–GA signaling in sorghum dormancy regulation (Benech‐Arnold & Rodríguez, 2018; Finch‐Savage & Leubner‐Metzger, 2006; Rodríguez et al., 2015) and demonstrate the value of cross‐species genomic information for identifying CGs. Such knowledge can guide marker‐assisted breeding and gene editing to improve PHS tolerance. Importantly, several QTLs identified here did not colocalize with known genes, orthologs, or previously reported sorghum QTL for dormancy or PHS. These novel loci may represent uncharacterized genes or regulatory elements, potentially acting through pathways beyond canonical ABA–GA signaling. Fine‐mapping, multi‐omics, and functional validation will be essential to uncover their roles, particularly for population‐ or environment‐specific responses, and to exploit these untapped alleles in breeding programs.

Haplotype blocks as predictive tools for breeding PHS‐resilient sorghum

Haplotype blocks comprising multiple SNPs in strong LD provide greater resolution than single biallelic SNPs, enabling the identification of functionally distinct alleles (Brinton et al., 2020). This multiallelic structure increases the precision of haplotype‐assisted forward breeding, a promising strategy for combining superior haplotypes to develop improved varieties (Varshney et al., 2021). In this study, we identified 36 haplotype blocks, including 30 for ArcG% and six for GI, with nine ArcG% blocks and all six GI blocks showing significant differentiation across all three datasets. In several QTL regions, local recombination history and occasional missing alleles at specific loci resulted in fragmented LD patterns, generating multiple haplotype blocks within the same QTL interval, despite clustering within a ±20 kb window based on LD decay (Boatwright et al., 2022). This fragmentation reflects underlying genetic heterogeneity and may help resolve multiple causal variants within a single QTL (Mohammadi et al., 2020). Genotypes carrying favorable haplotypes consistently exhibited lower GI and ArcG% under sprouting‐inducing conditions, indicating stronger dormancy and improved PHS tolerance. Many of these haplotypes occurred at low to moderate frequencies (5%–25%), suggesting ample allelic variation for targeted breeding. Overall, these results demonstrate the value of haplotype‐based selection as a powerful tool for improving PHS tolerance in sorghum.

To refine CG discovery, QTLs with significantly differentiated haplotype blocks were selected for gene mining, and 11 genes were prioritized based on their functional similarity to known dormancy‐ and PHS‐related genes in cereals. Expression profiling of these prioritized CGs provided additional context for their developmental relevance. Several genes exhibited high transcript abundance in seed‐related tissues such as embryo, endosperm, and pericarp (Table S8). Expression in these tissues corresponds to developmental stages linked with dormancy establishment and seed maturation, reflecting the spatial and temporal regulation typical of genes associated with dormancy and sprouting response in cereals (An et al., 2020; Matilla, 2024; Rodríguez et al., 2025; R. Yang et al., 2025). Gene‐based haplotype analysis identified 10 informative loci, with haplotypes from seven genes showing significant trait associations across all datasets. Notably, Hap4 Sobic.001G348100 , Hap3 Sobic.003G357600 , Hap3 Sobic.003G357700 , and Hap2 Sobic.003G358000 were consistently associated with lower GI values and occurred at low to moderate frequencies (∼5%–16%), making them promising targets for functional validation and haplotype‐assisted breeding. The genes Sobic.001G348100 (thiolase), Sobic.003G357600 and Sobic.003G357700 (multicopper oxidases), and Sobic.003G358000 (PfkB family carbohydrate kinase) have plausible functional links to PHS tolerance. Thiolases catalyze fatty acid β‐oxidation during germination, and reduced activity may limit energy release and delay germination (Germain et al., 2001). Multicopper oxidases contribute to lignin polymerization in seed coats, reducing permeability and reinforcing dormancy (Q. Zhao et al., 2013). PfkB kinases, such as OsPUKI in rice, promote ABA biosynthesis, a major pathway in dormancy regulation (Bao et al., 2025). Favorable CG‐based haplotypes were enriched among tolerant accessions and frequently shared across multiple loci, indicating that PHS tolerance is polygenic and additive rather than controlled by a single locus (Table S10). Tolerant lines typically carried several favorable haplotypes conferring low GI and ArcG%, whereas susceptible lines mostly harbored inferior alleles but occasionally retained one or more favorable variants. This mixed allelic configuration highlights the quantitative and multifactorial nature of PHS tolerance, influenced by allelic complementation, epistatic interactions, and environmentally driven modulation of gene expression, consistent with patterns reported in other cereals (Penning, 2023; Sweeney et al., 2022; B. W. Zhang et al., 2025).

Together, QTL‐ and CG‐based haplotype analyses provided complementary insights: QTL‐based haplotypes delineated broader genomic regions with stable phenotypic effects, while CG‐based haplotypes pinpointed functionally relevant allelic variants for direct application in breeding. Several prioritized genes involved in ABA signaling, energy metabolism, and seed‐coat development represent promising targets for functional validation and marker development. In addition, phenotypic evaluations were conducted under controlled conditions, which may not fully reflect variability under natural preharvest rainfall, and QTL intervals may still contain noncausal linked variants due to LD constraints (Gaut & Long, 2003). Future research should focus on fine‐mapping key QTL, validating CGs through CRISPR/Cas or expression assays, and integrating multi‐omics data to strengthen causal inference. We also recommend validating genotype performance using intact panicle assays under rain‐simulator or misting conditions and across diverse, rainfall‐prone environments. Such efforts will enhance the reliability of trait–marker associations and accelerate the deployment of validated haplotypes in breeding programs for PHS‐resilient sorghum adapted to rainfed agroecosystems.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides a comprehensive dissection of the genetic architecture underlying PHS tolerance in sorghum by integrating multi‐locus GWAS, haplotype analysis, and targeted CG discovery in a globally diverse panel. We identified 99 QTLs for GI and ArcG%, several of which were stable across environments and clustered in genomic regions enriched for dormancy‐related variation. Refinement into 36 haplotype blocks revealed superior, phenotypically consistent haplotypes, many at low to moderate frequencies, representing valuable yet untapped allelic diversity for breeding. We also identified sorghum orthologs of functionally validated PHS‐related genes from other cereals, along with known sorghum dormancy genes colocalizing with stable QTL. In parallel, we discovered novel loci and CGs with no prior association to PHS, thereby expanding the genetic landscape and providing new targets for functional characterization. Candidate gene mining within significantly differentiated haplotype blocks uncovered multiple loci linked to ABA and GA signaling, energy metabolism, and seed coat development, which are key processes in PHS tolerance. The combination of conserved dormancy regulators and novel, sorghum‐specific loci broadens the repertoire of targets for marker‐assisted breeding, genomic selection, and functional validation. As climate variability intensifies and rainfed systems face increasing PHS risks, deploying validated haplotypes from this study can accelerate the development of resilient and climate‐adaptive sorghum cultivars. Future work should focus on fine‐mapping prioritized QTL, validating CGs through gene editing, and conducting multi‐environment field trials to translate these genomic insights into durable, field‐ready solutions.
