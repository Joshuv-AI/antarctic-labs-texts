Immune-related adverse events between elderly patients with gastrointestinal and lung cancers: A real-world study
Authors: Infong Chan, Lingdong Kong, Bo Jia, Fengxiao Dong, Yuling He, Xumeng Ji, Guomei Ren, Lili Wang, Zhihao Lu, Lin Shen, Yuyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are known for their durable efficacy and favorable tolerance profiles. However, elderly patients are often underrepresented in clinical trials. The manifestation of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in elderly patients with different tumor types remains unclear. This study aimed to compare irAEs in elderly patients with gastrointestinal and lung cancers receiving ICIs and to provide real-world safety data.Methods:This retrospective study included elderly patients (≥70 years) with gastrointestinal (GI) tumors or lung cancer who received ≥2 cycles of ICIs across multiple departments within a single academic medical center between January 2016 and February 2022. A 2:1 propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics between the cohorts. The primary endpoint was the incidence of any-grade irAEs between GI tumors and lung cancer, whereas secondary analyses focused on organ-specific toxicities. Statistical comparisons were performed using the chi-squared test.Results:The overall incidence of any-grade irAEs was higher in elderly patients with lung cancer than in those with GI tumors (61.0% vs. 47.9%; P = 0.013). After matching for baseline characteristics, the lung cancer group still showed a trend toward a higher incidence of irAEs (61.7% vs. 50.8%; P = 0.056). Notably, patients with GI tumors had a higher incidence of skin toxicity (28.7% vs. 15.1%; P = 0.002), whereas patients with lung cancer had a higher incidence of thyroid dysfunction (28.1% vs. 11.9%, P <0.001).Conclusions:Elderly patients with lung cancer were more likely to experience irAEs during immunotherapy than those with GI tumors. However, dermatological toxicities were more common in elderly patients with GI tumors, whereas thyroid dysfunction was more frequently observed in those with lung cancer.

Introduction

With the increase in the geriatric population and the associated rise in the incidence of malignant tumors with age, elderly patients are expected to become a critical focus of clinical oncology in the future. In China specifically, lung and gastrointestinal (GI) tumors—primarily colorectal cancer, two malignancies central to this study—accounted for 681,124 and 333,821 new cases among older adults in 2024, respectively, with mortality rates among the highest worldwide.[1] Identifying appropriate treatment options for this patient population is therefore imperative.[2] Furthermore, elderly patients are more likely to experience comorbidities, decreased physiological fitness, or organ dysfunction, making clinical decision-making more complex.[3] Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have introduced new hope in cancer therapy and are now widely used across various cancer types, particularly antibodies targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4). Immunotherapy, which enhances the body’s immune response, has shown greater promise and appeal in elderly patients compared with traditional cytotoxic agents. Consequently, a thorough evaluation of immunotherapy regimens may have an even more profound impact on elderly patients than on their younger counterparts.[4,5]

Most studies have shown that the use of immunotherapy can provide definitive efficacy in elderly patients compared with younger patients. Age does not appear to impact ICIs efficacy, as evidenced by comparable outcomes in older and younger patients across non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) cohorts.[6–11] Regarding the safety profile and tolerability of ICI treatment in the elderly, most studies have concluded that they are similar to those observed in the overall population and that the overall toxicity of ICI treatment in elderly patients is lower than that reported for chemotherapy.[8,12–17] However, most clinical trials have included relatively few or carefully selected elderly patients, resulting in the data on immunotherapy in elderly patients being less representative.

Different tumor microenvironments (TMEs) are also believed to influence the development of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). As a result, the spectrum of irAEs is considered to vary across tumor types. A systematic review of 48 prospective ICI monotherapy trials (n = 6938) found that patients with melanoma had a higher incidence of gastrointestinal (GI) and cutaneous irAEs but a lower incidence of pneumonitis than those with NSCLC and RCC.[18] These differences may be explained by the organ-specific immune microenvironment and its interaction with immunotherapy. Translational research exploring the TME—specifically immune infiltration, adaptive immune responses, and neoantigen formation—may help clarify this variation.[19] In addition, compositional differences in local microbiota have been implicated in the development of immune-related events.[20] Moreover, the physiological and immune microenvironments of elderly patients are distinct from those of younger individuals. However, the incidence and characteristics of irAEs across different tumor types have not yet been specifically reported in the elderly population.

Therefore, this real-world study aimed to compare the differences in the occurrence of irAEs in elderly patients aged ≥70 years with GI tumors and lung cancer, including the severity (grade) of irAEs, treatment discontinuation due to irAEs, use of corticosteroid therapy, and the organs or systems involved. The study aims to provide new or supplementary safety data for elderly patients with GI tumors and lung cancer undergoing immunotherapy, with the goal of helping optimize individualized treatment strategies in the future.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was granted ethical exemption by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute due to its retrospective nature, and no ethical approval number was applicable. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. According to the current general definition of “elderly” in the field of geriatric oncology, patients aged ≥70 years were defined as elderly in our study. Data from elderly patients diagnosed with GI tumors and lung cancer and treated with ≥2 cycles of ICIs at Peking University Cancer Hospital between January 2016 and February 2022 were retrospectively collected. All patients were informed of the benefits and risks of immunotherapy and provided informed consent before receiving antitumor treatment. In addition to the matching characteristics, data on the type and grade of irAEs, use of corticosteroid therapy, and time from the initiation of immunotherapy to death or final follow-up were collected.

Evaluation criteria

irAEs were assessed by an experienced clinical oncologist to determine whether the adverse event was immune-related. The grade of irAEs was evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 4.03), with any irAE assessed as grade ≥3 considered a severe adverse event. The affected systems or organs were categorized as skin toxicity, thyroid dysfunction, pneumonitis, myocarditis, skeletal and muscular toxicity, hepatitis, GI toxicity, neurological toxicity, cytopenia or autoimmune hemolysis, nephritis, or nonspecific symptoms. Skin toxicity included rash, pruritus, reactive cutaneous capillary endothelial proliferation, leukoderma, psoriasis, and paronychia. Skeletal and muscular toxicities included myositis, myalgia, and arthralgia. GI toxicities included colitis or diarrhea, defined as watery stools in the absence of medication or infectious causes. Neurological toxicities included encephalitis, peripheral neuritis, myasthenia gravis, and dizziness. Nonspecific symptoms included fever, fatigue, weakness, headache, and nausea. The main outcomes were the safety of immunotherapy in elderly patients and the differences between GI tumors and lung cancer, focusing on the incidence and frequency of irAEs, time of onset, grades, affected systems, and required interventions.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as number (%), and continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess differences in categorical variables between the two cohorts. The independent samples t-test was used to compare the mean age between the cohorts. A propensity score was constructed to reduce confounding bias, and propensity score matching (PSM) was performed at a 2:1 ratio based on selected characteristics (sex, smoking history, alcohol consumption, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] score, and the combination of other treatment regimens), with the caliper set at 0.2. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized differences before and after matching, with post-match values <10% considered acceptable.[21] To evaluate the matching performance, baseline variables were compared using McNemar’s test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All significance levels were two-sided, with a P-value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Incidence of irAEs

A total of 407 patients receiving immunotherapy were enrolled in this study, including 261 patients with GI tumors and 146 patients with lung cancer. Other baseline characteristics were balanced between the two cohorts. Any-grade irAEs occurred in 214 (214/407, 52.6%) patients in the total population and were significantly more common in the lung cohort than in the GI cohort (GI vs. lung: 47.9% vs. 61.0%; P = 0.013 [Table 3]. The average and median ages of patients with irAEs were 73.5 ± 3.2 and 73 years, respectively, with no significant difference in age between the two cohorts (GI vs. lung: 73.9 ± 3.4 vs. 73.4 ± 3.0; P = 0.230). Incidence of irAEs in matched patients with GI tumors and lung cancer

To ensure comparability between the two groups, PSM was used to pair patients with similar baseline characteristics (including sex, smoking and drinking status, ECOG score, and concomitant therapies) at a 2:1 ratio, resulting in 197 patients in the GI cohort and 133 patients in the lung cohort [Figure 1]. Table 3 presents an overview of irAEs in the matched population. The incidence of any-grade irAEs in the matched population was comparable with that in the overall population (overall: 52.6% vs. matched: 55.2%), with a tendency for higher incidence in the lung cohort than in the GI cohort (total: 55.2%; GI: 50.8% vs. lung: 61.7%; P = 0.056). The incidence of grades 1–2 irAEs remained significantly higher in the lung cohort than in the GI cohort (GI vs. lung: 37.1% vs. 52.6%; P = 0.006); however, the incidence of grde 3–4 irAEs did not significantly differ between the two cohorts (GI vs. lung: 8.3% vs. 13.2%; P = 0.213), consistent with the results observed in the overall population. Furthermore, the number of patients who died due to irAEs was the same between the two matched groups. Similarly, the proportion of patients requiring glucocorticoid treatment for irAEs did not differ significantly between the groups (GI vs. lung: 12.7% vs. 8.3%; P = 0.280).

Differences in organs or systems involved in irAEs among the overall study population

Table 4 presents the distribution of organs or systems involved in irAEs. The most prevalent adverse event was skin toxicity (23.8%), with a significantly higher incidence in the GI cohort than in the lung cohort (GI vs. lung: 28.7% vs. 15.1%; P = 0.002). Thyroid dysfunction was the second most common adverse reaction (17.7%), occurring more frequently in the lung cohort than in the GI cohort (GI vs. lung: 11.9% vs. 28.1%; P <0.001). Other irAEs, ranked in descending order of prevalence, included hepatotoxicity (11.1%), cytopenia (8.4%), pneumonitis (7.6%), nonspecific symptoms (6.6%), GI-related symptoms (4.9%), cardiovascular events (3.7%), bone, joint, and muscle disorders (3.4%), pancreatic dysfunction (2.7%), kidney injury (2.7%), neurological disorders (1.7%), and pituitary or adrenal dysfunction (1.7%). No significant differences in the incidence of irAEs across these other systems were observed between the two groups.

Differences in organs or systems involved in irAEs among the matched populations

A total of 330 individuals were included in a 2:1 propensity score-matched comparison based on baseline characteristics (197 patients in the GI cohort and 133 patients in the lung cohort). Table 4 presents the distribution of organs or systems involved in irAEs in the matched population. Among the matched cohort, the most common irAE remained skin toxicity (21.5%), which was significantly more prevalent in the GI cohort than in the lung cohort, consistent with previous findings (GI vs. lung: 26.4% vs. 14.3%; P = 0.009). The second most prevalent irAE was thyroid dysfunction (19.1%), with a significantly higher incidence in the lung cohort than in the GI cohort (GI vs. lung: 12.7% vs. 28.6%; P = 0.001). The incidence rates of irAEs involving other organs or systems were nearly similar to those observed in the overall population, ranked in descending order as follows: hepatotoxicity (11.8%), cytopenia (8.5%), pneumonitis (7.9%), nonspecific symptoms (6.1%), GI-related symptoms (6.1%), cardiovascular disease (4.2%), bone, joint, and muscle disorders (3.6%), pancreatic dysfunction (3.0%), kidney injury (2.4%), pituitary or adrenal dysfunction (2.1%), and neurological disorders (1.8%).

Discussion

According to the American Cancer Society, lung cancer was the first leading cause of cancer-related death in 2023 (with NSCLC accounting for >85% of all lung cancers), and the second leading cause of death was colorectal cancer.[22] Lung cancer and GI tumors are the main solid tumor types among elderly Chinese individuals, and the incidence is increasing with age.[23] These two major malignant tumors continue to pose a major public health challenge.[24] ICIs have been widely used to treat lung cancer and GI tumors (the main targets are PD-1, programmed death-ligand 1 [PD-L1], and CTLA-4), and the proportion of ICIs used in the elderly population is similar to that in younger populations.[11] The side effects of immunotherapy may lead to treatment discontinuation and reduce long-term efficacy, and high-grade irAEs can be fatal in elderly individuals. This highlights the importance of paying attention to the safety of immunotherapy in elderly patients, particularly those with lung cancer and GI tumors. Therefore, this real-world retrospective study with a large sample size comprehensively compared the incidence of irAEs between elderly patients with lung cancer and GI tumors and provided supplemental safety data for elderly patients aged >70 years.

The toxicity of immunotherapy differs from that of conventional antitumor therapy and can occur in any organ/system, and it is usually associated with delayed onset and prolonged duration.[25,26] Most irAEs are assumed to be autoimmune diseases mediated by CD8+ T cells activated by ICIs, and some irAEs are also associated with the production of activated B cells and pathogenic antibodies.[27] The relationship between aging and changes in the immune system is complex and is primarily characterized by phenotypic and functional alterations in the adaptive immune system. These typically include reduced proliferation and function of B and T cells, increased numbers of regulatory cells, functional and numerical changes in cellular subpopulations, and altered signaling pathways—all of which may contribute to decreased T-cell activity.[28–32] ICIs can interfere with the internal immune balance in elderly patients; this effect is usually low grade, curable, and reversible; however, in rare cases, the effect can be severe and lead to permanent damage, resulting in treatment termination and reduced long-term efficacy. Corticosteroids and other immunomodulators are the main treatments, and they should be used carefully to minimize the likelihood of short- and long-term complications.[33,34]

Several studies have reported that the safety profile and tolerability of ICIs in the elderly are similar to those observed in the overall population, and the incidence of irAEs related to ICI treatment in elderly patients is lower than that of irAEs related to chemotherapy. CheckMate 153 demonstrated similar rates of grades 3–5 irAEs in elderly (>70 years) and poor performance status subgroups compared with the overall population.[8] Furthermore, a retrospective study found no statistically significant difference in the number, type, or severity of irAEs between patients aged ≥75 years and younger groups treated with ICIs for metastatic solid tumors.[12] Another retrospective study that compared patients aged ≥65 years and <65 years who received immunotherapy showed no significant difference in the incidence of irAEs.[13] A report from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center demonstrated comparable safety profiles in elderly patients receiving immunotherapy to published phase III data across all age groups.[14–17] However, most clinical trials included relatively few or carefully selected elderly patients, resulting in a lack of real-world research data on immunotherapy in elderly patients. In our real-world study, any-grade irAEs occurred in 147 (67.7%) patients of the total population, which is consistent with data reported in previous clinical studies. Elderly patients with lung cancer are more prone to irAEs than those with GI tumors, highlighting the need to emphasize individual differences when treating elderly patients with tumors with immunotherapy.

In our study, lung cancer patients exhibited a significantly higher incidence of irAEs than patients with GI tumor (61.0% vs. 47.9%; P = 0.013). This discrepancy may arise from the distinct immunobiology of the tumors microenvironment. The lung parenchyma contains dense infiltrates of alveolar macrophages (AMs) and tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM), which provide continuous surveillance against inhaled antigens.[35,36] ICIs disrupt local immune tolerance, triggering these cells to initiate pathological inflammatory responses.[37,38] Critically, smoking—a major risk factor for NSCLC—induces chronic airway inflammation, alters the respiratory microbiota (increasing pathogenic bacteria while reducing beneficial taxa), and promotes TNF-α secretion by activated macrophages.[39,40] Elevated pretreatment serum TNF-α levels predict severe (≥grade 3) irAEs in NSCLC patients, and smoking-associated conditions (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) increase severe pneumonitis risk by 3.5-fold by impairing mucosal repair in dysbiotic airways.[41] Conversely, GI tumors harbor protective microbial communities: Intratumoral clostridiales (e.g., lachnospiraceae) in colorectal cancer produce butyrate, which mitigates intestinal inflammation and correlates with both improved ICI efficacy and reduced irAE incidence.[42,43] In addition, GI cancer patients more frequently receive probiotic interventions, which may further modulate irAE risk—a potential strategy warranting exploration in NSCLC.

Cutaneous adverse events are the most common immunotherapy-related toxicity, occurring relatively early and mostly to a mild degree.[44] The median time for skin-related adverse reactions was 4 weeks; however, it can range from 2 to 150 weeks. Some patients continued to show toxicities even after the completion of immunotherapy.[45] In a study analyzing the severity of cutaneous irAEs, two-thirds of patients with cutaneous irAEs required systemic corticosteroids to treat rash, and 19% discontinued cancer immunotherapy owing to irAEs.[46] Fortunately, in a multivariate analysis of 559 patients with NSCLC treated with PD-1 monotherapy, the correlation between any-grade cutaneous irAEs and improved progression-free survival was the most robust among all adverse events.[47] However, previous studies did not compare the incidence of skin toxicities between patients with lung cancer and those with GI tumors. In our study, we observed a significantly higher occurrence of skin toxicity in the GI cohort (17.8% vs. 36.2%; P = 0.004). Patients with GI cancer commonly exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis. Among them, an increase in Bacteroidetes abundance can promote T helper 17 (Th17) cell differentiation. The interleukin-17 (IL-17) secreted by these Th17 cells subsequently induces elevated levels of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) family chemokines from keratinocytes, resulting in psoriasiform rashes.[48] This cascade may thus underlie the higher incidence of cutaneous toxicity in patients with GI cancer . In addition, elevated baseline autoantibody levels are associated with tissue-specific irAEs. Sullivan RJ[49] found that PB180 antibody titers against pemphigus foliaceus at desmosomes in the dermal–epidermal junction were associated with its occurrence. However, these cutaneous adverse effects may also be caused by other combination medications; for example, a retrospective analysis revealed that 80% of lichen planus patients developing rash during immunotherapy were also taking rash-inducing medications, with ICI potentially accelerating symptom development.[50] The mechanism underlying the higher occurrence of skin toxicity in GI tumors than in lung cancer still requires further investigation.

Thyroid dysfunction is the most prevalent irAE of endocrine damage induced by immunotherapy. However, it is easily underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed because of its mild symptoms and lack of specificity (e.g., malaise and fatigue).[51,52] The incidence of thyroid dysfunction during immunotherapy ranges from 6% to 20% and can initially manifest as thyrotoxicosis or hypothyroidism, but ultimately tends to manifest as hypothyroidism and could be permanent.[53] Similar to cutaneous irAEs, the development of endocrine-related irAEs has also been reported to have a positive correlation with immunotherapy efficacy.[54,55] In our study, we found a significantly higher incidence of thyroid dysfunction in elderly patients with lung cancer, with an incidence rate of 36.6%. The abundant blood supply of endocrine glands could increase their susceptibility to these pathways, thus making them one of the more frequently involved targets.[56] The embryological homology of lung-thyroid development mediated by thyroid transcription factor-1 suggests that its elevated expression in lung tumors may trigger thyroid immune dysfunction via antigenic mimicry, potentially explaining the increased incidence versus GI cancers.[57,58] Nevertheless, thyroid transcription factor-1 immunopositivity shows no established association with thyroid irAEs induced by ICI therapy.[58] Regarding thyroid dysfunction, the patient’s underlying thyroid status, TSH level, autoantibody titer (whether or not combined with underlying autoimmune disease), and tumor immune microenvironment status were associated with the occurrence of thyroid-related adverse effects during immunotherapy. Furthermore, elevated thyroid autoantibody titers caused by prior ICI use have been found to be a risk factor for the development of irAEs when restarting immunotherapy.[54,59–61]

Regarding the treatment of thyroid toxicity, the transition from thyrotoxicosis to hypothyroidism occurs in some patients during the course of the disease, and half of these patients have irreversible impairment of thyroid function and require lifelong hormone replacement therapy. Furthermore, Ascierto et al[61] reported that thyroid dysfunction induced by CTLA-4 inhibitors was dose-dependent compared with PD-1 inhibitors (particularly in patients with a CTLA-4 inhibitor dose of >10 mg/kg), suggesting that the drug dose is another important risk factor for thyroid damage.[62] Therefore, close monitoring, early diagnosis, adjustment of the treatment regimen, and appropriate interventions are required. Furthermore, close care should also be taken to distinguish thyroid dysfunction from pituitary inflammation.[56,63]

Our study supplements real-world data on immunotherapy in elderly patients and enables cross-tumor comparisons between lung cancer and GI tumors, highlighting the diverse irAEs in patients with different tumors receiving immunotherapy. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-center design with a relatively small sample size (particularly considering the heterogeneity of included tumor pathological subtypes) restricts the representativeness and generalizability of our findings. Future studies should prioritize multicenter collaborations with expanded cohorts and include diverse tumor stages/subtypes. Exploratory subgroup analyses may partially mitigate the stage-related biases in the current dataset. Second, stratification according to combination therapy type was challenging owing to inherent differences in first-line treatment regimens between NSCLC and GI cancer. Third, our study did not investigate potential correlations between irAEs and clinical efficacy endpoints (e.g., objective response rate and overall survival), which warrants dedicated investigations to simultaneously address safety and efficacy outcomes in elderly populations receiving immunotherapy. Furthermore, the lack of continuous follow-up of patients’ longitudinal clinical outcomes limits the robustness of prognostic interpretations.

In conclusion, elderly patients with lung cancer are more likely to experience irAEs when receiving immunotherapy than those with GI tumors; however, the incidence of dermatologically associated toxicity was higher in elderly patients with GI tumors, and thyroid function abnormalities were more common in elderly patients with lung cancer.
