Background

Pain affects the majority of people with advanced cancer and is a commonly feared symptom.1–3 Oxycodone is a commonly used opioid recommended for treatment of cancer pain.4,5 It produces analgesia through its central µ-opioid receptor agonist activity. 6 However, oxycodone also binds to µ-opioid receptors within the gastrointestinal tract, inhibiting local neural pathways to depress peristaltic contractions which result in delayed gastric emptying and slowed intestinal transit time. 7 This leads to opioid-induced constipation (OIC), a common adverse effect of oxycodone which is reported by up to 90% of patients taking opioids.8–10

Oxycodone/Naloxone Prolonged Release (OXN PR) consists of Oxycodone:Naloxone at a 2:1 ratio. 11 Naloxone is a competitive opioid receptor antagonist with greater µ-opioid receptor binding affinity than oxycodone, thus it is used to reverse opioid-related effects. 12 Compared to oxycodone, orally administered naloxone has low systemic availability (<2%), 13 and thus naloxone produces selective antagonism on opioid receptors in the gut to improve symptoms of OIC without affecting central opioid analgesia.14,15

Clinical trials comparing OXN PR with Oxycodone Prolonged Release (Oxy PR) have mostly focussed on non-cancer pain populations,16–21 demonstrating equivalent analgesic efficacy alongside significant improvements in OIC.17,19,20 However, these findings must be interpreted carefully in people with pain from advanced cancer. Cancer-related pain is inherently complex often arising in multiple locations of varying aetiologies from both the disease itself and its treatments, 22 as opposed to non-cancer pain, which is typically more chronic and degenerative in nature. 23 The management approaches for these two types of pain differ substantially. Unlike non-cancer pain, where opioid use is generally discouraged and limited to low doses, 23 opioids are strongly recommended as first-line therapy for moderate to severe cancer pain and are utilised in most individuals with advanced cancer.4,22 Furthermore, constipation is a common symptom experienced by most cancer patients, necessitating vigilant management to minimise its impact. 24 OIC may remain refractory to standard laxatives, and significantly diminishes quality of life. 24 Given the extensive use of opioids in this population, strategies to mitigate OIC should be rigorously studied to optimise patient care.

To date, the observed effects on pain and constipation outcomes have not been studied widely in people with pain due to advanced cancer, with only one randomised trial conducted over 10 years ago. 11 There were several limitations to this study which may restrict its applicability to usual clinical practice. Breakthrough opioid use occurs in >70% of people with advanced cancer pain at a frequency of two to six times per day. 25 However, the trial automatically withdrew participants who were taking >1 breakthrough opioid dose per day, leading towards a retention bias of those potentially more likely to experience controlled pain. Additionally, there was a relatively high participant drop-out rate (28%; n = 51/184). Furthermore, the mean daily opioid dose reported was <50 mg/day which is considered a low dose in cancer pain, 26 thus it is unclear whether the trial participants were representative of the usual population experiencing pain from advanced cancer. Oral bisacodyl was the only laxative used, and mean doses exceeded the usual recommended dose of 5–10 mg/day 27 by several fold. Thus, the translation of the non-statistically significant results of 20% reduction in bisacodyl use in the OXN PR arm are difficult to apply into usual clinical practice.

People with advanced cancer commonly experience liver dysfunction which is significant as naloxone plasma concentrations can increase by over 100-fold in those with moderate to severe liver dysfunction, 28 leading to inadvertent antagonism of central µ-opioid receptor opioid binding and the resultant unintentional reversal of analgesia. The Child-Pugh score has classically been used to identify people for whom OXN PR is contraindicated, however it is a poor predictor of first pass metabolism and poorly accounts for common causes of liver dysfunction in cancer (liver metastases, cirrhosis, portal-systemic shunting). 29 Additionally, the use of all elements required for the Child-Pugh score (bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time, ascites, encephalopathy) are less practical in the real-world setting when deciding if OXN PR is contraindicated for the particular patient. A practical method of deciding eligibility and safety for OXN PR use is needed.

The primary aim of this trial was to demonstrate the analgesic non-inferiority of OXN PR compared to Oxy PR in people with pain from advanced cancer receiving palliative care. The secondary aim was to demonstrate superiority in reducing constipation for the OXN PR arm. The hypothesis was that OXN PR is analgesically non-inferior to Oxy PR, and that constipation reduction is superior in the OXN PR arm compared to the Oxy PR arm. The detailed trial protocol has been published separately. 30

Methods

Design and population

This multi-centre, open-label, randomised, phase IV trial comparing OXN PR to Oxy PR was conducted in adult patients with cancer-related pain from advanced cancer. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Metastatic/unresectable solid tumour or haematological malignancy, (2) Numeric pain rating score ⩾ 4/10 at screening, (3) Clinically appropriate for Oxy PR 20–160 mg per day or OXN PR 20/10–160/80 mg per day as determined by the investigator, (4) Adequate liver/renal function within 14 days prior to randomisation using common biochemical markers: serum alanine aminotransferase < 2.5 × upper limit of normal (ULN) and/or serum bilirubin < 2.5 × ULN, serum albumin ⩾20 g/L, estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate ⩾50 mL/min (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) calculation), (5) Life expectancy of ⩾12 weeks.

Individuals were excluded if they were (1) unable to swallow and absorb the study medication (i.e. inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction or pseudo-obstruction, active diverticular disease, gastrointestinal haemorrhage, history of bowel perforation, history of ischaemic colitis), (2) unable to effectively metabolise the study medication (i.e. known cirrhosis, portal hypertension or liver metastasis), or (3) commencing new systemic anticancer treatment within 14 days, or radiotherapy to any abdominal area or site of pain within 4 weeks prior to randomisation.

Setting

The study was conducted across five tertiary metropolitan hospitals with cancer and palliative care services in Victoria, Australia. The study was approved by the lead site institutional Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/55080/PMCC-2019) and each participating centre obtained approval from their respective research governance office. All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected over four study visits across 5 weeks (Figure 1). Collected data included demographics, clinical and cancer characteristics, performance status (Australia Modified Karnofsky Performance Status 31 ). Liver and renal biochemistry (as above) were conducted at screening. Pain was assessed using the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF). 32 Constipation was measured using the Bowel Function Index (BFI). 33 The BFI is a validated tool for OIC has been widely used in similar trials due to its ease of administration and measurement. 11 It is a patient-rated questionnaire which scores degree of constipation, with higher scores corresponding to greater OIC. Out of a total score of 100, a difference of ⩾12 points represents a minimum clinically important difference (MCID). 34

Study flowchart.

Opioid dose was assessed by total dose of study drug used, and rescue analgesia use (total dose and frequency of immediate release opioid). Laxative use was assessed by total dose and frequency of laxative use. Quality of life was assessed using the EORTC-QLQ-C30. 35 Participants were instructed to record all rescue opioid use and laxative doses taken throughout the study period. The total daily dose of rescue analgesia as measured by the patient medication diary was converted to oral morphine equivalent daily dose (oMEDD) using the conversions from the Faculty of Pain Medicine. 36 Compliance of study drug was monitored via pill count and/or patient medication diary. Laxative doses were also converted to total daily laxative doses. One laxative dose was defined as: 50 mg docusate/8 mg sennoside tablet, 1 macrogol sachet, 20 ml lactulose, 20 ml agarol, 1 suppository or enema.

Adverse events were assessed during each assessment timepoint using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5). A treatment-related adverse event was any event that occurred or worsened after baseline, or otherwise reported serious adverse events as part of pharmacovigilance reporting.

Randomisation

Participants were randomised to receiving OXN PR or Oxy PR in a 1:1 allocation ratio, stratified by site using permuted block randomisation.

Statistical analyses and sample size

The primary outcome was average pain difference on Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF) at 5 weeks. Pre-specified equivalence margin was 1. Secondary outcomes were comparison of other pain outcomes (BPI-SF), constipation (Bowel Function Index, BFI), quality of life (EORTC-QLQ-C30), and opioid and laxative dose over 5 weeks.

Details of the sample size calculation can be found in the protocol paper. 30 In brief, based on the largest standard deviation (1.4) reported by Dupoiron et al., 12 the planned sample size of 48 participants per arm had 80% power to show that the mean BPI-SF score in the OXN PR arm is not more than 1 point worse to the Oxy PR arm. This assumed that the expected mean difference was 0, a standard deviation of 1.45 and 10% loss to follow-up. This sample size was also deemed to be large enough to detect a mean difference of 16 on the BFI between the two arms, assuming a standard deviation of 26.

Pain was measured at Weeks 0, 1, 3 and 5 during the main study phase. The primary analysis was a generalised linear mixed effects model for repeated measurements, adjusted for the stratification variable (site) with an identity link function. This model included pain score (Weeks 0–5) as the dependent variable, included a random effect for patient to allow for the repeated measurements and fixed effects for treatment arm assigned, time and site and an interaction between treatment arm and time. Model-based effect estimates (mean difference between treatment arms) over the 5 week period was be calculated, along with the 95% confidence interval (CI). This analysis followed the intention to treat principle by including all participants in the arm they were randomised to. The statistical analysis plan is included in the Supplemental Material 1.

If the 95% CI for the mean difference between treatment arms excluded the non-inferiority margin of 1, OXN PR were to be declared non-inferior to Oxy PR in terms of pain management. We also defined estimands, 37 including specifying how intercurrent events were handled. Details of the estimands defined are included in the Supplemental Material 2. A similar model was fitted to analyse the secondary outcomes, except that these outcomes were assessed using a superiority hypothesis.

Results

Participants were enrolled between September 2019 and December 2021. The study comprised 37 participants between the two arms (Figure 2). Due to COVID-19 pandemic restrictions, the study paused unexpectedly significantly limiting accrual to the planned sample size. Mean age was 63 years. Over half of the participants were male (57%), had solid cancer (87%) and bony metastases (68%; Table 1).

Primary outcome

At primary endpoint (5 weeks), the average pain score reported by the OXN PR arm was 2.6 (95% CI 1.2; 4.0) whereas the Oxy PR arm reported an average pain score of 4.1 (95% CI 2.9; 5.3; Table 3). The mean difference in average pain score between arms was −1.5 (95% CI −3.3; 0.4) favouring OXN PR, meeting analgesic non-inferiority with the a priori non-inferiority margin of 1. The mean difference in average pain score at 5 weeks (the average difference over the entire study period between the two arms) was −2.37 (−6.19; 1.45). Other pain scores (worst pain, least pain, pain now) were also lower in the OXN PR arm at Week 5. At each timepoints, average pain scores were lower in the OXN PR arm (Table 2 and Figure S1).

Secondary outcome

At primary endpoint (Week 5), the OXN PR arm reported a significantly lower mean score on the BFI (7.6 (95% CI −9.6; 24.8)) compared to the Oxy PR arm (32.5 (95% CI 17.8; 47.1), with a baseline adjusted mean difference between arms of 24.87 (95% CI 47.54; 2.21), favouring the OXN PR arm (p = 0.03), which is greater than its established MCID of ⩾12 points 34 (Table 3).

Total opioid dose (study drug + rescue opioid) was significantly lower in the OXN PR arm (−42.0 (95% CI −78.6; −5.5) mg, p = 0.026). Study drug dose was lower for the OXN PR arm at each observed timepoint (Figure 3). Among the quality of life scores, mean pain score was lower by −21.67 points (out of 100) (95% CI −45.91; 2.57, p = 0.08) in the OXN PR arm (exceeding its MCID of >9.4 points 39 ) and no significant differences seen within the other scores (Table 3).

Of the BPI-SF pain interference scores, the OXN PR arm reported significantly lower interference with performing normal work (−3.3 (95% CI −5.7; −0.9), p = 0.007) and lower scores for all other interference items.

Adverse events

There were eight treatment-related adverse events and no serious adverse events reported in the OXN PR arm compared to 25 adverse events and two serious adverse events in the Oxy PR arm (Table S2). The adverse events were common oxycodone-related adverse effects including constipation, nausea, dizziness and confusion. The serious adverse events related to one participant experiencing a fall leading to a fracture requiring hospitalisation.

Discussion

Pain outcomes

This is the only trial comparing analgesic and constipation outcomes between OXN PR and Oxy PR in people with pain from advanced cancer receiving palliative care. These outcomes build on the results of the only other trial examining cancer related pain, conducted over a decade ago. The results from this and other studies in the non-cancer populations have not found significant differences in pain scores between both arms.11,12,18,20 In this study, for the primary outcome, we observed that OXN PR was analgesically non-inferior to Oxy PR at 5 weeks demonstrated by average pain score exceeding the a priori equivalence margin of 1 between arms. The minimal clinically important difference for average pain score on the BPI-SF is 2 or greater, and over the 5-week trial period, the OXN PR arm demonstrated a lower score (−2.37 (95% CI −6.19; 1.45)), adding strength to the non-inferiority argument. Additionally, participants reported similarly lower scores for all other pain items of the BPI-SF (worst pain, least pain, pain now) in the OXN PR arm at 5 weeks, and average pain score was also lower at each timepoint, showing consistency of this effect. Furthermore, pain interference scores were better in the OXN PR arm, particularly with reduced interference in performing normal work (p = 0.007). These differences were seen despite lower opioid use in the OXN PR arm. Our results are concordant to that of other randomised clinical trials examining both drug preparations in other cohorts,11,12,18,20 and we conclude that from an analgesic perspective OXN PR is non-inferior to Oxy PR when used for pain from advanced cancer.

As previously mentioned, oxycodone binds to opioid receptors in both the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system. Oral naloxone undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism, and in individuals with normal liver function, oral naloxone doses below 120 mg have very low oral bioavailability (<2%).15,40 Therefore, clinically significant systemic absorption should not be expected in this cohort. Consistently, based on our study and others,12,13,18,20 OXN PR does not appear to result in inferior analgesic outcomes.

Constipation outcomes

At Week 5, the baseline-adjusted difference in BFI score between arms was large, representing a clinically meaningful difference (24.87 (95% CI 47.54; 2.21); p = 0.03), favouring the OXN PR arm. Compared to Oxy PR, OXN PR is superior in reducing OIC in those with pain from advanced cancer. These observed differences were not related to total laxative dose used which was no different between the two arms.

The significant reduction in BFI score was also seen by other trials with observational periods between 5 and 12 weeks,11,12,20 however to a lesser degree than reported in this study. Our study observed a greater reduction in BFI score with time, with maximum improvement at Week 5. One possible reason for this is that unlike the other studies, our study did not limit breakthrough opioid use. Additionally, the dose of OXN PR was not fixed. Proportionally, due to a reduction of breakthrough opioid use with time, OXN PR contributed to a larger proportion of consumed opioid at the end of the study (91%; 59.9 mg out of 63.3 mg) compared to in the beginning of the study (69%; 71.5 mg out of total 104.3 mg). The ideal proportion of naloxone to oxycodone (and other opioids) for prevention of OIC has not been studied and could potentially be investigated in future.

The Oxy PR arm had more adverse events (25 vs 8 in the OXN PR arm). Of these, most (18 out of 25) were related to known gastrointestinal dysmotility related effects from oxycodone (constipation, nausea, vomiting). Another trial which also reported on adverse events in detail also found greater incidences of the same adverse events in Oxy PR compared to OXN PR. 20 The comparatively low number of adverse events in the OXN PR arm provides evidence of its overall safety in this population.

Limitations

This study is small and did not reach its target sample size, however it was able to achieve its primary and secondary outcomes with the smaller sample, though noting that a larger sample would have allowed more precise estimation of the observed effects. The results of lower pain scores and lower study drug dose and rescue drug dose in the OXN PR arm was unexpected. We are unable to explain this finding based on demographic, cancer stage and other collected data, however the correlation of both lower pain scores and lower opioid dose suggests this was a true finding in this cohort. It is possible that with a larger sample size, a greater variation of participants may have been observed and this effect may potentially be diluted, although this remains to be studied. This trial was limited to a 5-week duration, however it is likely that where OXN PR remains clinically appropriate for use, the results of this trial may be extrapolated for longer-term use. Also, the Oxy PR arm required higher doses of study and rescue drug, but had more participants with neuropathic pain, thus this needs to be considered in future studies by accounting for neuropathic agent use or potentially excluding patients with neuropathic pain altogether. Finally, controlled study conditions may not reflect real-world clinical practice, however we have attempted to reflect usual practice by allowing usual laxatives, not limiting breakthrough medication use, and defining practical cut-offs for liver and renal dysfunction to simulate usual care.

Future directions

As the addition of naloxone to oxycodone does not negatively affect analgesia, future research could explore combining naloxone to other oral slow and immediate release opioid preparations. The ideal proportion of naloxone to oxycodone and other opioids is also not known and is important to study to optimise the prevention of OIC in this cohort. Pharmacoeconomic studies may also be helpful to investigate potential differences in health care expenditure.

Conclusions

OXN PR is analgesically non-inferior to Oxy PR, and superior in reducing constipation in people with pain from advanced cancer who do not have significant liver or renal impairment. These results suggest OXN PR as a suitable alternative choice of opioid preparation in this advanced cancer cohort.
