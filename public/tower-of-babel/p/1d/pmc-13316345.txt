Early outcomes following multivalve heart operation in octogenarians
Authors: Ahmed A. Abdelrehim, Archie Landrum, Christophe Hansen-Estruch, William Holman, James E. Davies, Panos N. Vardas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the rising incidence and complexity of valvular disease in the elderly population, data regarding surgical outcomes in octogenarians remain limited.MethodsIn this single-center, retrospective review of octogenarians who underwent simultaneous multivalve operations at our institution between 2019 and 2025, the primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications, intensive care unit (ICU) and total hospital length of stay (LOS), and discharge disposition. Univariable analyses were performed to assess factors associated with survival and factors associated with discharge disposition among survivors (home vs skilled nursing facility).ResultsForty-three patients (mean age, 82.0 years; 62.8% male; mean EuroSCORE II, 10.5%) underwent multivalve operations. At least 1 major comorbidity was present in all patients. Nine patients (20.9%) underwent redo sternotomy, and 6 (14%) underwent a triple-valve operation. In-hospital mortality occurred in 6 patients (14%). The need for mechanical circulatory support (MCS) was strongly associated with in-hospital mortality (P = .001), as were renal failure (P = .002), prolonged ventilation (P = .004), and prolonged ICU stay (P = .03). Redo sternotomy, triple-valve intervention, and concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting were not associated with increased mortality. Among 37 survivors, 21 (56.8%) were discharged home. Longer total LOS (P = .01), ICU LOS (P = .01), and transfusion requirement (P = .04) were significantly associated with skilled nursing facility (SNF) discharge.ConclusionsMultivalve operations in select octogenarians are feasible with a multidisciplinary approach. Need for MCS, renal failure, prolonged ventilation, and ICU LOS were associated with in-hospital mortality. Among survivors, transfusion requirement and longer ICU and total hospital LOS were associated with discharge to an SNF rather than home.

Central Message

Despite a high burden of comorbidities, multivalvular operation in octogenarians is feasible but requires a multidisciplinary heart-team approach and judicious patient selection.

Perspective

Data on the feasibility of multivalvular operations in octogenarians are limited. As this population continues to grow, they remain heterogeneous, with numerous factors to consider. To select the best intervention for each individual, decisions must be made on a holistic, case-by-case basis and through a multidisciplinary heart team approach at high-volume quaternary centers.

According to the US Census Bureau, 20% of the population is projected to be 65 years of age or older by 2030.1 The life expectancy and quality of life of the elderly population continue to increase at the cost of a growing prevalence of cardiovascular conditions, such as valvular heart disease. In population-based studies, increasing age has been strongly associated with a greater prevalence of valvular heart disease. For instance, the prevalence of moderate to severe valvular heart disease is 2.5% in the general population but 13.4% in those age >75 years, in whom it has been demonstrated to significantly increase mortality, with an adjusted mortality risk ratio of 1.36 (95% confidence interval, 1.15-1.62) compared to in the general population.2,3

Surgical intervention is often the treatment of choice in symptomatic, severe valve disease or in the setting of left ventricular dysfunction.4 As the population continues to grow, the number and complexity of elderly patients being evaluated for surgical intervention on multivalvular heart disease will continue to increase.5 Despite significant data on single-valve operations in octogenarians, there is a paucity of data on the feasibility as well as the outcomes of multivalve operations in this population. To address this, we aimed to assess the outcomes of multivalve surgical intervention in this age group and to analyze factors associated with these outcomes, leveraging a multidisciplinary and consistent management strategy in our single-institutional experience.

Methods

Data Source

The University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Institutional Review Board approved this study on August 9, 2023, with a waiver of the requirement for informed consent (IRB-300011174). Our UAB institutional database was queried to identify the population of interest. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons Adult Cardiac Surgery Database descriptions were used.

Patient Population

This study included patients age ≥80 years who underwent surgical multivalve operations at our institution between January 1, 2019, and June 30, 2025. This contemporary date range was selected to reflect current surgical practice, perioperative care standards, and referral patterns in the era of expanded transcatheter valve therapies. Demographic information, preoperative characteristics, operative details, perioperative course, and early outcomes related to survival, reoperation, and adverse events were collected. All operations were performed on an elective or urgent basis; none were performed on an emergent basis (ie, requiring operation within 24 hours).

Patient Selection

The decision to proceed with cardiac surgery for octogenarians (ie, patient selection and management strategy) has always been the product of multidisciplinary discussions at the UAB Heart Center. Our algorithm is further centered around clinical frailty and cognitive assessment, evaluation of organ reserve, and anatomic suitability, with an emphasis on Class I indications and those not amenable to percutaneous intervention (Figure 1, Appendix E1).Figure 1Patient selection algorithm for multivalve surgery in octogenarians. Measures and Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death during the index hospitalization. Secondary outcomes were stroke, major adverse cardiac events (MACE), renal failure (defined as a new requirement for dialysis postoperatively), permanent pacemaker, prolonged ventilation, and length of stay (LOS). MACE is defined as a 3-point composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke. Prolonged ventilation was defined as the use of mechanical respiratory ventilation for ≥24 hours postoperatively. Extracardiac arteriopathy was characterized by any noncardiogenic arterial disease, including aortic disease, peripheral artery disease, and carotid stenosis ≥50%. Given that most of the procedures were not eligible for Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score calculation, the EuroSCORE II was used to assess the risk of in-hospital mortality uniformly across all 43 patients.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Welch t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were reported as number (%) and compared using the Fisher exact test. A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Surgical Techniques

Reflecting our practice, the algorithms and protocols used were uniform with minor variations in practice styles. Noncritical aspects of the procedure were performed by trainees under supervision, while critical aspects of procedures performed during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and aortic cross-clamp periods were reserved for attending surgeons.

Postoperative Outcomes

Postoperative outcomes are summarized in Table 3. In-hospital mortality occurred in 6 patients (14.0%) at a median of postoperative day 14.5 (range days, 2-36). Prolonged ventilation occurred in 7 patients (16.3%). Renal failure occurred in 3 (7.0%) patients, all of whom required dialysis and died within 30 days of the index operation. Ten patients (23.3%) received mechanical circulatory support (MCS), 9 (20.9%) received an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP), and 1 (2.3%) underwent venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). In the IABP group, 4 patients (44.4%) died within 30 days, and the other 5 (55.6%) survived to discharge. The single patient requiring VA-ECMO died on postoperative day 2. Overall survival to discharge for the MCS subgroup was 50%. Reexploration was performed in 8 patients (18.6%), all due to bleeding. Delirium occurred in 6 patients (14.0%). All early deaths in our cohort were due to hemodynamic decompensation and consequent multiorgan failure postoperatively. The median ICU LOS was 3.0 days (interquartile range, 1.8-5.4 days), and the mean total LOS was 10.9 ± 7.0 days. Univariable Analysis: Predictors of In-Hospital Mortality

To identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality, a univariable analysis was performed comparing survivors (n = 37) to deceased patients (n = 6) (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3). Preoperative comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease (66.7% vs 35.1%; P = .193) and diabetes mellitus (50.0% vs 21.6%, P = .164), trended toward higher prevalence in the deceased group, and ejection fraction trended lower in the deceased group (39.2% vs 48.2%; P = .073), but no differences were statistically significant.

Among operative variables, the need for MCS was strongly associated with in-hospital mortality (83.3% vs 13.5%; P = .001), and intraoperative IABP use was significantly higher in the deceased group (50.0% vs 8.1%; P = .02). CPB and cross-clamp times were similar in the 2 groups. Redo sternotomy (P = .31), triple-valve intervention (P = .57), nonelective operative status (P = .58), endocarditis (P = 1.0), and concomitant CABG (P = .65) were not significantly associated with mortality. Notably, none of the 9 redo patients or 6 triple-valve patients died.

Postoperatively, the deceased group had significantly higher rates of renal failure (50.0% vs 0%; P = .002), prolonged ventilation (66.7% vs 8.1%; P = .004), and MACE (33.3% vs 2.7%; P = .047). Mean ICU LOS was significantly longer in the deceased group (12.8 ± 13.0 days vs 3.8 ± 3.2 days; P = .038). All 3 patients who developed postoperative renal failure required new dialysis and died during the index hospitalization.

Univariable Analysis: Predictors of Discharge Disposition

Among the 37 survivors, univariable analysis was performed comparing patients discharged home (n = 21; 56.8%) to those discharged to an SNF (n = 16; 43.2%) (Table E1). Preoperatively, the 2 groups were similar at baseline. Among operative variables, both CPB time (82.9 minutes vs 105.4 minutes; P = .068) and cross-clamp time (64.1 minutes vs 77.5 minutes; P = .073) trended higher in the SNF group. Redo sternotomy (P = .248), triple valve intervention (P = .206), nonelective operative status (P = .2), and concomitant CABG (P = .489) were not significantly associated with SNF discharge. Postoperatively, 3 variables were associated with SNF discharge: total LOS (mean, 12.2 ± 5.6 days vs 8.4 ± 4.4 days; P = .011), ICU LOS (mean, 5.5 ± 4.1 days vs 2.5 ± 1.4 days; P = .012), and transfusion requirement (75.0% vs 38.1%; P = .045). Prolonged ventilation also trended higher in the SNF group (18.8% vs 0%; P = .072). Among survivors, the use of MCS was not significant for patient discharge disposition, with rates of 9.5% for home versus 18.8% for SNF (P = .634).

Discussion

This study of 43 elderly patients undergoing multivalvular cardiac operations offers insights into the feasibility of multivalvular surgery in this rapidly growing patient population. We found that this population had a high burden of comorbidities, in-hospital mortality was 14.0%, and more than one-half of the survivors (56.8%) were discharged home. The need for MCS was the strongest predictor of in-hospital mortality, followed by postoperative renal failure, prolonged ventilation, and prolonged ICU stay. Notably, procedural complexity—including redo sternotomy, triple-valve intervention, and concomitant CABG—was not associated with increased mortality at high-volume centers. Among survivors, discharge disposition was driven by operative and postoperative factors, specifically total and ICU LOS and transfusion requirement (Figure 3).

Although disease of a single heart valve, usually the aortic valve or mitral valve, is most common, the prevalence of multivalvular disease is rising. A 15-year systematic review of valve operations in North America by Lee and colleagues6 found that 11% of operations involved multiple valves. One 2022 study demonstrated that based on morbidity, 30-day mortality, and overall mortality data, multivalve operations are a safe option in selected patients.7

In octogenarians, much of the literature evaluates the outcomes of single-valve operations, with reported 30-day mortality ranging from 13% for isolated aortic valve surgery to 13.8% for mitral valve surgery, with acceptable long-term survival.8, 9, 10 To date, the literature on multivalvular operations in patients age 80 to 89 years is limited. Maleszka and colleagues11 reported that of 55 octogenarian patients who underwent simultaneous aortic and mitral valve replacement, 9% died in the 30-day postoperative period. Another 2024 study evaluated elective multivalve surgeries in 101 octogenarians; however, in contrast to the present study, those authors excluded patients with concomitant surgeries, redo sternotomies, and nonelective operations and reported a 14% all-cause in-hospital mortality rate, similar to our study.12 Our data add to the current literature and support the feasibility of multivalvular heart operations in this high-risk population.

Several risk factors were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in our cohort. The need for MCS was the strongest predictor, followed by postoperative renal failure, prolonged ventilation, and prolonged ICU LOS. All 3 patients who developed renal failure required new dialysis and did not survive. A reduced preoperative ejection fraction trended toward significance, consistent with prior data.13 Comorbidities, including kidney disease and diabetes, were more prevalent in the deceased group, potentially serving as surrogate markers for diminished physiologic reserve, which is consistent with the existing literature on these comorbidities. With the current longevity of the elderly population, acquired disease prevention and management are essential for reducing mortality in this population.

Additionally, the average age of our cohort referred for surgery was 82 years, and 20% underwent repeat sternotomy, both of which are well-recognized risk factors that add to the morbidity of the operation. Referral timing becomes important in optimizing the risk:benefit ratio while also minimizing interventions throughout a lifetime, particularly for those who undergo valve replacement at an early age with limited prosthesis durability.

Furthermore, all our patients underwent multivalve surgery, 60.5% underwent concomitant operations, and 14% underwent triple-valve intervention. This high burden of valvular disease, as well as concomitant lesions, merits attention. We adopted a low threshold to address all valvular lesions during the index operation when feasible, as well as residual cardiac lesions. This approach is supported by the nonsignificantly higher risk of mortality in our cohort compared to octogenarians undergoing single valve operations reported in the literature, suggesting a higher risk but potentially higher reward substrate. Our approach is also supported by maximized efficiency in expert centers with reduced operative CPB and cross-clamp times; our average cross-clamp and bypass times were 69 minutes and 91 minutes, respectively. In addition, procedural complexity did not drive mortality in our cohort; repeat sternotomy and triple-valve intervention were not associated with in-hospital mortality. Finally, addressing CAD concomitantly with multivalve repair is a growing need in this population.

Among survivors, discharge disposition was driven by operative and postoperative course—specifically total and ICU LOS and transfusion requirement—rather than baseline risk profile. Of note, the rate of delirium in our cohort, which occurred in only 6 patients, appears lower than reported in some studies of this age group. We attribute this to early mobilization, selection of ICU rooms with windows, minimization of narcotic pain treatment and ICU LOS, and early removal of invasive lines and catheters.

Recently, the introduction of advanced transcatheter technologies in cardiac surgery has revolutionized the treatment of valvular disease in elderly and high-risk populations.14 These advances have made both staged multivalvular transcatheter procedures and hybrid procedures promising treatment options for multivalvular disease in octogenarians. Transcatheter valve replacement and repair are not panaceas for all types of heart valve disease, however, and surgery often is the sole feasible treatment. In our cohort, all patients were evaluated for percutaneous interventions. Endocarditis, unsuitable valvular anatomy, valve calcification or size not amenable to clips, and patient operative fitness were the main barriers to proceeding with surgery. Considering this, as the elderly population continues to grow, this remains a very heterogeneous group, with numerous factors to consider, and thus the “best” operation must be determined on a holistic, case-by-case basis and through a multidisciplinary heart-team approach to select the best intervention for each individual.

Limitations

As a retrospective study using patient data from a single institution's database, this work is subject to inherent limitations. Patient selection bias is prevalent, as poor surgical candidates were deferred, and referral bias is also prevalent owing to the institution’s function as a tertiary center. Another limitation is the small sample size. Frailty is an important predictor of outcomes in this population. While we did not use formal frailty assessment scores, clinical intuition was used in conjunction with clinical assessment of independence level in activities of daily living. Additionally, the maximized efficiency in expert centers, with reduced operative CPB and cross-clamp times, limits the generalizability of our findings. Third, the study population is characterized by significant clinical heterogeneity. By grouping all multivalvular interventions into a single cohort, we acknowledge that we may mask the diverse comorbidities and risk profiles associated with specific procedures; hence, surgical outcomes are highly context-dependent, and further identifiable risk factors were expanded on. Finally, this study is limited to early outcomes and discharge disposition. Long-term outcomes, including survival, functional status, and return to independent living, could not be assessed owing to the referral nature of our tertiary center. Future multicenter studies with longitudinal follow-up are needed to fully characterize the long-term benefit of multivalve surgery in this population.
