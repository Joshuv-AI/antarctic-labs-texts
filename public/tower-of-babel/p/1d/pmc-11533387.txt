Microbial degradation of the waste jute fiber and sawdust fiber-based epoxy composites in drainage system
Authors: Mohammad Salman Haque, MA Islam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe non-biodegradable nature of polymers presents a considerable environmental hazard, mainly when they accumulate in drainage systems and pollute the water. The application of natural fiber composites has the potential to offer a feasible resolution to address this problem, as natural fibers possess inherent biodegradability. This study examined the degradation ability of epoxy composites made with 5 wt% waste jute fiber and 5 wt% waste sawdust fiber when subjected to drainage water for one year. The composites comprising jute fiber and sawdust demonstrated a reduction in tensile strength by 57 % and 40 %, respectively, because of biodegradation caused by microorganisms. The microorganism consumed the fiber for energy, resulting in significant weight and flexural strength loss. The weathered waste jute and sawdust fiber composite samples displayed an additional peak at a wavenumber of 1700 cm−1 for FTIR analysis, indicating the presence of the C=O group and reflecting CO2 production. The SEM image reveals the presence of clusters, biofilms, and colonies of microorganisms on the surface of the composites, indicating that the microbes were utilizing the fibers and matrix as a substrate for growth and multiplication. This research contributes to the understanding of the degradation mechanisms of sustainable composites and provides insight into the advancement of eco-friendly materials to reduce water pollution.Keywords: Microbial degradation, Weathering, Waste natural fibers, Composite materials, Biodegradability

Introduction

Advanced composite materials like fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) and fiber-reinforced cement are slowly replacing concrete and steel as building materials. Polymer composites are expected to become increasingly popular because of their superior strength, low weight, corrosion resistance, and minimal maintenance expense. Epoxy resins are polymers with low molecular weight that include numerous epoxide groups. Unprocessed epoxy resins typically have inadequate mechanical, chemical, and heat resistance. The desirable qualities are obtained by reacting linear epoxy resin with appropriate curatives, resulting in the production of thermoset structures that are cross-linked in three dimensions. Furthermore, it can survive multiple loading and unloading cycles without substantial damage, making it appropriate for demanding applications such as wind turbine blades and parts for airplanes [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite these benefits, engineers are becoming more encouraged to "go green" in a variety of areas by developing more eco-friendly methods and new materials that are biodegradable or recyclable [4].

Composites developed of synthetic fibers outperformed natural fiber composites in various ways, including interfacial adhesion, stiffness, and strength. However, recyclability and increased demand as a result of its widespread application in different industries are now highly relevant challenges. Natural fiber-reinforced composites, also known as bio-based polymer composites, are gaining popularity due to their renewability and biodegradability, as well as their low cost and lightweight design [5]. The escalation of the global population and economic growth has resulted in the emergence of water pollution as a significant issue worldwide. This is attributed to the amplified agricultural, industrial, and domestic activities. The pollution caused by hazardous wastes like heavy metals and polymers has significantly contributed to the deterioration of the quality of both rivers and groundwater [6]. The presence of pollutants in drainage systems can also result in a multitude of adverse effects, such as compromised water quality, detrimental health outcomes, and harm to the surrounding ecosystem [7,8]. Non-biodegradable polymers can infiltrate the drain water supply through multiple pathways, such as seepage from waste disposal sites, surface runoff from roads and parking areas, and intentional discharge and log the water supply. This infiltration can result in water supply disruption and the generation of unpleasant odors. The incorporation of natural fibers has been employed as a means of addressing water pollution in drainage systems. This is because natural fibers possess complete biodegradability and can promote the biodegradability of polymeric materials [9,10].

Microbes like bacteria and fungi can break down the structural components of natural fibers like cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. These microorganisms can colonize the surface of natural fiber composites when they are exposed to the atmosphere and break down the natural fibers. When natural fibers break down, the composite structure weakens, which can encourage microbial development and speed up the degradation process [[11], [12], [13]]. Natural fibers are also renewable, biodegradable, and recyclable, and they leave a small carbon impact. The potential of natural fibers, such as jute fiber, coir fiber, banana fiber and sawdust fiber, to facilitate the decomposition of polymers has been demonstrated, thereby making a significant contribution to environmental sustainability. The incorporation of natural fibers into polymer composites has the potential to expedite their degradation process through the stimulation of microbial growth and enhancement of the material's ability to absorb water and oxygen. This may lead to a reduction in the amount of non-biodegradable waste present in the environment, thereby helping to alleviate the pollution of water [14,15]. The utilization of Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) has been employed to investigate the effects of biodegradability on the morphology and chemical characteristics of the samples in this research.

Experimental procedure

The waste Sawdust fibers and jute fibers were used in this study to evaluate the influence of natural fiber on the biodegradation of the epoxy-based composites. Waste jute fibers can be turned into biodegradable plastic composites, which are better for the environment than traditional plastics. Similar to the use of jute fibers, sawdust can be integrated into biodegradable composites to enhance their strength and sustainability. The waste jute fiber was obtained from a nearby jute mill, and the sawdust fibers were obtained from a carpenter shop. Jute fibers consist of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and other adhesive substances. Cellulose, comprising approximately 64 % of the fiber composition, provides their strength. Meanwhile, lignin, constituting around 12 %, imparts durability and hydrophobic properties, safeguarding the cell wall against degradation. Sawdust fibers also offer appealing properties as a strengthening material. These include low density, affordability, excellent specific strength and modulus, and biodegradability. Structurally, sawdust fibers form a three-dimensional polymeric material primarily composed of approximately 44 % cellulose, 26 % hemicellulose, and 24 % lignin [5].

After collecting, the fibers were washed, oven-dried, and cut to a length of 2 mm. A 1:10 vol ratio of methyl-tetrahydro-phthalic anhydride was mixed with the epoxy resin. Then, the mixture underwent a degassing process lasting approximately 10 min to eliminate any entrapped air or gas bubbles. Following the degassing process, the mixture was subsequently cast into the desired configuration through the act of pouring it into a mold.

The sawdust underwent a process of heating at a temperature of 100 °C for approximately 10 min to eliminate any existing moisture. The epoxy resin was placed in a beaker, and 5 wt% of the sawdust was added before being properly combined to maintain a consistent consistency throughout. Then, to ensure thorough mixing, the hardener (10 vol% of the resin) was added to the mixture. Subsequently, the amalgamation underwent a vacuum treatment lasting approximately 10 min, to eliminate any entrapped air or gas bubbles. Following the completion of the vacuum process, the mixture was subsequently transferred into a mold. A hand lay-up process was shown in Fig. 1a and subjected to a curing period of 5–6 h. The manufacturing process of jute fiber composites is like that of sawdust composites.

(a) Composite fabrication process; (b) as-cast condition and (c) 1-year weathered condition tensile test samples.

The rectangular-shaped tensile samples (ASTM D 3039-17) were fabricated through precise cutting techniques (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, the samples' peripheries were subjected to a smoothing process to eliminate any potential flaws or inconsistencies that may impede the mechanical evaluation. Subsequently, the composite samples were affixed onto the grips and subjected to tensile tests using a universal testing machine (Instron 3369, 50 KN capacity). The apparatus was configured to maintain a consistent deformation rate of 2 mm/min while monitoring the applied load during the elongation of the specimen until it reached its ultimate tensile strength. For one year, samples of epoxy-based polymer composites were kept in the drainage system with sawdust and jute fiber to study their biodegradability. After one year, the samples (Fig. 1c) were analyzed using the methods described above. The JCM-7000 SEM instrument was used to analyze the fracture surface and outer surface of the composites to understand of the effect of sawdust and jute fibers on biodegradation. FTIR analysis was utilized to determine the chemical changes that happened in the drainage system during the weathering process. Powder samples were made from composites of as-cast and weathered samples to perform the FTIR analysis.

Result and discussion

Effect of natural fibers on strength

The tensile strength of the pure epoxy resin measured approximately 25 MPa, while the flexural strength was around 62 MPa. It can be used in the construction of cars, snowboards, airplanes, and bicycles [3]. However, the incorporation of short jute fiber and sawdust fiber led to a noteworthy reduction in the both tensile strength and flexural strength of the raw composite material (Fig. 2). With the addition of short jute fiber, the tensile strength decreased by 13.5 %, whereas with the addition of short sawdust fiber, the strength decreased by 33 %. However, after the addition of 5 wt% chopped jute fibers and sawdust fibers, the flexural strength was reduced by 11 % and 15 %, respectively. In this study, sawdust and chopped jute fibres of approximately 2 mm were utilized, which is less than the fiber's critical length. This type of fiber fails to efficiently transfer the imposed load. There is still a gap between reinforcement particle to particle. Furthermore, short fibers in composites operated as stress raisers, which means that the stress at the ends of the fibers stayed higher than the stress in the surrounding matrix, causing the fracture observed in the SEM image. This crack then propagated through the matrix, leading to the failure of the composite shown in Fig. 3. The river pattern in the image indicates that the fracture was caused by a brittle fracture [16]. The inclusion of both fibers lowered tensile and flexural strength, although jute fiber resisted the strength reduction better than sawdust fiber. Jute fibers have a high aspect ratio, indicating that they are elongated and extremely thin. As a result, they can form stronger bonds with the matrix and distribute loads more efficiently than sawdust fibers, which have a lower aspect ratio than jute fiber [17].

Effect of jute fiber and sawdust fiber on biodegradation

The short jute fiber and sawdust fiber were distributed uniformly all over the matrix for the as-cast samples shown in Fig. 1a. After a year in drainage water, the epoxy sample turned brown, and the jute and sawdust fiber composites showed many cavities, indicating microbial damage, as shown in Fig. 1b. The composites samples exhibited substantial texture changes, acute damages, and several void formations due to fiber swelling and bacterial attacks.

Tensile test results show a notable decrease in tensile strength for both pure epoxy resin and composites after immersion in drain water, as shown in Fig. 4. The pure epoxy that was subjected to weathering exhibited a decrease in tensile strength of approximately 15 %, compared to the non-weathered sample. The jute fiber composite demonstrated a reduction of approximately 57 %, whereas the sawdust composite experienced a decrease of around 40 % following exposure to drain water for 1 year.

Tensile strength of non-weathered and weathered samples.

Fig. 5 displays the stress-strain curves, indicating a brittle fracture as no plastic region is evident. This is further supported by the SEM image in Fig. 3. Weathering in drain water, the tensile strength and elongation decreased due to weakened bonding between the matrix and fibers. After one year of weathering, the composite samples developed numerous voids and cracks, resulting in reduced strength (Fig. 1b). This was caused by water absorption and micro-bacterial attacks on the jute and sawdust fibers.

Stress-strain diagram of the Weathered and Raw Samples.

The pure epoxy resin that was exposed to weathering also showed a decrease in flexural strength by around 10 % compared to the non-weathered sample (Fig. 6). The jute fiber composite had a reduction of approximately 43 %, whereas the sawdust composite experienced a decrease in flexural strength of around 34 % after 1 year of exposure to drain water. The reduction in both tensile and flexural strength is caused by the swelling effect of the fiber, which distorts it, and micro-bacterial attack from drain water.

Flexural strength of non-weathered and weathered samples.

Fig. 7(a) and (b) illustrate that the jute fiber and sawdust fiber have several porosities, enabling them to easily absorb water when submerged in a drainage system. The high concentration of hemicellulose in these fibers contributes to their ability to absorb moisture [18,19]. Fig. 8 (a) illustrates the correlation between the water absorption rates of different samples and their types of fibers. Pure epoxy is hydrophobic, which means that it absorbs less moisture over 60 days in the drainage system. However, when 5 wt % sawdust fibers were added to the epoxy, the moisture absorption rate gradually increased over several days before stabilizing. On the other hand, the inclusion of 5 wt % jute fibers resulted in a higher degree of moisture absorption than sawdust fiber due to its lower lignin content [20].

(a) Moisture absorption rate and (b) thickness swelling rate for 60 days in the drainage system.

The poor absorption resistance of cellulosic material is caused by its polar groups that attract water molecules through hydrogen bonding [21,22]. This leads to moisture build-up in the fiber cell wall and fiber-matrix interface, causing changes in dimension, particularly thickness, in cellulose-based composites. The thickness swelling of natural fiber composites increases with immersion time until it reaches equilibrium, following a trend similar to water absorption behavior (as shown in Fig. 8b). Water can penetrate the inter-phase and lose cellular cellulose network structure over time, resulting in high water uptake. However, epoxy, as a hydrophobic component, has a very low water absorption rate and negligible thickness swelling rate. Fig. 8 (a, b) clearly show that the rate of water absorption and thickness swelling has remained constant for 48 days.

Biodegradation due to microbial attack

Microorganisms like bacteria and fungi can initiate biological deterioration in natural fiber-based epoxy composites. During the experiment, numerous microorganisms were found in the drainage system, and some were noticed by computer-based light microscopy as presented in Fig. 9a, b. Enzymes such as lipases and proteases can degrade the matrix material, as well as jute and sawdust fibers, in composites. Consequently, the structural and mechanical characteristics of lingo-cellulosic materials can be significantly degraded by these components [23].

Presence of microorganisms in the drain water at (a) initial period and (b) after 6 months.

According to Fig. 10, the weight of the weathered samples increased due to water absorption from the drainage system. However, after 90 days, both the jute and sawdust fiber composites underwent weight loss due to microbial attack, as the natural fiber was used as energy, forming CO2 as identified in the FTIR analysis. The weathered jute and sawdust fiber composite samples exhibited an additional peak at 1700 cm−1 wavenumber, reflecting the C=O bond, indicating CO2 production (Fig. 11a and b)

Compared to wood sawdust, jute fiber's lower lignin content rendered it more susceptible to microbial degradation. The exposure of jute fiber to drain water for a prolonged duration resulted in the oxidation and degradation of its cellulose component, leading to a reduction in its tensile and flexural strength. The higher concentration of lignin present in sawdust derived from wood has been found to enhance its resistance to weathering, thereby enhancing its stability and durability when exposed to the challenging conditions of a drainage system environment [20]. Nonetheless, the quality and strength of wood sawdust were subjected to 40 % degradation over 1 year due to weathering [[24], [25], [26]]. The moist conditions made the area vulnerable to bacterial degradation and bond weakening when subjected to drainage water. The degradation and weakening of composite strength containing sawdust can be attributed to this phenomenon.

SEM of the fractured surface

The images obtained from the SEM indicate that the fractured surfaces of the as-cast pure epoxy samples exhibited a comparatively smooth texture (Fig. 12a). SEM images reveal visual evidence of microbial-induced changes in epoxy resin, providing insights into the degradation process. The fractured surface of the weathered epoxy had revealed a rough appearance, with obvious cracks, pits, and other indications of degradation (Fig. 12b and c). Thus, the strength of the epoxy was decreased after weathering for 6 months and 1 year.

The SEM images of the fractured surface of the epoxy sample at (a) the as-cast condition; (b) the 6-month weathered condition and (c) the 1-year weathered condition.

The bacteria and fungi engaged in a collaborative effort to infiltrate the fiber cells, ultimately establishing their initial colonization of the parenchyma cells. They release digestive enzymes that degrade the polymer matrix and natural fibers. The degradation of fiber cell walls was caused by bacterial activity or incomplete hydrolysis [27,28]. The disintegration of fibers led to a fall in the bonding strength between the fibers and the epoxy matrix, thereby impeding the transfer of load and ultimately causing the failure of the composite material. When a composite material was submerged in drain water, microbes consumed and degraded organic components for energy. The enzymatic process weakens the material, causing the mechanical characteristics to degrade. As depicted in Fig. 4, Fig. 6, the mechanical strength of composite materials experienced a more pronounced decline after one year of exposure to weathering conditions in comparison to the decline observed after six months. The observed phenomenon can be accounted for by the gradual deterioration of the composites resulting from increased microbial activity [29,30].

Both the jute fibers and sawdust fibers seemed strong and undistorted for the as-cast composite samples (13a and 14a). However, after weathering in drain water, surface morphology and texture of the samples seemed to have changed, suggesting severe deterioration happened in the composites (Fig. 13, Fig. 14c). The image also indicated that there had been alterations to the structure of the samples, such as the disintegration of the individual fibers that constitute the composite producing numerous hollows and cracks by absorbing water and microbial attack. Cracks and cavities were found in the samples subjected to biodegradation of composite materials. These cracks and gaps have allowed bacteria to easily assault the material, resulting in its disintegration. Furthermore, Microbes can degrade individual fibers in the composite, indicating damage or fragmentation of the fibers. As a result, the material lost some of its physical characteristics, including its mechanical strength. Microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi, adhere to the surfaces of the jute and sawdust fibers. These fibers provide energy for both development and metabolism. Bacteria proliferate faster on jute fibers compared to sawdust fibers because jute has lower lignin content. Less lignin means easier microbial access and quicker breakdown. These microorganisms break down jute fibers quickly, producing a lot of nutrients. Biofilms, which are intricate colonies of microorganisms, are visible on both types of fiber according to SEM images (Fig. 13, Fig. 14c). Filaments and bacterial clusters attach to the fibers of the composite matrix. Jute fiber composites degrade faster than sawdust-based composites because of microbial activity. The efficient breakdown of jute fibers contributes to their ecologically friendly nature.

Conclusion

The main goal of this research was to investigate how natural fibers impact the biodegradation of epoxy resin composite materials. Extensive experimentation leads to the following findings.

•The study found that exposure to drain water resulted in a decrease in the strength of the pure epoxy by 15 %. Similarly, the 5 wt % waste jute fiber composite and sawdust fiber composite experienced reductions in strength by 57 % and 40 %, respectively due to biological oxidation from the presence of microorganisms like bacteria and fungi.

The study found that exposure to drain water resulted in a decrease in the strength of the pure epoxy by 15 %. Similarly, the 5 wt % waste jute fiber composite and sawdust fiber composite experienced reductions in strength by 57 % and 40 %, respectively due to biological oxidation from the presence of microorganisms like bacteria and fungi.

•Pure epoxy resin that had been subjected to weathering likewise manifested a 10 % reduction in flexural strength in comparison to the as-cast sample. After being exposed to drain water for a year, the flexural strength of the jute fiber composite decreased by about 43 %, whereas the flexural strength of the sawdust composite decreased by about 34 %.

Pure epoxy resin that had been subjected to weathering likewise manifested a 10 % reduction in flexural strength in comparison to the as-cast sample. After being exposed to drain water for a year, the flexural strength of the jute fiber composite decreased by about 43 %, whereas the flexural strength of the sawdust composite decreased by about 34 %.

•Enzymes produced by microorganisms facilitate the degradation of natural fibers of the composite materials. Microorganisms utilize the decomposed organic constituents as a means of generating energy, thereby resulting in the emission of carbon dioxide gas. This causes the mechanical characteristics to degrade and cause significant weight loss of the weathered composites.
