Directional dynamics in the entorhinal cortex of male mice driven by behavioral constraints
Authors: Ruojin Liu, Jun Hao, Xiang Zhang, Shidan Wen, Youran Yang, Haiqian Cai, Kai Gao, Emilio Kropff, Chenglin Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe medial entorhinal cortex of rodents contains a variety of functionally-defined cell types, including grid and head direction cells. While evidence indicates that functional types are not associated with clear molecular footprints, the factors that define them remain unclear. By comparing neural recordings in male mice exploring the same environment either freely or head-fixed to an autonomous-driving cart, we here show that, for a substantial portion of neurons, the head direction functional identity is dynamic, likely emerging through self-organization. Approximately half of free-foraging head direction cells reversibly lost their tuning during assisted navigation, while other neurons gained directional tuning, especially in environments enriched with sensory cues. This adaptive process is experience-dependent, progressively sharpening and stabilizing directional representations, while also integrating a spatial component. Our findings reveal a remarkable flexibility of entorhinal representations, demonstrating their capacity to reorganize and adapt based on how animals interact with their environment.Subject terms: Cortex, Spatial memory

Introduction

The medial entorhinal cortex (mEC) of rodents simultaneously encodes multiple variables related to spatial navigation through distinct, functionally-defined cell types, such as grid and head direction (HD) cells1–4. Although the mEC contains several anatomically defined cell classes with distinctive electrophysiological properties, functional and anatomical classifications do not map neatly onto one another5–7. Consequently, the factors determining the functional identity of neurons in the mEC—potentially involving hard-wiring or self-organization—remain an open question. Plasticity has so far shown to have an influence on tuning functions of several cell types, but not on their functional identity8–14.

Many highly interconnected brain areas represent direction through HD functionally-defined cells15. Vestibular, motor, and multimodal information are thought to be integrated by sub-cortical pathways, producing a robust representation sustained by attractor dynamics in the anterior-dorsal thalamic nucleus16. This pathway is required for entorhinal grid cells to express their hexagonal maps17. Recent evidence suggests the possibility of a differentiated cortical pathway that, while not independent from subcortical circuits, is more focused on sensory information18,19. This duality might be expressed in the mEC, where a distinction between theta modulated and non-modulated HD cells has been made, as only the latter modify their preferred direction in an environment as a consequence of sudden contextual changes20.

Passively transporting animals in a cart has proven useful for investigating the mechanisms underlying HD representations across the brain21–24. In the mEC, passive transport disrupts grid maps, suggesting that motor engagement is essential for their proper implementation25,26. Interestingly, passive transport has also been reported to enhance the directionality of some grid cells, but not conjunctive cells, while keeping HD cells mostly spared despite some mild loss in directional selectivity25. These contrasting effects may either represent specific deficits in distinct cell subpopulations or, alternatively, reflect a broader reconfiguration of entorhinal HD representations, a possibility that remains unaddressed. Here, we developed assisted navigation—similar to passive transport, but with tighter control over the two-dimensional trajectory, achieved by head-fixing mice to an autonomous-driving cart. Leveraging two-photon calcium imaging, this approach enabled us to comprehensively examine how behavioral constraints re-organize functionally-defined HD cells within the wider entorhinal network.

Results

Reconfiguration of directional codes

To study entorhinal representations during free and assisted navigation, we injected the adeno-associated virus AAV-hSyn-GCamMP6f in the entorhinal cortex of 12 mice. In each animal we implanted a prism that allowed calcium imaging through a two-photon miniaturized microscope (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1a). We selected a total of 11,818 cells (between 240 and 2245 per mouse) with high signal-to-noise ratio and extracted events through deconvolution and binarization of calcium activity raw traces using standard methods27 (Fig. 1c, d). A comparison of the autocorrelation decay of deconvolved calcium events and head direction confirmed that our neural recordings had sufficient temporal resolution to capture HD variations during both free and assisted navigation sessions (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b).

We first investigated the head-direction code during navigation in a 80 cm side square environment containing visual and odor cues. In this arena, mice had two sessions of free exploration separated by a session of assisted navigation, with 5 min resting in-between sessions (Fig. 1e). For assisted navigation, mice were head-fixed to an autonomous-driving cart that changed speed and direction at intervals between 100 and 1500 ms in a pseudo-random-walk fashion (Supplementary Movie 1). Ultrasound sensors were used to prevent the car from bumping against walls. Mean coverage of the arena (free: 91%, assisted: 93%) and mean speed (free: 9.0 cm s−1, assisted: 8.5 cm s−1) were comparable across conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2c–e).

For each of the 4528 cells recorded in this experiment (8 mice in 11 experimental days), we obtained head-direction (HD) tuning curves and characterized them by their mean vector length (MVL), angular information and stability (Fig. 1f). Previous work on passive transport inside a plexiglass cage pointed to a mild decay in selectivity of HD cells that did not alter their functional identity25, but did not address HD modulation in the general population, which we examined. Surprisingly, we found that during assisted navigation, average HD selectivity increased both in terms of mean vector length and HD information rate (Fig. 1f). This increase in HD selectivity was not due to sparse sporadic activation, since also stability, obtained as the Pearson correlation between HD maps corresponding to consequtive halves of the same session, increased during assisted navigation. Similar conclusions were obtained when using only one experimental day per animal (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), as well as when downsampling data to match either speed (Supplementary Fig. 3c–e) or angular velocity (Supplementary Fig. 3f–i) distributions of free and assisted sessions or considering only the center-most part of the arena (Supplementary Fig. 4). These results point to a reconfiguration of HD representations across navigation modalities, resulting in an overall increase rather than an impairment in directional selectivity in the mEC during assisted navigation.

To characterize neural activity across conditions, we next classified individual neurons as HD cells if their MVL was higher than the 99th percentile of the distribution obtained from a shuffling procedure pooling all data from the first free navigation session of each experiment (cutoff: 0.44; pool of 1000 shuffles for each cell; Supplementary Figs. 5–6a). We identified three groups of HD cells, which did not cluster anatomically (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). HDF cells passed the criterion during the first free navigation session (179 cells or 3.9%) but not during the assisted session (Fig. 1g, h). Conversely, HDA cells passed the criterion only during the assisted session, adding up to a higher proportion of the overall population (482 cells or 10.6%). A third group of cells, HDI (where I stands for invariant; see below), passed the classification criteria in both session (144 cells or 3.2%). This group, compared to HDA and HDF, exhibited lower mean acitivity levels, together with higher HD selectivity and stability across sessions (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 6b). The proportion of these cells was significantly larger than expected with a false positive probability of 0.01, given the classification criterion based on the 99th percentile of the shuffled distribution (binomial test p < 10−50 for all HD subpopulations). The tuning patterns of these cells remained stable across sessions and consecutive days (Supplementary Fig. 6c–e), further reinforcing the reliability of the directional tuning.

We next asked if HDI cells had the same preferred direction or remapped across conditions. For each cell, we quantified remapping by the Pearson correlation between the HD maps corresponding to different sessions (Fig. 1i). We found that HDI cells had the highest level of correlation across sessions, and it was not significantly different when comparing sessions with matched or non-matched navigation modalities. HDF cells, in contrast, had a significantly lower correlation when comparing sessions across modalities, indicative of remapping.

To investigate the reconfiguration of directional representations from a population coding perspective, we used a Bayesian decoder within a cross-validation framework (Fig. 1j). We trained and tested the decoder with data coming from either matched or non-matched navigation modalities. We observed that when datasets were matched, the same population of cells decoded direction better during assisted navigation than during free foraging (Fig. 1k). In addition, the decoded error was smaller for matched than non-matched datasets, further demonstrating the modulation of HD representations by navigation modality.

Put together, these results suggest that behavior constraints drive a reconfiguration of HD functional identity in the entorhinal cortex. Speciallized codes, represented by HDA and HDF cells, are navigation-modality specific, while HDI cells implement an invariant directional code. Overall stronger directional modulation during assisted navigation might reflect an adaptation to absent or unreliable self-motion inputs.

Population code reconfiguration

Given that HD cells in some brain areas are thought to follow an attractor dynamic16,28–31, we next asked whether the loss of directionality in HDF cells during assisted navigation could result from poor anchoring within an otherwise coherent population activity. If this were the case, temporal correlations between pairs of HDF cells with similar preferred direction should be preserved in the assisted session despite the loss in selectivity16,32,33. We divided free and assisted sessions into two halves and used one half to sort HD cells according to directional preference (first half of free session for HDF and HDI, second half of assisted session for HDA; Fig. 2a). We used the remaining data to assess the presence of a diagonal band in the cell-pair correlation matrix, quantifying it by calculating the ratio of in-diagonal to off-diagonal values. For HDF and HDA we found that this ratio was higher in session halves that matched the modality used for cell sorting, and was not different from 1 for the non-matched modality, indicating a complete loss of cell-pair correlation structure (Fig. 2b, c). In contrast, for HDI cells the diagonal was preserved: the ratio was higher than 1 and not different across sessions.

We also analyzed the normalized Frobenius distance between cell-pair correlation matrices corresponding to free vs. assisted sessions for each HD type, in order to quantify changes in the structure of temporal correlation across conditions. To quantify it, we used the mean of the squared difference between corresponding matrix elements, akin to the Frobenius distance (Fig. 2d). For HDF and HDA, the distance was comparable to that of the shuffled distribution, where cells were randomly sorted to construct each matrix. This suggests that the correlated activity between cell-pairs is primarily driven by either random or global fluctuations. In contrast, for HDI cells, the distance was significantly smaller than that of the shuffled distribution. These results point to the lack of attractor dynamic behavior specifically in HDF and HDA cells.

To address population activity through a different perspective, we decoded head direction from the activity of each HD population using a Bayesian decoder within a cross-validation framework (Fig. 2e, f). The decoder performed significantly better for HDF and HDA cells when trained and tested with data from matched modalities, consistent with our previous results and with their definitions, but equally well across modalities for HDI cells (Fig. 2e, f). Crucially, the confidence of the decoder, indicative of similar population activity patterns in training vs. testing data, followed similar trends, suggesting a reconfiguration of the population activity across sessions for HDF and HDA, but not HDI cells (Fig. 2f).

These results rule out the possibility that the reconfiguration of directional codes across modalities is due to a lack of anchoring to external cues, suggesting instead profound changes in population activity patterns due to behavioral constraints.

Assisted-navigation representations are sensory-focused

Since HD representatations are thought to rely on external sensory cues2,18,20 or internal proprioception signals34,35, we next asked if the activity of HDA cells is modulated by changes in these input sources. We first repeated the previous experiment (cue-rich) but this time using an environment deprived of most external sensory cues (cue-poor; Fig. 3a; 1808 cells from 11 experimental days in 4 mice). In contrast with our previous findings, mean directional selectivity and stability in the cue-poor environment were not higher in assisted than in free sessions (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Similarly, the proportion of cells classified as HD was not significantly different from the proportion observed during free exploration (Fig. 3c). Crucially, MVL, stability, HD information and the proportion of cells classified as HD were higher during assisted navigation for the cue-rich than for the cue-poor environment, highlighting the role played by sensory cues in the reconfiguration of directional codes (Fig. 3b). These differences between representations of cue-rich and cue-poor contexts were not due to prior experience, as similar results were observed when including only animals that were first exposed to the assisted navigation in each context (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). In line with this argument, a decoder trained with all recorded cells improved its performance during assisted navigation in the cue-rich but not the cue-poor environment (Fig. 3d). Such differences between cue-poor and cue-rich environments were much smaller, often not significant, during free foraging sessions (Fig. 3b).

We also asked if car speed modulated assisted directional representations in the cue-rich environment. We recorded activity during 4 consecutive sessions, each with a different speed regime, either random as in Fig. 1 or with an average speed set to the 30th (low), 50th (medium) or 70th (high) percentile of the free foraging speed distribution (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f; 6357 cells from 7 mice in 17 experimental days). We found no difference in mean directional selectivity, stability or proportion of cells classified as HD across speed sessions (Fig. 3e, f), which also applied to HDA and HDI considered separately (Supplementary Fig. 7 g). Furthermore, HDA cells had strikingly consistent maps across speed regimes, with levels of directional correlation across assisted sessions similar to those of HDI cells (Fig. 3e–h).

Put together, these results indicate that during assisted navigation, the directional code is strongly modulated by sensory cues.

Experience shapes assisted-navigation representations

Our results suggest a reversible dynamic in which HDA replace HDF cells for directional coding during assisted navigation. We next asked whether this new representation of a familiar environment is established instantaneously or gradually stabilized through learning. We divided the assisted session into five consecutive blocks (approximately 2 min each) and obtained HD maps for each block (Fig. 4a). We pooled data from cue-rich and speed experiments, all recorded in the cue-rich context, and divided them into two groups: sessions where assisted navigation was experienced for the first time by the mouse (naïve, 2233 cells from 7 mice in 7 experimental days) and sessions where it had prior exposure (trained, 3393 cells from 6 mice in 11 experimental days).

Using the Pearson correlation, and including all recorded cells, we first inspected the similarity between the HD maps for each block of the assisted session and the map corresponding to its last block. In naïve animals, but not in trained ones, we observed a significant increase in correlation across blocks, indicating an evolving representation (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). In contrast, no significant trends were found when correlating maps for each block vs. maps for the previous free session (Supplementary Fig. 8d). This observation suggests that, while assisted-navigation representations evolve along the naïve session, they are already independent of free representations as early as block 1. Average MVL also increased with time spent in the task during naïve sessions, but remained unchanged during trained sessions. When analyzing individual HD types, a significant improvement in directional map quality was observed exclusively for HDA cells during naïve sessions, but not during trained sessions or for HDI cells (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Although there were no naïve free-exploration sessions available for comparison, we also assessed these variables for HDF cells and found no significant changes across blocks (Supplementary Fig. 8f).

To understand these phenomena indicative of plasticity from the perspective of population coding, we decoded HD from population activity in each block, using the 4 remaining blocks as training data. We found that decoding error decreased as a function of time in the naïve session but not in subsequent ones (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 8e). Free exploration sessions did not exhibit a decrease in decoding error across blocks (Supplementary Fig. 8g).

These results suggest that plastic changes, occurring within minutes, shape assisted-navigation representations of direction into a new stable equilibrium. This newly established directional code is stored and remains readily accessible for use in subsequent experiences with assisted navigation.

Conjunctive codes for direction and space

Directional information, in some but not all brain areas where it is represented, is coded in conjunction with spatial information2,36–38. In conexts where correlations between behavioral variables such as position and direction are possible, the concept of unbiased information36 allows for disentangling the contribution of each variable by quantifying the amount of information that cannot be explained by the other. Adapting this to our data36, we asked if HDA cells represent space along with direction. For each cell, we generated a series of new calcium event trains using a binomial random generator. Each instance was obtained based solely on the cell’s original HD map and the animal’s current heading at each timestamp, but not its position or spatial map. We refer to it as a HD-locked shuffled instance of the cell’s event train, constructed to roughly preserve HD modulation and number of events. Unbiased spatial information was defined as the difference between the spatial information of the original map and the mean of the ones obtained for HD-locked shuffled instances (Fig. 5a). Conversely, we also produced spatially-locked shuffled instances and used them to define unbiased HD information. We found significant levels of unbiased spatial information for the pool of all recorded cells, as well as for all HD types considered separately (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 9-10a). In addition, we observed a positive correlation between unbiased spatial and directional information for HDA cells, pointing to a conjunction of these codes during assisted navigation (Fig. 5b). This phenomenon could not be attributed to the conjunctive coding of HD by grid cells. As expected from previous studies25,26, grid cells lost their spatial fields and exhibited low levels of unbiased spatial information during assisted sessions (Supplementary Fig. 11). These results point to entorhinal HD cells, and in particular HDA, as bearing a conjuntive code.

We next asked if spatial associations in HDA cells, as directional ones, were learned. We observed that unbiased spatial and directional information increased from the first to the second half of the assisted session during naïve but not trained sessions, for the pool of all recorded cells (Fig. 5c) and for HDA cells in particular (Supplementary Fig. 10f). In agreement with these findings, the decoding of spatial information improved along the assisted session for naïve but not trained animals (Fig. 5d).

We also asked if conjunctive coding was favored by the presence of sensory cues along the walls of the arena during assisted navigation. We observed that spatial fields of HD cells tended to peak close to the walls, and significantly more during cue-rich than cue-poor sessions (Fig. 5e). We next asked if the preferred direction of firing could also be influenced by the closest wall. To address this, we defined theta as the angle between a cell’s preferred direction and the direction from the center of its spatial field pointing toward the nearest wall (Fig. 5f). We found that the preferred direction of cells was significantly more skewed toward the closest wall in the cue-rich compared to the cue-poor environment (Fig. 5g). This wall-orientated preference, which could not be attributed to border-cell-like behavior (Supplementary Fig. 10a–e), was modulated by the distance between the field center and the wall: it was significant only in the cue-rich condition and for fields located within approximately 15 cm of the nearest wall. As fields farther away from the wall were included, the distribution of preferred directions became uniform. In contrast, in the cue-poor environment, preferred directions were uniformly distributed regardless of the distance to the nearest wall.

Our findings challenge the prevailing view that entorhinal HD cells represent a fixed cell class across behavioral conditions—a distinction that only becomes evident, however, through the partial reconfiguration of representations observed during assisted navigation. We show, instead, that cells can acquire a HD identity depending on the modality of navigation. While the population activity patterns of HDF and HDA cells are rapidly modified upon the first assisted experience, this reconfiguration involves cumulative, minute-by-minute changes that affect both the directional and spatial tuning of HD cells. As mice familiarize themselves with the new mode of navigation, these patterns gradually stabilize, bringing entorhinal directional maps to a new equilibrium. Importantly, this new equilibrium does not replace previously acquired representations of the environment but is added to a repertoire, remaining available for future use.

Discussion

Our main finding is that a substantial subset of entorhinal neurons can dynamically switch their functional role as head-direction (HD) cells in a familiar environment depending on the mode of navigation. Notably, other HD cells remain unaffected, arguing against the idea of impairment or complete remapping, suggesting instead an adaptation to behavioral constraints. Had we only observed a loss of function in HD cells, alternative explanations such as reduced attention could reasonably be hypothesized. However, the emergence of a stable new function—modulated by the availability of sensory cues—along with the overall increase in MVL and stability, is incompatible with such explanations. The functional role switch may result from the altered balance of sensory, motor, and vestibular inputs during assisted navigation, leading to new equilibria in the integration and coding of sensory and self-motion information39–42. The stability and reversibility of this switch, with the original HD representation restored when free navigation resumes, indicate that the entorhinal cortex has the capacity to store and select from multiple maps for the same environment based on the mode of navigation. The adaptability we observe points to a self-organizing process, for example aimed at harmonizing observations and predictions43, where the brain explores different solutions to maintain spatial and directional representations aligned with behavioral constraints. Together, these observations add to a growing body of evidence highlighting the flexibility of spatial and temporal representations in the entorhinal cortex9,10,44–46.

Different forms of navigation have been shown to modulate place cell responses in the hippocampus47–49. In the entorhinal cortex, studies of passive transport—a manipulation akin to assisted navigation but lacking head fixation—have reported an impairment in the coding of navigational variables by certain functional types, such as grid cells, while generally preserving the directional representation by HD cells25,26. In contrast, our manipulations result in approximately half of the HD cells recorded during free foraging sessions losing their tuning under assisted navigation. This discrepancy may stem from differences across species, as previous studies involved rats and ours utilizes mice. Additionally, a key feature in our manipulation is the head-fixed exploration of the open field during assisted navigation, which may disrupt feedback loops involving the vestibular and motor systems50. Numerous studies highlight differences in spatial coding between freely moving and fully head-fixed animals navigating virtual environments39,40,42. Evidence suggests that purely angular coordination between motor and vestibular inputs, with no forward coordination, is enough to maintain entorhinal HD coding intact in such setups41. A major distinction of our approach is that animals physically move in the environment while their head is fixed to the autonomous driving cart, providing more realistic vestibular and sensory feedback. Regardless of the technical factors that explain the discrepancies with previous results, the key insight of our findings lies in the emergence of a new population of HD cells during assisted navigation, compensating for the group that loses directional tuning.

Our results suggest that HD is encoded in the entorhinal cortex under two distinct regimes. On one hand, HDI cells exhibit an invariant directional representation that remains stable across different navigation modalities, consistent with attractor dynamics35,51–53. On the other hand, HDF and HDA cells are navigation-mode specific, with HDA cells showing a strong modulation by sensory enrichment. A somewhat similar distinction has been noted before between entorhinal HD cells affected vs. unaffected by contextual changes, although in that study contextual variability alone did not induce functional-role switches20. Similarly, a report of spontaneous remapping during virtual navigation has highlighted the entorhinal cortex’s capacity to store multiple spatial representations of a given virtual context42. However, in that case, remapping was not clearly linked to behavioral constraints, and neither functional type switches nor head direction coding were explicitly addressed, likely due to limitations inherent to navigation in one-dimensional virtual environments40,41. Our study, therefore, introduces the concept of an entorhinal HD code in which a fixed template coexists with a more flexible representation shaped by the dynamic ways in which animals interact with their environment.

HD cells across various brain regions are known to conjunctively encode spatial information, either in periodic patterns2 or more irregular, dispersed forms36. This mixture is also observed in the hippocampus, where place cells are modulated by goal locations or reference points37,38,54, and perhaps in the spatial-view responses observed in primates55. Leveraging the creation of new representations in a familiar environment during assisted navigation, our findings demonstrate that learning plays a crucial role in shaping the spatial tuning curves of entorhinal HD cells. Specifically, we found that HDA cells develop spatio-directional fields tuned to the walls of sensory-enriched environments, but show diminished tuning in sensory-deprived environments, highlighting the role of plasticity in associating cell activity with the salience of sensory cues.

Entorhinal representations are traditionally thought to arise from either hard-wired mechanisms or principles of self-organization. Recent evidence, for instance, suggests that substantial components of entorhinal56 or anterior-dorsal thalamic57 attractor dynamics are present before animals accumulate significant experience with the world, highlighting the critical role of processes that function as if they were innate or hard-wired during early development. Our findings, however, offer evidence of a complementary dynamic: the remarkable flexibility of entorhinal cells to adapt through learning, reshaping their functional identity in response to a shifting balance between sensory and self-motion inputs. Although we cannot exclude the possibility that these changes originate upstream, a self-organizing process provides the most parsimonious explanation, given the observation that cells improve their tuning during their first experience in assisted navigation. Together, hard-wired templates and self-organizing mechanisms may serve as complementary driving forces in entorhinal processing, providing a flexible framework capable of meeting the challenges posed by multimodal inputs and diverse forms of interaction with the external world.

Methods

Animals

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee at Peking University. Adult male C57BL/6 N mice (n = 12) were used for experiments, housed under 20 °C, 50% humidity in a 12 h light/dark cycle individually. Male mice were used because they exhibited more consistent running behavior in the openfield. Their total food intake (2–4 g of food per day) was controlled and monitored to maintain 90% of free-feeding body weight. Behavioral experiments were performed during the dark phase. All data for all mice were included for analysis.

Virus injection and prism implantation

All surgeries were performed on isoflurane (airflow: 0.8–1.0 L/min, 0.5–3% isoflurane, adjusted according to physiological condition) anaesthetized animals head fixed on a stereotaxic frame (KOPF). The hair was trimmed from the head, followed by the cleaning of the incision area with alcohol and betadine. After cleaning tissues and blood on the skull, a small square craniotomy was made with a thin drill over the right MEC. The dura was then removed by a blunt needle to expose the brain surface. For each mouse, two injections of total 0.8 μL of AAV-hSyn-GCaMP6f (titer: >2 × 1013 vg/mL) were injected into the right MEC at a rate of 30 nL/min with a glass micropipette (typical two injection sites: +3.1 to +3.7 mm ML, sinus + 0.3 mm AP, −1.8 to −1.4 mm and −1.5 to −1.3 mm DV), controlled by a Nanoliter 2020 Microinjection Pump (World Precision Instruments). The pipette was left in place for 10 min before being slowly withdrawn to allow virus diffusion. Next, a 3.5 × 2.5 mm2 prism (Grintech) was inserted between cerebellum and cerebrum with the flat surface targeted on dorsoventral surface of MEC. Craniotomy was then covered with Kwik-Sil (World Precision Instruments) and tissue adhesive glue (3 M) to protect and bond the skull with the scalp incision area. A headbar (Transcend Vivoscope) was added on the top, with the exposed skull as well as the gaps between the skull and the headbar filled with dental cement (Super bond). Animals were then placed on a heating pad until waking up and housed individually while being treated with cephalosporin for 1 week.

Miniaturized 2-photon microscope calcium imaging

Mice were allowed to recover for three weeks after surgery and then restrained with an adapter. Using a wide-field single-photon microscope, a field of view (FOV) was determined in which the largest number of neurons appeared clearly, and a baseplate was then attached to the headbar to fix the microscope. During calcium imaging, the calcium signals were collected with the miniatured 2-photon microscope (LFMTPM V2.0.1), and controlled by the GINKGO-MTPM software (Transcend Vivoscope Biotech Co., Ltd, China). Two-photon excitation of GCaMP6 was achieved using a laser (TVS-FL-01, Transcend Vivoscope Biotech Co., Ltd China) at 920 nm with a typical laser intensity of 70 mW. Images were acquired at 9.5 Hz at a resolution of 600 × 512 pixels corresponding to a field of view of 534 × 455 μm. The imaging data was recorded synchronously with the behavioral data using the GINKGO-MTPM software. After recording, behavioral data were aligned with the imaging clock and downsampled to the imaging frame rate (9.5 Hz) by linear interpolation.

Some animals were re-used after a month, which explains the discrepancy between number of mice and experimental days in some of our results. In those cases, the baseplate was re-attached to the headbar to obtain a better FOV. The new imaged neurons were added to the statistics, considering the two sessions with different FOV as independent from each other. While this procedure could potentially result in some bias, Supplementary Fig. 3a, b shows that our main observations hold robustly if only one experimental day per mouse is taken into account.

Histology

After finishing experiments, mice were first anaesthetized with a 0.6 mL injection of 2.5% tribromoethanol and then fixed on the perfusion table. An incision was made along the midline of the abdomen to the chest with dissecting scissors, and a perfusion needle was inserted into the left ventricle once an incision was made on the right atrium. Mice were then transcardially perfused with 40 mL saline, followed by 20 mL 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). After perfusion, the brain tissue was carefully removed, post-fixed in 4% PFA for more than 24 h at 4 °C, and then dehydrated in 30% sucrose until the brain sank. Next, the brain tissue was removed from the sucrose and mounted on a metal platform with OCT Compound (4583, Sakura, Tokyo, Japan). After freezing in the cryostat, the tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 30-μm.

Sections were first rinsed with PBS two times (300 μL each slide, 5 min each time), incubated with blocking solution (10% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by primary antibody (chicken anti-GFP, Invitrogen, Cat #A10262; rabbit anti-NeuN, Abcam, Cat #ab_177487) in diluted (1:1000) blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. After washing in PBS three times (300 μL each slide, 5 min each time), sections were then incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, Invitrogen, Cat #A11039; Alexa Fluor 568, Abcam, Cat #ab_175471) and DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) in diluted (1:1000) blocking solution for 2–4 h at room temperature. After rinsing with PBS two times (300 μL each slide, 5 min each time), sections were mounted on glass slides with Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech) and coverslipped. Imaging was processed using either Zeiss Axio Scan Z1 or Olympus Virtual Slide System to confirm the position of prism implantation and the expression of virus.

Free exploration task

After complete recovery from the surgery, mice were habituated in a 40 cm high and 80 cm side square arena for more than 3 days (at least 15 min per day). The walls and the floor of the arena were built using plastic. In the cue-poor environment, three cue cards (30 cm W × 21 cm H) with different symbols were placed in the centers of three walls, with the bottom edge 8 cm above the floor. In the cue-rich environment, all walls were further covered with gratings and an extra odor cue (pure ethyl acetate diluted to 5% v/v in mineral oil) was placed in the center of the fourth wall. Neural activity was recorded as the animal freely explored in the arena, searching for butter cookies (PAUL KEI) that were thrown into the arena one at 10–15 s intervals. Mouse behavior was monitored at 25 frames per second (fps) with an infra-red camera centered above the arena. The arena was cleaned with dust collector and 75% ethanol between sessions.

Assisted navigation task

Mice were restrained head-fixed to a custom-made autonomous-driving car and moved around the same arena. The car had one motor (WHEELTE) on each side and was controlled by an Arduino board powered by six rechargeable batteries (Delipow, 2700 mA). The two motors operated in the same direction during forward movements and in opposite directions during turning. To manipulate the car moving at low speeds, motors with high reduction ratio were used. To mimic the random foraging of mice, the car was set to change its moving direction after an interval of time drawn from a uniform distribution between 100 and 1500 ms. Unless a wall was encountered, there was a 92.5% chance of proceeding forward, a 7% chance of turning left or right, and a 0.5% chance of stopping. Once the choice was made, the car would execute the action until the next decision point. During the forward mode, the ultrasound sensors in front of the car detected the distance from the wall every 100 ms. The car would move backwards and make a turn away from the wall if the distance was smaller than 5 cm. In the default, random speed regime, the moving speed of the car changed at every decision point. The new speed was obtained from a pseudo-random uniform distribution between 2 and 30 cm s−1.

Speed modulation task

Speed modulation was implemented by changing the parameters of the Arduino code. The moving speed of mice during free exploration in previous experiments was collected, and its 30th, 50th, and 70th percentile were set to the car as a constant value for low, medium and high-speed conditions (see Supplementary Fig. 7e, f).

Pre-processing of 2-photon calcium signal

After recording, Ca2+ imaging data were first analyzed offline using Suite2P58 (https://github.com/MouseLand/suite2p) for motion correction, identification, and extraction of spatial footprints as well as the activity traces of region of interest (ROI). The outputs from Suite2P were further analyzed with custom-written programs in Matlab (R2020a, The MathWorks, Natick, MA) for calculating Ca2+ signals for each specified ROI as the relative fluorescence changes (ΔF/F)59. ΔF/F was then deconvolved using OASIS60 (https:// github.com/zhoupc/OASIS_matlab). To extract calcium transients from noise such as electrical and motion artifacts, significant transients were identified as periods in which ΔF/F was two standard deviations above the baseline fluorescence for more than 0.75 s, as established in previous studies61,62. For each ROI, a fluorescence change was identified as a true calcium event only during significant transients when its amplitude exceeded one standard deviation above the baseline. To count the cells recorded in each animal, CellReg63 (https://github.com/zivlab/CellReg) was applied to identify the same cells across days within each re-attachment.

Tracking of behavioral data

The raw behavioral data recorded by the camera was analyzed using DeepLabCut64 (https://github.com/DeepLabCut/DeepLabCut). For each frame, four body parts (free exploration: nose, neck, left and right ear; assisted navigation: nose, neck, left and right side of the headbar) were extracted to yield the position of the animal. The DLC model was trained from 50 manually labeled datasets with resnet_50 for 100,000 iterations. The identified positions located inside the arena were kept and further smoothed with a 15-point mean filter. Head direction (HD) was determined by the relative position of the animal’s neck and nose.

Spatial and directional rate maps

For each session, cells with more than 50 calcium events were extracted, and rate maps were generated to evaluate their calcium activity normalized by occupancy (sessions where a cell had less then 50 calcium events were not included in the analyses). The deconvolved calcium activity was first sorted into different bins (bin size: 3 × 3 cm2 for spatial maps and 3° for directional maps) according to the behavioral data, and then divided by the time spent in each bin. The resulting rate was further smoothed either using a 2D Gaussian kernel with a standard deviation of 6 cm for spatial maps or a 30° mean window filter for directional maps. For spatial rate maps, the firing fields were determined as the contiguous periods exceeding 60th percentile of the field’s peak rate, with the peak rate higher than 0.5 Hz. All data with running speeds lower than 2 cm per second were excluded in all calculations to rule out the effects of different behavioral states.

Analysis of head direction cells

The length of mean resultant vector (mean vector length, MVL) was calculated for each cell to evaluate HD selectivity. MVL was obtained by averaging the vectors corresponding to each bin of the directional rate map using the circular statistic toolbox in Matlab65. Shuffling was performed 1000 times for each cell. For a single shuffling instance, a shuffled directional map was obtained by time-shifting the deconvolved calcium activity of the cell along the animal’s path wrapping the end of the session to the beginning, with a 30 s exclusion window toward the future and the past. Shuffled MVL values were calculated and pooled for all cells in the first free foraging session to obtain the 99th percentile of the distribution, which was set as a criterion to classify cells as HD in all sessions. In all the experiments, cells that passed the criterion during the first free exploration session but not during the assisted session were classified as HDF. Conversely, cells that passed the criterion only during assisted sessions but not during any of the free exploration sessions were identified as HDA. Finally, cells that passed the criterion in both conditions were classified as HDI.

Analysis of spatial cells

Spatial information was calculated as HD information but using the spatial map and spatial bins. Shuffling was performed similarly, and a cell was identified as a spatial cell if its spatial information exceeded the 99th percentile of the spatial information for the pooled shuffled data. Spatial stability was calculated as the Pearson correlation of the spatial maps obtained for the two consecutive halves of a session.

The degree of spatial periodicity (‘grid score’) was obtained as the difference between the lowest correlation at 60° and 120° and the highest correlation at 30°, 90° and 150°. Shuffling was performed for each cell with 1000 permutations. For each cell, the control spatial map was obtained by time-shifting the deconvolved calcium activity of the cell along the animal’s path by a random number of frames taken from a uniform distribution including the whole session, but excluding 30 s into the future or the past, to ensure the complete loss of correlation between neural activity and behavior. A cell was identified as a grid cell if its grid score from the experimental data exceeded the 99th percentile of pooled shuffled data.

Calculation of unbiased information

Unbiased information was used to remove the part of spatial information strictly due to directional responses and vice-versa36,67,68. For a given cell, a spatially-locked shuffled instance was defined by a new calcium event train generated through a binomial random process. For every timestamp, this random process had a mean given by the value of the spatial map in the bin where the animal stood. Similarly, a HD-locked shuffled instance used the HD map to generate a new event train. Spatial and HD information were calculated for each of the 100 shuffles of each type per cell. The unbiased spatial information for a cell was defined as the difference between its actual spatial information and the mean of the shuffled spatial information. The unbiased HD information was defined as the difference between its actual HD information and the mean of the shuffled HD information.

Similarly, and to allow a comparison of variables with the same methodology, the unbiased grid score, unbiased border score and unbiased spatial information were calculated as the difference between the observed variable and the average over 100 spatial shuffles for each cell.

Calculation of directionality

For a collection of spatial bins (typically those inside the center square vs close to the walls), the behavioral-MVL for each bin was obtained similarly to the MVL but using the distribution of head directions instead of the rate map. The directionality of each bin was defined as the average behavioral-MVL across sessions.

Downsampling of behavioral data

To rule out the possibility that the reconfiguration across conditions was caused by differences in the distribution of speed during free and assisted session, behavioral data in each condition was downsampled to achieve similar moving speeds. For each recording, the behavioral data during assisted navigation was first downsampled in speed bins where it occurred in excess, to match the speed distribution of free sessions on the same day. The behavioral data recorded in free exploration was then downsampled where it occurred in excess, to match exactly the speed distribution that resulted from the first round of downsampling. Spatial and directional rate maps were then obtained from downsampled data.

Calculation of best rotated angle

To evaluate whether a HDI cell remapped across conditions, the best rotated angle was defined as the amount of rotation (in multiples of 3°) of the map corresponding to the first session required to maximize its Pearson correlation with the map corresponding to the second session.

Correlation between cell pairs

To study the structure of cell-pair activity correlations we first divided free and assisted sessions into two consecutive halves. To study HDF and HDI, the first half of the free session was used to sort cells according to their preferred direction. The correlation matrix was then calculated for each half of each session, where element (i,j) had the Pearson correlation between the corresponding event train of cells i and j. The presence of a diagonal in the correlation matrix, indicative of similar activity between similarly sorted cells, was evaluated for all sessions not used for sorting. HDA cells were studied similarly but using the second half of the assisted session to sort cells. In-diagonal elements were defined as the 10% closest to the diagonal, but excluding it, while off-diagonal elements were defined as the 10% furthest apart from the diagonal. For each HD type, only sessions containing more than 10 cells of that type were included in the analysis.

Shuffling instances of the correlation matrix were obtained by randomly sorting the cells and calculating the correlation between corresponding cell pairs.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. Sample sizes were chosen based on previous publications and prior experience in our laboratory. The study contains data from 12 mice. All collected data were analyzed, with inclusion critferia for specific analyses described in Methods. Cells with fewer than 50 events were excluded. Sessions containing fewer than 5 or 10 recorded cells were excluded from decoding and temporal correlation analyses, respectively, to minimize noise and improve reliability.

Animals were randomly allocated into each experiment group. Experimenters were not blinded to group allocation during data collection. However, neural activity during behavioral tasks could only be observed after automatic pre-processing. Experimenters were blinded to experimental conditions during data analysis. Each animal in the study is an independent experimental replicate performed separately. Results were consistent across animals.

Data analysis were conducted using MATLAB (R2022a), Python (3.8) and GraphPad Prism 9. Figures were generated using MATLAB, Python and GraphPad Prism 9 and arranged in Adobe Illustrator 2023. Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB, Python and GraphPad Prism 9.
