Quantitative comparison of explainable AI methods for interpreting deep learning–based classification of 3D gait kinematics
Authors: Zhengyang Lan, Mathieu Lempereur, Abdeldjalil Aïssa-El-Bey, Sylvain Brochard, François Rousseau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gait disorders can be caused by various reasons including cerebral palsy and neuromuscular diseases. 3D clinical gait analysis (3DGA) serves as a valuable clinical tool to assess gait abnormalities. Our previous research introduced a diagnostic tool that combines deep learning (DL) with 3DGA to evaluate childhood gait disorders. It achieved a promising diagnostic accuracy ranging from 0.77 to 0.99 across different pathologies. However, the lack of transparency limits their adoption. This research seeks to unveil the critical features that drive these models’ diagnoses, improving interpretability and building trust in their decision-making process. Four different explaining artificial intelligence (XAI) methods were applied: LIME, DeepLift, Integrated Gradients, and sequential feature selection. These methods were used on various network architectures applied to three separate datasets involving different gait disorders. The results show that the features highlighted by XAI methods are relevant and reliable for diagnostic purposes. Moreover, quantitative analysis indicated that Integrated Gradients is the most appropriate XAI method in this case. Further experiments demonstrate that using parts of the critical features can achieve better accuracy than using all of the features. In conclusion, this research identified the diagnostic basis of DL models through XAI methods, enhanced diagnostic accuracy by focusing on critical features, and improved clinicians’ understanding and trust in the DL diagnostic tool.

Introduction

Gait abnormalities are common in children with cerebral palsy (CP)1, Parkinson’s disease2 or neuromuscular diseases3. Dagnosis is typically based on disease history, clinical examination and medical imaging. In clinical practice, instrumented 3D gait analysis (3DGA)4,5 is often used due to its ability to provide a comprehensive assessment of gait data by computing biomechanical parameters of marker-based motion capture systems6. These parameters can include spatio-temporal metrics, kinematic, kinetic and electromyography data. They can be used to assess the effects of treatments4 and to assist in surgical decision making7–9. Additionally, 3DGA has the potential to distinguish between different pathologies, indicating that gait patterns contain critical information for initial diagnosis10.

The combination of 3DGA with deep learning enables the differentiation between cerebral palsy, typically developing children, and other pathologies11, as well as the detection of stroke onset11.

While data-driven models hold great promise for diagnosing and analysing data without relying solely on clinical expertise, their lack of transparency poses a significant challenge. Often referred to as ’black boxes’12, these models make predictions without revealing the reasoning behind them. This makes it difficult for healthcare professionals to understand and justify the model’s recommendations, hindering trust and potentially leading to biased or unfair outcomes, as observed with other Artificial intelligence (AI) systems13. These limitations constraint the current applications of deep learning, particularly in the sensitive field of medicine.

Related works

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has gained significant attention in recent years as a promising approach to address these limitations of opaque deep learning models, particularly in fields where interpretability and trust are critical, such as healthcare and biomechanics. In the context of 3DGA, several XAI methods have been successfully applied to uncover the decision-making process14.

For example, Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP)15 has been used to provide explanations for the classification of foot conditions based on kinematic data, offering insights into which biomechanical features drive model predictions16 or to interpret deep learning models classifying Parkinson’s disease severity from ground reaction force data17. Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)18 has been applied to gait classification in patients after total hip arthroplasty helping to interpret the contributions of input features to individual predictions19. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)20 has been used to clarify distinct gait patterns in children with different types of CP compared to typically developing (TD) individuals by highlighting relevant regions in combined ground reaction forces and kinematic data21. Similarly SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)22 has been employed to identify gait biomechanical parameters associated with anterior cruciate ligament injury, revealing key features that contribute to model decisions in musculoskeletal injury studies23.

The above research tends to show that XAI methods could provide new insights for deep learning-based gait analysis.

Objectives

A recent study by Lan et al.11 explored the development of a diagnostic tool using 3D gait kinematics data and deep learning models. The work incorporated data from 22 joint angles and investigated three different deep learning architectures: ResNet24,25, LSTM26, and InceptionTime27. Despite the promising diagnostic performance reported in our previous work11, the clinical applicability of deep learning models in gait analysis is limited by their lack of interpretability. In clinical settings, diagnostic tools must be accurate and transparent, enabling clinicians to understand and justify their decisions. Therefore, improving the interpretability of deep learning-based gait classification models is a necessary step towards their clinical integration.

The objective of this study is not to propose new deep learning architectures or claim general conclusions about the applicability of XAI to all gait datasets. Instead, this study specifically aims to investigate how different XAI methods behave when applied to multivariate 3D gait kinematics time series used for neurological diagnosis.

More precisely, this study addresses the following research questions: Which joint angles and kinematic patterns drive the diagnostic decisions of previously validated deep learning gait classification models?Do different XAI methods provide consistent explanations in this biomechanical time series context?Can models restricted to XAI-identified critical features maintain or improve diagnostic performance?Among several state-of-the-art XAI methods, which one provides the most robust and faithful explanations for 3D gait kinematics?

Gait dataset

The 3D gait data were collected from 2006 to 2022 at the motion analysis laboratory of CHU Brest (Brest University Hospital), France. Gait cycles were captured using infrared cameras (7 cameras: 2006-2007; 9 cameras: 2007-2016; 15 cameras: 2016-2022) to track markers placed according to Kadaba et al.6. The data from the tracked markers were filtered using a second-order, zero-lag Butterworth low-pass filter with a 6 Hz cut-off. Lower limb kinematics were computed using the PlugInGait biomechanical model from Vicon Nexus (version 2.12.1, Vicon Motion Systems Ltd., Oxford, UK) according to the conventional gait model and were time normalized to the gait cycle28..

Kinematics data (22 x 101 matrix: 11 joint angles per limb) included pelvic tilt, obliquity, rotation; hip flexion/extension (F/E), abduction/adduction (Ab/Ad), internal/external rotation (I/E Rot); knee F/E, Ab/Ad, I/E Rot; ankle F/E; and foot progression. In Lan et al.11, the performance of six datasets based on different pathologies were evaluated. In this study, the three most representative datasets were chosen and investigated.

The first dataset is a binary classification dataset, comparing unilateral cerebral palsy (CPu) to TD (denoted as TDvsCPu). The second dataset contrasts bilateral CP (CPb) with toe walkers29 (denoted as ToeWalkervsCPb) due to their similar gait patterns. Finally, the third dataset is a multiclass dataset that includes CPb, neuromuscular (NM) disease, and TD individuals (denoted as TDvsNMvsCPb). These datasets were selected considering the significant variation in gait patterns among the three classes.

For children with CPu, CPb, NM, and Toe Walker, the Gait Profile Score (GPS)30 was computed for both sides. The side with the higher GPS was designated as the “more affected side” and the side with the lower GPS was considered as the “less affected side”. The two sides were concatenated in the order of “more affected side” and “less affected side”.

For TD children, the order of the concatenation was a random arrangement of the right side and the left side. The number of children and gait cycles is detailed in Table 1.

Methods

Deep learning model

Three deep learning models, ResNet, LSTM, and InceptionTime, were evaluated for their classification performance on the three separate datasets. The data were preprocessed by splitting each dataset into training (60%), validation (10%), and test (30%) sets. The models were trained using the Adam optimizer to minimize categorical cross-entropy loss with early stopping and learning rate reduction applied (see Lan et al.11 for more details). To address data imbalance and prevent the model from favoring the majority class, a weighted loss function was used32. The weights were inversely proportional to the number of cycles per class for each dataset. The performance of the trained models is shown in Table 211.

Sequential feature selection

Feature selection33 is a perturbation-based and model-agnostic XAI method that outputs a global explanation of the model. Sequential feature selection is a greedy algorithm used to select important features from prediction of models.

Forward sequential feature works by iteratively training models with the information of each feature individually, starting with the information one feature and replace all other features with zeros, and choosing the feature that yields the best accuracy as the first important feature. Once the first important feature is selected, it is paired with the remaining features one by one and the models are trained again. The best combination is found by evaluating the accuracy, and the second important feature is obtained. This process is repeated until the order of all the features is obtained. The order of the important features found by this process is interpreted as the ranking of importance of each feature for the prediction. The change rate of the accuracy is a quantitative indicator of each newly added feature’s importance.

Similarly, backward sequential feature selection starts with all features and removes the information of them one by one by replace them with zeros one by one. The feature whose removal causes the greatest drop in accuracy is deemed most important. This process is repeated until all features are ranked by importance. The change in accuracy quantifies the importance of each removed feature.

DeepLift

DeepLIFT34 is an XAI method designed for deep learning based on backpropagation. It produces a local explanation of each prediction. It computes the contribution of each input feature to the final output by comparing the neuron activation value to a reference value (usually zero or the mean of the training dataset). The main idea is to quantify the effect of input features on the output by analyzing the amount of neuron change (relative to the reference value) in the neural network.

A simplified expression of the main idea is shown in Equation 2:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta f(y)=\sum _{i=1}^n \Delta y_{i} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta f(y)$$\end{document} is the amount of variation in the output, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{i}$$\end{document} is the amount of variation in each feature, and n is the total number of features. It shows that the main property of DeepLift is that the change in prediction is equal to the sum of the changes in marginal contributions resulting from changes in input features.

For the implementation it is organized in three steps. The first step is the selection of the reference value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{ref}$$\end{document}, which is usually the zero input or the mean value of the training dataset. This reference value is used to compute the activation value of the neuron for this input.

The second step is to compute the activation value. For a deep learning model, it computes the in-all-intermediate-layer activation values and output values for a given input input sample and its reference value by forward propagation.

The third step is to compute the differences by backpropagation. Starting from the output layer, it computes the difference in activation values for each neuron for the input of selected sample and reference sample, and attributes these differences to the neurons in the previous layer in a linear combination. Then repeat this process through backpropagation up to the inputs to get the final importance score for each input feature.

Evaluation of XAI methods

To evaluate the performance of the XAI method used in this study, two widely-used objective evaluation metrics were evaluated36,37: fidelity38–40 and robustness41,42. Fidelity measures how well the explanation reflects the true decision-making process of the model, while robustness assesses the stability of explanations under slight perturbations of the input. These two criteria allow us to quantitatively assess the reliability and consistency of the explanations provided. In this research, one robustness-based evaluation method and three fidelity-based evaluation methods are used to assess which XAI method performs best.

For the robustness-based approach, considering that the gait cycle signals used in this study have been preprocessed and that adding noise would corrupt the signals, the evaluation is based on the standard deviation of the XAI results computed with similar inputs. By default, we mask a fixed fraction of features (half of the ranked features in our main experiments), although other fractions can be used. Under a faithful ranking, masking important features should increase the loss, yielding a large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\Delta \ell }_{\textrm{imp}}$$\end{document}, whereas masking unimportant features should have a smaller effect, yielding a small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\Delta \ell }_{\textrm{unimp}}$$\end{document}. As an overall summary, we also report the gap \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\Delta \ell }_{\textrm{imp}}-\overline{\Delta \ell }_{\textrm{unimp}}$$\end{document}, where larger values indicate better separation between important and unimportant features. When aggregating results across datasets and model architectures, we compute the Overall statistics by concatenating per-sample values (i.e., pooling samples) before computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M \pm SD$$\end{document}.

To assess whether the observed fidelity differences are statistically reliable, we conducted two complementary statistical analyses. First, each XAI method was compared against a random-masking baseline (masking the same number of features) using one-sided paired tests, to verify that masking top-ranked features induces larger loss increases than random masking, while masking bottom-ranked features leads to smaller loss changes. Second, pairwise two-sided paired tests were conducted between XAI methods to quantify method-to-method differences. All test results (including t statistics, degrees of freedom, p values, and effect sizes) are reported in Supplementary Information 3.

Experimental process

In practice, the results of LIME, DeepLift, and Integrated Gradients were computed individually for each gait cycle in the test set. The results were subsequently normalized to the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-1, 1]$$\end{document}, and an average was computed for each class. Consequently, the absolute values of the averaged values for each class were summed in order to obtain a global interpretation of each joint angle. To better compare the impact of different joint angles, the results were further averaged over time, resulting in relevant values for 22 joint angles.

For feature selection, the full set of features used consisted of 22 3D joint angles and the absent features were replaced with zeros. In practice, all training were performed ten times, and the best features were selected based on average test accuracy to mitigate the effects of randomness.

In the case of LIME applied to the gait kinematic data and models, the shape of the kinematic data is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x\in \mathbb {R}^{22 \times 101}$$\end{document}, and it is implemented using raw data, resulting in an interpretable representation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z^{\prime }\in \mathbb {R}^{22 \times 101}$$\end{document}. After a grid search over LIME hyperparameters (see Supplementary Information 1), the width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} used is set to 100. Furthermore, the absent features (data points of joint angles) are replaced with zeros for each joint angle of each gait cycle. The number of sampled gait cycles for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z^{\prime }$$\end{document} is 5000. The non-zero weights in the Lasso model are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega \in \mathbb {R}^{22 \times 101}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _i$$\end{document} gives the importance of feature i.

For the evaluation of the XAI method, robustness and fidelity are evaluated on the test set. For robustness, the cycles of the same class in the test set are considered as similar inputs and the mean intra-class standard deviation of the outputs of the XAI method is computed for each class, and then the final result is obtained by averaging across all classes and models. For the computation of fidelity, the top half of the ranked features are considered as relevant features and the bottom half are considered as irrelevant features. Removal of features is done by using zero substitution, and the final result is also based on averaging the test set as input followed by averaging the results across different models.

Consistent with the DL-based diagnostic tool that inspired this study, the XAI framework was implemented in Python (version 3.9.11) using PyTorch Lightning (version 1.6.0)44 and executed on an Ubuntu workstation with an AMD Ryzen processor, 64 GB of RAM, and a NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU.

The XAI methods LIME, DeepLift, and Integrated Gradients were implemented using Captum, a Python library for model interpretability and understanding (version 0.6.0)45. The sequential feature selection relies on home-made implementation. The codes for our experiments are available at https://github.com/LANZhengyang/XAI_Gait_DeepLearning_Diagnostic_Tool

Results

Gradient-based XAI methods generate a relevance score for each feature, indicating its contribution to the prediction. The ranking of these relevance scores effectively demonstrates the criticality of each feature. In contrast, feature selection methods assess the impact of adding or removing each feature on the model’s accuracy. Consequently, the criticality of features can be inferred from the ranked changes in accuracy observed when each feature is used for prediction.

Discussion

Analysis of the results of XAI methods

The XAI methods consistently highlighted Knee F/E, Hip F/E, and Ankle F/E as the most critical angles across the various pathologies analyzed. This consistency indicates that the selected XAI methods cross-validated the reliability of the critical angles. Similarly, these three joint angles have been identified as important gait deviations in CP in Armand et al.46. In addition, Bell et al.47 demonstrated a decrease in peak flexion of these three joint angles over time compared to non-intervened individuals. Furthermore, in the Slijepcevic et al.21, similarly reported significant relevance of these three joint angles using different deep learning models and XAI methods or classifying orthopaedic gait disorders.

However, the results obtained using different XAI methods still showed some differences in terms of feature relevance rankings. The quantitative analysis based on robustness and fidelity indicated that Integrated Gradients was the most suitable method in this study. To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no studies comparing multiple XAI methods applied to gait analysis, and most existing works rely on a single XAI method to generate explanations16,21,48. Our work addresses this gap in the literature. Nevertheless, Integrated Gradients has also been recognized as a faithful and reliable XAI method in other research contexts where different XAI methods have been compared. For example, Miró-Nicolau et al.49 compared 16 XAI methods on TXUXI image datasets50 using fidelity metrics, showing Integrated Gradients and other gradient-based methods outperformed, and perturbation-based methods underperformed. In addition, in the evaluation based on time series data51, six XAI methods were compared and Integrated Gradients was identified as the second best XAI method while the top-ranked method (Shapley) was excluded due to its high computational cost. The alignment of these findings with previous studies supports the validity of the present research.

Finally, when inconsistencies were observed between the two sides of the same joint angle, the results generally showed that the more affected side has higher relevance. Our analysis suggests that this is because the information it contains for diagnosis is more obvious. Due to the greedy nature of gradient-based training and the interaction between contralateral joint angles (as described in the study of Kloter et al.52), deep learning models tend to assign greater weight to the more affected side, which contains more diagnostically useful information. Similar findings were reported by Slijepcevic et al.48 who also observed that the relevance of the affected side was more pronounced than that of the unaffected side.

Although the metrics used provide a reasonable assessment of the XAI methods, they do not capture all aspects of explanation quality. In particular, consistency across similar gait patterns and alignment with clinical knowledge are important considerations. Qualitatively, our explanations show stable patterns within diagnostic groups and highlight biomechanically relevant joints and kinematic phases, supporting the clinical interpretability and potential utility of the proposed XAI approach.

It is important to emphasize that the present findings do not introduce any new biomechanical insights into pathological gait patterns. The predominance of hip, knee and ankle flexion/extension is a well-established finding in clinical gait analysis. Instead, the contribution of this work lies in validating and quantifying this clinical knowledge within deep learning models. Our XAI framework shows that models’ decisions are driven primarily by biomechanically meaningful and clinically recognised joint kinematics rather than spurious or dataset-specific correlations. Furthermore, by quantitatively ranking feature importance, we demonstrate that a reduced subset of joint angles can preserve or improve performance, translating expert knowledge into measurable and reproducible model behaviour. Such validation is a critical step towards making AI-assisted clinical gait analysis trustworthy and clinically acceptable.

Clinical implications and limitations

The application of AI-based diagnostic tools, particularly those based on deep learning, has been hindered by a lack of credibility in the clinical setting53,54. This limitation is attributed to the black-box nature of these methods and their highly parameterized characteristics, which are neither easily interpretable nor quantifiable55. However, the use of XAI methods in this research enabled clinicians to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the rationale behind the decisions made by deep learning diagnostic tools, thereby enhancing their trust in these systems56. At the hospital (CHU Brest) where the experimental data were collected, clinicians have used the results of the proposed deep learning model11 together with the XAI findings from this study, as a reference in their clinical practice.

One relevant clinical application of the proposed framework is providing support for decisions relating to the diagnosis of ambiguous paediatric gait presentations. In practice, distinguishing between mild bilateral cerebral palsy and idiopathic toe walking can be challenging, particularly in the early stages when neurological signs are subtle. Furthermore, brain MRI findings may be normal or non-specific in some children with cerebral palsy, which makes functional assessments, such as 3D gait analysis, all the more important.

In this context, deep learning models could providing probabilistic classifications and interpretable information about joint angles and gait phases to inform decision-making processes. This would enable clinicians to verify whether the model’s reasoning aligns with biomechanical expectations, thereby promoting diagnostic consistency and complementing expert judgement. Combining quantitative analysis with transparent explanations could boost confidence and improve reproducibility in complex clinical cases.

Although this research has yielded promising results in identifying critical features for deep learning based diagnosis, several limitations remain. First, this study focused exclusively on identifying critical features for gait disorders using 3D kinematic data. However, other potentially potentially gait analysis data, such as kinetic, video, and EMG data, remain unexplored and warrant further investigation. Second, when multiple features share overlapping predictive information that is important for classification, current XAI methods are unable to explicitly quantify how much information is shared among these features. Future work should therefore explore methods capable of isolating features that contain primary diagnostic information and of characterizing shared predictive information across features, such as principal component analysis57,58.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper introduces four different XAI methods to explain the predictions of deep learning-based diagnostic models. All methods demonstrate consistent identification in the most critical joint angles. However, based on the results of the quantitative analysis, Integrated Gradients was identified as the most suitable XAI method.

This research enhances the credibility of deep learning based diagnostic tools and improves the understanding of clinical gait signals. Moreover, by masking joint angle features that are irrelevant to the pathology in order to minimize the introduction of noise, the deep learning-based diagnostic tools achieved higher accuracy compared to models using all available features.
