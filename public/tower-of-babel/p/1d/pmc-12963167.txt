Synthesis of chlorophyll b: a retrospective analysis of chlorophyllide a oxygenase
Authors: J. Kenneth Hoober, Laura L. Eggink, Daniel-Paul Bednarik, Steffen Reinbothe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chlorophyll (Chl) b is essential for maximal capacity of photosynthesis in green algae and plants. Along with broadening the absorbance spectrum, Chl b controls the translocation of light-harvesting complex apoproteins across the chloroplast envelope inner membrane and stabilizes light-harvesting complexes in thylakoid membranes. In the absence of Chl b, these Chl-binding proteins are retracted into the cytosol and degraded. A protein designated chlorophyllide (Chlide) a oxygenase (CAO) is required for Chl b synthesis. Conversion in vitro of Chlide a to Chlide b by purified recombinant CAO occurs at a low rate. In contrast, protochlorophyllide (Pchlide) a is converted rapidly and quantitatively to Chlide b by CAO in a membrane fraction from cells of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii y-1 when incubated in the presence of polyaromatic m-phenanthroline. This is surprising because Pchlide a per se is not a substrate for CAO. A retrospective analysis of our previous data suggests that phenanthroline mimics Chlide a and pairs with Pchlide a to lower its redox potential, thereby activating Pchlide a as a substrate. A conserved tyrosine residue occurs as a stable radical in CAO and initiates the transformation of the 7-methyl group of Pchlide a to the 7-formyl group of Pchlide b. Replacing this tyrosine with alanine abolished CAO activity. We conclude that Pchlide b is synthesized from Pchlide a by an unusual, radical-mediated/based mechanism that directly involves molecular oxygen. In vivo, subsequent reduction of the C17-C18 double bond by a light-independent Pchlide reductase activity would be facilitated by the electronegative carbonyl of the 7-formyl group to generate Chlide b.

Introduction: the chlorophyll biosynthetic pathway

Chlorophyll (Chl) b is commonly described as an accessory pigment that broadens the absorbance spectrum of photosynthetic systems. However, its primary and more significant functions are to retain the nucleus-encoded light-harvesting Chl-binding proteins (LHCPs) in the chloroplast envelope and subsequently to stabilize light-harvesting complexes (LHCs) in the thylakoid membranes (Reinbothe et al. 2006; Hoober et al. 2007). After synthesis on cytoplasmic ribosomes, the LHCP precursors normally bind to plastids and partially cross the outer and inner membranes of the plastid envelope by using specific protein translocon complexes. The transit sequence is removed by stromal proteases and the LHCPs are assembled into LHCs in the envelope inner membrane, the site of Chl b synthesis. Without Chl b, however, the LHCPs slip back into the cytosol where they are degraded (Park and Hoober 1997; Wolfe et al. 1997; Reinbothe et al. 2006).

The biosynthetic pathway leading to Chl a was elucidated by research accomplished between 1960 and 2000 (Bogorad 1976; Scheer 1991; Willows 2006), with the first step—formation of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), a C5 compound (Kannangara et al. 1988; Hoober et al. 1988)—and the final steps—esterification of geranylgeraniol with the C17 propionyl sidechain and reduction to phytol (Maloney et al. 1989)—among the last to be clarified. Two key reactions in the pathway are the insertion of Mg2+ into protoporphyrin IX by Mg2+ chelatase (Papenbrock et al. 1997; Kannangara and von Wettstein 2010) and the conversion of protochlorophyllide (Pchlide) a to Chlide a by reduction of the double bond between C17 and C18. This reaction is driven by the enzyme NADPH: Pchlide oxidoreductase B (PORB). Three POR isoenzymes identified in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana display different expression patterns and roles in planta. All require activation of the substrate for activity (see Reinbothe et al. 2010; for review).

Early extensive but controversial studies on the biosynthesis of Chl b were reviewed by Shylk (1971) and Bogorad (1976), who concluded that the precursor of Chl b is Chl a or a “compound that can be transformed to either Chl a or b.” Work in the Bogorad laboratory suggested that the pathway may branch at the stage of Pchlide a, giving rise to Pchlide b by involving an enzyme capable of performing this transformation. A gene designated PTC52 was cloned and shown to possess Pchlide a oxygenase activity (Reinbothe et al. 2019). The resulting product, Pchide b, is converted by PORA to Chlide b (see Reinbothe et al. 2010). Genetic studies using RNA interference and knock-out lines suggested that PTC52 plays an essential role for plant viability (Reinbothe et al. 2019). However, in the traditional concept, the pathway proceeds in a linear fashion from Pchlide a to Chlide a and finally the conversion of the methyl group on the C7 atom to the formyl group of Chl b (see Fig. 1 for structures of Chl a and Chl b) by an enzyme designated Chlide a oxygenase (CAO). Green algae and plants that carry a loss-of-function mutation in the gene encoding CAO fail to accumulate Chl b.

Several experiments that provided some clarification regarding the precursor of Chlide b involved exposure of etiolated bean seedlings to intermittent light (2 min light, 98 min darkness), which supported the synthesis of Chl a but not of Chl b (Argyroudi-Akoyunoglou and Akoyunglou 1970). Transfer to continuous light increased the rate of synthesis of Chl a and induced immediate synthesis of Chl b. Labeling of Chl a by incorporation of [14C]ALA during the intermittent light period led to appearance of labeled Chl b at the expense of labeled Chl a after transfer to continuous light, an indication that Chl b was made from Chl a (Argroudi-Akoyunoglou, Kondylaki and Akoyunoglou 1976). However, the specific radioactivity of Chl b was usually less than half that of Chl a, which suggested that labeled Chl b was diluted by the chlorin made by another pathway (reviewed in Hoober and Argyroudi-Akoyunoglou 2004). Complementary experiments with mustard seedlings showed that synthesis of Chl b did not occur without activation of phytochrome with red light (668 nm) during early greening. When seedlings were exposed to a saturating pulse of light to convert all Pchlide a to Chl(ide) a and then returned to the dark, those pretreated with red light formed Chl b stoichiometrically at the expense of Chl a; seedlings not pretreated did not make Chl b (Oelze-Karow and Mohr 1978). A common element in these experiments was a necessary level of newly synthesized Chlide a sufficient to achieve the synthesis of Chl b. Because Chl a and Chl b increased with similar time courses, Oelze-Karow and Mohr (1978) suggested that Chlide a is the common precursor of both.

Contrasting results were obtained in a series of experiments performed by Adamson and her colleagues, who demonstrated that young shoots of barley continued to accumulate Chl a, Chl b and a small amount of Pchlide a in the dark after a period of greening in the light (Adamson et al. 1985; Packer and Adamson 1986). Incorporation of [14C]ALA into Chl a in the dark demonstrated de novo synthesis, which led these investigators to propose the existence of a light-independent POR activity in barley. We provide an explanation for these apparently disparate observations at the end of this Report.

Schneegurt and Beale (1992) and Porra et al. (1994) found that the source of the 7-formyl oxygen atom of Chl b is molecular oxygen. Porra et al. (1994) discussed several alternative mechanisms as to how the formyl group of Chl b could be formed. One proposal involved a dioxygenase to produce a peroxide intermediate. In another pathway, consecutive monooxygenase reactions were proposed to produce a 7-dihydroxymethyl Ch(lide) a intermediate, which would be converted by loss of a water molecule to Chl(ide) b. Porra et al. (1994) pointed out that monooxygenation would require an enzyme with unusual properties catalyzing first the transformation of the 7-methly group to the 7-hydroxymethyl group and then to a formyl group, or else a two-enzyme system comprising the monooxygenase plus a non-specific chloroplast dehydrogenase.

Discovery of chlorophyllide a oxygenase (CAO)

The gene encoding CAO was isolated first from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Tanaka et al. 1998) and shortly thereafter from Arabidopsis thaliana (Espineda et al. 1999). Since then the gene has been sequenced from more than 20 plant and algal species, which revealed an extensive degree of sequence similarity (Dey et al. 2023). Ubiquitous in these proteins are sequences that form a Rieske (2Fe-2 S) cluster and a nonheme, mononuclear Fe center. Detailed data base searches revealed that the characterized CAO gene in plants and green algae that synthesize Chl b all contain an additional, evolutionarily conserved RYRxWR motif of thus far unrecognized function near the C-terminus (Dey et al. 2023; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, cited 2026 Feb 12). Green algae and primitive plants (Chlamydomonas, Volvox, Chlorella, Marchantia, etc.) contain the most arginine-rich motif (RYRRWR) whereas flowering plants contain the sequence RYRxWR, where x is frequently leucine (e.g., Arabidopsis, Hordeum, Triticum, etc.). Oster et al. (2000) achieved expression of a full-length cDNA clone of the Arabidopsis gene that led to an active enzyme when incubated with Chlide a, ferredoxin, ferredoxin reductase, NADPH and a NADPH-generating system. 7-Dihydroxymethyl Chlide a was proposed as an intermediate, which by loss of a water molecule would be converted to Chlide b. Because Chl a was not a substrate, the enzyme was named “Chlide a oxygenase.”

Conversion of Chlide a to Chlide b was extensively studied by Liu et al. (2022) with recombinant CAO of several plant and algal species that were expressed in Escherichia coli, with a focus on Rieske oxygenase chemistry. CAO from Prochlorothrix was more active than homologs from Chlamydomonas or Arabidopsis in a complex reaction mixture containing VanB (a bacterial ferredoxin reductase) and NADPH, yet only a small fraction of the Chlide a substrate was converted to Chlide b. The proposed intermediate, 7-hydroxymethyl Chlide a, was actively converted to Chlide b but the reaction required a relatively high substrate concentration (Km = 7.8 µM). However, 7-hydroxymethyl Chlide a was not detected as an intermediate when the Arabidopsis CAO cDNA construct, described by Oster et al. (2000), was expressed in a wheat germ transcription/translation system. A significant amount of Chlide a was converted to Chlide b in an optimized assay mixture that included 1 µM CAO, 2 µM Chlide a, ferredoxin, ferredoxin reductase, NADPH and a NADPH-generating system (Reinbothe et al. 2006). Activity was also found with purified and reconstituted CAO after expression in E. coli. Pchlide a was not a substrate in the assay of the enzyme in the wheat germ system.

Detection of a CAO tyrosyl radical

A glimpse of an alternate mechanism of how the CAO reaction may proceed was obtained by Eggink et al. (2004) who used electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy to probe the CAO structure and identify possible reaction intermediates. EPR spectra for the native, immunoprecipitated protein revealed the presence of a signal for a nonheme FeIII (g = 4.3) and a sharp, 9 gauss peak-to-trough signal (g = 2.0042) within a broad, 100 gauss peak-to-trough signal (g = 2.057) (Fig. 2A). The broad signal was observed only below 15 K, indicative of a Fe-S complex. To identify the residue containing the g = 2.0042 radical, CAO was immunoprecipitated from detergent-solubilized membrane fractions from Chlamydomonas cells and Arabidopsis seedlings and treated with Na125I, which reacts specifically with tyrosyl radicals. The samples were then digested with trypsin and analyzed by N-terminal sequence degradation. The highest release of radioactivity occurred in the first cycle. Tyrosine is next to a trypsin cleavage site only at position 422 in the sequence of CAO from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and at position 518 in the sequence from Arabidopsis thaliana.

The EPR spectrum of Arabidopsis CAO expressed in E. coli contained signals for the nonheme iron, the Rieske center and the tyrosyl radical (Fig. 2, B and C). Addition of Chl a to the sample quenched the tyrosyl radical but did not significantly affect the broad signal or the signal for the nonheme iron. The reaction of the tyrosyl radical (Em = + 0.94 V) with Chl a (E1 = + 0.83 to + 0.86 V) suggested the removal of a hydrogen atom from the 7-methyl group of Chl a. This reaction would activate a path for the direct involvement of molecular oxygen as the source of the formyl oxygen of Chlide b. To test this hypothesis, tyrosine residues in the C-terminal domain of CAO from Arabidopsis were replaced with alanine by site-directed mutagenesis. Activity of the mutated enzyme was determined with an optimized in vitro assay (Reinbothe et al. 2006). As shown in Fig. 3, replacement of Tyr518 with alanine abolished activity. Only small reductions in activity were found with replacements of other tyrosine residues, including Tyr222, Tyr283 and Tyr288 that are near or within the Rieske (2Fe-2 S) cluster and Tyr402, Tyr456 and Tyr511 that are also conserved in CAO. The lack of activity when Tyr518 was replaced with alanine does not support the hydroxylation pathway initially proposed by Oster et al. (2000) but does support an alternate, tyrosyl radical-based mechanism.

A notable—perhaps unique—feature of CAO is the spontaneous formation and stability of the tyrosyl radical. The free electron is expected to be delocalized across the phenolic ring structure. Arginine residues occur immediately adjacent to the radical-bearing tyrosine in the amino acid sequence of the Chlamydomonas (Fig. 4A) and Arabidopsis proteins (Fig. 4B), which provide a positively-charged, electrostatic environment that apparently accommodates the free electron (Warren et al. 2012). Interestingly, in an α-helical model of this sequence from Arabidopsis (Fig. 4B), the sidechain of the leucine that replaces one of the arginine residues in the Chlamydomonas sequence (Fig. 4A) would be on the opposite side of the helix from the phenolic group of Tyr518. This position would likely not contribute to stabilization of the radical.

Also of interest are the conserved histidine residues in the proposed substrate binding pocket (Espineda et al. 1999; Kunugi et al. 2013; Dey et al. 2023). The substrate is possibly bound by coordination bonding between the imidazole ring and the central Mg2+ of (P)Chlide a. Conversion to (P)Chlide b causes electrostatic repulsion of histidine by the Mg2+ atom and thus release of the product (Chen et al. 2005; Hoober et al. 2007).

The substrate

Chlamydomonas reinhardtii possesses both light-dependent (LPOR, using NADPH) and light-independent (LIPOR, involving ChlB) systems for reducing Pchlide to Chlide (Li et al. 1993; Li and Timko 1996; Reinbothe et al. 2010). In the pc-1 mutant that lacks the NADPH-dependent form, the LIPOR system is still operational and allows Chlide synthesis in the dark. However, the y-1 mutant of C. reinhardtii lacks LIPOR activity and thus is unable to make Chlide a in the dark. When dark-grown, yellow y-1 cells were exposed to light at 38 °C, Chl a and Chl b were synthesized at linear rates, without a lag, with a Chl a/b ratio of slightly greater than 1 (Maloney et al. 1989). The discrepancy between the robust activity in vivo and the low in vitro activity (referenced above) prompted us to examine more closely the in vivo reaction for evidence of additional factors needed for activity. The metabolism of Chlamydomonas cells is easily manipulated, which favors this organism as a model system to examine this question. Several cogent observations emerged from an analysis of data obtained previously by Bednarik and Hoober (1985a,b). A key discovery was the stimulation of Chlide b synthesis by phenanthroline. Whereas the iron-chelating isomer ortho-phenanthroline stimulated porphyrin synthesis in higher plants, most likely by preventing feedback inhibition of the pathway by heme, this effect was not observed in C. reinhardtii y-1. Instead, ortho-phenanthroline stimulated Chlide b synthesis. The non-chelating meta isomer was more effective than the chelating ortho isomer in experiments with the algal cells, which indicated that iron chelation by phenanthroline was not involved. Collectively, these studies revealed that phenanthroline promoted a reaction between protoporphyrin IX and Chlide b and more precisely between Pchlide a and Pchlide b. Whereas Chlide b was actively made in the in vivo experiments, neither Pchlide a nor Chlide a alone was a functional substrate for CAO. The requirement of the planar, polyaromatic phenanthroline led to the concept that phenanthroline mimics (P)Chlide a, which is required for Pchlide b synthesis as a ‘cofactor.’ It is possible, therefore, that phenanthroline pairs with Pchlide a to facilitate a reaction with the tyrosyl radical, which initiates a radical-based reaction with oxygen to produce Pchlide b. The optimal substrate for CAO may thus be a Pchlide a/Chlide a heterodimer.

That phenanthroline may substitute for (P)Chlide a as a cofactor in association with Pchlide a follows the known interaction of planar, aromatic compounds. A pairing of Pchlide a and phenanthroline would be stabilized by strong complementary electrostatic attraction and van der Waals forces. In a dimer of porphyrins, one molecule is slightly offset relative to the other (Abraham and Smith 1983; Hunter and Sanders 1990; Martinez and Iverson 2012). Pairing of Pchlide a with phenanthroline possibly lowers the redox potential to promote abstraction of a hydrogen atom from the 7-methyl group. As an example, the redox potential of monomeric Chl a is approximately + 0.85 V, whereas the potential of dimeric Chl a is near + 0.50 V (Watanabe and Kobayashi 1991).

Resolution

How may all of the previous and more recent observations be brought together and how is the tyrosyl radical involved in the unique reaction mechanism of CAO? The rate of conversion of Chlide a to Chlide b in vitro is low possibly because of the difficulty in activating a methyl group when a relatively small difference in redox potentials occurs between enzyme and substrate. Therefore, we propose that the optimal substrate of CAO is a Pchlide a dimer or Pchlide a in a complex with Chlide a in order to reduce the redox potential of the substrate. We found that Chl a quenched the tyrosyl radical (Fig. 2B) and possibly initiated a radical-based reaction with oxygen. As is common with other tyrosyl radicals in enzyme catalysis (Babcock et al. 1997), we hypothesize that the initial step in Chlide b synthesis is removal of a hydrogen atom from Pchlide a to produce the 7-methyl radical, Pchlide a-7-CH2•. This intermediate is expected to react rapidly with molecular oxygen, a diradical (containing two unpaired electrons), to produce a peroxyl radical, Pchlide a-7-CH2OO•. The latter (Em = + 1.0 V) would reoxidize the phenolic ring of tyrosine in CAO to restore the initial stable-radical state of the enzyme and thereby produce Pchlide a-7- hydroperoxide. To convert this intermediate into the final product, Pchlide b, an electron is required, which most likely occurs by reduction of the nonheme iron center to FeII by transfer of an electron from the Rieske iron-sulfur cluster. As found for other Rieske enzymes, the reduced iron center could cleave the hydroperoxide intermediate, resulting in a ferryl group and the release of a hydroxide ion, which combines with a proton to produce a water molecule (Fig. 5). The electronegative ferryl structure would then cause movement of electrons from a C-H bond to the C-O bond, with the release of a proton and the iron as FeII. After priming of the enzyme by reduction of FeIII to FeII, the 7-formyl group would be produced by an electron and proton-neutral cycle.

Conclusion

CAO has been described as an atypical Rieske oxygenase that catalyzes sequential hydroxylation reactions of Chlide a to produce 7-dihydroxymethyl Chlide a (Liu et al. 2022; Tian et al. 2023). Rieske oxygenases are generally characterized as enzymes that activate oxygen to produce oxidants such as FeIII-OOH or FeV=O(H) by FeII in the nonheme iron center after reduction by the Rieske (2Fe-2 S) center with electrons from NADPH (Barry and Challis 2013; Liu et al. 2022; Runda, de Kok and Schmidt 2023). These oxidants then perform either a dioxygenase or a monooxygenase reaction. In contrast, we provide evidence that CAO contains a spontaneously formed tyrosyl radical that initiates synthesis of Chlide b from Pchlide a by generation of a 7-methyl radical that reacts directly and rapidly with molecular oxygen. Moreover, the substrate is rendered more reactive by pairing with a polyaromatic compound such as Pchlide a or Chlide a to form (hetero)dimers. A consequence of the electronegative oxygen in the 7-formyl group of Pchlide b is withdrawal of π-electrons away from the C17-C18 double bond, across the X axis of the molecule, which in vivo would enhance the rate of a light-independent reduction to form Chlide b, thereby performing a function similar to the absorption of light in activating the substrate for Pchlide oxidoreductase (Archipowa et al. 2018).

Predicted structural models of CAO (Kunugi et al. 2013; Dey et al. 2023) suggest that the substrate binding site is distant from the Rieske complex but near the nonheme iron center, which would support electron transfer from NADPH through the Rieske complex to the iron center. Along with the predicted structure of PTC52, which catalyzes a reaction similar to that of CAO (Reinbothe et al. 2019), the substrate binding site appears to be capable of accommodating a dimeric substrate. Additional research is needed to confirm this proposal.

Interpreting the experiments with greening plants cited in the Introduction (Argyroudi-Akoyunoglou, Kondylaki and Akoyunoglou 1976; Oelze-Karow and Mohr 1978) from this perspective suggests that a Chl(ide) a dimer can serve as a substrate for synthesis of Chlide b. The primary requirement is a rate of synthesis of the substrate that is sufficiently high to provide pairing for CAO activity. The oxygenation of Pchlide a to Pchlide b (or Chlide a to Chlide b) is not light-dependent. Moreover, the reduction of Pchlide b to Chlide b is also light-independent. Metabolism in the well-documented Chl cycle (Tanaka and Tanaka 2019) involves the reduction of Chl b to 7-hydroxymethyl-Chl a by Chl b reductase, which is further reduced to Chl a by 7-hydroxymethyl-Chl a reductase. These enzymes complete a light-independent pathway for the synthesis of Chl a as described by Adamson et al. (Adamson et al. 1985; Walmsley and Adamson 1995).

Collectively, the results summarized in this perspective suggest that Chlide b is synthesized from Pchlide a by a light-independent, radical-mediated, mixed function reaction with Chlide a as a required cofactor. Key elements are firstly a dimeric substrate to lower the redox potential and secondly initiation of the pathway by a tyrosyl radical to overcome the difficulty of activating a methyl group in the substrate. This process requires a threshold level of Chlide a synthesis and provides an effective mechanism for regulation of Chlide b synthesis.
