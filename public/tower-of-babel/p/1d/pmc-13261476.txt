Facet-Selective Templating of Hydroxyapatite by Bioinspired Polycarboxylate Peptides: Distinguishing (001) Hydroxyapatite Stabilization from Octacalcium Phosphate Promotion
Authors: Yuan Li, Gregory P. Holland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The selective stabilization of hydroxyapatite (HAP) crystal facets by acidic biomolecules plays a central role in biomineralization, yet the mechanistic distinction between facet-specific HAP stabilization and promotion of metastable calcium phosphate phases remains unclear. Here, we systematically examine HAP crystallization in the presence of polycarboxylate ligandsEDTA, citrate, polyglutamate (E6 and E13), and the dentin-derived (DSS)4 repeatto determine how carboxylate spacing and ligand architecture influence calcium phosphate phase selection and morphology. Scanning electron microscopy, powder X-ray diffraction, and multinuclear solid-state NMR reveal that EDTA, citrate, and polyglutamate favor the formation of sheet-like HAP enriched in (001) surface exposure, whereas (DSS)4 promotes ribbon-like nanocrystals dominated by octacalcium phosphate (OCP). Hydrothermal treatment converts OCP-containing products to phase-pure HAP, enabling direct comparison of ligand-dependent defect structures. 1H MAS NMR identifies hydroxylated surface calcium sites across all samples and detects hydrogen phosphate and crystalline water environments diagnostic of OCP in (DSS)4-templated materials, while 31P CP-MAS spectra confirm complete OCP-to-HAP transformation after hydrothermal conversion. These results support a geometric recognition model in which short-spaced carboxylates preferentially stabilize the HAP (001) surface, whereas extended anionic spacing favors OCP-related lattice arrangements. This work establishes design principles for peptide-mediated control of calcium phosphate crystal growth and facet expression.

Introduction

Bone is a composite material composed of inorganic hydroxyapatite (HAP) nanoparticles (NPs) interwoven with an organic protein matrix. Its mineralization is a tightly regulated process that is controlled by a range of biomolecules within the extracellular matrix. As an inorganic phase, the morphological and crystallographic expression of HAP can be modulated by polyanionic motifs that selectively stabilize specific crystal facets, a strategy widely observed in both biomineralization and biomimetic systems. − The non-collagenous and collagenous matrix proteins, therefore, act as natural surfactants, guiding and facilitating the formation of bioapatite in mineralized tissues, including bone and teeth. −

Several functional proteins involved in bone mineralization, including bone sialoprotein (BSP), osteopontin, and osteocalcin, contain domains rich in acidic residues that play crucial roles in regulating the nucleation, crystallization, and metabolism of HAP (Table ). − The polyglutamic motifs in BSP have been shown to participate directly in HAP nucleation. Osteopontin contains polyaspartic acid (PAA) motifs and is highly expressed in bone, where it inhibits and regulates the HAP growth. , Osteocalcin is characterized by the presence of γ-carboxyglutamic acid residues within its “Gla helix” motifs, which are considered to stabilize the (001) lattice plane of HAP. ,− Beyond its role as a metabolic intermediate, citrate is found to occupy the bound layer of HAP nanocrystals, where it stabilizes specific crystal surfaces. − Similarly, dentin mineralization is initiated and regulated by acidic phosphoproteins such as phosphophoryn, which consists of repetitive (Asp-Ser-Ser) motifs with a high degree of phosphorylation. −

Acidic Domains in Non-Collagenous Peptides

During vertebrate mineralization, several metastable intermediates have been identified. Calcium and phosphate ions initially associate to form amorphous precursor species, , often described as tricalcium phosphate clusters (TCP) known as Posner’s clusters. − Aggregation of these clusters leads to amorphous calcium phosphate, which subsequently transforms into apatitic phases. Octacalcium phosphate (OCP) has been proposed as an epitaxial precursor to HAP and is frequently observed in early-stage in vitro mineralization studies. , However, there is no definitive in vivo evidence confirming OCP as a required intermediate in bone formation , The morphological similarity between ribbon-like enamel HAP and OCP, along with their topotactic relationship, has motivated continued interest in OCP during enamel mineralization. , Nevertheless, only OCP-like crystallites have been detected at the earliest stages of enamel formation. Dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (CaHPO4·2H2O, DCPD) is another proposed precursor to biological apatite and typically forms under acidic to near-neutral physiological conditions. ,, DCPD can subsequently transform into calcium-deficient HAP through dehydration and ion-mediated dissolution processes. ,

Here, we systematically incorporate a series of polycarboxylate ligands into HAP synthesis to modulate nucleation behavior and direct crystal growth. These ligands include the metal-chelating agent EDTA as well as bioinspired speciescitrate, polyglutamate (E6 and E13), and a dentin-derived (DSS)4 repeat. The resulting calcium phosphate nanomaterials are characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), and multinuclear solid-state NMR to distinguish facet-selective HAP stabilization from promotion of metastable calcium phosphate phases.

Experimental Section

Materials

Calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca­(NO3)2·4H2O, 99%), urea ((NH2)2CO, 98%), citrate (C6H3O7 99%), and jack bean urease (480 kDa) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Ammonium phosphate dibasic ((NH4)2HPO4, 99%), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid ([CH2N­(CH2CO2H)2]2, 99%) were purchased from Acros. Rink Amide ProTide Resin (loading: 0.18 mequiv/g substitution), Fmoc-l-glutamic acid 5-tert-butyl ester (Fmoc-Glu­(OtBu)–OH, C24H27NO6, 99%), Fmoc-l-aspartic acid 4-tert-butyl ester (Fmoc-Asp­(OtBu)–OH, C23H25NO6, 98%) and Fmoc-O-trityl-l-serine (Fmoc-Ser­(Trt)–OH, C37H31NO5, 98%) were purchased from CEM Corporation. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, C3H7NO, 98%), ethyl cyano­(hydroxyimino)­acetate (oxyma, C5H6N2O3, 98%), N,N′-diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC, C7H14N2, 99%), trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, CF3COOH, 99%), ethyl ether (C4H10O, 99%), 3,6-dioxa-1,8-octanedithiol (DODT, C6H14O2S2, 98%), triisopropylsilane (TIS, C9H22Si, 98%), acetonitrile (ACN, CH3CN, 98%), and ammonium hydroxide were purchased from Fisher Scientific. Piperidine (C5H11N, 99%) was purchased from Spectrum Chemicals. 3-(Trimethylsilyl)­propionic-2,2,3,3-d4 acid sodium salt (TMPS) was purchased from Cambridge Isotopes Inc.

Peptide Synthesis

Two acidic peptides that were inspired by HAP-related biomineralization protein motifs, E6, E13, and DSSDSSDSSDSS ((DSS)4), were synthesized using a CEM Liberty Blue high-efficiency solid-phase peptide synthesizer (HE-SPPS). Synthesis was performed on Rink Amide ProTide resin at a scale of 0.05 mmol, using DMF as the solvent for all reagents.

Each amino acid monomer was attached sequentially onto the peptide chain by coupling reaction using Fmoc-protected amino acids (Fmoc-AA–OH, 0.2 M in DMF, 5 equiv), activator (DIC, 0.25 M in DMF, 5 equiv), and activator base (oxyma, 0.5 M in DMF, 5 equiv). The reactions were performed under 90 °C for 4 min. Fmoc deprotection was carried out by treatment of piperidine (0.1 M in DMF, 3 equiv) at 90 °C for 1 min. The coupling and deprotection steps were repeated until the full sequence was completed. All the heating steps were achieved using microwave irradiation.

Peptides were extracted from the resin with a “cleavage cocktail” composed of 92.5% TFA, 2.5% H2O, 2.5% DODT, and 2.5% TIS, incubated at 38 °C for 45 min. The protecting groups were removed from the side chains simultaneously during this process. The liquid was isolated and concentrated under a gentle nitrogen stream to remove excess TFA. The peptides were then precipitated by adding cold diethyl ether at 3-fold the volume of residual TFA. These crude products were isolated by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min and washed two more times using diethyl ether to remove remaining byproducts and residual reagents.

The chemical identity and purity of the synthesized peptides were confirmed prior to mineralization experiments. Polyglutamate (E6 and E13) and the dentin-derived (DSS)4 peptide were characterized by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry and solution-state 1H NMR spectroscopy, confirming the expected molecular weights and chemical structures (Figures S1–S4).

Purification and Mass Analysis

The crude peptides were purified with a Biotage Isolera flash column system equipped with a Biotage Sfär Bio C18 with a particle size of 20 μm and pore size of 300 Å column using H2O/ACN with 0.1% TFA as mobile phase at room temperature.

The fractions with UV absorbance at 214 nm were collected and analyzed with an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with an Agilent Zorbax Extend C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm × 3.5 μm) combined with an Agilent 6530 mass spectrometer equipped with dual Agilent Jet Stream (AJS) electrospray ionization (ESI) operating in positive polarity. The ESI source conditions were set as follows: nebulizer gas pressure of 35 psi, fragmentor at 175 V, and skimmer at 65 V. Mass spectra were acquired over a range of mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) between 100 and 3000. Agilent G1969-85001, which contains standard compounds with m/z ratios of 121.1 and 922.0, was added as an internal reference.

After validating the molecular weight of the product, the fraction with purified product was freeze-dried in a Labconco FreeZone lyophilizer at −89 °C under vacuum pressure below 0.10 mbar for 72 h.

NMR Analysis

The purified peptide products were dissolved in a solution consisting of H2O:D2O (90:10, v/v) and 1 mM TMSP as an internal reference of NMR chemical shifts. For each NMR sample, ∼5 mg peptide was dissolved in 0.6 mL H2O:D2O. Ammonium hydroxide was added to solubilize E6 and E13 by adjusting pH to ∼5 until complete dissolution. Solution NMR spectra were collected with a Bruker 400 MHz spectrometer using a Bruker double resonance BBO probe at room temperature. 1D 1H spectra were collected to characterize the structure of the synthesized peptides.

HAP Synthesis and Characterization

HAP NP Synthesis with Peptide Ligands

HAP was synthesized following a modified enzyme hydrolysis method reported in previous studies. − Briefly, 16 mL starting solutions containing 20 mM (NH4)2HPO4, 12 mM Ca­(NO3)2, and 2.5 M urea with the initial pH adjusted to ∼5.0 by HNO3 were prepared. Different ligands, including 0.80 mM (DSS)4, 0.47 mM E6, 0.25 mM E13, 1.25 mM EDTA, and 1.67 mM citrate, were added into the mixture separately as capping agents to direct the mineralization of HAP in each synthesis. The molecular structures of these ligands are provided in Figure . The total carboxylate content normalized based on the numbers of carboxylate groups present in each ligand molecule was ∼0.05 mmol for E6, E13, and (DSS)4 and ∼0.08 mmol for EDTA and citrate. Jack bean urease (0.2 mL 0.1% (w/w)) was added to the starting solutions to start the hydrolysis reaction. A sample without any capping agents was also prepared as a control sample following the same protocol. Ammonia generated in this process will slowly raise the pH and eventually precipitate HAP. The products were collected at pH ∼8.2. The solid products were purified by dialysis in DI water and dried by vacuum at room temperature. For some of the as-synthesized HAP NPs, the OCP phase was converted to HAP by autoclave in DIW for 45 min at 120 °C.

Molecular structures of the ligands, including EDTA, citrate, E6, and (DSS)4 added in the HAP synthesis.

Crystallographic Characterization of the Peptide-Directed HAP NPs with XRD

The crystallinity of peptide-directed HAP NPs was examined with an X’pert XRD operating at Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) of 45 kV and 40 mA. 2θ was scanned from 3 to 60° with a scanning rate of 2°/min at room temperature. The XRD patterns were processed and baseline corrected with Origin software. The reference XRD patterns of HAP and OCP were simulated with VESTA based on the crystal structure from the Crystallography Open Database (COD entries 9011092 and 1534327, respectively). The simulated pattern was calculated with a wavelength of 1.54059 Å.

Electron Microscopy Imaging of Peptide-Directed HAP NPs

The sizes and morphologies of peptide-directed HAP NPs were examined with a FEI Quanta 450 FEG Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) at 20 kV. The products were fixed on carbon tabs supported by aluminum and coated with 6 nm of platinum. The sizes of each type of HAP were manually measured with ImageJ, and the reported values represent the mean ± standard deviation calculated from measurements of more than 60 particles for each sample.

Solid-State Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

1H MAS NMR spectra of synthesized HAP NPs were collected at a MAS rate of 40 kHz with a 1.6 mm Phoenix probe operating in double resonance mode (1H/13C) using a Varian 500 MHz VNMRS spectrometer. The spectra were collected with 1H π/2 pulse with a duration of 4 μs and a recycle delay of 15 s. The 1H spectra are referenced to adamantane (∼1.9 ppm). A total of 128 scans were collected for each 1H spectrum.

1H–31P cross-polarization magic-angle spinning (CP-MAS) spectra were collected using a 3.2 mm 1H/31P/31C triple-resonance MAS probe on a Bruker 600 MHz Avance III HD spectrometer. Experiments were performed at a MAS rate of 12 kHz with radiofrequency field strengths of 100 kHz for 1H and 88 kHz for 31P. The CP condition was optimized using ammonium dihydrogen phosphate as a setup standard, employing a 30% ramped amplitude on 1H and a contact time of 2.5 ms. 31P chemical shifts were indirectly referenced to 85% H3PO4 at 0 ppm. Proton decoupling during acquisition was achieved using swept-frequency two-pulse phase modulation (swf-TPPM) at an rf field strength of 100 kHz. , A total of 256 scans was collected with a recycle delay of 5 s.

Results and Discussion

Baseline Crystallization Behavior in the Absence of Ligands

The (001) HAP lattice plane is considered a less energetically favored crystal facet, which typically crystallizes within a narrow kinetic window in supersaturated solutions under mildly acidic conditions. ,, This facet is commonly deposited as sheet- or flake-like NPs on calcium phosphate substrates, such as β-TCP, ACP cores, and at the interface of aqueous solutions or amphiphilic media. ,− The crystal lattice plane of these sheet-like NPs is confirmed by high-resolution TEM. ,− Kinetically favored OCP with blade- or ribbon-like morphologies subsequently overgrows these surfaces along its c-axis, epitaxially aligned with the (100) lattice plane of HAP. , An illustration of the growth layers corresponding to these different crystal phases is presented in Figure exhibiting morphology consistent with previously reported observations. We note that the phase assignments associated with specific morphological features in Figure are inferred from established morphology–phase correlations and supported by bulk characterization, rather than direct spatially resolved measurements. Techniques such as TEM-SAED or dark-field TEM would be required to definitively confirm local phase identity at specific regions. Therefore, these assignments should be interpreted as consistent with the proposed mechanism, rather than direct proof of phase localization. Hydrothermal treatment is commonly employed to induce the phase transformation of OCP to HAP. Hydrolysis can be achieved by autoclaving at high temperature (120 °C) for 1 h or under mild incubation conditions for over 24 h. In the current study, the autoclave method was employed.

SEM images of layers of crystal depositions without any capping agent (HAP-NC), showing morphology consistent with the growth of HAP and OCP.

Ligand-Dependent Morphological Control of Calcium Phosphate Nanostructures

EDTA has been employed as a calcium-binding agent to selectively stabilize the (001) lattice planes and preserve the sheet-like morphology as reported in our previous study. In the present study, citrate, E6, E13, and (DSS)4 were introduced following the same synthetic protocols to further investigate the templating effects as a function of carboxylate content. The templating behaviors of these polycarboxylate capping agents containing different numbers of carboxyl groups were systematically examined.

The SEM micrographs of HAP NPs synthesized without polycarboxylate capping agents (HAP-NC), in the presence of EDTA (HAP-EDTA), citrate (HAP-CA), polyglutamate (HAP-E6 and HAP-E13), and (DSS)4 (HAP-DSS) are summarized in Figure .

SEM images of (a1) and (a2) HAP-NC, (b1) and (b2) HAP-EDTA, (c1) and (c2) HAP-CA, (d1) and (d2) HAP-E6, (e1) and (e2) HAP-E13, and (f1) and (f2) HAP-DSS.

The SEM image of HAP-NC shows NPs in large sheet-like morphologies with lengths of 7.24 ± 2.00 μm and widths of 1.70 ± 0.41 μm (Figure a). HAP-EDTA forms nanoflower assemblies composed of intergrown nanosheets with average lengths of 1.36 ± 0.37 μm and widths of 0.59 ± 0.15 μm (Figure b). Cross-linked nanosheet matrices are observed for HAP-CA, with average lengths below 1 μm (Figure c). HAP-E6 NPs exhibit a well-defined sheet-like morphology with average dimensions of 2.22 ± 0.593 μm in length and 0.916 ± 0.268 μm in width, which are larger sizes than those of HAP nanosheets templated by other polycarboxylate agents Figure d). HAP-E13 NPs are nanosheets with an average length of 1.79 ± 0.428 μm and a width of 0.763 ± 0.132 μm (Figure e). HAP-DSS generally shows a ribbon-like morphology with a length of 12.4 ± 7.98 μm and a width of 0.886 ± 0.289 μm (Figure f).

Phase Identification of Synthesized HAP NPs by Powder XRD

Because of the morphological similarities between the nanosized sheets and ribbons observed in SEM micrographs of crystalline HAP NPs, it is necessary to collect XRD for accurate phase identification. HAP shows characteristic reflections at 2θ angles of 25.90°, 31.79°, 32.92°, 34.09°, 39.92°, 46.73°, 49.65°, and 53.40°, corresponding to (002), (211), (300), (202), (310), (222), (213), and (004) lattice planes. For sheet-like HAP with the (001) lattice plane preferentially developed, the reflections of (002) and (004) are typically intensified. OCP shows characteristic reflections at 4.73° and 33.57°, corresponding to (100) and (700) reflections, respectively. These distinct diffraction patterns enable reliable differentiation between OCP and HAP crystal phases.

The XRD pattern of HAP-NC shows characteristic HAP reflections of (002), (211), and (202) planes, along with OCP (100) and (700) reflections, indicating the coexistence of both crystal phases (Figure a). HAP-EDTA exhibits strongly pronounced (002) and (004) reflections along with a weak reflection from OCP (100) and (700) lattice planes before hydrothermal treatment, signifying the presence of both OCP and HAP phases (Figure b, left). After hydrothermal treatment, OCP reflection disappears (Figure b, right), confirming the completion of phase transformation. The remaining strong reflections from HAP (002) and (004) lattice suggest a predominance of surface exposure of (001) lattice planes.

XRD patterns of (a) HAP-NC, (b) HAP-EDTA, (c) HAP-CA, (d) HAP-E6, (e) HAP-E13, and (f) HAP-DSS before (left panel) and after (right panel) hydrothermal treatment. (g) and (h) are the simulated XRD patterns of OCP and HAP.

HAP-CA shows distinct (002) and (004) HAP reflections and an intensified reflection from (211), with no evidence of OCP observed (Figure c). The XRD patterns of HAP-E6 resemble those of HAP-NC, with strong reflections from OCP (100) and HAP (002) (Figure d). Upon hydrothermal treatment, the OCP reflections disappeared, leaving the XRD pattern (Figure d, right) similar to hydrothermal-treated HAP-EDTA (Figure b, right). HAP-E13 displays predominantly HAP reflections with a minor OCP (100) reflection (Figure e, left) and turned slightly more crystalline following hydrothermal treatment (Figure e, right).

HAP-DSS exhibits reflections almost exclusively from OCP, specifically the (100) and (700) planes (Figure f), indicating that OCP is the predominant phase of this NP in this sample, as confirmed by comparison with the reference XRD pattern of OCP (Figure g). After hydrothermal treatment, these OCP reflections are eliminated and converted to an XRD pattern similar to standard HAP (Figure h).

Solid-State 1H MAS NMR of Synthesized HAP

In our previous studies, 1H, 31P, and 43Ca NMR were employed to characterize the crystallographic environments and monitor the phase transformations of sheet-like HAP NPs. , The 1H spectra typically display characteristic resonances at 0 ppm, ∼1 ppm, and ∼5 ppm, which correspond to structural hydroxyl groups, hydroxylated surface calcium sites (Ca–OH), and physisorbed surface water, respectively. Since these sheet- and ribbon-like HAP NPs were synthesized under acidic to near-neutral pH, their surfaces contain substantial numbers of Ca–OH defects, resulting in pronounced resonances at 0.8 and 1.3 ppm (Figure ).

1H NMR patterns of (a) HAP-NC, (b) HAP-EDTA, (c) HAP-CA, (d) HAP-E6, (e) HAP-E13, and (f) HAP-DSS before (left panel) and after (right panel) hydrothermal treatment.

A key feature distinguishing hydroxyapatite (HAP) from octacalcium phosphate (OCP) is the presence of a 1H resonance near 0 ppm. In HAP, this signal arises from structural hydroxyl groups located in columns aligned along the crystallographic c-axis. Because OCP lacks these hydroxyl columns, the corresponding resonance is absent or significantly reduced in intensity in OCP-containing materials. Conversely, the broad resonance at ∼12–15 ppm is a signature from OCP, originating from hydrogen phosphate groups (HPO4 2–) only present within OCP structures as reported previouly. −

The 1H NMR spectra of HAP-EDTA, HAP-CA, HAP-E6, and HAP-E13 (Figure b–e) show particularly intensified Ca–OH resonances compared to HAP-NC and HAP-DSS (Figure a and e). The coexistence of HAP and OCP in HAP-NC, HAP-EDTA, HAP-E6, and HAP-E13 is also reflected in the weak resonances at ∼13 ppm (Figure a, b, d, and e). Among all the samples, HAP-DSS (Figure e) shows the strongest OCP character, including diminished structural OH and pronounced HPO4 2– resonance, which is consistent with its XRD pattern (Figure e). HAP-CA is the only sample that shows no OCP-related features, which is also aligned with the XRD results (Figure d). The hydrothermal-treated samples (Figure b–d) show OCP resonances removed and structural OH peaks intensified remarkably.

Solid-State 31P MAS NMR of Synthesized HAP

The 31P CP-MAS NMR spectra demonstrated the evolution of phosphate environments during phase transformation from OCP to HAP, as displayed in Figure . The 31P CP-MAS spectrum of HAP-DSS (Figure a) exhibits a typical OCP pattern, with four resonances at 3.6, 3.2, 1.9, and −0.5 ppm, which is in agreement with the previously reported chemical shifts of OCP. , HAP-EDTA displays a clear HAP component present at ∼2.8 ppm besides the OCP resonances prior to the hydrothermal treatment, indicating it contains a mixture of HAP and OCP (Figure b). The multiple OCP resonances coalesce into a single broad resonance at ∼2.9 ppm, confirming the completion of phase transformation. Because HAP-EDTA, HAP-CA, HAP-E6, and HAP-E13 exhibit similar crystallographic profiles based on XRD and 1H NMR, HAP-EDTA was selected to be a representative model to demonstrate the phase transformation of these types of HAP products.

Discussion

In physiological environments, OCP formation is generally inhibited, although it appears to be kinetically favored under certain laboratory conditions. For example, in physiological environments, the presence of fluoride ions seems to be a factor that promotes the direct deposition of HAP, suppressing the formation of metastable calcium phosphate intermediates. A comparative analysis of HAP synthesized in the presence of various polycarboxylate compounds indicates that EDTA, citrate, and polyglutamate more effectively stabilize HAP, whereas (DSS)4 favors the formation of OCP-rich products. These differences are most plausibly attributed to distinct ligand assembly modes at the mineral–solution interface, which selectively stabilize different crystal surfaces and thereby bias growth along specific crystallographic orientations. ,

Surface stabilization effects of biomolecules are frequently rationalized in terms of geometric correspondence between ionic functional groups and surface-exposed lattice ions, often supported by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. − For example, Yang et al. demonstrated that the carboxyl groups in the SpSpEEEEEEEE motif, predicted to adopt an α-helical conformation in bone sialoprotein, align favorably with the equilateral arrangement of surface calcium ions on the HAP (001) plane, which exhibit an average Ca–Ca distance of approximately 6.29 Å (Figure a). Related surface recognition mechanisms have been proposed for osteocalcin by Hoang et al., while Hauschka et al. reported that Gla residues remain in a random coil conformation in the absence of calcium ions but adopt an α-helical structure upon Ca2+ binding. These simulation models provide molecular-level rationale for the templating effects of polyglutamate. However, most of the models lack direct experimental evidence that directly resolves the assembly conformations of these molecules within the protein-mineral composites. While direct spectroscopic evidence of ligand binding is not provided in the present study, the proposed coordination modes are consistent with prior reports of carboxylate–calcium interactions on hydroxyapatite surfaces.

Schematic representations of polycarboxylate–calcium interactions proposed to underlie ligand-dependent crystallization pathways. (a) Carboxylate groups from EDTA, citrate, and poly­(E) are shown in trans-extended conformations coordinating surface Ca2+ ions on the hydroxyapatite (HAP) (001) lattice plane. (b) Poly­(DSS) is depicted binding to linearly arranged Ca2+ ions on the epitaxial (100) lattice planes of HAP and OCP, with characteristic Ca–Ca spacings of approximately 6.9 and 9.5 Å, respectively.

Smaller polycarboxylate molecules such as EDTA and citrate, which possess simpler chelating structures, have also been shown to stabilize the HAP (001) plane. For citrate, the spacing between terminal carboxylate groups is approximately 7 Å, while for EDTA, the average separation between carboxylate groups on different amine centers is approximately 8 Å. The close spacing and conformational flexibility of these carboxylate groups enable effective coordination to the densely spaced Ca2+ on the HAP (001) lattice plane (Figure a). Similarly, E6 and E13, which also feature closely spaced carboxylates, both appear to preferentially stabilize the HAP (001) surface. The assembly structure of these polyglutamate molecules has yet to be fully elucidated; however, these observations suggest that precise geometric matching may not be the sole determinant. Instead, carboxylate density, ligand concentration, and effective saturation of surface calcium coordination likely contribute to stabilization of this facet.

In contrast, calcium ions are exposed on the epitaxial OCP (100) and HAP (100) planes in linear arrays with average Ca–Ca distances of ∼9.5 Å and ∼6.9 Å. , The DSS domain is often modeled as a β-sheet-like conformation, in which the aspartyl carboxyl groups are separated by ∼9–10 Å. This geometric correspondence provides a plausible explanation for the preferential stabilization of OCP (100) surfaces by (DSS)4, as schematically illustrated in Figure b. As a phosphorylated analogue of (DSS) n , PPn is proposed to adopt a β-sheet-like conformation with trans-extended side chains. In this configuration, PPn was found to preferentially passivate the OCP (010) surface, where the exposed calcium ions exhibit interatomic distances of ∼6.9 Å, matching the spacing of the anionic side chains. ,,

Taken together, comparisons across polycarboxylate ligands with varying carboxylate densities and architectures indicate that the effective spacing of anionic groups is a key determinant governing selective stabilization of calcium phosphate crystal planes. Elucidating how these bioinspired polycarboxylates assemble at mineral interfaces during different stages of mineralization remains an important target for future atomic-level experimental and computational studies. While this study provides comparative insights across ligand classes, further systematic variation of ligand architecture will be required to fully decouple the roles of carboxylate spacing, density, and molecular conformation.

Conclusion

The templating behaviors of bioinspired polycarboxylate compoundsincluding EDTA, citrate, E6, E13, and (DSS)4were systematically compared to elucidate their roles in directing calcium phosphate mineralization. Scanning electron microscopy, powder X-ray diffraction, and multinuclear solid-state NMR were collectively employed to evaluate the morphology, crystallinity, and local atomic environments of the resulting calcium phosphate nanomaterials.

These syntheses produced nanosheets or nanoribbons with distinct crystallinity and phase compositions. EDTA, citrate, and polyglutamate preferentially stabilized hydroxyapatite (HAP) with enhanced (001) surface exposure, whereas the presence of (DSS)4 favored the formation of octacalcium phosphate (OCP). OCP-containing products were converted to phase-pure HAP by hydrothermal treatment, as evidenced by the disappearance of the OCP reflections in XRD patterns. This transformation was further confirmed by the convergence of multiple phosphate environments into a single resonance and the loss of hydrogen phosphate signals characteristic of OCP in the 1H and 1,3P NMR spectra. The distinct templating behaviors observed for these polycarboxylate ligands are attributed to differences in carboxylate spacing and ligand assembly, which govern ionic interactions with surface-exposed Ca2+ ions on HAP and OCP lattice planes. Overall, this study demonstrates how tailored polycarboxylate motifs can selectively modulate calcium phosphate nucleation pathways and facet stabilization, providing a mechanistic framework for future molecular design and simulation efforts in biomineralization and bioinspired material synthesis.
