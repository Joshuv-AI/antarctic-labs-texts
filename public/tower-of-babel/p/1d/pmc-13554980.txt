Understanding dementia in Filipino Americans: risk factors, research gaps, and the role of community‐based participatory research
Authors: Rochelle‐Jan Dionisio Reyes, Ariel Guevarra, Eugenie Mamuyac, Alexander Ivan B Posis, Laura María Calderón Cuevas, Luis Martinez Roman, John Matthew Suntay, Lolita Kintanar, Michelle Caunca, Boon Lead Tee, Charles Windon, Serggio Lanata, Ivan Bondoc, Eduardo Europa, Jessica de Leon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFilipino Americans are the third‐largest Asian American, Native Hawaiians, and Pacific Islander (AANHPI) group in the United States. Previous studies showed that they have high incident rates of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) compared to other AANHPI groups. However, they are often aggregated into the “Asian” category in AD/ADRD research, masking distinct risk and mitigating factors. To date, no studies have directly explored AD/ADRD factors in Filipino Americans, a community that has unique characteristics from life experiences, immigration, historical and cultural context, and differential burden of genetic risk. In this perspective, we review known AD/ADRD risk and mitigating factors through the lens of the National Institute on Aging's Health Disparities Research Framework, spanning environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological domains, and discuss their relevance to the Filipino American community. By identifying current knowledge gaps and contextualizing AD/ADRD risk and resilience through this framework, we aim to improve understanding of the complex, multifactorial pathways contributing to AD/ADRD outcomes across communities. Lastly, we discuss our experiences and strategies in the recruitment of Filipino Americans for AD/ADRD research studies, outlining challenges, successes, and future directions.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, Asian Americans, community‐based participatory research, dementia, Filipino Americans

INTRODUCTION

Asian Americans, Native Hawaiians, and Pacific Islanders (AANHPI) are the fastest‐growing racial group in the United States. Those aged 65 and older expected to triple from 2.5 million in 2019 to 7.9 million in 2060. 1 The AANHPI group includes more than 50 heterogeneous communities that differ in language, culture, and sociodemographic backgrounds. Therefore, AANHPI individuals likely vary in their Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) risk and mitigating factors. 2

Filipino Americans account for 4.6 million people in the United States, making them the third‐largest AANHPI group, with approximately 38% residing in California, the largest state‐level Filipino American population in the country. 3 Nearly 2.1 million Filipino Americans live in urban, metropolitan regions, including about 790,000 people in the San Francisco and Los Angeles areas. 3 Filipino American households have a median income of $106,400 per year, and 95% have some form of healthcare insurance coverage. 3

There are important generational differences in immigration status among Filipino Americans; about 52% were born in the United States. 3 US‐born Filipino Americans have a median age of 21.8 years old, with 40% of the population under age 18 and only 4% aged 65 and older 3 ; 86% speak English, whereas only 10% speak Tagalog. 3 About one‐third have some college education, and another third hold a bachelor's degree. 3 The largest occupational group (17%) works in management, business, or financial positions. 3

These demographics differ from those of the 48% of Filipino Americans who are immigrants and whose median age is 51.3 years old, with 26% aged 65 or older. 3 Language use also differs: 76% speak Tagalog, and only 16% speak English, 42% hold a bachelor's degree, and 21% work as healthcare practitioners or in technical positions. 3 Consequently, the population over 65, and therefore most at risk for AD/ADRD, is more likely to be immigrant and Tagalog‐speaking.

AD/ADRD studies often group Filipino Americans with other AANHPI populations. While the AANHPI group as a whole may have the lowest AD/ADRD incidence rates compared to other racial and ethnic communities, a study that disaggregated Asian American subgroups showed heterogeneity in AD/ADRD incidence and prevalence, with Filipino Americans having the highest rate across Chinese American, Filipino American, Japanese American, and South Asian American groups. 2 However, this study included only three East/Southeast Asian American communities and aggregated five South Asian communities into one group due to limited sample size, which may have reduced the precision and reliability of the incidence estimates. 2 The authors also noted limited healthcare utilization data, missing dementia subtype information, and a lack of culturally and linguistically tailored assessment tools. 2 The lack of tailored tools, combined with a limited number of bilingual or bicultural providers, may contribute to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis of AD/ADRD in AANHPI populations, including Filipino Americans. Consistent with this, a national study of Medicare beneficiaries found that Asian American individuals were less likely than White beneficiaries to receive a timely dementia diagnosis and received fewer diagnostic evaluations, 4 highlighting obstacles in dementia recognition that may affect those who are bilingual or have limited English proficiency, which is a risk factor for AD/ADRD. 5

Despite their high rates of AD/ADRD and large US population, Filipino Americans remain underrepresented in AD/ADRD studies, even within an AANHPI context. A recent scoping review found that Filipino Americans were represented in only eight of 177 published AD/ADRD studies that included AANHPI subgroups and that they were underrepresented or omitted in the design of several large AANHPI studies. 6 This lack of representation limits understanding of community‐specific AD/ADRD risks within the broader AANHPI population, highlighting the need to better understand the factors that impact AD/ADRD risk and resilience in this group and those it may share with others.

To date, no studies have directly explored AD/ADRD risk factors in Filipino Americans, and it remains unknown why they have higher AD/ADRD incidence than other AANHPI subgroups. In this Perspective, we synthesize current literature on known AD/ADRD risk and mitigating factors relevant to Filipino Americans, offer insight from our own community‐based work, and discuss future directions.

RISK AND MITIGATING FACTORS INFLUENCING AD/ADRD

Many established and emerging AD/ADRD risk factors are modifiable. In this section, we examine potential risk and mitigating AD/ADRD factors in the Filipino American community through the lens of the National Institute on Aging's Health Disparities Research Framework, which organizes disparities into environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological categories (Figure 1). 7

Risk factors and mitigating factors for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) in the Filipino American community within the context of the National Institute on Aging's Health Disparities framework of environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological factors.

Environmental risk factors

Environmental factors include an individual's geographical and political factors, socioeconomic factors, and healthcare access. Several are relevant to the Filipino American community.

Air pollution

Air pollution is an emerging AD/ADRD risk factor. 8 A systematic review found evidence linking higher air pollutant exposure to increased dementia risk, 9 drawing on studies with exposure periods ranging from approximately 1 year to long‐term cumulative exposure. Although findings on duration exposure were inconsistent and populations were limited to older adults, the overall evidence supported an association between greater air pollution exposure and dementia risk.

For Filipino Americans, exposure to air pollutants can vary with life experience and migration history. Approximately two million Filipino Americans have immigrated to the United States, 3 where average fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentration is 8.6 µg/m3 per year, above the World Health Organization's guideline limit of 5 µg/m3 (Reference S1). Filipino American immigrants may have experienced even higher levels of air pollution in the Philippines, which has an average PM2.5 concentration of 14.8 µg/m3 and ranks 74th of 134 countries for air quality (Reference S2). Urbanicity further influences exposure, as urban areas generally have higher PM2.5 concentrations than rural areas, and many Filipino Americans reside in urban centers such as Los Angeles, San Francisco, and New York City. 3 Given the growing evidence linking air pollution to AD/ADRD risk, 8 elevated exposure in both the Philippines and the United States may contribute to AD/ADRD and cognitive outcomes in Filipino American immigrants.

Limited access and utilization of green spaces

Green spaces, such as parks and nature reserves, are another emerging environmental factor for AD/ADRD. A recent study associated areas with more green spaces with better cognitive functioning and overall cognitive health. 10 Filipino American immigrants may have limited access to green spaces both in the Philippines and the United States. Satellite mapping of Metro Manila from 2005 to 2023 showed a significant decrease in green space over 18 years due to megacity expansion and urbanization. 11 In US urban cities, green spaces are not accessible or programmed equitably across neighborhoods, particularly low‐income communities and communities of color, and 100 million people in the United States lack access to parks within 10 min of home (Reference S3).

Studies on green space use across racial and ethnic communities are limited. A 2016 study of US neighborhood parks reported that Asian Americans, combined with other non‐Hispanic races, had the lowest park use relative to neighborhood representation (0.42), compared with Hispanic (1.54), White (0.86), and Black (0.95) communities, 12 which the authors attributed to social context of park use and park features rather than a specific identified cause. 12 Because Asian Americans were aggregated and represented by a small sample, it is unclear whether these findings generalize to Filipino Americans specifically, for whom no research to date has investigated green space access, proximity, or use and its connection to cognition. Given the general evidence that green spaces may protect cognitive health, future research should examine whether they represent modifiable risk or protective factors for AD/ADRD in this community.

Influence of occupation on AD/ADRD risk

Research on occupation suggests differences in AD/ADRD outcomes across occupational groups, independent of educational attainment, 13 with higher cognitive demand occupations (i.e., those involving data analysis, creative thinking, and interpretation) potentially protective against AD/ADRD. 13 Occupational patterns of Filipino American immigrants have shifted depending on when they entered the United States: Those arriving in the early 1900s were mostly laborers in agriculture and domestic service, 14 while more recent migration periods starting in the 1960s have included a higher percentage in management, business, science, and arts‐related professions alongside continued clerical, service, and domestic work. 14 As the number of aging Filipino American immigrants increases, many of today's older adults likely belonged to earlier waves with predominantly manual or unskilled, lower‐cognitive‐demand work. In addition, many more recent Filipino Americans immigrants work in healthcare, particularly nursing, 14 which often involves shift or night work, a reported risk factor for AD/ADRD (Reference S4). These differences in occupations and occupational complexity point to the need for further study of their role in AD/ADRD outcomes in this community.

Adverse pre‐ and post‐immigration life experiences

Immigrants generally have a higher prevalence and risk of AD/ADRD, including undiagnosed AD/ADRD. One study reported that immigrants identifying as non‐Hispanic White, Hispanic, and Other (including AANHPI and American Indian individuals) all had higher dementia prevalence than their US‐born counterparts, 5 potentially reflecting differences in life‐course experiences before and after immigration. 5 Filipino Americans may have experienced stressors, including adverse childhood experiences, World War II trauma, post‐war economic challenges, 1970s martial law, ongoing human rights violations, vulnerability to severe climates, and natural disasters, with exposure varying by year of immigration. 15 Adverse pre‐immigration experiences may be associated with AD/ADRD risk 16 and early‐life adversity, stress, and sociocultural experiences are important to understanding cognitive aging disparities within Asian American populations generally (Reference S5). After immigration, Filipino Americans may face adjustment challenges 15 and social isolation 5 along with protective factors such as improved socioeconomic status. 3 , 15 Therefore, their lifetime exposure to AD/ADRD‐relevant adverse or protective factors likely varies by immigration timing. The interplay among pre‐ and post‐immigration exposures and their influence on AD/ADRD warrants further research.

Sociocultural risk factors

Cultural, social, and psychological factors are critical contributors to AD/ADRD health disparities. Studying the unique sociocultural backgrounds of Filipino Americans can clarify how their lived experiences impact AD/ADRD development and trajectory.

Immigration‐related stressors and barriers

Incidence rates of AD/ADRD may be higher in foreign‐born than US‐born Filipino Americans, 17 with increased risk among immigrants potentially influenced by diet, language barriers, acculturation and acculturative stress, social isolation, healthcare access, and help‐seeking behaviors shaped by stigma and dementia‐related knowledge, 5 , 18 as well as epigenetic changes to biological mechanisms, and possibly contributing to diabetes in immigrants (Reference S6). With 47% of Filipino Americans in the United States identifying as immigrants, understanding these mechanisms is crucial. 3

Acculturation‐related risk factors

Acculturation, the complex process by which immigrants adapt to a new host country's culture, traditions, and practices, 15 has been associated with worse cognitive performance, reduced integrity of cerebral white matter, and AD/ADRD perceptions inconsistent with current biomedical knowledge when acculturation levels are low. 19 , 20 , 21 Acculturation can also indirectly influence AD/ADRD risk through other factors, including cardiometabolic conditions and behavioral changes such as diet and smoking. 18 , 22 Acculturative stress, which arises from adapting to a host culture, can also affect cognitive health. 23 Filipino Americans have reported forms of acculturative stress, including racial discrimination and workplace management insensitivity, both associated with poorer physical and mental health. 24 The connection between acculturation and cognitive decline has been studied in other immigrant populations, including Hispanic and Latino communities, 19 , 20 but it remains unexplored in Filipino Americans.

Negative stigma and outcomes based in cultural beliefs

Community stigmas 6 , 21 , 25 and low levels of help‐seeking behaviors 26 , 27 may adversely affect diagnosis and treatment of AD/ADRD and its risk factors in Filipino Americans. Compared with two other Asian American groups, Filipino Americans have been reported to hold more misconceptions about AD/ADRD, such as beliefs that the disease inevitably leads to death and is associated with increased aggression, 21 which may delay diagnosis or treatment‐seeking. 25 One study of overseas Filipino workers noted only 8% sought traditional health providers, while 36% self‐medicated for illnesses. 27

Another cultural belief that may impact AD/ADRD care is colonial mentality, an internalized belief in the inferiority of one's own culture, widely attributed to nearly 400 years of Spanish (1565 to 1898) and United States (1898 to 1946) colonial rule and Japanese occupation (1942 to 1945) in the Philippines. The lasting impact of this prolonged foreign control continues to shape this perception of cultural and ethnic inferiority. 22 This mindset can heighten psychosocial stress and affect help‐seeking behaviors and healthcare interactions. Filipino Americans report high levels of psychosocial distress but low levels of help‐seeking, 26 while also exhibiting diverse resilience and coping strategies 23 , 26 that require further exploration in the context of AD/ADRD.

Filipino Americans may adhere to core Filipino values to varying degrees, and these may negatively affect attitudes and interactions related to healthcare and research participation. A belief that memory loss is part of “normal aging” may make people less likely to seek care for cognitive symptoms. 6 The concept of hiya, a sense of shame or a loss of propriety, 15 can affect attitudes toward AD/ADRD diagnosis and treatment. 6 , 25 A strong wish not to be a burden to others, associated with patitiyaga, or the need to personally persevere and endure, and lakas ng loob, or the need to be strong and resilient in the face of hardship, 15 may reduce help‐seeking from family and community and could contribute to social isolation, a known dementia risk factor. Filipino Americans may also hold the value of bahala na, sometimes translated as “come what may,” a surrender to fate or God's will. Those who adhere to this mindset often approach life with minimal worry and limited sense of personal control, willingly accepting situations as they unfold. 15 In the context of AD/ADRD, this mindset may shape health‐seeking behaviors and delay recognition, diagnosis, and treatment. Overall, these values impact Filipino Americans’ perspectives on AD/ADRD diagnoses, treatment, and care. Dementia care across AANHPI populations should account for population‐specific cultural beliefs such as these.

Psychological stressors

Filipino Americans may face a unique combination of psychological stressors, an established dementia risk factor. 26 These include racial discrimination, with some reports indicating greater perceived discrimination than in other Asian American groups, 26 often tied to being treated as a foreigner (even if US‐born) or a model minority or to day‐to‐day incidents related to race and ethnicity. 22 , 26 They also face high rates of employment‐related stress. 24 These stressors may not only affect cognitive health directly but could also exacerbate other biological, sociocultural, and behavioral AD/ADRD risk factors, such as cardiometabolic conditions, sleep, substance use, diet, and physical activity. 22

Behavioral risk factors

Behavioral factors, including lifestyle‐ and health‐related behaviors, are associated with cognitive decline and AD/ADRD. They are important to study in Filipino Americans, whose patterns of risk and resilience may differ from those in other communities.

Low levels of help‐seeking behaviors

Filipino Americans tend to have low levels of healthcare‐related help‐seeking 26 , 27 and utilization 2 , 27 for physical and mental health conditions, likely due to logistical and cultural factors, including insurance access, mistrust, cultural norms, and a preference for care and support through family, friends, and/or faith rather than health professionals. 26 Although further research is needed on help‐seeking in the context of AD/ADRD specifically, these patterns likely also affect timely treatment of AD/ADRD risk factors and symptoms (Reference S7).

Sleep‐related risk factors

Sleep duration, quality, and efficiency can affect daily activities and have also been associated with dementia. 8 A recent study of foreign‐born individuals found Filipino Americans had the highest prevalence rate of short sleep (under the recommended 8 h) across non‐Hispanic White, Black, Hispanic and Latino, and other Asian subgroups, 28 with no moderating effect of length of US residence. The authors suggest that broader structural and sociocultural factors (e.g., immigration and acculturation‐related conditions, socioeconomic status, language barriers, systemic racism, discrimination, and potential underreporting) may contribute to these disparities. 28 Filipino Americans also report poorer sleep quality than Black, Chinese, and other Asian groups, 28 , 29 including more difficulty staying asleep thank non‐Hispanic Black and other Asian groups but not non‐Hispanic White participants. 28 The extent to which these sleep disparities contribute to AD/ADRD risk remains unclear, particularly given the high representation of Filipino Americans in shift‐based occupations such as healthcare. 14

Substance use

Long term, heavy substance use, including smoking and alcohol use, contributes to cognitive impairment and AD/ADRD. 8 Filipino Americans report high rates of alcohol use and smoking prevalence comparable to aggregated AANHPI rates, 30 though lower cigarette use than Korean Americans, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islanders, and multicultural groups. 30 These rates may reflect psychosocial stressors, coping mechanisms, historical context, and cultural norms, including inuman (social drinking gatherings), a well‐recognized practice in Filipino culture (Reference S8).

Poor nutrition and physical inactivity

Diet and physical activity interact with other AD/ADRD risk factors, including cardiometabolic and sociocultural factors. Filipino American diets can be high in fat, sodium, and sugar and low in fruits and vegetables 22 ; for example, 75% of Filipino Americans reported adding salt to food in one study. 31 These patterns may reflect diets in the Philippines, where processed foods, foods high in saturated fats, and low fruit and vegetable intake are common due to urbanization, colonization, and limited access to healthy foods, 32 as well as changes from immigration and acculturation to Western diets, which are linked to higher sugar and fat intake. 33 Filipino Americans also report low adherence to physical activity guidelines, 31 with only 24.5% meeting the recommended 150+ min of moderate exercise per week in one study. 31 These behavioral factors can increase risk of cardiometabolic conditions that are themselves risk factors for AD/ADRD. 22

Biological risk factors

Physiological and differential burden of genetic risk factors are crucial biological variables that are important in understanding AD/ADRD risk and resilience. Although previous research assessed cardiometabolic and cerebrovascular disease in Filipino Americans, their association with AD/ADRD and the contribution of genetic risk factors remain understudied in this community.

Presence of cardiometabolic conditions

Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity are established AD/ADRD risk factors, and Filipino Americans disproportionately experience higher rates of all four compared to non‐Hispanic Whites and other Asian subgroups. 22 , 34 Reported hypertension prevalence ranges from 30% to 67%, 31 , 35 with one study finding a rate of 41.3%, among the highest compared to White, Hispanic, Chinese, and South Asian communities. 36 Diabetes prevalence is high relative to other AANHPI groups and non‐Hispanic White groups, higher than or comparable to non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Native American communities 37 , 38 and lower only compared to Pacific Islanders. 35 Dyslipidemia is also highly prevalent, with Filipino Americans showing the highest rate compared to non‐Hispanic White, Native Hawaiians, and Pacific Islanders and six Asian American subgroups. 35 The proportion who are overweight or obese ranges from 44% to 66% by international body mass index (BMI) guidelines and up to 60% to 80% under World Health Organization Asian recommendations, 33 though findings are mixed: Some studies report lower obesity odds compared to Non‐Hispanic Whites, 39 while others find higher prevalence relative to other Asian subpopulations. 34 , 35 This high prevalence of cardiometabolic conditions may elevate AD/ADRD risk and should be further studied in this population.

Cerebrovascular disease burden

Stroke, a known AD/ADRD risk factor, has a prevalence of around 2% in Filipino Americans, 34 , 40 with higher odds than among Chinese Americans and odds comparable to those of other Asian subgroups. 40 These elevated rates may relate to higher rates of cardiometabolic conditions; one study found Filipino American stroke patients had higher rates of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia than non‐Hispanic White stroke patients. 41 The contribution of stroke to AD/ADRD risk in Filipino Americans remains largely unexplored.

Genetic risk factors

Genetic risk factors for ADRD have been little studied in Filipino Americans. Population‐based studies in the Philippines found lower rates of apolipoprotein E ε4 allele, a major AD/ADRD risk allele, 42 and its frequency did not differ between those with and without Alzheimer's dementia, 43 suggesting other genetic factors may contribute to the community's high AD/ADRD rates. Given the high burden of cardiometabolic conditions in Filipino Americans, genes associated with these conditions may represent another pathway to AD/ADRD risk. 21 Emerging genome‐wide studies are beginning to characterize genetic contributors to cardiometabolic health in this population, which can inform future work linking cardiometabolic disease to AD/ADRD genetically (Reference S9).

Mitigating factors

It is equally important to consider potential protective factors. Filipino Americans have high rates of educational attainment (50% with a bachelor's degree or higher), which can delay AD/ADRD onset, 3 , 8 along with relatively high income compared to other Asian American subgroups, English proficiency, health literacy, and health insurance rates. 3 These factors may mitigate risk through improved healthcare access and AD/ADRD knowledge. 5 , 25

While certain cultural beliefs may delay AD/ADRD recognition and diagnosis (Section 2.2.3), other Filipino cultural values may instead foster resilience and mitigate risk. Values such as kapwa and pakikisama, which emphasize togetherness, social harmony, collective well‐being, and deference and respect for elders, reinforce strong family and community ties and prioritize caring for others, including loved ones with AD/ADRD. Relatedly, the value of utang na loob, a norm of reciprocal debt, 14 , 15 may lead younger generations to feel obligated to care for loved ones with AD/ADRD.

More broadly, coping and resilience responses are shaped not only by cultural beliefs but also by environmental and sociocultural factors such as immigration, acculturation, and social support. 23 , 26 Among Filipino Americans, higher resilience has been associated with lower acculturative stress, consistent with previous findings of resilience being a protective factor against acculturative stressors. 23 Filipino Americans may manage stressors through distinct coping strategies, including rationalization, mind‐body connection practices, seeking community support, religious coping, and escape–avoidance (i.e., distancing from or not confronting the stressor). 26 Given that coping and resilience may mitigate AD/ADRD risk and cognitive decline, 44 their role in AD/ADRD prevention and progression in this community warrants further study.

Bilingualism is also emerging as a protective factor against AD/ADRD onset. 45 A meta‐analysis of 21 studies found bilingualism moderately protective in delaying symptom onset in AD (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.32), with weaker effects on disease incidence (SMD = 0.10). 45 With at least 33% of Filipino Americans proficient in English and speaking another language at home, this population is well suited for studying bilingualism's protective effects in AD/ADRD. 3

Current efforts and future directions

Current efforts

Our team utilizes community‐based participatory research (CBPR) methods previously used in both AD/ADRD research and in research with AANHPI populations. 46 , 47 Specifically, our efforts are informed by the CBPR Conceptual Model's four interrelated domains: context, partnerships, research, and outcomes. 48 The preceding section contributes to the context domain by synthesizing evidence on biological, social, cultural, and environmental factors that may influence AD/ADRD risk among Filipino Americans. The following section highlights our current CBPR efforts in the partnerships, research, and outcome domains through collaborations with Filipino American communities to develop culturally and linguistically tailored AD/ADRD research and inform future directions (Figure 2).

Examples of our current efforts to address AD/ADRD in Filipino American community within context of Community‐Based Participatory Research (CBPR) Conceptual Model's framework of context, partnerships, research, and outcomes.

Partnerships

Our team has partnered with over 20 community stakeholders and organizations across the San Francisco Bay Area (Figure 3) to address barriers to research participation, including language and cultural considerations, mistrust, and limited knowledge regarding the purpose of research. We maintain close collaborations with organizations, such as the Filipino Service Providers Network and Canon Kip Senior Center to connect with service providers, caregivers, and health centers serving Filipino Americans, and with academic and research institutions for methodological expertise.

Types and number of community organizations, stakeholders, and research partners we collaborate with to support education, research, and diagnosis of AD/ADRD in Filipino Americans.

Community members partner at all stages of our AD/ADRD research, including planning, implementation, data interpretation, analysis, and dissemination. Our group includes bilingual English/Tagalog, bicultural Filipino American/Canadian members across all roles, including students, research coordinators, data analysts, and faculty. This has been integral in building rapport and delivering a culturally and linguistically tailored experience. Team members have also built expertise through formal and informal training in professional translation, working with diverse populations, and the linguistic, sociocultural, and historical context of Filipino Americans.

Overall, the CBPR conceptual model is culturally fitting because it aligns with Filipino American values of working together to support and serve our kababayan, or fellow Filipinos, while respecting the wisdom and expertise of our elders.

Research

Our community partnerships directly informed the design of culturally responsive recruitment and research practices. Social media, particularly Facebook, has been a helpful recruitment source, and in collaboration with the Community Outreach Program at UCSF, we provide educational events on brain health, including presentations, tabling, and dance and visual arts workshops. For example, we provide weekly music‐based workshops at senior centers serving the Filipino American community, where participants engage in singing, percussion and rhythm activities, improvisation, and dance. These engagements foster trust and community, proving to be fruitful recruitment methods, often after several points of contact. We have also found success through snowball recruitment and participant referrals, which may reflect participants’ positive research experiences.

In comparison, recruitment through electronic health records has been less successful, as demographic fields historically have not allowed disaggregation of individuals self‐identifying as Asian, making it difficult to target Filipino Americans specifically. Because records typically capture only preferred language and many Filipino Americans are bilingual and/or report English as preferred, identifying suitable research participants by spoken language is also difficult. Lastly, this approach limits the ability to build rapport before introducing research opportunities.

Community partnerships have also informed the conduct of our research visits. We address participants using honorifics and titles of respect (e.g., ate, kuya, tita, tito, lola, lolo) and offering refreshments as a sign of hospitality. Because underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of AD/ADRD may disproportionately affect AANHPI populations, including Filipino Americans, due in part to limited availability of culturally and linguistically tailored cognitive assessment tools, researchers have developed such tools for diverse populations. 47 , 49 Our team developed the Culturally Adapted Tagalog‐language Screening (CATS) battery to improve the evaluation of cognitive function among Tagalog‐speaking Filipino Americans, 47 allowing Tagalog‐dominant individuals to participate with fewer linguistic and sociocultural barriers. Bilingual study staff can also code‐switch between English and Tagalog to optimize participant understanding, and we offer certain study visit components via telehealth to reduce participant burden.

Outcomes

Our CBPR efforts extend beyond the research visit to emphasize reciprocal dissemination and sustained engagement. Following each visit, participants meet with a neurologist to review their cognitive and imaging results and receive culturally tailored feedback, helping them better understand their cognitive health and ensuring research participation offers direct value. Beyond individual participants, we maintain ongoing engagement with the broader Filipino American community through an annual symposium that shares study updates, recognizes participants’ contributions. This also gives community members and partners a forum to ask questions, provide feedback, and inform future research directions, strengthening trust and continued investment in AD/ADRD research.

Our CBPR efforts have also contributed to community‐informed research initiatives, including the CATS battery, community‐engaged studies of Filipino American caregiving experiences, and initiatives to improve participation in clinical research. 47 , 50 We disseminate findings through peer‐reviewed publications, conference presentations, and educational talks. We collaborate with the Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, the Frontotemporal Dementia: Genes, Images and Emotions Program Project Grant, and the Brain Aging Network for Cognitive Health programs at UCSF to enrich Filipino American inclusion in their cohorts, facilitate exchange of community‐engaged research practices, and inform future directions for equitable AD/ADRD research. We also advocate for disaggregation of Asian American data to improve Filipino American visibility in AD/ADRD research.

Future directions

Despite growing efforts to characterize AD/ADRD risk and protective factors and increase Filipino American research participation, representation remains limited, and key literature gaps persist. While snowball recruitment and participant referrals align with community values and have been effective for building trust, these approaches may reinforce systematic biases by concentrating participation within existing social networks and may disproportionately capture specific sociocultural or behavioral experiences, limiting generalizability. Expanding recruitment approaches and geographic scope is critical to building a more representative study population and advancing knowledge of the factors shaping cognitive aging in Filipino Americans.

Efforts to broaden recruitment must be paired with a deeper examination of the factors shaping AD/ADRD risk in this community. Beyond the areas outlined in Section 2, additional contextual domains should be investigated. One is social isolation, particularly in the context of immigration, where separation from community and family may disrupt social support systems and influence downstream behavioral and sociocultural pathways relevant to cognitive health. Another is the examination of gender differences across sociocultural and behavioral factors, as gender may influence exposure to AD/ADRD‐related risk factors among Filipino Americans. For instance, substance use may differ by gender within the context of inuman, a culturally embedded and gendered social drinking practice.

CONCLUSION

Filipino Americans constitute one of the largest AANHPI groups, yet they remain understudied and underrepresented in AD/ADRD research. Investigating their environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological risk and mitigating factors is important, since these may differ from those of other populations. Future studies and expanded outreach efforts to improve Filipino Americans’ research participation can help inform more effective and culturally tailored approaches to AD/ADRD prevention, diagnosis, care, and treatment in this community.
