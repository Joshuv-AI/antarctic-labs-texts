Biofilm forming capacity, sanitizer tolerance, and genetic characterization of persistent and non-persistent Listeria monocytogenes from artisan cheese processing environments
Authors: Eurydice F. Aboagye, Emily C. Forauer, Aislinn J. Gilmour, Hannah L. Blackwell, Lara Cushman, Calleigh S. Herren, Pablo D. Farace, Sophia Denaro, Lily Felten, McKenzie Ballard, Andrea J. Etter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Listeria monocytogenes is known to colonize food production environments and cross-contaminate finished foods. We investigated 30 L. monocytogenes isolates collected from artisan raw milk cheese production facilities in Vermont from 2006 to 2008, Sixteen of which represented a putatively persistent ribotype (DUP-1042B) found in one facility over two years. To determine persistence mechanisms, we evaluated the whole genome of the isolates as well as their sanitizer tolerance and biofilm formation capacity. For a representative subset (six isolates), we evaluated their mature biofilm properties on stainless steel. Isolates of the putatively persistent ribotype all aligned into ST191 and were 0-6 Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) different, confirming they represented a persistent strain. SSI-1 and an enrichment of genes in DNA repair and biosynthetic pathways were also exclusively confirmed in the core and accessory genome respectively, of the persistent strain. However, no significant differences in sanitizer tolerance were found between persistent and non-persistent strains, and none of the 30 isolates carried sanitizer resistance genes (bcrABC or qacH). Some ST 191 isolates formed visually more complex biofilms as shown by SEM and maintained significantly higher biomass over five days on polystyrene but were indistinguishable from non-persistent strains over ten days on stainless steel. No clear persistence mechanism was thus identified, but evidence for biofilm formation was stronger in the ST191 isolates than in the non-persistent strains. Our results highlight the importance of regular environmental testing and strain typing for rapid detection of L. monocytogenes colonization attempts while they can still be removed without major renovations or equipment replacement.

Introduction

Listeria monocytogenes is a naturally sturdy bacterium that is facultatively anaerobic and capable of growing in broad temperature ranges, from as low as −1 °C to as high as 45 °C in some temperature-adapted strains [[1], [2], [3]]. It is, therefore, ubiquitous in the environment and common in food processing environments [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. L. monocytogenes is the causative agent of listeriosis, a rare form of bacteraemia, meningitis, and encephalitis, that has the highest hospitalization rate, and is the fourth-highest cause of death among foodborne pathogens in the United States [10]. When L. monocytogenes successfully colonizes and establishes persistence in a food environment, it is more likely to cross-contaminate a finished ready-to-eat product, and removing it can be extremely challenging [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]].

One of the most important pathways through which L. monocytogenes establishes persistence is biofilm formation [[16], [17], [18]]. Biofilms are complex microbial communities that are attached to a surface and embedded in a protective extracellular matrix [19]. The cells in this community develop an altered growth rate and transcriptome, leading to a uniquely stress-tolerant phenotype [19]. L. monocytogenes biofilms are known to have an increased ability to resist antimicrobial agents and sanitizers and an increased tolerance for high temperatures, low pH, low water activity, UV rays other food processing control mechanisms [17,19].

Perhaps because of this increased stress tolerance, biofilm-associated bacteria have been tied to numerous outbreaks. Cherifi et al. found that persistent pulsotypes of L. monocytogenes in Canadian slaughterhouses were more likely to be detected in food and human clinical cases than transient strains [20]. A six year-long listeriosis outbreak involving Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France and Sweden, was also traced back to a strain of L. monocytogenes which was found to be persistent in the premises of a fish smoking facility in Finland [21]. A small outbreak in Washington state was traced back to an L. monocytogenes strain that contaminated a milkshake machine in a hospital and persisted for a year [22]. There is, therefore, ample evidence of the risk of persistent strains of L. monocytogenes contaminating finished products to cause outbreaks.

L. monocytogenes persistence can be disproportionately devasting for artisanal dairy processors who, unlike their industrial-scale counterparts, are less likely to have the legal and marketing resources to recover from a mass product recall or the loss of consumer confidence [11,[23], [24], [25]]. Thus, outbreaks of listeriosis from these small operations hold not only significant public health concerns, but also the potential loss of important livelihoods and cultural identities tied to artisanal cheese making [11,25,26]. While a few studies have investigated the phenotypic and genotypic characteristics of persistent L. monocytogenes strains isolated from small-scale dairy operations [27,28], there is little data on isolates from raw milk processing environments, a niche but relevant group of processors [29,30].

D'Amico and Donelly [31], conducted environmental sampling of nine farmstead cheesemaking operations, all of whom produced raw milk cheese and obtained raw milk from their own animals on the same farm on which they were raised. The first sampling event, spanning three months, was conducted in 2006 [31] and found that one facility had a highly prevalent and recurring ribotype (ECORI 210-506-S-5/DUP-1042B). In 2008, most of these facilities were again sampled [32]. The researchers identified a few resident Listeria spp. strains which were recovered from multiple sites across all sampling events [32]. Most significantly, they identified a specific ribotype of L. monocytogenes (DUP-1042B) that was repeatedly isolated from the same facility (facility F in 2006 and Facility A in 2008) and at the same sampling sites (predominantly floors and drains) in both the first sampling event, and then two years later [31,32]. This strain persisted despite improved cleaning and sanitation regimens implemented within the facility and measures to decrease cross-contamination events between the farm and the processing area [32].

We inherited Cathy Donnelly's extensive L. monocytogenes collection, including strains from her 2006 and 2008 studies of artisan dairy environments [31,32], and we hypothesized that the DUP-1042B ribotypes isolates represented a persistent strain and were persisting through enhanced biofilm formation ability or sanitizer tolerance [8,9,17]. We therefore investigated the biofilm-forming capacity as well as the architecture of the resulting biofilms of the DUP-1042B ribotypes from the two D'amico and Donnelly studies [31,32] grown under nutrient rich (1X) and nutrient poor (1/20X) conditions and conducted whole genome sequencing to determine the genetic relationships and quaternary efflux pump carriage of these isolates.

Materials and methods

Storage, recovery and preparation of working stock of isolates

Frozen stocks of 30 L. monocytogenes isolates, stored in 25% glycerol and trypticase soy broth (TSB, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ) at −80 °C, were streaked onto Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, Difco, Life Technologies, Detroit, MI) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. An isolated colony was then selected from each to inoculate BHI broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 18-24 h. The overnight broth culture was added at a 1:1 ratio of culture to 50% glycerol in cryovials for storage at −80 °C as working stock for the duration of these experiments.

Microtiter assay for planktonic L. monocytogenes sanitizer tolerance

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for sanitizers was adapted from methods outlined by EUCAST and used by Wang et al. (“Determination of minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antibacterial agents by broth dilution,” 2003/08/01; [9]). Isolates were recovered from the working stock solutions by streaking 5 μL onto BHI agar and incubating at 37 °C overnight, after which a single isolated colony was cultured overnight at 37 °C in BHI broth. 100 μL of overnight broth cultures were then suspended in 1 mL sterile deionized water to a range of OD600 0.119-0.126 as read by the microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VT) for an average of 1.94 × 108 CFU/ml. The adjusted culture was then diluted 1:100 into 1 mL of 2x concentration BHI or 1/10x concentration BHI and further diluted with the addition of sanitizers, resulting in full strength or 1/20x BHI to approximate nutrient rich and nutrient poor conditions. All inoculations were carried out within 30 min of culture adjustments to maintain cell densities.

The 96-well plates were thus inoculated resulting in 100 μl each of 3200, 1600, 800, 400, 200, and 100 ppm sodium hypochlorite (SH; Ecolab XY-12 Disinfectant with Sodium Hypochlorite; Ecolab, Naperville, IL), or 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, and 3.125 ppm quaternary ammonium compound (QAC) sanitizer (J-512 quaternary ammonium sanitizer; Diversey, Fort Mill, SC), and 1X and 1/20X BHI, with negative and positive controls. Plates were read at hour 0 immediately following inoculation to determine baseline OD600, and again to measure growth following static incubation for 24 h at room temperature (22 °C). The means and standard deviations (SD) of OD600 of sterile background media were also measured to distinguish true growth from noise. Growth in a test well was thus defined as values greater than +3SD (of sterile media) when baseline readings (OD600 at 0 h) were subtracted from OD600 at 24 h. MIC was defined as the minimum concentration of sanitizer (ppm) which prevented visible growth of the L. monocytogenes test strain.

Standard crystal violet biofilm assay

Crystal violet attachment assays were performed as previously described [9]. Briefly, overnight cultures were diluted in BHI broth and adjusted to between OD600 0.05-0.10 using the BioTek Epoch spectrophotometer (Biotek, Winooski, Vermont). The adjusted culture for each isolate was then added in triplicate to the wells of three 96-well microplates. The negative control was prepared by adding sterile BHI broth to three wells in each plate. The microplates were then incubated at room temperature (22 °C) for one, three, or five days. Wells were washed to remove non-adherent cells prior to crystal violet staining. Absorbance was to determine crystal violet retention as an approximation of biomass generated. Experiments for each isolate under all test conditions were repeated on 4 separate occasions.

Evaluating sanitizer efficacy on mature L. monocytogenes biofilm

Preparation of culture

Frozen stocks of L. monocytogenes isolates were streaked onto BHI agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. An isolated typical colony was aseptically selected to inoculate BHI broth. Broth was incubated at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 18-24 h. Liquid culture was added to either full strength media (1X BHI) or reduced strength media (1/20X BHI) and then OD600 adjusted between 0.06 and 0.10, read on the BioTek Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (Biotek, Winooski, Vermont).

Preparation of stainless-steel coupons

Stainless-steel coupons (AISI 304, 1.2 mm thick, 10 mm diameter, machined by UVM Instrumentation and Model Facility) were soaked in acetone for 30 min to remove residues from manufacture, then scrubbed with constant agitation in liquid dish detergent for 5 min and rinsed five times with deionized water to remove soap residue, followed by a 70% ethanol rinse before autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min to sterilize. Sterile coupons were then aseptically transferred to empty sterile petri dishes prior to inoculation. After each experiment, coupons were autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min and washed and sterilized as previously described before being re-used.

Formation of biofilms

Petri dishes containing four coupons each were inoculated with 10 mL OD-adjusted liquid culture. Submerged coupons were then incubated statically at room temperature (22 °C) for 10 days. To prevent nutrient exhaustion, spent media was removed and BHI broth was replaced on days two, four, six, and eight of incubation. Experiments were repeated three times on separate days.

Sanitizer application and enumeration

After 10 days, the broth was removed from the Petri dish and coupons rinsed three times with 20 mL aliquots of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to remove unadhered cells. Rinsing was also done to remove the organic load from the growth media, thus simulating a pre-sanitization cleaning step. Four rinsed coupons were then transferred using sterile forceps to test tubes containing 1 mL of 0, 50, 100, or 200 ppm sanitizer. We wished to simulate real-world conditions of sanitizer misuse at sub-optimal concentrations (50 ppm and 100 ppm) on surfaces such as wet drains. After the manufacturer-recommended contact time of 60 s for QAC and 2 min for SH, Dey-Engley neutralizing broth was added to tubes to halt the action of the sanitizer. Sterile glass beads (2 mm, 1 ± .1g) were then added and vortexed for 2 min to remove adherent cells into suspension. Vortexing at high-speed with glass beads again simulated abrasive mechanisms used in cleaning surfaces in food processing environments. Samples were serially diluted in buffered peptone water, and spot plated onto BHI agar. Plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C prior to enumeration. The total surface area (Two circular faces and a cylindrical side) was determined as: 2×(πr2) + 2πr × thickness. Cell population within biofilms were thus reported as; Colonies per cm2 = Total Cells ÷ Surface Area.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

Two coupons, one treated with 200 ppm sanitizer (QAC or SH) and the other rinsed with sterile distilled water (control) were prepared for SEM imaging. Biofilm tissues were fixed in Karnovski's fixative (0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2) for 60 min at 4 °C. Coupons were then rinsed three times for 5 min at 4 °C in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, PH 7.2. Biofilms were then fixed with 1% osmium tetraoxide in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2 for 1 h at 4 °C. This was followed by another three-fold rise for 5 min at 4 °C in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer. The coupons in six well plates were then wrapped in parafilm and stored in a refrigerator. The next day, the coupons were rinsed once for 5 min in 0.05 M cacodylate buffer followed by a three-fold rinse for 5 min in double-deionized water (ddH20). Rinsed coupons were then incubated with 0.5% uranyl acetate in ddH20 for 1 h at room (22 °C) temperature in darkness (covered with a cardboard box). The samples were then serially dehydrated in ethanol as follows: 35% ethanol, 50% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 85% ethanol, 95% ethanol for 10 min each, and finally 100% ethanol three times for 5 min. The dehydrated samples were then critical point dried with liquid CO2 and mounted on an aluminum specimen holder with conductive paint and dried overnight, before sputter coating with AU/Pd and examining in the Jeol JSM-6060 SEM (Peabody, MA) at the UVM microscopy core.

Genomic analysis of potentially persistent L. monocytogenes from farmstead cheese production environments

Nanopore sequencing

DNA was extracted from overnight cultures of 30 L. monocytogenes using the phenol:chloroform extraction protocol. The extracted DNA was prepared for sequencing on the Oxford Minion sequencer (Oxford, United Kingdom), using the ligation kit and prepared for sequencing with standard Oxford nanopore protocols. Post-sequencing, genomes were trimmed for quality and assembled de novo using the long-read genome assembler Flye (v.2.9.1) [33]. Genome assembly quality was then assessed with BUSCO (v.5.4.4) [34], polished with Medaka (v.1.7.2) [35] and then run through BUSCO again to reassess assemblies quality post-polishing.

Illumina sequencing

The Vermont Department of Health performed DNA extraction library preparation with Nextera DNA Flex and generated paired-end reads with an Illumina MiSeq instrument in 2022 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA). These data were submitted through Pulsenet to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) website. The paired-end Illumina reads were downloaded with SRA-toolkit v.2.9.6 with the fastq-dump (split-reads option). The sequence quality was analyzed with Fast-QC v.0.11.3 to determine coverage using the reported read length, number of reads, and L. monocytogenes reference genome (NC_003210.1) size. Draft genomes were assembled with SPAdes v.3.15.5 [36].

Hybrid assembly: short-read and long-read genomes were combined using Unicycler v.0.5.0 [37]. All isolates except for FML-007 assembled into hybrid genomes; FML-007 failed at the contig assembly stage.

In Silico Genotyping. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was performed with MLST v.2.0.9 on both Illumina fastq files and hybrid assembly files [38]. The MLST classification was used to determine the lineage with schemes available through the Institut Pasteur [39]. Typing of extra-chromosomal features was performed with PlasmidFinder v.2.1 [40]. Antimicrobial and stress resistant genotypes were detected with the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance feature on NCBI and ResFinder [41]. These searches were not specific to L. monocytogenes gene databases, so ubiquitous genes in the L. monocytogenes genome that confer intrinsic resistance to antimicrobials (ex. fosX) were excluded from the reported results [42]. L. monocytogenes virulence genes of interest inlA, inlB, and prfA, were identified with VirulenceFinder v.2.0.3 [43]. The presence or absence of inlB was noted directly from the output hits. Nucleotide mutations in prfA were detected with a BLAST search with the reference gene; isolates with base mutations were tested for amino acid changes with the Expasy translate tool (“Expasy Translate Tool,”). The Expasy translate tool was also used to detect premature stop codons in inlA, which were then characterized based on position in the gene (“Expasy Translate Tool,”; [44]). We also compared our genotyping results with the virulence and sanitizer tolerance predictions generated by the new tool ListPredv.0.2.0 [45] for virulence and sanitizer tolerance.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) Analysis: Illumina paired-end reads were mapped to the reference genome L. monocytogenes strain H34, and variants were called with Snippy v4.6 [46] to generate a core-genome SNP alignment. Isolates were run in batch using snippy-multi, and recombinant regions were identified and masked with Gubbins [47]. The recombination-filtered polymorphic sites alignment was converted into a core SNP alignment using snp-sites [48], and a maximum-likelihood phylogeny was inferred with FastTree v2 [49] under a GTR nucleotide substitution model. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL v6 [50].

Pan-genome analysis: Draft genomes for all 30 L. monocytogenes isolates were annotated with Bakta [51] using default parameters to generate GFF3 files. The resulting annotations were used as input for Roary [52] to construct the pan-genome, with a minimum BLASTp identity threshold of 95% and a core-gene definition of genes present in ≥99% of isolates. Roary output included a gene presence–absence matrix for all gene clusters, which was queried (query_pan_genome) to infer accessory gene content differences between isolates.

Stress survival islet 1 (SSI-1) detection: A BLAST search for highly similar sequences (megablast) was used to determine the presence of SSI-1 with the “align two or more sequences” option. Gene sequences for lmo044-lmo0448 from the L. monocytogenes reference genome EDG-e (NC_003210.1) were used as a query against the subject contigs generated from the SPAdes pipeline [53,54]. SSI-1 positive isolates had all genes present with at least 99% identity for each gene.

Statistical analyses

To determine variations in MIC between isolates and between nutrient concentrations, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), after satisfying conditions of homogeneity and normality (Shapiro-Wilk Tests, QQ plots), was used. For the crystal violet assay data set, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model, accounting for repeated measures with Mean OD600 as the response, fixed effects for Day, Nutrient Concentration and their interaction, and random intercepts for Isolate (MLST type). To account for non-normality, we computed parametric bootstrap confidence intervals (R = 1000). Finally, to evaluate the efficacy of sanitizers on mature biofilms grown under nutrient rich and poor conditions, the primary outcome variable, log reduction, was determined by subtracting the log10 CFU/coupon after sanitizer treatment, from the log10 CFU of the untreated (control) coupon. A factorial ANOVA was then used to determine the effects of sanitizer type, sanitizer concentration and nutrient concentration, as well as the interactions between these variables on log reduction. A Student T-test was additionally carried out to compare the effects of the two sanitizers. Across the 30 isolates, non-synonymous SNPs were detected in multiple biofilm-associated genes spanning all functional classes, with a markedly higher amount in Lineage II (Supplementary Fig. S1). In Lineage I (exclusively ST 191), non-synonymous variants were restricted to a limited subset of loci, primarily internalins (inlA, inlB, inlC, inlH, inlI, inlJ, inlK, inlP), surface adhesins (vip, lapB), flagellar and motility genes (fliM, flgB, flgK) and selected cell wall remodeling, purine metabolism and LTA modification genes. In contrast, Lineage II harbored non-synonymous SNPs in essentially all biofilm-associated genes examined, including adhesion/surface factors, cell wall hydrolases, multiple flagellar and motility components, purine biosynthesis genes, the stress regulator sigB and the dlt operon involved in teichoic acid D-alanylation, often in a larger number of isolates per gene than observed in Lineage I.

We further assessed unique SNPs among the four ST191 isolates selected for mature biofilm assessment (Supplementary Table S1), seeking to understand if any of them might explain the variations we saw in SEM. FML-01-007 had the poorest biofilm formation of the four; it also had the largest number of unique SNPs (11). These were primarily missense mutations. FML-F1-007 had three unique nonsynonymous SNPs in gene B7L24_RS03120 (DF4097 family beta strand repeat containing protein) and one unique SNP each in genes B7L24_RS08555 (o-succinylbenzoate coA ligase) and B7L24_RS10445 (LPXTG cell wall anchor domain-containing protein, but five unique SNPs in gene B7L24_RS10460 (BspA family leucine-rich repeat surface protein); all coded for missense mutations. Additionally, FML-01-007 had a conservative in-frame insertion In gene B7L24_RS01780 (class one internalin IntJ).FML-F1- 0010, which appeared to have the most complex biofilm formation in SEM, had unique SNPs in gene B7L24_RS02305 (condensation domain containing protein) and in genes B7L24_RS02440 and B7L24_RS13195 (hypothetical protein); all conferred missense mutations. Isolate FML-F1-019 had no unique nonsynonymous SNPs, but it lacked 47 SNPs present in the other three ST191 isolates assessed for mature biofilm. Isolate FML-F1-029 had extremely dense mature biofilms in SEM, and it carried nine unique nonsynonymous SNPs. Among these were two frameshift-inducing SNPs in gene B7L24_RS05270 (copper homeostasis protein CutC) and in gene B7L24_RS05740 (GNAT family N-acetyltransferase). In genes B7L24_RS05785 (immunity 26/phosphotriesterase HocA family protein) and B7L24_RS09330 (Lmo1799 family Asp-Ala repeat surface protein), this isolate had disruptive in-frame insertions. Finally, FML-F1-029 had five unique missense-encoding SNP in genes B7L24_RS02415 (BspA family leucine-rich repeat surface protein), B7L24_RS05740 (GNAT family N-acetyltransferase), and hypothetical proteins B7L24_RS14675 and B7L24_RS14680 (this gene had two SNPs). Overall, none of the SNPs present in the four isolates were in genes that were previously associated with biofilm formation capacity. However, the prevalence of mutations in surface-associated proteins was intriguing.

No antimicrobial resistance genes were detected, and none of the isolates had arsenic tolerance gene arsD or QAC tolerance genes bcrABC or qacG. All isolates, regardless of QAC MIC, had the mdrL efflux pump. This gene was initially reported to confer QAC tolerance; subsequent research shows this gene does not substantially affect sanitizer tolerance [56,57]. Additionally, multiple studies (including ours) found it in both QAC tolerant and sensitive isolates, suggesting it may be intrinsic to the L. monocytogenes genome [[56], [57], [58]]. SSI-1 was present in all lineage I isolates, but it was not detected in any of the lineage II isolates. No isolates had inlB gene deletions or inlA premature stop codons, indicating all should in theory be fully virulent. Mutations in prfA were present in 67% (20/30) of isolates, with all 16 lineage I isolates and four lineage II isolates having identical silent nucleotide substitutions. The prfA mutations did not alter the amino acid sequence and were not further analyzed.

We further ran the assembled hybrid genomes of these isolates through the new machine learning-based tool ListPred, to test the tool's prediction for our isolates' sanitizer tolerance and virulence [59]. ListPred accurately assessed all of our isolates as QAC susceptible, but it predicted that isolates from ST361 had high virulence, while the remainder of the isolates (STs 29, 191, 222, and 451) were classified as moderately virulent [59]. As we did not detect any mutations associated with reduced virulence in the ST191, 222, and 29 isolates, this prediction is more likely based on the clinical frequency of these sequence types than any specific mutations.

Pangenome analysis

The pangenome of the 30 artisan cheese isolates comprised 3664 gene clusters, which clearly separated into two main lineages at the accessory genome level (Supplementary Fig. S2). Lineage I–specific genes were predominantly associated with central metabolism and biosynthetic pathways, including glycolysis, de novo IMP and menaquinone/cobalamin biosynthesis, amino acid and thiamine metabolism, as well as base-excision repair and DNA restriction–modification systems. In contrast, lineage II–specific genes showed a stronger representation of functions related to CRISPR defense, transposases, protein secretion, metal and iron transport, riboflavin transport, and ribosomal subunits, together with several amino acid and cofactor biosynthetic processes (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Sanitizer tolerance of planktonic cells

At room temperature (22 °C), average MICs of QACs in nutrient rich conditions ranged from 4.69 to 18.75 ppm, and from 3.12 to 10.94 ppm in nutrient poor conditions. These MICs were lower than the recommended working concentration of 200 ppm for food contact surfaces and 400 ppm for non-food contact surfaces, indicating low tolerance of the planktonic L. monocytogenes isolates to QAC. Discussion

We sought to investigate (i) whether the DUP-1042B ribotype previously repeatedly re-isolated in a single Vermont artisanal cheesemaking environment represented a persistent strain by using more sensitive strain typing techniques and (ii) whether the repeated re-isolation of this ribotype could be attributed to an inherent genetic or inherent phenotypic trait that influenced biofilm formation and subsequent sanitizer tolerance and (iii), the ribotype's virulence/outbreak potential.

WGS-based MLST typing [38] of isolates was largely similar to the previously performed ribotyping [31,32]; however, it was not identical. All the DUP-1042 isolates in our dataset, which came from a single facility [31,32], belonged to ST191 and showed 0-7 SNPs difference from each other; however, one additional isolate from DUP-1006 also typed as ST-191 and grouped with two other DUP-1042 isolates (0 SNPs difference). Thresholds from ≤6 high-quality SNPs to up to 22 SNPs have been proposed for identifying the same strain/persistence via SNP analysis [[60], [61], [62]]; given this, the ST191 isolates can be confidently identified as a persistent strain within the facility they came from. It is also not surprising that there were minimal changes over a two-year period, as L. monocytogenes strains have been found to persist in food processing environments for up to 17 years, with minimal genetic changes [[61], [62], [63], [64], [65]].

ST191 is a rare type in the Pasteur Institute's database of L. monocytogenes strains [39,66], with only nine isolates mapping to that type. Of these nine, two were clinical isolates from blood, two were food isolates, two were isolated from soil or compost, two were from animals, and one was from the production environment (Anonymous). Additionally, only a handful of published studies exist that include isolates from ST191. In these studies, ST191 were rare, but found among samples collected from bulk tank milk, milk filters, and milk processing equipment [67]; from ruminant listeriosis cases in the US [68,69] and Europe [70]; in the meat processing environment in Poland [71]; and in trout meat from French fisheries [72]. Consequently, the ST191 isolates in our study could potentially have originated in an infected animal in the farmstead cheese producer's dairy herd. Whether from there it was introduced to the cheesemaking facility via L. monocytogenes in the milk [73] or was brought in on contaminated footwear or equipment is impossible to know; however, given the widespread nature and persistence of the strain in the cheese production facility [31,32], the company's sanitation protocols were unable to eradicate it once introduced.

We did not find any genetic elements associated with increased tolerance to QACs in any of the five MLST types identified [56]. Ivanova et al., recently completed a massive analysis of phenotypic and genotypic concordance for L. monocytogenes tolerance to QACs; they found that only bcrABC, emrC, emrE, or qacH increased QAC tolerance [56]. While all our isolates contained mdrL, which was previously reported to increase QAC tolerance [57,74], this gene has been reported by others to be found in both QAC tolerant and QAC-sensitive isolates [56]. Our data also suggested that mdrL presence did not significantly increase sanitizer tolerance, as it was present in all the ST groups, regardless of MIC. However, we cannot rule out whether differences in gene expression [57] may have been present in our isolates and contributed to differences in sanitizer tolerance.

It was also interesting that of the MLST groups identified, only ST191 was recovered from both food contact and non-food contact surfaces. All other strains were exclusively found on non-food contact surfaces and while we cannot rule out sampling bias in the original study, it is worth considering as it could provide some insight into the persistence of the strain. We cannot prove directionality, but ST 191 was moving/spreading between the two types of surfaces where the others were not, which raises questions as to why. ST191 was the only strain from lineage I and the only one of the five that had SSI-1 (Fig. 1). In studies where phylogenetic lineage has been associated with persistence, lineage II strains were demonstrated to be better adapted to common stressors present in food [28,59,[75], [76], [77]]. It is however important to note that there are niche differences in food environments and sanitation practices that aggregated data may not always account for [78]. Indeed, in our data, we showed that ST 191 was remarkable in its ability to sustain biofilm growth and attachment beyond day 3 where the other STs declined under chronic nutrient stress despite similar early biofilm profiles [79]. ST191 also exhibited a conserved non-synonymous SNP profile in a focused subset of biofilm and stress-related loci (Supplementary Fig. 1), and while its pangenome (Supplementary Fig. 2) showed fewer accessory functions overall, its enrichment in core metabolic and DNA-maintenance pathways may support better survival under chronic environmental stress [79]. Pan-genome analysis among ST191 strains revealed minimal accessory-genome diversity, with only five variable genes (three hypothetical proteins and two restriction endonuclease subunit S genes) identified across the isolates (Fig. 3). Because these variable loci were not obvious biofilm-associated determinants, the differences observed were more likely reflects subtle allelic variation [80]. Together, these findings suggest that ST 191 persistence in the cheese environment was more likely driven by biofilm maintenance and environmental stress tolerance than by an enhanced initial attachment. This may not be universal across all surfaces or nutrient regimes given the fact that our data showed no distinction between the ST types in mature 10-day biofilms grown on stainless steel (Fig. 6).

The ListPred tool accurately predicted the low QAC tolerance of our isolates, which is unsurprising, as it is based on phenotypic QAC tolerance data from a large number of L. monocytogenes isolates primarily from Europe and the U.S. [56,59,81]. It is less clear whether its virulence predictions are accurate [59]. While the ListPred tool draws heavily on MLST-type clinical associations for virulence predictions, four of the five MLST types represented by our isolates (STs 191, 361, 222, and 29) had fewer than 50 isolates in the Pasteur MLST database [39]. While all had at least some clinical isolates--75% (3/4) of ST361 isolates were clinical, while 59.5% (22/37) of ST222 isolates, 55.8% (29/52) of ST451 isolates, and 22.2% of ST191 (2/9) isolates, respectively were clinical—the numbers were too small to draw any conclusions as to ListPred's accuracy. Additionally, we did not find any mutations in key virulence genes, such as PMSCs in inlA [66,82,83], inlB deletions or truncations [[84], [85], [86]] or prfA mutations/truncations [87].

Conclusions

In the original studies by D'Amico and Donnelly, the authors posited that there might be enhanced sanitizer tolerance in the DUP-1042B isolates [31,32]; however, we did not observe this when we tested sanitizer tolerance. Instead, we discovered that the DUP-1042B isolates mapped to a unique, rare sequence type, and showed hints of enhanced attachment capacity, which could have supported their persistence in the cheesemaking environment. Their accessory genome also was enriched for biofilm-supporting functions. Our biofilm assays, however, had mixed outcomes and we were thus unable to establish a clear persistence mechanism. Our experimental design also did not account for microbial interactions present in the sampling sites which may have influenced biofilm formation and other physiological traits of the persistent L. monocytogenes strain. Nonetheless, this research provides valuable insights into the persistence of a rare ST of L. monocytogenes in the artisan cheese environment.
