AI-driven virtual histology with optical coherence tomography for comparing acute and short-trem performance of bioresorbable scaffolds and drug-eluting stents
Authors: Chengpeng Liu, Jiasheng Yin, Yaojun Zhang, Chenguang Li, Hao Lu, Gang Zhao, Juying Qian, Yizhe Wu, Li Shen, Junbo Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncomplete stent expansion is an important mechanism leading to adverse events. However, few detailed OCT studies have compared the immediate performance of bioresorbable scaffolds (BRS) and drug-eluting stents (DES). The aim of this study was to compare the acute and short-term performance differences between polymeric BRS and metallic DES, and to explore the underlying mechanisms of immediate stent underexpansion and eccentricity reduction.MethodsOur study consecutively enrolled 298 patients who underwent optical coherence tomography (OCT) pullbacks after BRS or DES implantation, with 178 patients in the BRS group and 120 patients in the DES group. To reduce confounding, propensity score matching generated 64 balanced pairs of patients. The core laboratory used AI-driven virtual histology technology to measure immediate vascular and plaque parameters. Clinical follow-up was conducted for 1 year post-procedure, and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were recorded.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, the BRS group had similar luminal and stent parameters to the DES group, with lower mean and minimum stent eccentricity indices (SEC). Furthermore, the BRS and DES groups had similar characteristics of fibrous plaque (FP) and calcific plaque (CP). More severe CP and FP restricted adequate expansion of BRS and DES. The incidence of 1-year MACE did not differ significantly between the BRS and DES groups (log-rank p = 0.685).ConclusionsThe BRS appears to have comparable acute performance and reduced SEC when compared to DES. The stent expansion and eccentricity were associated with the characteristics of CP and FP. BRS and DES showed no significant difference at 1-year follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05675-2.Keywords: bioresorbable scaffold, drug-eluting stent, optical coherence tomography, plaque composition, stent eccentricity index

Introduction

Bioresorbable scaffolds (BRS), hailed as the fourth revolution in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), were designed to release the rigid constraints of permanent stents and promote positive vascular remodeling. However, the higher incidence of thrombosis has limited their application [1, 2]. Scaffold thrombosis is primarily attributed to stent strut thickness, suboptimal stent sizing, over- or under-expansion, and malapposition during deployment [3].

Despite these limitations, BRS remains attractive to PCI operators and cardiologists. The implementation of the predilatation, sizing, post-dilatation (PSP) strategy is expected to enhance the clinical outcomes of BRS [4]. The full utilization of PSP techniques has demonstrated favorable long-term results with BRS [5].

The MUSIC study results indicated a correlation between stent deployment and expansion parameters, such as stent eccentricity index (SEC) and minimal lumen area, demonstrating that optimized stent implantation decreases the occurrence of adverse events [6]. Brugaletta et al. reported a higher SEC in drug-eluting stents (DES) compared to BRS based on intravascular ultrasound analysis [7]. Unduly low SEC contributes to pathological hemodynamic flow patterns, thereby increasing the risk of long-term thrombosis and in-stent restenosis [8, 9].

But an optical coherence tomography (OCT) study revealed similar SEC between DES and BRS [10]. Previous OCT-based studies have identified correlations between the SEC, stent expansion index (SEI) of BRS with plaque characteristics, but these studies had limited sample sizes and lacked strict adherence to the PSP strategy, requiring further research for validation [11, 12].

Therefore, we collected OCT pullbacks from patients treated with BRS and DES to evaluate their acute performance and the impact of plaque characteristics on stent deployment and expansion.

Methods

Study population

This study consecutively enrolled patients who underwent implantation of DES and BRS with post-PCI OCT pullback at Zhongshan Hospital (Shanghai, China) from March 2021 to December 2023.

The devices utilized in the DES group were the Excel (JWMS, Weihai, China), Firebird2 (MicroPort, Shanghai, China), Helios (Kinhely, Shenzhen, China), Promus PREMIER (Boston Scientific, Natick, Massachusetts), Resolute Integrity (Medtronic Vascular, Santa Rosa, California), while the BRS group received the XINSORB BRS (HuaAn Biotech, Shandong, China), with the key parameters of stents detailed in supplemental material, Table 1S. Selected lesions measured less than 30 mm in length, with reference vessel diameters ranging visually from 2.75 to 3.5 mm. Exclusions were poor-quality OCT pullbacks, thrombus-containing lesions, and in-stent restenosis. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Study flow chart. BRS = bioresorbable stents; DES = drug-eluting stents; ISR = in-stent restenosis; MACE = major adverse cardiovascular events; MI = myocardial infarction; ST = stent thrombosis; TVR = target vessel revascularization

In accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, our research was approved by the Ethics Committee in Zhongshan Hospital, shanghai (B2021-026R). Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Procedure

In this study, DES were implanted in accordance with standard clinical practice. For the BRS, we employed an optimized implantation strategy based on the PSP technique [4]. Prior to the intervention, patients were administered 0.3 g of Aspirin and 300 milligrams of Clopidogrel as part of the pre-procedure medication regimen. For patients at high risk of bleeding, Bivalirudin was used as a substitute for unfractionated heparin (100 U/kg). All strategies were performed under OCT guidance by experienced operators.

Quantitative coronary angiography computation

Researchers utilized the AngioPlus system (Pulse Medical Imaging Technology Inc, Shanghai, China) to perform quantitative coronary angiography analysis. The evaluated parameters comprised lesion length, minimal lumen diameter, reference vessel diameter, rate of diameter stenosis and quantitative flow ratio [13].

OCT performance and offline analysis

OCT images were obtained using the Dragonfly catheter (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at pullback speeds of 18 mm/s or 36 mm/s, in combination with the Ilumien Optis OCT system (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Initially, the OCT catheter was positioned distal to the target lesion, and the pullback sustained until it either reached the guiding catheter or the maximum pullback length. Following the final optimization, post-PCI OCT imaging was performed to assess plaque and stent characteristics. All imaging data were anonymized and analyzed by investigators blinded to the clinical data.The analysis of cross-sectional OCT images was performed at 100–200 μm per frame intervals, with the AI-driven OCT measurements conducted by OctPlus (Pulse Medical Imaging Technology Inc., Shanghai, China). The excellent diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility of this technique have been previously validated [14, 15].

The definitions of the various parameters are as follows. The SEC is calculated by dividing the minimum by the maximum stent diameters. SEI is characterized by the division of the cross-sectional area per frame by the mean reference lumen area. We assessed both the overall SEC and SEI of the stent, as well as those specific to frames containing analysed plaques. Definition of lumen area stenosis rate as one minus minimal lumen area divided by reference vessel area expressed as a percentage (Fig. 2). The separation of the stent from the vessel wall is known as incomplete strut apposition. Tissue prolapse refers to the protrusion of tissue into the lumen between the struts. Stent dissection is identified by the disruption of the artery luminal surface at the edge of stent, accompanied by a flap of at least 300 μm. Stent fracture indicates structural failure with isolated struts in the lumen without being linked or covered by adjacent stent struts [12].

Calculation of Stent and Lumen Parameters. a, c Reference vessel areas were calculated using proximal and distal RVAs. b An example of calculation of the minimum eccentricity index and area stenosis rate

The Octplus system was utilized for AI-drivenvirtual histology analysis, enabling automatic segmentation of vascular and plaque components. We selected the frame with the maximum plaque thickness for analysis, tracing the plaque contours to measure its cross-sectional area [16]. Previous studies have established that lipid plaques exhibit relatively soft characteristics and possess the ability to undergo shape alterations, thereby exerting minimal constraints on stent expansion [17]. Consequently, our study primarily focused on analysing the characteristics of calcific plaques (CP) and fibrous plaques (FP). OCT images of FP were characterized by homogeneous, signal-rich areas, CP were characterized by well-defined, poorly signaled areas [18]. The thickness of CP and FP was measured at their maximum points from the plaque edge to the lumen. The arc angle of CP and FP was obtained utilizing a protractor positioned at the lumen’s center. The depth of CP was quantified as the length from the bottom boundary of CP to the lumen (Fig. 3). Plaque characteristics were assessed by two independent observers, with discrepancies adjudicated by a cardiologist.

Follow-up

Patients underwent 1-year clinical follow-up, with the primary endpoint defined as major adverse cardiac events (MACE), including death, myocardial infarction (MI), target vessel revascularization (TVR), and stent thrombosis (ST).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were displayed as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Normally distributed data were reported with their mean and standard deviation, and were analyzed by the t-test. Non-normally distributed data were represented by median and IQR, and inter-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. To mitigate confounding, we performed propensity score (PS) matching using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement and a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit PS, which created 64 well-balanced patient pairs with equivalent baseline and procedural profiles. The matched covariate set and standardized mean differences are shown in supplemental material, Table 2S. Linear regression models with stepwise covariate adjustment were used to conduct sensitivity analysis, aiming to verify the robustness of the effect of stent type, baseline characteristics, and procedural features on the immediate outcome. In the mediation analysis, the Baron & Kenny stepwise method combined with the Bootstrap resampling test was adopted to quantify the contribution of procedural characteristics to SEC and SEI. In all plaque-level analyses, cluster-robust standard errors (clustered by patient) were employed to address the non-independence of multiple observations per lesion. Continuous plaque metrics were analyzed using restricted cubic splines to capture non-linear relationships, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate method. Restricted cubic spline models were used to explore potential nonlinear relationships between plaque characteristics and acute outcomes (SEC, SEI) in different groups, with false discovery rate correction applied. Cumulative Incidence Curve and the Log-Rank test were used to analyze the survival data. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0) and R software (version 4.4.3).

Following PS matching, the mean Sect. (0.88 ± 0.05 vs. 0.91 ± 0.04, p < 0.001) and minimal Sect. (0.71 ± 0.09 vs. 0.79 ± 0.06, p < 0.001) remained lower in BRS, with no differences in stent and lumen parameters or immediate adverse event rates between groups.

The results of the stepwise covariate-adjusted sensitivity analysis are presented in supplemental material, Table 3S. A significant and consistent association was observed between stent type and Mean/Min SEC across all covariate-adjusted models (all p < 0.001), but no association with SEI. The mediation analysis did not identify any procedural characteristics that demonstrated a statistically significant mediating effect on the relationship between stent type and immediate performance metrics (supplemental material, Table 4S).

Plaque characteristic

OCT analysis identified 220 CP and 269 FP in the BRS group and 198 CP and 168 FP in the DES group. The plaque characteristics of CP and FP were similar in the two groups. Notably, the SEC at the plaque analysis frame was significantly lower in the BRS group for both CP (0.84 ± 0.09 vs. 0.87 ± 0.08, p = 0.011) and FP (0.85 ± 0.08 vs. 0.88 ± 0.07, p < 0.001) when compared to the DES group (supplemental material, Table 5S, 6S).

Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed significant and differential nonlinear associations between plaque characteristics and immediate stent morphology. Analysis of CP revealed distinct morphological influencers for DES and BRS. In the DES group, SEC was significantly associated with plaque area, arc angle, and thickness, while SEI correlated primarily with plaque depth. Conversely, in the BRS group, SEI emerged as the principally affected parameter, demonstrating dependence on plaque area. Evaluation of FP showed notably different patterns. BRS exhibited particular susceptibility in these lesions, with both SEC and SEI substantially influenced by plaque area and arc angle. For DES, SEC was notably linked to arc angle, while SEI showed a significant association with plaque area (supplemental material, Table 7S, Fig. 1 − 4S).

The clinical outcomes at 1 year are shown in Fig. 4; Table 5. The cumulative incidence of MACE showed no statistically significant differences between BRS and DES (p = 0.685). No deaths or MI were recorded in either group, the TVR and ST rates between the two groups were similar.

Discussion

This study represents one of the larger single-center OCT analyses to date, utilizing OCT to compare the immediate performance of BRS and DES. By implementing an optimized implantation strategy for BRS based on the PSP technique, BRS achieved acute luminal and stent parameters similar to those of DES. Notably, BRS demonstrated a significantly lower SEC than DES. Furthermore, this study conducted a detailed OCT analysis of BRS and DES, identifying potential associations between plaque characteristics and immediate performance, framing this as a hypothesis-generating study.

To facilitate a more precise analysis of stent expansion, eccentricity, and plaque morphology, OCT was chosen over intravascular ultrasound. Leveraging its superior resolution, OCT enables the precise delineation of luminal borders and stent struts, alongside the quantification of the incomplete strut apposition and the prolapse area. Additionally, OCT for accurate assessment of plaque composition, including the determination of cross-sectional area, thickness, and arc angle [19, 20].

Prior studies have demonstrated that the implantation of BRS optimized through the PSP technique correlates with improved acute geometric parameters and superior clinical outcomes [21, 22]. Among the entire population, lesions treated with BRS showed higher SEI and lumen area stenosis rates compared to those treated with DES. This could be due to the more rigorous PSP technique application in BRS group, with more aggressive pre-dilation, deployment pressure, and post-dilation compared to DES group. Upon analysis of the PS-matched cohort, with adjustments for baseline and procedural characteristics, BRS and DES showed equivalent geometrical parameters and acute OCT-defined adverse events.

The comparable 1-year MACE rates between BRS and DES cohorts, coupled with the absence of mortality and myocardial infarction events, indicate no significant difference detected in short-term clinical management. These results suggest that with OCT guidance to select appropriate lesions for BRS and optimize implantation strategies can achieve immediate expansion outcomes and short-term outcomes equivalent to those of DES [23].

We found that BRS had a lower mean and minimal SEC compared to metallic DES. This result was consistent with the research by Brugaletta et al., which documented an SEC of 0.85 ± 0.08 for ABSORB BRS and 0.90 ± 0.06 for XINNCE DES, as measured by intravascular ultrasound [7]. However, Mattesini et al.’s study found no significant difference in SEC between BRS and DES, which may be attributed to the inclusion of complex lesions such as chronic total occlusion, severe calcification, and bifurcation lesions. The average (0.85 ± 0.08) and minimal Sect. (0.73 ± 0.09) in their study were similar to ours [10].

A low SEC indicates a non-circular lumen shape, which can lead to plastic deformation of the stent and alter the stress distribution, resulting in suboptimal clinical outcomes [24]. Investigations into blood flow dynamics have revealed that lesions with reduced SEC are inclined to form areas of elevated wall shear stress, which amplified the risk of plaque disruption and thrombogenic events [25]. Moreover, eccentric plaques can lead to the maldistribution of anti-proliferative medications, making regions with lower drug concentration more susceptible to in-stent restenosis [26]. The MUSIC trial demonstrated that SEC of ≥ 0.7 is beneficial for acute performance and 6-month angiographic outcomes [6]. Suwannasom et al. also found that lower SEC was correlated with higher incidence of adverse events [26]. However, due to the lower-than-expected MACE rate, no statistical association was found between luminal and stent parameters and MACE in our study.

The composition, morphology, and burden of plaque significantly influence stent expansion and eccentricity. The differential relationships between plaque characteristics and immediate procedural outcomes for DES versus BRS suggest that acute performance is not solely determined by lesion characteristics, but also by stent mechanical properties. This divergence may be attributed to variations in mechanical properties between the two device platforms [27]. The first consideration is the comparatively lower mechanical strength of BRS, which confers a structural vulnerability to compressive forces from plaque, leading to deformation and consequent luminal and scaffold eccentricity. Additionally, BRS struts (160 μm) are substantially thicker than those of DES (80–100 μm). Previous computational fluid dynamics simulations have demonstrated that thicker struts promote more extensive flow recirculation zones and generate hemodynamic profiles characterized by non-physiologic shear stress and an elevated oscillatory shear index. These adverse flow conditions foster a pro-thrombotic milieu and elevate the risk of in-stent restenosis [8, 28].

Furthermore, 14.0% of FP exhibited arc angles greater than 240°, and 24.4% exceeded 220°. The nearly circular FP may contribute to a more homogeneous lumen loss, while more severe FP lesions are more likely to undergo aggressive pre-dilatation. Regarding stent expansion, we observed that the SEI for both BRS and DES was compromised by a greater burden of CP and FP. Our findings are consistent with the small sample study by Shaw et al., which demonstrated that the plaque burden of CP and FP significantly affects the eccentricity and expansion of ABSORB BRS [11]. However, Boeder et al. found that BRS was only influenced by FP features in terms of SEC, and SEI was unrelated to plaque characteristics. This discrepancy could be explained by the limited sample size of 15 participants, where SEI showed a trend dependent on CP burden (P = 0.06) [12]. The use of different brands of BRS in the three studies may also contribute to the observed variations.

Different types of plaques exhibit varying mechanical strengths. Mechanical testing has shown that the static stiffness of CP is significantly higher at 354.5 ± 245.4 kPa compared to FP at 81.7 ± 33.2 kPa and non-fibrous tissue at 41.2 ± 18.8 kPa [29]. Earlier investigations have demonstrated that rigid CPs tend to cause lumen deformation and biomechanical alterations [2]. CPs exhibit greater elastic recoil, reducing lumen gain and leading to suboptimal stent expansion. The protrusion of CP towards the lumen contributes to eccentric lumens and stents [30, 31]. Therefore, early identification of CP and adequate pre-dilation optimize immediate efficacy. In comparison to non-compliant balloons, the use of cutting balloons for treating calcified lesions results in higher SEC and larger minimum stent area [32]. Despite having lower mechanical strength than CP, FP typically present with greater area, thickness, and arc angle. The increased size of these plaques results in higher expansion resistance and elevated stent struts stress, consequently diminishing the SEC and SEI [33].

In this study, all patients were maintained on dual antiplatelet therapy throughout the one-year follow-up period. Notably, the potential transition to single antiplatelet therapy in DES group during subsequent management warrants further investigation through extended follow-up. Although BRS were primarily deployed in non-complex lesions, persistent concerns exist regarding heightened thrombotic risk in cases featuring severe CP or FP that result in suboptimal SEC or SEI. For lesions demonstrating adverse performance on OCT assessment, implementation of personalized antithrombotic strategies becomes imperative [34].

We acknowledge that enhancing the immediate performance of BRS, particularly in increasing the SEC, poses a significant challenge. Initially, enhancing the mechanical properties of BRS is a conventional strategy to address this issue. The new generation of BRS, which employs more reliable biodegradable metallic materials or modifies the composition and molecular arrangement of polymers, demonstrates potential in achieving acute performance comparable to that of traditional DES [35, 36]. Concurrently, there is an imperative need to refine the PCI procedures and the selection of lesions suitable for BRS, particularly avoiding lesions with extensive FP. This suggests that intravascular imaging should optimally be applied before and after BRS implantation, with increased utilization of auxiliary devices, such as non-compliant balloons, cutting and scoring balloons, in areas of significant FP and CP.

Limitations

Firstly, Retrospective single-center data reflecting local practice patterns limits the external validity of the study. There are differences in the indications for BRS and DES. Although PS matching was employed, the possibility of confounding bias persists. In addition, this study only focuses on the acute performance and short-term outcomes of the stent, a prospective, randomized controlled trial is required to determine whether the observed stent immediate expansion and eccentricity parameters can affect long-term clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

This is a large-scale detailed OCT analysis comparing the acute stent performance of BRS with DES. BRS demonstrated acute luminal and stent parameters comparable to those of DES when treating similar lesions with equivalent procedure, but exhibited lower SEC. The stent expansion and eccentricity are correlated with the characteristics of CP and FP, as well as the stent mechanical properties. BRS and DES showed no significant difference at 1-year follow-up.
