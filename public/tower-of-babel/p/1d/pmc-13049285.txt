Epidemiological trends in bacterial resistance in diabetic foot infections across the COVID-19 timeline: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Fabiana Giarritiello, Loredana Deflorio, Luigi Regenburgh De La Motte, Carmen Sommese, Sofia Benedetti, Enrico Brocco, Lorenzo Drago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Six-year analysis of antimicrobial resistance trends in diabetic foot infections.•Decline in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa across COVID-19 timeline.•Emerging carbapenem resistance in Enterobacterales.•Polymicrobial infections decreased post-pandemic.•Long-term surveillance informs antimicrobial stewardship.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects over 460 million people globally, with projections reaching 643 million by 2030 [1,2]. In Italy, approximately 5.3% of the population has diabetes, with a prevalence of 16.5% in individuals over 65 years [3]. A major complication of DM is diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), affecting 6.3% of diabetics and representing a leading cause of non-traumatic lower-limb amputations [4]. Diabetic foot infections (DFIs) are a critical concern, contributing to recurrent hospitalizations, increased healthcare costs, and a 5-year mortality rate comparable to some cancers [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Early during the COVID-19 pandemic, a potential interaction between COVID-19 and diabetic foot disease was hypothesized, mainly from a pathophysiological and conceptual perspective, although supporting epidemiological data were initially lacking [11]. During the pandemic, the organization and delivery of diabetic foot care were profoundly affected by healthcare system reorganization, reduced outpatient access, and delayed referrals, with potential implications for infection severity and hospitalization patterns [12].

The microbiological profile of DFIs varies across regions and time, with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) representing a growing clinical challenge. Pre-pandemic epidemiological studies from Italy had already documented a high burden of resistant pathogens in DFIs, particularly involving Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative bacteria [13]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, studies in Italy reported a sharp rise in AMR, with resistant strains detected in up to 63% of cases in 2020 compared with 36% in 2019, possibly reflecting changes in healthcare access and antibiotic exposure during lockdown periods [14]. Despite these observations, available studies are often limited by relatively small sample sizes, restricted geographic coverage, and short observation periods. To address these gaps, we conducted a large-scale epidemiological analysis of bacterial isolates from DFIs treated at IRCCS MultiMedica, Milan. Unlike previous investigations, our study spans 6 years and encompasses three distinct phases pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID—allowing a comprehensive assessment of temporal variations in pathogen distribution and AMR patterns. By adhering to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) and EARS-Net guidelines, our methodological approach ensures comparability with national and international surveillance data. Given Milan’s high population density and its role as a major healthcare hub, our findings provide a broader perspective on the evolving epidemiology of DFIs and AMR trends over time.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Laboratory of Clinical Medicine with Specialized Areas, IRCCS MultiMedica, Milan, Italy. The study analyzed microbiological and AMR data from biopsy samples of hospitalized patients diagnosed with DFIs between June 2018 and June 2024. Patients were selected based on clinical and microbiological criteria to ensure consistency across the study periods.

Eligible participants included hospitalized patients with a confirmed diagnosis of DFI who underwent microbiological culture analysis through biopsy. Only samples containing at least one viable bacterial isolate were considered. Patients who did not undergo biopsy or whose samples yielded negative cultures were excluded. To avoid data redundancy, multiple samples from the same patient were not included in the final analysis. The temporal stratification of the study into pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID periods was based on a longitudinal epidemiological framework previously applied by our group in the analysis of AMR trends in another clinical setting [15]. To assess temporal variations, patients were grouped into the three periods: pre-COVID (June 2018-December 2019, n = 577 isolates), COVID (January 2020-July 2022, n = 732 isolates), and post-COVID (August 2022-June 2024, n = 439 isolates).

Microbiological analysis and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Microbiological analyses were conducted in accordance with EUCAST and AMCLI (Italian Association of Clinical Microbiologists) guidelines. Standard culture methods were employed to isolate bacterial pathogens under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Identification of isolates and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using VITEKⓇ® 2 (bioMérieux), with interpretations based on EUCAST breakpoints. Quality control measures included the use of standard reference strains (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, S. aureus ATCC 29213, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853) to ensure the reliability of results. Only bacterial species with at least 10 isolates per study period were included in the AMR trend analyses. Species with lower prevalence were documented in the Supplementary Materials (Figure S1) for completeness but excluded from statistical evaluation.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate bacterial prevalence across the three study periods, the prevalence of polymicrobial infections, and AMR trends, different statistical methods were applied. The χ² test for independence was used to compare bacterial prevalence and AMR rates across the three periods when the assumptions for χ² testing were met. Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were small.

For AMR outcomes (binomial proportions), pairwise comparisons between the pre-COVID and post-COVID periods were performed using a Z-test for two proportions when the normal approximation was appropriate (i.e., both np and n (1−p) ≥5 in each group); otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was preferred.

Polymicrobial infection was analyzed as a patient-level outcome (presence of >1 clinically relevant microorganism isolated from the same biopsy sample) and compared between periods using χ² testing, with Fisher’s exact test applied when indicated. Statistical significance was set at P <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalcⓇ statistical software (Version 23.1.7; accessed March 21, 2025 - Ostend, Belgium; available at: https://www.medcalc.org).

Pathogen selection and AMR mechanism evaluation

The study focused on the most frequently isolated and clinically significant pathogens in DFIs, ensuring that the analysis captured relevant epidemiological shifts [16]. The Gram-positive bacteria examined included S. aureus, particularly methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), and Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium, with emphasis on vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE).

Among Gram-negative bacteria, P. aeruginosa was evaluated for carbapenem resistance (CRPA), while Klebsiella pneumoniae, E. coli, Enterobacter cloacae, and Serratia marcescens were assessed for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales and phenotypic resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (3GC-R). In this study, 3GC-R refers to phenotypic resistance to third-generation cephalosporins detected by routine AST and interpreted according to EUCAST criteria. This phenotype may reflect Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamases (ESBL) production or other resistance mechanisms; molecular confirmation was not performed. The analysis also included the Acinetobacter baumannii complex, given its association with extensively drug-resistant phenotypes (XDR-AB). Bacterial resistance was characterized based on methicillin resistance in S. aureus, vancomycin resistance in Enterococcus spp., carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa and Enterobacterales, and phenotypic resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (3GC-R) in E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Enterobacter spp.

Study objectives and rationale

This study aimed to provide a comprehensive epidemiological assessment of bacterial prevalence and AMR patterns in patients with DFIs over time. The temporal stratification into pre-, during, and post-COVID periods enabled a comparative description of variations in bacterial etiology, shifts in monomicrobial vs polymicrobial infections, and the evolution of AMR mechanisms.

Monomicrobial vs polymicrobial infections

The analysis of patients with DFIs across the three study periods showed a progressive reduction in the proportion of polymicrobial infections, defined as the isolation of more than one clinically relevant microorganism from the same biopsy sample. Specifically, polymicrobial infections were identified in 147 out of 401 patients (36.66%) in the pre-COVID period, 162 out of 478 patients (33.89%) during the COVID period, and 95 out of 327 patients (29.05%) in the post-COVID period (Figure 1), indicating a downward temporal trend. As expected, the total number of bacterial isolates exceeded the number of patients, reflecting the intrinsic polymicrobial nature of DFIs, whereby multiple pathogens may be recovered from a single biopsy culture. When polymicrobial infection was analyzed as a patient-level outcome, statistical comparison between the pre-COVID and post-COVID periods demonstrated a significant difference, as assessed by the χ² test (P = 0.0303) and confirmed by Fisher’s exact test (P = 0.0328). These findings indicate that the observed reduction in polymicrobial infections across the study timeline is unlikely to be attributable to random variation alone. No further isolate-based comparative analyses were performed for this outcome, as polymicrobial infection represents a clinical characteristic at the patient-level rather than an isolate-level variable. Antibiotic resistance patterns

The analysis of AMR among bacterial isolates from DFIs across the three study periods revealed heterogeneous temporal trends across different pathogens and resistance mechanisms. A statistically significant reduction in MRSA was observed over time, with resistance rates decreasing from 67% in the pre-COVID period to 50% during the COVID period and 48% in the post-COVID period. This downward trend was confirmed by both the χ² test (P = 0.004) and the Z-test for proportions comparing the pre-COVID and post-COVID periods (P = 0.005). A similar pattern was observed for CRPA, which declined from 25% in the pre-COVID period to 7% during the COVID period and remained stable at 7% post-COVID. This reduction was statistically significant (χ² test P = 0.0004; Z-test pre-COVID vs post-COVID P = 0.0038). In contrast, no statistically significant variations were detected for VRE, with resistance rates remaining relatively stable across the three periods (approximately 30% pre-COVID, 25% during COVID, and 27% post-COVID; Fisher’s exact test P = 1.000). Given the limited number of isolates, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Among Enterobacterales, divergent trends were observed. 3GC-R E. cloacae showed a numerical decrease from 36% pre-COVID to 14% post-COVID; however, this change did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test P = 0.37). Similarly, declines in 3GC-R Proteus mirabilis (from 43% to 19%) were not statistically significant. Conversely, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae demonstrated an increasing trend, with resistance rates rising from 45% in the pre-COVID period to 50% during COVID and reaching 67% post-COVID, although this variation did not achieve statistical significance. Carbapenem-resistant E. coli was not detected in the pre-COVID period and emerged in the post-COVID period, with a resistance rate of 13%; however, this finding did not meet the criteria for statistical significance. No carbapenem resistance was observed in P. mirabilis or E. cloacae across the study periods. XDR-AB displayed a fluctuating pattern, decreasing from 84% in the pre-COVID period to 71% during COVID, followed by an increase to 90% post-COVID. These variations were not statistically significant. Overall, the AMR analysis highlights both statistically significant reductions in selected resistance phenotypes, such as MRSA and carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa, and non-significant but notable temporal variations among Enterobacterales and the non-fermenting Gram-negative pathogen A. baumannii complex. Discussion

This study provides a 6-year descriptive analysis of the microbiological profile and AMR patterns observed in DFIs, comparing three distinct time periods spanning the pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID phases. Two main findings emerge from the analysis: (i) a progressive reduction in the proportion of polymicrobial infections and (ii) heterogeneous temporal trends in AMR, characterized by statistically significant declines in selected resistance phenotypes alongside non-significant but noteworthy variations among Gram-negative pathogens.

Microbiological trends across the study periods

Although no statistically significant shifts were observed in the distribution of individual bacterial species or in the overall balance between Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, a significant reduction in polymicrobial infections was detected when analyzed as a patient-level outcome. This finding suggests a temporal change in the microbiological complexity of DFIs over the study period; however, its interpretation requires caution. Several non-mutually exclusive factors may have contributed to this observation. Changes in healthcare organization and patient referral patterns across the study periods may have influenced the clinical characteristics of admitted cases, potentially resulting in differences in infection severity at presentation. In particular, variations in access to care and admission criteria may have altered the proportion of advanced or long-standing infections, which are more frequently polymicrobial in nature. Importantly, the retrospective design of the study and the absence of detailed clinical variables, such as infection severity scores, ischemic status, or prior outpatient management preclude definitive conclusions regarding the mechanisms underlying the observed reduction in polymicrobial infections. Moreover, microbiological data were derived from routine culture-based diagnostics, which may underestimate the true microbial diversity of DFIs, particularly with respect to fastidious organisms or anaerobes. As such, the observed trend should be interpreted as a descriptive epidemiological finding rather than evidence of a causal shift in DFI pathogenesis.

Temporal variations in AMR patterns

The analysis of AMR patterns revealed divergent temporal trends across different bacterial species and resistance mechanisms. Statistically significant reductions were observed for MRSA and CRPA, indicating a consistent decrease in the prevalence of these resistance phenotypes over time. While these findings are encouraging, the study design does not allow attribution of these trends to specific interventions or external factors. Similar analyses conducted during the pandemic period have reported heterogeneous AMR trends in DFIs across different settings. In particular, a single-center Italian study focusing on the early COVID-19 phase described an increase in AMR among hospitalized patients with DFIs, underscoring the variability of AMR dynamics depending on local epidemiology, patient case mix, and observation timeframe [14]. These differences highlight the importance of extended longitudinal analyses to contextualize short-term fluctuations observed during healthcare system disruptions. In contrast, resistance patterns among Enterobacterales displayed greater variability. Although increases in carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae and the emergence of carbapenem-resistant E. coli were observed in the later study periods, these changes did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Nonetheless, the presence of these resistance phenotypes remains clinically relevant, given their association with limited therapeutic options and adverse outcomes in DFIs. The fluctuating pattern observed for extensively drug-resistant A. baumannii further underscores the persistent challenge posed by non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria in the hospital setting. Taken together, these findings highlight the dynamic nature of AMR in DFIs and reinforce the importance of continuous, long-term surveillance to detect emerging resistance trends and guide empirical therapy.

Clinical and research implications

The findings of this study underscore the importance of continuous surveillance of AMR patterns in DFIs, particularly in the context of pathogens associated with limited therapeutic options. Although statistically significant reductions were observed for selected resistance phenotypes, such as MRSA and CRPA, resistance among Enterobacterales remained heterogeneous, with the presence of carbapenem-resistant strains representing an ongoing clinical concern. From a clinical perspective, these results highlight the need for sustained attention to local epidemiological data when guiding empirical antibiotic therapy in DFIs. Long-term, institution-specific surveillance may help support antimicrobial stewardship strategies by identifying stable trends and emerging resistance patterns, rather than relying on short-term or cross-sectional observations. From a research standpoint, future investigations should aim to address the limitations inherent to retrospective microbiological analyses. In particular, multicenter epidemiological studies would be valuable to determine whether the temporal trends observed in this cohort are reproducible across different healthcare settings and geographic regions. In addition, molecular approaches, such as whole-genome sequencing may provide deeper insight into the genetic mechanisms underlying resistance evolution and the potential dissemination of high-risk clones, as suggested in previous studies [17]. Finally, longitudinal studies integrating microbiological data with detailed clinical and organizational variables may help clarify how broader healthcare system changes over time influence AMR dynamics in DFIs [17,18].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the results to other institutions or healthcare systems. Second, the analysis was based on routinely collected microbiological data, without access to detailed clinical variables, such as infection severity, ischemic status, prior antibiotic exposure, or outpatient management, which may have influenced both pathogen distribution and resistance patterns. Furthermore, culture-based diagnostic methods may underestimate the true microbial complexity of DFIs, particularly with respect to anaerobic or fastidious organisms, potentially leading to an underrepresentation of polymicrobial infections. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a large number of isolates, a prolonged observation period spanning 6 years, and consistent microbiological and susceptibility testing methodologies applied across all study periods. IRCCS MultiMedica is a high-volume referral center for diabetic foot care in Lombardy, a region characterized by a substantial burden of AMR and significant healthcare system pressures during the study period. While this context may influence local epidemiology, it also provides a stable framework for longitudinal surveillance, allowing meaningful comparisons over time. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive of temporal trends within a specialized tertiary-care setting rather than as evidence of causal relationships or broadly generalizable effects.

Conclusion

This study provides a 6-year descriptive overview of microbiological patterns and AMR trends in DFIs within a tertiary-care setting. By comparing pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID periods, the analysis highlights both favorable and concerning temporal variations across different pathogens and resistance mechanisms.

A progressive reduction in polymicrobial infections and a statistically significant decline in MRSA were observed over time, alongside a sustained decrease in CRPA. In contrast, resistance patterns among Enterobacterales remained heterogeneous, with the persistence and emergence of carbapenem-resistant and 3GC-R strains representing an ongoing clinical challenge. Although the study design does not allow causal inferences regarding the drivers of these trends, the findings underscore the dynamic nature of AMR in DFIs and the importance of long-term, institution-specific surveillance. Extended observation periods may provide more informative insights into resistance evolution than short-term analyses, particularly in complex infections such as DFIs. Overall, these results support the need for continued epidemiological monitoring and cautious interpretation of temporal resistance trends in diabetic foot care. Future studies integrating molecular analyses, multicenter data, and detailed clinical variables will be essential to better characterize resistance dynamics and to inform evidence-based strategies for the management of MDR infections in chronic wounds.
