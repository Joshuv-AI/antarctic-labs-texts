HIV-1 drug resistance and molecular transmission network analysis of newly diagnosed people living with HIV in Beijing, China: a retrospective study from 2015 to 2023
Authors: Li Li, Defu Yuan, Fei Zhao, Yanhua Shen, Can Cui, Christiane Moog, Lijun Sun, Bei Wang, Lifeng Liu, Tong Zhang, Bin Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the annual number of newly reported human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections in Beijing has shown a continuous decline since 2016, the population of people living with HIV (PLWH) has maintained a persistent upward trend. This retrospective study aimed to analyse data from newly diagnosed PLWH from 2015 to 2023 in Beijing to develop precision interventions.MethodsAll newly diagnosed PLWH were subjected to sequence splicing, quality control, information matching, and analysis for pretreatment drug resistance (PDR) and molecular transmission network. The Stanford Drug Resistance Database was used to analyse drug resistance, and Hyphy and Cytoscape software were used to construct a molecular transmission network with the gene distance threshold of 0.015.ResultsA total of 3,569 newly diagnosed PLWH were included in this study. A total of 42 HIV-1 subtypes were identified, with CRF01_AE being the most common subtype, followed by the CRF07_BC and B subtypes. However, for the first time, the dominant strain shifted from CRF01_AE to CRF07_BC in 2023. A total of 340 drug-resistant sequences were obtained, and the overall prevalence of PDR was 9.53% from 2015 to 2023. The most common mutations were distributed among V179, K103, M184, S68 and M46, which presented diverse distributions and combined mutation features. A total of 64 transmission clusters were identified in the network, among which CRF07_BC was dominated by large spreading clusters, whereas CRF01_AE was dominated by small- and medium-sized spreading clusters. The largest cluster for CRF07_BC expanded rapidly from 8 cases in 2015 to 161 cases in 2023.ConclusionsThis study revealed the prevalence of HIV-1 drug resistance and molecular transmission network in Beijing. The change in the dominant HIV strain of participants should be emphasized. Subtype CRF07_BC is prone to forming fast-spreading clusters, and targeted interventions should be designed to prevent high-risk transmission sources and reduce new HIV infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02985-3.

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection seriously threatens public health. At the end of 2023, approximately 39.90 million people worldwide were infected with HIV, of which 1.30 million were newly infected, and 30.70 million were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1]. The number of people living with HIV (PLWH) reported in China in 2024 was approximately 1.35 million, and the number of newly diagnosed HIV cases was approximately 101,600 [2]. By the end of October 2023, Beijing reported a total of 40,840 cases of HIV infection, with 1,404 new cases added that year [3].

The use of ART worldwide has led to dramatic reductions in HIV-related morbidity and mortality. ART was introduced in China at the end of 1999 [4], and the National Free Antiretroviral Treatment Program (NFATP) was implemented in 2002 [5, 6]. Since 2016, all PLWH have been recommended to receive antiviral treatment in China [7, 8]. The government provided several free antiretroviral drugs, such as zidovudine (AZT), stavudine (D4T), didanosine (DDI), nevirapine (NVP), and lamivudine (3TC). Initially, the most popular drug combinations were AZT/D4T + 3TC in NVP-based antiretroviral therapy as a first-line treatment regimen [9]. With the widespread use of ART, the challenge of drug resistance mutations (DRMs) has increased dramatically, resulting in virological failure, and the spread of pretreatment drug resistance (PDR) and HIV transmission among high-risk individuals. PDR is defined as drug resistance detected among antiretroviral drug (ARV)- naïve people starting ART therapy or people with prior ARV drug exposure [10]. In China, the overall prevalence of PDR was 6.80% in 2017 [9]. Another cross-sectional survey conducted to examine the prevalence of PDR among HIV-positive individuals in 8 provincial-level administrative divisions in China revealed that the overall prevalence of PDR was 7.40% in 2022 [10]. The overall prevalence of PDR was 12.90% in Beijing [11], 17.40% in Shanghai [12], 8.30% in Guangxi [13], 13.50% in Tianjin [14], and 18.30% in Xi’an [15], indicating obvious regional differences. Compared with PDR, acquired drug resistance (ADR) accounts for a greater proportion of drug resistance; the overall prevalence in China was 44.96% from 2003 to 2022 [16], and a meta-analysis of the overall prevalence of ADR in China revealed that it was 54.78% [17]. There were significant differences in prevalence due to the different study designs, social customs, economic development levels, and geographic locations such as Shanghai (48.00%) [18], Liaoning (64.84%) [19], Hefei (38.60%) [20] and Beijing (27.80%) [11]. Therefore, monitoring the DRMs plays an important role in preventing ART failure and limiting the spread of PDR.

HIV has high genetic variability. Individuals carrying genetically similar strains of the virus are closely associated through direct or indirect transmission [21]. On this basis, a new genetic distance (GD)-based model has been developed for inferring potential transmission networks [22]. This model has been recommended by the United States (U.S.) Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [23], and the technical guidelines for HIV transmission network monitoring and intervention have been published successively in both the U.S. and China [21]. HIV molecular networks can be constructed on the basis of the abundant data resources of the HIV-1 pol gene, which has been widely used in HIV drug resistance (DR) analysis [24]. In recent years, the use of HIV-1 molecular network analysis has increased [25, 26]. Consequently, continuous molecular surveillance of HIV is an effective measure to address changes in prevalent HIV strains, genetic diversity, and DR trends. Genetics can be used with social transmission networks to identify new transmission events, high-risk spreaders and potentially infected individuals to enable precise interventions.

Long-term longitudinal studies are necessary to track the forefront of the HIV epidemic and develop prevention and treatment strategies [27]. Beijing Youan Hospital, as a specialized hospital for infectious diseases, has been conducting baseline HIV resistance testing for newly diagnosed PLWH since June 2014, and a stable HIV follow-up cohort has been collected. In this study, we used DR testing data from newly diagnosed PLWH from 2015 to 2023 to assess the molecular epidemiology and PDR of HIV-1 in Beijing and to construct a molecular transmission network (MTN) to evaluate the implementation of targeted interventions.

Methods

Study design and data collection

A retrospective analysis of DR data from all newly diagnosed PLWH at Beijing Youan Hospital from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2023 was conducted in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18 years and above with complete general information (sex, age, ethnicity, residence, transmission route, education, marital status, etc.); (2) newly diagnosed with PLWH; and (3) agreed to participate in drug resistance testing and with a pol sequence fragment containing at least 1,000 bases (HXB2: 2,253–3,253). This study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Youan Hospital (approval number: 2024 − 100), and all the participants’ data were analysed anonymously. The peripheral blood plasma was separated within 4 h of blood collection and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

RNA extraction and sanger sequencing

Peripheral blood plasma was collected for HIV-1 drug resistance testing. HIV RNA was purified from 300 µL of plasma via a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Fragments of the HIV pol gene, including the protease (1–99 amino acids) and partial reverse transcriptase (1–255 amino acids) genes, were amplified via in-house nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as described by Wang et al. [28]. The PCR products were then sent to Biomed Co. (Beijing, China) for Sanger sequencing.

Sequence analysis

The sequences stored in the DR database were edited via ChromasPro 1.6 software and then uploaded to the Chinese HIV gene sequence data platform (https://nmdc.cn/hiv/) and the HIV-1 Sequence Quality Control Tool (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/content/sequence/QC/index.html) for quality control, including the removal of sequences with lengths of fewer than 1000 bp and mixed bases ≥ 5%. In addition, the standard reference strain sequences were downloaded from GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, Supplementary Materials Page 2). The sequences were aligned, edited, and analysed with BioEdit 7.2 software. The sequences were trimmed from the beginning of the pol region (HXB2 base 2253, including protease (1–99 amino acids) and partial reverse transcriptase (1–255 amino acids) genes, and the final sequence of approximately 1,100 bp was retained and stored in Fasta format. All sequences were subjected to splicing, quality control, information matching, and sequence analysis to construct the final database for the analysis.

Subtyping and DR analysis

The sample sequences were submitted to the Context-based Modeling for Expeditious Typing (COMET) HIV-1 online typing tool for initial subtyping [29]. Additionally, the BLAST tool (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/content/sequence/BASIC_BLAST/basic_blast.html) was used to compare the sequences against 10 reference sequences. The subtype was determined on the basis of the typing results obtained via both methods. In cases where the results from these two methods were inconsistent, reference sequences were downloaded from the HIV Sequence Database (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/content/index), and an approximately maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed via IQ-Tree software (version 1.6.12). Standardized parameters were applied, with the nucleotide substitution model set to GTR + G + I. Node support values were calculated via the Shimodaira‒Hasegawa-like approximate likelihood ratio test. On the basis of the topological structure and evolutionary relationships of the ML tree, the HIV-1 subtype of the pol region sequence was confirmed, and the final subtype of the sample was determined. Moreover, the Recombination Identification Program (RIP) online subtyping tool (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/content/sequence/RIP/RIP.html) was used to identify the subtypes together.

The sequences were uploaded into the Stanford Drug Resistance Database (https://hivdb.stanford.edu/) and analysed for DR via the HIVdb Program (5 nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs), 7 nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs), and 8 protease inhibitors (PIs) were included in the analysis; see Supplementary Materials Page 3 for details). The database provided information on the DRM sites on each HIV fragment for each sequence and the sensitivity of the sequence to each drug. These mutations were classified as sensitive (total score: 0–9), potential (total score: 10–14), low (total score: 15–29), intermediate (total score: 30–59), or high-level resistance mutations (total score ≥ 60). In the present study, resistance to a particular drug was defined as low-level resistance or above. The DR rate was calculated as follows: DR rate = number of drug-resistant strains ÷ (number of drug-resistant strains + number of nondrug-resistant strains) ∗ 100.

MTN analysis

In accordance with the “Technical Guidelines for HIV Transmission Network Monitoring and Intervention (2021 trial version)” published by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, the gene distances between the matched sequences were calculated via Hyphy (version 2.2.4) software with the TN93 model. The sequences were screened for inclusion in the transmission network according to a gene distance threshold of 0.015 [27]. The visualization and annotation of the transmission network was completed via Cytoscape (version 3.10.2). In this study, large networks were defined as those with ≥ 10 cases, and small/medium networks were defined as those with ≥ 2 cases [30]. The characteristics of the network were described including the number of sequences (nodes), links (degrees/edges), and clusters (groups of linked sequences). The degree of each individual was defined as the number of edges connected with other individuals.

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.4.1) was used for statistical analysis. The data frequency and percentage calculations, the comparison of categorical variables between groups (chi-square test), and the image rendering was performed with R software, and the significance level was set at two-sided 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

During the period from 2015 to 2023, our institution newly reported 6,228 HIV-infected cases. Among these, the pol gene sequences were successfully retrieved from the drug resistance database for 3,712 individuals (Fig. 1). After rigorous quality control and data matching, 143 sequences were excluded on the basis of the following criteria: 20 integrase sequences, 5 sequences with ≥ 5% mixed bases, 28 sequences shorter than 1000 bp, 64 sequences lacking basic demographic information, 2 duplicate test records, and 24 sequences from individuals under 18 years of age. Consequently, 3,569 high-quality pol sequences (96.15% of the retrieved sequences) were included in the final analysis. Comparative analysis between the 3,569 sequenced cases and the total newly reported 6,228 cases revealed no statistically significant differences in demographic characteristics except for educational attainment (Supplementary Table S1).

The study population predominantly consisted of individuals currently residing in Beijing (83.41%, 2,977/3,569), males (96.13%, 3,431/3,569), and those of Han ethnicity (93.36%, 3,332/3,569). The 25–34 years age group represented the largest proportion (44.75%, 1,597/3,569), followed by the 35–44 years age group (20.79%, 742/3,569). Educational attainment was primarily at the college level or higher (61.39%, 2,191/3,569), with unmarried individuals constituting the majority (64.81%, 2,313/3,569). Homosexual transmission accounted for 79.49% (2,837/3,569) of infections, whereas 74.67% (2,665/3,569) had no history of sexually transmitted disease (STD) at diagnosis. Longitudinal analysis revealed that the proportion of individuals aged ≥ 55 years increased from 1.28% in 2015 to 8.44% in 2023. Concurrently, the proportion of Beijing residents at diagnosis increased from 75.64% to 86.54%, whereas nonlocal cases decreased from 24.36% to 13.46% during the same period. The comprehensive demographic characteristics are detailed in Table 1 and Supplementary Figures S1-S8. Further analysis of the male individuals who underwent DR testing was performed in this study since males accounted for 96.13% of the population. A significant difference (χ2 = 11.662, P = 0.003) emerged in infection routes between the male individuals tested (3,431 cases) and all individuals tested (3,569 cases) (Supplementary Table S2), with male individuals demonstrating a greater proportion of homosexual infection (82.69% vs. 79.49%).

Subtype distribution

In addition to unique recombinant forms (URFs), a total of 42 HIV-1 subtypes and circulating recombinant forms (CRFs) were detected in this study (Table 2). The most common subtype was CRF01_AE (41.83%, 1,493/3,569), followed by CRF07_BC (33.31%, 1,189/3,569). The other subtypes identified were B (7.62%, 272/3,569), CRF114_0155 (2.89%, 103/3,569), CRF55_01B (2.58%, 92/3,569), URFs (1.40%, 50/3,569), CRF65_cpx (0.87%, 31/3,569), CRF79_0107 (0.87%, 31/3,569), CRF113_0107 (0.84%, 30/3,569), and CRF80_0107 (0.78%, 28/3,569) (Fig. 2A). Since 2015, the proportion of CRF07_BC in PLWH has gradually increased from 15.38% to 37.99% in 2023, whereas the proportion of CRF01_AE and B subtypes has decreased from 61.54% and 16.67% to 35.36% and 6.60%, respectively, in 2023 (Table 1). Notably, the proportion of CRF07_BC among individuals undergoing DR testing has gradually increased (Fig. 2B) since 2015, and CRF01_AE approached this value for the first time (37.68% vs. 37.96%) in 2020. Finally, CRF07_BC surpassed CRF01_AE (37.99% vs. 35.36%) and became the most prevalent subtype in Beijing in 2023. Further analysis of the DR in male individuals revealed that the distribution of subtypes significantly differed according to age (χ2 = 51.709, P < 0.001) and ethnicity (χ2 = 12.127, P = 0.033). CRF01_AE (42.03%, 1,442/3,431), CRF07_BC (33.26%, 1,141/3,431) and B (7.37%, 253/3,431) were the common subtypes in males in this study (Supplementary Table S3).

DR analysis

Drug resistance analysis was performed on 3,569 pol gene sequences, revealing 340 drug-resistant sequences (9.53%, 340/3,569) demonstrating low-level or high resistance to (at least one) NNRTI, NRTI, or PI (Fig. 3A). The resistance distribution revealed that 192 sequences (5.38%, 192/3,569) were resistant to NNRTIs, 77 (2.16%, 77/3,569) were resistant to NRTIs, and 121 (3.39%, 121/3,569) were resistant to PIs (Table 3). Cross-resistance analysis revealed 48 sequences exhibiting resistance to multiple drug classes, with dual NNRTI/NRTI resistance predominating (87.50%, 42/48), followed by NNRTI/PI and NRTI/PI coresistance (10.42% each, 5/48), and triple-class resistance in 2 cases (4.17%, 2/48). Chi-square analysis incorporating three infection routes revealed a significantly greater proportion of “others” infection routes among drug-resistant individuals (2.06%) than among drug-sensitive individuals (χ²=6.695, p = 0.035). However, no statistically significant differences emerged in the infection route distribution between drug-resistant and drug-sensitive individuals when the analysis was restricted to predominant infection routes: homosexual infection accounted for 81.68% vs. 80.03%, whereas heterosexual infection accounted for 18.32% vs. 19.97%, respectively (Supplementary Table S4).

Among the 340 resistant sequences, the reverse transcriptase region contained 33 mutation sites, including 17 NNRTI-associated sites with a total of 304 mutations. Construction of the MTN

With thresholds of gene distance ≤ 0.015, 10.90% (389/3,569) of the sequences were finally connected with at least one other sequence and included in the MTN (Fig. 4). There were no significant differences in sex, age, ethnicity, education degree, marital status, or routes of infection between people with sequences within and outside of the network, but there was statistically significant difference in DR (Table 4). The proportion of individuals with HIV within the MTN who are sensitive to antiretroviral drugs was greater than that of individuals outside the MTN (93.32% vs. 90.13%). A total of 389 sequences formed 684 edges within 64 transmission clusters, with the number of nodes in each cluster ranging from 2 to 161 in the network. There were 37 transmission clusters consisting of the CRF01_AE subtype (99 sequences), with the largest cluster containing 11 nodes; 10 clusters composed exclusively of the CRF07_BC subtype (184 sequences), compared with CRF01_AE, CRF07_BC were prone to form larger clusters with the largest cluster containing 161 nodes; 6 clusters composed of the B subtype, with a maximum of 4 nodes; 3 clusters composed of the CRF114_0155 subtype, with one cluster containing 14 nodes and the other two clusters containing 3 and 2 nodes, and 2 clusters made up of the CRF55_01B subtype, with the largest cluster containing 10 nodes. Additionally, the CRF59_01B, CRF65_cpx, CRF76_01B, CRF80_0107, CRF113_0107 and CRF119_0107 subtypes each formed a cluster (Fig. 4A).

Within the MTN, 6 transmission clusters contained ≥ 10 nodes, accounting for 55.78% (217/389) of all individuals included in the network. Notably, two transmission clusters were identified as continuously growing clusters, with new sequences entering the network each year since the clusters’ identification. The largest transmission cluster consisted of 161 nodes (Fig. 4B), all of which were of the CRF07_BC subtype. Most individuals in this cluster were male (96.89%, 156/161), unmarried (57.76%, 93/161), acquired the infection through homosexual transmission (82.61%, 133/161), and had a college degree or above (56.52%, 91/161). This cluster included 8 sequences reported in 2015, with the highest numbers of reported sequences observed in 2016 (34 sequences) and 2017 (39 sequences). In 2018 and 2019, the cluster included 24 and 21 new individual sequences, respectively. The number of newly reported sequences decreased to 4 in 2020 and remained relatively stable in subsequent years (2021: 10 sequences; 2022: 12 sequences; 2023: 9 sequences). The second continuously growing cluster consisted of 14 nodes belonging to the CRF114_0155 subtype (Fig. 4B). All individuals in this cluster were male, with the majority being unmarried (78.57%, 11/14), acquiring the infection through homosexual transmission (85.71%, 12/14), and holding a college degree or above (78.57%, 11/14).

Among the 389 sequences included in the network, 16.20% (63/389) were infected through heterosexual sex, of which 54 (85.71%) and 9 (14.29%) were male and female, respectively. The female individuals were distributed in 5 clusters (7.81%, 5/64), which consisted of both male and female individuals, and the remaining 59 clusters (92.19%, 59/64) consisted exclusively of male individuals. While 38.98% (23/59) of the clusters consisted of male individuals, at least one individual was infected with HIV through a heterosexual transmission route. The data of female and male individuals infected with HIV via heterosexual transmission route are showed in Fig. 4C.

We also observed that 26 of the 340 DR sequences (7.65%, 26/340) were included in 10 clusters (Fig. 4D). Seven clusters contained a mix of drug-resistant and drug-sensitive sequences, and 3 clusters included only drug-resistant sequences. The individuals in the PDR clusters were male and infected via the homosexual transmission route (80.77%, 21/26), and the subtype was CRF07_BC (38.46%, 10/26).

Discussion

China is a country with very complex HIV genetic diversity [31]. In the past five years, more than 40 CRFs have been reported in China, including CRF109_0107, CRF114_0155, CRF101_01B, and CRF102_0107 [32–35], and many URFs have also been reported by various provinces and cities throughout the country. As a political, economic and cultural center of China, Beijing has experienced a continuous flow of people, and the number of PLWH has been increasing since the first acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) case was reported in 1985. This study retrospectively analysed the relationships among demographic characteristics, subtype diversity, PDR incidence and the transmission network of newly diagnosed PLWH in a hospital in Beijing over 9 consecutive years from 2015 to 2023. The results revealed that the prevalence of PDR was 9.53%, and 42 HIV-1 subtypes and 64 transmission clusters were identified, which revealed the complexity and diversity of the AIDS epidemic in Beijing. Moreover, the results revealed an annually increasing trend among individuals aged ≥ 55 years and Beijing residents.

The present study revealed that there were more than 40 subtypes and multiple URFs, but CRF01_AE, CRF07_BC and subtype B were still the most common evolutionary branches in Beijing, which is consistent with several studies in this region [11, 36, 37]. Notably, CRF01_AE and subtype B showed a gradual downwards trend, whereas CRF07_BC showed a rapid upwards trend, which was consistent with the overall trend of HIV subtype variation in China [38]. CRF01_AE dominated from 2015 to 2018, but the proportion of CRF07_BC among individuals has gradually increased since 2015, and was approached for the first time in 2020 in this study. In addition, CRF07_BC surpassed CRF01_AE (37.99% vs. 35.36%) and became the most prevalent subtype in Beijing in 2023. One study revealed that the number of CRF07_BC infections in PLWH in Hebei Province in 2021 exceeded that of CRF01_AE infections for the first time (unpublished data). The fact that Beijing and Hebei share a border also confirms the spatial possibility of a shift in the dominant strain in Beijing. In addition, CRF114_0155, which was first reported by Li Yang et al. [33] in 2021 as a novel CRF originating from Henan Province, was ranked fourth after subtype B in this study. The genome of CRF114_0155, which is the first HIV-1 third-generation CRF and the first CRF55_01B offspring, consists of five segments, three of which are inherited from CRF01_AE Cluster 4 and two from CRF55_01B. The emergence of CRF114_0155 reflects the increasing complexity of the HIV-1 subtype. The rapid emergence of diverse recombinant subtypes poses a substantial challenge to the prevention and control of HIV/AIDS in China, making monitoring the molecular epidemiology of HIV-1 critically important.

Drug-resistant variants of HIV-1 may develop in most cases as a result of drug pressure or may be acquired by transmission in untreated individuals. Most of these variants rapidly revert to wild type (WT) without drug selection pressure, but some variants may persist in latent infected cells long after transmission to a new host, limiting the options for ART [39]. Therefore, monitoring DR is critical to clinical care. We determined the PDR prevalence in Beijing for 9 consecutive years; the overall DR rate was 9.53%, and the rate of NNRTI resistance was 5.38%, which was moderate (5–15%) according to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition [9]. The overall DR rate and NNRTI and NRTI resistance rates tended to decrease from 2015 to 2017, which was consistent with the findings of Shi et al. [11]. This result may be related to the ‘Treat for all’ policy that began in 2016. However, from the lowest point in 2017 to 2023, the prevalence of PDR gradually increased, possibly because NNRTIs and NRTIs as the first-line drugs for anti-HIV treatment have been widely used in China for more than 1 year. Florence Doualla-Bell et al. reported that 7 of the 23 individuals whose DDI-based regimens failed presented with K65R substitution after exposure to combination DDI/D4T therapy for only 8 months (range, 4 to 18 months) [40]. This may explain why the overall DR rate began to rise gradually beginning in 2017. The rate of PDR in this study was higher than those reported in Hebei (8.30%) [41], Guangzhou (7.40%) [42] and Nanning (8.97%) [43], which may be related to the high rate of DR in 2015–2016 and to differences in the follow-up and management of PLWH in different regions and different periods reported in the literature. Compared with that reported from other hospitals in Beijing (from 2013 − 2020) [11], the trend of PDR was basically consistent.

Among the three kinds of antiretroviral drugs, the frequency of NNRTI-associated PDR mutations was significantly greater than that of NRTI-associated and PI-associated PDR mutations, with the most frequent mutation being V179 (25.33%, 77/304). The mutation sites were essentially consistent with those reported by other hospitals in Beijing [11], Tianjin [44] and Shanghai [45]. V179D/E mutations can lead to potential low-level resistance to NVP (nevirapine), EFV (Efavirenz), ETR (etravirine), and RPV (rilpivirine) [46], whereas the combination of V179D with other mutations, such as K103R, can increase HIV DR by 16–27 times [47]. Considering that the majority of NNRTI-associated PDR mutations are caused by a high proportion of V179 (25.33%) mutations, caution should be taken in regimen selection. More importantly, PDR to NNRTIs represented the major contribution of HIV DR; hence, the WHO recently recommended phasing out the use of EFV and NVP in first-line regimens at any DR rate, and not only at the 10% threshold [48]. For NRTI-resistant sequences, the mutation frequency of M184 was the highest (24.84%, 38/153), which was consistent with reports from Ningbo [49]. The mutation M184 can cause high resistance to FTC and 3TC, and low resistance to abacavir (ABC). S68G often cooccurs with K65R, with a frequency of 21.1%–61.7% in various subtypes, but the study also suggested that S68G may be a natural polymorphism and compensatory mutation of K65R and that this mutation does not affect susceptibility to NRTIs [50]. For PI-resistant sequences, the most common mutation was M46 (87.50%, 49/56), which is associated with nelfinavir (NFV) and tipranavir/ritonavir (TPV/r) and has little effect on lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/r) [51]. However, most PI-resistant viruses require multiple accessory mutations outside of proteases to play a role in resistance, and these additional requirements contribute to the high genetic barrier to resistance of increased PIs [52]. In our study, the prevalence of PI-associated PDR was low, the high frequency of M46 mutation alone may not affect the effect of PIs, and further studies with broader sizes are needed to monitor the prevalence of DRMs for PIs.

We further analysed the important role of MTN in identifying individuals at high risk of HIV transmission. We observed demographic differences between the participants within and outside of the network, and found that individuals aged 25–34 years and 35–45 years, Han Chinese, married or cohabiting individuals, HIV-infected individuals without PDR, homosexual individuals, and those with high or technical secondary degree were more likely to be included in the network. Moreover, we analysed the potential factors of subtype conversion in Beijing through the construction of a transmission network. In the present study, 99 CRF01_AE sequences formed 37 clusters, whereas 184 CRF07_BC sequences constituted 10 clusters, suggesting that individuals with the CRF07_BC subtype are more prone to link with other cases and build large clusters, leading to a more extensive spread of CRF07_BC, which is evidenced by the largest growing transmission cluster containing 161 nodes in our study. This largest cluster formed by CRF07_BC has gradually expanded from 8 cases in 2015 to 161 cases over 9 years with rapid annual growth, which was consistent with reports of large clusters of the CRF07_BC subtype in MSM populations in the northern region [53], whereas the small- and medium-sized transmission clusters play a major role in CRF01_AE transmission. Therefore, in terms of cluster size and transmission speed, coupled with more frequent population movement, the large and annual growth transmitting clusters of the CRF07_BC subtype were the driving force for the shift from CRF01_AE to CRF07_BC in Beijing. Notably, the proportion of newly diagnosed individuals in the largest cluster began to decline in 2017, showing an “inverted-V” curve, with the peak appearing in 2017, subsequently displaying a stable or decreasing state. This trend is similar to that reported by Ye et al. [54]. This suggests that the expansion of large clusters is slowing down and flanked by a decrease in the number of newly infected individuals, indicating the effectiveness of regular interventions. However, targeted intervention and supervision of high-risk individuals are still needed to interrupt the spread of HIV due to the continuous emergence of new subtype clusters, such as the CRF114_0155 cluster, and various subtypes reported by Ye et al. [54],

The transmission of PDR is an important factor in assessing cluster risk. Studies have shown that individuals with PDR are at greater risk of being included in the network than are individuals without PDR [55]. A total of 7.65% (26/340) of the PDR sequences were included in 10 clusters, and 9 sequences were in the largest transmission cluster (161 nodes) formed by CRF07_BC. PDR occasionally appeared but did not constitute the majority of transmission nodes. However, given the potential danger of the widespread transmission of PDR, close and continuous monitoring is still needed.

Compared with the size of the networked population, clusters that include multiple transmission routes pose greater communication risk [55]. In the largest CRF07_BC cluster, multiple nodes were related to heterosexual transmission. Women and men who have sex with both men and women (MSMWs) became bridges between cluster members and those outside the cluster. The emergence of multiple and complex transmission routes increases the risk of HIV spread [56], and strict intervention should be implemented for the target individuals.

Limitations of the study

This study had several limitations. First, all the results of this study were obtained from laboratory data and theoretical analysis. The application of targeted interventions in high-risk individuals based only on genetic transmission networks remains insufficient in actual clinical practice. Second, some MSM may have not declared their status due to stigma, and the mode of transmission of some people may not have been accurately reported. Therefore, precise interventions need to be implemented in conjunction with epidemiological investigations for validation. Additionaly, our study population was drawn from a specialized infectious disease hospital in Beijing, and all conclusions are based on nine years of continuous data from this hospital. To avoid potential bias, further studies with larger sample sizes should be conducted.

Conclusion

This study reveals the diversity and complexity of the HIV-1 subtype distribution and genetic transmission network among newly diagnosed PLWH in our hospital in Beijing. The dominant strain changed in 2023, with CRF07_BC becoming the dominant strain. In addition, the prevalence of PDR was moderate, and the most common mutations were distributed among V179, K103, M184, S68 and M46. A total of 64 transmission clusters were identified in MTN, and the largest cluster for CRF07_BC expanded rapidly from 8 cases in 2015 to 161 cases in 2023. The data suggest that the construction of molecular networks can help rapidly identify high-risk individuals, implement surveillance, and comprehensive interventions for key fast-growing clusters in combination with actual epidemiological investigations to reduce the potential risk for HIV transmission.
