Targeting GD2 with naxitamab overcomes GD3 synthase-driven immune suppression in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Vivek Anand, Bolutyfe Oderinde, Maryam Siddiqui, Anudishi Tyagi, Jenny Borgman, Chong Wu, Michael Andreeff, V. Lokesh Battula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gangliosides are acidic glycosphingolipids involved in cell-adhesion, signal-transduction and tumor progression. GD3 synthase (GD3S/ST8SIA1), a key enzyme in ganglioside biosynthesis, is upregulated in many cancers, including GD2+ breast cancer stem-like cells (BCSCs) in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Here, we demonstrated the immunomodulatory role of GD3S and identified a fully humanized anti-GD2 antibody, naxitamab, as a therapeutic tool to target GD3S/GD2+ breast tumors. GD3S expression correlates with immune-checkpoint activation and reduced immune infiltration. Ectopic overexpression of GD3S suppressed macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and NK or T cell-induced cell death in BC cells. Lipidomic analysis identified GD2 as the major effector ganglioside altered upon GD3S overexpression in TNBC cells. Moreover, naxitamab treatment enhanced macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity and inhibited tumor growth in a TNBC patient-derived xenograft model. Our findings highlight GD3S-driven immunosuppression and provide proof-of-concept that naxitamab, with activated immune cells, reverses this effect, revealing its therapeutic potential in treating GD2+ BC.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the most prevalent cancer affecting women across the United States, with an estimated one in eight facing this diagnosis in their lifetime1,2. Despite a significant 40% reduction in BC mortality from 1975 to 2017, the challenges of therapeutic resistance and disease recurrence persist3–5. Among the various forms of BC, triple-negative BC (TNBC) is the most lethal, often showing resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies6–9. The complexity of BC lies in its heterogeneity, characterized by various cell types, each characterized by distinct biomarkers10–12. Within this diverse cellular landscape, cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) are particularly significant, representing a small yet critical fraction in both primary and metastatic tumors13. These CSCs share biological characteristics with normal stem cells and are often found to be in a quiescent state; they possess the ability to initiate tumors, regenerate, and proliferate. Such traits predispose CSCs to metastasis and resistance to conventional chemotherapy14–17.

Recent studies indicate that the transient acquisition of stemness is a mechanism by which tumors evade the immune system and develop resistance to therapies18–21. Our research has identified the ganglioside GD2 as a marker of BC stem cells (BCSCs) and demonstrated that the enzyme GD3 synthase (GD3S/ST8SIA1) is upregulated in GD2+ BCSCs22–27. Gangliosides are distributed across the cell membrane, where they regulate various cellular functions, such as adhesion, migration, proliferation, and immune modulation28–36. GD3S is a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of B- and C-series gangliosides37,38. We recently reported that GD2 is expressed in approximately 65% of primary TNBC tumors and is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the chimeric anti-GD2 antibody dinutuximab targets GD2+ BCSCs, inhibiting tumor development by directing natural killer (NK) cells to GD2+ breast tumors and inducing antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC)27. Additionally, we found that GD3S is significantly upregulated in TNBCs with TP53 mutations, which promotes tumor progression by activating the mTOR pathway, and that inhibition of GD3S expression completely abrogated tumor growth and metastasis in animal models. Of particular relevance, recently we observed that p53 with specific gain-of-function (GOF) mutations can directly upregulate GD3S expression in TNBC, thereby promoting the GD2+ BCSC phenotype and conferring a survival advantage to breast cancer cells39,40.

Gangliosides are sialylated glycosphingolipids; most ganglioside species exhibit prominent immunosuppressive activities, acting as “checkpoint molecules” that counteract ongoing anti-tumor immunity36,41,42. They can disrupt cell-mediated immune responses by inhibiting T cell activation and proliferation, promoting the recruitment and function of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and interfering with the cytotoxic activity of NK cells. Several studies have highlighted the critical role of gangliosides and their key regulatory enzymes in shaping the tumor microenvironment (TME) and suppressing anti-tumor immunity, making them important targets for cancer immunotherapies28–30,32,43–48. Targeting these molecules is a promising immunotherapeutic approach, with several clinical trials currently ongoing for therapies in neuroblastoma (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05990751, NCT06528496, and NCT06485947). In this study, we investigated the role of GD3S in immune regulation and identified GD3S-regulated GD2+ BC cells as a potential therapeutic target in vitro and in vivo. We demonstrated that GD3S regulates macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, T cell–mediated cytotoxicity, and NK cell–mediated ADCC of BC cells. Additionally, we explored the role of the novel humanized anti-GD2 antibody naxitamab (hu3F8) in targeting GD2+ BC cells and inhibiting BC growth by enhancing NK cell–mediated ADCC and macrophage-mediated antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP).

Results

GD3S expression is associated with immune checkpoint protein expression and reduces immune cell infiltration in BC

GD3S is a key regulatory enzyme in the biosynthesis of B- and C-series gangliosides, including GD2/GD3, and these gangliosides are known to inhibit immune cell proliferation and activity37,38,41–43,49. Since evasion of the antitumor immune response is a hallmark of tumor progression and metastasis, we examined the potential role of the ganglioside machinery in promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment. To this end, we analyzed the co-expression of GD3S (ST8SIA1) mRNA and key immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) markers in the Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Pan-Cancer datasets50. We found a significant (P < 0.001) positive association between GD3S and CTLA-4 (Pearson r = 0.38, 95% CI [0.3412–0.4166]), LAG-3 (r = 0.33, 95% CI [0.2888–0.3674]), and PD-1 (r = 0.32, 95% CI [0.2767–0.3560]) levels in 2509 BC patients in the METABRIC dataset. These correlations remained significant after adjusting for multiple testing (adjusted p values < 0.001). Similarly, GD3S expression was positively associated with CTLA-4 (Pearson r = 0.48, 95% CI [0.4308–0.5232]), P < 0.001), LAG-3 (r = 0.36, 95% CI [0.3043–0.4083], P < 0.001), and PD-1 (r = 0.41, 95% CI [0.3607–0.4597], P < 0.001) levels in 1084 BC patients in the TCGA dataset. GD3S expression was also positively correlated with the expression of CD25, a well-known marker of regulatory T cells, in both METABRIC (r = 0.38, 95% CI [0.3376–0.4133], P < 0.001) and TCGA (r = 0.48, 95% CI [0.4321–0.5241], P < 0.001) patient datasets (Fig. 1A). In addition to the immune checkpoint markers discussed above, we also observed a significant positive correlation between GD3S and additional key ICI markers, including TIGIT, VISTA, TIM-3, and PD-L1, in both METABRIC and TCGA breast cancer datasets, indicating the role of GD3S in shaping an immunosuppressive TME (Fig. S1A).Fig. 1GD3S expression is correlated with increased immune checkpoint proteins and reduced immune cell infiltration in BC.A Analysis of GD3S (ST8SIA1) mRNA expression in METABRIC and TCGA (pan cancer) BC patient datasets shows significant correlation with immune checkpoint inhibitors. To validate these findings, we assessed GD3S expression and key immune cell infiltration in FFPE primary tumor tissue samples (n = 20) by Lunaphore Comet fluorescence IHC analysis. We designed a panel of protein markers that were detected simultaneously by specific antibodies on a single tissue section. The tumor region, distinguished from the stroma by pan-cytokeratin (PANCK) expression, showed increased cancer cell proliferation as indicated by Ki67 staining, which was positively correlated with the tumor region (Pearson r = 0.64, 95% CI [0.2637–0.8483], P = 0.003), as shown in Figs. 1B and S1B. In tissues with high GD3S expression, there was reduced infiltration of macrophages into the primary tumors (identified by CD45+CD86+ and CD45+CD163+), with most cells detected in the stroma. Similarly, T cells (CD45+CD8+ and CD45+CD4+) were not able to infiltrate the primary tumors easily, with most cells lining up in the stroma. In contrast, tissues with low GD3S expression showed increased infiltration of both macrophages and T cells into the primary tumors (P < 0.0001, P = ns; Figs. 1B, C and S1C, S1D). Interestingly, Foxp3 exhibited a significant positive correlation with high GD3S expression in the tumor region (Pearson r = 0.93, 95% CI [0.7313 to 0.9841], P < 0.0001), in contrast to tumors with low GD3S expression, where it showed a weak negative correlation (Pearson r = −0.17, 95% CI [−0.7519 to 0.5531], P = 0.65) (Figure S1E). There was no significant difference in NK cells (CD45+CD56+) in these tissue sections (Fig. 1B, C). Overall, the spatial arrangement in these sections suggests that increased GD3S expression inhibits immune cell infiltration into primary breast tumors, contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor environment.

Overexpression of GD3S inhibits macrophage-, NK cell–, and T cell–mediated killing of BC cells

Given the observation that increased GD3S expression in primary tumors is associated with reduced immune cell infiltration, and vice versa, in BC patients, we sought to further elucidate the influence of GD3S overexpression on immune cell activity. To this end, first, we stably overexpressed GD3S in MCF7, SUM159, and BT549 BC cell lines using lentivirus. Next, to determine the role of GD3S in immune cell–mediated killing, we co-cultured the empty vector control (EV-CTRL) or GD3S-overexpressing (GD3S-OE) cells with ex vivo expanded healthy donor peripheral blood–derived macrophages, NK cells, and T cells. Live cell imaging using the pH-sensitive pHrodo dye in the IncuCyte system revealed that macrophage-mediated phagocytosis was significantly inhibited in MCF7-, BT549-, and SUM159-overexpressing GD3S cells compared to control cells (P < 0.0001, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A, B and S2A). Moreover, we observed a significant decrease (P < 0.0001) in annexin V binding when co-cultured with primary NK cells, indicating reduced NK cell–mediated apoptosis in GD3S-overexpressing cells compared to in control cells (Figs. 2C, D and S2B). Interestingly, when we co-cultured these BC cells with T cells, we also observed a substantial decrease (P < 0.0001) in T cell–mediated killing in GD3S-overexpressing cells compared to in their control counterparts (Figs. 2E, F and S2C). Absence of GD3S expression promotes macrophage-, NK cell–, and T cell–mediated killing of BC cells

On the basis of the above results, we hypothesized that the absence of GD3S would have the opposite effect on immune cell function from that of GD3S and enhance tumor cell killing. To test this hypothesis, we deleted GD3S expression in SUM159 and BT549 TNBC cell lines using the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system and assessed its impact on immune cell–mediated killing. The absence of GD3S (GD3S-KO) completely abrogated the expression of ganglioside GD2 in these cells, as confirmed by a flow cytometry analysis (Fig. S3A–C).

These stable GD3S-KO cells, along with their control counterparts, were co-cultured with macrophages, NK cells, and T cells, and the effect on immune cell–mediated killing of BC cells was measured using the IncuCyte system. As expected, co-culture with macrophages resulted in a significant increase in phagocytosis in GD3S-KO SUM159 (P < 0.0001) and BT549 (P = 0.0113) cells compared to their control counterparts (Figs. 3A and S4A). Similarly, a significant increase in NK cell–mediated apoptosis was observed in GD3S-KO SUM159 (P < 0.0001) and BT549 (P < 0.0001) cells compared to controls (Figs. 3B and S4B). Additionally, an increase in T cell–mediated killing was observed in GD3S-KO SUM159 (P < 0.0001) and BT549 (P < 0.0001) cells (Figs. 3C and S4C). Ganglioside GD2 downstream of GD3S mediates the immunosuppressive effects of GD3S in BC

In the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway, the enzyme GD3S has been reported to regulate the biosynthesis of B- and C-series gangliosides (Fig. 4A). To identify the key gangliosides regulated by GD3S that are responsible for mediating downstream effects, we conducted lipid metabolomic analysis using both positive and negative ionization to detect lipidomic shifts between SUM159 and MCF7 control and GD3S-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4B). A comprehensive data processing and statistical analysis of unbiased peak characterization and metabolite identification revealed 974 distinct features, of which 369 were confidently identified. Further statistical examination of lipid profile variations between cell lines highlighted significant changes in ganglioside abundance. Significant alterations in ganglioside levels were defined by a log2 fold change greater than 1 (Log2fc > 1) and an adjusted p-value below 0.05. Notably, GD2 exhibited the highest level of upregulation among all detected gangliosides, with a log2 fold change exceeding 7 (Log2fc > 7) and an adjusted p-value of less than 0.001. The most striking shifts included reductions in GM2 and GM3 and enrichments of GD2 and GD3 (Fig. 4C). GM3 and GM2 are A-series gangliosides positioned upstream of GD3S, whereas GD3 and GD2 are B-series gangliosides located downstream of GD3S, as illustrated in the heatmap and volcano plots (Fig. 4C, D). To further validate our mass spectrometry–based lipidomic analysis, which indicated that GD3S overexpression leads to the upregulation of gangliosides GD2 and GD3, we generated stable cell lines overexpressing GD3S in both TNBC (SUM159, BT549) and non-TNBC (MCF7) cells (Fig. 4E). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in GD2 expression (P < 0.0001) across all GD3S-overexpressing cell lines, with more than 90% GD2-positive cells in all lines compared to baseline GD2 levels of only 9.99%, 11.1%, and 2.28% in SUM159, BT549, and MCF7 cells, respectively (Fig. 4F). Additionally, GD3S overexpression showed variable effects on ganglioside GD3 levels compared to control cells. BT549 cells exhibited an increase from 1.47% GD3 in control cells to 46.6% in GD3S-overexpressing cells, while MCF7 cells showed an increase from 4.42% to 12.1% (Fig. S5). These results further substantiate the lipidomic findings, demonstrating that GD3S overexpression predominantly drives the upregulation of B-series gangliosides GD3 and GD2.

Naxitamab induces NK cell–mediated ADCC and macrophage-mediated ADCP in BC cells

We have previously shown that the chimeric anti-GD2 antibody dinutuximab (clone 14.G2a) targets GD2+ BCSCs by enhancing NK cell–mediated apoptosis27. Recently, a humanized (IgG1) anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody, naxitamab (clone hu3F8), received accelerated approval from the US FDA for the treatment of pediatric patients with high-risk neuroblastoma51. Subsequent studies demonstrated that naxitamab, in combination with PBMCs and polymorphonuclear leukocytes, induces ADCC against neuroblastoma51,52. To determine whether naxitamab enhances the efficacy of NK cells against BC cells, we co-cultured Hs578T and HCC1395 BC cell lines, both with relatively high GD2 levels, with or without activated NK cells at different E:T cell ratios (1:2); we then treated them with naxitamab or hIgG at 5 μg/ml. To measure NK cell–mediated ADCC, we assessed apoptosis by annexin V binding to target cells using the IncuCyte live cell analysis system. Different cell-based immunotherapies have shown promising outcomes in hematological malignancies, but their efficacy in solid tumors is less well-known53–56. M1 macrophages, recognized for their proinflammatory properties, can inhibit tumor progression through direct phagocytosis of tumor cells57,58. In this study, we hypothesized that naxitamab could also enhance ADCP of BC cells by M1 macrophages derived from healthy donors. To test this hypothesis, we co-cultured HCC1395 and Hs578T BC cells, with or without macrophages, at a 1:1 E:T ratio and treated them with naxitamab or hIgG in a dose-dependent manner (5, 10, 20 μg/ml). Treatment with naxitamab resulted in a significant increase in macrophage-mediated phagocytosis (P < 0.0001) compared to hIgG treatment or macrophages alone (Figs. 5C–F and S6B). To further validate these findings, we performed a 3D tumor spheroid assay using RFP-labeled Hs578T cells to generate tumor spheroids, which were co-cultured with M1 macrophages at a 10:1 (E:T) ratio, followed by treatment with hIgG, naxitamab, anti-SIRPα antibody (SE5A5), or their combination. Both naxitamab and anti-SIRPα monotherapies significantly reduced spheroid size compared to untreated or hIgG controls (P < 0.0001); however, the combination treatment completely eliminated the spheroids (P < 0.0001). These findings suggest that dual targeting with naxitamab and anti-SIRPα synergistically enhances macrophage-mediated ADCP, leading to complete elimination of TNBC tumor spheroids (Fig. 5G, H). Overall, these findings strongly suggest that naxitamab enhances both NK cell–mediated ADCC and macrophage-mediated ADCP, offering a promising therapeutic approach to targeting GD2+ BC cells and potentially improving the efficacy of immunotherapies in solid tumors.

Naxitamab in combination with NK cells and macrophages inhibits primary tumor growth in the TNBC PDX model

Our in vitro analysis confirmed that naxitamab upregulates NK cell– and macrophage-mediated killing of BC cells. To validate naxitamab’s therapeutic efficacy in vivo, we tested its effect on TNBC PDX growth in combination with activated human peripheral blood–derived NK cells in immunodeficient NSG mice. We orthotopically implanted PIM005 TNBC PDX cells with over 35% GD2 expression into the fourth inguinal mammary fat pad of immunodeficient NSG mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ; n = 10). Once the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomized into tumor-size-matched treatment groups: (1) untreated, (2) hIgG + NK cells, and (3) naxitamab + NK cells. In groups 2 and 3, 20 mg/kg of hIgG or naxitamab was administered via tail vein injection, along with recombinant human IL-2 (rhIL2; 30,000 U per mouse) and 1 × 107 NK cells, twice a week for 5 weeks, starting in the fifth week after tumor implantation (Fig. 6A). Simultaneous treatment with naxitamab and NK cells resulted in a significant reduction (>75%; P < 0.0001) in both tumor volume and tumor weight (Fig. 6B–D). Surprisingly, contrary to our expectations, no decrease in tumor volume was observed in control mice treated with hIgG and NK cells. Discussion

In recent years, significant advancements have been made in tumor immunotherapy. However, its success in solid tumors has been more modest than that in hematologic malignancies, largely due to the immunosuppressive microenvironment that these tumors foster53,56, which impairs the cytotoxic functions of immune effector cells, such as NK cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages54,55,58–60. Our study identified the critical role of GD3S (ST8SIA1) and its downstream ganglioside GD2 in promoting an immunosuppressive TME in BC, particularly in TNBC. Notably, we also observed that elevated GD3S/GD2 expression diminished immune cell activity not only in TNBC but also in ER/PR+ MCF7 cells, suggesting that the immunosuppressive influence of this pathway transcends subtype boundaries and may hold broader significance across breast cancer. GD2 has been reported to interact with Siglec receptors on immune cells, which deliver inhibitory signals that suppress immune cell activation61,62. For the first time, we have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of a novel humanized anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody, naxitamab, in inhibiting BC growth by activating NK cells, T cells, and macrophages to mediate the killing of BC cells. These findings lay the preclinical groundwork for assessing the safety and therapeutic value of naxitamab in BC patients.

BC is a highly heterogeneous disease, with TNBC representing the most aggressive and difficult-to-treat subtype6–8,10,63. TNBC is characterized by the absence of hormone receptors (ER−/PR−) and HER2 expression, making it unresponsive to hormone therapy and HER2-targeted treatments. This subtype is also enriched with BCSCs, which are capable of self-renewal, metastasis, and immune evasion associated with a higher prevalence of BCSCs, which contribute to therapeutic resistance, metastasis, and immune evasion64,65. We previously identified ganglioside GD2 as a functional marker of BCSCs and demonstrated that the enzyme GD3S is upregulated in GD2+ BCSCs, which is also associated with poor clinical outcomes, particularly in TNBC patients, and is linked to enhanced tumorigenic properties, including mTOR pathway activation and apoptosis resistance22–27. Our recent study revealed a mechanistic link between p53 and GD3S expression, showing that certain gain-of-function (GOF) p53 mutations directly upregulate GD3S, thereby promoting the GD2⁺ BCSC phenotype and enhancing tumor survival, establishing a direct mechanistic link between tumor suppressor dysfunction and ganglioside-mediated immune evasion in breast cancer39,40. These mechanistic insights align with our current results, reinforcing the rationale for targeting the GD3S-GD2 axis to counter immune suppression in TNBC.

In the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway, GD3S serves as a crucial regulatory enzyme, governing the synthesis of all downstream B- and C-series gangliosides, including GT2, GT3, GD3, GD2, and GD1b37,38. While various gangliosides have been implicated in different tumor types, the involvement of gangliosides other than GD2 in BC has not been explored in detail41–43,49. Our comprehensive metabolomic analysis clarified that GD2 and GD3 are the primary gangliosides upregulated in response to GD3S overexpression, with GD2 levels being notably higher than GD3 levels, thereby suggesting a limited involvement of other B- or C-series gangliosides in BC. Mass spectrometry-based lipidomics followed by flow-cytometry validation strongly suggests that GD3S overexpression primarily drives GD2 upregulation across multiple BC subtypes, underscoring the pivotal role of GD3S and GD2 in modulating immune surveillance in BC. This provides critical insights into BC biology and potential therapeutic targets.

Our findings demonstrate that GD3S plays a crucial role in the regulation of immune responses in BC. We observed a positive correlation between GD3S expression and immune checkpoint inhibitors such as CTLA-4, LAG-3, and PD-1, as well as the myeloid cell inhibitory receptor SIRPα in BC tissues. These immune checkpoints are well-known for their roles in suppressing the immune response, allowing tumor cells to evade immune surveillance and cytotoxic activity66–68. An immunohistochemical analysis further corroborated these findings, showing reduced infiltration of T cells and macrophages into primary tumors with high GD3S expression. This suggests that GD3S contributes to immune evasion in BC.

Our in vitro data demonstrated that overexpression of GD3S in BC cells led to a significant increase in GD2 levels, which in turn impaired the cytotoxic functions of immune cells. Specifically, GD3S-overexpressing cells showed reduced susceptibility to macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, NK cell–mediated apoptosis, and T cell–mediated killing. These results suggest that GD3S not only promotes the synthesis of immunosuppressive gangliosides but also actively inhibits the cytotoxic activity of key immune cells47,48. Mechanistically, these effects are mediated through GD2’s engagement with inhibitory Siglec receptors (e.g., Siglec-7/9 on NK cells and macrophages) to transmit signals that dampen immune cell activation and cytotoxicity, facilitating tumor immune evasion61,62. In addition, GD2-enriched lipid rafts have been shown to inhibit CD8+ T cell recruitment to the TME via activation of the c-Met/AKT/IRF-1 signaling pathway, and suppression of GD2 biosynthesis reverses this effect69. Conversely, knocking out GD3S in BC cells resulted in a marked increase in immune cell–mediated killing, reinforcing the idea that GD3S is a central regulator of immune evasion in BC by modulating the expression of GD2.

Naxitamab, a humanized anti-GD2 antibody, has shown promising results in targeting GD2+ neuroblastoma cells by promoting antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)51,52. Although a co-crystal structure is not available, computational modeling has shown that naxitamab binds the pentasaccharide head group of GD2, stabilized by hydrogen bonds, π–CH interactions, and a critical Arg95-sialic acid interaction, highlighting its molecular specificity as a targeted immunotherapy70–72. Our study extends these findings to BC, where we demonstrated that naxitamab significantly enhances primary immune cell–mediated killing of BC cells. In vitro assays confirmed that naxitamab effectively induces apoptosis and cytotoxicity in GD2+ BC cells when co-cultured with NK cells and T cells, respectively. Moreover, in a three-dimensional TNBC tumor spheroid-macrophage co-culture assay, treatment with ex vivo expanded M1 macrophages exhibited increased phagocytosis of BC cells in the presence of naxitamab, significantly reducing tumor spheroid size, whereas its combination with SIRPα-targeting antibody exhibited synergism and completely eliminated tumor spheroids. The impact of naxitamab in this model demonstrates its potential to activate macrophage-driven tumor cell elimination. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of naxitamab in combination with macrophage-targeting strategies to develop more effective treatments for TNBC.

Naxitamab’s ability to potentiate the immune response against TNBC cells was further validated in vivo using a PDX model. The combination of naxitamab with activated NK cells led to a significant reduction in tumor volume, exceeding 75%, while no significant tumor reduction was observed in control groups treated with hIgG and NK cells. Encouraged by these results, we explored the novel use of M1 macrophages in the same TNBC PDX model. Our results indeed showed that treatment with naxitamab significantly reduced tumor size by over 65% compared to control treatments. These findings highlight the potential of combining naxitamab with NK cell– and macrophage-based therapies to overcome immunosuppressive barriers in solid tumors like TNBC.

In conclusion, our study shows that GD3S plays a crucial role in breast cancer immune regulation and provides proof-of-concept that targeting GD2 with naxitamab offers a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of TNBC immunotherapies, particularly when combined with activated immune cells. This approach not only targets cancer stem cells but also leverages the immune system to combat tumor growth and metastasis, paving the way for more effective and personalized treatments. Modulating GD3S activity presents a synergistic strategy to improve outcomes in GD2-expressing cancers. Using in vitro and in vivo models, we demonstrate naxitamab’s efficacy in enhancing immune cell-mediated cytotoxicity, especially involving NK cells and macrophages. However, the use of immunodeficient mice in xenograft models may not fully capture the complexity of the human immune system. Future studies using humanized mouse models or early-phase clinical trials will help validate these findings. Our proposed Phase I/II trial of naxitamab in TNBC, set to begin in Q3 2025, aims to translate these preclinical insights and explore its broader application as a promising new strategy for treating GD2+ TNBC.

Sex as a biological variable

In this study, we used tumor samples from female patients and conducted experiments in female mice because the disease modeled is primarily relevant to females.

Antibodies

Antibody conjugate anti-GD2 (allophycocyanin) (Biolegend, cat. #357306) for flow cytometry was purchased from Biolegend and BD Biosciences. Unconjugated anti-GD3 antibody (clone R24) was purchased from Millipore Sigma (cat. #MABC1112). The secondary anti-mouse antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (cat. #A21235) was purchased from Life Technologies. We also purchased the following antibodies: pan-Cytokeratin (Proteintech, cat. #26411-1-AP, rabbit host) along with GD3S (Proteintech, cat. #24918-1-AP, rabbit host), CD45 (Fortislife, clone BL-178-1267, cat. #A700-012, rabbit host), Ki67 (Bethyl, clone BLR021E, cat. #A700-021CF, rabbit host), CD3 (Fortislife, clone BL-298-5012, cat. #A700-016F, rabbit host), CD8 (Fortislife, clone c8/144B, cat. #A500-012A, mouse host), CD4 (Bethyl, clone BL-155-1C11, cat. #A700-015, rabbit host), FOXP3 (Bethyl, clone BLR034F, cat. #A700-034, rabbit host), CD68 (Fortislife, cat. #A500-018ACF, mouse host), CD163 (Invitrogen, clone I0D6, cat. #MA5-11458, mouse host), CD86 (CST, clone E2G8P, cat. #76755SF, rabbit host), CD56 (Fortislife, clone BLR152J, cat. #A700-152, rabbit host) and human IgG1 Kappa antibody (hIgG, cat. #0151K-14, SouthernBiotech). The SIRPα-specific antibody SE5A5 was produced at the University of Tübingen (Germany). The humanized anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody, naxitamab, was kindly provided by Y-mAbs Therapeutics.

Cell lines and patient-derived xenograft model

All cell lines used in this study, except SUM159, were obtained from ATCC. Upon receipt, master stocks were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen (–196 °C) following controlled-rate freezing at –80 °C using the recommended conditions. SUM159 cells were a kind gift from Dr. Naoto Ueno’s lab (currently at the University of Hawaii Cancer Center). To minimize genetic drift, all experiments were performed using cells within 10 passages of revival. Mycoplasma contamination tests were routinely conducted to ensure culture integrity. Throughout the study, cells were maintained at 70–80% confluency at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

The GD2-expressing TNBC patient-derived xenograft (TNBC-PDX) model, obtained from the Cazalot Breast Cancer Model Resource at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, was initially engrafted and propagated for at least one generation in female NSG (NOD.Cg-Prkdc Il2rg/SzJ; Jackson Laboratory) mice. All subsequent experiments were conducted within five passages of expansion.

TCGA and METABRIC data analysis

To assess the association between GD3S (ST8SIA1) mRNA expression and key immune checkpoint markers, we performed Pearson correlation coefficient analysis using breast cancer patient datasets from the Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC, n = 2509) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Pan-Cancer (n = 1084) cohorts. Gene expression data were obtained from publicly available repositories (https://www.cbioportal.org/), and log-transformed transcripts per million (TPM) values were used for correlation analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients (r), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values were calculated to evaluate co-expression relationships between ST8SIA1 and immune checkpoint markers, including CTLA-4, LAG-3, PD-1, CD25, CD47/SIRPα, TIGIT, VISTA, TIM3, and PD-L1. Multiple testing corrections were applied where necessary to account for false discovery rates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Immunohistochemical analysis using sequential multiplex immunofluorescence

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary triple-negative breast tumor tissues (n = 20) were obtained from the MD Anderson Cancer Center Tissue Bank, with approval from the Institutional Review Board (protocol #PA19-0732). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Sequential fluorescence immunohistochemical (SeqIF) analyses were conducted to assess the expression of various immune cell markers, GD3S, cytokeratin (PancK), and Ki67 using the COMET instrument (Lunaphore Technologies). Sequential multiplex immunofluorescence (SeqIF) was performed using the COMET instrument (Lunaphore Technologies). FFPE tissue slides were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in a graded alcohol series, and blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed with EZ-AR2 Elegance buffer (BioGenex) at 107 °C in an EZ-Retriever system V.3 (BioGenex) for 15 min. The processed slides were then immersed in a multistaining buffer (cat. #BU06, Lunaphore, Technologies) bath until ready for use. Next, A microfluidic chip (9 × 9 mm imageable area) was mounted onto each tissue section to create a sealed reaction chamber. Reagents were delivered through microfluidic channels under controlled conditions using the COMET platform, which enabled automated, multiplexed sequential immunofluorescence staining and imaging. Each slide underwent 10 iterative staining and imaging cycles, with elution of primary and secondary antibodies at the end of each cycle.

The primary antibodies were pre-diluted based on preliminary titration tests to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio. This dilution was achieved in multistaining buffer, supplemented with 3% BSA (cat. #8806; Millipore Sigma) and 1% horse serum. The secondary antibodies anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (cat. #A-21428; Invitrogen) and anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (cat. #A-21235; Invitrogen) were used at 1:200 and 1:400 dilutions, respectively, in multistaining buffer. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI (1:2000 dilution in BU06). Imaging was performed using the integrated epifluorescent microscope at 20× magnification with the DAPI (80 ms), TRITC (400 ms), and Cy5 (200 ms) channels. Initial autofluorescence images were captured and subtracted during post-acquisition processing. For each cycle, primary antibodies were incubated for 4 or 8 min based on marker-specific optimization, and secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 min. Antibody elution between cycles was performed over 4 min to ensure complete removal and prevent signal overlap. A 20-plex staining template was designed and executed using COMET Control software. Image quantification and data normalization: Image analysis was performed using Visiopharm Image Analysis Software version 2023.09 ×64 (Visiopharm, Inc.). First, non-tissue areas on the slide were excluded from analysis. Tissue segmentation was then conducted to differentiate tumor and stromal regions based on pan-keratin expression, an epithelial tumor marker. Cell segmentation was performed using a pretrained deep learning algorithm that identified nuclei via the DAPI channel and delineated cell boundaries.

Phenotypic characterization of cells was carried out using Visiopharm’s unbiased auto-clustering module, which categorized cells into distinct subpopulations based on marker expression intensity. For quantification, the expression level of each marker was calculated as mean fluorescence intensity per cell. To control for batch effects and variability across slides, fluorescence intensities were normalized against internal DAPI controls and background-subtracted autofluorescence images. Data were expressed as relative expression normalized to the maximum observed intensity per marker, enabling cross-comparison across different tissue regions and samples.

Primary NK cell, macrophage, and T cell cultures

NK cells were generated from PBMCs from healthy donor blood following a 14-day culture protocol. NK cells were expanded and activated ex vivo by being co-cultured with irradiated K562 cells expressing IL-21 in ex vivo 15 media containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 2 weeks, as previously described by Somanchi et al. On day 14, the expanded NK cells were identified and selected by expression of activation marker CD1673. Macrophages were generated by culturing PBMCs following a macrophage-generating protocol from PromoCell. PBMCs were cultured in macrophage-based medium containing macrophage-colony-stimulating factor (cat. #300-25, Preprotech) cytokine for 7 days. On day 7, macrophages were activated with interferon-gamma (cat. #300-02, Preprotech) cytokine.

T cells were generated by culturing PBMCs following a 7-day protocol. PBMCs were cultured with complete RPMI-1640 media (cat. #R8758, Millipore Sigma) containing 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and IL-2.

Flow cytometry

BC cells (SUM159, BT549, MCF7, Hs578T, and HCC1395) were subjected to trypsin and washed once with phosphate-buffered saline. The cells were then incubated for 30 min with 4 μL/reaction of allophycocyanin-conjugated purified anti-human ganglioside GD2 antibody (cat. #357302, BioLegend). For the GD3 expression analysis, cells were stained with 3 μL/reaction of unconjugated anti-GD3 antibody (clone R24, Millipore Sigma) for 30 min on ice and then washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. For secondary staining, the cells were incubated for 30 min with 100 μL of diluted goat anti-mouse antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (cat. #A21235, Life Technologies). Staining with DAPI (cat. #D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to exclude dead cells. Allophycocyanin-conjugated mouse IgG2a (cat. #400201, BioLegend) was used as the isotype control for GD2, and unconjugated mouse IgG3κ (cat. #401301, BioLegend) served as the isotype control for GD3 to account for non-specific antibody binding. After incubation, the cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.5 μg/mL DAPI and analyzed with an LSR II (BD Biosciences) or a Gallios (Beckman Coulter) flow cytometer. Ten thousand events were acquired for each sample. All experiments were performed in duplicate, and all flow cytometry data were analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10).

Tumor spheroid assay

To assess the effect of naxitamab-induced macrophage-mediated ADCP on the spheroid-forming ability of TNBC cells, 5000 Hs578T-RFP cells were seeded in ultra-low–attachment 96-well plates (cat. #7007, Corning) in triplicate (100 µL/well of growth medium). After 3 days of incubation to allow spheroid formation, the spheroids were co-cultured with 50,000 healthy donor-derived M1 macrophages per well and treated with 20 µg/ml of hIgG, naxitamab, anti-SIRPα (SE5A5), or their combinations (Naxitamab + anti-SIRPα), or left untreated. Spheroid formation and growth were monitored using the IncuCyte live cell analysis system (Model-S3, cat. #4647, Sartorius) to measure the brightfield and red-fluorescent area every 6 h for a total duration of 6 days.

Total RNA isolation and gene expression by real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (cat. #74104, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression was assessed by quantitative real-time PCR using the TaqMan assay system on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). All reactions were performed in triplicate. For each sample, cDNA was synthesized using 1 μg of RNA and SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Invitrogen). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed with the QuantStudio3 system using TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (both from Applied Biosystems), as described previously. The relative fold changes in specific RNA expression were calculated using the comparative cycle of threshold detection method, and values were normalized to those of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase. Fold changes in gene expression were calculated using the 2–∆∆CT method. All primer pairs for human tissue samples (Table S1) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

GD3S overexpression

To assess the effect of GD3S (ST8SIA1) overexpression in breast cancer cells, we employed a lentiviral packaging system using the ST8SIA1-plenti-III-CMV (cat. #LV701751; Applied Biological Materials), ST8SIA1-pLX304 (cat. #EX-OL01535-LX304; Genecopoeia), and their corresponding empty vector controls, plenti-III-Blank (cat. #LV587; Applied Biological Materials) and pLX304 (Genecopoeia). HEK293T cells were transfected with either the GD3S overexpression (GD3S-OE) plasmid or the empty vector control (EV-CTRL), along with packaging vectors pMD2.G (cat. #12259, Addgene) and psPAX2 (cat. #12260, Addgene), using jetPRIME transfection reagent (cat. #114-07, Polyplus) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Seventy-two hours after transfection, the lentivirus-containing medium was harvested and incubated with the target cells for 24 hours. The transduced cells were then selected using puromycin (0.5 µg/mL; cat. #ant-pr-1, Invivogen) for 3 days or blasticidin (10 µg/mL; cat. #ant-bl-05, Invivogen) for 5 days. GD3S expression in the resulting stable cell clones was confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

GD3S knockout

GD3S (ST8SIA1) was knocked out in breast cancer cells using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Guide RNAs (gRNAs; gRNA1–gRNA4) were designed to target exons 1 and 2 of GD3S, with a focus on exon 2 to disrupt all ST8SIA1 splicing variants (Table S2). For the knockout (KO) experiment, gRNA4 was selected for its superior efficiency. The gRNAs were cloned into the lenticrispr v2 vector (cat. #52961, Addgene). HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the gRNA construct and the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using JetPrime transfection reagent. Seventy-two hours post-transfection, lentivirus-containing supernatants were collected and used to infect breast cancer cells. The infected cells were selected with puromycin (0.5 µg/mL) for 3 days. Single-cell clones were generated through limiting dilution and cultured in 96-well plates for 2–3 weeks. Positive clones were initially screened by Western blot to confirm loss of GD3S protein expression and validated by flow cytometry to assess the corresponding decrease in GD2 levels (Fig. S3A–C). Successful gene editing was further confirmed using T7 endonuclease assays and Sanger sequencing.

Lipidomics by mass spectrometry

We conducted an unbiased metabolomic analysis using mass spectrometry to explore the effects of GD3S overexpression on the lipid profiles, including ganglioside levels, in SUM159 and MCF7 breast cancer cell lines. Lipid extraction was performed according to standard protocols tailored for polar metabolites, which were then analyzed using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (HILIC/MS). The analysis utilized a standard linear gradient for elution with specific flow rates suitable for lipidomic profiling. Mass spectrometry was carried out over a mass range of 300–1800 Da, achieving a resolution of 120,000 at a scan rate of 1 scan per second. Both positive and negative ion modes were employed to enhance the detection of diverse lipid species. MS/MS data acquisition was performed in a data-dependent mode, utilizing an isolation window of 1.3 m/z for targeted MS/MS fragmentation. This comprehensive approach allowed for both quantitative and qualitative assessments of changes in cellular and membrane lipid composition due to GD3S overexpression in these cell lines. The methodology was designed to detect subtle alterations in lipid species, providing insights into the changes induced by altered GD3S expression.

Western blotting

Cells (3 × 106) were lysed, and proteins were extracted using Laemmli buffer containing 50 μL/mL β-mercaptoethanol. A protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail was added to the lysates according to the manufacturer’s recommended dilution. Fifteen microliters of each protein sample were loaded onto 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast gels (cat. #4561086, Bio-Rad Laboratories) for protein separation. Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, which was blocked with 5% milk in TBS-T (0.05% Tween-20 in PBS) to prevent non-specific binding. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ST8SIA1 (cat. #24918-1-AP; Proteintech) and GAPDH (cat. #MAB374; Millipore Sigma) in PBS-T with 1% milk. Afterward, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, goat anti-rabbit (31460, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or goat anti-mouse (31430, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence Western blotting substrate kit (32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The membranes were washed three times with TBS-T and imaged using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Protein quantification was performed with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Phagocytosis assay

GD3S was stably overexpressed in BT549, MCF-7, and SUM 159 cells via lentiviral transduction, while GD3S was deleted in BT549 and SUM 159 cells using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. To assess the role of GD3S in macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, BC cells were stained with pHrodo dye (cat. #4649, Sartorius)—a pH-sensitive fluorogenic dye that detects phagocytic activity, before co-culturing with macrophages at 1:1 effector-to-target (E:T) cell ratios. The acidic environment generated by macrophage activity increases red fluorescence in BC cells in real time, indicating ongoing phagocytosis. To assess the effect of naxitamab treatment on macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of BC cells, Hs578T and HCC1395 cells pre-stained with pHrodo were co-cultured with macrophages at a 1:1 effector-to-target ratio and treated with either human IgG antibody (hIgG) at 20 µg/ml or naxitamab at 5, 10, and 20 µg/ml. Time-lapse fluorescence imaging was performed every hour for 24 hours using the IncuCyte live-cell imaging system to monitor phagocytosis by tracking increased fluorescence. All experimental conditions were conducted in biological triplicates to ensure reproducibility.

Apoptosis assay

BT549, MCF-7, and SUM 159 control cells, along with clones either overexpressing or lacking GD3S, were stained with Cytolight Red dye (Sartorius). NK cell–induced apoptosis of BC cells was measured by adding Annexin V Green reagent (cat. #4642, Sartorius), a widely used marker for apoptotic cells, to the co-culture of BC cells with NK cells at an E:T ratio of 2:1. To assess the effect of naxitamab treatment on NK cell-mediated ADCC of BC cells, Hs578T and HCC1395 cells were co-cultured with NK cells at a E:T ratios of 1:1 and 2:1 and treated with either human IgG antibody (hIgG) or naxitamab at 5 µg/ml. Time-lapse fluorescence imaging was performed using IncuCyte to assess apoptosis induction every 2 h for up to 80 h. The percentage of green or red cells was normalized to the percentage of confluence and expressed as target cell death. All experimental conditions were conducted in biological triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Cytotoxicity assay

BT549, MCF-7, and SUM 159 control cells, along with clones either overexpressing or lacking GD3S, were stained with Cytolight red dye (cat. #4706, Sartorius). T cell-mediated cytotoxicity was measured by adding Cytotox green reagent (cat. #4633, Sartorius), a widely used marker for cytotoxic cell death, to the co-culture of BC cells with T cells at an E:T ratio of 4:1. Time-lapse fluorescence imaging was performed using IncuCyte to assess cytotoxicity every 2 h for 72 h. The percentage of green or red cells was normalized to the percentage of confluence and expressed as target cell death. All experimental conditions were conducted in biological triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Tumor xenografts

All animal experiments were approved by MD Anderson’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol # 1972-RN01) and conducted in compliance with state and federal regulations. Six- to eight-week-old female NSG mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdc Il2rg/SzJ; Jackson Laboratory) were orthotopically implanted with 2 × 106 PIM-005 TNBC-PDX cells in the fourth inguinal mammary fat pad. For xenograft studies, we ensured >90% power to detect differences such as 20% vs. 40% survival at a significance level of 0.05. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (α = 0.01) was applied to control for multiple testing, maintaining ≥82% power, even with potential reductions in sample size or effect size variations. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, and the researcher measuring tumor size was blinded to group allocations. Tumor size was measured weekly using calipers. When tumor volumes reached 100 mm³, approximately 5 weeks after implantation, mice were randomized into treatment groups on the basis of matched tumor size and received bi-weekly tail vein injections of naxitamab (20 mg/kg) or hIgG (20 mg/kg) for 4 weeks. In one experimental group, 10 × 106 NK cells were administered along with the antibody treatments via tail vein injection. In another group, 3 × 106 macrophages were injected in combination with the treatments. The therapeutic response and survival were monitored throughout the study. Animals that succumbed for undetermined reasons were excluded from the analysis. Mice were humanely euthanized using CO2 as per guidelines when control group tumor sizes reached 2 cm, and tumors were subsequently harvested for further analysis and visual examination.

Data acquisition and statistical analysis

Pearson correlation coefficient (r) analysis was used to assess the correlation between GD3S and different immune-checkpoint marker’s mRNA expression. Histochemical scores (H-scores; calculated by multiplying the staining intensity by the percentage of positive cells) were calculated for GD3S, and different immune cell marker expression was reported as cell counts (means ± SD) in high and low GD3S-expressing samples. Differences between two groups were analyzed using t-tests. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare means across more than two groups. For in vitro co-culture experiments conducted in biological triplicates, a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was employed to assess the effects of cell type and treatment on immune cell–mediated killing. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant, and multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure were considered where appropriate. All analyses of patient and cell line data were performed using Prism software (Version 10, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Figures were generated using the Graphpad Prism software.
