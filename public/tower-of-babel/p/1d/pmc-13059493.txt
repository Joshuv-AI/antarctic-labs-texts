Serum iron is associated with the prognosis in sepsis based on a large-scale database
Authors: Ju Luo, Ning Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies on serum iron levels and prognosis in sepsis are limited. We conducted retrospective research based on a public database to investigate the relationships between the serum iron levels and clinical outcomes in sepsis.Methods2225 sepsis patients according to the Sepsis-3 criteria were enrolled in the study. Sepsis patients whose serum iron level was detected within 24 hours after admission were included. Univariate regression analysis was utilized to assess the relationships between different variables and patient prognosis including 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality rates. The relationship between serum iron levels and prognosis in three models were explored including model I: adjusted for none, model II: adjusted for age; gender, and model III: adjusted for potential confounders. Kaplan-Meier analysis for 1-year mortality rate among sepsis patients was performed.ResultsAge, anion gap, total bilirubin, ferritin, lactate, magnesium, phosphate, platelet, urea nitrogen, APSIII, and SOFA were associated with prognosis in sepsis. After adjusting for all potential confounders, for every 10-ug/dl increases in serum iron level, the ORs for 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality were 1.04, 1.06, 1.06, and 1.06, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated a significantly lower survival probability in the highest serum iron level quartile than in the other quartiles.ConclusionHigher serum iron levels were associated with increased 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality in sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12810-z.

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as a sophisticated syndrome that develops as a dysregulated organ dysfunction due to infection, which is characterized with comparatively high mortality and poor prognosis [1, 2]. In 2017, although the incidence and mortality of sepsis respectively decreased by 37.0% and 52.8% from 1990, it was worldwide reported that the number of new diagnosed cases of sepsis and sepsis-related were around 48.9 million and 11.0 million, respectively. In addition, it accounted for 19.7% of the global deaths toll. The incidence and mortality rates in sepsis vary in different regions, with the highest burdens especially in countries with lowest level economy [3]. In China, one recent study demonstrated that about 20% patients admitted in intensive care unit (ICU) were affected by sepsis with a 35.5% of 90-day mortality [4].

A study illuminates that a higher level of triglyceride glucose index (TyG index) is related with an elevated risk of poor prognosis in sepsis [5]. The higher stress hyperglycemia ratio is closed to an elevated risk of worse outcomes, which shows that it might be a potential biomarker of worse outcomes in sepsis [6]. A prospective study in Ecuador shows that if the number of organ dysfunction more than two, the sepsis mortality will significantly increase, which makes it maybe a risk factor for sepsis [7]. Another study in USA indicated that various factors including comorbidities, abnormal levels of laboratory findings and delayed culture results were associated with readmission and mortality in sepsis [8]. However, few studies have focused on the body nutrients, for example, iron and mortality in sepsis.

Iron, a vital biological element of life, plays important roles in various biological and physical functions including mitochondrial metabolism, gene regulation, and cell proliferation [9]. In certain infectious diseases, not only do pathogens require iron to sustain their metabolic functions, but mammalian cells also depend on it. For microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi, iron serves as a nutrient that plays a critical role in disease progression, as excessive iron levels can trigger oxidative stress and promote inflammation [10]. Intracellular infection could be promoted by the accumulation of cellular iron. In malaria, iron supplementation might be susceptible to the infection [11, 12]. Moreover, in macrophage cells, iron accumulation also enables the rapid growth of different intracellular microbes [13]. During septic shock, dopamine can directly bind to iron, causing the pathogen of Salmonella to absorb iron and express hepcidin, leading to the replication and growth of Salmonella [14].

Previous studies demonstrate that with the increasement of iron levels, oxidative damage and cell death happens. Furthermore, iron-related disturbance is correlated with the disease severity in sepsis, which indicates that both iron and ferritin could be the diagnostic and predictive markers for sepsis [15–17]. Studies on serum iron levels and prognosis in sepsis are limited. Thus, we conducted retrospective research based on a public database to investigate the relationships between the serum iron levels and clinical outcomes in sepsis.

Methods

Materials and study cohort

We performed this study based on the public database: Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database [18–20]. The website address was “https://mimic.mit.edu/iv/”. Patients with sepsis based on the Sepsis 3.0 definition (n = 9592) were included in our study [21]. Patients were excluded as follow: without serum iron value in 24 hours after admission (n = 7044); with >5% individual data missing (n = 323); younger than eighteen-year-old (n = 0).

Research end-points

1-year mortality was the primary research endpoint. 28-day mortality, 90-day mortality, and 180-day mortality were the secondary research endpoints.

Data extraction and variables

PostgreSQL 9.6 software was utilized for data extraction. We extracted the variables which were detected within 24 hours after admission.

First, the data of general characteristics were extracted as follow: age, gender (male, female), pathogens (Escherichia coli (E.coli), Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pseudomonas, Anaerobia), past comorbidities (coronary heart disease (CAD), hypertension, diabetes, renal disease), complications (sepsis associated encephalopathy (SAE), septic shock, acute kidney injury (AKI)), managements (renal replacement therapy (RRT), vasopressors use, ventilator use), scores of acute physiology score III (APSIII) and sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), clinical outcomes (length of stay (LOS) in hospital and intensive care unit (ICU), mortalities of 28-day, 90-day, 180-day and 1-year). Second, basic vital signs including diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP) and respiratory rate (RR) were collected. Third, laboratory variables including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), serum iron, white blood cell (WBC), creatinine, ferritin, total bilirubin, hemoglobin, lactate, magnesium, phosphate, platelet (PLT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), urea nitrogen, anion gap (AG), total calcium and sodium were collected.

Statistical analysis

The software including Empower Stats (http://www.empowerstats.com) and the R Project for Statistical Computing (http://www.R-project.org) were used. When a P-value less than 0.05, it was defined as statistically significant.

Serum iron in this study is the total elemental iron content in the serum. The whole cohort of sepsis patients (n = 2225) were divided into groups (Q1(n = 532), Q2(n = 547), Q3(n = 576), Q4(n = 570)) based on the quartiles of serum iron levels (first quartile: <20ug/dl, second quartile: 20–32 ug/dl, third quartile: 33–57 ug/dl, fourth quartile: >57ug/dl).

We used median for expressing continuous variables and n (%) for expressing categories variables. Kruskal-Wallis and Chi-squared tests were utilized for statistical analysis between different groups. Univariate regression analysis was utilized for the associations between variables and prognosis. The relationship between serum iron levels and prognosis were explored in models (model I: crude model, model II: adjusted for gender and age, and model III: adjusted for potential confounders). Covariates were included as potential confounders in the final model (model III) if they changed the estimates of ferritin on 1-year mortality in sepsis by more than 10% or were significantly associated with clinical outcomes in sepsis [22, 23]. The calculating steps were showed in Supplementary materials. The following covariates were selected a priori on the basis of established associations and/or plausible biological relations and tested as follow: age, AG, AST, total bilirubin, ferritin, hemoglobin, lactate, magnesium, phosphate, PLT, urea nitrogen, renal disease, APSIII and SOFA. In addition, gender and some comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, CAD) as common confounders in previous studies [24, 25], were also added to be adjusted in the final models (model III).

The odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for every 10 ug/dL increment in serum iron level were utilized. Kaplan-Meier analysis for 1-year mortality in sepsis patients was performed. Subgroups and interaction analyses were also made. The receiver-operator characteristic (ROC) analysis of serum iron for predicting clinical outcomes in sepsis were also constructed.

The median age of the study cohort was 64-year-old. The proportions of males and females were 55.28% (n = 1230) and 44.72% (n = 995), respectively. Pathogens were detected in the patients including E. coli 13.98% (n = 311), Streptococcus pneumoniae 4.94% (n = 110), Pseudomonas 6.61% (n = 147) and Anaerobes 1.48% (n = 33). The proportions of comorbidities including hypertension, CAD, diabetes and renal disease were 18.07% (n = 402), 7.91% (n = 176), 3.01% (n = 67) and 4.54% (n = 101), respectively. The median scores of APSIII and SOFA were 58.00 (46.00–75.00) and 3.00 (2.00–5.00), respectively. The incidences of complications including AKI, septic shock and SAE were 74.92% (n = 1667), 41.98% (n = 934) and 32.99% (n = 734), respectively. The median days of LOS in ICU and hospital were 6.34 (2.67–14.01) and 16.74 (8.89–28.93), respectively. The proportions of clinical managements including RRT, ventilator use and vasopressor use were 21.71% (n = 483), 83.33% (n = 1854) and 25.66% (n = 571), respectively. The 28-day, 90-day, 180-day and 1-year mortalities of the sepsis patients were 19.91% (n = 443), 27.69% (n = 616), 29.66% (n = 660) and 30.97% (n = 689), respectively.

Discussion

In this large retrospective public database study, after adjusting for all potential confounders, for every 10 ug/dl increase in serum iron, the ORs for 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality were 1.04, 1.06, 1.06, and 1.06, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier curves revealed a significantly lower survival probability in the highest serum iron quartile than in the other quartiles.

Sepsis patients in the ICU have high mortality rates. The ICU mortality of sepsis range from 16.0% to 23.0% [26–28]. A meta-analysis reported that the mortality of ICU patients with hospital-acquired sepsis was 52.3% [29]. In this study, we reported that the 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality rates of the sepsis patients were 19.91%, 27.69%, 29.66%, and 30.97%, respectively. Considering that the prognosis of sepsis patients in the ICU is very poor, identifying the risk factors associated with mortality and identifying potential treatments are clinically important.

In this study, we found that age, AG, total bilirubin, ferritin, lactate, magnesium, phosphate, PLT, urea nitrogen, APSIII, and SOFA score were significantly associated with mortality in patients with sepsis. Previous studies have also analyzed the data of sepsis based on the MIMIC-IV database. One study reported that five variables including four lab findings (albumin, APTT, hemoglobin, and total bilirubin) and LOS in the ICU were independently associated with 28-day mortality in sepsis [30]. Another study revealed that factors such as PLT, prothrombin time, mechanical ventilation, and RRT are associated with high-risk patients [31]. The risky indicators associated with the clinical outcomes of sepsis were inconsistent across these studies. Differences in the sample size and the extracted risk factors may have caused these inconsistencies.

In this study, we found that the serum iron level was correlated with the severity of sepsis. One study based on two-sample Mendelian randomization suggested that there was a causal relationship between the predicted high-speed rail status by genes and the risk of sepsis hospitalization [32]. A large retrospective study revealed that higher serum iron levels were significantly associated with higher risk of 90-day mortality in sepsis [33]. Although the relatively low odds ratio reported in this study, its impact on clinical decision-making appears limited. And, our study does not compare the predictive strength of serum iron to other biomarkers. Further study with multiple centers and more samples will be done for validating our results.

The potential mechanisms may include the following points. First, some intracellular pathogens can seize iron in the cytoplasm directly or by siderophores. One recent meta-analysis focusing on intravenous iron randomized controlled trials revealed that intravenous iron might cause the increasement of risk in infection [34, 35]. Second, cells damage or death could occur by ferroptosis due to the elevated levels of labile iron [36]. Third, the amplification of inflammatory response can be achieved through the action of free iron, which is absorbed and metabolized by damaged stored red blood cells through the monocyte macrophage system [37]. Fourth, organ damage including acute brain injury, acute kidney injury, and liver dysfunction could be caused by iron overload [38–40], and vital organ damage may worsen the condition of sepsis patients.

Previous studies have shown that iron metabolism disorders can increase the risk of poor prognosis in sepsis. Lower or higher serum iron levels can increase the risk of mortality in sepsis [41]. Serum iron plays a dual role in the pathophysiological process of sepsis. On the one hand, excessive serum iron can promote the proliferation of pathogens, and the accumulation of iron can lead to excessive inflammatory reactions, oxidative stress, and damage in organs [42]. On the other hand, low serum iron can lead to anemia, ischemia and hypoxia in organs, and exacerbate organ dysfunction [43]. In addition, infection could also affect the iron metabolism. Extracellular pathogens such as Escherichia coli can induce cells to develop an iron retention phenotype, characterized by downregulation of the key iron transport protein ferritin, upregulation of the iron transport protein transferrin receptor 1, and corresponding induction of the iron storage protein ferritin, which impact on iron metabolism [44].

Therefore, maintaining a relatively balanced serum iron level is important in sepsis patients. Based on our research, sepsis patients with normal serum iron levels might not require additional iron supplementation. Therefore, standardized iron management strategies may not be applicable in sepsis patients. The iron supplementation strategy should be based on the patient’s serum iron level, disease severity, and organ function, and a personalized plan should be developed [45].

However, limitations in our study should be illuminated. First, due to the retrospective study and some data missing in the database, some important confounders such as transfusions, iron supplementation, and erythropoietin use were not accounted for. And some potential confounders, like C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, vasopressors and fluid resuscitation were also not analyzed, which might limit the generalizability and adaptability of our results in some other cohorts. In addition, the study is retrospective and observational, which limits causal inference. The causation between serum iron and prognosis in sepsis couldn’t be identified. Second, laboratory data was measured by a single-point method. We didn’t analyze the dynamic changes of serum iron during hospitalization due to the lack of data. Hence, we couldn’t expand the analysis to examine the predictive role of serum iron in different phrases including intermediate and late-phase mortality peaks in sepsis. Third, serum iron levels were associated with pathogens, making the pathogen very important. However, the detection rate of pathogens was low in this study, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, the present study was conducted in a specific population and dataset, and the inclusion of different populations and external validation is needed to confirm the findings. Further study with multiple regions and larger samples should be done for better investigate the relationship between serum iron and outcomes in sepsis. Fifth, malnutrition can influence both iron metabolism and sepsis severity. The nutritional indicators including albumin, prealbumin or BMI could confound the relationship between serum iron and outcomes. Due to the data missing, we didn’t enroll this nutritional indicators. Sixth, some more statistical methods such as target trial emulation could be applied to investigate the causal association between the serum iron and outcome in sepsis [46]. But, the definitive conclusion is difficult to made due to retrospective study design.

Conclusion

Higher serum iron levels were significantly associated with increased 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality rates in sepsis.
