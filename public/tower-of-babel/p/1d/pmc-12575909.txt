Immunotherapy with and without radiotherapy following the diagnosis of bone metastasis for stage IV non-small cell carcinoma
Authors: Jeffrey Beyon, Jennifer E. Collins, Christine A. Welch, Amir Kamran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis retrospective study compared the impact of radiotherapy (RT) and immunotherapy (IO) on survival in patients with stage IV non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) following a diagnosis of bone metastasis.MethodsThe TriNetX database (2013–2024) was queried for adults (≥ 18 years) with NSCLC who received IO after diagnosis of bone metastases. Patients were then divided into cohorts based on whether they also received RT after bone metastasis diagnosis.ResultsA risk assessment revealed RT + IO was associated with significantly improved survival at 3 months (92% vs. 86% alive, p < 0.001), 6 months (79% vs. 72%, p = 0.002), and 1 year (59% vs. 54%, p = 0.014). In patients with adenocarcinoma, RT + IO was associated with improved survival at 3 months (92% vs. 88%, p = 0.046) but not at 6 months or 1 year. Similarly, in patients with squamous cell carcinoma, IO + RT was also associated with higher survival (94% vs. 85%, p = 0.040) at 3 months but not at 6 months or 1 year. A Cox proportional hazards model found significant lower hazard of death in the RT + IO group (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.83) and several covariates were associated with higher hazard, including adrenal metastasis (HR = 1.7), liver metastasis (HR = 1.4), lymph node metastasis (HR = 1.3), hypoalbuminemia (< 3.45 g/dL; HR = 1.5), and inpatient or observation care (HR = 1.4).DiscussionThis study highlights the potential importance of combining RT with IO, particularly in the early period after bone metastasis diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06303-w.

Purpose

According to the American Cancer Society, lung cancer is the second most common cancer in both men and women, and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States, with more deaths than colon, breast, and prostate cancers combined (2024). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises 80–90% of cases annually, while 10–15% of lung cancers are small cell lung cancer (2024). The 8th edition TNM classification system further subdivides stage IV NSCLC into stages IVA, IVB, and IVC to reflect the burden and distribution of metastasis. All stage IV subgroups are associated with poor survival outcomes (2022a). The five-year survival rates for stage IV NSCLC and SCLC are just 8% and 3%, respectively (2022b). The most utilized treatment options for stage IV NSCLC include chemotherapy, radiotherapy (RT), and more recently immunotherapy (IO) (2022b; Altorki et al. 2023; Chang et al. 2023; Theelen et al. 2021). IO enhances the bodies' anti-tumor responses by enabling immune checkpoint blockade, while RT induces direct tumor cell death and may promote immunogenic cell death, potentially amplifying systemic immune activity (2019a, 2019b). These modalities can be administered alone or in combination, with growing interest in their synergistic potential based on preclinical and clinical evidence (Theelen et al. 2021; Turchan et al. 2021).

Bones are the third most common site of metastatic disease with carcinoma being the most common etiology. Pathogenesis notably involves interaction between tumor cell receptors and stromal cells of the bone marrow and bone matrix. From these interactions, there is subsequent release of growth factors, cytokines (IL-6, IL-8), and angiogenic factors (VEGF) that both support tumor growth and activate osteoclasts (Jayarangaiah et al. 2023). There is also some evidence the metastatic disease begins earlier due to the discontinuous nature of bone sinusoid endothelia. Once diseased cells take up residence, they are afforded relative immunity from both innate and adaptive immune systems (Esposito et al. 2018). Drug resistance further blunts therapeutic efforts through multiple mechanisms. For example, different microenvironments, or niches, enforce quiescence thus facilitating evasion from cytotoxic agents (Byrne et al. 2019). Another especially pertinent to radio- and immunoresistance attributes hypoxia in the marrow and sinusoids (Chen et al. 2023).

There has been increasing interest in patient outcomes when RT and IO are combined. Foster et al. (2019) conducted a large retrospective National Cancer Database (NCDB) analysis of over 14,000 patients with stage IV NSCLC and found thoracic RT combined with IO was associated with improved overall survival as compared with IO alone, particularly in patients with limited metastatic disease. For example, patients receiving stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) plus IO had a median overall survival (OS) of 18.2 months, compared with 14.3 months for those receiving SRT plus chemotherapy (Foster et al. 2019). Current trials, including an ongoing randomized controlled study comparing IO + RT versus standard of care (IO ± chemotherapy), are expected to yield results in 2025 (Lieverse et al. 2020). Preliminary data support a survival benefit with combination therapy (Foster et al 2019; Li et al. 2022; Lieverse et al. 2020; Liu and Chi 2023; Popp et al. 2024), although there have been some conflicting findings. The THUNDER-2 study from China, a retrospective study of 120 patients treated with IO + RT at 3 oncology centers in Shandong Province, found no significant differences between IO alone (median survival: 9 months) and IO combined with external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) (median survival: 10 months) (Liu et al. 2022). Additionally, many existing studies have not utilized real-world data, examined treatment timing, or stratified by histologic subtype and could be strengthened using data sources informed by electronic health records, such as TriNetX, which allow for temporal alignment of treatment relative to diagnosis of metastasis, inclusion of clinical laboratory markers (e.g., albumin), and histology-specific analyses. Our study aimed to incorporate these elements to provide a more granular, real-world assessment of the impact of RT + IO on survival in patients with metastatic lung cancer.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX research network, a federated platform of de-identified electronic health records from participating healthcare organizations in the United States. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with stage IV NSCLC between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2024, and were identified using ICD-10 codes and histologic classifications. Eligible histologies included adenocarcinoma, not otherwise specified (AC-NOS; ICD-O-3: 8140/3), squamous cell carcinoma, not otherwise specified (SCC-NOS; ICD-O-3: 8070/3), and TriNetX’s curated NSCLC designation. Patients were required to have a documented diagnosis of bone metastasis (ICD-10: C79.5) and to have received at least one of the following immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) after diagnosis of bone metastases: nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, or avelumab. Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on the presence or absence of radiation therapy after the diagnosis of bone metastases. Radiation therapy was defined using the following CPT codes: (1) 77,385 (IMRT delivery, simple), (2) 77,386 (IMRT delivery, complex), and (3) 77,427 (radiation treatment management, 5 treatments). Patients with any record of these codes or radiation planning (77,295) were excluded from the non-radiation cohort. Because CPT codes do not specify anatomical site, the irradiated location could not be directly determined. To minimize this limitation, the analysis was restricted to patients who received radiotherapy within 90 days of the bone metastasis diagnosis, a window that strongly suggests bone-directed RT in most patients. Propensity matching

Baseline characteristics were assessed up to the day of bone metastasis diagnosis. Propensity score matching was performed within the TriNetX platform using a 1:1 ratio using nearest neighbor matching. All statistical outputs were generated directly through TriNetX’s built-in analytics which uses de-identified aggregate data from participating health systems. Variables for matching were selected based on initial baseline differences and clinical relevance, but all variables listed in Table 1 were compared, including comorbidities, chemotherapies (VA classification lines 1, 2, and 3), laboratory data, and the use of bone-targeting agents. For the entire group and the AC-NOS subset analysis, cohorts were matched on: (1) age at index, (2) sex, (3) white race, (4) non-Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, (5) brain metastases, (6) liver metastases, (7) pleura metastases, (8) lymph node metastases, (9) albumin (< 3.5 g/dL vs. ≥ 3.5 g/dL), (10) lactate dehydrogenase (LDH ≤ 250, 251–400, > 400 U/L), (11) body mass index (BMI; 0–18.5, 18.5–25, 25–30, 30–35, 35–40, > 40 kg/m2), and (12) chemotherapy line (VA classification lines 1, 2, and 3). Due to limited sample size, the SCC-NOS subset was matched using a smaller number of clinically relevant variables with the greatest baseline imbalance: (1) age at index, (2) lymph node metastases, (3) brain metastases, and (4) liver metastases. Statistical analysis

Analyses included descriptive statistics, risk measures, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and Cox proportional hazards modeling. For both Kaplan–Meier and risk-based outcomes, patients with outcomes occurring before the start of a time window were excluded. Risk of death was assessed at 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year intervals after the diagnosis of bone metastasis and reported as risk difference (cohort 1 minus cohort 2), risk ratio, and odds ratio with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests for group comparisons, and hazard ratios were generated within the TriNetX platform which uses R’s survival package (v3.2.3). Censoring was applied by TriNetX to patients lacking follow-up during the analysis window or those with outcomes that occurred before the time window began but after the index event. Censoring occurred at the start of the time window.

A Cox proportional hazards model was constructed with time zero set to the date of bone metastasis diagnosis and outcome defined as death. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and retained using backward stepwise elimination of non-significant terms. To address the observation of non-proportional hazards over time separate time-restricted Cox models were also run for (1) 0–100 days (Model 2) and (2) 100 days to 1 year (Model 3). These stratified models allowed for the evaluation of early and delayed treatment effects over time.

As a sensitivity analysis, all primary analyses were repeated using the full unmatched cohort. These results are presented in the supplemental material and demonstrated consistent direction and magnitude of effects across all histology groups, confirming the robustness of findings.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Utilizing the TriNetX Research Network, 1698 stage IV lung cancer patients with NSCLC histology were identified who had received one of the indicated ICIs (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, or avelumab) after diagnosis of bone metastases. These patients were divided into those who also received RT up to 3 months after their bone metastasis diagnosis (Cohort 1: RT + IO, n = 991) and those who received IO only (Cohort 2: IO only, n = 707). Among the 991 patients who received RT, 55% received radiation before starting immunotherapy, 38% received it after, and 7% received both within a 1-day window. Baseline statistics were compared between cohorts for general demographic variables, select comorbidities, sites of additional metastasis, chemotherapy and bone-targeting therapeutic agents, and select laboratory values. After 1:1 propensity score matching, each group included 623 patients. Matching substantially improved balance across covariates, with most standardized mean differences (SMDs) falling below 0.1.

Before matching, the RT + IO group was slightly younger (mean age at index: 64.5 vs. 66.9 years, SMD = 0.23), but age was balanced after matching (66.1 vs. 66.5 years, SMD = 0.003). The proportion of female patients was 37% vs. 41% before matching and balanced at 40% of each group after matching. Race and ethnicity distributions were comparable post-matching. Comorbidities, including coronary artery disease, diabetes, hypertension, and hypothyroidism, were evenly distributed. All patients had bone metastases by definition, and rates of other metastatic sites (including brain, liver, adrenal, and lung) were similar after matching. Imbalances in liver (SMD = 0.14) and adrenal (SMD = 0.11) metastases seen before matching were reduced (SMDs = 0.03 and 0.01, respectively). Chemotherapy was similar between groups post-matching, with carboplatin, cisplatin, paclitaxel, and pemetrexed use showing minimal differences. Roughly one-quarter of each group had received first-line chemotherapy (24%), and small differences in agents like carboplatin, paclitaxel, and pemetrexed were reduced with matching. Bone-targeting agent use (zoledronic acid and denosumab) remained low in both cohorts. Laboratory values including BMI, albumin, calcium, phosphate, LDH, and hemoglobin were well balanced. The proportion of patients with albumin < 3.5 g/dL was 30% vs. 38% before matching and equal at 36% in both groups after matching. Overall, the two treatment groups were well balanced across demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables following propensity-score matching.

Measures of association

The measures of association included 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year mortality outcomes for all stage IV NSCLC histologies combined, as well as for the individual subsets of AC-NOS and SCC-NOS (Table 2). Additional subtypes were not analyzed separately due to insufficient sample size, so patients in the cohort who had NSCLC histologies other than AC-NOS or SCC-NOS were not included in a subset analysis. RT was associated with significantly improved outcomes in the overall cohort at both 3 months (risk difference [RD] = − 6.1%, 95% CI − 9.5%, − 2.6%, p = 0.001) and 6 months (RD = − 6.9%, 95% CI − 12%, − 2.2%, p = 0.004). At 1 year, the difference was not statistically significant (RD = − 4.4%, 95% CI − 9.9%, 1.0%, p = 0.108).

In the AC-NOS subset, which included 353 patients per group after matching, RT was associated with a significant reduction in 3-month mortality (RD = − 4.0%, 95% CI − 7.9%, − 0.05%, p = 0.046). No significant difference was observed at 6 months (RD = -2.3%, 95% CI − 6.8%, 2.2%, p = 0.129) or 1 year (RD = − 0.66%, 95% CI − 7.6%, 6.3%, p = 0.418). For the SCC-NOS subset, which included 99 patients per group after matching, outcomes at 3 months (RD = − 8.6%, 95% CI − 16%, − 0.34%, p = 0.040) favored the RT + IO group and reached statistical significance. However, differences at 6 months (RD = − 6.0%, 95% CI − 17%, 5.0%, p = 0.266) and 1 year (RD = − 0.9%, 95% CI − 14%, 12%, p = 0.764) were not statistically significant. These results may be limited by the smaller sample sizes in this subgroup.

As a sensitivity analysis, the same comparisons were repeated without propensity score matching and are presented in Supplemental Table 5. These results were consistent with the primary analysis. In the unmatched analysis, RT was again associated with significantly lower mortality for the overall cohort at 3 months (RD = − 7.8%, 95% CI − 11%, − 4.8%, p < 0.001), 6 months (RD = − 8.5%, 95% CI − 13%, − 4.0%, p < 0.001), and 1 year (RD = − 4.6%, 95% CI − 9.4%, 0.13%, p = 0.056). In the AC-NOS subset, RT was associated with significantly improved 3-month (RD = − 6.9%, 95% CI − 11%, − 3.1%, p < 0.001) and 6-month (RD = − 6.3%, 95% CI − 11%, − 1.3%, p = 0.013) mortality, with no difference observed at 1 year (RD = − 2.3%, 95% CI − 8.3%, 3.7%, p = 0.446). In the SCC-NOS subset, RT was associated with a statistically significant reduction in 3-month mortality (RD = − 8.2%, 95% CI − 16%, − 0.68%, p = 0.028), while results at 6 months (RD = − 9.9%, p = 0.064) and 1 year (RD = − 7.9%, p = 0.198) again favored RT, but did not reach significance.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier log-rank testing was used to assess 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year survival outcomes for all histologies combined, as well as for the individual histologic subsets of AC-NOS and SCC-NOS (Table 3). RT was associated with significant improvement in survival in the overall cohort at 3 months (HR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.38–0.76, p < 0.001), 6 months (HR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.87, p = 0.002), and 1 year (HR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.68–0.96, p = 0.014). At 1 year, the RT + IO group had a survival probability of 59%, compared with 54% in the IO-only group (Fig. 1A), reflecting a 5% absolute improvement in survival. Overall survival was also compared, and while the median survival was not significantly different between groups, median survival was 16.0 months for RT + IO and 14.7 months for IO only. In the AC-NOS subset, RT was associated with significantly better survival at 3 months (HR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.39–0.997, p = 0.046). Differences at 6 months (HR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.56–1.1, p = 0.129) and 1 year (HR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.71–1.2, p = 0.418) were not statistically significant. At 1 year, the survival probabilities of the RT + IO group were 62% and were 61% for the IO-only group (Fig. 1B). In the SCC-NOS subset, RT was associated with significantly improved survival at 3 months (HR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.15–0.99, p = 0.040). Results at 6 months (HR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.43–1.3, p = 0.266) and 1 year (HR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.64–1.4, p = 0.764) again favored the RT + IO group but were not significant. Survival probabilities for both groups were 44% at 1 year. As a sensitivity analysis, survival outcomes were also evaluated in the full, unmatched cohort (Supplemental Table 6). Results were consistent with the matched analysis. For all histologies combined, RT was associated with significantly better survival at 3 months (HR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.32–0.60, p < 0.001), 6 months (HR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.51–0.77, p < 0.001), and 1 year (HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.68–0.92, p = 0.002). Survival at 1 year was 59% for the RT + IO cohort and 54% for the IO-only cohort. In the unmatched AC-NOS subset, RT + IO was associated with significantly improved survival at 3 months (HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.31–0.69, p < 0.001), 6 months (HR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.88, p = 0.004), while 1-year survival (HR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.69–1.0, p = 0.085) was not significant. 1-year survival in the RT + IO group was 61%, with 58% in the IO-only group. In the unmatched SCC-NOS subset, RT was again associated with significantly improved 3-month survival (HR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.20–0.95, p = 0.031) and 6-month survival (HR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.39–1.0, p = 0.048). At 1 year, the difference again favored RT + IO but was not significant (HR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.52–1.0, p = 0.083) with 53% survival in the RT + IO group and 44% survival in the IO-only group. These findings align with the matched results, confirm the robustness of the observed associations, and suggest the survival benefit associated with RT persists even in the absence of matching.

Cox proportional hazards model

A Cox proportional hazards model (Model 1) was constructed using time from the diagnosis of bone metastasis as time zero, with death as the outcome with a time frame of up to 1 year. RT + IO was associated with a significantly lower hazard of death (HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.92, p = 0.002). Additional covariates independently associated with increased hazard included adrenal metastasis (HR = 1.8, 95% CI 1.4–2.2, p < 0.001), liver metastasis (HR = 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7, p < 0.001), lymph node metastasis (HR = 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.5, p = 0.001), albumin < 3.45 g/dL (HR = 1.5, 95% CI 1.2–1.7, p < 0.001), and inpatient or observation care services (HR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.2–1.6, p < 0.001). Phosphate > 4.40 mg/dL was associated with a lower hazard of death (HR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.56–0.97, p = 0.032), which may reflect underlying nutritional or metabolic differences, or possibly serve as a surrogate for better overall clinical status. Age at index showed a modest but significant association (HR = 1.01, 95% CI 1.002–1.017, p = 0.009). Full model results are shown in Table 4.

Because survival curves between groups appeared more similar during the early follow-up period, suggesting a potential violation of the proportional hazards assumption, two stratified models were also assessed. In Model 2, covering the 0–100 day window (Supplemental Table 7), RT was associated with a substantially lower hazard of death (HR = 0.48, 95% CI 0.37–0.64, p < 0.001), suggesting the protective effect of RT may be strongest in the early period following bone metastasis. Adrenal, liver, and lymph node metastases, low albumin, inpatient/observation care, and older age were also significantly associated with increased mortality in this interval. Phosphate > 4.40 mg/dL was not significantly associated with early mortality (HR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.57–1.42, p = 0.650).

In Model 3, covering the 100-day to 1-year window (Supplemental Table 8), there was no difference in hazard between the RT + IO and IO-only groups (HR = 1.0, 95% CI 0.84–1.2, p = 0.958). Adrenal metastases (HR = 1.7, 95% CI 1.3–2.2, p < 0.001) and liver metastases (HR = 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.67, p = 0.016), low albumin (HR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.2–1.7, p < 0.001), and inpatient or observation care (HR = 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.6, p = 0.006) remained significant predictors of mortality. Phosphate > 4.40 mg/dL was associated with improved survival (HR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.48–0.95, p = 0.025). Age (HR = 1.0, 95% CI 1.0–1.011, p = 0.494), and lymph node metastases (HR = 1.2, 95% CI 0.99–1.4, p = 0.071) were not significant in this later window.

Discussion

In this study, we observed a survival benefit when radiation was added to the treatment regimen in patients receiving immunotherapy for metastatic, stage IV lung cancer with bone metastases. The cytotoxic effect of ionizing radiation is not a novel concept, with one of the first theories guiding its use (Dessauer’s target theory) introduced in the 1920s (Hazout et al. 2025). The therapeutic use of RT for cancer dates as early as 1899, just three years after the discovery of the X-ray by Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen (2014a). More recently, however, we have come to understand radiation enhances the systemic antitumor response when combined with immunotherapy (Formenti and Demaria 2013). Formenti and Demaria (2013) proposed the exposure of tumor antigens secondary to radiation damage primes T-cell response, much like responses induced by vaccination. Radiation is now viewed as a critical adjuvant to improve survival even among patients with advanced or metastatic diseases. In a large cohort of patients with stage IV NSCLC, we found combination therapy with IO and RT after the detection on bone metastasis was associated with lower 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year mortality rates in both propensity-matched and unmatched patient cohorts. Survival was significantly improved with RT + IO at all timepoints, with a 5% absolute improvement in 1-year survival.

Notably, in this study, sensitivity analyses showed the greatest reduction in mortality in response to the combination therapy occurred in the first 100 days after diagnosis of bone metastasis. Stratified regression models suggested the survival benefit of RT is concentrated in the early period following bone metastasis diagnosis or early after the initiation of combination therapy, and this effect diminishes over time. Notably the effect of several covariates differed substantially between the time windows examined, confirming non-proportional hazards. For example, adrenal metastases were not a significant predictor of early mortality but were strongly associated with increased hazard in the later period. This shift may reflect a delayed systemic impact or treatment resistance associated with adrenal involvement. These findings underscore the importance of stratifying by time interval when evaluating dynamic prognostic factors following bone metastasis diagnosis.

The survival benefit we observed in patients with metastatic lung cancer when radiation was added to immunotherapy is consistent with the findings of others, including a similar trend found by pooling the results of two randomized controlled trials (Theelen et al. 2021). When Theelen and colleagues pooled the findings of two small clinical trials of RT + IO in patients with metastatic NSCLC that individually lacked the statistical power to demonstrate improved outcomes, they identified a survival benefit of the combination therapy (Theelen et al. 2021). In contrast to our study, only patients who were IO-naïve and only lesions that had not been previously irradiated were included in the two clinical trials of RT + IO. Additionally, only a single immunotherapeutic (pembrolizumab) was administered. The use of real-world data from electronic health records allowed us to consider the potentially beneficial effects RT with many different IO agents and forced us to evaluate outcomes without regard for previous therapeutic interventions and with all possible orders of administration of the two modalities. We chose to focus only on patients with bone metastases because bone is one of the most common metastasis sites (Riihimäki et al. 2014) and the unique environment of in bone tissue presents a challenge for mounting robust immune responses despite the use of immunotherapy (Joseph et al. 2023).

Multiple retrospective studies and prospective phase I/II clinical trials have demonstrated the utility of adjuvant radiotherapy in supplementing immunotherapy in patients with advanced NSCLC and other metastatic cancers (Li et al. 2022; Liu and Chi 2023; Popp et al. 2024; Turchan et al. 2021; Foster et al. 2019). Foster and colleagues conducted a large, retrospective analysis using the real-world data from the NCDB. They identified > 40,000 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic, stage IV lung cancer; 5800 (13%) received IO. Overall survival was better in patients treated with IO if RT, in the form of either external-beam radiotherapy or stereotactic radiotherapy, was also administered (Foster et al. 2019). The scale, retrospective nature, and real-world derivation of the patient population of this study were similar to our study, but there are several key differences. The NCDB contains information from the initial diagnosis of NSCLC and details first-line treatments. In contrast, our study using the TriNetX database focused only on treatments given after the detection of bone metastasis, and patient inclusion was not dependent on the absence of prior therapies or NSCLC stage at the time of the patient’s initial diagnosis. Additionally, we examined both propensity-matched and unmatched cohorts.

Our study also differed from previous studies, including the analysis of Foster and colleagues using the NCDB, in that we examined therapeutic responses in patients stratified by NSCLC histology (AC vs. SCC). In the patients with stage IV NSCLC with AC-NOS histology, radiotherapy was associated with a significant reduction in 3-month mortality, but mortality was not significantly different at 6 months or 1 year. This pattern suggests an early benefit from RT does not extend long term. One possibility is the disease progresses faster than the treatment can offset, or other factors beyond radiosensitivity are driving long-term outcomes. This was further supported by the Cox model, which showed the strongest survival benefit in the first 100 days after diagnosis of bone metastasis. It is also worth noting smoking remains the most significant risk factor for lung cancer overall (2014b) and may play a role in limiting the impact of local therapies. These findings reinforce the importance of combining treatment with long-term disease control strategies, including addressing modifiable risk factors. In the patients with stage IV NSCLC with SCC-NOS histology, radiotherapy was associated with significantly improved 3-month survival in the matched cohort and borderline significant results at 6 months, while 1-year survival was similar between groups. The survival curves in patients with advanced SCC separated later, with differences becoming more apparent around 60–90 days, suggesting a delayed effect. This could reflect slower onset of benefit in patients with SCC histology or limitations in statistical power due to a smaller sample size. Stratified Cox modeling supported this, showing benefit in the early period and less effect after 100 days. Overall, these findings support the use of radiotherapy in combination with immunotherapy for stage IV NSCLC with bone metastases, particularly given the early survival benefit observed across groups. The improvement in mean survival was modest—around 2–3 weeks—but consistent. While this may not be considered a large effect for all patients, it may be meaningful in the setting of limited options and poor prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, the modest survival benefit of 5% at 1 year may be viewed as small and may not outweigh the additional burden of treatment for all patients. Second, we were unable to fully evaluate radiation dose, timing, or technique. Although modern approaches to radiotherapy have evolved rapidly, there remains disagreement over the optimal dose, fractionation, and timing when combined with immunotherapy (Burr et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2022). In addition, CPT codes used to identify radiotherapy do not specify the treated site, and ICD-10 coding for treatment location was infrequently reported. While limiting the analysis to patients receiving radiotherapy within 90 days of diagnosis of bone metastasis likely captured bone-directed RT in most patients, this was not verifiable. Similarly, the temporal relationship between administration of radiation and immunotherapy varied. The subgroups created when attempting to compare order of treatment were too small for adequately powered stratified analysis, which limited our ability to examine optimal sequencing. Third, access to radiation may be influenced by geography, insurance, or health system resources. As a result, some of the observed benefits may reflect differences in the patient populations receiving radiotherapy. Fourth, the use of a platform-based analytics system limited our ability to customize statistics. Finally, patients with less common NSCLC histologies, such as large cell carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, and other rare subtypes, were not included in the histology-specific subgroup analyses due to limited numbers. Future work should examine how RT regimen, sequence, and delivery method influence survival in these patients, and whether certain subgroups benefit more than others. Efforts to include patient-reported outcomes and address disparities in access to care would also strengthen future studies.

Conclusion

Treatment of NSCLC with metastasis to the bone presents a challenge even with first-line therapy. When adjuvant radiotherapy is employed in combination with immunotherapy, mortality was significantly lower at all three time-points (3 months, 6 months, 1 year) assessed in our study. Response to the combination therapy was particularly pronounced soon after the diagnosis of bone metastasis and initiation of treatment. Even if prognosis remains poor, combined immunotherapy and radiotherapy represents a strong and viable consideration for patients with few treatment options remaining.
