Role of MEG3-miRNAs in immune-inflammatory regulation in age-dependent wool follicle remodeling in Tan sheep
Authors: Letian Zhang, Jiajie Yao, Zhengwei Zhao, Lina Ma, Qing Ma, Jiankui Wang, Xuemei Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe “nine-curve” crimped wool is a key economic trait of Tan sheep, which peaks at 35 days of age and disappears in adulthood. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying this age-dependent trait remain unclear. This study aimed to decipher these mechanisms by analyzing the skin of Tan sheep across critical developmental stages.ResultsWe integrated small RNA sequencing, mRNA sequencing, and proteomics, combined with phenotypic observation and histological analysis, to examine the skin tissues of Tan sheep at three critical developmental stages (embryonic day 120, trait initiation stage; 35 days of age, trait peak stage; 24 months of age, trait disappearance stage). Phenotypic and histological results confirmed that this crimped trait depends on medullated fibers derived from primary follicles; with individual aging, the ratio of secondary to primary follicles increases, and primary follicles degenerate in adulthood, ultimately leading to the loss of the crimped trait. Transcriptomic analysis showed that the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway is the core hub mediating “immune-follicle” crosstalk. Proteomic results revealed a “transcriptional reserve-translational delay” characteristic in keratin genes such as KRT5 and KRT71, confirming that the phenotype at 35 days of age is a continuation of the developmental program initiated at embryonic day 120. Small RNA analysis revealed a "miRNA storm" that peaks at embryonic day 120 (41 miRNAs in total, 40 of which are derived from the MEG3 (GTL2)-miRNA cluster on chromosome 18), and the expression level of this storm gradually decreases with age. Multi-omics integration analysis found that these MEG3-miRNAs can target six key genes (HOXC6, RILPL2, RAB10, ARL1, MX1, and ST7) and are significantly enriched in immune-inflammatory related pathways, suggesting that they may participate in the development and remodeling of wool follicles by regulating immune-inflammatory responses, thereby affecting the formation of the crimped trait.ConclusionsThis study identifies MEG3-miRNAs as key epigenetic regulators of wool crimping and highlights the role of the immune microenvironment in follicle remodeling. We propose that these MEG3-miRNAs likely inhibit immune activation at embryonic day 120 to support primary follicle development, whereas postnatal reduction of these miRNAs relieves immune suppression, triggering overactivation and primary follicle degeneration. This study provides guidance for Tan sheep breeding, reveals conserved epigenetic‒immune synergy in mammalian skin appendages, and offers a model for studying human age-related hair disorders and cross-species age-dependent traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12688-w.Keywords: Tan sheep, Hair follicle, MEG3-miRNAs, Immune inflammation, Multi-omics

Introduction

Tan sheep, an important local breed in China, are renowned for their wool quality. Particularly at the lamb stage (one month old), they exhibit a unique wool characteristic—beautiful white “nine-curve” morphology—which holds high economic value. However, this “nine-curve” trait fades as the sheep age, with the wool losing its morphology and patterned appearance, thereby reducing its economic value. From a biological perspective, the “wool follicle” in sheep is structurally identical to the “hair follicle” in mammals, but exhibits significant functional divergence in fiber yield, cycle regulation, and breed specialization due to long-term directional selection. Ovine follicles are further classified into primary follicles (PFs), which produce medullated fibers (coarse wool), and secondary follicles (SFs), which produce non-medullated fibers (fine wool) [1, 2]. As a coarse-wool breed, Tan sheep display breed-specific characteristics in follicle type ratio and fiber composition, manifesting as a unique fur phenotype at the lamb stage that disappears in adulthood. To address this issue, efforts have been made to develop strategies for preserving lamb-stage fur traits, such as genetic or environmental interventions.

Previous studies have initially explored the epigenetic regulation of Tan sheep fur traits. Liu et al. found that age-associated DNA methylation differences are enriched in MAPK and WNT signaling pathway genes, indicating an epigenetic basis for hair follicle degeneration [3]. Additionally, comparative miRNA profiling between lambs and adult sheep revealed several significantly differentially expressed miRNAs that target key pathways, such as the MAPK signaling pathway, steroid biosynthesis, and metabolic processes [4]. Our preliminary study also revealed 34 MEG3 (GTL2)-derived miRNAs that are significantly upregulated exclusively in 1-month-old lambs, with partial overlap with Liu et al.’s findings [5]. In addition to encoding the MEG3 lncRNA, the MEG3 locus itself harbors a large miRNA cluster. While MEG3 lncRNA often functions as a ceRNA to sponge miRNAs [6], the present study focuses not on this ceRNA mechanism but on the miRNA cluster encoded by this locus—which exhibits fetal-specific high expression in Tan sheep skin, s suggesting its direct regulatory role independent of the MEG3 lncRNA. Given the regulatory potential of these miRNAs in critical signaling pathways, it is imperative to conduct in-depth investigations from a miRNA-centric perspective to elucidate their precise roles in postnatal development and fur trait formation in Tan sheep.

Hair follicle development is also influenced by other factors, including immune inflammation, hormonal levels, oxidative stress, and nutritional metabolism [7–10]. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that immune inflammation—such as cytokine dysregulation, T-cell infiltration, or the collapse of immune privilege—disrupts follicular homeostasis, leading to hair loss or impaired differentiation [11–14]. Conversely, inhibiting inflammation or restoring immune privilege can promote follicle regeneration or cell cycle progression [15–17]. While this regulatory role has been well documented in human hair loss disorders and model animals, its significance in the hair growth cycle across other animal species remains largely understudied.

The embryonic stage is the critical period for determining follicle type [18]. Research has confirmed that fetal day 120 (E120d) in sheep represents a key developmental node: it marks the complete formation of primary follicles and the entry of secondary follicles into an active growth phase [19–21]. Therefore, establishing a comprehensive analytical system covering the complete developmental stages of “embryo (trait initiation) → juvenile (trait peak) → adult (trait disappearance)” is highly important. Based on the above background and gaps, this study uses multiomics technologies (miRNA-seq, mRNA-seq, proteomics) to analyze molecular profiles of Tan sheep skin at E120d, 35d, and adulthood. By integrating multiomics profiling across three critical developmental stages (E120d, 35d, and adulthood) and focusing on the regulatory relationship between MEG3-miRNAs and immune-inflammatory pathways, this study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the age-dependent formation and loss of the “nine-curve” trait.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

All the animal experiments followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University (License No. SKLAB-2012-04-07). The Tan sheep used in this study were sourced from a breeding farm in Yanchi County, Wuzhong city (Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China), with dedicated staff ensuring that they were kept under identical conditions. A total of 9 healthy Tan sheep were selected: three 120-day-old fetuses (E120d group), three 35-day-old lambs (35d group), and three adult sheep (Adult group). Skin and wool tissue samples were collected from the scapular region of all 9 sheep at the same time and location, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and later stored at -80 °C for subsequent small RNA sequencing and transcriptomic experiments. Additionally, wool clusters from the same region were collected for phenotypic evaluation.

Phenotypic evaluation

The number of curls in each wool tuft was recorded (with each raised part of the wool counted as a single curl), and its natural length was measured via a ruler. Three sheep per group were set as biological replicates, and wool tufts were collected from different small areas of the scapular region of each sheep. The number of wool tufts used for statistical analysis was 17 in the 120-day embryo group (E120d), 14 in the 35-day-old lamb group (35d), and 20 in the adult group (Adult). Skin tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After dehydration with ethanol, clearing with xylene, and embedding in paraffin at a constant temperature, the samples were sectioned at a thickness of approximately 5 μm. The sections were stained with hematoxylin to stain the cell nuclei and with eosin to stain the cytoplasm. After dehydration and clearing, the sections were mounted with neutral resin. Using an optical microscope, transverse sections were examined to identify primary follicles (PFs) and secondary follicles (SFs) on the basis of characteristic markers. Several fields of view were randomly selected per sample, and the numbers of primary and secondary follicles were counted using ImageJ image analysis software. The ratio of secondary to primary follicles (S/P ratio) was then calculated. All phenotypic data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to evaluate the significance of differences between groups, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant and P < 0.01 considered extremely significant.

Total RNA extraction

Total RNA from all tissue samples was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). The RNA concentration and purity were assessed via a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), and the RNA integrity was measured via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA). RNA samples with an OD260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.2 and clear 28S and 18S rRNA bands (with a 28S rRNA band intensity approximately twice that of the 18S rRNA band) were used for subsequent RNA sequencing.

Small RNA library construction and sequencing

Small RNA libraries were prepared via the NEBNext® Illumina® Small RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) with 2 µg of total RNA per sample. Specifically, adapters were ligated to the 3’ and 5’ ends of miRNAs, siRNAs, and piRNAs. M-MuLV reverse transcriptase was then used to synthesize the first strand of cDNA, followed by PCR amplification. DNA fragments of 140–160 bp were purified. cDNA libraries were assessed via the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with a DNA high-sensitivity chip, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. To ensure high library saturation and adequate detection sensitivity for the regulatory complexity of different developmental stages, the raw data output was controlled based on library concentration and diversity. Specifically, the sequencing depth for the embryonic group (TE group) was 9.28–11.65 Gb per sample, while the lamb and adult groups (Tan group) were 2.83–4.18 Gb per sample. All libraries exhibited high quality, with an average raw Q20% ≥ 92%. To ensure cross-stage comparability despite the variations in raw output, the clean data were normalized using the CPM (Counts Per Million) method for all subsequent analyses. Raw FASTQ data were generated after the conversion of image files, and low-quality reads were trimmed and discarded when their average sequencing quality score was less than 20. In this study, small RNA sequencing was newly performed on embryonic samples, and the corresponding data were uploaded. The small RNA data for the lamb and adult samples were obtained from our previous work (project ID: PRJNA967624) [5].

Small RNA identification

After quality control of the small RNA clean reads, the numbers of reads corresponding to miRNAs (including known and novel miRNAs), rRNAs, tRNAs, snRNAs, snoRNAs, and repeats were counted. The distribution patterns of different types of small RNAs in Tan sheep skin tissues at different developmental stages were analyzed. The clean reads were aligned to the sheep reference genome version Ramb_v3.0 (ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0) via HISAT2 v2.2.1 [22]. Transcript assembly was performed via StringTie v2.2.3, and the corresponding miRNA transcripts were identified. Known sheep miRNA sequences were retrieved from the miRBase database (http://www.mirbase.org/), and the types of identified miRNAs were compared across groups.

mRNA library construction and sequencing

mRNA was extracted from total RNA via oligo(dT) magnetic beads through two rounds of purification. The mRNA was fragmented with a magnesium ion-based fragmentation kit and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. E. coli DNA polymerase I and RNase H were used for second-strand synthesis, incorporating dUTP solution to generate blunt-ended double-stranded DNA, which was then A-tailed and ligated with adapters containing T bases. Size selection and purification were performed via magnetic beads. Double-stranded DNA was treated with the UDG enzyme to digest the second strand and amplified via PCR to construct libraries of 300 bp ± 50 bp fragments. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform in PE150 mode. Strict quality control was implemented, with an average sequencing depth of 6 Gb per sample and Q30 base percentage ≥ 90%. Raw data in FASTQ format were subjected to quality control, including adapter trimming, duplicate removal, and filtering of low-quality reads, via Fastp software.

Differential expression analysis (miRNA, mRNA)

The differential expression of miRNAs was analyzed via the DESeq2 package (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) in R (v4.3.2) on the basis of TPM values calculated from the alignment and assembly results. Lowly expressed miRNAs with TPM < 1 in more than 50% of samples were filtered out prior to analysis. Pairwise comparisons between the E120d, 35d, and Adult groups were made, with thresholds of adjusted P value (padj) < 0.05 and |log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 for identifying differentially expressed miRNAs; the median of ratios normalization method was applied for data normalization in DESeq2 analysis. For mRNAs, clean reads were aligned to the Ramb_v3.0 sheep reference genome via HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with parameters --max-intronlen 500,000 --no-mixed --no-discordant, and transcript assembly and quantification were performed via StringTie (v2.2.1) with default parameters. For mRNA differential expression analysis, gene-level raw read counts were obtained via featureCounts (v2.0.1). DESeq2 was used with the same thresholds (padj < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1).

MiRNA target gene prediction and network analysis

3’ UTR sequences from the sheep reference genome (Oar_v4.0, Nov. 2015) were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome-asia.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables). The target genes of the differentially expressed miRNAs were predicted via the miRanda online tool (v3.3a, http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/local_miranda_miRNA_target_prediction_120) with core parameters: gap open penalty = -9, gap extension penalty = -4, score threshold ≥ 140, free energy ≤ -1 kcal/mol. Targets meeting the above criteria were selected as potential candidates. The interaction network between miRNAs and their target genes was visualized via Cytoscape software (v3.10.1, https://cytoscape.org/) with the ForceDirected layout algorithm, and nodes were filtered by a minimum interaction score of 140 for clarity. Due to the unavailability of ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0 3‘UTR sequences in public databases, miRNA target prediction was performed using the Oar_v4.0 annotation. The resulting gene symbols were used directly for functional enrichment analysis and for examining their expression trends in both transcriptomic and proteomic datasets (based on ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0).

Protein extraction, digestion and peptide preparation

Total proteins were extracted from frozen samples via lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 1% SDS, and protease inhibitors. After tissue grinding (3 times, 180 s each), cryogenic sonication (30 min), and centrifugation (16000 × g, 8 °C, 30 min), the protein concentration of the supernatant was determined via a BCA assay, followed by SDS‒PAGE. For digestion, 100 µg of protein was resuspended in 100 mM TEAB, reduced with 10 mM TCEP (37 °C, 60 min), alkylated with 40 mM IAM (room temperature, 40 min in darkness), and then trypsin-digested (1:50 ratio) at 37 °C overnight. The digested peptides were desalted with HLB, dried, and quantified with NANO DROP ONE.

Mass spectrometry and data analysis

Peptides were analyzed via VanquishNeo coupled with timsTOF Ultra2 mass spectrometers (Majorbio, Shanghai) in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode with UPAC C18 column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) or homemade C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) and solvent systems (A: 2% ACN + 0.1% formic acid; B: 80% ACN + 0.1% formic acid) over 8-minute runs with a gradient elution program: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–6 min, 2–35% B; 6–7 min, 35–90% B; 7–8 min, 90% B. Spectronaut 19 searches data with the following parameters: peptide length 7–52, trypsin/P (max 2 missed cleavages), fixed carbamidomethylation (C), variable oxidation (M) and N-acetylation, FDR ≤ 0.01 (protein/peptide), and peptide confidence ≥ 99%. Raw mass spectrometry data were normalized by total ion current (TIC) before quantification, and MaxLFQ algorithm was used for protein quantification. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified with the thresholds of FC > 1.2/<0.83 and FDR-corrected P < 0.05. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) were performed using Metascape online platform (https://metascape.org).

Functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed miRNAs and genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses via the DAVID online tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The enrichment results were visualized via the ggplot2 package in R and Cytoscape software. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for differentially expressed genes in sheep skin tissues was conducted via the online tool https://www.omicstudio.cn/. Trend analysis of the expression patterns of differentially expressed miRNAs and genes was performed via the OmicShare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/), with a correlation coefficient threshold of > 0.7 and an enrichment significance P value < 0.05.

Results

Embryonic establishment at E120d dictates postnatal wool phenotypes: primary follicle-derived medullated fibers drive the nine-curve crimp transition in Tan sheep

Research indicates that the growth and maturation of wool follicles in Tan sheep skin tissue are completed by embryonic day 120 (E120d). However, the distinctive “nine-curve” wool trait observed in 35-day-old lambs (35d) completely disappears in adulthood. To investigate the molecular genetic mechanisms regulating the pelt characteristics of Tan sheep, we examined skin tissues from three developmental stages: 120-day embryos (E120d), 35-day-old lambs (35d), and 24-month-old adult sheep (Adult).

The results of the wool phenotype measurements revealed that the number of wool crimps in the 35 d group was the highest (7–9), which was extremely significantly greater than that in the E120d group (3–5, number of wool tufts counted = 17) and the adult group (1–2, number of wool tufts counted = 20) (one-way ANOVA, P < 0.01) (Fig. 1A). When separating the medullated and nonmedullated fibers within wool fiber clusters, we found that the medullated fibers were thicker and longer, maintaining the original morphology of the wool cluster, whereas the nonmedullated fibers were finer, shorter, and softer. These findings suggest that the curl morphology of Tan sheep wool is primarily maintained by medullated fibers (Fig. 1A and C). Additionally, the natural length of wool significantly increased with age (number of wool tufts for wool phenotype statistics: E120d = 17, 35d = 14, Adult = 20) (Fig. 1B). Further statistical analysis of the curl-to-length ratio (C/L) revealed no significant difference between the E120d group and the 35 d group, but the C/L ratio in both groups was extremely significantly greater than that in the adult group (Fig. 1D).

H&E staining of skin sections revealed that the density of secondary follicles significantly increased with age, and the ratio of secondary to primary follicles (S/P ratio) clearly increased. The figures clearly show that primary wool follicles exhibited severe morphological damage and degeneration in adulthood (Fig. 1E). During development, the S/P ratio was negatively correlated with the curl/length ratio (C/L ratio) (Fig. 1D and F). These findings collectively indicate that: (1) the characteristic “nine-curve” morphology of 35-day-old Tan sheep wool is structurally dependent on medullated fibers derived from primary wool follicles; (2) this trait is developmentally predetermined by embryonic day 120 (E120d), with the 35-day phenotype representing persistence rather than de novo establishment, as evidenced by comparable C/L ratios between E120d and 35d; and (3) age-dependent degeneration and morphological damage of primary wool follicles directly reduce the proportion of medullated fibers, leading to the loss of crimped morphology in adulthood.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals stage-specific immune-follicle cross talk mediated by the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway

Transcriptomic analysis of Tan sheep skin tissues across three developmental stages revealed distinct gene expression patterns. Specifically, a comparison between the E120d and 35d groups revealed 3,651 upregulated and 4,206 downregulated genes, whereas the E120d versus adult comparison revealed 3,990 upregulated and 3,962 downregulated genes. The 35 d versus adult comparison yielded 981 upregulated and 859 downregulated genes. On the basis of the aforementioned phenotypic data, we focused on the expression patterns of ACTA2 and MYH11, which are marker genes specific to the arrector pili muscles uniquely present in primary wool follicles. The results revealed that both genes were significantly downregulated in adulthood. Moreover, KRTAP6-1, a gene reported in previous studies to be highly expressed in the skin, where fine wool grows from secondary wool follicles, exhibited extremely significant upregulation with increasing age. In contrast, AQP5, a gene derived from sweat gland epithelial cells unique to primary wool follicles, was no longer expressed in adulthood. The expression profiles of these genes collectively reveal the molecular mechanisms underlying the phenotypic changes (Fig. 2A). To go beyond differential expression analysis and capture global transcriptional trends, we performed GSEA clustering and pathway enrichment analysis on all expressed genes (Fig. 2B, S1, S2), which revealed notable features related to immune regulation. Notably, genes downregulated in the E120d group compared with both the 35d and adult groups were significantly enriched in several core immune pathways, including cytokine‒cytokine receptor interactions, the chemokine signaling pathway, the JAK‒STAT signaling pathway, the Toll‒like receptor signaling pathway, and the retinol metabolism pathway (which has immune regulatory functions). A further comparison between the 35 d and adult groups revealed that downregulated genes remained enriched in cytokine‒cytokine receptor interactions and chemokine signaling pathways, suggesting that immune regulation gradually intensifies with age. These GSEA results indicate that immune-inflammatory responses exhibit significant stage-specific changes and that this dynamic characteristic is likely to have an important impact on wool follicle development, warranting in-depth investigation.

We next identified stage-specific genes via stringent criteria (FPKM ≥ 10 in the target group vs. < 1 in the comparison group). The 61 E120d-specific (adult-absent) genes, 32 E120d-specific (35d-absent) genes, and 19 35d-specific (adult-absent) genes were enriched in myocyte cytoskeleton organization and motor protein pathways. In contrast, 53 adult-specific (E120d-absent) genes were enriched in TNF signaling and retinol metabolism (Fig. 2C). These findings indicate that fetal/juvenile-specific genes primarily participate in muscular development and skin appendage formation, whereas adult-specific genes are associated with metabolic maturation, skin barrier maintenance, and immune function.

To uncover how immune-inflammatory regulation drives development of skin with wool follicles, we focused on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified in comparisons between the E120d group and both the 35d and adult groups—specifically, those that serve as critical nodes linking folliculogenesis pathways (BMP, Notch, Wnt, Hippo, ECM-receptor interaction, and TGF-β signaling) and immune-inflammatory pathways (T-cell receptor, chemokine, Toll-like receptor, NF-κB, TNF, and JAK-STAT signaling)—and constructed interaction networks (Fig. 2D). Notably, compared with the 35 d and adult groups, the E120 d group presented a significantly greater proportion of downregulated genes across these intersecting pathways, with key genes involved in BMP, Notch, Wnt, and Hippo signaling being markedly downregulated—a trend suggesting that dynamic shifts in immune‒inflammatory signals may be tightly coupled to stage-specific transitions in follicle development.

Critically, the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway emerged as a central hub bridging follicle growth and immune responses: its members not only participate in distinct folliculogenesis-related and immune-related pathways but also exhibit opposing expression patterns (PD-1 (PDCD1) is significantly upregulated at E120d, whereas PD-L1 (CD274) is significantly downregulated) (Fig. 2D), implying a role for immune checkpoints in balancing embryonic folliculogenesis and immune tolerance. Furthermore, genes such as PIK3CB, PIK3CD, PIK3CG, and AKT2 were significantly downregulated across multiple folliculogenesis-related pathways (PI3K/Akt, IL17, FGFR, etc.), whereas JAK1, STAT1, STAT3, and STAT5A were concurrently downregulated in immune pathways (JAK/STAT, chemokine, Toll-like receptor, and Th cell differentiation). These coordinated changes in cross-regulatory molecules suggest that immune-inflammatory regulation serves as a core driver of age-dependent wool follicle remodeling.

Proteomics reveals immune-associated stage-specific characteristics and potential roles of posttranscriptional regulation (miRNA-mediated) in Tan sheep skin

Given that there may be discrepancies between transcriptional regulation and the actual functional execution of proteins and that the activation of immune‒inflammatory pathways ultimately relies on protein-level signal transduction and interactions, we simultaneously conducted proteomic analyses to further validate the association between immune regulation and wool follicle remodeling revealed by the transcriptome, as well as to dissect the functional implementation of its molecular mechanisms. By intersecting genes and proteins with consistent expression patterns from transcriptomic and proteomic data (Fig. 3A), we observed a substantial number of instances where transcriptional and protein expression trends did not align. This finding suggests that the expression of a substantial portion of genes is subject to posttranscriptional regulation.

The keratin family has received focused attention because of its direct involvement in determining wool traits by regulating wool follicle development and wool shaft formation. Among them, KRT5/KRT14 (markers of the outer root sheath proliferative layer) [23, 24], KRT15 (a classic marker of bulge stem cells) [25], KRT17 (a cytoskeletal protein) [26], and KRT71 (associated with curly wool formation) [3] exhibited distinct expression patterns: at the transcriptome level, their expression was highest in the E120d group and significantly lower in the 35d group than in the other two stages, whereas at the protein level, their expression in the 35d group was significantly greater than that in the other groups (Fig. 3B). This phenomenon indicates that keratin genes are actively transcribed during the embryonic stage (E120d), where they accumulate a large reserve of mRNAs. By 35 d, despite a significant decrease in transcriptional levels, protein levels increased, suggesting that the stored mRNA was efficiently translated. This “transcriptional reserve-translational delay” model provides a sufficient keratin supply for the dense and high-quality “nine-curve” phenotype of Tan sheep wool at 35 d. This not only avoids potential interference with structural development caused by premature and excessive keratin expression during the embryonic stage but also functionally confirms that the 35d wool phenotype is a continuation of developmental characteristics established at E120d rather than a newly formed trait. Given that keratins are structural proteins specifically expressed in wool follicles and hair shafts, this post-transcriptional regulatory mode may represent a fine-tuned mechanism for spatiotemporally controlling wool trait formation.

A total of 9756 proteins were identified in the proteomic data of Tan sheep skin tissues across the three stages. Trend analysis revealed three significantly enriched expression trends (P < 0.05) among these proteins (Fig. 3C and D): proteins highly expressed in fetal skin (E120d ≥ 35d > Adult, Profile 3) were characterized by core functions in skin barrier construction, wool follicle morphogenesis (e.g., cytoskeleton in muscle cells, cornified envelope, tight junction), signal transduction, cell fate determination, and innate immune defense (e.g., pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, Fc gamma receptor-mediated phagocytosis). This reflects the high proliferative demand and enhanced immune defense against pathogens in immature fetal skin and wool follicles. Proteins upregulated in adult skin (E120d ≤ 35d < Adult, Profile 4) were related to energy and substance metabolism (e.g., glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, carbon metabolism) and protein processing (e.g., endoplasmic reticulum processing, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis), adapting to long-term functional maintenance while showing a consistent weakening of early structural and defense pathways. Proteins with a “V-shaped” trend (E120d > 35d < Adult, Profile 2) were enriched in pathways such as the cell cycle, DNA replication, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling, reflecting their dynamic roles in promoting cell proliferation and wool follicle development during the embryonic stage, transient suppression in the neonatal period, reactivation in adult tissue repair and wool follicle regeneration. Notably, Cytoskeleton in muscle cells was enriched across all three trends, potentially involved in the formation of arrector pili muscles—a structure that is exclusive to primary wool follicles in sheep skin and distinguishes them from secondary fine wool follicles —strongly suggesting its critical regulatory role in wool follicle morphology and wool traits (e.g., curvature, strength).

Interestingly, in the KEGG enrichment analysis of proteins with high expression in Profile 0 and Profile 1 of the E120d group, the RNA degradation pathway was significantly enriched. Moreover, in the KEGG enrichment analysis of all differentially expressed proteins (P < 0.05) between the E120d and Adult groups, these differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in the “microRNAs in cancer” pathway (Fig. 3C). Both enrichment results converge on posttranscriptional regulation, highlighting its role in coordinating spatiotemporal gene expression during skin development from E120d to adulthood. This also provides a direction for our subsequent research on miRNAs.

A 41-miRNA storm at the MEG3 locus peaks in fetal skin and declines postnatally in Tan sheep

We analyzed the genomic distribution of known sheep miRNAs via miRBase annotations (155 in total). As shown in Fig. 4A, these miRNAs are distributed across multiple chromosomes, with 103 miRNAs concentrated on chromosome 18, accounting for a significant proportion (Fig. 4A). Overall, the miRNA expression patterns showed clear intergroup separation and high intragroup reproducibility (Fig. 4B), and the miRNA expression levels in the E120d group were greater than those in the 35d and Adult groups (Fig. 4C). This result indicates that the E120d group has a distinct miRNA expression pattern compared with the subsequent developmental stages.

A total of eight expression trends (3(3-1)-1=8) were identified, labeled Profiles 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7. F Heatmap showing the expression levels of miRNAs in Profile 0

To further explore the dynamic expression characteristics of miRNAs, we performed trend analysis on all known differentially expressed miRNAs. The results revealed that these miRNAs exhibited 8 expression patterns across the three groups (3(3−1)-1 = 8), among which only Profile 0 (containing 41 miRNAs) showed a significant correlation (correlation coefficient > 0.7) and enrichment significance (P < 0.05). These 41 miRNAs exhibited a consistent expression pattern: the highest expression was detected in the E120d group, followed by the 35d group, and the lowest was detected in the Adult group (Fig. 4E). Notably, with the exception of oar-miR-133, the remaining 40 miRNAs were located at the MEG3-miRNA locus on chromosome 18 (Fig. 4D), suggesting that this locus may play a key role in regulating skin development across different age groups. On the basis of these findings, we designated this 41-miRNA set as a “miRNA storm”.

Heatmap analysis of the 41 miRNAs in Profile 0 further confirmed that their expression levels in the E120d group were significantly greater than those in the other two groups: the transcripts per million (TPM) values of these miRNAs in the E120d group ranged from several thousand to over ten thousand, whereas they were several hundred in the 35d group and close to baseline in the Adult group (Fig. 4F). These findings suggest that the “miRNA storm”, which has ultrahigh expression in the embryonic stage, plays an important regulatory role in skin development and phenotypic transformation.

Deciphering key regulatory networks of wool traits in Tan sheep via miRNA target prediction and multi-omics integration

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of the 41 miRNAs in Profile 0 during skin development in Tan sheep, we predicted their target genes via the miRanda algorithm. miRanda identifies potential binding sites between miRNA sequences and mRNA 3’UTRs, evaluates binding stability via a thermodynamic model (ΔG), and filters conserved binding sites through cross-species alignment. This analysis constructed a network of 788 interactions involving 33 of the 41 storm miRNAs and 260 target genes (Fig. 5A). These target genes include multiple immune cytokines, such as FOS, IL6, CXCL8, TNF, and IL1B, as well as a number of previously reported genes associated with hair follicle development, including FGF5, FGF10, WNT2, IGFBP2, CTNNB1, KRT71, KRT25, and BMPR1B (Fig. 5A). Further KEGG functional enrichment analysis of the target genes revealed that the top 25 pathways were mainly immune and inflammatory response-related pathways and signal transduction pathways (Fig. 5B).

To further explore the potential regulatory roles of miRNAs in sheep skin development, we performed an intersection analysis of target genes of 41 miRNAs, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the transcriptome data (E120d vs. Adult group), and downregulated proteins in the proteome data (E120d vs. Adult group). Through this analysis, a total of 6 candidate miRNA target genes were identified (Fig. 5D). Among these candidates, three genes (HOXC6, RILPL2, RAB10) exhibited expression patterns consistent with miRNA-mediated translational repression: their mRNA levels were either upregulated or unchanged in the E120d group compared with the Adult group, whereas their protein levels were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5C and E). The remaining three genes (ARL1, MX1, ST7) displayed patterns indicative of miRNA-induced mRNA degradation, with both mRNA and protein levels significantly lower in the E120d group than in the Adult group (Fig. 5C and F). In conclusion, the expression of these 6 genes at both the transcriptional and protein levels is consistent with the expression characteristics of miRNA-targeted regulation, and were identified as candidate target genes of the miRNA storm.

Discussion

Based on the integrated multiomics analyses of Tan sheep skin at E120d, 35th-day and adult stages, we propose a regulatory model that underpins the age-dependent changes in the “nine-curve” wool trait (summarized in Fig. 6). A robust miRNA storm was detected specifically at the E120d stage, and its intensity decreased gradually with increasing age. Target genes of these differentially expressed miRNAs were significantly enriched in multiple immune-inflammatory-related pathways, with key genes in the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway acting as molecular bridges linking immune signaling and primary wool follicle developmental pathways. We infer that this E120d-specific miRNA storm suppresses the immune response, creating a favorable low-inflammatory microenvironment that supports the vigorous development of primary wool follicles—the structural basis of the “nine-curve” wool trait. As the individuals mature, the attenuation of the miRNA storm relieves this immune suppression, leading to immune overactivation. The overactivated immune responses may subsequently attack the primary wool follicle structure, resulting in primary wool follicle degeneration and the ultimate disappearance of the juvenile-specific “nine-curve” wool trait. This model provides a clear molecular explanation for how epigenetic regulation (miRNAs) interacts with the immune microenvironment to modulate a key economic trait in Tan sheep, highlighting the critical role of immune-follicle crosstalk in skin appendage development.

Schematic diagram of age-dependent wool follicle remodeling driven by immune-inflammatory regulation mediated by MEG3-miRNAs

Primary follicle dynamics underpin the loss of “nine-curve” wool morphology in Tan sheep

In the fetal skin of sheep, PFs begin to form around embryonic day 65 (E65), followed by the initiation of SFs around embryonic day 85 (E85) [27]. This characteristic serves as a crucial basis for investigating hair follicle evolution in the field of comparative biology—coarse-wool sheep (e.g., Tan sheep) are characterized by the vigorous development of primary follicles, whereas fine-wool sheep (e.g., Merino sheep) are dominated by secondary follicle proliferation. Our findings establish that the age-dependent increase in S/P ratio directly reflects the decline of primary-follicle-derived medullated fibers—the sole structural basis of the “nine-curve” trait. This mechanistic insight positions Tan sheep as a valuable model for studying how shifts in follicle type composition drive breed-specific wool characteristics. The dynamic change pattern of the age-dependent decline in the proportion of primary follicles is highly consistent with the expression trend of MEG3-miRNAs and the activity dynamics of immune-inflammatory pathways, suggesting that this phenotype may be closely associated with the synergistic effect of epigenetic regulation and immune microenvironment remodeling.

Spatiotemporal MEG3-miRNA storm: a key link to wool phenotype in sheep skin

In this study, we observed that the expression levels of 41 miRNAs exhibited a trend of “E120d (embryonic day 120) > 35 days of age > adulthood”—the only significantly enriched expression trend across the three developmental stages, indicating the formation of an “miRNA storm” before the lamb stage. Among these miRNAs, 40 (excluding oar-miR-133) were localized to the MEG3-miRNA locus, a core segment of the mammalian DLK1-GTL2 (MEG3) imprinted region and the largest mammalian-specific miRNA cluster [28]. Multiple mouse studies have demonstrated that this epigenetic regulatory module is essential for embryonic development and the maintenance of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) pluripotency, and exhibits strict spatiotemporal expression specificity [29, 30].

From the perspective of animal epigenetics, this MEG3-miRNA storm reflects the strict spatiotemporal regulatory characteristic of the imprinted locus in skin development. Yufang Liu et al.’s study on miRNAs in the skin of 1-month-old lambs and adult sheep also verified this spatiotemporal regulatory pattern [4]; our study, by supplementing embryonic-stage samples, further confirmed that the high miRNA expression in postnatal juveniles is a continuation of the embryonic expression state, and its expression level gradually declines with individual aging. In addition, Ana-Mihaela Lupan et al. performed next-generation sequencing on cardiac fibroblasts isolated from young and aged mice, revealing that miRNAs derived from this locus are significantly downregulated during aging; further evaluation indicated that the suppression of miRNAs from this locus is a universal feature of multi-organ aging in mammals [31]. This cross-species expression pattern reveals the core role of the MEG3-miRNA imprinted cluster as an epigenetic regulatory hub linking embryonic development and organ senescence. These findings position the MEG3-miRNA cluster as an evolutionarily conserved epigenetic timer that links embryonic development to postnatal follicle remodeling in sheep.

Previously, our research group compared individuals with the ancestral-like coarse (ALC) wool phenotype in a fine wool sheep population with their genetically matched fine wool phenotype siblings. We found that the ALC wool phenotype of Merino sheep is regulated by epigenetic mechanisms of the MEG3-miRNA imprinted gene cluster on sheep chromosome 18 [32]. Compared with this atavistic phenomenon in fine-wool sheep, the change pattern of the S/P ratio in Tan sheep (a coarse-wool breed) in this study was highly similar—both increased with age. Moreover, our miRNA data analysis identified MEG3-miRNAs as the major regulatory elements. This result further confirms the conservation of the epigenetic regulatory mechanism by which MEG3-miRNAs participate in early wool follicle development.

MEG3-miRNAs mediated immune-inflammatory regulation drives age-dependent wool follicle remodeling in Tan sheep

Integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and small RNA sequencing data, we identified the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway as a central hub linking MEG3-miRNA dynamics to age-dependent follicle remodeling. This multi-omics convergence supports a model in which the MEG3-miRNA cluster drives the age-dependent remodeling of wool follicles and the loss of the “nine-curve” phenotype in Tan sheep by targeting the immune-inflammatory network.

As the body’s largest immune organ, the skin serves as the first line of defense against external pathogens and maintains local microenvironmental homeostasis via sophisticated regulatory mechanisms [33, 34]. As key skin appendages, hair follicles not only support hair growth but also possess unique immune privilege (IP) properties. This property protects follicular stem cell activity and ensures normal hair growth by minimizing immune system attacks during the hair follicle cycle [35]. Previous studies have confirmed that excessive activation of immune inflammation disrupts the immune privilege of hair follicles, leading to follicular regression or impaired hair growth [36]; furthermore, dynamic changes in cutaneous immune-inflammatory responses can modulate the follicular microenvironment, thereby regulating the cyclic growth and regenerative capacity of hair follicles [37, 38]. These findings provide an important theoretical background for the “immune‒follicle association” observed in this study.

At the molecular pathway level, our study further confirmed that the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint signaling pathway acts as a key regulatory hub mediating crosstalk between immune-inflammatory responses and hair follicle development: key genes of this pathway (PIK3CB, PIK3CD) and core immune pathway genes (JAK1, STAT1) were significantly downregulated in PI3K/Akt, IL17 (follicle development-related pathways) and JAK/STAT (immune-associated pathways), respectively. Existing evidence has demonstrated that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is essential for maintaining cutaneous immune homeostasis. On one hand, it negatively regulates the activation of effector CD8⁺ T cells and reduces the secretion of inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, thereby preventing excessive cutaneous inflammatory responses [39, 40]. On the other hand, cutaneous mast cells highly express PD-L1, which synergistically modulates CD8⁺ T cell activity—a regulatory pattern conserved in both murine and human skin. Additionally, PD-1 signaling participates in the early colonization and functional programming of cutaneous T cells, establishing a stable local immune microenvironment conducive to hair follicle growth and development [41]. Based on these findings, we speculate that downregulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway may disrupt the homeostatic balance between cutaneous immunity and follicle development, and potentially affect follicle growth as well as the formation of the wool “nine-curve” morphology by regulating downstream immune-inflammatory and follicle development pathways.

Building on the aforementioned immune-follicle associations, this study identified HOXC6, RILPL2, RAB10, ARL1, MX1, and ST7 as key miRNA target genes via integrated multiomics analysis and cross-validation. Currently, there are no reports directly linking these genes to hair follicle development; however, existing studies have demonstrated that HOXC6, RAB10, and RILPL2 play critical roles in immune-inflammatory regulation, providing important references for exploring their potential functions in follicle development. Specifically, HOXC6 is a transcription factor that is overexpressed in various cancers, and its core function is to regulate the tumor immune microenvironment: in gliomas, its overexpression is associated with high immune cell infiltration and upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules [42]; in colorectal cancer, it can increase the infiltration of M2-type macrophages by regulating CCL2 and promoting IL-6 secretion, thereby constructing an inflammatory microenvironment that favors epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) and cancer progression [43, 44]. HOXC6’s known role in shaping immune microenvironments suggests a potential mechanism for influencing the follicle niche. Notably, its homolog HOXC13—a well-characterized core regulator of hair follicle keratinization—binds conserved motifs in hair keratin gene promoters; knockout of hoxc13 completely ablates hair keratin homolog expression and blocks keratinized structure formation [45]. Given the conserved functional patterns of HOX family genes in developmental regulation, HOXC6 may maintain hair follicle niche homeostasis via analogous transcriptional mechanisms. The function of RAB10 is closely dependent on the phosphorylation of its threonine 73 (pT73–Rab10) by LRRK2 kinase: this modification not only serves as an inflammatory biomarker associated with motor dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease but also directly participates in innate immune responses (e.g., neutrophil degranulation) and regulates immune cell responses by mediating macropinosome maturation and CCR5 signal transduction [46–48]. RAB10’s central role in regulating immune cell function provides a theoretical clue for its potential to influence the hair follicle immune niche and indirectly participate in hair follicle development. The literature reports that RILPL2 is only an adaptor protein in migrasomes that works synergistically with phosphorylated RAB10 and CAV1 to regulate vesicle transport [49]. RILPL2, an adaptor protein functionally linked to RAB10 [49], may also participate in vesicle-mediated immune signaling within the follicle niche, though this hypothesis requires direct experimental validation.

Limitations

A primary limitation of this study is the small sample size (N = 3 per group), constrained by the inherent difficulty in collecting stage-specific Tan sheep skin samples. Consequently, future validation in larger cohorts is essential to ensure the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, while this study established a statistical correlation among the MEG3-miRNA axis, immune regulation, and wool follicle remodeling, the direct regulatory interplay between MEG3-miRNAs and targets like HOXC6 and RAB10 lacks functional validation. Future research should prioritize functional assays to bridge the gap between observed correlations and biological causation, specifically exploring the crosstalk between immunity and follicle development. Moreover, due to the anatomical characteristics of Tan sheep, we adopted a time-series sample design to focus on the intrinsic regulatory mechanism of wool follicle remodeling but were unable to include wool-free skin control samples. As multi-omics integration has become a powerful tool for dissecting complex traits [50, 51], incorporating single-cell RNA sequencing will be crucial to resolving cellular heterogeneity [52, 53]. Therefore, a systematic, multi-omic approach is required to fully deconstruct the molecular landscape of Tan sheep wool follicle remodeling.

Conclusions

This study revealed that an imprinted miRNA cluster derived from the MEG3 locus on chromosome 18 in Tan sheep acts as a key epigenetic regulator of immune microenvironment homeostasis during wool follicle development. A total of 40 miRNAs from this cluster, together with oar-miR-133 (which is located elsewhere), formed a transient “miRNA storm” at the embryonic E120d stage. This storm significantly suppresses immune-inflammatory pathways by targeting genes such as HOXC6 and RAB10 and synergizing with the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, thereby establishing a low-inflammatory microenvironment that supports primordial follicle formation. After birth, the decrease in miRNA expression releases this immune suppression, leading to excessive inflammation and accelerated follicular degeneration. These findings identify the MEG3-miRNA cluster as a potential target for genetically improving wool traits in sheep and provide a comparative framework for understanding immune‒follicle crosstalk in human inflammatory hair disorders. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying the age-dependent decline in miRNA expression remain unclear, and future studies should focus on elucidating the transcriptional regulatory network of this miRNA cluster.
