Impact of ablation energy on mortality after oesophageal fistula and injury complicating atrial fibrillation ablation procedures: results from a worldwide FDA database, the POTTER-AF 2 study
Authors: Roland R Tilz, Sorin S Popescu, Helmut Pürerfellner, Karl-H Kuck, Kun Xiang, Ekin C Uzunoglu, Christian-H Heeger, Julia Vogler, Harikrishna Tandri, Fabrizio Assis, Ghanshyam Shantha, José L Merino, John N Catanzaro, Frieder Braunschweig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsOesophageal fistulas (OF) and injuries (OI) are rare, but life-threatening complications following catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF). Data about their incidence, management, and outcome are scarce. This study investigates the clinical characteristics and outcomes of OF and OI following AF ablation.Methods and resultsAll OF and OI reported between August 2009 and August 2019 in the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database of the Food and Drug Administration were analysed. A total of 1274 device adverse events following AF/atrial flutter (AFL) ablation were reported in the MAUDE database. Of them, 60 (4.7%) represented patients with OF or OI. A total of 47 patients exhibited OF, while 13 OI without perforation. A total of 35 (58.3%) patients underwent radiofrequency (RF)-based ablation, 20 (33.3%) cryoballoon (CB)-based PVI, and 5 (8.3%) a laser-based PVI. The mortality was 63.3%, but significantly higher in the OF group as compared to the OI group (76.6% vs. 15.4%; P < 0.001). When analysing only the patients exhibiting OF, the mortality was 71.0% among the RF patients, 86.7% among the CB patients, and 100% among those receiving laser ablations (P = 0.427). Among patients exhibiting OF only, the mortality was 80% for those treated surgically, 80% for those treated exclusively endoscopically, and 100% for those treated conservatively.ConclusionAlmost one-third of the patients developing OF underwent CB-based ablation. More than three-fourths of the patients died, without significant difference between the ablation energy used. All patients with OF treated conservatively died. OF may occur despite oesophageal temperature monitoring.Keywords: Catheter ablation, Atrial fibrillation, Radiofrequency energy, Oesophageal fistula, Cryoenergy, Oesophageal injury

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained cardiac arrhythmia in adults.1,2 Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) serves as the cornerstone of interventional AF treatment and is increasingly used worldwide through various ablation energy sources and techniques. It is generally accepted that PVI is a safe and effective therapeutic approach, with similar results among the different energy sources utilized.3–7 Moreover, technical improvements and new technologies have continuously enhanced the safety profile of this procedure.8–11

Oesophageal fistula (OF) is a rare but devastating complication of catheter-based AF ablation, most likely caused by thermal lesion to the oesophagus during energy delivery to the posterior wall of the left atrium (LA). The incidence of this complication is low, ranging from 0.016 to 0.1%.12–17 The recently published POTTER-AF (PrOgnosis following oesophageal fisTula formaTion in patients undergoing cathetER ablation for AF) study is the largest publication to date, providing data on the incidence, management, and outcome of OF after AF ablation.16 The overall incidence of OF in this study was 0.025%, with 0.038% among patients who underwent radiofrequency (RF)-based ablation and 0.0015% among those who underwent cryoballoon (CB)-based PVI.16

The aim of the present POTTER-AF 2 study was to evaluate the procedural characteristics and outcome of patients reported to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for exhibiting postablation OF and oesophageal injury (OI), with a particular focus on the impact of the energy source on mortality.

Methods

The Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database comprises the medical device reports (MDRs) submitted to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) by mandatory reporters (manufacturers, importers, and device user facilities) and voluntary reporters such as health care professionals, patients, and consumers.18

We searched the MAUDE database for adverse events related to AF and atrial flutter (AFL) ablation procedures reported between 01/08/2009 and 31/08/2019. The searched product class was Catheter, Percutaneous, Cardiac Ablation, For Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation and Atrial Flutter. All reports were manually reviewed by two investigators (J.C. and K.X.). Only adverse events associated with OF and OI formation were included. The selected reports were then independently analysed by two other investigators (R.R.T. and S.S.P.), who collected data on reporting modality, location, energy source, catheter type and manufacturer, clinical manifestations, type of lesion, diagnosis methods, treatment, and survival. All suspected or diagnosed OF, atrio-oesophageal fistula, oeso-pericardial fistulas, and oesophageal perforations were classified as OF. All oesophageal lesions without oesophageal wall perforation were classified as OI. However, the exact type of lesion was not available for all patients. The event type was classified as ‘Death’ or ‘Injury’.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as absolute and relative frequencies, n (%). They were compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. They are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) in case of normal distribution and as median and interquartile range (Q1, Q3) otherwise. The continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t test when normally distributed and the corresponding nonparametric test (Mann–Whitney U) otherwise.

All the P values are two-sided, and a P < 0.05 was considered significant. The statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS software, version 29 (IBM SPSS Statistics).

Results

Data reporting and technical issues

Among 1274 reported device adverse events following AF/AFL ablation, 68 (5.3%) were identified as potential oesophageal lesions (fistula formation or injury). Of these, eight reports were subsequently excluded as follows: three because of double reporting, one because of injury caused by the transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) probe but not by the ablation procedure, one because of a late reporting of an event from 2006, and three because of gastrointestinal symptoms postablation, with no mention/proof of OI or fistula. Of the remaining patients, 47 (78.3%) exhibited OF and 13 (21.7%) OI (Figure 1).

A total of 16 (26.7%) cases were reported by the manufacturers after noticing them in the literature, but not directly by the responsible physicians. The reporter was a healthcare professional in 36/50 cases (72.0%). A considerably higher number of reports was noted in the later years (66.7% of events in the second half of the period) (Figure 2). One OF was reported following an AFL ablation (not specified if typical or atypical). One AF ablation was performed epicardially due to the presence of a venous malformation (inferior vena cava draining into a persistent left superior vena cava).

Year and geographical distribution of the reports. Year of reports (upper panel) and location of reports (lower panel) (grey = no reports, scale of blue: light blue = fewer reports, dark blue = more reports).

The country where the event occurred was available for 44 reports as follows: United States 25 (56.8%), Japan 7 (15.9%), Australia 5 (11.4%), France 4 (9.1%), United Kingdom 2 (4.5%), and Canada 1 (2.3%) (Figure 2).

A single (1.7%) device was sent back to the manufacturer for further investigations. Five (8.3%) reports identified different technical issues during procedures. In one RF-based procedure, the physician suspected that the energy generator delivered more power than intended. In a second RF procedure report, the temperature reading on the generator increased to 40–42°C with an irrigation setting of 20 mL/min. In this case, the catheter was removed, and the irrigation function was tested. Even though no flow issues were noted, the irrigation was considered hampered by visual inspection, and the catheter was replaced. The evaluation of the catheter by the manufacturer could not identify any issues with the product. In two CB-based intervention, the presence of air in the coronary arteries was noted by coronary angiography and a catheter air leak was suspected. In a third CB-based PVI, the system detected an electrical component failure with the catheter, but the report concluded that the adverse event was not associated with a device or use problem.

Among patients exhibiting OF or OI, 35 (58.3%) underwent RF-based PVI, 20 (33.3%) underwent CB-based PVI, and 5 (8.3%) a laser procedure (Figure 3). The minimal CB temperature was reported in five (25.0%) patients, with a mean value of −60.4 ± 8.9°C. In two patients, the CB temperature was lower than −60°C, with a minimum of −75°C. The minimum number of CB applications/patient was available for 10 (50.0%) procedures, with a median of 13.0 (9.0, 17.0) and a maximum of 30 applications per patient.

Presentation and symptoms

The mean time to symptoms onset/hospital presentation was 22.5 ± 12.8 days, with a maximum of 42 days. One patient with OF exhibited significant pain already during the procedure, while the next earliest onset of symptoms was 3 days postprocedural. There was a trend towards a longer time to symptoms onset or hospital presentation in the OF group as compared to the OI group (23.7 ± 12.5 days vs. 14.0 ± 14.0 days; P = 0.235) (Figure 4).

Time to symptoms onset and symptoms at presentation. Time to symptoms onset in each group (upper panel) and symptoms at presentation, stratified by type of oesophageal lesion (lower panel).

The initial symptoms were reported in 38 (63.3%) patients (63.8% in the OF group and 61.5% in the OI group). The most common form of presentation was with stroke or neurological deficit in 20 (52.6%) patients, followed by thorax pain in 16 (42.1%) patients and fever in 14 (36.8%) patients (Figure 4). No patient exhibiting OI showed stroke or fever. Five (13.2%) patients showed leucocytosis/elevated blood count at presentation.

Lesion type

A total of 47 (78.3%) patients exhibited OF, while 13 (21.7%) developed only OI without perforation.

Of patients treated by means of RF, 88.6% developed OF and 11.4% OI; of those undergoing CB-based ablation, 75.0% exhibited OF and 25.0% OI, while 20% of patients undergoing laser-based ablation developed OF and 80% of them OI (overall P = 0.002).

Of patients exhibiting OF, 66.0% were treated using RF energy, 31.9% using cryoenergy, and only 2.1% using laser energy (Figure 3). Among patients exhibiting OI, the proportions were 30.8, 38.5, and 30.8%, respectively.

Diagnosis method

The diagnosis method was reported in 22 (36.7%) patients. In 16 (72.7%) of them, diagnosis was achieved by computed tomography (CT; thorax CT was performed in 11 patients and cranial CT in 3), followed by endoscopy in 5 (22.7%) patients, autopsy in 4 (18.2%) patients, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in 3 (13.6%, 2 mentioned as cranial MRI) patients, echocardiography in 3 (13.6%) patients, exploratory surgery in 3 (13.6%) patients, and cardiac catheterization in 3 (13.6%) patients. Blood cultures were reported in two (9.1%) patients, while X-ray scans and oesophagograms in one (4.5%) patient each. One patient was diagnosed by means of telemedicine.

Among the five patients who underwent endoscopy for diagnostic, two were diagnosed with OF and three with OI. In three cases, CT imaging was also performed; however, the sequence of diagnostic procedures could not be determined. Notably, the two patients for whom endoscopy was the sole reported diagnostic modality had OI, and both survived.

Treatment

Details regarding treatment were available in 36 (60.0%) patients. A total of 14 (38.9%) patients received surgical treatment only, 11 (30.6%) isolated endoscopic interventions only (e.g. oesophageal stenting), while a combined treatment was performed in 1 (2.8%) patient. For 10 (27.8%) patients the treatment was reported to be conservative. When analysing only the cases with OF, the proportions were 55.6% (15/27) for surgical treatment, 37.0% (10/27) for endoscopic treatment only, and 7.4% (2/27) for conservative treatment, while the proportions for the OI patients were 0, 11.1, and 88.9%, respectively (overall P < 0.001) (Figure 5).

Survival

The mortality among patients developing these complications was 63.3%. The patients exhibiting OF had significantly higher mortality as compared to those exhibiting OI (76.6% vs. 15.4%; P < 0.001).

When stratifying by the treatment received, the mortality was 80.0% for patients receiving surgery, 72.7% for those receiving endoscopic treatment only, and 30.0% for those receiving conservative treatment (P = 0.030). However, when analysing only the patients with OF, the mortality was 80.0% for the surgery group, 80.0% for the endoscopic treatment only group, and 100% for the conservative group (P = 0.782) (Figure 5). The differences between surgical and interventional treatment as well as between conservative and non-conservative treatment among patients with OF were also not significant.

The mortality in patients receiving RF-based ablation was 65.7%, in those receiving CB-based ablations 70.0%, and in those undergoing laser ablation 20% (P = 0.105). When analysing only the patients exhibiting OF, the mortality was 71.0% among the RF patients, 86.7% among the CB patients, and 100% among those receiving laser (P = 0.427) (Figure 5).

Discussion

This is the first study to analyse the mandatory and voluntary MDRs sent to FDA and stored in the MAUDE database comprising patients exhibiting OF and OI after catheter-based AF ablation. It also represents, to the best of our knowledge, the second-largest report on OF after catheter-based AF ablation worldwide.

PVI is the cornerstone of interventional treatment for AF as part of a rhythm control strategy.1 Catheter-based ablation procedures are widely used globally and are considered safe and effective interventions.3–5,9,19,20

OF represents a dreadful complication of catheter-based AF ablation, with a mortality ranging between 56 and 83%.12,13,15,16 Along with stroke, it is considered the second most frequent cause of mortality following AF ablation.21 The epidemiology of this complication is challenging to describe, primarily due to its low incidence. In our study, the number of oesophageal lesions reported to FDA increased over time, from 1 case reported in 2010 to 18 cases reported in 2018. However, the exact incidence of these complications could not be determined. The annual rate of catheter-based electrophysiology procedures performed in the USA increased steadily between 2013 and 2019, rising from 323.73 to 675.01 per 100,000 beneficiaries.22 Moreover, in a French nationwide survey published by Gandjbakhch et al., despite the continuous increase of AF/atrial tachycardia ablations over time, the incidence of atrio-oesophageal fistula remained constant.13 Thus, the increase in the number of reported OF and OI cases in our study might reflect the higher volume of ablation procedures performed worldwide or a change in the reporting behaviour, rather than a true increase in incidence.

The recently published POTTER-AF study reported a significantly higher incidence of OF following RF-based procedures compared to cryoenergy procedures.16 Although we could not directly assess the incidence of these complications, the number of patients developing OF after RF-based procedures was higher than those receiving CB-based or laser balloon-based procedures. However, it is important to note that 31.9% of patients exhibiting OF in our study underwent CB-based ablation, compared to only 2.5% in the previously mentioned study. Moreover, the large French nationwide survey reported 33 atrio-oesophageal fistulas among 129 286 procedures, none of which occurred following CB-based procedures.13 Our results emphasize that although OF is primarily a complication associated with RF procedures, it can also affect a significant proportion of patients undergoing cryoenergy-based and even laser-based ablations. This study reports the largest population of OF following CB-based AF ablations to date.23 The potential for oesophageal lesions following cryoenergy ablation has also been confirmed in endoscopic studies. Sarairah et al. reported oesophageal thermal lesions in 22% of patients undergoing cryoenergy-based ablation, although most were mild.24

Interestingly, five patients in our study exhibited oesophageal lesions after laser-based PVI. Of these, one (20%) developed an OF, while the others presented with OI. In the worldwide POTTER-AF study, only one (0.8%) patient was reported to exhibit OF following laser PVI, while in a meta-analysis of 292 laser balloon procedures, only one case of OI was identified.16,25 To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest reported population of patients with oesophageal lesions following AF ablation using a laser balloon device. Notably, the use of an oesophageal temperature probe was reported in three (60%) patients in this group, and all exhibited a temperature rise above the established threshold.

The mean time to symptoms onset or hospital presentation was 22.5 ± 12.8 days in our study, consistent with previous reports.13,16 Notably, the earliest oesophageal lesion-related symptoms were observed during the ablation procedure itself, while the longest time to symptom onset was 42 days post-procedure. Patients who developed OF tended to have a longer time to symptom onset compared to those with only OI. This may be explained by the pathophysiology of OF, which typically progresses from oesophageal ulceration to later perforation.26 Another possibility is that all patients exhibiting OI have the potential to develop OF, but those who exhibit symptoms earlier are investigated and treated more aggressively, thus preventing OF formation. A previous study from Halbfass et al. including patients who underwent endoscopy post AF ablation showed that only those with initial oesophageal ulcers subsequently developed oesophageal perforation.27 Therefore, patients with OI who develop symptoms earlier in the course of their condition might receive more thorough examination and treatment, potentially preventing progression to OF, while those with later-onset symptoms are often investigated only after OF has already developed. It is important to note that the OI population is likely underestimated in this study, as this complication is often asymptomatic, unrecognized, or considered insufficiently severe for reporting. Consequently, reports to the FDA likely represent only the most severe, symptomatic cases with significant lesions or complicated clinical courses.

In this study, five patients underwent endoscopy for diagnostic evaluation. It is important to emphasize that endoscopy should be avoided when OF is suspected, as it carries a risk of air embolism with potentially fatal consequences.

In our study, the overall mortality was 63.3%, but significantly higher in the patients exhibiting OF as compared to those exhibiting OI only. Two patients with OI died. Of them, one died due to gastrointestinal bleeding, while the cause of death for the other was not reported. Since OI alone is not a common cause of death among patients undergoing AF ablation, it is likely that the two cases were misdiagnosed or misreported as OI instead of OF.27,28

In the POTTER-AF study, the mortality rate after OF was 65.8%, with significantly lower mortality in patients who underwent interventional or surgical treatment compared to those treated conservatively.16 A large metanalysis by Han et al. reported a mortality rate of 55%, while the French nationwide survey a mortality of 60% following atrio-oesophageal fistula.13,29 Both studies demonstrated that conservative treatment was associated with significantly higher mortality compared to interventional and surgical treatments.13,29 In our study, the mortality between the treatment modalities was not significantly different. However, it is important to note that both patients treated conservatively in our study died. The lack of statistical significance might be explained by the reduced number of patients with available treatment data.

The mortality rate was similar between patients treated with RF and those treated with cryoenergy who exhibited OF. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the survival between OF following RF and cryoenergy-based AF ablations. A previous study published in 2017 reported a 64% mortality rate among 11 patients who developed atrio-oesophageal fistula after CB-based PVI, including those listed in the MAUDE database at that time. However, a direct comparison with RF-based procedures was not conducted.23

The oesophageal temperature was monitored in 9 (25.7%) patients undergoing RF-based ablation, and in 3 of these cases, no temperature rise over the predefined cut-off or over 39°C was reported. This suggests that oesophageal temperature monitoring may not be effective in preventing OF. A meta-analysis from Koranne et al., including 411 patients with and without oesophageal temperature monitoring during RF-based AF ablation, found no difference in the rate of oesophageal thermal injury between the groups.30 Similarly, another meta-analysis published in 2019 did not find any difference in the incidence of endoscopically detected oesophageal lesions with or without the use of an oesophageal temperature probe.31 However, the results of individual studies were heterogeneous: two favoured temperature monitoring, three favoured no temperature monitoring, and one found no significant difference.31 We previously reported that the incidence of oesophageal injuries can be reduced by combining lower ablation power at the posterior wall with the termination of energy delivery when oesophageal temperature reaches 41°C.32 In our study, 33.3% of the patients undergoing RF ablation who had available oesophageal temperature monitoring data did not experience a temperature rise but still developed OF. This finding suggests that oesophageal temperature monitoring alone may not be effective in preventing OF.

However, innovations such as active oesophageal cooling may provide adequate temperature control and prevent oesophageal lesions33,34 A large study by Sanchez et al. included 14 224 patients undergoing RF ablation with active oesophageal cooling and 10 962 patients with luminal oesophageal temperature monitoring. No cases of OF were observed in the cooling group, whereas 16 cases occurred in the temperature monitoring group, corresponding to an incidence of 0.146% (P < 0.0001).34 Another study analysing the MAUDE database reported an OF incidence of 0.0001% with oesophageal cooling devices—markedly lower than rates observed without their use.35 These findings demonstrate that active oesophageal cooling markedly reduces the risk of OF and highlight this novel technology as a promising strategy to prevent this devastating complication.

Another important aspect is the use of non-thermal ablation, such as pulsed field ablation (PFA), which is considered selective for the myocardial tissue and has the potential to spare the oesophagus.36,37 It should be noted, though, that PFA has also been associated with a significant increase in oesophageal intraluminal temperature.38 However, the recently published Eso-PFA study, which included 106 patients undergoing left atrial posterior wall isolation using PFA with postprocedural esophagogastroduodenoscopy, reported no thermal, ablation-related oesophageal lesions and no long-term oesophageal adverse events.39 These findings underscore the favourable safety profile of PFA in preventing oesophageal lesions and question the continued reliance on thermal energy as the standard of care for AF ablation, particularly regarding oesophageal risk. Furthermore, the absence of reported OF cases after PFA ablation to date, despite the high procedural volume, provides additional compelling evidence of the safety of this novel technology.37

It is important to note that the MAUDE database is a mandatory reporting system for complications that may have caused or contributed to death or serious injury following the use of medical devices. The POTTER-AF 3 study, however, demonstrated substantial underreporting of complications in this mandatory FDA database compared with a voluntary, invitation-based registry. This finding raises concerns regarding the integrity of MAUDE data and its suitability as a reliable source for clinical research.40 Underreporting, incomplete data, and contributions from non-healthcare professionals pose significant limitations, potentially underestimating incidences, omitting key details, and allowing misinterpretation. Nevertheless, for rare complications such as OF, international reporting systems like MAUDE remain essential.

Limitations

The present study is a retrospective analysis of FDA reports and is subject to the limitations inherent in such studies. The variability between the reports is considerable, as they are submitted independently. Additionally, many reports were not written by healthcare professionals, which may affect both the quality and consistency of the data provided. The classification of cases into OI and OF was based on the information provided in the reports. Double reporting is possible, as some events were identified through literature searches. Information on the baseline characteristics of the patients is limited. Additionally, the exact number of ablations performed during the specified time interval could not be determined, making it impossible to calculate the incidence of these complications. The reported symptoms and the time to symptoms are not exclusively the initial symptoms but also symptoms reported at hospital presentation. The type of ablation lesions, which may represent an important determinant of OF risk, was not consistently reported in the MAUDE database, thereby precluding inclusion of this parameter in the analysis. Treatment details were not provided for all patients, which may lead to an overestimation of the rate of surgical and interventional treatments. Furthermore, conservative treatment was defined as cases where only medical treatment was mentioned, without reference to interventional or surgical interventions, which may not accurately reflect the actual treatment given. Survival time data were not available, nor were data on the sequelae in patients who survived.

Conclusions

Almost one-third of the patients developing OF underwent CB-based ablation. More than three-fourths of the patients died, with no significant difference between the ablation energy used. All patients with OF treated conservatively died. OF may occur despite oesophageal temperature monitoring.
