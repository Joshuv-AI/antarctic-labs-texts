Fenbendazole-Associated Drug-Induced Liver Injury with Concomitant Use of Tauroursodeoxycholic Acid
Authors: Alexander Satinsky, Nancy Mayer, Charles Swanson, Brian Sasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Influenced by the internet and social media, a growing number of individuals are taking hepatotoxic amounts of prescription and nonprescription drugs leading to drug-induced liver injuries (DILIs). We present a case of fenbendazole-associated DILI with concomitant use of tauroursodeoxycholic acid as an intended hepatoprotective agent. While fenbendazole-associated DILI has been increasingly reported, this case is unique due to associated tauroursodeoxycholic acid use, and, to our knowledge, represents the first published report of this exposure combination. This case builds on the growing literature of DILI due to nonprescription drugs and highlights the importance of patient-centered counseling.

INTRODUCTION

Misinformation in health care continues to expand with increasing influence of internet and social media sources. While fenbendazole (FBZ), an anthelmintic veterinary medication, has been suggested in preclinical studies to have anticancer properties, internet sources promote unverified protocols for off-label use in cancer and parasitic infections. These protocols also recommend tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) for “liver and bile support”.1 Both FBZ and TUDCA have demonstrated promise in preclinical models; however, clinical evidence in humans remains limited.2 The post-COVID trend toward self-directed therapy based on online information represents a growing public health concern, particularly due to risk of adverse outcomes such as drug-induced liver injury (DILI).3,4 We present the first case of FBZ-associated DILI with concomitant use of TUDCA as an intended hepatoprotective agent.

CASE REPORT

A 55-year-old previously healthy woman presented with 1 week of nausea, malaise, and dark urine without jaundice. Her vital signs and physical examination were unremarkable without abdominal tenderness, hepatomegaly, or rash. She was found to have abnormal liver chemistries including alanine aminotransferase >3,300 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 1,854 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 255 U/L, total bilirubin 1.7 mg/dL, and international normalized ratio 1.11. She reported taking FBZ 222 mg daily with TUDCA 500 mg daily for hepatoprotection, for 4 weeks with her last dose 3 days before presentation. Her reasoning was to undergo a “parasite cleanse” identified online, as her living conditions on a farm with chickens made her concerned of an occult parasitic infection, despite no prior symptoms. She denied alcohol use, other medications or supplements, and personal or family history of liver disease. Poison control recommended N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and close inpatient monitoring. Abdominal computed tomography with contrast and right upper quadrant ultrasound with Doppler were unremarkable for liver, gallbladder, biliary, or vascular abnormalities. Serum analysis included normal acetaminophen levels, viral serologies, ceruloplasmin, anti-nuclear antibody, alpha-1-antitrypsin, anti-mitochondrial antibody, and smooth muscle antibody. Her iron studies were elevated (ferritin 975 ng/mL and transferrin saturation 88%); however, HFE gene testing was negative. Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) score was 9, indicating FBZ as a highly probable cause of DILI. After improvement in liver biochemistry (Table 1 and Figure 1), the patient was safely discharged after completing 72 hours of NAC. She was counseled on safe healthcare practices and referred to hepatology. Her laboratory values normalized nearly 4 weeks after her hospital admission (Table 1 and Figure 1).

DISCUSSION

Fenbendazole has gained attention due to antineoplastic properties demonstrated in preclinical studies. In animal and cell models, FBZ has been shown to be an inhibitor of glycolysis, increase p53 expression, and promote microtubule destabilization; however, there are no clinical trials for FBZ. Case reports describe patients self-administering FBZ for malignancy treatment with mixed outcomes and adverse effects, including hepatoxicity.5 Off-label use of FBZ has gained traction on popular social media platforms, where it has primarily influenced those affected by cancer and has been identified as a DILI culprit.4,6–9 Websites provide dosing regimens for cancer treatment and prophylaxis, and parasite treatment, often recommend concomitant hepatoprotective supplements, such as TUDCA, and offer interactive question and answer forums.10 With our patient using FBZ off-label for parasitic purposes and TUDCA for hepatoprotection, our case adds to the growing literature of concerns regarding unsubstantiated safety and efficacy claims of drugs and supplements supported throughout the internet.11

DILI is commonly categorized as intrinsic or idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity. Intrinsic injury is dose-dependent and predictable, exemplified by acetaminophen toxicity, whereas idiosyncratic injury demonstrates variable latency periods ranging from days to years. Liver injury patterns include, but are not limited to hepatocellular, cholestatic, fatty liver, vascular, neoplastic, and chronic fibrosing phenotypes.10,12 All reported cases of FBZ hepatotoxicity demonstrate a hepatocellular injury, consistent with this patient's R-value of ≥5. Typical latency periods of FBZ-associated DILI have been reported between 4 and 6 weeks, like our patient, but has been seen as early as 9 days.4,6–8 Histologic findings in prior reports include centrilobular necrosis, portal lymphocytic infiltrate, and acidophil bodies, compatible with hepatocellular injury.4,6–9 In vitro studies suggest structurally similar benzimidazoles, including FBZ, induced proinflammatory cytokines IL-8 and TNF-alpha, providing potential mechanistic insight.13

Clinical presentations of FBZ-associated DILI have been variable, generally including fatigue and jaundice, with no reports of acute liver failure or death.4,6–9 To our knowledge, concomitant TUDCA use has not been previously described. TUDCA is a conjugated bile acid proposed to exert hepatoprotection by reducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and modulating apoptosis pathways. While studied in cholestatic liver disease and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, evidence supporting clinical benefit remains limited, particularly in hepatocellular injury.2,14 Despite concomitant TUDCA use, this patient developed significant hepatocellular injury, highlighting uncertainty regarding its protective role.

Evaluation of suspected DILI includes calculation of the R-value to determine injury pattern and use of causality assessment tools such as RUCAM.10,12 Investigating for underlying chronic liver disease is also helpful for DILI prognostication.15 While hereditary hemochromatosis does not present as an acute liver injury, it can become clinically apparent in women after menopause due to physiologic iron losses in reproductive years.16 This patient's HFE testing was negative; therefore, her abnormal iron studies likely represented acute phase reactants.17 Liver biopsy is not routinely required but may be considered when autoimmune hepatitis is suspected, liver tests fail to improve, liver failure develops, or the implicated medication is clinically necessary.17

Management of DILI consists of prompt discontinuation of the offending agent and supportive care. NAC is a standard therapy for acetaminophen toxicity and may be considered in early acute liver failure unrelated to acetaminophen exposure in adults.10 In this case, NAC was administered per poison control recommendation despite absence of acute liver failure.

The rapid dissemination of online medical information has increased patient-driven use of alternative therapies without physician guidance, particularly in the post-COVID era.18 With more than 100,000 herbal and dietary supplements available, clinicians must remain vigilant in recognizing emerging self-medication trends and use resources such as LiverTox to identify hepatotoxic agents.10 A thorough medication history and nonjudgemental, patient-centered counseling are essential to promote trust and reduce harm associated with unverified therapies.
