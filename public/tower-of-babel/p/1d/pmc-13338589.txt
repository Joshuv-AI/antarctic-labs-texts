Synergistic effects of lipopolysaccharide and titanium ions on monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cell expansion in gingival tissue: implications for peri-implant bone health
Authors: Eun-Young Jang, Seok Bin Yang, Jung-Mi Lee, Kyu Hwan Kwack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTitanium particles and ions detected in peri-implant tissues have been implicated in crestal bone loss around dental implants. However, their precise effects on adjacent peri-implant structures remain insufficiently understood. This study investigates the impact of foreign body reactions induced by titanium ions on peri-implant bone levels, with a specific focus on monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs), which have been reported to possess osteoclast precursor potential.MethodsBone marrow, spleen, and gingival cells were exposed to titanium ions and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to evaluate M-MDSC differentiation using flow cytometry. In addition, an in vivo mouse model with titanium implants was employed to assess M-MDSC proliferation, phenotypic characteristics, and associated peri-implant bone changes.ResultsTitanium ions enhanced the differentiation of M-MDSCs and macrophages from bone marrow–derived precursors. In vitro experiments demonstrated that titanium ions and LPS synergistically increased M-MDSC populations. In vivo, mice with ligature-induced peri-implantitis surrounding titanium implants exhibited a significant increase in M-MDSCs within peri-implant tissues. Notably, these M-MDSCs showed upregulated expression of the osteoclastogenic receptor c-Fms (CD115), indicating a primed state for osteoclast differentiation. This cellular shift was accompanied by a corresponding reduction in peri-implant bone mineral density and bone volume when compared with mice bearing implants alone.ConclusionsTitanium ions and inflammatory stimuli synergistically promote not only the expansion of M-MDSCs but also their osteoclastogenic priming. These findings suggest that primed M-MDSCs may function as key cellular mediators in the pathogenesis of peri-implantitis. Furthermore, they highlight M-MDSCs as a potential therapeutic target for improving implant longevity and underscore the importance of controlling peri-implant inflammation.Keywords: Dental implants, Immunomodulation, Lipopolysaccharides, Myeloid-derived suppressor cells, Osteoclasts, Peri-implantitis, Titanium

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants have emerged as a revolutionary solution in restorative dentistry, offering a reliable and durable option for replacing missing teeth. Despite their high success rates, implants are significantly challenged by peri-implantitis, an inflammatory condition. It primarily manifests as peri-implant soft tissue inflammation and progressive bone resorption, potentially compromising surrounding structures and leading to implant failure [1,2]. The progression and clinical presentation of peri-implantitis resemble those of periodontitis, and it is therefore classified as an inflammatory disease driven largely by bacterial infection [3]. However, in comparison with periodontitis, peri-implantitis is characterized by more extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and more rapid and severe alveolar bone destruction [4]. Consequently, the pathological processes underlying peri-implantitis cannot be fully explained by bacterial factors alone.

Due to their long fatigue life, corrosion resistance, and excellent biocompatibility, titanium and its alloys are currently the most widely used materials for dental implants [5]. Nevertheless, interactions between the implant surface and surrounding bone, mechanical wear induced by biomechanical loading, and tribocorrosion effects inevitably lead to the release of titanium particles into adjacent tissues [6]. These particles act as foreign bodies to immune cells, triggering diverse immune responses and promoting the differentiation and migration of monocytes associated with bone resorption [7,8]. Therefore, elucidating how titanium particles influence the host immune system, including oral mucosal immunity, is critically important. Such knowledge may facilitate the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing implant-related diseases and reducing the risk of implant failure.

Myeloid cell subsets play essential roles in both immune regulation and bone homeostasis. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which are well recognized for their immunosuppressive functions, represent a heterogeneous population derived from the myeloid lineage that expands under chronic inflammatory conditions [9,10]. MDSCs are broadly classified into 2 major subsets: polymorphonuclear (PMN)-MDSCs and monocytic (M)-MDSCs. In mice, these subsets are phenotypically defined using CD11b, Ly6C, and Ly6G, with PMN-MDSCs identified as CD11b+Ly6ClowLy6G+ cells and M-MDSCs as CD11b+Ly6ChiLy6G− cells [11,12]. Importantly, the gold standard for MDSC identification requires not only phenotypic characterization but also functional validation of their defining capacity to suppress T cell proliferation [11]. Several studies have demonstrated the plasticity of M-MDSCs, showing that they can serve as osteoclast precursors in pathological conditions associated with bone destruction, including osteoarthritis, aging, and periodontal disease [10,13,14]. This differentiation process is critically dependent on signaling through key receptors, such as the macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) receptor c-Fms (CD115) [15]. A better understanding of M-MDSC dynamics within peri-implant tissues may therefore provide important insights into the mechanisms driving implant failure and reveal novel therapeutic targets to enhance implant longevity. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the role of M-MDSCs in the peri-implant bone microenvironment, with particular emphasis on their interactions with titanium particles.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Isolation of tissue cells

Bone marrow cells were obtained by flushing the femur, and splenocytes were obtained by mincing the spleen in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). To obtain gingival cells, the gingival tissue surrounding the maxillary molars (or implant sites) was carefully excised under a stereo microscope. Great care was taken to harvest only the soft tissue collar while excluding any alveolar bone fragments or tooth structures to ensure a pure gingival cell population. The collected tissues were then minced and incubated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and treated with collagenase type II (2 mg/mL; Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ, USA) and DNase I (1 mg/mL; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in a shaker incubator for 60 minutes at 37°C and 200 rpm. Erythrocytes were lysed by treating the cells with ACK lysis buffer (Gibco, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The resulting cell suspension was passed through a 70-µm cell strainer (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. To mimic oral infection, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from Porphyromonas gingivalis (100 ng/mL, InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) was used. For LPS pre-treatment, cells were exposed to LPS-containing medium for 2 days, followed by replacement with LPS-free medium. For LPS post-treatment, LPS was added to the culture medium during the final 2 days.

Preparation of titanium ions

A commercially available titanium plasma standard solution (1,000 mg/L in 2%–5% nitric acid, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was used as the titanium ion source to ensure purity and consistency. Working solutions were prepared by diluting this standard solution into cell culture medium to achieve final titanium ion concentrations of 1, 2, 5, and 10 ppm. To neutralize acidity originating from the nitric acid solvent and to prevent pH-induced cytotoxicity, the pH of the culture medium was carefully monitored and adjusted to 7.4 using NaOH, following established protocols [16]. These concentrations were selected to replicate the pathological microenvironment of peri-implantitis, as clinical studies have reported titanium levels in inflamed peri-implant tissues ranging from approximately 1 to 45 ppm [17,18,19].

Cell viability assay

Prior to investigating immune modulation, a cell viability assay was performed to determine the non-cytotoxic range of titanium ion concentrations for this study. Previous in vitro studies have demonstrated that titanium ion concentrations up to 9–10 ppm do not induce significant cytotoxicity in osteoblasts or macrophages [16,20]. To confirm these findings under our experimental conditions, bone marrow cells (1×105 cells/well) were seeded into 12-well plates and exposed to titanium ion concentrations of 0, 1, 2, 5, and 10 ppm. Cell viability was assessed on days 1, 4, and 7 after exposure. Harvested cells were washed with Dulbecco’s PBS and stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (ab14085, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The proportions of live and dead cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Verse, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and the resulting data were processed using FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

Flow cytometry and M-MDSC characterization

Single-cell suspensions derived from bone marrow, spleen, and gingival tissues were prepared and pre-treated with anti-CD16/32 Fc block (BD). Cells were then stained with anti-CD11b APC (M1/70.15.11.5), anti-Ly6G PerCP-Vio700 (REA 526), anti-Ly6C FITC (REA 796), and anti-CD115 PE (AFS98). All antibodies were purchased from Miltenyi Biotec (Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). M-MDSCs were identified by flow cytometry as CD11b+Ly6ChiLy6G− cells.

T cell proliferation assay

Because gold-standard characterization of M-MDSCs requires confirmation of their hallmark immunosuppressive function in addition to surface phenotyping, a co-culture assay was performed to functionally validate that the phenotypically identified CD11b+Ly6ChiLy6G− population represented bona fide M-MDSCs. M-MDSCs were isolated from bone marrow cultures using a Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Separately, total splenocytes were isolated from the spleens of naïve C57BL/6 mice and labeled with CellTrace Violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to enable tracking of cell division. Labeled splenocytes (2×105 cells/well) were co-cultured with isolated M-MDSCs at a 1:2 ratio of M-MDSCs to splenocytes. T cell proliferation was induced by stimulation with soluble anti-CD3 (3 µg/mL) and anti-CD28 (5 µg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) antibodies. After 3 days of incubation, cells were harvested and stained with anti-CD4-PE-Cy7 (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) and anti-CD8-APC-Vio770 (Miltenyi Biotec). Proliferation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was quantified based on dilution of the CellTrace Violet signal using flow cytometry (BD Verse, BD Biosciences). All data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.10; FlowJo, LLC).

Mice

All animal studies and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyung Hee University Hospital (approval number: KHMC-IACUC-2023-033) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Nara Biotech (Seoul, Korea) at 3 weeks of age and maintained under controlled temperature and environmental conditions with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. To induce inflammation, ligation of the maxillary second molar was performed using 6-0 silk, as described in a previous study [10].

Titanium implants

The custom-made titanium implants (Dentium Co., Seoul, Korea) were fabricated from commercially pure Grade 4 titanium, which is widely used as a bulk material in clinical implant applications. Importantly, no additional surface treatment was applied in order to maximize titanium ion release and to model a “worst-case” peri-implant environment. The implant length was 1.3 mm and the implant diameter was 1.0 mm.

Extractions and implantation

To simulate clinical conditions of implant placement, the maxillary first molar was extracted under general anesthesia. An 8-week healing period was allowed to ensure adequate wound closure and bone healing at the extraction site. Following this healing period, a 0.6 mm hole was drilled for implant placement using custom-made tools, with the implant fixture positioned slightly anteriorly to minimize mandibular interference. Four weeks were allowed for osseointegration following implantation. Mice were then randomly assigned to 2 groups using a simple randomization method: the control implant group (Imp) and the ligation-induced peri-implantitis group (Lig + Imp). In the Lig + Imp group, peri-implant inflammation was induced by placing a 6-0 silk ligature around the neck of the implant. One week after ligature placement, corresponding to a total of 5 weeks post-implantation, all mice were sacrificed to collect gingival tissues for flow cytometric analysis of M-MDSCs. The maxillae were dissected, fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin, washed, and stored in 70% ethanol at 4°C for subsequent micro-computed tomography (µCT) analysis. Assessments by flow cytometry and µCT were performed by examiners blinded to experimental group allocation to minimize bias.

µCT

Before scanning, samples were allowed to equilibrate to room temperature. Changes in bone structure were assessed using a Scanco Medical µCT 100 scanner (Scanco Medical, Brüttisellen, Switzerland). The scanner was operated at 70 kVP and 114 µA, with a field of view of 15.2 mm, 500 projections over 180°, an integration time of 500 ms, and a voxel size of 12 µm. The region of interest was defined as the 120 µm of bone surrounding the implant, and measurements of bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %) were performed using AnalyzePro software (AnalyzeDirect, Inc., Overland Park, KS, USA).

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes (n=3–4 per group) were determined based on our previous studies [10,13], which demonstrated sufficient power to detect significant differences in immune cell populations and bone-related parameters. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. For normally distributed data, statistical significance was evaluated using the unpaired Student’s t-test for 2-group comparisons or 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Homogeneity of variance was verified using the Brown-Forsythe test. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.4 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition to P values, effect sizes were calculated for key comparisons using Cohen’s d for the t-test and partial η2 for ANOVA to quantify the magnitude of observed effects.

RESULTS

Titanium ions enhance the differentiation of M-MDSCs in vitro

It has been reported that titanium ions are released from titanium implants [17], and these ions have the potential to affect multiple immune cell populations [21]. Therefore, we investigated whether titanium ions could induce the differentiation of M-MDSCs from bone marrow cells, which serve as direct precursors of oral gingival M-MDSCs. In addition, we examined whether titanium ions also influence macrophages, which differentiate from the same precursor population and possess the potential to become osteoclasts. Initially, cytotoxicity was assessed by exposing cultured bone marrow cells to increasing concentrations of titanium ions. No significant difference in cell viability was observed compared with the control at concentrations up to 5 ppm; however, at 10 ppm, a significant reduction in viability was detected (Supplementary Figure 1). Next, we evaluated the differentiation of both M-MDSCs and macrophages by treating bone marrow cells with titanium ions at non-cytotoxic concentrations (the gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Figure 2). Across cultures maintained for 1, 4, and 7 days, increasing concentrations of titanium ions were associated with expansion of both the M-MDSC and macrophage populations (Figure 1A and B). These findings indicate that titanium ions enhance the differentiation of these cell populations from bone marrow–derived precursors. To confirm that the gated population represented bona fide M-MDSCs, a T cell proliferation assay was performed, demonstrating suppression of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell proliferation and thereby confirming the immunosuppressive function of these cells (Figure 1C). In splenocytes, similar to bone marrow cells, exposure to titanium ions for 4 days resulted in increased M-MDSC and macrophage populations (Figure 1D).

LPS exposure modulates M-MDSC differentiation dynamics

To examine changes in M-MDSCs under inflammatory conditions resembling periodontitis, we performed an in vitro analysis of M-MDSC responses following exposure of bone marrow and spleen cells to LPS. Consistent with previous reports [22], exposure to LPS resulted in expansion of the M-MDSC population in both bone marrow and spleen cells (Figure 2). However, when LPS was removed after a 2-day exposure (Pre-LPS), the expanded M-MDSC population partially reverted, although its frequency remained elevated compared with the control condition (Figure 2C and D).

Synergistic acceleration of M-MDSC expansion by titanium ions and LPS

This study next examined the effects of titanium ions on bone marrow and spleen cells under inflammatory conditions. In bone marrow cultures, M-MDSC frequencies were significantly increased when titanium ions were added to LPS-containing medium compared with cultures treated with titanium ions alone (Figure 3A). In contrast, when cells were exposed to titanium ions after the removal of pre-existing LPS, only a modest increase in the M-MDSC population was observed relative to titanium ion treatment alone (Figure 3A). Because M-MDSC levels remained elevated compared with controls following LPS pre-treatment, these findings suggest that the effect of titanium ions is limited once LPS has been removed. However, when cells were exposed to titanium ions followed by subsequent LPS treatment (Post-LPS), the M-MDSC population increased to a level comparable to that observed with simultaneous exposure from the outset. A similar pattern of M-MDSC modulation was observed in splenocytes (Figure 3B). Collectively, these results indicate that removal of LPS prior to titanium ion exposure mitigates M-MDSC expansion, whereas LPS exposure following or concurrent with titanium ion treatment promotes marked increases in the M-MDSC population.

Gingival M-MDSCs expand and upregulate the osteoclastogenic receptor CD115 in response to in vitro inflammatory stimuli

Consistent with findings in bone marrow and spleen cells, M-MDSCs in cultured gingival tissue increased following LPS exposure and decreased after LPS removal (Figure 4A and C). Moreover, the expansion of gingival M-MDSCs was particularly pronounced when LPS and titanium ions were present simultaneously (Figure 4D). To determine whether these inflammatory conditions also affected the osteoclastogenic potential of M-MDSCs, we analyzed expression of the M-CSF receptor c-Fms (CD115). M-MDSCs cultured in the presence of both LPS and titanium ions exhibited a significantly higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD115 compared with cells cultured with titanium ions alone (Figure 4E). This upregulation of CD115 suggests that an in vitro inflammatory microenvironment mimicking peri-implantitis not only promotes expansion of the M-MDSC population but also enhances their responsiveness to osteoclastogenic signaling, thereby priming them for differentiation.

M-MDSC proliferation and priming for osteoclastogenesis correlate with bone destruction in an experimental implant model

To investigate the in vivo relevance of these findings, we established a murine peri-implantitis model by placing titanium implants followed by ligature-induced inflammation (Figure 5A and B). Consistent with the in vitro results, the frequency of M-MDSCs in gingival tissue was significantly higher in mice with Lig + Imp group than in mice with implants alone (Imp) (Figure 5C). To determine whether these expanded M-MDSCs were primed for osteoclast differentiation, expression of c-Fms (CD115) was assessed. M-MDSCs from the Lig + Imp group exhibited significantly higher CD115 MFI compared with the Imp group, indicating enhanced responsiveness to osteoclastogenic cues (Figure 5D). This expansion of a primed M-MDSC population was associated with bone destruction, as µCT analysis revealed significant reductions in both BMD (Cohen’s d=2.92) and BV/TV (Cohen’s d=3.24) around the implant site in the Lig + Imp group (Figure 5E-G). Together, these in vivo data indicate that peri-implant inflammation is associated with expansion of M-MDSCs exhibiting osteoclastogenic potential, which may contribute to peri-implant bone loss.

DISCUSSION

Titanium is one of the most widely used materials for dental and medical implants because of its excellent mechanical properties and high biocompatibility, which contribute to favorable long-term survival rates [23]. However, titanium implants are not without risk, as they can fail because of complications such as aseptic loosening or infection [24,25]. Over time, titanium particles and ions may be released during implant placement or as a result of long-term wear, accumulate in surrounding tissues, and be recognized as foreign bodies by the host immune system. This recognition can initiate chronic inflammatory responses that ultimately lead to peri-implantitis and bone destruction [26]. Notably, the titanium ion concentrations used in our in vitro experiments (1–10 ppm) fall within the range reported in inflamed peri-implant tissues in clinical studies, thereby supporting the physiological relevance of our experimental model.

A key player in bone resorption is the mature osteoclast, the only cell type in mammals capable of degrading bone tissue. Our study observed that exposure to titanium ions increased the prevalence of M-MDSCs, which are established osteoclast precursors, in both bone marrow and gingival tissues. Crucially, our data revealed that M-MDSCs in the inflammatory peri-implant environment upregulated the M-CSF receptor, c-Fms (CD115), a key regulator of osteoclast survival and differentiation. This suggests that titanium exposure may trigger an inflammatory cascade that promotes M-MDSC proliferation. The concurrent presence of LPS, a bacterial endotoxin, was found to further accelerate this expansion and priming. Given that these primed M-MDSCs have the potential to differentiate into osteoclasts, their increased and activated presence may contribute to peri-implant bone resorption and could compromise osseointegration.

The mechanisms underlying these observations may involve activation of multiple immune cell populations by titanium ions, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines through processes such as inflammasome activation [21,27,28,29]. These mediators collectively establish a microenvironment conducive to M-MDSC expansion. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that titanium particles activate macrophages and promote osteoclastogenesis through pathways including RANKL signaling [17,20]. Our results further suggest that titanium exposure enhances differentiation of bone marrow precursors toward the monocytic lineage, encompassing both macrophages and M-MDSCs. The synergistic effect observed between titanium ions and LPS likely reflects their engagement of distinct yet complementary signaling pathways, with titanium ions primarily eliciting a foreign body response and LPS activating potent TLR4-mediated inflammatory cascades [30]. In our analyses, “synergism” was operationally defined as a significant interaction between titanium ions and LPS in 2-way ANOVA, whereby the combined treatment produced a greater increase in M-MDSC expansion than would be expected from the sum of their individual main effects. Clinically, the “Pre-LPS” condition may model implant placement into a site with pre-existing inflammation, such as residual periodontitis, whereas the “Post-LPS” condition may reflect the development of peri-implant mucositis or peri-implantitis around an already osseointegrated implant. This convergence results in heightened immune activation and a robust expansion of M-MDSCs with an osteoclast precursor-like phenotype, thereby creating a microenvironment favorable for bone destruction.

These findings have important implications for dental implantology. Accumulation of M-MDSCs may not only reflect an active inflammatory state but could also serve as a cellular biomarker for predicting peri-implantitis progression. Moreover, delineating the role of M-MDSCs offers a potential avenue for immunomodulatory intervention. Targeting pathways that regulate M-MDSC expansion or their differentiation toward osteoclasts may represent a novel strategy for improving long-term implant success. Collectively, our data demonstrate that titanium ions and LPS expand a monocytic myeloid population with an osteoclast precursor–like phenotype, characterized by increased CD115 expression, and that this expansion is associated with reduced peri-implant BMD and bone volume in vivo. By contrast, the direct differentiation of these M-MDSCs into mature, bone-resorbing osteoclasts and their causal contribution to peri-implant bone loss remain inferred from prior studies, as we did not directly perform TRAP staining, resorption pit assays, or in vivo depletion or functional blockade of M-MDSCs in the present work.

However, this study has several limitations. First, although CD115 upregulation on M-MDSCs provides strong evidence of osteoclastogenic priming, we did not perform direct in vitro osteoclast differentiation assays to confirm their functional capacity to generate mature, bone-resorbing osteoclasts. Second, our in vivo model employed custom-made titanium implants without surface treatment to maximize titanium ion release, which may not fully reflect the clinical use of modern sandblasted, large-grit, acid-etched (SLA), SLActive, or other surface-modified implants and could therefore overestimate peri-implant titanium exposure. Future studies should directly compare Grade 4 implants with clinically relevant surface-treated implants within the same ligature-induced peri-implantitis model and quantitatively assess peri-implant titanium accumulation, including both ions and particles, to enhance translational relevance. In addition, some of our proposed preventive implications are derived from in vitro associations and require further validation in preclinical in vivo models. This study also relied on a single LPS species, which simplifies the complex polymicrobial environment characteristic of clinical peri-implantitis. Finally, although gingival organ cultures provide valuable ex vivo insight, they do not recapitulate the intact vascular and neural components present in vivo. Future investigations should therefore address these limitations to more comprehensively define the role of M-MDSCs in peri-implantitis progression.

In conclusion, this study provides mechanistic insight into the role of M-MDSCs in mediating inflammatory responses to titanium implants. Our findings demonstrate that titanium ions and bacterial components synergistically promote expansion of a myeloid population with an osteoclast precursor phenotype. These results highlight the potential utility of M-MDSCs as biomarkers of disease progression and as targets for therapeutic intervention. Ultimately, strategies aimed at mitigating inflammatory responses and modulating M-MDSC activity may be critical for improving implant longevity and patient outcomes.
