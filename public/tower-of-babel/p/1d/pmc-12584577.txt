ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInvasive coronary physiology including fractional flow reserve (FFR), instantaneous wave‐free ratio (iFR), and coronary flow reserve (CFR) are guideline‐endorsed tools to guide the management of coronary artery disease (CAD). Complex factors impact and confound these assessments, and discordance between modalities complicates clinical management. iEquate is a prospective observational trial that combines multi‐modality coronary physiology and optical coherence tomography (OCT) to identify the determinants of pressure‐wire derived myocardial ischemia and iFR‐FFR discordance.MethodsAll patients referred for invasive angiography who met the pre‐selection criteria were screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients underwent physiological assessment using iFR and FFR, and CFR was performed as indicated. OCT was performed on all suitable lesions. Data were collected on baseline demographics, angiographic results, OCT findings, coronary physiology, and procedural details. Multivariate analysis was performed.ResultsA total of 124 vessels were analyzed from 107 patients. The mean age was 67.6 ( ± 10.8) years, and 23 patients (21.5%) were female. Eighty (74.8%) presented with stable angina, and the most common vessel assessed was the left anterior descending artery (n = 84, 67.7% of patients). The incidence of iFR‐FFR discordance was 20.7%, with 48% of these iFR+FFR−. Calcific plaque was associated with discordance (OR 6.15 [95% CI [1.05−36.13]; p = 0.04), largely with a iFR+FFR− pattern (OR 7.62 [95% CI 0.38−152.15]; p = 0.184). Other morphological features were not predictive of FFR, iFR, or CFR findings. Mean CFR was significantly lower in the iFR+FFR− subgroup compared to FFR+iFR− (mean difference 1.4; p < 0.01).ConclusionsiFR‐FFR discordance is common, and impaired vasodilatory capacity (assessed using CFR) may account for some of these cases. In this study, calcific plaque was associated with discordance, while other OCT‐derived plaque morphological characteristics were not associated with invasive coronary physiology findings. Cross‐sectional plaque eccentricity is associated with lower FFR values.Keywords: coronary physiology, discordance, FFR, iFR, OCT, plaque morphology

Introduction

For patients with stable angina and moderate coronary disease, invasive coronary physiology, otherwise known as pressure wire assessment, has become the standard of care to determine whether percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) should be performed in addition to optimal medical therapy (OMT), or OMT alone [1, 2]. There are several coronary physiological modalities in clinical use, including resting indices such as instantaneous wave‐free ratio (iFR) [3, 4], or hyperemic indices such as fractional flow reserve (FFR) [5, 6]. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) and index of microvascular resistance (IMR) are also invasive assessments used to assess vasodilatory capacity and microvascular dysfunction, respectively [7]. iFR and FFR have well‐defined thresholds based on large‐scale studies to determine whether PCI should be performed or deferred [1, 2].

Central Illustration

Within the coronary tree, multiple factors influence the pressure wire result depending on the modality used. This includes the target vessel and myocardial supply area [8, 9], the severity of the stenosis [10], the length of the stenosis [11], the pattern of the coronary disease [12], and the vasodilatory response [13]. These factors may also impact one modality more than the other, resulting in a phenomenon known as iFR‐FFR discordance [13], which may complicate clinical management.

There has been some research into the impact of plaque morphology on coronary haemodynamics, yielding mixed results [14, 15, 16, 17]. The consensus from the available literature is that plaque morphology and subsequent disturbance to coronary flow reflect overall plaque burden rather than the histological subtype. Most of these studies have examined the link between FFR and plaque morphology, with limited literature on the impact of non‐hyperemic indices such as iFR. Furthermore, there have been studies investigating the impact of plaque geometric features on invasive coronary physiology; however, this has been limited to intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) based studies so far [18].

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to incorporate optical coherence tomography (OCT) assessment of plaque morphological and geometric features, with the spectrum of invasive coronary physiology, including iFR, FFR, and CFR, to assess the impact of plaque morphology and geometry on coronary haemodynamics.

Methods

Patient Population

This was a prospective study undertaken between July 2021 and March 2023 at two tertiary hospitals in Sydney, Australia. Before undergoing an indicated invasive coronary angiogram, patients were approached for written consent for participation in the research protocol. The protocol was conducted after invasive coronary angiography if the inclusion and exclusion criteria were met.

Physiology Procedure

Patients who were subsequently identified to have moderate (50%−75%) stenosis angiographically undergo invasive hemodynamic assessment using a 0.014” coronary pressure wire. As per standard protocol, intra‐coronary nitrate (glyceryl trinitrate 50−200 mcg) was administered before advancing the coronary wire. After zeroing the pressure wire to the aortic pressure at the ostium of the artery, the coronary pressure wire was advanced as distally as possible, after which resting pressure gradients were recorded. If the pressure wire modality did not record an iFR (e.g., Combowire or Coroventis pressure wire), raw hemodynamic pressure data was captured every 0.005 s and sent to a Core Lab to provide a validated iFR value (Figure 1) [19]. Following this, adenosine was administered either as an intracoronary bolus (240−480 mcg) or as an intravenous infusion to stimulate hyperemia, after which FFR was recorded (Figure 2). CFR analysis was not mandated in this protocol. If recorded, this was simultaneous with the FFR and used either Doppler‐derived (Figure 2) or thermodilution‐derived (Figure 3) technology, and intravenous adenosine was used for hyperemia. Following physiological recordings, a pullback was performed at the end of the physiology procedure to confirm the lack of drift, in which case the measurement would be recorded again. No drift was accepted. An iFR of ≤ 0.89 was considered positive, whereas an FFR of ≤ 0.8 was considered positive based on previously defined thresholds for ischemia [3, 6]. Discordance was defined as a difference in ischemic classification between FFR and iFR, and was categorized either as an iFR positive/FFR negative (iFR+FFR−) pattern or an FFR positive/iFR negative (FFR+iFR−) pattern.

OCT Procedure and Definitions

After the coronary physiology procedure was performed, an OCT of the lesion was performed by advancing the OCT imaging catheter over the coronary pressure wire used for physiological assessment. Contrast clearance was used for image acquisition, and performance and analysis of the OCT performed according to the Consensus Standards for Acquisition, Measurement, and Reporting of Intravascular Optical Coherence Tomography Studies [20]. Geometric measures such as minimal lumen area (MLA), lesion length, and distal reference vessel area (RVA) were recorded, and measurements were performed offline following the procedure. The OCT images were analyzed for complex geometric properties, including the presence of plaque eccentricity and surface roughness. Plaque eccentricity was divided into cross‐sectional eccentricity (CE) or longitudinal eccentricity (LE) [18]. CE was defined as having a ratio of maximum to minimum plaque thickness at the site of the MLA of more than 3. LE was defined as the MLA occurring abruptly at either the proximal or distal quarter of the stenotic segment. Surface roughness was defined as a saw‐tooth pattern on the longitudinal axis of the OCT image [18].

Plaque morphology was analyzed at the lesion involving the MLA, and was categorized by the predominant consistency of the plaque, including thin‐cap fibroatheroma (TCFA), calcific plaque, fibrous plaque, fibrofatty plaque, lipid‐rich plaque, and positive remodeling. Post‐Procedural Care

Routine post‐procedural care involved 4 h of nursing observation and medical review pre‐discharge to determine whether there had been a procedural complication, including a review of the access site, review of the electrocardiogram, and clinical history to ensure no chest pain or other concerning symptoms.

Follow‐Up

Follow‐up was performed at 30 days via phone call and at 1 year via medical record assessment. The endpoint for follow‐up was target lesion failure (TLF), defined as a composite of clinically driven target lesion revascularisation (CDTLR), target lesion myocardial infarction (TLMI), and target lesion‐related cardiac death (TLCD). Acute and delayed complications of the procedural protocol were assessed, including access‐related complications, stroke, and myocardial infarction.

Objectives

The primary objective was to determine the impact of plaque morphological subtypes on invasive coronary physiology. The secondary objectives were to determine the impact of plaque geometry on invasive coronary physiology and the link between vasodilatory capacity (assessed using CFR) and iFR‐FFR discordance.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 14.0, StataCorp LP). The baseline characteristics of study patients were analyzed on a per‐patient basis. Coronary physiology and OCT findings were analyzed and presented on a per‐vessel basis in terms of frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Multivariate analysis was conducted to assess the association between different baseline characteristics and plaque morphological and geometric features with the hemodynamic impact of the plaque on a per‐vessel basis. Focal lesions were assessed separately to analyze the independent impact of plaque morphology on invasive coronary physiology. Pearson's correlation coefficient tests were used for a linear correlation between continuous parameters and Spearman's correlation coefficient tests were used for ordinal parameters. The odds ratios and associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) for variables in the final model were reported. The statistical significance level for this model was considered as a p < 0.05. Correlations among the predictors included were checked to assess for collinearity.

Sample Size

Due to the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of established data for formal sample size estimation, a sample size of 100 recruited patients was proposed as a pilot to observe trends. This preliminary analysis will inform future studies that require more rigorous sample size calculations.

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was approved by the NSLHD Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) (Study ID‐2021/ETH00129). This trial was registered in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry on 22 November 2021 (Trial ID: ACTRN12621001591842).

Coronary Physiology Characteristics

FFR was performed on all 124 vessels. The mean FFR was 0.824 (±0.075). iFR data was available on 121 (96.8%) vessels. The mean iFR was 0.914 (±0.059). CFR was performed on 47 (37.9%) vessels, with a mean CFR of 2.37 (±0.82).

Patterns of Physiological Discordance and Their Predictors

From the 121 vessels with both FFR and iFR values, discordant results were demonstrated on 25 occasions (20.7%), of which 12 (48%) were iFR+FFR−. The distal RVA was significantly higher in the iFR+FFR− group than the FFR+iFR− group (7.5 mm2 (95% CI: 5.92–8.93) vs. 4.59 mm2 (95% CI: 3.50–5.63), with a mean absolute difference of 2.91 mm2; p< 0.0001). Mean CFR was significantly lower in the iFR+FFR− subgroup compared to the FFR+iFR− subgroup (1.87 [95% CI: 1.40–2.33] vs. 3.27 [95% CI: 2.49−4.04]), with a mean absolute difference of 1.14 (p = 0.009). The factors associated with discordance are summarized in Table 4. Sensitivity analyses restricted to vessels without CFR available (n = 74) yielded results consistent with the main analysis. Calcific plaque remained associated with discordance (OR 2.23, 95% CI: 0.57−8.74); however, this did not reach statistical significance due to a reduced sample size (p = 0.251).

Plaque Geometry

There was a statistically significant relationship between lesion length and the FFR value, independent of the MLA and distal vessel diameter (r = −0.002; p= 0.004) (Figure 8). Distal RVA was also associated with FFR (r = 0.005; p = 0.045).

A statistically significant relationship existed between lesion length and iFR, adjusted for MLA and distal vessel diameter (r = −0.002; p= 0.002) (Figure 8). Distal RVA was significantly associated with iFR (r = 0.006; p = 0.005).

MLA was not associated with FFR or iFR values after adjustment for RVA and lesion length (r = −0.003; p= 0.666 and r = −0.003; p= 0.607, respectively) (Figure 9).

Relationship between MLA and FFR/iFR.

Sensitivity analysis was performed on vessels without CFR available, yielding results consistent with the main analysis. Specifically, the relationship FFR/iFR and lesion length remains statistically significant (R = −0.002; p= 0.003 and R = −0.002; p= 0.005, respectively), as did the relationship between RVA and iFR/FFR. After adjustment for RVA and lesion length, the association between MLA and iFR/FFR remained nonsignificant in vessels without CFR measured.

There was no statistically significant correlation between CFR and lesion length or distal RVA (r = −0.022; p= 0.698 and r = −0.01; p= 0.541, respectively). Furthermore, there was no statistically significant correlation between baseline average peak velocity (APV) and distal RVA, when adjusted for MLA and lesion length (r = 0.34; p= 0.768).

Plaque Eccentricity

CE was associated with a lower mean FFR compared to the absence of CE (0.801 [±0.076] vs. 0.834 [±0.072]; p = 0.023). This was also true for iFR (0.891 [±0.088] vs. 0.920 [±0.053]; p= 0.025). This trend was not observed in LE, with no difference in mean FFR (0.814 [±0.065] vs. 0.828 [±0.077]; p= 0.389) and iFR (0.919 [±0.044] vs. 0.909 [±0.071]; p= 0.488).

Surface Roughness

The presence of surface roughness was associated with higher odds of positive iFR (OR 1.83 [0.29−11.53]; p= 0.518), higher odds of discordant physiology (2.04 [0.35−11.87]; p= 0.429), and lower odds of positive FFR (OR 0.19 [0.02−1.58]; p= 0.125); however, these associations did not meet statistical significance.

Clinical Outcomes at 30 Days and 1 Year

There were no complications due to the conduct of this protocol. TLF occurred at 1 year for three vessels (2.4%), all due to CDTLR, and all occurring in iFR‐FFR− lesions. Two lesions were predominantly fibro‐fatty plaque, and one was predominantly lipid‐rich plaque.

Discussion

In this study, we identified several important factors impacting invasive coronary physiology findings. The main findings of this study are: (1) Calcific plaque was associated with discordance, with a trend toward an iFR+FFR− pattern of discordance; other morphological characteristics were not predictive of FFR, iFR, or CFR. (2) OCT‐derived lesion length and distal RVA were the geometric measurements most strongly associated with FFR and iFR. (3) CE was associated with lower mean FFR and iFR values. (4) iFR+FFR− discordant physiology is associated with a lower CFR value than FFR+iFR− physiology. (5) Discordant physiology was not associated with TLF at 1 year.

Regarding the impact of plaque morphological characteristics on invasive coronary physiology, the findings of this study are consistent with previous studies showing no association between plaque morphology and FFR [14, 15, 16]. Discordant physiology was found in 20.7% of cases in our study, consistent with the published incidence in the FIGARO trial [13]. Regarding the patterns of discordance, iFR+FFR− physiology was associated with a lower CFR value than FFR+iFR− physiology, indicating that in patients with impaired vasodilatory capacity, the threshold for myocardial ischemia is more likely to be met with iFR. This finding has been also demonstrated in a study by Cook et al. [21] which showed a mean CFR value of 2.36 in the FFR+iFR− cohort and 1.44 in the iFR+FFR− cohort. CFR is a modality now incorporated into current pressure wire technologies [22, 23], that can be performed simultaneously with FFR (after baseline flow measurements have been obtained) and therefore may be a useful adjunct in physiological assessments, particularly in the setting of discordant physiology.

Calcific plaque was shown to be associated with discordance, with a trend toward an iFR+FFR− pattern, though this pattern was not statistically significant. To the author's knowledge, this hypothesis‐generating finding has not been demonstrated in any other study. Until further validation is performed, this observation remains purely exploratory. CFR was not affected by plaque morphology in the focal lesions assessed in our study; however, the subset of patients who had CFR performed was a small proportion of the patients with calcific plaque and discordance. Therefore, a larger cohort may be required to examine this link further. A study by Caliskan et al. [24] has demonstrated that coronary calcium scores are inversely related to CFR. It is possible that calcific plaque is associated with more complex geometries, and this is the mechanism of discordance, rather than the calcific plaque morphology independently. It is also further possible that the distensibility of a calcified vessel is less than a non‐calcified vessel, reducing the vasodilatory capacity of the vessel. Further research is needed to identify the reasons why calcific plaque may lead to increased discordance, perhaps using 3D‐QCA to examine the distensibility of calcific plaque and how this may impact FFR and iFR readings.

The distal RVA was also significantly larger in the iFR+FFR− subgroup. The modest relationship between baseline APV and the distal RVA does not fully account for this finding, and a larger cohort may be required to identify a statistically significant correlation between baseline flow and distal RVA to account for iFR+FFR− discordance.

FFR and iFR were more likely to be positive in the LAD compared to all other vessels as has been previously demonstrated [25]. This is driven by a larger myocardial supply area [8], with higher coronary flow resulting in a higher trans‐stenotic gradient [26]. Furthermore, there is a hydrostatic pressure gradient [27] due to the superior location of the distal segment of the LAD relative to where the pressure wire is equalized to the aortic pressure, which results in a lower value both for FFR and iFR [28]. Proximal lesions were also more likely to be both FFR and iFR positive as has been previously demonstrated [25], likely due to higher baseline and hyperemic flow in the proximal vessel compared to the distal vessel associated with a greater myocardial supply area [25]. This trend was not statistically significant in our cohort.

Regarding other geometric predictors of ischemia, lesion length was found to have a statistically significant correlation with both FFR and iFR, independent of MLA or distal RVA. This is consistent with a previous study showing that lesion length was the strongest predictor of pressure‐wire‐derived myocardial ischemia [29], and is explained by Poiseuille's law of fluid dynamics, which stipulates that the change of pressure through a stenosis is a factor of blood viscosity, coronary flow rate, vessel length, and the radius of the lumen [12]. Unexplained by Poiseuille's law; however, MLA was not found to be an independent predictor of myocardial ischemia in our study, despite accounting for RVA and lesion length. One possible explanation is the inability of the OCT catheter to cross the stenosis in several cases of critical stenosis; therefore, the impact of MLA on pressure wire results may have been underestimated by not capturing this group of severely stenosed vessels. Furthermore, if the radius of the lumen was reduced in a very focal segment, as opposed to a diffuse segment, this would reduce the trans‐stenotic gradient according to Poiseuille's law.

Regarding plaque eccentricity, CE was associated with a significantly lower mean FFR and iFR, while this was not observed for LE. Eccentricity is associated with a longer post‐stenotic recirculation zone and lower FFR values, as demonstrated in a CFD‐based study by Javadzadegan et al. [30]. Recirculation will result in energy dissipation and reduced pressure in the post‐stenotic segment, thereby increasing the trans‐stenotic pressure gradient. In contrast, LE alters lesion geometry in the axial dimension, and depending on the relative location of the MLA, may not result in the same degree of lateral flow separation, possibly accounting for the absence of a significant hemodynamic effect in our cohort. Further CFD‐based studies may be useful in identifying the difference in flow properties between CE and LE stenoses to help explain why CE was associated with greater energy loss in our study.

Limitations

Despite the novelty of this study, there are several limitations. The main limitation is statistical power due to the small sample size, thereby limiting the analysis of determinants of myocardial ischemia and discordant physiology due to a low event rate. There was a low absolute number of TLF; therefore, no analysis of risk factors for TLF in this cohort can be performed. Furthermore, the protocol did not mandate CFR and was performed in 37.9% of cases. Coronary flow and vasodilatory capacity are important confounders in assessing coronary physiology, particularly in assessing discordant physiology. Lastly, multiple pressure wire technologies were used in this project. There may be variability between thermodilution‐derived CFR and Doppler‐derived CFR which may confound the results of this assessment study.

While certain geometric and plaque morphological features were statistically associated with invasive physiological indices, statistical significance alone does not equate to clinical significance. Although we have reported effect sizes with 95% CIs to reflect the magnitude and precision of these associations, the clinical relevance remains uncertain. Establishing whether these vessel‐level features translate into differences in patient‐level outcomes such as TLF, myocardial infarction, or mortality will require validation in larger cohorts with linked longitudinal follow‐up.

Conclusions

iFR‐FFR discordance is common, and impaired vasodilatory capacity may account for some of these cases. In this study, calcific plaque was associated with discordance, while other OCT‐derived plaque morphological characteristics were not associated with invasive coronary physiology findings. This observation is hypothesis‐generating and requires validation in larger cohorts. Cross‐sectional plaque eccentricity is associated with lower FFR values, while other patterns of complex geometry do not appear to have an association with invasive coronary physiology.
