Impact of ABO blood groups on residual pulmonary vascular obstruction in patients with unprovoked pulmonary embolism∗
Authors: Floriane Samaria, Ohanna C. L. Bezerra, Lénaïck Gourhant, Marine Germain, Gaëlle Munsch, Robert Olaso, Delphine Bacq, Catherine A. Lemarié, Grégoire Le Gal, Jean-François Deleuze, France Gagnon, Marc A. Rodger, Francis Couturaud, David-Alexandre Trégouët
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•RPVO is observed in ∼30% patients with unprovoked PE and is associated with long-term complications such as recurrent PE and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.•Although ABO is a known risk factor for PE recurrence and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, this work shows that ABO has little to no influence on the risk of RPVO.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the third most frequent cause of cardiovascular death in the world, right after myocardial infarction and stroke.1, 2, 3 As the most severe clinical manifestation of venous thromboembolism (VTE), PE is associated with a high short-term mortality rate, with nearly 1 in 4 patients experiencing sudden death in high-risk groups of patients.4 Fortunately, the short-term prognosis of PE has markedly improved in recent years driven by advances in diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatments,5 leading to lower mortality rates among patients with PE over the years.1 Although the management of PE primarily focuses on its acute manifestation, the development of chronic long-term complications raises new challenges in PE management.6 This is especially evident in unprovoked PE, defined as PE occurring in the absence of any major identified risk factors.7,8 Indeed, after 3 to 6 months of standard anticoagulant therapy, ∼30% of patients with unprovoked PE still have residual thrombotic material that did not fully resolve in the pulmonary arteries, forming persistent obstructive scars adhered to their walls.9,10 Unless surgically removed, this residual pulmonary vascular obstruction (RPVO) persists within the pulmonary circulation. RPVO measured after 6 months of uninterrupted anticoagulation therapy has been shown to be a strong predictor of VTE recurrence in patients with a first unprovoked PE.10, 11, 12 Moreover, in 10% of unprovoked PE cases, RPVO is associated with the development of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).13 These patients have a reduced life expectancy, with an estimated 3-year survival of ∼90% for those undergoing surgery (ie, thromboendarterectomy, the only curative treatment for CTEPH) and 70% for those who are deemed inoperable.13

The pathophysiology of RPVO remains poorly understood. Despite growing interest, the underlying mechanisms causing RPVO, recurrent VTE, or CTEPH are still unclear. Recent research has increasingly focused on identifying RPVO risk factors and elucidating its underlying mechanisms. To date, advanced age, elevated initial obstruction percentage at PE diagnosis, elevated factor VIII (FVIII) plasma levels, and some fibrinolysis-related biomarkers have been reported as risk factors and remain the only known predictors.5,6,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Regarding fibrinolysis, Planquette et al proposed that RPVO may result from impaired fibrinolysis, because perfusion defects have been associated with fibrinolysis resistance in patients with PE.18,19 Furthermore, patients with RPVO exhibited reduced fibrin clot permeability and prolonged clot lysis time compared with those without RPVO.17

ABO is a major genetic risk factor for first VTE,20 with a stronger association reported for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) than for PE.21 ABO is also linked to VTE recurrence risk.22 In patients with DVT, non-O blood groups have been associated with residual vein obstruction.23 Furthermore, patients with CTEPH more frequently carry non-O blood groups than those with nonthromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.24 Finally, ABO is known to regulate FVIII levels through the von Willebrand factor–FVIII complex.25

All these elements point out that ABO may represent a possible common denominator underlying RPVO-related traits, a hypothesis that has so far, to our knowledge, never been investigated deeply. Demonstrating an association between ABO blood groups and the risk of RPVO could have important clinical implications for post-PE follow-up and monitoring, by improving the identification of patients at increased risk of CTEPH or recurrent PE. This study addresses this gap by examining the relationship between genetically determined ABO blood groups and RPVO across 3 independent clinical studies.

Methods

Studies and participants

This work builds upon 2 French studies, EDITH (Etude des Déterminants/Interaction de la Thrombose veineuse)26, 27, 28, 29 and PADIS-PE, (Prolonged Anticoagulant During Eighteen Months vs Placebo After Initial Six-month Treatment for a First Episode of Idiopathic Pulmonary Embolism),11,30 as well as 1 Canadian study, REVERSE-I (Recurrent Venous Thromboembolism Risk Stratification Evaluation).31, 32, 33

EDITH is a prospective observational cohort study launched in 2000 aimed at improving the understanding of VTE risk factors. Since its inception, it has enrolled >5000 patients with VTE. For this study, we focused on patients aged >18 years who experienced a first episode of unprovoked PE, completed 3 to 6 months of anticoagulation therapy, underwent a ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan for RPVO measurements within 3 to 24 months after treatment, and had available DNA materials. PE was defined as unprovoked in absence of the following factors in the past 3 months or past years before PE: cancer, pregnancy, postpartum period, oral contraceptive use, immobilization on the inferior member, and surgery involving long immobilization during or after operative phase. The EDITH study was approved by Brest University Hospital scientific and ethics board, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent for the study and DNA analysis was obtained from all patients.

PADIS-PE is a randomized clinical-trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00740883) conducted between 2007 and 2014 in 14 French care centers. PADIS-PE enrolled 371 unprovoked PE cases during this period. The goal was to identify the benefits and drawbacks of 18 months of treatment vs 6 months. Patients aged >18 years, with a first episode of unprovoked PE and V/Q scan for RPVO measurements available right after 6 months of uninterrupted treatment were included. All exclusion criteria are set out in the study protocol.11 The PADIS-PE study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki, good clinical practice, and relevant French regulations regarding ethics and data protection. The protocol and amendments were approved by a central independent ethics committee and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

REVERSE-I is a Canadian multicentric prospective study which enrolled 646 patients between 2001 and 2006 after their first episode of VTE. The aim was to derive a new clinical decision rule that would facilitate identification of patients at low risk of recurrent VTE after completion of treatment. All exclusion criteria can be found elsewhere.32,33 Patients were included the study if they were aged >18 years, with V/Q scan for RPVO measurements available right after completion of their 5 to 7 months oral anticoagulant therapy and had a first unprovoked PE event. Unprovoked PE was defined as PE occurring in the absence of the following: leg fracture or lower extremity plaster cast, immobilization for >3 days or surgery using a general anesthetic in the 3 months before the index event, diagnosis of malignancy in the past 5 years at the time of enrollment, pregnancy or 6 weeks postpartum statement, or use of oral contraceptive and hormone replacement. Institutional research ethics board approvals were obtained by all participating centers (Ottawa Hospital research ethics board). Protocols to study genetics in REVERSE-I was approved by the University of Toronto research ethics board.

RPVO measurements

In the 3 contributing studies, V/Q scans were performed to measure RPVO values. The protocol proposed by Meyer et al34 to correctly assess RPVO from these measures was adopted. Independent investigators scored mismatched perfusion defects by examining air and blood flow in the lungs5,35 to obtain a global score of obstruction (in percentage). Briefly, radioactive tracers are IV injected and blocked on their first passage through the pulmonary capillaries so that imaging is readable. A perfusion score is then attributed to each 5 lung lobes, to whom a weight based on the percentage of vascularization is primarily given. Then a global perfusion score is obtained by summing all the perfusion scores, individually multiplied by their respective weights. Finally, the percentage of RPVO is obtained by subtracting from 1 the global perfusion score (S) such that RPVO = (1-S) × 100. A threshold of ≥5% is commonly used to define the presence of RPVO. However, its value varies across studies,12,18,29,36, 37, 38, 39 and most investigations on RPVO have been case-control studies using different obstruction thresholds. To date, no consensus has been reached within the scientific and medical community regarding the optimal cutoff, although Picart et al26 showed that a ≥5% threshold is sufficient to characterize RPVO. In this study, we use a continuous measurement of RPVO, allowing us to account for the degree of obstruction in each patient, because RPVO values can range from 0 to 100.

ABO blood group determination

ABO blood groups were genetically determined from 5 common ABO single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as described in Goumidi et al20: rs8176719-delG (tagging O1), rs41302905-T (O2), rs2519093-T (A1), rs1053878-A (A2), and rs8176743-T (B).

Because DNA samples from EDITH, PADIS-PE, and REVERSE-I participants were typed for high-density genotyping arrays, we used best-guessed genotypes from imputed data to infer ABO blood groups. The description of genotyping, imputation and derivation of principal components from genome-wide genotype data in each of the 3 studies have already been provided in Munsch et al.40 Briefly, individuals from EDITH and PADIS-PE were genotyped by the Illumina Infinium Human Global Screening Array GSAMD-24v3 at the French National Center of Human Genomics Research and data were further imputed on the 1000 genomes phase 3, version 5, panel. REVERSE-I participants were genotyped on the Illumina Global Screening Array at Génome Québec and genotype data further imputed using TOPmed Eagle, version 2.4, panel. The imputation quality scores of ABO blood groups tagging SNPs in each of the 3 studies are shown in supplemental Table 1. All 5 ABO tagging SNPs have imputation quality of >0.90 across all 3 studies, except the uncommon O2 tagging variant with imputation quality of 0.83 in REVERSE-I.

Statistical analyses

For descriptive analyses, continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages.

In each of the 3 contributing studies, the distribution of RPVO measurements was characterized by an excess of 0 values followed by positive continuous values (Figure 1; Table 1). Consequently, we used a compound Poisson-gamma (CPG) model41,42 to investigate the association of clinical and biological variables (including age, sex, body mass index [BMI], PE associated or not with DVT, and ABO blood groups) with RPVO. In CPG modeling, the exponential of a regression coefficient represents the multiplicative change in the RPVO measurement associated with a 1-unit increase in the given covariate. Association analyses were conducted in each study while adjusting for age and sex. The association analysis of ABO blood groups with RPVO was conducted assuming additive allele effects and was adjusted for the first 4 principal components derived from the genotypic data. The O1 blood group was used as reference. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.1.0 software (R Core Team [2021]; https://www.R-project.org/), cplm 0.7.12 package45 for CPG analyses, and metafor 4.6-0 package44 for fixed-effect meta-analysis.

Power analysis

A simulation study was conducted to assess the power of our study to detect a potential effect of ABO blood groups. Our simulation followed these steps: first, we estimated the parameters required to simulate RPVO values from a Tweedie distribution, which appropriately reflects the semicontinuous nature of this phenotype.41,42 Specifically, we estimated:•ρ, the index parameter defining the distribution’s shape;•ϕ, the dispersion parameter; and•μ, the expected individual mean based on selected covariates.

These parameters were obtained by applying a CPG model, adjusted for age, sex, and study cohort, to the combined data set from the EDITH, PADIS-PE, and REVERSE-I studies.

Second, we simulated 1000 data sets for various minor allele frequencies (MAFs) and odds ratios. For each simulation settings, we created a sample of 586 individuals (a number that corresponds to the final size of our study when EDITH, PADIS-PE, and REVERSE-I are combined) with genotype data generated based on the corresponding MAF, assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. RPVO values were then generated according to the CPG model defined in the first step and the fixed effect of each additional copy of the risk allele on RPVO.

Finally, a CPG model was fitted to each simulated data set to estimate the association of alleles with RPVO. Statistical power was calculated as the proportion of simulations in which a significant association (α = 0.05) was detected.

Results

The main subject characteristics are shown in Table 1. A total of 165 unprovoked PEs with RPVO measurements and genetic data available were reported in EDITH, 306 in PADIS-PE, and 115 in REVERSE. Some common characteristics were observed across the 3 studies, with comparable proportions of women and mean BMI. Approximately 50% of participants are women in the 3 studies, and the mean BMI was ∼28 kg/m2. The distribution of RPVO did not significantly differ (P = .12) across the 3 studies, despite some trend for quartiles of RPVO being lower in REVERSE-I. In contrast, some differences were detected, reflecting distinctions between EDITH and the more similar PADIS-PE and REVERSE-I. Patients from EDITH were older, on average (aged 65.8 years), than patients from PADIS-PE (aged 59.3 years) and REVERSE-I (aged 54.6 years). Similarly, the proportion of DVT associated with unprovoked PE was higher in EDITH (48.5%) than PADIS-PE (30.8%) and REVERSE-I (27.5%). The same pattern was observed for the proportion of recurrent VTE and death events. Regarding the time to recurrence and follow-up, EDITH and REVERSE-I displayed longer times to events (2.6 years and 5.4 years for recurrence; 6.6 years and 5.9 years for death events, respectively) than PADIS-PE (1.0 and 1.58 years, respectively).

Results of the association of clinical/biological factors with RPVO are shown in Table 2. Overall, age was the only variable found to be significantly associated with RPVO, with homogenous effect across the 3 studies. Specifically, each additional year of age was associated with a 4% increase in RPVO (exp[β] = 1.04; P = 5.56 × 10–7 in the meta-analyzed samples). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the role of genetically determined ABO blood groups with RPVO in patients with unprovoked PE. Moreover, it is the first to apply the CPG model to analyze RPVO measurements while accounting for the semicontinuous nature of this clinical phenotype. This statistical approach has recently been shown to be well suited for genetic association studies of continuous variables with excess of 0 values.42

Although Dentali et al23 reported that non-O blood groups were associated with a threefold increased risk of RVO in 268 patients with DVT, we did not find any significant association of ABO blood groups with RPVO in patients with unprovoked PE. With a sample size of 586 patients, we had ∼90% statistical power (α = 0.05) to detect a risk increase of at least 1.20 for non-O blood groups, assuming a frequency of 0.58 (supplemental Table 5). For a SNP with a MAF as low as 0.03, quite similar to the rare A2, B, and O2 blood group tagging variants, our study had ∼80% power to detect an allelic effect corresponding to at least an 80% increase in risk. Similarly, our study had sufficient power (>80%) to detect an increased risk in RPVO of >1.30 for any polymorphism with a MAF of >0.20. These findings suggest that if ABO blood groups influence RPVO, the effect is likely more modest and would require much larger studies to be reliably detected. Bonderman et al24 reported that non-O blood groups were associated with a twofold increased risk of CTEPH, for which RPVO is a known risk factor.6,15,46,47 The lack of association between ABO blood groups and RPVO observed in our study suggests that ABO may influence the risk of CTEPH via mechanisms independent of RPVO. We could not test this hypothesis in our study because the number of patients with unprovoked PE who developed CTEPH during the follow-up was too small (n = 12). This hypothesis should be evaluated in a large clinical prospective cohort of patients with PE with available DNA and RPVO measurements. Of note, exclusion of these 12 patients from the analysis did not alter the results of the haplotype association study (supplemental Table 4).

In conclusion, our study found no evidence of an association between ABO blood groups and RPVO in patients with unprovoked PE. Nonetheless, it offers a useful foundation for further exploration of the interindividual variability in RPVO and of the potential role of other genetically determined molecular determinants of RPVO.
