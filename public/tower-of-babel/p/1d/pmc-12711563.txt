SARS-CoV-2 infection induces pro-fibrotic and pro-thrombotic foam cell formation

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
COVID-19 and long COVID are characterized by a dysregulated immune response. However, the role of macrophages during viral infection is poorly defined. Here we demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 infection results in increased macrophage numbers and extensive formation of enlarged lipid-laden macrophages or foam cells using humanized mice, rhesus macaques and post-mortem human lung tissue. Notably, infection by other coronaviruses tested, SARS-CoV-1, MERS-CoV and two bat coronaviruses (SHC014-CoV or WIV1-CoV), did not result in macrophage proliferation or foam cell formation. Foam cells in SARS-CoV-2-infected human lung tissue display a pro-fibrotic and pro-thrombotic phenotype as they are enriched for genes associated with platelet activation and aggregation, as well as extracellular matrix organization and collagen synthesis. After viral clearance, macrophage numbers remain elevated, and lung fibrosis and thrombi persist. Importantly, we show that pre-exposure prophylaxis or early treatment with a SARS-CoV-2 antiviral, EIDD-2801, prevents increases in macrophage cell numbers and foam cell formation, and reduces fibrosis markers. These observations highlight the contribution of macrophages to lung inflammation and tissue injury leading to the pulmonary fibrosis observed in COVID-19 patients.

Main

A rapid and efficient innate immune response is considered the first line of defence against SARS-CoV-2. COVID-19 severity and the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome have been linked to patients with increased numbers of inflammatory myeloid cells1–4. However, studies of the early events of the alveolar macrophage response to acute SARS-CoV-2 infection have been limited. During SARS-CoV-2 infection, peroxisome function is decreased and inflammasome activation is increased, leading to an inflammatory state that can persist after infection and impede the repair of lung tissue5,6. Why these inflammatory-promoting dysfunctions persist in some patients is unknown. Continuous myeloid cell activation can contribute to the inflammation that characterizes long COVID4,7–9. Animal models can help define the role of lung macrophages during SARS-CoV-2 infection and can be used to analyse early infection events10,11. Lung-only humanized mice (LoM), immune deficient mice implanted with human lung tissue12–14, recapitulate features of acute human lung pathology, including fibrin thrombi formation, following SARS-CoV-2 infection15.

Here we used LoM to evaluate the human macrophage response during acute SARS-CoV-2 infection. SARS-CoV-2 infection significantly increased macrophage numbers in LoM human lung implants. In addition, infection of human lung implants induced the formation of foam cells. Foam cells are enlarged macrophages that contain lipid droplets. How lipid sequestering affects macrophage function is controversial16–19. In atherosclerotic plaques, foam cells can be either pro- or anti-inflammatory with M1 and M2 polarization. In addition, foam cells may possess no polarization and instead display a pro-fibrotic phenotype17,19. We show that during SARS-CoV-2 infection, fibrosis and thrombi formation-associated genes were increased in foam cells. Infection also increased markers of fibrosis such as smooth muscle actin (SMA) and collagen. This response and the downstream sequelae were reduced by EIDD-2801 pre-exposure prophylaxis and early treatment. The presence of a functional systemic human immune system in humanized mice resulted in even greater increases in human macrophage and foam cell numbers in human lung tissue following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Importantly, even after infection was cleared, macrophage numbers remained elevated, and pathology persisted. SARS-CoV-2-infection-induced increases in macrophage numbers were also observed in a mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 model, a non-human primate (NHP) model and in post-mortem tissue samples.

Results

SARS-CoV-2 infection induces foam cell formation

LoM human lung implants possess human tissue resident macrophages (hCD68+) that arise from the transplanted tissue, as there are no human myeloid progenitor cells in their bone marrow or human monocytes in their blood14. Human CD68+ cells were quantified in LoM human lung tissue following infection with bat coronaviruses SHC014-CoV or WIV1-CoV, or with human coronaviruses SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV or MERS-CoV (Fig. 1a). Macrophage numbers (counts mm−2) were significantly lower in LoM infected with bat coronaviruses SHC014-CoV (P = 0.018) and WIV1-CoV (P = 0.004), and human coronaviruses SARS-CoV (P = 0.008) and MERS-CoV (P = 0.008) compared with uninfected controls (Fig. 1a). In contrast, macrophage numbers were significantly higher in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM compared with uninfected LoM (P = 3.16 × 10−11) and LoM infected with the other coronaviruses (Fig. 1a). By 14 days post exposure, human macrophage numbers were over 200% higher compared with uninfected LoM (Fig. 1b). Enlarged macrophages in alveolar lumen were noted in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissue (Fig. 1d; enlarged macrophage in blue, unenlarged in red). Enlarged macrophages had a ‘foam’-like appearance with lipid droplets (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1b). The vacuoles of enlarged macrophages contained debris, lipids and lamellar bodies (Extended Data Fig. 1b). The average size of macrophages in the interstitial tissue was 39.29 µm2, which is significantly smaller than that of enlarged macrophages (325.80 µm2 per cell, P ≤ 1 × 10−14) in the lumen (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Co-staining of hCD68+ macrophages with adipophilin, a lipid-droplet marker, indicated that these enlarged macrophages were foam cells (Fig. 1e)20,21. Almost 90% of the foam cells identified using the criteria above were adipophilin positive (Extended Data Fig. 1d). As with the total macrophage number, the number of foam cells was 350% higher at day 6 (P = 0.024) and 475% higher at day 14 (P = 0.002) post exposure compared with uninfected LoM (Fig. 1e). Interstitial macrophage (IM) numbers were also significantly higher at day 2 (P = 1.5 × 10−5), day 6 (P = 1.56 × 10−4) and day 14 (P = 3.16 × 10−6) post exposure compared with uninfected IM numbers (Extended Data Fig. 1e). PCNA expression was significantly increased by >20% in foam cells in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung implants (P = 0.023) (Extended Data Fig. 1f), indicating that the observed foam cell number increase is driven by macrophage proliferation.

Transcriptional analysis of foam cell- or IM-enriched areas in uninfected LoM human lung tissue was performed via GeoMx digital spatial profiling22 (Extended Data Fig. 1g). Foam cells had a transcriptional signature similar to alveolar macrophages in healthy human lungs (Extended Data Fig. 1h). The expression of 28 genes was increased and that of 276 genes was decreased in foam cells compared with IMs (Fig. 1f). Of note, genes involved in inflammation and the innate immune response, GPNMB, CTSB and CCL18, were among the highest expressed genes in foam cells compared with IMs. In addition, foam cells stained positive for the M2 marker CD206, whereas IMs did not (Extended Data Fig. 1i). These results indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection significantly increased macrophage numbers in human lung tissue and led to the formation of foam cells with distinct tissue distribution.

Few macrophages harbour SARS-CoV-2 antigen

In uninfected LoM human lung tissue, proSP-C, a marker for alveolar type II pneumocytes (AT2 cells), co-localized with hACE2 (Extended Data Fig. 2a). In SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissue, proSP-C + AT2 cells co-stained for SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (Fig. 2a). Human CD68+ macrophages in uninfected and SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissue did not stain positive for hACE2 (Extended Data Fig. 2b). However, at 2 days post-SARS-CoV-2 exposure, viral nucleocapsid staining was noted in a small minority of macrophages with a punctate cytoplasmic pattern of staining, unlike the uniform cytoplasmic staining seen in infected AT2 cells (Fig. 2a). Foam cells show a pro-thrombotic gene signature

Thrombi were observed in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissue (Fig. 2d) but not in uninfected controls. Digital spatial RNA-seq analysis of foam cells in human lung tissue demonstrated higher expression of genes associated with thrombi formation processes such as, platelet activation and aggregation in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM (Extended Data Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 1).

In uninfected LoM, foam cells had higher expression of antigen presentation, lipoprotein phagocytosis and extracellular remodelling genes compared with IMs (Fig. 2e). Specifically, in uninfected LoM, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of foam cells, relative to IM, revealed higher expression of genes involved in fibrotic processes, such as extracellular matrix organization, collagen biosynthesis, collagen chain trimerization and collagen-modifying enzymes (Fig. 2e, blue). The increased numbers of pro-fibrotic foam cells during infection led us to assess pulmonary fibrosis development via deposition of key extracellular matrix fibre, collagen A1. At days 14 and 28 post-SARS-CoV-2 exposure, more collagen A1 was present in LoM human lung tissue compared with uninfected LoM (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 2d). Immunofluorescence staining also confirmed a sustained increase in SMA in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissue (Fig. 2g) that reached significance at days 14 (P = 1.26 × 10−5) and 28 post exposure (P = 0.002) (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Therefore, SARS-CoV-2 infection leads to an increase in foam cells that innately express pro-fibrotic and pro-thrombotic genes, suggesting that foam cells could contribute to fibrosis and thrombi formation.

EIDD-2801 reduces the macrophage response and fibrosis

We previously showed that EIDD-2801, a ribonucleoside analogue23,24, significantly reduced viral titres in LoM human lung tissue (titres reproduced in Supplementary Table 2)15. We used LoM human lung tissues collected from this experiment (Fig. 3a) to evaluate the effect of EIDD-2801 pre-exposure prophylaxis and early post-exposure treatment on the macrophage response to SARS-CoV-2 infection and fibrosis development. Importantly, EIDD-2801 treatment of SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM at all timepoints led to a significant reduction (35–47%) in total macrophage numbers in human lung tissue compared with vehicle-treated SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM (−12 h: P = 5.58 × 10−4; +24 h: P = 4.94 × 10−4; +48 h: P = 0.003) (Fig. 3b). Notably, EIDD-2801 treatment 12 h before or 24 h post infection prevented an increase in macrophages. Macrophage numbers in EIDD-2801-treated mice were equivalent to those in uninfected LoM (−12 h: P = 0.793 and +24 h: P = 0.466) (Fig. 3b). However, when EIDD-2801 treatment was initiated at 48 h post infection, total macrophage numbers in LoM human lung tissues were significantly decreased compared with vehicle-treated LoM, but were significantly higher compared with uninfected LoM (P = 0.057) (Fig. 3b). Total macrophage numbers were 150%, 230% and 250% higher in the human lung tissue of LoM administered vehicle 12 h before (P = 0.003) or at 24 or 48 h post-SARS-CoV-2 exposure (P = 2.26 × 10−6 and P = 2.73 × 10−7, respectively) compared with uninfected controls (Fig. 3b). Foam cell numbers in human lung tissue of SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM administered vehicle at 24 (P = 0.011) or 48 h (P = 0.003) post exposure were significantly higher compared with uninfected LoM (Fig. 3c). As expected, based on our LoM time-course analysis (Fig. 1e), there was no significant difference in foam cell numbers in the human lung tissue of uninfected LoM compared to SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM administered vehicle 12 h before exposure (P = 0.203) (Fig. 3c), as these animals were necropsied and analysed at 2 days post exposure when there was no significant change in foam cell numbers. When EIDD-2801 treatment was initiated 24 or 48 h post exposure, foam cell numbers in SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissue were significantly lower compared with vehicle-treated infected LoM (P = 0.018 and P = 0.017, respectively) (Fig. 3c). These results indicate that EIDD-2801 treatment initiated at 24 and even 48 h post exposure prevented virus-induced foam cell development, as the number of foam cells in human lung tissue was equivalent to uninfected LoM (P = 0.738 and P = 0.931, respectively) (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM receiving EIDD-2801 treatment exhibited reduced collagen and SMA expression compared with vehicle-treated controls (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Fig. 3d). Taken together, our results indicate that EIDD-2801 pre- or post-exposure treatment can prevent SARS-CoV-2-induced increases in macrophage numbers and the deposition of fibrosis-associated fibres.

BLT-L mice efficiently control SARS-CoV-2 replication

Bone marrow-liver-thymus-lung (BLT-L) mice, in addition to human lung tissue implants, possess an autologous innate and adaptive human immune system13,14. We confirmed hACE2 expression on human epithelial cells (cytokeratin-19+) in uninfected BLT-L human lung tissue (Fig. 4a). The presence of hACE2 + AT2 cells was established by co-staining of proSP-C and hACE2 (Fig. 4a). To evaluate the effect of an autologous systemic human immune system on SARS-CoV-2 infection and to characterize the macrophage response, human immune cell levels in the peripheral blood of BLT-L mice were analysed before SARS-CoV-2 infection (Supplementary Table 3). Human lung tissue was collected at 2, 6 and 14 days post exposure (Fig. 4b). SARS-CoV-2 replicated in the human lung tissue of BLT-L mice with a mean infectious virus titre of 1.06 × 106 ± 7.97 × 105 PFU g−1 at 2 days post exposure (Fig. 4c). By 6 days post exposure, the mean infectious virus titre was 1 log lower and most notably, 4 of the 9 human lung tissue implants analysed had no detectable infectious virus (limit of detection (LOD) = 50 PFU g−1) (Fig. 4c). By 14 days post exposure, no infectious virus was detected in any human lung tissue implant analysed (Fig. 4c), indicating that unlike LoM, which supported sustained viral replication15, BLT-L mice efficiently controlled SARS-CoV-2 replication. We performed bulk RNA-sequencing analysis on human lung tissue from uninfected and SARS-CoV-2-infected BLT-L mice (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 4). Significantly higher expression of interferon alpha (IFNα) and interferon (IFN) stimulated genes was observed at days 2 and 6 post exposure compared with uninfected controls (Fig. 4d). By day 14 post exposure immunoglobulin-related genes became the most highly differentially expressed genes (Fig. 4d). Gene pathways that were significantly upregulated at 2 days post exposure focused on the host response to viral replication (Fig. 4e). At 6 days post exposure, immune response-associated gene pathways were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4e). By day 14 post exposure, adaptive immune response-associated gene pathways were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4e). The upregulation of immune response-related gene pathways in BLT-L mice at days 6 and 14 post exposure is consistent with decreased infectious virus titres.

This led us to investigate the expression of IFN-stimulated, IFNα and cytokine genes in the human lung tissue of SARS-CoV-2-infected BLT-L mice (Fig. 4f). At days 2 and 6 post exposure, we observed increased expression of IFNα genes and IFN-stimulated genes (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 5). IFNα gene expression was highest at 2 days post exposure, coinciding with the highest viral titres detected in the human lung tissue of BLT-L mice. Gene expression of multiple cytokines, including IL6 was also increased at days 2 and 6 post exposure (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 5). Notably, there was increased expression of macrophage/monocyte chemoattraction-related genes (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 5). By 14 days post exposure, IFN-stimulated genes displayed expression levels similar to uninfected BLT-L mice (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 5). However, at 14 days post exposure, IFNα gene expression was still higher compared with uninfected controls (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 5). CCL7, CXCL8, CXCL2 and IL17C expression also remained elevated at 14 days post exposure (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 5). These results show that BLT-L mice mounted an immune response to infection that efficiently suppressed virus replication.

Fibrin thrombi were identified in the human lung tissue of BLT-L mice at days 6 and 14 post exposure but not in uninfected BLT-L mice (Fig. 4g). Picrosirius red staining of collagen networks revealed significantly elevated collagen deposition in the human lung tissue of SARS-CoV-2-infected BLT-L mice (day 6, P = 0.041; day 14, P = 9 × 10−4) (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 5a). SMA staining was higher in BLT-L mice at day 6 (19.60 ± 4.53%) and was significantly higher at day 14 (P = 6.0 × 10−4) post exposure compared with uninfected BLT-L mice (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 5b). Increased collagen and SMA levels were observed in BLT-L mice at 14 days post exposure, even though no detectable infectious virus was present. The presence of fibrin thrombi and fibrosis markers indicates that COVID-19 pathology persisted in human lung tissue after infection was cleared.

Foam cells in SARS-CoV-2-infected BLT-L mice and NHP

No significant difference in total macrophage numbers in human lung tissue was observed between uninfected and SARS-CoV-2-infected BLT-L mice at 2 days post exposure (P = 0.821) (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 6a). However, at 6 days post exposure, total macrophage numbers were significantly higher in SARS-CoV-2-infected BLT-L mice compared with uninfected animals (P = 3.43 × 10−5) (Fig. 5a). At 14 days post exposure, when infectious virus was no longer detected, the total number of macrophages was 300% higher compared with uninfected animals (P = 1.64 × 10−11) (Fig. 5a). Lung macrophage levels were also analysed using samples from previous experiments conducted in a mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 (MA10)10 model and an NHP model of SARS-CoV-2 infection (Extended Data Fig. 7a, and Fig. 5c, respectively)26. Modestly increased levels of mouse macrophages (mCD68+) were observed in mice at 7 and 15 days post exposure with SARS-CoV-2 (MA10) compared with uninfected controls (day 7, P = 0.006; day 15, P < 1.0 × 10−14) (Extended Data Fig. 7b). However, mCD68+ cells were not readily observed in the alveolar spaces of MA10-infected lungs. Importantly, no foam cells were observed in lung tissues of MA10-infected mice. In contrast, lung tissue analysed from NHPs infected with SARS-CoV-2 2019 WA-1 for 7 days showed a 500% increase in total macrophage numbers and a 600% increase in foam cell numbers compared with uninfected animals (P = 7.73 × 10−6, P = 3.15 × 10−5) (Fig. 5c,d). Discussion

We describe the sustained increase in tissue macrophage numbers in lung tissue that occurs as a sequela of SARS-CoV-2 infection. We report increases in IMs and enlarged macrophages in alveolar spaces. These enlarged macrophages co-stain with adipophilin, a marker of foam cells20,21. Importantly, these observations were confirmed in SARS-CoV-2-infected humans and NHPs.

During SARS-CoV-2 infection, foam cells in the lung expressed CD206, a marker characteristic of M2 macrophages, and had a pro-fibrotic gene expression signature. The observed increase in fibrotic markers is probably a combination of effects associated with foam cells and other general repair processes induced by infection-mediated lung damage. Foam cells in the lung also exhibited higher expression of antigen-presenting genes and processes related to lipid uptake. During SARS-CoV-2 infection, foam cells exhibited higher expression of genes involved in platelet aggregation and activation, which contribute to thrombi formation29. SARS-CoV-2 infection can induce inflammation leading to an increased risk of blood clots forming in the lungs or other parts of the body. Thrombi formation is more frequent in individuals with heart disease, diabetes or a history of blood clots30. People hospitalized with COVID-19 and requiring intensive care are also at higher risk for thrombi formation31. The virus alters blood properties, making clot formation more likely32. No direct connection between virus infection and clot formation has been proposed33,34. Our results demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 infection induces the formation of foam cells, which are potential drivers of this important pathological aspect of COVID-19. One limitation of this study is the lack of information regarding the presence of foam cells in circulation. This is important because clotting post infection is not limited to the lungs.

Virus was sometimes observed in multinucleated cells, supporting the notion that viruses enter macrophages principally via phagocytosis of infected cells. Antigen staining in macrophages could also be due to engulfed debris or mRNA-encoded SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein. Others have reported that monocytes and macrophages do not support SARS-CoV-2 replication8,35–37. Notably, in macrophages, we observed virus in the Golgi near the endoplasmic reticulum, but did not see virus egress from cells.

Unlike in LoM, foam cells in BLT-L mice can derive from macrophages within the human lung tissue or from circulating blood human monocytes. At 14 days post exposure, there was no detectable infectious virus in any BLT-L mouse analysed. However, human macrophage numbers in BLT-L human lung tissue were highest at this timepoint. Increased inflammatory gene expression continued through day 14 post exposure. Unresolved lung macrophage accumulation and foam cell formation could potentially contribute to long COVID.

Interestingly, we did not observe foam cells in the alveolar spaces of the mouse-adapted model of SARS-CoV-2 described in (ref. 11). NHP and BLT-L mice had similar robust increases in both macrophage and foam cell levels after 6 days post exposure. NHPs exposed to SARS-CoV-2 were not age matched. Higher levels of macrophages were also noted in the post-mortem lung samples analysed from SARS-CoV-2 patients. However, direct comparison to our experimental models cannot be made due to the unknown length of infection in the human samples.

EIDD-2801 treatment prevented macrophage proliferation and foam cell formation in human lung tissue, and reduced fibrosis biomarkers, collagen and SMA. These results indicate that macrophage perturbation is a direct result of viral infection of lung tissue. It will be important to determine whether EIDD-2801 treatment initiated at symptom onset would prevent or delay fibrosis and thrombosis. Foam cells and COVID-19 lung pathology persisted after viral clearance, suggesting that even individuals who do not experience severe COVID-19 symptoms could incur lasting and potentially permanent lung damage. The increase in foam cells may explain thrombi formation during COVID-19. We therefore propose that SARS-CoV-2 infection induces foam cell formation, facilitating the development of blood clots and extensive pulmonary pathology.

Methods

Ethics statement

All research complies with ethical regulations set forth by the institution where the research occurred. Mouse experiments were performed at the University of North Carolina (UNC)-Chapel Hill according to protocols approved by the Institutional Use and Care Committee at UNC-Chapel Hill (protocol no.: 18-307, 23-217, 21-122, 20-235 and 17-288) and in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Rhesus macaque studies were performed at the California National Primate Research Center according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of California, Davis (protocol no.: 21735). Post-mortem material was collected from 3 patients who had COVID-19 and from 4 healthy patients. Ethics approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at UNC-Chapel Hill (protocol no.: 03-1396 and 21-1316), and the study was conducted according to the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. For all samples, donor/family consent was obtained in line with US law and the above Ethics approval.

Roles and responsibilities among investigators were extensively discussed during the analysis and write-up of the data.

Human participants

Tissue slides of uninfected and infected lung samples were provided by the Marsico Lung Institute at UNC-Chapel Hill. Lung tissues from uninfected donors were obtained before the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic from the UNC Tissue Procurement and Cell Culture Core. SARS-CoV-2-infected donors were confirmed positive shortly before or after death. None of the SARS-CoV-2-infected patients were intubated or had a history of lung disease. Basic donor demographics of infected post-mortem lung tissue are as follows: a 40-year-old male, a 91-year-old female and a 77-year-old male. Basic donor demographics for uninfected post-mortem lung tissue are as follows: a 17-year-old female, 27-year-old female, a 17-year-old female and a 19-year-old female. Additional information on donor tissue is available. There was no compensation for participants38–41.

Biosafety

All experiments were performed with approval from the Institutional Biosafety Committee at UNC-Chapel Hill (approval no. 96229) or UC Davis (BUA R1858) in compliance with the Potential Pandemic Pathogen Care and Oversight Committee at the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Disease (NIAID). All experiments performed with infectious virus were done in BSL-3 conditions using approved standard operating protocols and personal protective equipment. BSL-3 facilities met safety requirements recommended by the Center for Disease Control, National Institutes of Health, and university Environmental Health and Safety departments.

Humanized mice

In brief, LoM were created by surgically implanting two pieces (~3 mm2) of human lung tissue (Advanced Bioscience Resources) subcutaneously into the back of male and female 12–21-week-old NOD.Cg-Prrkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ mice (NSG mice) (The Jackson Laboratory)13–15. After 8 weeks implantation, the size of implants was assessed by palpation13–15. BLT-L mice were created by implanting human thymus and liver tissue under the kidney capsule of irradiated (200 rad) 6–8-week-old NSG mice. Autologous human lung tissue was implanted subcutaneously into the back of each mouse. BLT-L mice received a bone marrow transplant via intravenous injection of autologous human CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells. BLT-L mice were monitored for human immune cell levels in the peripheral blood via flow cytometry (Supplementary Table 3 and Extended Data Fig. 4)13,14. Engraftment of lung tissue was assessed by palpation before experimentation. Mice were kept in vivariums maintained by the Division of Comparative Medicine at UNC-Chapel Hill. Temperature (20–23 °C), humidity (30–70%) and light cycle (12 h light/dark cycle) were maintained in the vivarium.

Production of coronaviruses and in vivo infection

A clinical isolate of SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV/USA-WA-1/2020) was obtained from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (GenBank accession number MN985325.1) and passaged twice in Vero E6 cells (CRL-1586, ATCC) to create a passage-5 working stock15. Infectious virus clones based on published metagenomic sequences were used to derive wild-type SARS-CoV, SHC014-CoV and WIV1-CoV42–45. MERS-CoV (HCoV-EMC/2012) was originally provided by Dr Bart Haagmans, Erasmus Medical Center, Rotterdam, the Netherlands. Viruses were titred on a confluent monolayer of Vero E6 (SARS-CoV, SHC014-CoV and WIV1-CoV) or Vero CCL-81 cells (MERS-CoV) (ATCC) 14,15. SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM human lung tissues analysed at days 2, 6 and 14 post exposure and analysed following EIDD-2801 or vehicle administration were previously collected during experiments described in ref. 15. LoM and BLT-L mice were exposed to coronaviruses by injection15. Briefly, anaesthetized mice were shaved over the human lung implant, and virus (1–3 × 105 PFU in 100 µl PBS) was injected directly into the implant. EIDD-2801 (500 mg kg−1) or vehicle control (10% PEG and 2.5% Cremophor RH40 in water) was administered every 12 h by oral gavage15. Viral titre was determined by plaque assay using Vero E6 cells. Human lung implants were weighed post necropsy and homogenized before titration was performed15. Uninfected human lung implants were collected at the same time or close to the same time as SARS-CoV-2 infected human lung implants were collected and used as the basis for all quantifications. Lung samples analysed from mice infected with mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 virus (MA10) originated from experiments described in ref. 11. A mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 virus, MA10, was generated by serial passaging in BALB/cAnNHsd mice every 2 days until 10 passages was achieved10. BALB/c/cAnNHsd mice (10-week and 1-year-old female) (Envigo strain 047) were intranasally infected with 105 PFU SARS-CoV-2 MA10 (ref. 11). Clinical signs of disease were monitored daily, and mice were euthanized by isoflurane before their lungs were prepared for histological analysis. Two 50–77-month-old rhesus macaques were infected with WA-1 intratracheally (2 × 106 PFU) and intranasally (0.5 × 106 PFU), and were euthanized after 7 days for histological analysis; these animals were also placebo-treated control animals of previously published studies46,47. In addition, two 14–16-month-old macaques were infected with B.1.617.2 (delta) intratracheally (2 × 106 PFU) and intranasally (1 × 106 PFU), and were euthanized at 7 days post exposure26. During infection, animals were monitored for clinical signs. Lung tissue was also collected and processed in a similar manner from 2 uninfected 20–36-month-old rhesus macaques as a control. Infected macaques were kept in BSL-3 conditions and were housed in steel cages, scaled to the animals’ size as is national standard. Rooms maintained a 12-h light/dark cycle, 64–84 °F and 30–70% humidity26,46,47.

GeoMx digital spatial profiling

Human lung tissue collected from uninfected LoM and SARS-CoV-2-infected LoM at 2 and 6 days post exposure from previously published experiments15 were fixed, paraffin embedded and sectioned (5 µm). Tissue sections were deparaffined and stained overnight according to NanoString Technologies GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP) Leica protocol. Human lung implant tissue morphology was visualized via immunofluorescence staining for hCD68 (KP1, Alexa Fluor 647, 1:50), Syto13 and SMA (1A4, Alexa Fluor 594, 1:200). Human Whole Transcriptome Atlas (121401102, Nanostring) and COVID-19 Immune Response Atlas (121303314, Nanostring) probes were hybridized, and slides were washed twice in fresh 2× saline-sodium citrate (SSC) before being loaded on the GeoMx DSP. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected at ×20 magnification on the basis of macrophage (CD68) staining and morphology. Regions containing foam cells in alveolar spaces, IMs, as well as representative areas of lung tissue that contained no macrophages were selected. UV light at 666 nm was used to cleave hCD68 oligos, 525 nm for SMA oligos, and 615 nm for Syto13 oligos. IM ROIs were collected by segmentation to ensure collection of only hCD68+ cells. Cleaved oligos were collected in a 96-well plate for library preparation and were sequenced by the UNC-Chapel Hill Translational Genomics Lab. In total, 71 ROIs were collected and pooled for sequencing by a NextSeq2000 P2 at 2 × 27 base pairs according to NanoString GeoMx specifications (NanoString). Slide preparation, stain optimization and ROI collection were performed by the UNC-Chapel Hill Pathology Services Core.

Analysis of GeoMx DSP data

Raw data were uploaded to the DSP server and preprocessed to generate counts and 3rd quartile (Q3) normalized data. Macrophages are often spread throughout lung tissue at low density. As a result, several ROIs or segments had relatively low nuclei counts (<20). Data were filtered by minimal expression using mean Q3 normalized values of negative control probe sets as a threshold. Count data were used for differential expression analysis using the Bioconductor R package ‘variancePartition’ (1.31.0 Bioconductor). The counts were transformed using voom. The ‘dream’ function allows fitting of mixed-effect models to account for ROIs obtained from the same animal and assay slides as random-effect factors. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes that passed the filters of Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P value (Padj) < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1. The model was: ‘gene_expression ~ 0 + cell + (1|mouse) + (1|Scan_name)’, where ‘cell’ refers to regions enriched for foam cells (‘foamy’), regions with IMs (‘interstitial’), or control regions with no strong hCD68 staining (‘control’). Specific contrasts were then made between each pair of cell groups. We also performed this analysis using RUVseq (v.1.34.0) and Deseq2 (v.1.40.1) and came to qualitatively similar results48,49. Sets of DEGs were tested for enrichment using GO and GSEA from the Bioconductor R package ‘fGSEA’ (v.1.26.0, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp)50. Gene set collections were obtained from Gene Ontology Biological Process51 and Reactome pathways52. GSEA was performed using the React5 database and the top 18 pathways as determined by an adaptive multilevel split Monte Carlo scheme50 were analysed for comparison between uninfected foam cells and uninfected IMs while removing olfactory responses. GSEA of infected foam cells compared to IMs removed viral metabolism-related processes. Cell type proportion deconvolution of GeoMx data was performed using the Bioconductor R package ‘SpatialDecon’ and published human lung scRNA-seq data53. Column and row clustering was done using Euclidean distance. Data were analysed using RStudio (v.2023.030+386). Code is available in GitHub at https://github.com/UNCBARC/Garcia_BLTL_covid.

Immunohistochemical analysis of macrophages

Formalin-fixed (10% formalin) tissues were paraffin embedded and sectioned (5 µm). Sections were mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific), incubated at 60 °C for 1 h, then deparaffinized with xylene and graded ethanol. Following rehydration (10 min double distilled H2O), sections were incubated in DivaDecloaker (BioCare Medical) for antigen retrieval. Non-specific binding was blocked using Background Sniper (BioCare Medical). Tissue sections were incubated with a primary antibody against human CD68 (Dako, KP1, 1:200, M0814) and F4/80 (Invitrogen, BM8, 1:50, MF48000) overnight at 4 °C. Tissue sections incubated with mouse IgG were used as isotype controls. Tissue sections were washed in TBST buffer followed by hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Staining was developed using the MACH-3 polymer system (BioCare Medical) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Vector Laboratories). Tissues were counterstained with haematoxylin and mounted. To verify that the anti-hCD68 antibody did not display cross reactivity with mouse cells, we used it to stain the lungs of NSG mice. We saw no staining with the anti-hCD68 KP1 antibody (Supplementary Fig. 1). As a further control, we confirmed that the lungs of NSG mice stained positive for mouse F4/80 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Staining of mouse macrophages was performed using Ventana’s CC1 (pH 8.5) for antigen retrieval. Then tissue sections were stained with an anti-mouse CD68 antibody (1:100) and developed with DAB followed by haematoxylin counterstaining. Tissue sections were imaged on a Nikon Eclipse Ci microscope using Nikon Elements BR software (v.4.30.01), with a Nikon Digital Sight DS-Fi2 camera. ImageJ/Fiji (v.2.0.0-rc-69/1.51w) and Adobe Photoshop (v.CS6) were used to adjust brightness, contrast and white balance on whole images.

Immunofluorescence analysis of human lung tissue

Tissue sections were incubated at 60 °C for 1 h, then deparaffinized with xylene and graded ethanol. Following rehydration, sections were incubated in DivaDecloaker (BioCare Medical) for antigen retrieval. Following antigen retrieval, tissue sections were incubated with a 10% normal donkey serum solution with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS to block non-specific antibody binding. Tissue sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Primary antibodies were directed against hCD68 (Dako, KP1, 1:50, M0814), PCNA (1:300), MCM2 (1:50), Ki-67 (SP6, 1:200), hCD206 (1:500), adipophilin (1:50), proSP-C (1:500), hACE2 (1:200), SARS nucleocapsid (1:500 or 1:1,000), mCD68 (1:100), cytokeratin-19 (A53-B/A2, 1:250), collagen A1 (1:250) and SMA (1:400). Secondary antibodies were donkey anti-mouse IgG heavy and light chains–AlexaFluor 488 (1:1,000), donkey anti-rabbit IgG heavy and light chains–AlexaFluor 488 (1:1,000), donkey anti-goat IgG heavy and light chains–AlexaFluor 594 (1:1,000), donkey anti-mouse IgG heavy and light chains–AlexaFluor Plus 594 (1:1,000), donkey anti-rabbit IgG heavy and light chains–AlexaFluor Plus 594 (1:1,000), and donkey anti-mouse IgG heavy and light chains–AlexaFluor 647 (1:1,000). Background autofluorescence was quenched using 0.1% Sudan black B in 80% ethanol before staining with DAPI. Appropriate negative (no primary antibody) and isotype (mouse or rabbit IgG) controls were also imaged using the same conditions as matching stained sections. Widefield fluorescence images were taken using an Olympus BX61 upright widefield microscope using Volocity software (v.6.3), with a Hamamatsu ORCA RC camera. Confocal images were taken using a Zeiss LSM710 laser scanning confocal microscope and Volocity software (v.6.3). Confocal z-stack images were deconvolved using Autoquant software (X3.1.3). After deconvolution, maximum projection images were compiled using ImageJ/Fiji (v.2.0.0-rc-69/1.51w). Brightness, contrast and pseudocolouring were adjusted on whole confocal and widefield images using ImageJ/Fiji (v.2.0.0-rc-69/1.51w).

Macrophage counts were obtained by taking random non-overlapping images (tissue navigation to ensure non-overlapping sections was completed using the DAPI channel). Cells were counted using the cell counter plugin in Fiji (v.1.54.g). In areas with extensive foam cell formation where distinct cells were difficult to observe, nuclei were counted. Cells were counted in each image and reported per mm2. Cell counts were not averaged.

Collagen A1 and SMA staining were assessed by taking random non-overlapping images; tissue space was defined using the ROI tool in Fiji/ImageJ (v.1.54.f). The Fiji plugins ‘BX61 ConvertFIJIv3_2’ and ‘RGBmakerv3_2’ were utilized to adjust brightness and contrast equally on all images and to generate a stacked TIFF image. ROIs considered were areas that were free of large airway spaces, cartilage and connective tissue. Image channels were split, and a threshold was applied to remove noise; the DAPI channel was used to define tissue space. ROIs in an image were combined and measured using the ‘Measure’ function to obtain the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). The percent change in fluorescence intensity was calculated using the MFI of SMA or collagen A1 staining in donor-matched uninfected human lung implants. Uninfected human lung implants were collected at the same time or close to the same time as SARS-CoV-2 infected human lung implants were collected and used as the basis for all quantifications.

Macrophage characterization

We quantified total macrophage numbers on the basis of positive hCD68 staining. Foam cell numbers were quantified using the following criteria: positive hCD68 staining, their relative size, which is significantly larger than IMs, and their location in alveolar lumen. IMs were quantified using similar criteria: positive hCD68 staining, their relatively small but homogeneous size, and their interstitial location. Macrophage cell sizes were determined using the Measure function in FIJI/ImageJ (v.1.54.f). The size threshold for foam cells was two standard deviations above the mean size of interstitial macrophages, 68.17 µm2. Representative images from each experimental timepoint and condition were analysed in triplicate (Extended Data Fig. 1c).

Picrosirius red staining

Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated as described above. Tissue sections were submersed in Picrosirius red solution (Abcam, ab150681) for 60 min before sections were quickly rinsed in two changes of 0.5% acetic acid solution. Slides were then rinsed and dehydrated in two changes of absolute alcohol. Cover slips were mounted using Cytoseal XYL (Thermo Scientific, 8312-4). Random, non-overlapping images were taken using an Olympus BX61 upright widefield microscope using Volocity software (v.6.3), with a Retiga camera. A colour composite image was generated and channels were then split in Fiji (v.1.54.f). A threshold was applied to the green channel. Mean pixel intensity was quantified by first defining the size of the area that contained human lung tissue and not open space. Open spaces in the tissue were masked. The Measure function was then utilized to determine the mean pixel intensity in areas that contained human tissue. As mean pixel values were inverted, the values were subtracted from the highest possible pixel value and percent change was calculated against mean pixel intensity of images from donor-matched uninfected human lung implants. Uninfected human lung implants were collected at the same time or close to the same time as SARS-CoV-2 infected human lung implants were collected and used as the basis for all quantifications.

Electron microscopy

Small pieces of human lung tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.15 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, for 2 h at room temperature. Tissues were next placed in 10% formalin for 7 days. Samples were washed in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), post fixed with 1% cacodylate-buffered osmium tetroxide, washed with 0.05 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.0) and treated with 1% tannic acid in 0.05 M sodium cacodylate buffer. After washing and dehydrating, the tissues were placed through two exchanges of propylene oxide before being embedded in PolyBed 812 epoxy resin. Sections (70 nm) were cut, mounted on mesh copper grids, and stained with 4% aqueous uranyl acetate and Reynolds’ lead citrate. Images were taken using a JEOL JEM 1230 transmission electron microscope operating at 80 kV (JEOL), with a Gatan Orius SC1000 CCD digital camera and Gatan Microscopy Suite software (v.3.0, Gatan).

Bulk RNA-sequencing

RNA-sequencing analysis of BLT-L mice was performed using the protocol followed for LoM15. In brief, human lung tissues from mice were collected in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher) and kept at 4 °C for 24 h before storage at −80 °C. Samples were thawed and homogenized using a MagNA Lyser (Roche) for 30 s at 4,000 g in a tube containing 1 mm glass beads and 1 ml Trizol. Homogenate was stored at −80 °C before RNA extraction. RNA was extracted using a Trizol Plus RNA extraction kit (Thermo Fisher), followed by quantification using a Qubit RNA assay kit (Thermo Fisher) and quality checks using a Bioanalyzer RNA600 Nano kit (Agilent). Libraries were constructed in two batches using 1 mg RNA of each sample. Batch 1 was performed with a single index NEBNext Ultra II library prep kit (NEB) with poly(A) RNA selection. Due to a small amount of observed barcode hopping54, we used dual index KAPA RNA HyperPrep (Roche) for the second batch. Barcoded libraries were sequenced using a Novaseq 6000 system at 2 × 100 bp (Illumina). RNA samples from day 6 were prepared using both methods and sequenced to provide a control for comparing across batches. After demultiplexing, reads were mapped to the human, mouse and SARS-CoV-2 reference genomes (GRCh38.p13, GRCm38.p6 M25 and NC_045512, respectively) using the BBsplit function in BBmap (v.38.86). We used ComBat-seq to compensate for batch effects among library and sequencing batches55. For visualization, we corrected TPM values using batch-corrected tximport counts and tximport per-sample effective gene lengths (courtesy of Michael Love, personal communication; https://support.bioconductor.org/p/91218/#9143066). Except as noted, all downstream analyses used human mapped reads. Data were analysed using RStudio (v2023.030+386). Code is available in GitHub at https://github.com/UNCBARC/Garcia_BLTL_covid.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to determine sample size or randomization of mice/samples to experimental groups. Investigators were blinded for macrophage counts in SARS-CoV-2-infected rhesus macaques and MA10-infected mice. Counts of total macrophages and foam cells and BLT-L human lung implant viral titres were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by correction for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate and the two-stage step-up method of ref. 56. Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism (v.10.2.1 (395)). Additional statistical details are in the GeoMx and bulk RNA-seq sections.
