Sex differences in longitudinal changes of episodic memory-related brain activity and cognition in cognitively unimpaired older adults with a family history of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Alexandria D. Samson, Sricharana Rajagopal, Stamatoula Pasvanis, Sylvia Villeneuve, The PREVENT-AD Research Group, Anthony R. McIntosh, M. Natasha Rajah
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Sex-specific declines in cognition and memory.•Sex similarities in longitudinal retrieval-related episodic memory brain activity.•Changes in episodic memory retrieval brain patterns evident in males only.•Cognitive reserve effects may explain our null findings in females.•Preclinical sex-specific changes potentially involved in AD were found.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia (Gauthier et al., 2022). It is a progressive, fatal, neurodegenerative disorder that is biologically characterized by the presence of senile plaques (amyloid-β proteins) and neurofibrillary tangles (tau proteins) in the brain (i.e., neuropathology) (Scheltens et al., 2021). AD-related neuropathology is apparent decades before the onset of clinical symptoms (Jack et al., 2013, Meyer et al., 2019) and affects brain regions implicated in episodic memory - defined as the ability to encode, store and retrieve past personal experiences in rich spatial and temporal contextual detail (Adamson et al., 2011, Bäckman et al., 2001, Buckner, 2004, Dickerson et al., 2004, Tulving, 1972, Westlye et al., 2012). Thus, it is not surprising that episodic memory impairment is one of the earliest clinical symptoms of AD (Almkvist, 1996, Bäckman et al., 2005, Dubois et al., 2010, Sperling et al., 2011) and can be detected several years before the emergence of other observable clinical symptoms (Bäckman et al., 2001, Boraxbekk et al., 2015). Therefore, conducting longitudinal studies of episodic memory and related brain function in cognitively unimpaired adults who possess non-modifiable AD risk factors, such as individuals with a first-degree family member with AD (+FH) (Donix et al., 2012b, Haussmann et al., 2018, Mohs et al., 1987) and/or apolipoprotein E ε4 (+APOE ε4) carriers (Liu et al., 2013), holds the potential to detect early changes in brain mechanisms associated with episodic memory decline and enhance our understanding of the natural progression of AD.

Several cross-sectional studies have explored how +FH and +APOE ε4 status affects episodic memory in cognitively unimpaired adults. Generally, cross-sectional studies have not detected significant group differences in episodic memory task performance between cognitively unimpaired adults with versus without these risk factors (O’Donoghue et al., 2018; but see Striepens et al., 2011). In contrast, task fMRI studies have consistently reported group differences in medial temporal lobe (MTL) and hippocampal activity during the performance of episodic memory tasks in +FH compared to -FH (Fleisher, 2005, Johnson, 2006, Rajah et al., 2017) and in +APOE ε4 compared to -APOE ε4 (Bondi et al., 2005, Bookheimer et al., 2000, Borghesani et al., 2008) cognitively unimpaired older adults. In one longitudinal study, Donix et al., (2012a) reported baseline differences in cognitive performance, including on episodic memory tasks, between +FH, compared to -FH cognitively unimpaired older adults, but not in +APOE ε4 compared to -APOE ε4 adults. Specifically, +FH adults had lower cognitive scores at baseline compared to -FH adults. However, no longitudinal declines in cognition were observed in either at-risk group. To our knowledge, no prior task fMRI study has explored longitudinal changes in the neural correlates in cognitively unimpaired +FH older adults, with and/or without APOE ε4 genotype. Also, it is important to note that the majority of studies to date have used verbal recognition memory paradigms (e.g., Han et al., 2007, Lind et al., 2006) that may be insensitive in detecting early deficits in episodic memory and related brain functions. Using paradigms that are more sensitive to detecting episodic memory deficits, i.e. associative memory tasks (Sperling et al., 2010) and object-location spatial context memory tasks (Cansino, 2009), in a longitudinal design could provide additional insight into the natural progression of episodic memory changes in cognitively unimpaired adults at elevated risk of developing AD.

Importantly, females make up 70 % of AD cases worldwide (Alzheimer’s Association, 2023, World Health, 2023). This may be partly explained by females' longer life expectancy (Mielke, 2018). However, there is also emerging evidence that females may be more susceptible to developing AD neuropathology, such as the abnormal accumulation of amyloid-β and tau proteins in the brain (Perl, 2010). For example, one study found that compared to age-matched healthy males, healthy females with elevated levels of amyloid-β in the brain had higher levels of tau protein deposition in the entorhinal cortex, a region involved in episodic memory (Buckley et al., 2019).

Recent work indicates that there may also be sex differences in the effect of +FH and +APOE ε4 carrier status (Green, 2002), on increasing AD risk. One study showed that +FH status increased females’ risk of developing AD by 10 % compared to their male counterparts (Green, 2002). Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated that almost half of cognitively unimpaired +FH older males and females (Mage = 74.5 years) met the criteria for preclinical AD based on AD-like cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker levels compared to 21 % of –FH age-matched controls (Lampert et al., 2013). Thus, it is evident that individuals with +FH are at high risk of developing the disease themselves compared to –FH individuals (Green, 2002, Loy et al., 2014). Moreover, female +APOE ε4 carriers are at a greater risk of developing AD at a younger age compared to male +APOE ε4 carriers (Altmann et al., 2014, Fisher et al., 2018, Gamache et al., 2020, Poirier et al., 1993). However, in a recent review (Subramaniapillai et al., 2021), it was found that greater educational attainment reduced older females’ risk of developing AD. This was not observed in older males. Therefore, in addition to there being sex differences in AD prevalence and risk, there may also be sex differences in what factors help to support memory and brain aging. Nonetheless, the interplay between sex and other non-modifiable AD risk factors (i.e., +FH and +APOE ε4) on longitudinal episodic memory and related brain function in cognitively unimpaired older adults is less understood.

Our goal was to investigate whether there are sex differences in longitudinal changes in cognition and episodic memory-related brain activity in cognitively unimpaired +FH older adults. To this aim, we conducted a task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study in which participants were scanned at both encoding and retrieval phases of an object-location (spatial source) episodic memory task. This task was chosen because deficits in spatial memory are typically seen in the early stages of AD progression (Hallab et al., 2020). The task design allowed us to examine longitudinal changes in +FH older adults ability to correctly reject novel items as ‘new’ (novelty detection), correct spatial source retrieval, and correct recognition of objects without retrieval of spatial context (item recognition) (Bäckman et al., 2005, Sperling et al., 2011). Changes in neuropsychological scores over two years in cognitively unimpaired +FH older adults were also analyzed, as well as the potential effect of +APOE ε4 carrier status. We hypothesized that, consistent with prior studies, we would observe greater episodic memory decline in +FH and/or +APOE ε4 females compared to males, and this would be related to longitudinal changes in the MTL, prefrontal, and midline cortical activity at encoding and/or retrieval.

Methods

Participants and protocol

Data used in this study were obtained from the Pre-symptomatic Evaluation of Experimental or Novel Treatments for Alzheimer’s Disease (PREVENT-AD; https://douglas.research.mcgill.ca/stop-ad-centre) program. Enrollment criteria for the program and a description of the study protocol and tasks performed by the participants are described elsewhere (Tremblay-Mercier et al., 2020). All participants included in the study were within 15 years of age prior to that of one parent or multiple siblings who were diagnosed with sporadic AD or a condition suggesting Alzheimer’s-like dementia (see below for details on determination of +FH status). Task fMRI episodic memory behavioural performance and neuropsychological data from baseline (Month 0) and approximate 2-year follow-up (Month 24) visits were used in the current analysis. Only participants from the PREVENT-AD program data release 5.0 (i.e., up until November 5th, 2020) were included. The average time between baseline and follow-up visits was 24.7 ± 1.5 months (range 21.2 months to 32.3 months). Event-related task fMRI data were collected for 196 participants at both time points. Two participants were excluded due to too much motion (>1 mm framewise displacement) during scanning. The final sample was 194 older adults (54 males and 140 females; Mage at baseline = 63.6 ± 4.9 years; Meducation = 15.0 ± 3.5 years). Individuals’ biological sex was determined by self-report.

Determination of family history (+FH) status

A brief questionnaire from the Cache County Study on Memory Health and Aging (Tschanz et al., 2013) was used to determine whether all participants had a parent or multiple siblings with AD. Briefly, participants were asked to provide information about their biological parents’ and/or siblings’ memory problems, the age at which the memory problems began, and whether a physician’s diagnosis was received. Each relative was classified as having suspected AD if a physician had given a diagnosis of AD or, if they were lacking a diagnosis, a relative must have had memory problems that worsened over time, causing limitations to daily activities (Hayden, 2009) and were not an obvious consequence of a stroke or other sudden disease or condition.

Apolipoprotein e (APOE) genotyping

As previously described (Gosselin et al., 2016), APOE genotyping was completed by drawing blood. Genomic DNA was isolated from 200 μl of the blood sample using QIA Symphony and the DNA Blood Mini QIA kid (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). APOE genotype profiling was performed through PCR, followed by pyrosequencing with PyroMark Q96 (Qiagen, Toronto, ON, Canada). For further detail on the methods used for this cohort, see Tremblay-Mercier et al. (2020).

Neuropsychological tests

The Alzheimer-Dementia Eight Scale (AD8; Galvin et al., 2005) was used for dementia screening. It is typically completed by a study partner as the assessment relies on observations and insights of someone familiar with the individual’s daily life (e.g., spouse). The assessment was designed to assess general cognition as well as changes in cognitive function and behaviour over time. Only participants who were screened as cognitively unimpaired were included in the current study. We did not analyze changes in AD8 scores given the limited range in our cognitively unimpaired sample.

Cognitive abilities were assessed at both time points using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neurocognitive Status (RBANS; Randolph et al., 1998). The RBANS measures five different cognitive domains; immediate memory, visuospatial/constructional, language, attention, and delayed memory. Each domain/index consisted of two to four subtests. The immediate memory index had a list learning and a story memory test, the visuospatial/constructional index had a figure copy and a line orientation test, the language index had a picture naming and semantic fluency test, the attention index included a digital span and a coding test, and the delayed memory test had a list recall, list recognition, story recall, and figure recall tests. The total RBANS was derived from the index scores. The RBANS was completed by the participant, and the scores were adjusted for 60 – 69 years. At the follow-up visit, every participant completed a different version of the RBANS to prevent practice effects.

MRI data acquisitions

Blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) images were acquired during the encoding and retrieval phases of a spatial source memory paradigm using a single-shot T2*-weighted gradient echo-planar imaging pulse sequence with TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, and FOV = 256 mm x 256 mm. Brain volumes with 32 oblique slices of 4 mm thickness (with no slice gap) were acquired along the anterior-posterior commissural plane with an in-plane resolution of 4x4mm. T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired after the encoding phase of the fMRI task using a 3D gradient echo MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, 176 1 mm sagittal slices, 1x1x1mm voxels, FOV = 256 mm x 256 mm) and were used for image normalization.

Task fMRI: Behavioural protocol

Details of the task fMRI protocol have been published previously (Rabipour et al., 2021, Tremblay-Mercier et al., 2020). In brief, task fMRI was conducted on a 3 T Siemens Trio scanner at the Douglas Institute Brain Imaging Centre in Montreal, Canada. Participants were asked to lie supine while wearing a standard 12-channel MRI coil. Task stimuli were back-projected and visible to participants via a mirror mounted inside the head coil. Participants that required glasses for visual acuity wore corrective plastic glasses. A mixed rapid event-related design (Dale & Buckner, 1997) was employed as participants performed an object-location spatial source memory task programmed in E-Prime version 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Inc.). Participants were scanned during the encoding and retrieval phases. At encoding, participants were asked to memorize a series of 48 coloured line drawings of common objects from the Bank of Standardized Stimuli (BOSS) database (Brodeur, Guérard, & Bouras, 2014) and their spatial location (i.e., left or right of a central fixation cross). Each object was presented for 2000 ms followed by a variable inter-trial interval (ITI durations of 2220, 4400, or 8800 ms; mean ITI = 5.13 s) to add jitter to the fMRI data collection. By introducing jitter to the fMRI experimental design, the statistical power of the analysis is enhanced and collinearity is effectively mitigated between event timing and the BOLD response (Dale & Buckner, 1997). Following a 20-minute break, during which a structural MRI was acquired, participants performed the retrieval phase. They were presented with 96 objects; 48 “old” stimuli (i.e., presented in the encoding phase) and 48 “new” stimuli (i.e., novel objects), in randomized order. Each object was presented in the centre of the screen for 3000 ms, with variable ITI (2200, 4400, 8800 ms). See Fig. 1 for more information on the fMRI episodic memory task. All participants used two four-button inline fibre optic response boxes to choose between four-alternative answers: i) “The object is FAMILIAR, but you don’t remember the location”; ii) “You remember the object and, it was previously on the LEFT”; iii) “You remember the object, and it was previously on the RIGHT”; iv) “The object is NEW”. Thus (i) responses reflected item recognition if it was an “old” object; (ii) and (iii) responses reflected source correct if it was an “old” object and both the object and location were recollected correctly, but if the location was incorrectly recollected this was a source misattribution; and (iv) responses reflected either correct rejection if the object was “new” object or miss if the object was “old”. Statistical analysis

Neuropsychological and task-fMRI behavioural analyses

Neuropsychological test data and fMRI episodic memory behavioural data were analyzed using SPSS v29 (IBM Statistics). Three separate repeated multivariate analysis of variances (MANOVAs) were used to assess the participant’s RBANS sub-scores and total score, task fMRI episodic memory accuracy scores, and task fMRI episodic memory RTs, respectively. These analyses were run separately due to their variable N’s available for each measure. For all the models, time (M0, M24) and self-reported sex (male, female) were the independent variables, while the neuropsychological or episodic memory measures were the dependent variables. RBANS data was available for 191 participants (females: N = 138, males: N = 53) and task fMRI episodic memory performance (accuracy and RTs) were available for 194 participants (females: N = 140, males: N = 54) across both time points. For the episodic memory task mean accuracy scores and RTs for the following six response types were assessed: 1) source correct; 2) item recognition; 3) correct rejection; 4) source misattributions; 5) false alarms; 6) misses.

Preprocessing of fMRI data

Statistical Parametric Mapping software version 12 (SPM12; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) and the Pairwise Longitudinal Registration toolbox were used for longitudinal preprocessing of the fMRI data. Images from the first 10 s of scanning were discarded to allow equilibration of the magnetic field. Each participant's fMRI scans were motion-corrected within each longitudinal session and then registered across the two longitudinal sessions. The T1 scans from the two sessions were registered using SPM’s Pairwise Longitudinal Registration toolbox, and a longitudinal T1 template was created for each participant. The motion-corrected fMRI scans were then co-registered to the longitudinal T1 scan. A study-specific template was created by averaging all participants’ longitudinal T1 scans using the Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie (DARTEL) toolbox in SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The participants' motion-corrected, co-registered fMRI scans were then normalized to the study-specific template at 4x4x4mm voxel resolution and smoothed using an 8 mm full-width half maximum (FWHM) isotropic Gaussian kernel. The normalization step included an affine registration to a Tissue Probability Map (TPM) provided by SPM to ensure that the normalized scans were closely aligned to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space (Dale, Fischl, & Sereno, 1999). Participants were excluded from further analyses if their head motion exceeded 4 mm in the x, y, or z axis during the encoding and retrieval fMRI task phases and/or if participants' head motion could not be sufficiently repaired, resulting in distorted brain images, as judged by the examiner.

Task fMRI analyses

Multivariate task partial least squares (T-PLS) was used to conduct longitudinal event-related task fMRI analysis (McIntosh, Chau, & Protzner, 2004) using PLSGUI software (https://www.rotman-baycrest.on.ca/index.php?section=84). Two between-group longitudinal T-PLS analyses were run to identify: 1) Subsequent memory analysis - changes in brain activity during the episodic encoding across time that was associated with subsequent successful retrieval “hits”, 2) Retrieval analysis - changes in brain activity during successfully episodic retrieval “hits” and novelty detection (correct rejections) across time. For these analyses we pooled encoding and retrieval event onsets for the following condition types to reflect encoding “hits” and retrieval “hits” = source correct + item recognition + source misattributions. The rationale for combining these response types into a single ‘hit’ event-types was to ensure we had enough individual events (total # of event onsets > 9) to accurately model episodic encoding and retrieval at baseline and follow-up. Other events, such as misses, were not included in analyses because there were too few events for most participants (Table S1 in Supplementary).

The fMRI data matrix was stacked by group (i.e., sex) and event-type /task condition wherein conditions were nested within sex. The conditions within sex were stacked as follows for the T-PLS of subsequent memory analysis: i) encoding events in which participants subsequently had a ‘hits’ response at baseline (ENC-Hits-M0); ii) encoding ‘hits’ at follow-up (ENC-Hits-M24). The conditions within sex were stacked as follows for the T-PLS retrieval analysis: i) retrieval ‘hits’ events at baseline (RET-Hits-M0); ii) events for which participants correctly identified novel objects as new (correct rejections) during the retrieval phase at baseline (RET-CR-M0); iii) retrieval ‘hits’ at follow-up (RET-Hits-M24); iv) correct rejections at follow-up (RET-CR-M24). The data for the group of females were stacked above the data for the group of males. The stacked matrices contained the fMRI data for each event onset (time lag = 0) and seven subsequent time lags, representing a total of 14 s of activation after event onset (repetition time [TR] = 2 s * 7 = 14 s). There was no signal at lag 0 because data were baseline corrected to the event onset. Therefore, the signal in subsequent lags was expressed as the percentage deviation from event onset.

The fMRI data were grand mean-centred column-wise for the episodic memory task’s encoding and retrieval conditions. The mean-centred matrix was then decomposed with singular value decomposition to identify the dominant patterns of cross-covariance between the fMRI data and task conditions as a set of mutually orthogonal latent variables (LVs). T-PLS produced LVs consisting of three elements: i) a diagonal matrix of singular values indicating the proportion of the covariance attributed to each LV (cross-block covariance); ii) design saliences represented the difference between tasks across groups and time; and iii) a singular image with the numerical weights (brain saliences) assigned to each voxel at each TR/time lag (i.e., the contribution of a region at each TR), which indicates the statistical pattern that corresponds to the contrast effect identified by the design salience plot.

T-PLS also outputs temporal brain scores, which are the dot products of the participant’s singular image brain activity and the paired design salience on a particular LV at each time lag (Krishnan et al., 2011). To interpret the LV, the brain scores are plotted for each task condition alongside the paired singular image of each LV. We report the temporal brain scores and the peak coordinates from time lags at which the temporal brain score profiles maximally differentiated (lags 2––5 post condition onset, 4 – 10 s after condition onset, spatial extent threshold = 5 voxels) for each significant LV. The peak coordinates were converted to Talairach space using the icbm2tal transform (Lancaster et al., 2007) as implemented in GingerAle 3.0.2 (Eickhoff et al., 2009). Brodmann area localizations were identified with the help of the Talairach Deamon database system (Lancaster et al., 2007), which utilizes the Talairach and Tournoux atlas (Talairach & Tournoux, 1988).

The significance of T-PLS results was assessed using permutation testing for the singular values, and the stability of saliences was assessed with bootstrapping. We report T-PLS results that were significant at P = 0.05 based on 1000 permutations and bootstrap ratios of ± 3.3 (approximately equivalent to a z-score confidence interval of ∼ 99 %) based on 500 bootstraps. To validate our interpretations of the T-PLS findings repeated MANOVAs were performed on the brain scores of significant LVs; the LV brain scores for specific conditions were the dependent variables, while sex (males, females), time (M0, M24) were the independent variables. For primary and post-hoc analyses, we used a significance threshold of P = 0.05.

Neuropsychological performance results

The MANOVA performed on the RBANS measures identified significant main effects of sex (F (6, 374) = 4.621, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.069) and time (F (6, 374) = 3.596, P =.002, ηp2 = 0.055) but the interaction effect was not significant (P =.21). The between subject effects from the MANOVA indicated that the main effect of sex in the MANOVA was due to there being sex differences in immediate memory (F (1, 379) = 19.865, P <.001), language (F (1, 379) = 4.766, P =.030), delayed memory (F (1, 379) = 12.249, P <.001), and total RBANS (F (1, 379) = 8.341, P =.004) scores; females performed better than males on all these measures. The main effect of time in the MANOVA was primarily influenced by the decline in RBANS language sub-scores (F (1,379) = 10.039, P =.002) and delayed memory sub-scores (F (1,379) = 4.84, P =.028). For the language sub-scores, there was a decrease in performance demonstrated for both sexes over time while the delayed memory sub-scores demonstrated an increase in performance for both sexes over time. Interestingly, the two-way ANOVA on RBANS language sub-scores also revealed a significant sex-by-time effect (F (1,379) = 7.072, P =.008), whereby the females experienced a steeper decline in language scores compared to the males. To summarize, males performed worse than females on the RBANs at both time points; both sexes exhibited declines in language and increases in delayed memory over time; and the decline in language ability was steeper in females compared to males. Post-hoc analyses. Given that +APOE ε4 carrier status is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD (Loy et al., 2014); +APOE ε4 genotype is more common among females than males (Yaffe et al., 2000); and there is growing evidence of sex-specific effects of APOE ε4 genotype on AD risk, such that females with APOE ε4 genotype may be at greater risk of AD than males (Altmann et al., 2014, Fisher et al., 2018, Gamache et al., 2020, Mofrad et al., 2020, Poirier et al., 1993), we conducted post-hoc analyses to explore if there were sex*APOE ε4 genotype or sex*APOE ε4 genotype*time interaction effects on neuropsychological scores for which we identified significant sex and/or time effects on. Of the 54 males included in the study 21 were +APOE ε4 and 33 were -APOE ε4. Of the 140 females included in the study 51 were +APOE ε4 and 89 were -APOE ε4.

A MANOVA was performed on RBANS total score, immediate memory, delayed memory and language scores with sex, time, and APOE ε4 carrier status as the independent variables. No significant sex*APOE ε4 genotype or sex*APOE ε4 genotype *time interactions were identified.

Considering the superior performance of females in our sample on various neuropsychological scores, as well as the high educational attainment in our sample, we conducted post-hoc analyses to explore the association of education on these measures. A MANOVA was performed on RBANS total, immediate memory, delayed memory and language scores with sex, time, and years of education (EDU) as the independent variables. To mitigate any potential skewness, years of education were log-transformed. EDU had 18 levels in this analysis as it was a continuous variable. The goal of this post-hoc was to examine if education was associated with neuropsychological performance in males and/or females. We observed a significant sex*EDU interaction (F (48, 1284) = 1.92, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.067). The between subjects effects indicated this significant interaction was due to there being a sex*EDU interaction on language scores (F (12, 321) = 2.01, P =.023, ηp2 = 0.07), and RBANS total scores (F (12, 321) = 2.47, P =.004, ηp2 = 0.085).

We also conducted within-group (sex) MANOVAs to assess whether the effects were stronger within one group. There was a significant effect of EDU on test scores in males (F (48, 324) = 2.03, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.229) and females (F (68, 960) = 2.195, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.135). In males, this was due to EDU having a significant effect on language (F (12, 81) = 2.32, P =.013, ηp2 = 0.255) and delayed memory (F (12, 81) = 1.98, P =.037, ηp2 = 0.227) scores. In females, this was due to EDU having a significant effect on immediate memory (F (17, 240) = 2.34, P =.002, ηp2 = 0.142), delayed memory (F (17, 240) = 3.99, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.220), and RBANS total (F (17, 240) = 4.96, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.260) scores, but no significant effect on language scores (F (17, 240) = 1.586, P =.068, ηp2 = 0.101). Thus, education was significantly associated with most of the RBANS measures within females, except for language (P >.05). Females with higher education performed better on these RBANS sub-scores. Therefore, education benefited RBANS memory scores in both sexes, but the effects were stronger in females based on F and P-values.

Task fMRI behavioural results

The MANOVA conducted on task fMRI accuracy scores identified a significant multivariate effect for sex (F (5,380) = 5.553, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.068) and time (F (5,380) = 4.658, P <.001, ηp2 = 0.058), but no significant interaction effect (P =.291). Tests of between subjects’ effects indicated the significant main effect of sex was due to females having a significantly higher rate of correct rejections (F (1,384) = 13.575, P = <0.001) and item recognition accuracy (F (1,384) = 4.32, P =.038), and having a significantly lower rate of source misattributions (F (1,384) = 17.366, P <.001), false alarms (F (1,384) = 11.428, P <.001), compared to males. The main effect of time resulted from changes in false alarm (F (1,384) = 7.087, P =.008) and correct rejection (F (1,384) = 13.178, P <.001) in both sexes. Males and females exhibited increases in false alarms and declines in correct rejections across time. Interestingly, there was trend for males exhibiting a steeper decline in correct rejections compared to females across time (F (1,327) = 3.744, P =.054). Therefore, females performed better on the task fMRI paradigm than males. In terms of task fMRI RT performance, there were no significant effects of sex, time, or sex*time interaction on RT (all P >.05).

Post-hoc analyses. A post-hoc MANOVA was conducted to test for sex*APOE ε4 genotype and sex*APOE ε4 genotype*time interactions in fMRI episodic memory accuracy for correct rejections, false alarms, and source misattributions. No significant interaction was identified. We also explored the effect of education on fMRI episodic memory accuracy due to the females generally outperforming the males on the episodic memory task. Two within sex MANCOVAs were run on correct rejection, false alarms and source misattribution accuracy scores with time (M0, M24) as the independent variable and education as the covariate. There was no significant effect of education on episodic memory accuracy in males (F < 1), and in females there was a trend a (F (3,275) = 2.34, P =.074), due to higher education being related to lower rate of source misattributions (F (1,277) = 5.270, P =.022). Therefore, there was a trend towards higher education being associated with better task fMRI accuracy in females.

Task fMRI results

Two participants were excluded from the following T-PLS analyses because of extreme brain score values. Removing these participants did not change the main results; therefore, the final sample included 192 participants (52 males and 140 females) for these analyses.

There were no significant LVs extracted by the T-PLS analysis looking at encoding conditions across time and sex (P >.75). Two significant LVs were identified by the T-PLS retrieval conditions analysis. The first significant LV (P <.001) accounted for 78.70 % of the cross-block covariance and depicted a main effect of condition (Fig. 2). This was confirmed by a post-hoc paired t-test for condition type which indicated that there were significant differences in brain activity during hits compared to correct rejections (t (383) = −19.099, P <.001). Post-hoc two-way ANOVA performed on the LV1 brain scores for hits and correct rejections indicated there was no significant effect of sex or time (P >.05) in the pattern of activity identified by LV1. In both females and males, there was greater activation in positive salience regions during successful novelty detections (correct rejections) compared to successful retrieval (hits) at baseline and follow-up. Positive salience regions included bilateral ventral occipital cortices, right superior temporal gyrus, and left medial prefrontal, precentral, and postcentral gyri. In contrast, both females and males exhibited greater activation in negative salience regions during successful retrieval compared to novelty detection. Post-hoc analyses. We conducted post-hoc analyses to assess the potential interaction of sex*APOE ε4 genotype and sex*APOE ε4 genotype*time on fMRI brain activity. Two MANOVAs were used to examine the differences in brain scores for each significant LV with sex, APOE ε4 carrier status, and time as the independent variables. The post-hoc analysis of T-PLS LV1 demonstrated no significant sex by APOE ε4 carrier status effects. Given that LV2 was a male-specific effect, we conducted a MANOVA to determine if there was a significant APOE ε4 genotype and/or APOE ε4 genotype*time effect on LV2 brain scores within males. There was a significant effect of APOE ε4 genotype on LV2 brain scores in males (F(2, 99) = 4.202, P =.018), but no significant APOE ε4 genotype*time interaction. This was due to +APOE ε4 males exhibiting lower mean brain scores across conditions and time, compared to -APOE ε4 males (Figure S1 in Supplementary).

Within sex repeated MANCOVAs were also conducted to determine if brain scores from LV1 and LV2 varied by education in males and females. For LV1 we observed a significant effect of education on brain scores in females (F (2, 276) = 4.034, P =.019), but not males (F < 1). This was due to females with higher education exhibiting less negative brain scores during retrieval hits (F (1, 277) = 7.852, P =.005). Since LV2 effects were only apparent in males, we explored if years of education covaried with brain scores for LV2 only in males. We did not observe a significant effect of education on LV2 brain scores in males.

Discussion

In the current study, we investigated whether there were sex differences in longitudinal changes in episodic memory and related brain activity in cognitively unimpaired +FH older adults. Given the higher prevalence of AD in older females compared to males (Mielke, 2018), and past work suggesting +FH and +APOE ε4 status may have more detrimental effects on females compared to males (Green, 2002, Buckley et al., 2019), we hypothesized that +FH females would exhibit more episodic memory decline across time and show associated changes in the MTL, prefrontal, and midline cortical activity at encoding and/or retrieval compared to +FH males. However, our results contrasted with these predictions, whereby, in general, +FH females outperformed +FH males on episodic memory and cognitive abilities across time. Furthermore, +FH males, not +FH females, demonstrated a sex-specific longitudinal change in brain activity which may have been related to the observed episodic memory declines.

Overall, +FH males performed significantly worse than +FH females at baseline and at follow-up on the neuropsychological assessments of episodic memory and on the task fMRI episodic memory paradigm. This finding aligns with previous research involving cognitively unimpaired older adults, whereby older females exhibit superior performance compared to their male counterparts in various cognitive and episodic memory tasks, particularly those that demand fewer spatial abilities (e.g., Asperholm et al., 2019, McCarrey et al., 2016, Munro et al., 2012). However, we did not observe negative effects of +APOE ε4 status on task fMRI performance or RBANS performance at either time point in males or females. In relation to the effect of sex on task fMRI episodic memory performance, we found that +FH males exhibited higher false alarm and source misattribution rates and lower correct rejections and recognition rates compared to +FH females at baseline and at follow-up visits. However, both +FH males and females exhibited significant increases in false alarms and decreases in the ability to make correct rejections. False alarms are when one erroneously labels a ‘new’ object as ‘old’; whereas, making a correct rejection refers to when correctly identifies a ‘new’ item as ‘new’. Thus, the observed increase in false alarms and decrease in correct rejections across time in +FH females and males reflect a deficit in discriminating ‘new’ from ‘old’. Past neuropsychological studies suggest that increases in false alarms reflect prefrontal dysfunction (Kahn et al., 2004, Swick et al., 2006). Based on the dual process model of episodic retrieval, this may reflect a reduction in cognitive control processes and a lowering of the criterion, or signal detection threshold, needed for identifying a stimulus at ‘old’ (Belleville et al., 2011, Dobbins et al., 2000, Naveh-Benjamin et al., 2009). Deficits in correct rejections have been interpreted as reflecting hippocampal and MTL dysfunctions (Tautvydaitė et al., 2022) and a bias towards making familiarity and/or gist-based memory retrieval decisions (Eichenbaum et al., 2007). Previous research has highlighted increases in false alarms and impairments in novelty processing in amnestic mild cognitive impairment and AD patients (Bastin et al., 2019, Bennett et al., 2006, Meyer et al., 2022). Taken together, the current task fMRI results indicate that longitudinal changes in feelings of familiarity towards new stimuli are an early sign of episodic memory decline in +FH older adults.

The event-related fMRI results suggest that these behavioural declines in episodic memory were not related to changes in brain activity at encoding. However, a significant main effect of condition and a significant sex-by-time interaction were identified during episodic retrieval. Specifically, LV1 from the retrieval T-PLS analysis indicated that +FH males and females exhibited similar patterns of event-related brain activity during successful retrieval compared to novelty detection across time. In both sexes, successful retrieval was associated with increased activity in the parahippocampal cortex, bilateral frontoparietal, and posterior medial cortices at both time points. This is consistent with the behavioural results, which indicated that both sexes exhibited no significant differences in spatial source and recognition retrieval accuracy across time. Moreover, our results corroborate findings from previous fMRI studies of episodic memory and indicate that activity in frontoparietal, posterior medial cortices, and posterior parahippocampal gyrus at retrieval is associated with successful episodic retrieval (Diana et al., 2007, Rugg and Vilberg, 2013, Wagner et al., 2005). Prior task fMRI studies of AD have reported hyperactivation of frontoparietal regions during the performance of episodic memory tasks in AD patients, compared to controls (Pariente et al., 2005, Dhanjal and Wise, 2014, Sperling et al., 2010). Similarly, increased precuneus/posterior medial activity at encoding and/or retrieval has also been associated with greater AD risk (McDonough, Festini, & Wood, 2020). Future research comparing longitudinal memory-related activation in -FH, compared to +FH, older adults is necessary to determine if the patterns observed here reflect preclinical hyperactivation of these brain regions. In general, the current findings suggest that cognitively unimpaired older males and females with a family history of AD exhibited maintenance of source and recognition retrieval and related brain activation across time.

LV1 also indicated that successful novelty detection (correct rejections) was associated with increased activity in bilateral occipital cortices at both time points. This is consistent with past work showing the successful differentiation of false from true memories is related to greater activity in the ventral occipital cortex (Dennis et al., 2014). However, as discussed above, both sexes exhibited decreased correct rejections over time, which is in line with the hypothesis that early changes in episodic memory function in adults at risk of developing AD may be observed in altered familiarity and novelty processing (Schoemaker et al., 2014, Koen and Yonelinas, 2014). Moreover, +FH males showed fewer correct rejections than females at both time points, and in +FH males there was a trend toward a steeper decline in correct rejections from the baseline to the follow-up visit in comparison to +FH females. As mentioned previously, declines in novelty processing (i.e., correct rejections), have been seen in AD patients and those in progressive stages of the disease (Bastin et al., 2019, Bennett et al., 2006, Meyer et al., 2022, Tautvydaitė et al., 2022). Therefore our findings suggest that +FH males may be showing a faster rate of cognitive decline compared to +FH females. However, in the section below (“Explanation of null findings in +FH females”), we explain why we believe this sex-specific difference may have been observed, especially when the opposite was hypothesized for this cohort.

In addition to these behavioural observations in +FH males, we identified a sex-specific longitudinal change in brain activity, represented in LV2, which may have contributed to males’ poorer episodic memory performance compared to females. LV2 indicates that males demonstrated greater activity in the left red nucleus and parahippocampal gyrus across event types at baseline compared to follow-up. Increased activity of the red nucleus during a memory retrieval task has been observed in healthy adults (Sung et al., 2022). Also, increased MTL activity during novelty detection has been previously reported in +FH older adults (Johnson, 2006). Here, in +FH males only, we see reduced activity in these regions over time, which was paralleled by a decrease in novelty detection. This decrease in brain activity was complemented by increased activity in the right precuneus and left caudate, prefrontal and claustrum over time. These increases in brain activity at follow-up may reflect a change in neurocognitive approach to the retrieval task in +FH males over time, perhaps as a compensatory response to declines in the red nucleus and MTL activation (Borghesani et al., 2008, Wang et al., 2012, Xu et al., 2008). Alternatively, the increase in prefrontal cortex activity in tandem with the decrease in some episodic memory abilities over time may be a reflection of reduced efficiency/neural dedifferentiation in +FH males, which has been previously reported in cognitively unimpaired older adults (Morcom & Henson, 2018).

Explanation of null findings in +FH females

The current study identified a longitudinal decline in correct rejections in +FH females. In addition, our results indicate that +FH females experienced a more substantial decline in language performance over time when compared to males. However, we did not identify any longitudinal differences in memory-related brain activity in females, as we did in males (LV2). Moreover, in contrast to our hypotheses, +FH females performed better than +FH males on the neuropsychological tests and task fMRI memory paradigm. We, therefore, decided to explore if the high level of education in the current cohort, may have benefitted neuropsychological and task fMRI performance in females more so than males and thus provided greater neuroprotective effects in older +FH females, compared to +FH males, as hypothesized in a recent review by Subramaniapillai et al. (2021). Although there was no significant sex difference in years of education between males and females in this study (females Meducation = 14.71 ± 3.55 years; males Meducation = 15.58 ± 3.37 years), the average participant had high educational attainment (i.e., the average participant had a university education). Yet, on average, only 29 % of adults aged 65 years and older in the United States reported having a university education in 2018 (United States Census Bureau, 2022). Moreover, females aged 65 years old and above are generally less educated than males of the same age (Angrisani, Lee, & Meijer, 2020), suggesting that the high level of education of the females in the current study may have contributed to the null longitudinal findings in +FH females. As such, we conducted post-hoc analyses of the neuropsychological, task fMRI behavioural, and task fMRI activation results to determine if there were sex-specific benefits of education in +FH females compared to +FH males. Detailed findings of our post-hoc analyses can be found in the Results section. Consistent with our post-hoc explanation, we found that higher education was more beneficial to RBANS performance in +FH females than +FH males. Specifically, higher education was related to higher total RBANS, immediate memory, and delayed memory scores in +FH females. Although +FH males with higher education performed better on immediate and delayed memory scores, the effects were weaker than what was observed in females. Moreover, only in +FH females, was there a trend for higher education being related to better task fMRI episodic memory performance accuracy but higher education was not significantly associated with better task fMRI episodic memory performance or task fMRI activation in either +FH males or females. These sex-specific benefits of higher education on RBANS scores in +FH females also corroborates our interpretation that null findings in longitudinal declines in brain activity in +FH female sample may have been due to neuroprotective benefits associated with their high level of education (Stern, 2006). Indeed, Subramaniapillai et al. (2021) found that education may confer greater cognitive reserve in older females than males. As such, the general female advantage over time in memory and cognitive scores, as well as the null findings for females’ retrieval-related brain activity identified in LV2, may be demonstrative of greater cognitive reserve effects experienced by the females in our sample.

Moreover, we also considered how health parameters may have influenced our results, recognizing that certain health/lifestyle factors have previously exhibited associations with more pronounced cognitive decline (e.g., see Duron & Hanon, 2008). However, our dataset was constrained to incorporate a select subset of health and lifestyle variables, specifically encompassing body mass index and blood pressure measurements. Although we did observe a significant sex difference in systolic blood pressure at both time points, with females exhibiting lower blood pressure levels compared to males, we believe that the available health parameters were not sufficiently comprehensive to conduct an in-depth analysis of their impact on our null findings among +FH females compared to +FH males. Future research with similar cohorts would be enhanced by the inclusion of additional health metrics, such as longitudinal changes in cholesterol levels, physical activity levels, and diabetes status, among others, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of how these factors may have influenced the outcomes.

Conclusions

The current study highlights how examining sex differences in longitudinal changes in cognition and brain function within cognitively unimpaired older adults at risk of AD can advance our understanding of AD progression at asymptomatic stages. Given that +FH individuals are at increased risk of developing AD themselves (Wolters et al., 2017), our study suggests that declines in linguistic ability and memory-related novelty detection may be early markers of neurocognitive dysfunction in older adults with this non-modifiable risk factor. In addition, contrary to previous research, we did not observe longitudinal declines in memory-related brain function in +FH females but instead observed such effects in +FH males. We speculate that this may be due to sex differences in the benefits of higher education on neurocognitive function (Subramaniapillai et al., 2021).
