Not All Crescents Are Intramural Hematomas
Authors: Yoshito Kadoya, Kevin Emery Boczar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: computed tomography, imaging, positron emission tomography, vascular disease

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Reguera et al1 describe a 57-year-old man with dizziness, aortic regurgitation, and complete atrioventricular block initially diagnosed with aortic intramural hematoma (IMH). Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) revealed intense aortic wall uptake, shifting the diagnosis to clinically isolated aortitis and redirecting management toward immunosuppression. This case highlights an important diagnostic challenge: distinguishing inflammatory from hemorrhagic aortic pathology on conventional imaging.

The distinction has direct consequences for treatment. Type A (ascending aorta) IMH generally warrants urgent surgical repair, whereas uncomplicated type B (descending aorta) IMH is managed with blood pressure control and serial imaging.2 Aortitis, by contrast, requires immunosuppressive therapy.3 On CT, both entities may present as crescent-shaped aortic wall thickening, an overlap that can mislead clinicians.

Standard teaching holds that IMH shows hyperattenuating crescentic thickening on noncontrast CT without postcontrast enhancement, whereas aortitis exhibits circumferential wall thickening with contrast enhancement (Table 1, Figure 1).4 In this case, however, the crescent-shaped CT appearance reflected an atypical form of isolated aortitis.

The FDG uptake pattern in this case was focal and heterogeneous, unlike the diffuse pattern typically seen in large vessel vasculitis such as giant cell arteritis. Whether clinically isolated aortitis exhibits distinct metabolic imaging features remains an open question. In one comparative study, none of the patients with isolated aortitis showed FDG uptake in the aortic wall, while most of the patients with giant cell arteritis did.5 Additionally, 31% of patients with histopathologically confirmed noninfectious aortitis had no detectable FDG uptake preoperatively.6 These observations indicate that the absence of diffuse uptake should not exclude aortitis, and that focal or absent uptake may characterize a distinct imaging phenotype.

The diagnostic value of 18F-FDG PET/CT is apparent in this case. Markedly elevated uptake in the aortic wall, including the aortomitral junction, provided evidence of active inflammation that CT alone could not delineate.4 Normalization of uptake after immunosuppressive therapy reinforced both the diagnosis and the therapeutic response. When PET/CT is unavailable, cardiac magnetic resonance may serve as an important alternative for detecting aortic wall inflammation.

A noteworthy feature is the association between aortitis and complete atrioventricular block, with partial conduction recovery after immunosuppression. Conduction disturbances are well recognized in conditions affecting the aortic root, but their association with noninfectious aortitis has been rarely reported.7 The anatomical basis involves inflammation of the aortomitral junction, adjacent to the atrioventricular conduction system. The fact that immunosuppression led to partial recovery suggests the disturbance was at least partly reversible, but the need for permanent pacing is a reminder that inflammatory injury may not fully resolve when treatment is delayed.

This case draws attention to clinically isolated aortitis, which is poorly understood and likely underdiagnosed. Unlike classic large vessel vasculitis, it may present without systemic features, with minimally elevated laboratory markers, and disease confined to a single aortic segment.8 The lack of established diagnostic criteria and treatment exposes the limited evidence available and underscores the need for further research.

In summary, the case offers 3 key teaching points. First, crescent-shaped aortic wall thickening on CT should not be automatically attributed to IMH; aortitis must be considered, especially in younger patients or those with atypical features. Second, 18F-FDG PET/CT should be considered early when the pathology may be inflammatory. Third, conduction disease in the context of aortitis could indicate active inflammation with the potential for reversibility, highlighting timely immunosuppressive therapy. Future studies are needed to better characterize the imaging features that distinguish each aortitis phenotype.
