Clinical outcomes of LAN microhook-assisted goniotomy combined with phacoemulsification in mid-to-late primary angle-closure glaucoma with cataract
Authors: Mei Li, Yuanhui Jin, Jian Wu, Jiangjian Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the clinical efficacy of goniotomy assisted by the LAN microhook goniotomy knife (LAN microhook), combined with phacoemulsification, in managing mid-to-late-stage primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) with cataract in a prospective observational context.MethodsA total of 33 patients (53 eyes) diagnosed with mid-to-late-stage PACG and cataract were recruited between February 2024 and November 2024. Preoperative and six-month postoperative data were collected, including intraocular pressure (IOP), antiglaucoma medication use, anterior chamber depth (ACD), peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA, logMAR), and surgical complications.ResultsAt baseline, IOP was 18.26 ± 4.52 mmHg and declined to 14.11 ± 2.87 mmHg at Month 6. The mean number of antiglaucoma medications were 1.91 ± 0.81 preoperatively, and all patients discontinued medications by Month 6. ACD increased from 2.36 ± 0.39 mm preoperatively to 3.68 ± 0.75 mm at Month 6.ConclusionsGoniotomy using the LAN microhook combined with phacoemulsification appears to effectively deepen the anterior chamber, lower IOP, improve visual acuity, and eliminate the need for antiglaucoma medication in patients with mid-to-late-stage PACG and cataract.Keywords: LAN microhook, Intraocular pressure, Goniotomy, Phacoemulsification, Cataract, Primary angle-closure glaucoma

Introduction

Glaucoma is a progressive optic neuropathy characterized by irreversible optic nerve damage, often associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). By 2040, its prevalence is projected to reach 111.8 million, with primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) being predominant in Asia [1, 2].

PACG is primarily caused by anterior chamber angle closure, leading to aqueous humor outflow obstruction and subsequent optic nerve damage. Since glaucomatous damage is irreversible, lowering IOP is the primary treatment goal to prevent disease progression. Treatment options include medication, laser therapy, and surgical interventions. In patients with PACG and cataract, surgical approaches often provide superior outcomes [3].

Traditionally, phacoemulsification combined (phaco) with goniosynechialysis (GSL) has been employed to enhance aqueous humor outflow and lower IOP [4]. However, in cases where synechial closure exceeds 180°, residual trabecular meshwork (TM) damage may limit long-term IOP control [5, 6]. To address this, minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) techniques, such as goniotomy, have been introduced. Studies indicate that around 120° goniotomy effectively reduces IOP in mid-to-late-stage PACG [7], yet evidence regarding the efficacy of a 90° goniotomy remains limited. Various goniotomy tools, including the neodymium: YAG (Nd: YAG) laser, Tanito hook, Kahook Dual Blade (KDB), and LAN microhook, have been utilized for this procedure [8].

Goniotomy techniques differ in whether the TM is incised or excised and in the geometry of the working tip. The LAN microhook is a non-excisional instrument with a single-hook, low-profile, rounded tip. It is an integrated goniotomy–synechialysis device: one end features a low-profile (slimmer), rounded tip for goniotomy, while the opposite end is a micro-textured (sandblasted) goniosynechialysis spatula that facilitates controlled synechialysis. Unlike excisional blades, the LAN microhook enables atraumatic peeling of the TM’s inner wall while preserving Schlemm’s canal (SC). When peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) are present, a Limited GSL can be performed solely to expose the TM, followed immediately by a controlled 90° goniotomy confined to the nasal quadrant under the same gonioscopic view (Fig. 1). The Tanito microhook is likewise non-excisional but differs in tip and shaft geometry and is used only for goniotomy [9], whereas the KDB performs an excisional goniotomy by removing a strip of TM [10]. In PACG, where access to the angle is often limited, a low-profile, single-hook design may facilitate less-traumatic entry and controlled tissue manipulation. These mechanistic distinctions provide the rationale for evaluating phaco combined with LAN microhook-assisted goniotomy (LAN-goniotomy) —a 90° goniotomy confined to the nasal quadrant performed with the LAN microhook in PACG. Evidence from cohorts with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) indicates that excisional goniotomy with the KDB yields similar 12-month outcomes for 90° versus 120° treatment arcs [11]. However, data in PACG—particularly with non-excisional microhooks—remain limited [12].

LAN microhook assisted workflow for combined GSL and goniotomy

In POAG, ab interno microhook trabeculotomy (µLOT)—alone or combined with phacoemulsification—has demonstrated clinically meaningful reductions in IOP and medication burden with acceptable safety. Representative large series and comparative studies using the Tanito microhook corroborate this efficacy [13–15]. The LAN microhook is a newer instrument within this class; to our knowledge (as of August 2025), peer-reviewed POAG outcomes specific to the LAN microhook have not yet been reported.

Accordingly, this study evaluated the safety and efficacy of phaco combined with LAN-goniotomy, defined as a 90° goniotomy confined to the nasal quadrant performed with the LAN microhook in mid-to-late-stage PACG, focusing on IOP, medication burden, anterior chamber depth (ACD), optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the optic nerve head with peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) analysis, visual field indices, and prespecified complications over Month 6.

Methods

This prospective observational study was carried out at Dongyang People’s Hospital to evaluate the clinical utility of the LAN microhook in goniotomy combined with phaco for mid-to-late-stage PACG with cataract. Consecutive patient recruitment took place from February to November 2024. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. 2024-JS-004). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

This was a single-center, single-arm consecutive case series of PACG patients undergoing phaco combined with a 90° nasal, non-excisional microhook goniotomy. No randomized or concurrent control arm (e.g., phaco alone) was included.

When nasal PAS was present, a Limited GSL was performed solely to expose the TM along the planned 90° nasal arc prior to goniotomy; no additional synechialysis was undertaken beyond this area.

The study included patients who met the following criteria: (1) best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) worse than 0.52 logMAR (equivalent to < 0.3 in Snellen decimal), attributed to cataracts, (2) gonioscopic assessment indicating angle closure with PAS extending beyond 180°, (3) IOP exceeding 21 mmHg despite the use of antiglaucoma medication, and (4) evidence of glaucomatous optic neuropathy, characterized by either a cup-to-disc ratio of ≥ 0.7 or an inter-eye asymmetry exceeding 0.2, (5) visual field abnormalities, such as paracentral scotomas, nasal steps, arcuate defects, or tunnel vision, were identified using Humphrey perimetry, with a mean deviation (MD) of ≤ −6 dB [16]– [17]. Patients fulfilling criteria (2), (3), and either (4) or (5) were classified as having mid-to-late-stage PACG.

Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) diagnosed with secondary PACG, (2) a prior history of ocular surgery or trauma, (3) the presence of severe systemic conditions that could interfere with surgical outcomes, or (4) coexisting ocular diseases requiring surgical intervention.

The primary outcome was IOP at prespecified postoperative visits. Secondary outcomes included BCVA, ACD, peripapillary RNFL thickness, antiglaucoma medication burden, and surgical complications.

Comprehensive ophthalmic examinations were performed pre- and postoperatively according to a standardized protocol: slit-lamp biomicroscopy; IOP measurement by Goldmann applanation tonometry; gonioscopy; BCVA assessment; ACD measurement; OCT with peripapillary RNFL thickness analysis; and standard automated perimetry (SAP). Macular OCT was obtained when visual acuity decreased or when protocol triggers occurred. Baseline IOP refers to measurements obtained while patients were still on antiglaucoma medication. All examinations were performed by trained ophthalmologists using standardized protocols.

Postoperative visits were scheduled at Day 1, Week 1, and Months 1, 3, and 6. At each visit, the aforementioned examinations were performed; macular OCT was obtained when indicated.

Prespecified safety endpoints included hyphema; early postoperative IOP spike (≥ 10 mmHg above baseline within the first postoperative week); cystoid macular edema (CME); malignant glaucoma; capsular phimosis; and corneal edema. Hyphema, capsular phimosis, and corneal edema were assessed by slit-lamp biomicroscopy; IOP spike by Goldmann applanation tonometry; CME by macular OCT; and suspected malignant glaucoma by clinical assessment with ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) when indicated. When clinically indicated, gonioscopy was performed to visualize the anterior chamber angle and the goniotomy cleft.

Complications were qualitatively graded as mild (self-limited, no treatment), moderate (managed with medical therapy or office-based laser, without sequelae), or severe (vision-threatening or requiring escalation), and management and resolution were documented for each event.

For primary IOP analyses, visits complicated by clinically significant anterior chamber hyphema or steroid-induced ocular hypertension (SIOH) were excluded a priori as transient postoperative confounders not specific to the procedure; eyes were retained for non-IOP endpoints. All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon under sub-Tenon’s anesthesia. Phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation were performed under the operating microscope. Under direct gonioscopy with an MR-JP20 goniolens (Mingren, China), a Limited nasal goniosynechialysis was performed as needed for exposure. This was followed, within the same gonioscopic view, by a 90° goniotomy confined to the nasal quadrant using the LAN microhook. Blood reflux from the cleaved trabecular meshwork was used as an intraoperative sign of SC entry. Viscoelastic was removed, and corneal wounds were hydrated and checked for leakage.

To prevent infection, patients were advised to instill levofloxacin eye drops into the affected eye four times per day, beginning three days prior to surgery. After the procedure, the postoperative regimen comprised tobramycin-dexamethasone, levofloxacin, and pranoprofen eye drops, all administered at the same frequency to control inflammation and reduce the risk of infection.

This study collected the following preoperative variables: IOP, BCVA, ACD, visual field MD, peripapillary RNFL thickness, the extent of PAS, and the number of antiglaucoma medications. This single-arm, exploratory, hypothesis-generating study specified a 6-month endpoint; therefore, no formal a priori sample-size/power calculation was performed. The sample reflected consecutive eligible eyes enrolled during the study window. We prioritized estimation and report point estimates with 95% confidence intervals for key outcomes; post hoc power was not calculated because precision is conveyed by CI width. Two-sided tests used α = 0.05, and analyses were performed per protocol by visit as prespecified.

Continuous data were expressed as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between preoperative and postoperative values were conducted using paired t-tests, with a two-tailed significance threshold of p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed utilizing SAS JMP software (JMP 14 Pro). Subgroup/sensitivity analysis. After excluding eyes with malignant glaucoma or steroid-related ocular hypertension, we compared eyes with vs. without limited nasal GSL. Month-6 IOP was tested with Welch’s t test and by ANCOVA adjusted for baseline IOP with patient-level cluster-robust standard errors.

Results

Thirty-three patients (53 eyes) were included. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. All eyes (53/53, 100%) were receiving antiglaucoma medications preoperatively: 18 eyes (33.96%) on 1 agent, 24 (45.28%) on 2 agents, 9 (16.98%) on 3 agents, and 2 (3.77%) on 4 agents. Findings related to the presence of nasal PAS requiring limited GSL, the extent of PAS, visual field MD, and baseline peripapillary RNFL thickness are presented in Table 1.

BCVA (logMAR) improved from 0.82 ± 0.39 at baseline to 0.39 ± 0.27 at Month 6 (P < 0.001). ACD increased from 2.36 ± 0.39 mm preoperatively to 3.68 ± 0.75 mm at Month 6 (P < 0.001). Peripapillary RNFL thickness increased from 63.42 ± 18.00 μm preoperatively to 66.96 ± 18.57 μm at Month 6 (paired t-test, p = 0.0085).

Two eyes had protocol-defined exclusions from the primary IOP analyses at specific visits: one eye at Day 1 and Week 1 due to clinically significant anterior Chamber hyphema, and one eye at Week 1, Month 1, and Month 3 due to SIOH; no other exclusions occurred.

Table 2 presents IOP measurements at different follow-up intervals. A statistically and clinically meaningful reduction in IOP was noted following surgery, decreasing from 18.26 ± 4.52 mmHg to 13.15 ± 4.09 mmHg (p < 0.001). Postoperative IOP fluctuated between 13.15 ± 4.09 mmHg and 14.63 ± 4.18 mmHg. By Month 6, IOP stabilized at 14.11 ± 2.87, with all eyes remaining medication-free (Fig. 2).

Discussion

As summarized in the Introduction, cataract extraction deepens the anterior Chamber and phaco with GSL can reopen synechial angles. Building on that background, we focus here on the incremental effect of adding LAN-assisted 90° nasal goniotomy in PACG. In our cohort, the anterior Chamber depth increased from 2.36 mm preoperatively to 3.68 mm postoperatively (see Results), a change that facilitates angle opening and reduces outflow resistance; nevertheless, the additional IOP reduction observed is more likely related to the goniotomy component rather than lens surgery alone. In this study, a small but statistically significant increase in peripapillary RNFL thickness was observed at six months postoperatively. Given that glaucomatous optic neuropathy involves irreversible axonal loss, this increase is unlikely to reflect true regeneration. Instead, it may result from improved signal clarity after cataract removal, transient postoperative edema, or measurement variability. Moreover, recent studies suggest that normalization of IOP may lead to a biomechanical “rebound” in the nerve fiber layer, in which axonal bundles previously compressed by elevated pressure partially restore their volume and shape. This pattern likely represents a biomechanical rebound of axonal bundles previously compressed under elevated IOP rather than true axonal regeneration. Therefore, the observed increase in peripapillary RNFL thickness is most plausibly attributable to pressure-related structural rebound rather than genuine neural restoration. Given the limited short-term sensitivity of peripapillary RNFL thickness and the potential floor effect in advanced disease, peripapillary RNFL thickness changes were interpreted cautiously in the context of stable or improving IOP [18, 19]. Transient early increases after IOP reduction were considered more consistent with biomechanical rebound of axonal bundles previously compressed by elevated IOP than with true glaucomatous progression [20, 21].

It is acknowledged that angle status varied among patients, with some eyes requiring GSL to expose the trabecular meshwork prior to goniotomy. While this additional step may theoretically influence surgical outcomes, previous research has demonstrated that cataract extraction significantly deepens the anterior chamber and, in some instances, independently lowers IOP. However, in mid-to-late-stage PACG—particularly with extensive synechiae—prolonged closure can cause ultrastructural degeneration of the trabecular meshwork (including possible mitochondrial abnormalities), leading to irreversible cellular damage [22, 23]. As a result, phaco with GSL may be insufficient to restore TM function. In our protocol, GSL was performed solely as a Limited exposure maneuver confined to the planned 90° nasal arc to visualize the trabecular meshwork for the LAN microhook procedure. Prior reports show mixed effects—series/comparative cohorts suggest angle reopening with phaco with GSL, whereas a randomized trial found no additional IOP lowering versus phaco alone [24]. Accordingly, any standalone IOP-lowering effect of GSL in our study is expected to be minimal. The incremental outflow modification therefore arises from the nasal goniotomy, which bypasses the trabecular inner wall to access SC and distal collectors.

This study provides an early evaluation of a 90° nasal, non-excisional goniotomy using the LAN microhook (with phaco) in PACG and achieved IOP control comparable to reported outcomes from wider-angle goniotomies [25], although cross-study comparisons should be made cautiously given differences in population, arc length, and device (non-excisional vs. excisional), likely reflecting the segmental nature of trabecular outflow and nasal-predominant SC caliber in humans [26, 27]. Consistent with the exposure-only role of GSL in this protocol, an exploratory subgroup analysis excluding malignant and steroid-related eyes found no between-group difference in Month 6 IOP (see Results); both groups were medication-free at Month 6, supporting a minimal independent IOP-lowering effect of GSL (underpowered; interpret cautiously). While many prior reports have examined goniotomy arcs of ≥ 120° [28], extending the treated arc beyond 90° has not consistently yielded greater IOP reduction. In a KDB cohort including POAG/NTG, 12-month IOP outcomes did not differ significantly between 90° and 120° of treated trabecular meshwork [11]. Against this background, this study evaluates a 90° nasal, non-excisional microhook goniotomy combined with phaco in PACG and demonstrates statistically significant IOP reduction at Month 6.

In our cohort, mean IOP decreased from 18.26 ± 4.52 mmHg at baseline to 14.11 ± 2.87 mmHg at Month 6 (Δ − 4.15 mmHg; −22.7%) while all eyes were medication-free. Across angle-closure cohorts, the 12-month IOP reduction with phaco alone varies with baseline IOP. Randomized trials with baseline IOP around 22–23 mmHg report ~ 7–8 mmHg decreases, whereas observational series with lower baseline IOP typically show ~ 2–5 mmHg [29, 30]. A multicenter randomized trial found no additional IOP lowering when GSL was added to phaco [24]. Studies of excisional goniotomy combined with phaco (e.g., KDB) have reported larger absolute decreases in selected settings [25]. Against this backdrop, our Month 6 reduction lies near the lower bound of phaco alone benchmarks and below many combined-procedure reports; given the relatively low baseline IOP and complete medication washout, this still indicates a clinically meaningful effect, though head-to-head attribution remains limited. While cross-study comparisons warrant caution, isolating the incremental effect beyond lens extraction requires prospective, adequately powered comparative trials.

Taken together, these data support that a 90° nasal, non-excisional microhook goniotomy can suffice in many eyes. In a limited-arc, tissue-preserving strategy, the rationale is to minimize intraocular manipulation while recruiting an active outflow segment. Potential risk–benefit advantages (e.g., less exposure to bleeding, focal TM trauma, pigment dispersion, postoperative inflammation, and PAS recurrence) remain mechanistic/biologic plausibility pending adequately powered comparative trials.

Why a 90° arc can be sufficient (risk–benefit perspective). As noted above, aqueous outflow is segmental and the nasal quadrant is often favored anatomically and functionally. Goniotomy seeks to bypass the trabecular meshwork to access an active outflow segment; once SC and distal collectors are recruited, extending the arc beyond 90° may yield diminishing returns [28]. In PACG eyes with synechial, inflamed angles, restricting the incision to a 90° nasal arc reduces intraocular manipulation and treated clock hours, which may theoretically lower exposure to bleeding, focal trabecular trauma, pigment dispersion, postoperative inflammation, and PAS recurrence, while maintaining clinically meaningful IOP lowering. These considerations align with our Month 6 outcomes; nevertheless, the study was not powered to compare safety across arc lengths, so any potential safety advantage remains mechanistic/biologic plausibility pending adequately powered comparative trials.

By qualitatively labeling severity and explicitly describing management and resolution, this study offers a more complete safety profile. Complications were predominantly mild to moderate and responded to conservative or office-based interventions. No irreversible vision-threatening outcomes occurred during the 6-month follow-up.

Follow-up was Limited to Month 6. In this cohort, IOP lowering and medication reduction were maintained through Month 6. Mechanistically, lens extraction deepens the anterior chamber and widens the angle, reducing appositional closure and the propensity for new synechiae [31]; a 90° microhook goniotomy establishes a direct pathway to SC that may remain functionally patent, making persistence of benefit beyond Month 6 plausible [13]. Conversely, chronic PACG may involve structural and functional alterations of the trabecular meshwork and distal outflow, and re-adhesion or synechial recurrence could attenuate effect over time, particularly after non-excisional goniotomy; these are hypotheses that require confirmation [22]. Accordingly, we refrain from definitive claims beyond Month 6 and will evaluate time-to-failure (e.g., Kaplan-Meier for qualified/complete success), angle status, and any re-interventions with continued 12–24-month follow-up.

This single-center, single-surgeon, all-Asian single-arm case series enrolled a modest sample and was not powered a priori; thus, generalizability may be limited and small-to-moderate effects or subgroup differences (e.g., by GSL status) may have gone undetected. Larger, adequately powered comparative studies are required to confirm durability and effect size. The absence of a phaco alone or phaco with GSL comparator Limits causal attribution to the 90° LAN-goniotomy and precludes isolating the incremental IOP-lowering effect beyond cataract extraction; cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given differences in baseline IOP, disease spectrum, surgical technique, and follow-up. Finally, this study did not directly compare different goniotomy extents (e.g., 90°, 120°, 180°), so the optimal arc cannot be determined. These findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant prospective, adequately powered randomized or well-matched comparative studies with longer follow-up.

In summary, this study confirms that a 90° goniotomy in combination with phaco effectively lowers IOP and enhances visual acuity in patients. The absence of postoperative antiglaucoma medication further supports its efficacy. With its substantial IOP-lowering effect and low complication rate, this technique presents a viable and safe surgical option for mid-to-late-stage PACG coexisting with cataract. These findings support the effectiveness and safety of LAN-goniotomy as a minimally invasive option for PACG. Nevertheless, given the study’s single-center nature, limited sample size, and relatively short follow-up duration, further validation through multicenter prospective studies is necessary to assess its long-term effectiveness.
