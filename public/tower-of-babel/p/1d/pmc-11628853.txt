Introduction

The slow pathway region (SPR) approached from the right heart is the most common target for ablation of atrioventricular node reentry tachycardia (AVNRT).1

Although uncommon, idiopathic ventricular arrhythmia (VA) originating from the SPR has been characterized.2 Left bundle branch block morphology, positive forces in leads I and aVL, and negative forces in lead aVR are characteristic of VA localized to this region. Other features include a relatively narrow QRS, a positive QRS vector in lead II, negative forces in lead III, and variable precordial transition within V2–V5. VAs originating from this area can be treated with thermal ablation (both radiofrequency [RF] and cryoablation).2,3

To our knowledge, premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) originating from the SPR following ablation for symptomatic AVNRT have not been reported. Here, we detail a case of a patient with PVCs localized to the SPR occurring as a consequence of successful SPR ablation for symptomatic AVNRT.

Case report

A 46-year-old female patient with a medical history of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, adrenal tumor status post-adrenalectomy, underwent an AVNRT ablation 4 years prior and had a recurrence requiring repeat SPR ablation one year later (Figure 1). She presented with fatigue and premature beats noted on her wearable mobile electrocardiographic wristwatch.

Electroanatomic map with radiofrequency energy applications overlying slow pathway region (red dots) and his bundle signal location (yellow dots) during second atrioventricular node reentry tachycardia ablation.

Her initial AVNRT ablation was performed at an outside center. Available records reveal that RF energy was used resulting in no evidence of slow pathway conduction following completion of procedure despite attempted provocation. Baseline measurements included an AH interval of 69 ms and HV of 33 ms. AVNRT was inducible preablation but was unsustained in nature. Dobutamine provocation induced AVNRT peri-procedurally. Her second AVNRT ablation, performed at our center, also used RF energy via temperature-controlled mode (nonirrigated catheter with maximum power of 60 watts with goal of 50°C.) There were no postprocedural AV nodal echoes or AH jumps, which indicated a successful ablation. AH and HV intervals were similar to prior ones: 67 ms and 47 ms, respectively.

Because of subsequent palpitations, Holter monitoring (HM) was performed. It showed frequent monomorphic PVCs with a total burden of 19%. Her electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed normal sinus rhythm with frequent and monomorphic PVCs. The PVCs displayed left bundle branch block morphology, QRS duration of 133 ms, a positive QRS axis in leads I, II, aVL, and negative forces in leads aVR and III (Supplemental Figure 1). Of note, these PVCs were not present on multiple ECGs performed during the several months between her second AVNRT ablation and eventual presentation with her symptomatic PVCs. A transthoracic echocardiogram and an exercise stress test were both unremarkable. Medical therapy with diltiazem and flecainide was prescribed, and HM was repeated. The total burden of PVC was reduced to 16%; however, given ongoing symptoms, PVC ablation was planned.

Activation mapping using the EnSite X EP System (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL) localized the PVC to the interventricular septum near the anatomic SPR (Figure 2). Pace mapping via Score MapTM from this site revealed >98% pace match (Figure 2) The preablation voltage map revealed significant low voltage and voltage-heterogeneity within and around the SPR at the sites of previous ablation (Figure 3A). Ablation of the PVCs was performed using a Tacticath DFSE catheter (Abbott) targeting the earliest activation site 28 ms pre-QRS. After delivery of 9 RF lesions (Figure 3B) no further PVCs were observed. An additional electrophysiology study was deferred, and the procedure was concluded. Diltiazem and flecainide were discontinued. On reevaluation 1 month later, the patient was asymptomatic, and repeated HM showed total PVC burden had been reduced to 0.05%.

Discussion

Based on the morphology of the clinical PVCs observed and their appearance after multiple slow pathway ablations, we hypothesize that the etiology of the PVCs was due to substrate changes from the anatomic SPR ablation. Although the mechanism of arrhythmia remains unclear, we believe that the PVCs were likely mediated by enhanced automaticity or triggered activity. Another consideration is that PVCs serendipitously arose from this region, and would have done so, despite previous procedures, necessitating eventual need for ablation. The pre-PVC ablation voltage map (Figure 3A), however, displays marked voltage reduction (indicating the presence of scar) and heterogeneity (implying the presence of an arrhythmogenic milieu) within the SPR, creating substrate that contributes to formation and propagation of arrhythmia.

SPR ablation for AVNRT is common, safe, and effective, and it appears that long-term proarrhythmic consequences have been rarely reported,4 despite the fact that RF ablation for AVNRT has long been considered to be proarrhythmogenic given the inhomogeneous nature of RF lesions.5 Histologically, RF lesions have irregular edges contributing to formation of nonuniform anisotropy.6 One series with 120 patients undergoing AVNRT ablation with a mean follow up of 10 years reported various atrial arrhythmias following ablation including premature atrial complexes, atrial tachycardia, AF, and atrial flutter, but no PVCs.6 The main reported complications from AVNRT ablation are high-grade AV block and pericardial tamponade.4

PVCs following accessory pathway RF ablation with origins localized to the accessory pathway ablation targets are also rare but have been described previously.7,8 This contrasts with PVCs localized to the SPR following ablation for AVNRT which appear to not have been reported until now.

Conclusion

We hypothesize that the substrate modification from SPR promoted the subsequent initiation of PVCs from the anatomically adjacent region. While VAs originating from the SPR are rare and can occur idiopathically, we posit that they may result from SPR modification after AVNRT ablation.
