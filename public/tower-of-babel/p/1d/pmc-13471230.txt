Thrombin generation parameters provide independent information on plasma coagulation potential in pediatric patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a prospective, single-center pilot study
Authors: Dino Mehic, Katie L Clouthier, Nina Buchtele, Adam Miszta, Feng-Chang Lin, Marian A Rollins-Raval, Katherine C Clement, Alisa S Wolberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) provides circulatory and respiratory support in pediatric patients with life-threatening organ failure but can alter coagulation.ObjectivesTo assess the relationship between a plasma thrombin generation (TG) assay and conventional coagulation assays (activated partial thromboplastin time, antifactor Xa [anti-Xa], and D-dimer) in pediatric ECMO.MethodsPlatelet-poor plasma was collected every 4 hours during the first 48 hours and then daily after ECMO initiation. TG was measured by calibrated automated thrombography.ResultsFifteen ECMO (65% venoarterial and 35% venovenous) patients (median age, 3.3 months; IQR, 0.3-17.5) were included in this prospective, observational pilot cohort study. Median ECMO duration was 7 days (IQR, 5.5-20.5). Seven patients experienced bleeding events, and 5 developed circuit- or cannula-associated thrombosis; of these, 2 experienced both. TG parameters showed substantial interindividual variability. After an initial increase in the median thrombin peak, TG potential declined over the first 48 hours. Neither activated partial thromboplastin time nor D-dimer correlated significantly with TG parameters. Anti-Xa activity correlated significantly with TG peak (r = −0.84; P = .001) and endogenous thrombin potential (r = −0.46; P = .01). At anti-Xa levels ≥ 0.4 IU/mL, TG approached zero, indicating little additional suppression of procoagulant activity at higher anti-Xa levels.ConclusionOur data show substantial interindividual and temporal variability in TG parameters. Only TG peak and endogenous thrombin potential correlated with anti-Xa activity. The TG assay may capture plasma coagulation features not reflected by routine assays.Keywords: anticoagulation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, hemorrhage, thrombin generation, thrombosis

Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a highly invasive, temporary advanced life support technology used in patients with reversible life-threatening respiratory and/or cardiac failure. Contact between the patient’s blood and the ECMO circuit triggers a potent inflammatory and prothrombotic response, necessitating continuous anticoagulation to prevent circuit and systemic thrombosis [[1], [2], [3]]. Administration of unfractionated heparin during ECMO establishment, typically as an initial bolus followed by continuous infusion [4], reduces thrombin generation (TG) and thrombosis risk by facilitating antithrombin-mediated inactivation of thrombin and factor (F)Xa, but increases bleeding risk [2,5]. Bleeding risk is further increased by consumptive coagulation factor deficiencies, thrombocytopenia, impaired platelet function, and dysregulated fibrinolysis [6,7]. The first 48 hours of ECMO anticoagulation are particularly challenging [8,9]. Per the International Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) database, up to 41% of neonates and 47% of pediatric patients on ECMO experience hemorrhagic or thrombotic events, which significantly contribute to decreased survival [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Excessive bleeding is the most common cause of premature circuit separation from ECMO before adequate cardiopulmonary recovery [11].

Anticoagulation in ECMO is largely based on expert opinion and is complicated by heterogeneous patient populations, differences in clinical management across centers [13], and age-related physiological changes in coagulation system development [14]. The unique physiology of the pediatric coagulation system increases challenges in identifying optimal anticoagulation strategies [15,16]. Children have a lower capacity to generate thrombin [17], but infants and young children often require higher heparin doses to achieve effective anticoagulation during ECMO [18,19]. Evidence to guide pediatric anticoagulation is sparse [20], and there is a large heterogeneity across centers [21]. Current recommendations for monitoring anticoagulation in pediatric ECMO include 1 or more “time-to-clot” assays (eg, activated clotting time [ACT], activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], and/or viscoelastic tests) in combination with anti-FXa (anti-Xa) activity assays [20].

Traditional coagulation assays (eg, ACT and aPTT) measure the time to initial fibrin formation but do not capture the overall TG capacity [22]. The ACT is influenced by platelet count and function, hypothermia, hypofibrinogenemia, and hemodilution [23] and correlates poorly with heparin dose [24]. aPTT is affected by elevated FVIII levels and other coagulopathies, and it shows significant age-related variation, especially in young children [25,26]. Anti-Xa assays provide a direct measure of heparin activity but do not capture functional coagulation potential and are influenced by hemolysis, hyperbilirubinemia, low antithrombin levels, and elevated fibrinogen levels [4,27]. In pediatric ECMO, anti-Xa activity is a better correlate of heparin levels than aPTT or ACT [28]. Notably, however, the ACT, anti-Xa, aPTT, thromboelastography parameters, and heparin infusion rates do not correlate well with each other, and none of these assays reliably predict bleeding or thrombosis [9,[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. Thus, improved methods to monitor coagulation status and anticoagulation and avoid bleeding and thrombotic complications, are needed.

TG assays measure the capacity of plasma to generate thrombin ex vivo and provide parameters to describe the kinetics and magnitude of thrombin production and inhibition [15,33,34]. TG testing can identify both hypo- and hypercoagulable states and has elucidated abnormal coagulation mechanisms in disorders such as liver disease, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, and myeloproliferative neoplasms [15,22,33,34]. In this observational pilot study, we analyzed a pediatric ECMO cohort to determine whether TG analysis provides information distinct from that of conventional coagulation assays.

Materials and Methods

Patient cohort and study design

This single-center observational cohort study enrolled 20 children (aged 0-18 years) who were sequentially admitted to the University of North Carolina Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and cannulated for ECMO (venoarterial [VA] or venovenous [VV]) between October 2018 and June 2019. In total, 15 of 20 patients in whom TG analysis could be performed were analyzed. Five patients were excluded due to insufficient heparin exposure, lack of corresponding anti-Xa measurements, or insufficient residual sample material for reliable TG and repeat testing. Excluded patients had similar clinical characteristics as included participants, including sex distribution (60% male), ECMO mode (80% VA-ECMO), ethnicity (40% Black), ECMO indication (80% respiratory), and median ECMO duration (5 [2-11.5] days). Median age was 33 months (0.55-104), slightly higher compared with included patients.

Human participant approval was obtained from the University of North Carolina Institutional Review Board (18-2049), which, on October 17, 2018, determined that the study-specific rationale that involved the use of deidentified waste blood obtained from routine clinical testing, was sufficient to justify a waiver of informed consent for research.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was to assess the relationship between a plasma TG assay and conventional coagulation assays in pediatric ECMO. Exploratory analyses were performed to examine relationships between TG parameters and ECMO type (VA or VV), as well as bleeding or thrombotic events.

ECMO circuit and anticoagulation management

The ECMO circuit consisted of a Cardiohelp centrifugal pump (Getinge). Circuits were primed with Plasma-Lyte (Baxter) in children weighing ≥ 13 kg, whereas those weighing < 13 kg received a blood-primed circuit. All patients were managed according to institutional guidelines for anticoagulation during ECMO. Unfractionated heparin was administered as a bolus (50-100 Units/kg at the time of cannulation), followed by continuous infusion. Infusion rates were typically 20 Units/kg/h in neonates and 10 to 15 Units/kg/h in older children and adolescents, and were titrated to maintain anti-Xa activity between 0.3 and 0.7 IU/mL. Details on heparin dosing are in the Supplementary Table. Lower anti-Xa targets were applied during clinically significant bleeding.

Clinical definitions and blood product administration

Bleeding and thrombosis were defined as per ELSO registry criteria [35]. Briefly, hemorrhagic complications were defined as transfusion of > 20 mL/kg packed red blood cells within a 24-hour period or need for a surgical or endoscopic procedure to control bleeding. Any intracranial hemorrhage was classified as a hemorrhagic event, regardless of transfusion requirement. Circuit thrombosis was defined as clot formation within ECMO system components (eg, oxygenator, pump, tubing, or cannulae) that may result in impaired circuit performance or hemolysis, or necessitate circuit exchange. Noncircuit thrombosis were events occurring in the patient (eg, deep vein thrombosis, arterial thrombosis, or ischemic stroke).

Blood products were administered per ECMO guidelines when deemed clinically appropriate. Briefly, red blood cells were transfused at 10 to 15 mL/kg when hematocrit was <30%, aiming to keep hemoglobin levels > 10 g/dL in neonates and > 9 g/dL in most other patients. Platelets were transfused for counts < 100,000/μL, aiming to keep platelets > 50,000/μL unless clinically bleeding. Plasma was transfused at the clinical discretion of the provider, typically for an international normalized ratio > 2 or 2.5, or with clinically significant bleeding. Cryoprecipitate was transfused for fibrinogen levels < 150 mg/dL.

Clinical laboratory measurements and outcomes

Participant demographics, medical history, ECMO indication, duration, type (VA and VV), and clinical outcomes were recorded. Routine coagulation parameters, including aPTT (HemosIL SynthASil, ACL Tops 750, Werfen), anti-Xa (HemosIL Liquid Anti-Xa assay, Werfen), and D-dimer (HemosIL D-dimer HS 500, ACL Tops 750, Werfen), were measured every 4 hours.

Plasma preparation

Residual blood collected in 3.2% buffered sodium citrate for routine anticoagulation management was processed to platelet-poor plasma by centrifugation (7 minutes, 3065 rpm [2205 × g]) at room temperature, aliquoted, and frozen at −80 °C within 4 hours of collection. Samples were deidentified; only encrypted medical record numbers were stored to allow tracking of individual participants for data collection.

TG assay

Plasma TG potential was assessed on freshly thawed samples by calibrated automated thrombography using 0.5 pM tissue factor, 4 μM phospholipid vesicles (20 mol% phosphatidylserine, 60 mol% phosphatidylcholine, and 20 mol% phosphatidylethanolamine), 0.416 mM Z-Gly-Gly-Arg-AMC fluorogenic substrate, and 16.6 mM CaCl2 (final, materials from Diagnostica Stago) as described [36,37]. TG parameters were calculated using Thrombinoscope software (Thrombinoscope BV).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.6.1) and R (www.r-project.org; RStudio v2025.05.1+513). Continuous variables are reported as medians with IQRs, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rho. Significance was defined as P < .05. Given the exploratory nature of the study, correction for multiple testing was suspended.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patient cohort

Demographic data are shown in the Table. Patients were 0 days to 14 years old, with a median age of 3.3 months (IQR, 0.3-17.5). Ten individuals (66.7%) were cannulated for respiratory indications, 3 (20%) for cardiac indications, and 2 (13.3%) during cardiopulmonary arrest for extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Cannulations were VA (60%) and VV (40%). The median ECMO duration was 7 days (IQR, 5.5-20.5). Survival to ECMO decannulation was 66.7%. Per ELSO reporting criteria at the time of study implementation [35], 7 patients experienced bleeding complications involving a total of 11 sites: 2 pulmonary hemorrhages or cardiac tamponades, 2 central nervous system hemorrhages, 2 peripheral cannula site bleeds, 3 gastrointestinal hemorrhages, and 2 surgical site bleeds. Five patients developed thrombotic complications, comprising 5 clots in total: 4 circuit- or cannula-associated clots and 1 clot in the circuit bridge segment. Of these, 1 patient with central nervous system hemorrhage also developed a circuit clot, and 1 patient with pulmonary hemorrhage also had a circuit clot.

Conventional clinical laboratory assays (aPTT, anti-Xa, and D-dimer) show considerable variability among individuals and over time

Routine coagulation parameters, including aPTT, anti-Xa activity, and D-dimer, were monitored during the first 72 hours of ECMO. Median values for all patients and time points over the analyzed timeframe were as follows: aPTT 127 seconds (IQR, 120-153), anti-Xa 0.32 IU/mL (IQR, 0.27-0.35), and D-dimer 2230 D-dimer units ng/mL (IQR, 1507-2960). We observed substantial interindividual variability across all 3 metrics, with marked intraindividual fluctuations within the first 48 hours of ECMO (Figure 1). Most patients exhibited a modest increase in anti-Xa levels and D-dimer during this period (Figure 1).

Plasma coagulation capacity measured by TG parameters reveals substantial variability across donors and time points

We observed substantial interindividual variability across all TG parameters (Figure 2). After an early increase in median TG following establishment of ECMO and initiation of anticoagulation, the median TG lag time and time-to-peak prolonged, and the velocity index, peak, and endogenous thrombin potential (ETP) declined within 48 hours, followed by a slight recovery thereafter. Although the study was not powered to compare VA- and VV-ECMO or clinical outcomes (bleeding or thrombosis), exploratory analyses did not reveal differences in TG parameters between these groups (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2).

Anti-Xa activity correlates significantly with TG peak and ETP

To identify relationships between routine coagulation assays (aPTT, anti-Xa activity, and D-dimer) and TG parameters, we performed Spearman correlation analyses of all measurements from all patients and time points (158 total measurements). The aPTT correlated moderately but nonsignificantly with anti-Xa activity. Neither the aPTT nor anti-Xa activity correlated with D-dimer (Figure 3A, B). As expected, TG parameters were interrelated, wherein lag time and time-to-peak correlated positively, and both of these parameters correlated negatively with the velocity index (Figure 3A, B). The velocity index also correlated strongly and positively with the thrombin peak (r = 0.80; P = .005), and the peak correlated strongly and positively with ETP (r = 0.69; P = .02). Neither aPTT nor D-dimer correlated significantly with TG parameters. However, anti-Xa activity correlated strongly and significantly with both TG peak (r = −0.84; P = .002) and ETP (r = −0.46; P = .009).

Anti-Xa activity greater than 0.4 IU/mL is associated with fully suppressed TG

Since the only significant correlations were between anti-Xa activity and TG peak and ETP, we directly visualized these measures across all patient samples and time points (Figure 3C, D). Interestingly, in both cases, for anti-Xa levels > 0.4 IU/mL (marked by a dotted red line), TG peak and ETP approached zero, indicating that this level of anticoagulation fully suppressed plasma TG potential, with little additional anticoagulation benefit at higher anti-Xa levels.

Discussion

Pediatric patients supported with ECMO face substantial risks of thrombosis and bleeding, underscoring the need for improved strategies to assess coagulation potential and monitor anticoagulation [29]. We analyzed plasma TG to determine whether TG parameters could provide information on coagulation status different from that gleaned from established clinical laboratory assays. We observed marked inter- and intraindividual variability in TG parameters. Among routine metrics, only anti-Xa activity correlated strongly with TG peak and ETP. Notably, at anti-Xa levels > 0.4 IU/mL, both TG peak and ETP approached zero, suggesting relatively modest heparin levels fully suppressed plasma TG potential. These findings suggest the TG assay provides complementary and independent insights into plasma coagulation status in pediatric ECMO and support continued efforts to characterize TG testing in this population.

Previous studies have documented considerable interindividual variability in traditional coagulation assays and biomarkers during cannulation and initiation of ECMO, particularly before anticoagulation is fully established [19,29,38,39]. A small number of studies tested plasma TG in adult ECMO and reported high interindividual and temporal variability [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. Our findings in pediatric ECMO are consistent with these observations, showing substantial variability both between patients and across time points within the first 48 hours after cannulation, especially in a subset of individuals. Whether this variability reflects changing endogenous mechanisms (eg, coagulation proteins, cells, or vasculature), contact activation, heparin levels, factor consumption, and/or administration of blood products and other therapeutics is unclear. Interestingly, despite variability across both laboratory assays and TG parameters, only a subset of these measures correlated. Although increased TG has been correlated with D-dimer [38], this was not evident in our cohort. The strong inverse correlation between anti-Xa levels and TG peak and ETP suggests the TG assay captures anticoagulant effects similar to anti-Xa activity measurements. The lack of significant correlations between TG parameters and other clinical metrics suggests these assays reflect different and nonoverlapping features.

Initial recommendations to maintain heparin at 0.3 to 0.7 IU/mL were extrapolated from non-ECMO populations [27]. In infants aged < 6 months, achieving anti-Xa levels of 0.35 to 0.7 IU/mL is difficult and associated with high bleeding rates [18], suggesting higher anti-Xa targets may lead to overanticoagulation. Recent International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis guidance for adult ECMO recommends a narrower target of 0.3 to 0.5 IU/mL [27,42,43]. Our observation that TG potential is almost completely suppressed > 0.4 IU/mL suggests pediatric ECMO patients may be adequately anticoagulated at the lower end of this range. However, it is unknown whether using lower anticoagulation targets improves bleeding or thrombotic outcomes or survival in pediatric ECMO [20]. Indeed, multiple assays may be needed to ensure that both the heparin level and overall coagulation status are quantified [27]. Determining whether integrated algorithms combining laboratory tests and TG can enable more precise anticoagulation titration and help define a therapeutic heparin window at population and/or individual levels will require larger prospective cohorts and warrants continued efforts.

Although this single-center study was limited by a relatively small cohort, the cohort size enabled intensive temporal sampling—every 4 hours during the first 48 hours of ECMO support—providing a unique time-resolved view of coagulation dynamics during a highly unstable and complication-prone period. To our knowledge, this is the first report of TG potential in a pediatric ECMO cohort. The study was not powered for subanalyzes of ECMO type (VV vs VA), clinical indication, participant age, or to assess associations between TG parameters and bleeding or thrombosis, so it is unclear whether the lack of associations in these exploratory analyses reflects the small cohort size, limitations of plasma TG testing, or challenges in accurately identifying bleeding or clot formation in real-time. Although all samples were processed similarly, the 4-hour patient-to-freezer timeframe may have altered the TG parameters. As with clinical laboratory assays (eg, aPTT, anti-Xa, and prothrombin time), TG assays of platelet-poor plasma are not sensitive to potential contributions from cells. Although plasma TG can reflect contributions from extracellular vesicles released by activated blood and vascular cells [39,44], it may not detect other mechanisms contributing to bleeding and thrombosis, including vascular damage, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, and/or other disease-specific coagulopathies [7,42,45]. Future studies measuring whole-blood TG may provide more information on coagulation status.

In conclusion, we characterized clinical laboratory assays and plasma TG dynamics during an early and critical phase of pediatric ECMO. Despite marked variability, anti-Xa activity showed a strong inverse correlation with TG peak and ETP up to anti-Xa levels of 0.4 IU/mL, with limited additional suppression of TG above this threshold. The lack of correlation between other clinical laboratory assays and TG parameters suggests TG analysis captures aspects of plasma hemostatic capacity not reflected by conventional assays. These data establish a framework for prospective studies to validate these findings in larger cohorts and over longer observation periods, highlight the complexity of coagulation and anticoagulation monitoring in pediatric ECMO, and establish a basis for future studies aimed at optimizing anticoagulation management to reduce bleeding and thrombotic complications in critically ill children.
