Deep Groundwater Total Dissolved Solids Mapping in the Dakota Group, Williston Basin, USA
Authors: Michael J Stephens, Bennett E Hoogenboom, Lyndsay B Ball, Will Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrowing concern about the quantity of available freshwater around the world has led to interest in surveying groundwater total dissolved solids (TDS) below water well depths. Deep TDS has not been systematically mapped, and there is much to learn about the distribution and controls on deeper groundwater. In sedimentary basins across the United States, groundwater resources often overlie hydrocarbon resources, providing an opportunity to use borehole geophysical data collected for hydrocarbons to characterize groundwater and pore space resources. This study adapts a recently developed subsurface geostatistical and geophysical modeling approach to continuously map groundwater TDS, porosity, and temperature in the Dakota Group of the Williston Basin—an undercharacterized regional aquifer system overlying deeper hydrocarbon reservoirs. Groundwater TDS in the Dakota Group ranges from approximately 4800 to 26,900 mg/L. TDS patterns are stratified with higher TDS in the lower and upper Dakota Group, and relatively lower TDS in the middle Dakota Group. The lower TDS in the middle zone may represent a preferential regional flow path for lower‐TDS meteoric recharge from the west. The alternating pattern of TDS may also be evidence of higher‐TDS inflows into the Dakota Group from underlying and potentially from overlying aquifers. Porosity is lower near the center of the Williston Basin and tends to be higher to the east, which may be related to grain size distributions. The new regional TDS and porosity modeling serves as a quantitative reference for water users and provides supporting evidence for hypotheses on Dakota Group recharge.

Introduction

In some regions in the United States, surface water supplies are stressed and unpredictable leading to more reliance on groundwater resources. The median depth of groundwater wells in the United States is 44 m (Perrone and Jasechko 2019), defining the conventional depth of groundwater development. These shallow groundwater systems are strained from over‐pumping and are exposed to surface contamination resulting in significant groundwater depletion, degraded water quality, and even land subsidence in some aquifers (Konikow 2013; Faunt et al. 2024). As a result, there is widespread interest in exploring deeper groundwater resources as part of sustainable water use management. However, deep groundwater usability is largely controlled by natural total dissolved solids (TDS) content, and groundwater TDS generally increases with depth (Kharaka and Hanor 2003; Gillespie et al. 2017; Stanton et al. 2017; Metzger and Landon 2018; Stephens et al. 2019). Currently, the natural TDS distributions of deeper aquifers in sedimentary basins across the United States are poorly documented despite the potential for deeper, brackish (3000–10,000 mg/L TDS) groundwater to augment freshwater (<3000 mg/L TDS) supplies (Ferguson et al. 2018b).

Quantifying TDS distributions is a fundamental step in exploring the viability of deeper water resources. Measurements of TDS in water samples from both shallower water wells and deeper hydrocarbon wells can help characterize TDS in commonly utilized aquifers and reservoirs. However, in many sedimentary basins in the United States, there is a vertical gap in TDS data below water wells and above hydrocarbon wells (Ferguson et al. 2018b). Furthermore, data from water and hydrocarbon wells can be offset spatially, and estimating TDS in the horizontal and vertical gaps can be challenging. Although groundwater TDS generally increases with depth, variations in horizontal and vertical gradients can be significant (Kharaka and Hanor 2003; Gillespie et al. 2017; Hoff and Dutton 2017; Ball et al. 2020; Flowers et al. 2022). Horizontal and vertical variations in TDS directly impact the viability of using deeper water resources by affecting the cost associated with drilling, producing, and treating brackish water. Therefore, a detailed understanding of groundwater TDS can benefit water managers and users.

Analysis of hydrocarbon well geophysical logs is an established approach to fill horizontal and vertical gaps in sample‐derived TDS data (Hamlin and de la Rocha 2015; Gillespie et al. 2017, 2019, 2022; Hoff and Dutton 2017; Stephens et al. 2019, 2021, 2025; Texas Water Development Board 2019, 2021; Terry et al. 2021; Flowers et al. 2022). These analyses rely on petrophysical relationships based on Archie's Law, which relates the formation resistivity and porosity from well logs to groundwater resistivity (Archie 1942). TDS can then be estimated from groundwater resistivity if the formation temperature and the dominant ions are known (Schlumberger 2009). Methods developed by Stephens et al. (2025) have made it possible to include discrete TDS measurements from samples, formation resistivity, porosity, and temperature into an integrated joint Gaussian process model that continuously interpolates each quantity while using empirical relationships (e.g., Archie's Law) to estimate TDS from borehole geophysical data. These methods allow for higher‐resolution TDS mapping than is possible with discrete TDS measurements alone, because the model allows for existing TDS measurements from water samples to be combined with hydrocarbon‐well geophysical data.

Many sedimentary basins in the United States are also hydrocarbon‐producing regions where drought and demand for freshwater can lead to water supply stress. The Willison Basin in North Dakota and Montana is continuing to experience an energy boom due to the unconventional petroleum resource development in the Bakken and Three Forks Formations (Bartos et al. 2022). The hydrocarbon production is an important component of the United States energy supply but also places additional stress on local water supplies. Hydrocarbon development (e.g., drilling wells, hydraulic fracturing) in North Dakota and Montana uses ~17.8 million cubic meters of water per year, which is commonly supplied from the same shallow freshwater sources used by domestic, agricultural, and other industrial users (McShane et al. 2020). Deeper, brackish groundwater may provide an opportunity to supplement shallower water supplies; however, deep groundwater resources are infrequently utilized, and therefore limited information is known about the ambient quality and quantity in the Williston Basin. As part of the U.S. Geological Survey mission to provide objective appraisals of the Nation's natural resources, this paper applies the recently developed modeling techniques to map groundwater TDS, porosity, and temperature in the Dakota Group in the Williston Basin (Figure 1). The Dakota Group is a major regional aquifer system that lies vertically below traditionally utilized groundwater resources and above hydrocarbon reservoirs. Geophysical data from hydrocarbon wells throughout the Williston Basin fill in data gaps within the Dakota Group (Figures 1 and 2). Temperature distributions have been mapped in detail by Gelman (2023), but this work created high‐resolution maps of TDS and porosity distributions that can be utilized by resource managers and the energy industry to plan use of the Dakota Group as both a water supply and waste disposal zone. The goal of this study was to describe natural TDS distributions as they were before fluid disposal associated with hydrocarbon development occurred in the Williston Basin and build upon generalized TDS mapping from available sample data by Downey and Dinwiddie (1988) and Bartos et al. (2022).

A better understanding of the 3‐dimensional distribution of natural groundwater TDS and porosity in relation to regional fluid flow dynamics would provide foundational information for consideration of potential expansion of targeted water extraction and/or injection.

Study Area Background

Geologic Setting

The Williston Basin is a roughly circular intracratonic basin spanning parts of North Dakota, Montana, and South Dakota in the United States as well as the Saskatchewan and Manitoba Provinces of Canada (Figure 1). The Williston Basin was formed in Ordovician time when lithospheric warming was followed by cooling, causing subsidence that likely formed the Williston Basin (Crowley et al. 1985; Gelman 2023). Episodic sedimentation has filled the Williston Basin with Paleozoic to Cenozoic sedimentary rocks more than 4800 m thick at its center (Figure S1).

Three major structures in the Williston Basin include: the Cedar Creek anticline, the Nesson anticline, and the Poplar dome (Figure 3). The Nesson anticline initiated in the Ordovician and the Cedar Creek anticline started in the Devonian; movement on both anticlines ended in the Paleogene. Movement of the Poplar dome started in Mississippian time with significant uplift happening after the Paleocene. These structural features are important traps for the hydrocarbons found in Paleozoic rocks in the Williston Basin.

This study is focused on Mesozoic rocks, specifically the Lower Cretaceous Dakota Group (Figure S1; Murphy et al. 2009; Bartos et al. 2022; Gelman 2023). The top of the Jurassic Swift Formation is the lower bound for this work. The base of the Dakota Group is comprised of the Inyan Kara Formation. Above the Inyan Kara Formation is the Skull Creek Shale, the Newcastle Sandstone (also known as the Dakota/Muddy Sandstone), and the Mowry Formation (also known as the Mowry Shale). The Inyan Kara Formation was deposited in a low‐relief coastal setting with rivers flowing across the coastal plain (Bader 2019). The Inyan Kara Formation is nonmarine sandstone with interbedded marine shales and generally has three facies: the base is sandstone, the middle is shale, and the upper portion is sandstone (Murphy et al. 2009). The Skull Creek Shale is interpreted as an offshore marine deposit. The Newcastle Sandstone contains shallow marine, shoreline, and fluvial sandstone with shale interbeds (Anna 1986). The Mowry Formation is an offshore marine shale and is the upper bound for this work. For the purposes of this study, we informally consider the top of the Inyan Kara Formation as a divide between the “upper Dakota Group” and “lower Dakota Group.” The Dakota Group thickness in the Williston Basin study area averages ~429 m. The Colorado Group and the Pierre Shale overlying the Dakota Group are offshore marine shales and form a substantial confining unit separating the Lower Cretaceous from the Upper Cretaceous and surficial aquifers. The Fox Hills and Hell Creek Formations comprise the Upper Cretaceous and are overlain by Tertiary rocks and Quaternary glacial deposits (Thamke et al. 2014).

The top of the Inyan Kara Formation and major structural features are shown on Figure 3 (data from Gelman 2023). The Dakota Group elevation is shallowest at the crest of the Cedar Creek anticline and Poplar dome in the western portion of the study area, deepens in the center, and shallows again to the east.

Hydrogeologic Setting

The Dakota Group aquifer system is recharged at outcrops along the margins of structural uplifts (e.g., Black Hills, central Montana) and potentially from groundwater flow (i.e., leakage) from stratigraphically lower and higher rocks where confining units are thin and/or fractured (Downey and Dinwiddie 1988; Bartos et al. 2022). Regional groundwater flow directions are from the west‐southwest to the east‐northeast. The Lower Cretaceous aquifer system discharges as lateral leakage and upward leakage in eastern South Dakota and North Dakota to overlying rocks and near the surface and into glacial deposits—making it a classic example of an artesian system (Bartos et al. 2022).

The Upper Cretaceous and Cenozoic aquifer system is recharged from rainfall, snowmelt, stream infiltration, and groundwater flow from adjacent areas (Thamke et al. 2014; Bartos et al. 2022). Discharge is baseflow to streams and withdrawals from wells. Flow directions are generally from west to east, like the Lower Cretaceous aquifer system.

Oil and Gas Operations

The first gas produced in the Williston Basin was in 1912 near the Cedar Creek anticline and then in 1951, the first oil was found along the Nesson anticline (Gelman 2023; Figure 3). Since then, nearly every formation from the Paleozoic has produced hydrocarbons. However, 92% of the total hydrocarbon production in the Williston Basin (in North Dakota and Montana) has come from the Bakken Formation (67.3%), the Madison Group (15.1%), and the Red River Formation (9.6%; Gelman 2023). Bakken Formation discoveries in the 2000s, along with advanced horizontal drilling technologies, made the Bakken Formation hydrocarbon accumulations the largest in the Williston Basin (Gelman 2023). The Bakken Formation discoveries also facilitated water‐use transformation in the Williston Basin (Valder et al. 2018; McShane et al. 2020; Bartos et al. 2022). The number of hydrocarbon production wells, the population, and the demand for water resources for both oil production and public supply all increased sharply post‐Bakken Formation discovery. This has led to increased water use in the Williston Basin (McShane et al. 2020). The Dakota Group has been used as a disposal zone for produced water generated from hydrocarbon production since the 1950s (Bartos et al. 2022).

Methods

This section briefly describes the data and methods used to model groundwater TDS in the Dakota Group. This approach follows methods developed by Stephens et al. (2025) and is explained further in the Supporting Information. The quantities modeled are TDS measurements from water samples, formation resistivity and porosity from borehole logs, and formation temperature from previous temperature modeling by Gelman (2023). Observations were discrete point data in space, and quantities of different types were not always collocated. Dispersed quantities of the same type were related with Gaussian processes (Rasmussen and Williams 2006), and collocated quantities of different types were related with empirical geophysical laws (Archie 1942; Schlumberger 2009). All data types were jointly modeled simultaneously with a single Gaussian process that was parameterized using maximum likelihood. After fitting the model to the data, we can make predictions of each quantity at any location to create continuous maps for interpretation.

All model input data were selected to be spatially and temporally distant from known produced water disposal locations and are intended to represent natural, pre‐injection regional conditions in the Williston Basin (Chesley‐Preston et al. 2014). Furthermore, model input data were derived from sandstone intervals to maintain petrophysical relationships (refer to Section S1); therefore, the modeled quantities shown on maps in this work should be considered our best estimate if sands are present. Detailed sand/shale mapping was beyond the scope of this work, and modeled quantities may not be representative in shale lithologies. For example, we may not necessarily expect the modeled TDS inside the Mowry Formation—a marine shale—to be representative if water inside the Mowry Formation has not exchanged with adjacent sandstone intervals. Data from sandstones are well distributed throughout the Williston Basin in the Dakota Group except for the Mowry Formation. Zones of relatively low estimated uncertainty can be used as a proxy for sandstone since uncertainty is lower near input data, which were derived from sandstone intervals.

Gaussian process model predictions for each quantity take the form of a normal distribution described by a mean and variance. The means of the predictions are shown as the quantities (e.g., TDS) on maps throughout this work. The square root of the predicted variance (standard deviation) is shown as the uncertainty on maps throughout this work. The predicted variance was derived from a combination of (1) the probabilistic trend model that models non‐stationary correlations, (2) the covariance functions that describe stationary correlations, and (3) the observation model that accounts for uncorrelated and site‐correlated noise in the data (Section S3). A full explanation of estimating uncertainty in Gaussian process models can be found in Rasmussen and Williams (2006).

Results and Discussion

Using the methods described above, observations of TDS from water samples, borehole formation resistivity, porosity, and temperature were used to model groundwater TDS continuously throughout three‐dimensional space. Distributions of formation temperature are described in Section S4 and are shown on Figures S3 through S5. Finally, we discuss interpretations of potential explanatory factors for the mapped TDS patterns.

Porosity Distributions

Modeled porosity in the Lower Cretaceous Dakota Group is shown in map view on Figure 4, and the associated predicted standard deviation for the natural logarithm of porosity is shown on Figure S6. The z‐coordinate of the surfaces on Figure 4 and Figure S6 are 70 m below the modeled top of the Inyan Kara Formation shown in Figure 3. Porosity is also shown in cross section from west to east across the northern extent of the study area in Figure 5. Porosity fractions range from approximately 0.17 to 0.30 throughout the Dakota Group. Porosity is lowest (0.17–0.20) near the center of the Williston Basin and east of the Cedar Creek anticline (Figure 4). Porosity increases to 0.24–0.28 to east of the Nesson anticline. West of the Nesson anticline, porosity patterns are stratified with higher values in the upper Dakota Group and lower values in the middle Inyan Kara Formation (Figure 5a). Uncertainties for porosity predictions are higher at the margins of the Williston Basin where input data are scarce (Figure S6). Generally, uncertainties are also higher in the upper Dakota Group (e.g., Mowry Formation) where less sandy facies are available to collect data (Figure 5b).

Porosity data and modeling show porosity is lower near the center of the Williston Basin, and higher porosity is more common on the margins, particularly in the Inyan Kara Formation east of the Nesson anticline (Figure 4). Decreasing porosity with depth is generally expected due to higher overburden pressure and compaction; however, the spatial trends across the Inyan Kara Formation show more variation than a simple depth dependency. The mapped porosity trends in Figure 4 are consistent with previously mapped grain size distributions in the Inyan Kara Formation (Anna 1986, plate 11, figure B). Porosity is generally lower where finer grain sizes are mapped, particularly in western North Dakota. In central North Dakota, average grain size is larger and corresponds to higher porosity. These correlations suggest that the regional porosity trends are most strongly controlled by the depositional setting.

Major structural features in the Williston Basin seem to exhibit some control on groundwater TDS. At the Cedar Creek anticline, higher‐TDS groundwater is located at the anticline limbs where elevation is deeper, whereas lower‐TDS values are at higher elevations at the crest of the anticline (Figures 3 and 6). The modeled TDS pattern at the Cedar Creek anticline is developed based on TDS measurements from wells in the area (Figure 1); however, there are not many data points, and uncertainty for TDS predictions is elevated, particularly west of the anticline (Figure S7). TDS is also higher in the northern part of the study area between the Poplar dome and the Nesson anticline (Figure 6). Density‐driven flow may have caused higher‐TDS water to stagnate in lower elevations between the structures (Ferguson et al. 2018a).

Cross section A is from west to east across the northern extent of the study area from Poplar dome, through the Nesson anticline, to the eastern edge of the study area (Figure 7). Groundwater TDS near Poplar dome in the Dakota Group is ~8000–10,000 mg/L and increases downdip to the east reaching ~14,000 mg/L near the center of the Williston Basin. To the east of the Nesson anticline, TDS lowers to ~6000 mg/L as the Dakota Group elevation increases to the east. Uncertainty in the Dakota Group is higher toward the margins of section A and in the upper Dakota Group, and lower near the Nesson anticline inside the Inyan Kara Formation (Figure 7b).

Cross section B shows a north to south view across the Williston Basin (Figure 8). Here, groundwater TDS in the Dakota Group ranges from ~6900 to ~25,000 mg/L. It is common for TDS to increase with depth in groundwater systems (Kharaka and Hanor 2003; Gillespie et al. 2017; Metzger and Landon 2018; Stephens et al. 2019, 2021, 2025); however, there is a TDS inversion throughout much of the southern portion of section B. The inversion is accompanied by vertical stratification of TDS—again, particularly in the southern half of section B. There is a lower‐TDS band within the upper portion of the Inyan Kara Formation that is most pronounced near the center and southern parts of the Williston Basin. The inversion in the south is characterized by a zone of ~7000–10,000 mg/L water in the upper Inyan Kara Formation with ~15,000–25,000 mg/L above and below. The TDS inversion and stratification are less apparent in the northern half of the section. The TDS inversion is well constrained by resistivity input data, evidenced by the higher certainty throughout the Dakota Group, except far south in the Inyan Kara Formation (Figure 8b). The sample TDS data are also consistent with the inversion; however, sample TDS data are sparse and lack detail without the accompanying resistivity data.

Cross section C is from west to east through the center of the Williston Basin (Figure 9). The lowest TDS in the Dakota Group (~4800 mg/L) is found inside the Inyan Kara Formation near the center of the Williston Basin (~180 km along section, Figure 9a). Just below is the highest TDS in the study area at ~26,900 mg/L, creating the strongest vertical stratification of TDS resolved in this study. Furthermore, TDS in the upper Dakota Group at this location is ~19,000 mg/L, creating a TDS inversion. The stratification and inversion dissipate to the east as TDS decreases up‐dip. TDS patterns in the Dakota Group in section C are well constrained by input data except for some of the eastern portion, particularly in the upper Dakota Group (Figure 9b).

Groundwater Flow in the Dakota Group

The Dakota Group aquifer system is a regionally high‐flowing artesian system (Downey and Dinwiddie 1988; Bartos et al. 2022), and thus connate water in the Dakota Group has likely been flushed by large fluxes through the system (Peter 1982). Water currently in the Dakota Group is hypothesized to be a mix of

Meteoric water recharged from outcrops (recharge process #1, RP1),

Meteoric water recharged from outcrops (recharge process #1, RP1),

Groundwater inflows from underlying aquifers (recharge process #2, RP2), and

Groundwater inflows from underlying aquifers (recharge process #2, RP2), and

Groundwater inflows from overlying aquifers (recharge process #3, RP3).

Groundwater inflows from overlying aquifers (recharge process #3, RP3).

Understanding the processes controlling groundwater flow in the Dakota Group can provide critical hydrogeologic context for potential uses such as water supply and disposal. Detailed groundwater TDS patterns can be interpreted as a proxy for generalized regional flow paths by considering initial TDS conditions and subsequent flushing. Initial TDS values, or connate water TDS, would be near seawater TDS (~35,000 mg/L) in marine rocks and brackish‐to‐saline (>10,000 mg/L) in rocks deposited in marginal marine environments. Even while considering TDS can change from post‐depositional processes with the host rock (e.g., mixing, dissolution of evaporites, and other interactions), places where TDS is significantly lower than expected have likely been flushed by recharge (Peter 1982; Kharaka and Hanor 2003; Stephens et al. 2019, 2021). The detailed groundwater TDS structure mapped in this work provides more evidence to understand the complex recharge processes in the Dakota Group of the Williston Basin.

Compelling evidence for meteoric recharge (RP1) is found in mapped outcrops, measured infiltration rates, and elevated bicarbonate in groundwater near outcrops in central Montana (Peter 1982; Downey and Dinwiddie 1988; Bartos et al. 2022). To investigate inflows from surrounding rocks (RP2, RP3), Peter (1982) used geochemical trends along flow paths from central Montana outcrops to the Williston Basin and found that sodium (Na) plus potassium (K) and chloride (Cl) increase downgradient toward the Williston Basin. One interpretation is that Na, K, and Cl are entering the lower portion of the Inyan Kara Formation by upward leakage and begin to follow Dakota Group lateral regional flow paths. In this scenario, the ions are thought to be sourced from evaporites in underlying Paleozoic rocks (LeFever and LeFever 2005). The Paleozoic rocks have higher potentiometric surfaces than the Lower Cretaceous rocks, which supports this hypothesis (Downey and Dinwiddie 1988). Another potential explanation for increases in ion concentrations along flow paths could be water‐rock interactions as water residence time increases inside the Dakota Group. However, Dakota Group‐sourced ions may be less likely if connate water in the Dakota Group has been flushed by recharge.

Groundwater TDS modeling in this work shows patterns that support RP2. Elevated TDS in the lower Inyan Kara Formation occurs throughout the Williston Basin (Figure 10; uncertainty on Figure S8) and underlies lower‐TDS water within the upper Inyan Kara Formation, particularly in the center of the Williston Basin where potentiometric surfaces in the Paleozoic rocks are higher than the Lower Cretaceous. Additionally, the zones of highest TDS in the lower Inyan Kara Formation in the center of the Basin are collocated with evaporite deposits in underlying Paleozoic rocks (Downey and Dinwiddie 1988; Bartos et al. 2022). Therefore, the higher‐TDS in the Inyan Kara Formation is collocated with (1) an ion source and (2) upward groundwater potentials from the Paleozoic rocks. The higher‐TDS water in the lower Inyan Kara Formation (15,000–27,0000 mg/L) is denser than the overlying lower‐TDS water (4800–10,000 mg/L), suggesting that density‐driven flow may also contribute to groundwater flow dynamics and TDS patterns. Higher‐TDS water may preferentially stay lower in the Inyan Kara Formation, allowing for lower‐TDS flow above. Stagnation of saline groundwater is an accepted conceptual model for deeper Paleozoic rocks in the Williston Basin and other basins in North America (Downey and Dinwiddie 1988; Ferguson et al. 2018a). The mapped TDS patterns are consistent with density‐driven stratification and may also occur in the Dakota Group. Collectively, newly mapped TDS patterns, previous ion‐mapping along flow paths, hydraulic potential differentials, and density‐driven flow dynamics support the occurrence of groundwater inflows from underlying aquifers (RP2) in places in the lower Dakota Group.

Groundwater inflow into the upper Dakota Group (e.g., Newcastle Sandstone) from overlying rocks in the study area is poorly understood (RP3). Overall, there has been little evidence to support inflows into the upper Dakota Group from overlying rocks; however, groundwater TDS modeling in this work shows elevated TDS in the upper Dakota Group (up to ~20,000 mg/L, Figures 7, 8, 9, 10) that may align with RP3. Inflows from three potentially higher‐TDS source rocks are considered: (1) the Pierre Shale, (2) the Colorado Group, and (3) the Mowry Formation from within the Dakota Group representing intraflow. The potentiometric surface in the Upper Cretaceous aquifer is higher than the Lower Cretaceous (Peter 1982; Downey and Dinwiddie 1988), providing a potential gradient for downward flow toward the Dakota Group. Previous work suggested a potential pathway via fractures in the Pierre Shale overlying the Dakota Group, although fracture‐mapping work has focused only on South Dakota (Kolm and Peter 1982; Neuzil et al. 1982). The marine Pierre Shale connate water would likely be close to modern seawater TDS (~35,000 mg/L) which could be the source of higher TDS in the upper Dakota Group. Provided transmissive fractures exist connecting the upper Dakota Group and Pierre Shale, the mapped TDS patterns in the upper Dakota Group may represent inflows. The same fracture hypothesis could be applied for the marine Colorado Group, which would also have elevated TDS, but again, evidence is lacking. Alternatively, entrapped connate water in the shallow marine Newcastle Sandstone may explain the elevated TDS values in the upper Dakota Group. Entrapped connate water may be less likely given the relatively high‐flow nature of the Dakota Group, unless flow is restricted in the upper sections leaving them relatively isolated. Perhaps the most likely explanation for elevated TDS in the upper Dakota Group is connate water from the Mowry Formation mixing with the Newcastle Sandstone, given the proximity of the Mowry Formation and the lack of counter evidence. More evidence may be needed to conclude significant inflows into the upper Dakota Group (or mixing within the Dakota Group); however, detailed TDS mapping in this work provides another line of evidence to support such a process occurring.

The lower‐TDS values inside the upper Inyan Kara Formation under the Skull Creek Shale may represent a preferential regional flow path for lower‐TDS meteoric recharge (RP1, unlabeled magenta arrows on Figure 10). Historical evidence shows recharge originates from the west and using groundwater TDS as a proxy, flow follows the Dakota Group down‐dip to the center of the Williston Basin generally staying north of the Cedar Creek anticline (Figure 6), then travels up‐dip to the east and northeast indicated by the lower‐TDS trends on Figure 10. Along the flow paths, higher‐TDS water from underlying rocks (RP2) and potentially overlying rocks/Mowry Formation (RP3), along with density‐driven flow dynamics, may contribute to the stratified and inverted TDS patterns in the Dakota Group aquifer.

There are many advantages to the joint Gaussian process modeling used in this work such as the fact that discrete point data for all quantities can be included, and all the parameters (e.g., geophysical, geostatistical, and observational) are optimized at once using maximum likelihood. The Gaussian process model can model the four quantities in 3‐dimensional space despite anisotropic, trended, noisy data with multiple scales of covariance patterns. However, a potential limitation of this approach is that the geophysical parameters are not allowed to spatially vary. Conceivably, the rock properties (pore space geometry, degree of cementation, clay content) upon which the Archie's Law relationship depends may vary across the basin‐scale model, but our model lacks the flexibility to account for this, which may lead to localized inaccuracies in TDS prediction in a basin‐scale model. We verified the model is overall unbiased with a spatially dependent cross validation scheme (Section S3), but we cannot prove that petrophysical relationships never vary, since TDS sample data are not available evenly throughout the study area.

Conclusions

This work applied advanced groundwater TDS modeling techniques at the basin scale to map deep groundwater and pore space resources. Characterizing deep resources can benefit sustainable resource management plans in sedimentary basins around the globe. Incorporating hydrocarbon well geophysical data to map groundwater systems can also provide insights into deeper, understudied hydrological processes.

Groundwater TDS and porosity distributions in the Dakota Group at the Williston Basin were modeled in high resolution with existing sample data and newly analyzed hydrocarbon borehole geophysical data. TDS modeling produced detailed maps that provide support of some previous hypotheses on recharge pathways and is a new quantitative reference for water users in the region.
