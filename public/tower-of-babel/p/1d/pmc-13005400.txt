A Challenging Pulmonary Embolism: When Acute Meets Chronic Thrombus
Authors: Jeffrey CY Lee, Nickpreet Singh, Rahul Sakhuja, Rory Gallen, Maya Serhal, Robert Schainfeld, Kenneth Rosenfield
Published: 2026

A 24-year-old man with refractory hypercoagulability (not otherwise specified) was admitted for repeated high-risk pulmonary embolism (PE). His first presentation years ago required veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) support followed by pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE). Recently, while on warfarin, he developed another large saddle PE with right ventricular (RV) thrombus. Treatment with heparin resulted in massive hemoptysis, and ultimately, he underwent a second PTE with RV thrombus removal. Despite upgrading anticoagulation to low molecular-weight heparin, he developed a third episode of saddle/massive PE months later. This was treated with a combination of catheter mechanical thrombectomy plus catheter-directed lysis. He recovered well, and an echocardiogram at this point showed normal RV function with a systolic pressure of 40 mm Hg. He was discharged with a supratherapeutic dose of home low molecular-weight heparin. On follow-up, he reported being asymptomatic, although a ventilation-perfusion scan showed the possibility of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension. Unfortunately, even with a peak anti-Xa level of 1.29 IU/mL (usual target range: 0.6-1.0 IU/mL), he still developed his fourth (current) high-risk PE in close succession.

On assessment, he was tachycardic and required 6L O2 supplement. Computed tomography imaging showed acute on chronic PE. The echocardiogram now showed severe right heart strain with a RV systolic pressure of 59 mm Hg. There was also a tricuspid valve (TV) thrombus, associated with severe regurgitation and pulmonary hypertension (Supplemental Video 1). The PE response team (PERT) decision was made for percutaneous TV, followed by pulmonary thrombectomy.

After bilateral femoral venous access, an AngioVac F18 catheter (AngioDynamics) was directed toward the TV under transoesophageal echo guidance. A large amount of mobile thrombus was debrided with good tricuspid regurgitation (TR) reduction (Figure 1; Supplemental Video 2). Right heart catheterization showed a pulmonary arterial pressure of 99/42 mm Hg (mean: 60 mm Hg) and a cardiac index (by Fick) of 1.9 L/min/m2, reflecting the severity of his condition. This was followed by right-sided pulmonary thrombectomy, which conversely yielded only a few clots. At this point, the patient deteriorated rapidly and required VA-ECMO cannulation. Repeat angiogram showed significantly worsened pulmonary perfusion and segmental perfusion defects, revealing multiple chronic branch pulmonary artery occlusions.

Initial computed tomography showed acute bilateral PE in the main PAs (highlighted in red) (A). Intraprocedure transoesophageal echo showed a dilated right ventricle and guided the Angiovac catheter toward the TV thrombus (circled in red) (B). It was successfully removed (circled in blue) (C). Pulmonary angiogram showed severe perfusion defects bilaterally with chronic total occlusions (E, F). Pulmonary blood flow significantly worsened despite thrombectomy attempts (D, G, H). BPA of the right and left lung was performed (I, J) with partial improvement. A small amount of chronic and organized thrombus was retrieved (K). The procedure was concluded with EKOS placement (L). BPA, balloon pulmonary angioplasty; PE, pulmonary embolism; TV, tricuspid valve.

There was concern with continuing venovenous bypass aspiration. PERT was reconvened to consider surgery, but the risk of a third PTE and resternotomy in an emergency setting was deemed prohibitive. Thrombectomy was repeated with a large-bore catheter (FlowTriever, Inari), which yielded scanty chronic and organized thrombi. Recognizing futility, balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) was performed using a 4.0 × 20 mm Takeru balloon (Terumo), which partially restored pulmonary blood flow. The left lung was treated similarly with FlowTriever aspiration followed by BPA with a 5.0 × 40 mm Armada balloon (Abbott). Attempting to maximize lung perfusion, 6 hours of EKOS catheter-directed lysis (Boston Scientific) was performed at the end, with a plan for inpatient staged BPA. Unfortunately, despite advanced therapies, the patient died from infectious complications.

These images show a challenging and drastic presentation of acute on chronic PE. Upfront BPA may be considered to restore pulmonary blood flow in desperate cases. Deliberation should also be given to the choice of interventional devices, factoring in the patient’s hemodynamics.1
