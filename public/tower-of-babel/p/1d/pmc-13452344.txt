Pregnancy in a patient with glycogen storage disease type Ia (von Gierke disease): case report and management strategy
Authors: Camila Andrea Camargo Rodríguez, Cristian Rolando Avellaneda-Garnica, Rafael Leonardo Aragón-Mendoza, Yaisa Maritza Castaño Toro, David Rodriguez Buritica
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glycogen storage disease type I (GSDI) is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the gene encoding the glucose-6-phosphatase complex. Deficiency of this enzyme in the liver, kidneys, and intestines leads to glycogen accumulation in these organs and, consequently, to inadequate glucose production due to impaired glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. The condition is rare, and only a limited number of cases have been reported in pregnant women with favorable maternal and perinatal outcomes. This is partly because GSDI has been associated with significant morbidity, including maternal metabolic disturbances, renal and/or hepatic disease, preeclampsia, and fetal growth restriction, among other complications. We present the case of a 23-year-old woman with a diagnosis of GSDI who was evaluated at 19 weeks of pregnancy and managed according to a multidisciplinary protocol, resulting in a favorable perinatal outcome. This case highlights the need for clinical management protocols with specific strategies during the preconception period, pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum, aimed at preventing complications and improving maternal and perinatal outcomes.

Introduction

Glycogen storage diseases comprise a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders caused by defects in enzymes or transport proteins involved in glycogen metabolism, leading to abnormal accumulation of this polysaccharide in various tissues.1 Glycogen storage disease type I (GSDI) is an autosomal recessive inherited disorder, first described by von Gierke in 1929, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 per 100,000 to 300,000 individuals.2, 3, 4, 5 This condition results from a defect in the glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) enzyme complex, which plays a central role in both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis by catalyzing the hydrolysis of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to generate free glucose.2

G6Pase is a multicomponent complex composed of a catalytic subunit located on the luminal side of the endoplasmic reticulum and one or more membrane transporters.2 Defects in the catalytic subunit are classified as GSDIa, whereas deficiency in the microsomal transport protein is classified as GSDIb.2,3 Impaired G6Pase activity in the liver, kidneys, and intestine leads to glycogen accumulation in these organs as well as to inadequate glucose production (Figure).2FigurePathophysiology and maternal–fetal implications of glycogen storage disease type Ia during pregnancy. GSDIa can be suspected as early as 6 months of age due to symptoms related to hypoglycemia, which may initially be nonspecific, such as feeding difficulties or apnea, or manifestations associated with the hypoglycemic response, including tremors, pallor, sweating, seizures, drowsiness, coma, and lactic acidosis.3,4 The condition may also be associated with hepatomegaly, which typically becomes more evident with increasing age.3 Diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical history, biochemical abnormalities, molecular genetic testing, and/or enzymatic findings obtained from liver biopsy.3 Over the past decade, DNA mutation analysis has become the primary diagnostic method,6 typically through full sequencing of the G6PC gene in patients with GSDIa, or, when liver biopsy tissue is available, through assessment of G6Pase enzymatic activity.3,7

GSDIa is associated with several metabolic abnormalities, including hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperuricemia, and hyperlactatemia (Figure).4 Clinical manifestations may also include xanthomas, pancreatitis, and cholecystitis because of markedly elevated lipid levels. In addition, hepatocellular adenomas have been reported in affected patients, carrying a risk of malignant transformation. Progressive renal disease may also occur, characterized by hyperfiltration, proteinuria, renal failure, and secondary hypertension. Hematologic abnormalities such as anemia and platelet dysfunction have also been described.2,4

The main goal of treatment is to prevent hypoglycemia and secondary metabolic disturbances. Management consists of a strict dietary regimen that includes restriction of lactose, galactose, fructose, and sucrose, administration of uncooked cornstarch at regular intervals, and multivitamin supplementation. This approach is often combined with medications aimed at controlling metabolic complications, such as allopurinol, antihypertensive drugs, and lipid-lowering agents.4,6

Given the low prevalence of the disease, only a limited number of case reports describing pregnancies in patients with GSDIa have been published.3,6 This poses significant challenges for clinicians, as appropriate management and close monitoring of the condition are required from the preconception period through pregnancy, delivery, and the postpartum period to achieve optimal perinatal outcomes.7 Because the available literature remains scarce and lacks a unified management protocol, the aim of this article is to present the case of a woman at 19 weeks of pregnancy with a diagnosis of GSDIa who was managed through a multidisciplinary approach with a favorable perinatal outcome, and to propose an institutional management protocol to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 23-year-old primigravid woman at 19 weeks of gestation who was asymptomatic during prenatal care and had a diagnosis of GSDIa, homozygous for the pathogenic variant commonly observed in individuals of Ashkenazi ancestry, p.Arg83Cys (R83C), associated with markedly reduced G6PC enzymatic activity. She was under follow-up by the multidisciplinary Inborn Errors of Metabolism team at a quaternary-level university hospital.

Prior to pregnancy, the patient experienced a severe episode of hypertriglyceridemia (>3000 mg/dL) due to poor adherence to treatment, which required plasmapheresis followed by management with dietary measures, choline fenofibrate, rosuvastatin, and icosapent ethyl. These medications were discontinued once pregnancy was recognized due to their contraindication and potential risks during gestation. At the beginning of prenatal care, she had evidence of nephropathy characterized by pregestational nephrotic-range proteinuria without prior treatment, with preserved glomerular filtration rate. She also had a history of acute pancreatitis 1 year before the current pregnancy, secondary to severe hypertriglyceridemia.

Regarding her gynecologic and obstetric history, the patient was a primigravida who had not attended a preconception consultation; however, the pregnancy was desired, and prenatal care was initiated late. At the first prenatal visit at 19 weeks of gestation, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with a body mass index of 28 kg/m², a uterine height of 18 cm, and a single live fetus. The obstetric examination was within normal limits. A multidisciplinary follow-up of the pregnancy was conducted according to the institutional protocol created based on the available literature, published case reports, and multidisciplinary expert consensus. Nutritional management was supervised by the nutrition team, with glycemic control monitored using a continuous glucose monitoring system. The institutional glycemic target during pregnancy was 70 to 120 mg/dL, aiming to minimize hypoglycemia while maintaining metabolic stability. Although detailed CGM metrics were not available for retrospective review, analysis of 230 documented capillary glucose measurements showed a mean glucose value of 92.9 mg/dL (range 53–140 mg/dL). Approximately 91% of values were within the target range. Only 11 measurements were below 70 mg/dL, and 4 were below 60 mg/dL. No severe hypoglycemic episodes requiring emergency intervention or hospitalization occurred during pregnancy. Adjustments were made to the dose and timing of uncooked cornstarch administration, with doses of 35 g every 3 hours during the day (7:00 am–7:00 pm) and 50 g every 3 hours at night (7:00 pm–7:00 am), to maintain adequate glycemic without episodes of hypoglycemia. The patient achieved a total gestational weight gain of 16 kg, with adjustments to the cornstarch dose, increasing by 1 gram per dose per week, according to information from both the continuous glucose monitor and the glucometer.

During pregnancy, lipid monitoring revealed severe hypertriglyceridemia early in gestation, with triglyceride levels of 1731 mg/dL at 15 weeks, decreasing to 620 mg/dL at 23 weeks and 399 mg/dL at 31 weeks of gestation. At 23 weeks, total cholesterol was 190 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol 35 mg/dL, and uric acid 4.1 mg/dL. Management consisted of dietary intervention and quarterly lipid profile monitoring, without the need for additional pharmacological treatment. By the third trimester, triglyceride levels had reached the target value of <531 mg/dL (<6.0 mmol/L). Despite severe hypertriglyceridemia during early pregnancy, no episodes of pancreatitis occurred throughout gestation. Renal function tests revealed a 24-hour proteinuria of 756 mg/d with a creatinine clearance of 264 mL/min. Quarterly follow-up evaluations were performed without deterioration of renal function or increase in proteinuria. Low-dose aspirin was initiated at first trimester gestation for preeclampsia prophylaxis because of the patient’s underlying nephropathy, which was discontinued at 36 weeks of gestation.

Additional laboratory tests were performed during pregnancy, including perinatal infection screening with serologic tests for HIV and syphilis, as well as liver function and thyroid function tests, all of which were within normal limits. A quarterly ultrasound evaluation of the liver and biliary tract was performed, which showed no evidence of hepatic adenomas but revealed hepatic steatosis. Serial obstetric ultrasounds were conducted to monitor fetal well-being, demonstrating an appropriate fetal growth trajectory and no structural abnormalities.

At 38 weeks of gestation, a cesarean section was performed without complications, resulting in the delivery of a healthy live newborn weighing 2960 g with APGAR scores of 8/9/9. Prior to the procedure and during the postpartum period, the patient received an infusion of dextrose in distilled water with a metabolic flow of 120 mL per hour, followed by early initiation of oral intake, with the aim of maintaining blood glucose levels between 70 and 100 mg/dL, given that prolonged fasting must be avoided in these patients. Peripartum metabolic monitoring included lactate assessment, with values of 1.79 and 1.75 mmol/L, both within normal limits. The patient had an adequate postoperative course without complications, tolerated discontinuation of the dextrose infusion, and was discharged on postoperative day three with a copper T intrauterine device. At birth, the newborn remained clinically stable and asymptomatic. Initial capillary glucose measurement was 91 mg/dL and did not require intervention. The infant demonstrated adequate feeding behavior, normal urine and meconium output, and no episodes of apnea or clinical manifestations suggestive of neonatal hypoglycemia. At 6 months of follow-up, the newborn continued to show no evidence of metabolic abnormalities and remained under surveillance by genetics and neonatology services. The patient also remained free of complications and continued multidisciplinary follow-up for her underlying condition.

Discussion

GSDIa, also known as von Gierke disease, is caused by defects in the G6Pase complex in the liver, kidneys, and intestine, leading to the accumulation of glycogen in these organs and consequently to inadequate glucose production.2, 3, 4 The case presented above provides an example of the multidisciplinary approach that should be taken with a patient with this disease who is facing pregnancy. Multidisciplinary management, including dietary recommendations such as frequent meals, glucose infusions, and cornstarch therapy, improves prognosis and reduces (although does not eliminate) the frequency of long-term complications.7 The scarcity of reported cases of pregnancy in this disease has made it difficult to establish standardized management recommendations.7,8

In patients with a desire for pregnancy, a preconception consultation is recommended in order to optimize metabolic control, assess the risk or presence of polycystic ovary syndrome, suppress increases in serum uric acid levels, and manage dyslipidemia, nephropathy, and hepatic adenomas.9 From the prenatal period onward, it is essential to maintain adequate control of the disease to allow women to achieve pregnancy, even in those with severe disease-related complications such as renal failure or significant metabolic disturbances.8

A comprehensive baseline evaluation should be established, including a lipid profile, liver and kidney function tests, 24-hour proteinuria, and liver imaging, with the aim of estimating the risk of metabolic and obstetric complications that may arise in patients with this condition.9 Liver imaging should be performed due to the risk of adenoma development or growth and the risk of bleeding, which is higher in adenomas larger than 5 cm.9 During pregnancy, energy requirements increase.7 Pregnant patients with GSDIa are usually treated with strategies aimed at preventing hypoglycemia, including continuous glucose monitoring and progressive adjustments in caloric intake through cornstarch supplementation, increasing the dose by approximately 1 gram of cornstarch per dose per week, depending on metabolic control.9 A multidisciplinary approach with specialized prenatal care is essential, with monitoring of the most common metabolic complications,10 and surveillance of fetal growth and well-being, with the aim of establishing an individualized delivery plan.11,12

In most cases reported in the literature, delivery has been performed by cesarean section. The optimal mode of delivery in women with GSDIa remains individualized and should be based on obstetric indications and the ability to maintain metabolic stability. Previous reports have demonstrated that successful vaginal delivery is feasible when strict metabolic monitoring, continuous glucose supplementation, laboratory evaluation, and measures to avoid prolonged fasting can be ensured. It is very important to note that lactate levels may rise significantly due to the physiological stress of labor, potentially increasing maternal and fetal risk.11,12 In our case, although no absolute obstetric indication for cesarean delivery existed, a planned cesarean section at 38 weeks was chosen after multidisciplinary discussion to minimize prolonged fasting and the risk of catabolic decompensation. This decision also reflected the practical limitations of maintaining continuous metabolic surveillance during a potentially prolonged labor in our healthcare setting.

An association between GSDI and severe preeclampsia as well as fetal growth restriction has been described. Placental examinations in these women have demonstrated accelerated villous maturation, subchorionic pseudoinfarctions, and reduced placental size, suggesting that GSDIa and its associated metabolic disturbances may directly affect placental structure.8,11 The condition has also been linked to other perinatal complications, including preterm birth, macrosomia, and nonreassuring fetal status.8 However, in most cases reported in the literature involving pregnant patients with GSDIa, newborns were healthy and showed normal psychomotor development (Figure).9

GSDIa follows an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance.13 Because the mother carries biallelic pathogenic variants in G6PC, the newborn is expected to inherit one pathogenic allele. Paternal carrier status was not assessed. Nevertheless, the absence of metabolic abnormalities during follow-up makes GSDIa unlikely. Therefore, genetic counseling and consideration of neonatal evaluation are recommended when the paternal genotype is unknown, given the risk of neonatal hypoglycemia.7 The newborn should be able to tolerate a fasting period of 3 to 4 hours. If blood glucose levels are found to be below 60 mg/dL, further diagnostic evaluation should be initiated by the genetics team.7

In our case, the patient demonstrated adequate control of her disease prior to pregnancy, with stable proteinuria, preserved renal function, normal liver function, and no evidence of hepatic adenomas. Although a formal preconception consultation had not been conducted, the appropriate management of her underlying condition likely contributed to a reduced risk of maternal and perinatal complications. During pregnancy, care was coordinated under an institutional protocol involving a multidisciplinary team that included maternal–fetal medicine, endocrinology, nutrition, psychology, genetics, anesthesiology, and neonatology services.

Although pregnancies in women with GSDIa have been previously reported, this case contributes to the limited body of evidence available on pregnancy outcomes in this rare metabolic disorder. To the best of our knowledge, it represents the first published case from Colombia and one of the few reported from Latin America. Given the rarity of GSDIa and the limited clinical experience in many healthcare settings, this framework may provide a practical reference to support the management of pregnant women with GSDIa and facilitate coordinated multidisciplinary care.

Conclusion

GSDI is a rare condition with few reported cases associated with pregnancy, and currently, there are no standardized management guidelines. It has been associated with maternal morbidity, including metabolic disturbances, renal disease, hepatic disease, and preeclampsia, as well as fetal morbidity such as fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, macrosomia, and nonreassuring fetal status.

The development of clinical management protocols for pregnant patients with this condition, integrating a multidisciplinary approach during the preconception period, pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum, may help prevent complications and improve perinatal outcomes.
