Predictors of organizational commitment among nurses in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Yoseph Worku, Belayneh Lulseged, Hirut Megersa, Mahlet Seyoum, Jemal Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrganizational commitment among nurses is a critical job attitude that affects overall success, effectiveness, and quality of healthcare. There is a dearth of evidence regarding the degree of commitment among nurses and the potential predictors that can influence their organizational commitment in Africa, including Ethiopia. This study assessed the magnitude of organizational commitment and its predictors among nurses working at public hospital medical colleges in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in 2024.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study and employed multistage sampling to randomly select nurses working at three public hospital medical colleges in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. We computed the commitment of the nurses based on the 20-item organizational commitment scale, which uses a five-point Likert scale. To analyze the data, we employed descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 411 nurses participated in the study. Females comprised 51.8%, and the median age was 29.0 years (interquartile range: 27.0–34.0). The mean score of organizational commitment among nurses was 62.9 (standard deviation = 13.6) out of 100. Four factors were identified as independent predictors of the organizational commitment scores of nurses: age (B = 0.256, p = 0.010), job satisfaction (B = 2.586, p < 0.001), relationships with colleagues (B = 1.836, p = 0.001), and perceived organizational support (B = 0.631, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe mean score of organizational commitment among nurses working in hospital medical colleges in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, was 62.9, indicating a need for improvement. The key predictors of the organizational commitment of nurses were age, job satisfaction, relationships with colleagues, and perceived organizational support. Enhancing job satisfaction, perceived organizational support, and relationships among coworkers would be important strategies that can improve the organizational commitment of nurses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13561-4.Keywords: Organizational commitment, Nurse, Predictors, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia

Introduction

In the modern, complex, competitive, and ever-evolving health sector, the success of health facilities is determined by several factors related to the performance of health professionals. Employees’ job attitudes are influential determinants of organizational effectiveness and success [1, 2]. Job attitudes encompass the feelings, beliefs, and predispositions that employees have toward their job, work environment, and organization [3, 4]. Organizational commitment is one of these job attitudes and is defined as the emotional attachment, loyalty, and identification that employees feel toward their organization. It is manifested through three dimensions: affective commitment, continuance commitment, and normative commitment. Organizational commitment is a key factor that can influence both employee performance and organizational success [1–5].

Nurses are core healthcare professionals, serving as primary caregivers and patient advocates while playing pivotal roles in the sector’s success. The Ethiopian Health Sector Transformation Plan indicates that there is low motivation and satisfaction, high turnover, and a scarcity of specialized professionals, including nurses, among the health workforce. These challenges in the health sector are associated with the suboptimal quality of services. Enhancing the job attitudes of nurses, including their organizational commitment, can help address these challenges in the health sector [6].

Studies have revealed that higher organizational commitment among nurses is linked to better organizational performance, patient safety, patient care, operational effectiveness, and lower turnover or intent to leave [7–15]. Decreased organizational commitment is associated with various unfavorable outcomes, such as medical errors, repeated hospitalizations, and increased duration of hospital stay [16].

Despite the influential role of nurses’ organizational commitment in overall organizational performance and employee outcomes, some studies indicate that little attention is given to it in the health sector—more than 85% of nurses’ time is dedicated only to activities that are directly or indirectly related to patient care [17]. The evidence shows that the organizational commitment of nurses is low in different health settings in Ethiopia and other parts of the world [17–22].

Several factors can determine the organizational commitment of nurses. Although there are inconsistent research findings, some of the factors include age, years of experience, educational status, marital status, job position, salary and benefits package, cohesion and work relationships with coworkers, level of support from supervisors or hospitals, good mentorship from supervisors, leadership style, and relationships with supervisors [19–21, 23–29]. In several studies, job satisfaction has been identified as another important predictor of organizational commitment among nurses [19–22, 28–34]. Similarly, some studies have shown that perceived organizational support is a significant predictor of organizational commitment among nurses [19, 21, 22, 25].

Nurses’ organizational commitment is one of the least researched topics in Africa, including Ethiopia. There is a lack of consistent evidence in previous studies. We have limited local knowledge in Ethiopia regarding organizational commitment, specifically in public hospital medical colleges in Addis Ababa. This gap hinders our ability to develop evidence-based policies and strategies that can enhance nurses’ performance and job satisfaction, ultimately improving the overall performance of the health sector. Therefore, we investigated the magnitude of organizational commitment and its predictors among nurses working at public hospital medical colleges in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in 2024.

Methods

Study design and setting

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted among nurses working at three public hospital medical colleges located in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from April to June 2024. The hospitals included St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC), Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College (Y12HMC), and Menilik II Referral Hospital/Medical College, which are the only institutions operating as hospital colleges in the City Administration. Approximately 2,100 nurses were employed at the three colleges.

Study participants

In the study, the following criteria were used for eligibility to participate: nurses who were employed by the colleges/hospitals as permanent staff, had worked for a minimum of six months, and were available during the study period.

The estimated sample size for the second objective was larger, and 450 study participants were enrolled in the study.

Based on the total number of eligible nurses working in the three hospitals, the 450-sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital: 214 for St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, 129 for Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College, and 107 for Menilik II Hospital/Medical College. The allotted sample size was proportionally allocated to each department based on the total number of eligible nurses working in the respective departments. From each department, study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique from a sampling frame prepared based on the official list of active nurses who were on duty during the data collection period.

Data collection instruments and procedure

The data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire that was initially prepared in English, translated to Amharic, and then translated back to English to check the consistency of the translations. The study participants completed the Amharic version online via Google Forms. The questionnaire was developed by the investigators based on reviewed literature [19–22, 31] and a conceptual framework [35]. It consists of six sections that assess the participants’ demographic characteristics, extrinsic (or work-related) characteristics, perceived organizational support, job satisfaction, and organizational commitment. To facilitate data collection, eight nurses with research experience were recruited, along with three health professionals with Master’s degrees as supervisors. Both the data collectors and the supervisors received a one-day training. The questionnaire was pretested at Ras Desta Damtew Memorial Hospital, with responses from 25 nurses (5.6% of the estimated sample size). Following the pretest, ambiguous and unclear questions were revised, and unnecessary questions were eliminated. Data collected from the pretest were not used in the final data analysis.

Variables and measurements

The dependent variable of the study was organizational commitment among nurses. The independent variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, level of education, marital status, years of service, place of work), extrinsic variables or work-related characteristics (rank, position, salary and benefits package, working environment, professional autonomy, relationship with supervisor and other staff, opportunities for professional and career development), perceived organizational support, and job satisfaction.

Organizational commitment was assessed using the method developed by Alan and Meyer (1990). The tool consists of 20 questions with three dimensions: the affective commitment scale (ACS), the continuance commitment scale (CCS), and the normative commitment scale (NCS). The self-rating used a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree) [36]. Each participant’s organizational commitment score was represented by the total score derived from 20 response items. The overall organizational commitment score was then calculated as the mean of all participants’ individual scores.

Perceived organizational support was assessed using the Survey of Perceived Organization Support (eight-item form) developed by Eisenberger et al. in 1997. This form has eight questions that are answered using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree). Two of the items were scored in reverse order. It is a validated and widely used tool [37]. Each participant’s perceived organizational support score was represented by the total score derived from eight response items. The overall perceived organizational support score was then calculated as the mean of all participants’ individual scores.

Job satisfaction was assessed using a single-item global measure of overall job satisfaction as recommended by other researchers based on its acceptable validity and reliability compared with multiple-item scales. The tool uses a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 7 (1 = very strongly disagree and 7 = very strongly agree) [38].

Reliability and validity of the data

To enhance the reliability and validity of the data, the data collectors and supervisors received training; the questionnaire was pretested and revised; close supervision was maintained throughout the data collection; validated and widely used tools were employed to measure perceived organizational support and organizational commitment across diverse populations and settings; and the questionnaire was reviewed and critiqued by subject matter experts. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were used to assess the internal consistency of the perceived organizational support and organizational commitment variables, yielding values of 0.828 and 0.891, respectively—both indicating good reliability.

Data analysis

The collected data were coded and cleaned before analysis. The completeness and consistency of the data were checked. The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range, minimum, and maximum values, were used to compute and summarize sociodemographic and other variables. Furthermore, all necessary statistical assumptions for the multiple linear regression model were evaluated. Model fit was assessed using coefficient of determination (R2). The distribution of the outcome variable was examined for normality using histogram, Q-Q plot, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The linearity of the residuals was evaluated using a P-P plot. The homoscedasticity was assessed graphically via scatter plots. The autocorrelation of the independent variables and residuals was tested using the Durbin-Watson statistic. The multicollinearity among the independent variables was examined using the variance inflation factor (VIF). To control for confounding variables and identify independent predictors of organizational commitment, a multiple linear regression model with backward elimination technique was employed. Unstandardized coefficients were utilized to assess the magnitude of the effect of the independent variables on the dependent variable, on organizational commitment. Unstandardized coefficients were preferred over standardized coefficients to maintain the original measurement units and interpret the effects based on the actual observed changes. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and organizational profile of the respondents

Out of the 450 nurses recruited for the study, 411 responses were complete and eligible for analysis, resulting in a response rate of 91.3%. Two hundred thirteen (51.8%) were female, the median age was 29.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 27.0–34.0), and 193 (47%) were married. Three hundred ten participants (75.4%) held a diploma or first degree. The median monthly income of the participants was 8008.50 Ethiopian Birr, with an IQR of 7000–10,000.

Approximately half of the participants (51.6%) were from St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, whereas 104 (25.3%) and 95 (23.1%) were from Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College and Menilik II Hospital/College, respectively. Regarding their place of assignment, 99 (24.1%), 79 (19.2%), 72 (17.5%), and 47 (11.4%) of the participants were from surgical, pediatrics, internal medicine, and academic or research departments, respectively. Approximately three-fourths of the participants (73.5%) were staff nurses with no leadership or supervisory role. The median length of service for the participants at the college or hospital was 5.0 years (IQR: 4.0–8.0) (Table 1).

Predictors of organizational commitment using multiple linear regression model

All necessary statistical assumptions for multiple linear regression were evaluated, and none were violated. The assumption of normality was met, as evidenced by the Q-Q plot and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p = 0.200). The scatter plot revealed no signs of heteroscedasticity. Autocorrelation was not detected, with a Durbin-Watson statistic of 1.991. Multicollinearity was also ruled out, as all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below 1.164, indicating that there was no substantial correlation among the independent variables.

A multiple linear regression model was employed to identify the independent predictors of nurses’ organizational commitment. The final model, derived using the backward elimination technique, explained 36% of the variability in nurses’ organizational commitment (R2 = 0.358 and F (4, 406) = 56.721, p < 0.001). Four variables were retained in the final model, each demonstrating a significant association with nurses’ organizational commitment: age, relationships with colleagues or coworkers, perceived organizational support, and job satisfaction.

For each additional year of age, nurses’ organizational commitment increased by 0.256 units (95% CI: 0.061, 0.452). For every unit increase in job satisfaction, nurses’ organizational commitment increased by 2.586 units (95% CI: 1.964, 3.208). For each unit increase in self-rated relationships with colleagues or coworkers, nurses’ organizational commitment increased by 1.836 units (95% CI: 0.759, 2.914). Similarly, for a one-unit increase in perceived organizational support, nurses’ organizational commitment increased by 0.631 units (95% CI: 0.453, 0.809) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study investigates organizational commitment, a key yet underexplored job attribute in the health sector, and its determinants among nurses at public medical colleges in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The study revealed that the overall organizational commitment score of nurses was relatively low, and the predictors of organizational commitment were age, job satisfaction, relationships with colleagues, and perceived organizational support. The mean score of organizational commitment among nurses working in public hospital medical colleges in Addis Ababa was 62.9 (SD = 13.6), or 62.9% (95% CI: 61.5%, 64.2%). This score is lower than the findings from the Jimma University Teaching Hospital study (mean score: 70.45) conducted in 2017 among nurses [21] and the Benchsheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia study (percentage mean score: 74.6%) conducted in 2021 among health professionals [22]. However, the mean commitment score in the current study was notably higher than that reported in a multicenter study conducted in 2022 in Addis Ababa among health professionals (percentage mean score: 48.6%) [19], as well as in the study by Fantahun et al. (2021) among health professionals working in public hospitals in southwestern Oromia, Ethiopia, which reported 48.8% organizational commitment [20]. The discrepancies among the studies mentioned above might be related to differences in study settings, types of health professionals, and the years in which the studies were conducted. This insufficient organizational commitment may be associated with unfavorable outcomes in the workplace, such as high staff turnover, decreased job satisfaction, lower performance, and reduced quality of healthcare. Therefore, hospitals, the health bureau, and the Ministry of Health should work to increase nurses’ commitment by identifying specific barriers.

One key determinant of the organizational commitment score of nurses was the age of the participants. For each additional year of age, the nurses’ organizational commitment increased by 0.256 units. A similar finding was reported in a study by Arage et al. that investigated the predictors of the organizational commitment of health professionals in Addis Ababa [19]. This might be related to stability due to experience in their roles, consideration of the long-term duration of career investment, established relationships with colleagues, alignment of staff values with organizational values, family commitments, and retirement plans. To improve overall organizational commitment among nurses, hospitals and managers should provide more attention, mentorship, and support to younger staff, who constitute a larger segment of the study participants. Conversely, studies by Israel et al. [21] and Alemayehu et al. [22] reported that age was not a significant determinant of organizational commitment. These inconsistencies might be attributed to differences in study settings and analytical approaches, for example, age was analyzed as a continuous variable in the current study, whereas one of the previous studies treated it as a categorized variable.

Job satisfaction among nurses was positively associated with their organizational commitment. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in Addis Ababa [19], southwestern Oromia [20], Jimma [21], and Benchsheko Zone in Southwest Ethiopia [22], which revealed a positive relationship between job satisfaction and the organizational commitment of health professionals, including nurses. Similarly, other studies conducted in different parts of the world have shown that job satisfaction is associated with nurses’ organizational commitment [11, 14, 30]. Nurses with greater job satisfaction are likely to exhibit greater organizational commitment due to shared and aligned values, a conducive work environment, increased motivation, emotional attachment, and supportive relationships with colleagues. To improve the organizational commitment of nurses, hospitals and supervisors should address any bottlenecks or factors that lower employee job satisfaction.

Another factor influencing nurses’ organizational commitment is their relationships with colleagues. For each one-unit increase in nurses’ self-rated relationships with colleagues or coworkers, their organizational commitment increased by 1.836 units. Similar findings were reported by Israel et al., who assessed the determinants of organizational commitment among nurses at Jimma University Teaching Hospital [21] and by Arage et al., who investigated the factors associated with the organizational commitment of health professionals at Addis Ababa [19]. The positive association between nurses’ relationships with their colleagues and organizational commitment may stem from a conducive work environment that is created by trusting and collaborative relationships. Therefore, to increase nurses’ organizational commitment, hospitals should consider improving working relationships among staff.

The perceived organizational support score was another factor positively associated with the organizational commitment of nurses. For a one-unit increase in nurses’ perceived organizational support, their organizational commitment increased by 0.631 units. This finding is consistent with other studies that assessed the predictors of organizational commitment in Ethiopia [19, 21, 22] and China [25]. The positive association between perceived organizational support and organizational commitment scores has been documented in the literature [3, 4]. This correlation arises because nurses who feel supported by their organization are more likely to be satisfied, emotionally attached, and committed to their organization.

In the current study, neither leadership roles nor working departments were significantly associated with nurses’ organizational commitment. This contrasts with findings from Alemayehu et al. in the Benchsheko Zone in Southwest Ethiopia, where leadership role was a key determinant [22], and Israel et al. at Jimma University Teaching Hospital, where department affiliation significantly influenced organizational commitment [21]. These discrepancies likely reflect contextual differences in study settings, including the level and number of health facilities, as well as departmental characteristics.

Although the study revealed interesting and relevant findings, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. Recall bias may have occurred if respondents did not accurately remember past events when completing the questionnaire. Additionally, the self-reported nature of the data may have introduced social desirability bias.

Conclusions

The study revealed that the overall mean score of organizational commitment among nurses was modest, highlighting the need for improvement. The predictors of organizational commitment among nurses in public hospital medical colleges include age, job satisfaction, relationships with colleagues, and perceived organizational support. These findings underscore the importance of fostering a supportive work environment and targeted retention strategies within hospital settings to increase staff commitment and continuity of care.

Recommendations

The Ministry of Health, Addis Ababa City Administration Health Bureau, and hospitals should work to improve nurses’ organizational commitment by formulating policies and implementing programs that promote a supportive work environment, enhance job satisfaction, reinforce perceived organizational support, and introduce mentorship initiatives for nurses with low levels of commitment. Additionally, hospitals, managers, and supervisors should give particular attention to younger staff when designing interventions and setting priorities, as organizational commitment tends to increase with age.
