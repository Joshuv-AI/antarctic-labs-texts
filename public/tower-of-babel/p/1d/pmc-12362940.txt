Association between dietary inflammatory index and hepatic steatosis: cross-sectional evidence from the NHANES 2007–2020
Authors: Zhounan Wu, Yuhang Liang, Li Zeng, Jiayi Fang, Jinshen He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough inflammation and dietary habits have been linked to obesity-associated hepatic steatosis, the role of diet-mediated inflammation in the course of hepatic steatosis remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the association between the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and hepatic steatosis in the US population.MethodsThe cross-sectional portion of this study included participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning from 2007 to 2020. Our investigation employed weighted logistic regression to explore the association between DII score and hepatic steatosis prevalence. Subgroup analyses were performed to identify potential confounding factors, while smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis were utilized to determine any non-linear relationships. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to identify the key nutrients associated with the risk of hepatic steatosis. Subsequently, a nomogram model based on these major dietary factors was constructed. The diagnostic power of the predictive model to screen for hepatic steatosis risk was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calibration curves.ResultsThis study included a substantial cohort of 27,655 participants, representing 181 million residents in the U.S. Weighted logistic regression revealed a positive association between elevated DII scores and the risk of hepatic steatosis (DII as a continuous variable: OR = 1.07, 95% CI = 1.05–1.10; Q4 compared to Q1: OR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.21–1.50). The nomogram model exhibited considerable power in disease risk assessment (the area under the curve (AUC) of the training set: 0.714, 95% CI = 0.706–0.721; the AUC of the validation set: 0.707, 95% CI = 0.697–0.716). The results of the sensitivity analyses indicated that a history of current or past hepatitis B infections and excessive alcohol did not interfere with the association between DII and hepatic steatosis.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a significant association between the DII score and hepatic steatosis. This suggests that dietary patterns with lower inflammatory potential may be associated with a reduced prevalence of hepatic steatosis. However, given the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional study design, further longitudinal studies or intervention trials are necessary to ensure such an association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01015-w.

Introduction

Hepatic steatosis, arising from an imbalance between lipid accumulation and processing [1], stands as a consequential metabolic and hepatic pathology deeply entwined with the prevailing obesity epidemic [2]. Its etiology is diverse, ranging from viral infections, metabolic disorders, and toxic agents, all of which can lead to different forms of chronic liver disease, impacting the progression of hepatic steatosis [3]. Among its various forms, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most prevalent [4], characterized by a strong association with obesity and metabolic comorbidities [5]. Given the increasing global prevalence of diabetes and obesity [6], NAFLD has become a significant public health concern, affecting almost a quarter of the worldwide population [7]. Additionally, NAFLD imposes a considerable economic burden on both affected individuals and society [8], with the economic burden of this disease in Europe reaching an astonishing €3.5 billion in 2019 alone [9]. Consequently, hepatic steatosis represents a significant clinical and economic challenge that deserves attention.

Inflammation plays a key role in the progression of hepatic steatosis [10], and diet is a potential factor affecting systemic inflammation levels [11]. However, the specific impact of diet-mediated changes in systemic inflammation levels on the development of hepatic steatosis remains uncertain. Research has found that typical Western dietary patterns, characterized by high calories and high fat, are associated with significantly elevated levels of systemic inflammation and a higher prevalence of NAFLD [12, 13]. In contrast, the Mediterranean diet (MD), abundant in polyunsaturated fats, polyphenols and vitamins, stands out for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-lowering properties, making it a potentially effective strategy for mitigating NAFLD [14]. Furthermore, the consumption of specific micronutrients, each with distinct inflammatory effects, can also exert an impact on liver health [15]. Therefore, understanding the complex relationship between hepatic steatosis and the inflammatory consequences of dietary choices offers critical insights for both obese individuals and those with liver diseases, aiding in the prevention and management of liver health.

To assess the inflammatory potential of dietary factors, the dietary inflammatory index (DII) was initially proposed by Shivappa et al. as a literature-derived index [16]. Emerging evidence has suggested a positive association between DII scores and the risk of NAFLD [17, 18]. However, hepatic steatosis primarily denotes an excess accumulation of fat in the liver [2] and may also occur in other liver diseases like viral hepatitis [19]. In contrast, NAFLD encompasses various liver pathologies, ranging from simple hepatic steatosis to fatty liver accompanied by hepatocellular injury and liver fibrosis [20]. Consequently, the conclusions drawn from studies on NAFLD may not directly apply to hepatic steatosis alone. In this case, we initiated an observational study to investigate the association between DII and hepatic steatosis.

Materials and methods

Study population

The analysis encompassed a cohort of 66,148 adults (aged ≥ 18) in the NHANES 2007–2020 Pre-pandemic cycle. Participants with missing DII information (n = 9712), missing hepatic steatosis data (n = 18107), missing hypertension information (n = 1244), missing smoke-related details (n = 5809), missing HbA1c values information (n = 47), participants whose age < 20 (n = 807), participants with incomplete TC (total cholesterol), TG (Triglyceride), HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein) information (n = 12), missing education information (n = 22), missing income information (n = 2733) were excluded from the study. After these requisite exclusions, the final analytical dataset comprised a total of 27,655 participants.

Assessment of dietary information

The DII concept offers a valuable tool for quantifying the inflammatory nature of an individual’s diet. In the context of the NHANES 2007–March 2020 dataset, dietary information was sourced from the average of two 24-hour dietary recall interviews for each participant. These data were then linked to a global reference database, allowing for robust estimation of the mean intake and standard deviation of each of the 45 dietary components used in the calculation of the DII [16]. To control for differences in total energy intake, we employed an energy-standardized version of the global reference database to convert each participant’s intake of dietary components into corresponding Z-scores. This was done by subtracting the global energy-adjusted mean intake from the participant’s intake and dividing the result by the global standard deviation. The resulting Z-score was then converted into a percentile, which was doubled and subtracted from one to minimize skewness. This percentile score was subsequently multiplied by the specific inflammatory effect score assigned to each food parameter [21]. The cumulative DII score for an individual was derived by summing the specific DII scores of all food parameters. A higher DII score is indicative of a more pro-inflammatory dietary pattern, whereas a lower score suggests a more anti-inflammatory pattern. Given that the NHANES database does not comprehensively account for the intake of all 45 nutrients, we used 26 dietary parameters from the NHANES survey in the calculation of the DII, including energy, proteins, carbohydrates, dietary fiber, total fats, saturated fatty acid, monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA), polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA), cholesterol, beta-carotene, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid, iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, caffeine, alcohol, Vitamins A/B6/B12/C/D/E. In our analysis, the DII score was first analyzed as a continuous variable, and then the total sample was divided into quartiles according to DII for further examination.

Definition of hepatic steatosis

The extent of hepatic steatosis was assessed using the hepatic steatosis index (HSI). This index was computed using the formula: 8× (alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) ratio) + body mass index (BMI) + 2 (if diabetic) + 2 (if female), as established in prior literature [22]. Notable correlations between HSI and the extent of hepatic steatosis have been convincingly demonstrated in previous research where an HSI threshold of 36 was used to assess the presence of hepatic steatosis [23, 24].

Covariates

This study considered ten potential confounding factors that could affect the relationship between DII score and hepatic steatosis prevalence. These factors included gender, race, income, education, smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, age, and gamma-glutamyl transferase(GGT) levels. Respondents were categorized into four racial groups: Latino (including Mexican backgrounds and other Hispanics), non-Hispanic white, African American, and other races. Education was classified as “less than high school” for individuals below the 12th grade or with a 12th-grade education without a diploma and “high school or above” for those with a high school diploma or higher education [25]. Smoking status was assessed by considering a cumulative cigarette consumption of 100 or more. Hypertension was defined based on an average systolic blood pressure equal to or exceeding 140 mm Hg or an average diastolic blood pressure equal to or exceeding 90 mm Hg, calculated from multiple measurements [26]. Hyperlipidemia and diabetes were diagnosed based on biochemical data from multiple laboratory tests, and individuals were classified as having these conditions if they met the criteria [27, 28]. In addition, the sensitivity analysis should also account for data on hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and alcohol consumption. HBV infection status was ascertained through serological profiling using three biomarkers: hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc), hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs). Participants who were simultaneously seronegative for all three biomarkers were categorized as either HBV-uninfected or unvaccinated [29, 30]. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020 and the American Heart Association’s dietary guidelines recommend that alcohol consumption should not exceed two drinks per day (28 g/day) for men and one drink per day (14 g/day) for women [31, 32]. Therefore, we categorized men consuming more than 28 g/day and women consuming more than 14 g/day as having excessive alcohol intake. Specific criteria for excessive alcohol intake, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia are illustrated in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

In line with the NHANES analytical guidelines, our statistical analyses were conducted with the incorporation of appropriate sampling weights, enhancing the validity and generalizability of the study findings to the broader population. To elucidate the possible association between DII and hepatic steatosis, we employed various statistical methods. Differences in baseline characteristics between the control and hepatic steatosis groups were assessed using survey-weighted linear regression for continuous variables and the survey-weighted Chi-square test for categorical variables. In addition, weighted logistic regression was used to assess the association between DII score and hepatic steatosis prevalence while adjusting for potential confounders. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, race, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia were performed to assess heterogeneity in this study. Furthermore, this study included additional analyses. Interaction tests were performed, stratified by covariates. A P value < 0.05 for each interaction indicated statistical significance in the subgroup analysis. We also utilized smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis to examine the nonlinearity of the association between DII and hepatic steatosis.

The sensitivity analysis of this study was divided into two parts: (1) Epidemiological investigations have consistently demonstrated a reduced prevalence of hepatic steatosis in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients compared to general population cohorts, and the underlying mechanism may be an inverse correlation between HBV replication activity and hepatic steatosis [33]. To rule out a potential confounding effect of HBV on the development of hepatic steatosis, we used strict exclusion criteria to exclude participants with current or past hepatitis B infections. (2) Both alcoholic (ALD) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) involve hepatic fat accumulation, but their pathogenic mechanisms are not the same [34]. While alcohol induces liver injury through complex mechanisms, including oxidative stress and immune activation [35], this study specifically examines diet-mediated inflammation on steatosis. We therefore stratified subjects by alcohol intake to isolate the interference of alcohol’s hepatotoxicity on the conclusions. All weighted logistic regression analyses were conducted through three sequentially adjusted models. Model 1 constituted the crude association without covariate adjustment. Model 2 incorporated primary adjustments for demographic characteristics (gender, age, race) and socioeconomic determinants (educational level and ratio of family income to poverty). Model 3 extended these adjustments by including metabolic covariates (GGT, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia) along with behavioral risk factors (smoking status).

The logistic least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) is a regression method that performs variable selection by shrinking the coefficients of less relevant predictors toward zero, thereby reducing multicollinearity and yielding a more parsimonious and interpretable model. This study employed LASSO binomial regression to identify the optimal predictive dietary factors associated with hepatic steatosis to construct a simplified model. By leveraging the top predictive dietary factors identified through LASSO regression, demographic characteristics and hepatic steatosis-related risk factors, we constructed predictive models employing a nomogram. The effectiveness of the nomogram models in identifying the risk of hepatic steatosis was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calibration curves. It’s important to note that we did not incorporate the NHANES survey weights into the prediction model, as the R package for “rms” does not support weighting procedures. LASSO regression was conducted using the “glmnet” package in R, while the nomogram model was established using the “DynNom” package.

All the above statistical analyses were conducted using EmpowerStats software (version 4.2) and Project R (version 4.1.3), with a nominally significant threshold set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

An overview of the study design is displayed in Fig. 1. The study included 27,655 participants aged ≥ 20 years, representing 181 million multiracial adults in the U.S., 16,212 (58.6%) of whom had hepatic steatosis. Significant differences in demographic and clinical characteristics were evident between these groups. Compared to those in the control group, participants in the hepatic steatosis group were characterized by older age (48.17 vs. 46.52 years, P < 0.001); a higher DII indicating a more proinflammatory diet (0.88 vs. 0.57, P < 0.001); a greater representation of women (52.57% vs. 49.82%, P < 0.001); and a greater risk of hypertension (15.65% vs. 12.82%, P < 0.001), diabetes (20.45% vs. 4.88%, P < 0.001) and hyperlipidemia (75.29% vs. 58.28%, P < 0.001). In addition, GGT levels in the hepatic steatosis group were significantly greater than those in the control group (5.80 vs. 5.13 U/L, P < 0.001). The detailed clinical and biochemical baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Characteristics of nutrient intake

The scores for each component of DII are shown in Table 2. Overall, the total DII was significantly greater in patients with hepatic steatosis than in the control group (0.88 vs. 0.57, P < 0.001). When focusing on individual nutrients only, participants with hepatic steatosis had higher inflammation scores for dietary fiber, total fatty acid, saturated fatty acid, cholesterol, vitamin E, vitamin A, beta-carotene, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B6, folic acid, vitamin C, vitamin D, magnesium, and alcohol than controls. In addition, this study assessed the association between individual nutrient intake and its association with confounding factors, as shown in Fig. 2. The present study revealed that there was a general correlation between the nutrients consumed, except for beta-carotene, vitamin C, vitamin D, caffeine, and alcohol. A strong correlation was found between total fat and saturated fatty acids (correlation coefficient = 0.92) and monounsaturated fatty acids (correlation coefficient = 0.97). Furthermore, nutrients were associated with varying degrees with confounding factors such as demographic information, biochemical indicators, and health status, but the correlations were not strong (the absolute values of correlation coefficients were all < 0.4). The statistical descriptions of the differences in the intake of various nutrients between individuals with hepatic steatosis and individuals without hepatic steatosis are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

LASSO regression and nomogram prediction model

The initial LASSO regression model included the 26 dietary components that constitute DII. Furthermore, demographic variables and risk factors associated with hepatic steatosis, such as smoking, diabetes, blood pressure, hyperlipidemia, and GGT levels, were also considered in the analysis. The penalty parameter lambda (λ) was chosen through 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 5A). For accuracy, the algorithm underwent 1,000 iterations in this study. Consistent with previous publications, the largest λ within one standard error range of the minimal binomial deviation (λ = 0.0065) was chosen as the optimal model (Fig. 5B). Following LASSO regression analysis, a refined selection process retained 18 optimal variables among the initially included variables. Ultimately, 11 variables that were statistically significant to the model were included in constructing the nomogram model. The study cohort was randomly partitioned into training (9695 cases, 6898 controls) and validation (6517 cases, 4545 controls) sets at a 3:2 ratio, and the prediction outcomes are shown in Fig. 6A. The ROC curves’ results indicated that the nomogram model exhibited good discriminative power, supported by the area under the curve (AUC) values (for the training set: 0.714, 95% CI = 0.706–0.721; for the validation set: 0.707, 95% CI = 0.697–0.716) (Fig. 6B). Consistently, the internal validation of the nomogram was carried out by the Bootstrap repeated self-sampling method in this study (as shown in Fig. 6C and D). After 1000 repetitions of Bootstrap self-sampling, the calibration curve of the nomogram for screening Hepatic steatosis showed that the absolute errors in the training set and the validation set between the simulated curve and the actual curve were 0.006 and 0.007, respectively, which indicated good consistency between the prediction result and the actual result.

Sensitivity analysis

A total of 20,309 subjects were included in the sensitivity analysis after excluding individuals with current or past hepatitis B infections. The demographic and clinical characteristics of these participants are detailed in Supplementary Table 10. In the fully adjusted model, DII, as a continuous variable, was positively associated with hepatic steatosis (OR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.03–1.09). Individuals in high DII quartiles exhibited increased susceptibility to developing hepatic steatosis, as shown in Table 5. In addition, we categorized our sample population into excessive and non-excessive alcohol intake groups. The results of the subgroup analysis on alcohol intake indicated that excessive alcohol did not interfere with the association between DII and hepatic steatosis (refer to Supplementary Table 11). These findings from the sensitivity analyses reaffirm the robustness and reliability of our study’s conclusions.

Discussion

Our study aimed to explore the association between DII and hepatic steatosis among a nationally representative sample of U.S. individuals. Our cross-sectional study, which included 27,655 subjects, yielded the following key findings: (1) an increase in DII score was also significantly associated with an increase in the prevalence of hepatic steatosis, even after considering potential confounders. (2) Subgroup analyses indicated that the positive association between DII score and hepatic steatosis remained consistent and stable across race, gender, age, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. In contrast, smoking and hypertension were identified as key factors influencing this positive association. (3) Threshold effect analysis revealed inverted U-shaped relationships between DII and hepatic steatosis in individuals over 60, those with hypertension, smokers, and male participants. A stable association was observed only in regions where DII scores were below these inflection points. (4) A nomogram model utilizing key dietary factors from our study demonstrated considerable effectiveness in identifying the risk of hepatic steatosis. (5) The results of the sensitivity analyses indicated that a history of current or past hepatitis B infections and excessive alcohol did not interfere with the association between DII and hepatic steatosis.

Dietary patterns play a crucial role in systemic inflammation. The Western diet, characterized by high fat and calorie intake, elevates systemic inflammation by disrupting the balance of gut flora and increasing oxidative stress [36, 37]. Conversely, a ketogenic diet with low carbohydrate intake promotes physiological ketosis and may curb the development of hepatic steatosis by effectively reducing oxidative stress levels to regulate the release of inflammatory factors [38]. Despite this, the effects of dietary patterns, specifically the inflammatory effects mediated by dietary intake, on liver health remain poorly understood. The DII, an innovative tool widely used to assess the inflammatory potential of dietary intake, offers a promising approach to address this gap [39]. Our research conducted a comparative analysis of DII and its 26 components between individuals with and without hepatic steatosis. The findings revealed that participants with hepatic steatosis exhibited higher mean DII than those without the condition. Additionally, a significant positive association between DII and hepatic steatosis was observed across diverse populations, with non-linear relationships identified through smooth curve fittings. Notably, the effect of DII on hepatic steatosis was particularly pronounced among non-hypertensive patients and non-smokers. Previous studies have demonstrated that smoking and hypertension are potential risk factors for hepatic steatosis [40, 41], which makes it possible that the effects of dietary inflammation may not be readily observed in smoking and hypertensive populations. According to the smooth curve fitting models, we identified inflection points in the correlation between DII score and hepatic steatosis among men, elderly individuals, and populations with hypertension and smoking. On the left side of the breakpoint measurement, a positive association was observed, but on the right side, no relationship was detected. This finding suggests that the DII has a significant threshold effect on hepatic steatosis. In summary, our findings corroborate prior research by demonstrating that elevated DII scores are associated with an increased risk of hepatic steatosis.

The potential biological mechanisms underlying the association between a pro-inflammatory diet and hepatic steatosis may involve several interrelated pathways. Firstly, chronic systemic inflammation and fat deposition induced by a long-term high-fat diet stimulate hepatocytes to synthesize and secrete dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4), which promotes inflammation and insulin resistance (IR) in both hepatocytes and adipose tissues [42, 43]. IR can lead to hepatic fat accumulation by increasing free fatty acid (FFA) delivery to the liver, promoting de novo lipogenesis (DNL), and reducing hepatic fatty acid oxidation [44]. Secondly, excessive fat deposition activates fatty acid oxidation within mitochondria, generating large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), oxidative stress, and toxic dicarboxylic acids. These toxic substances accumulate and further damage mitochondria, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [1]. Thirdly, the gut microbiome plays a crucial role in the development and progression of hepatic steatosis through the gut-liver axis. Long-term consumption of pro-inflammatory dietary components, such as saturated fats and refined carbohydrates, may alter the composition of the gut microbiota and disrupt intestinal barrier function. This allows bacterial components and metabolites, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), to enter the portal circulation [45]. LPS binds to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on Kupffer cells and activates the NF-κB pathway, thereby exacerbating liver inflammation and lipid synthesis [36]. Taken together, these mechanisms underscore how diet-induced inflammation disrupts hepatic lipid homeostasis, thus providing a plausible explanation for the observed association between DII and hepatic steatosis. However, all potential biological mechanisms are based on literature review and hypothesis, and we are unable to make definitive interpretations at this time owing to data limitations, thus the conclusions need to be interpreted with caution and anticipate more detailed future studies.

While DII is recognized for reflecting dietary inflammatory potential and systemic inflammation levels [46], it has not been applied to predict hepatic steatosis. Therefore, this study aimed to predict the risk of hepatic steatosis by identifying the dietary factors most strongly associated with this condition, in conjunction with relevant demographic characteristics and risk factors. Carbohydrates, dietary fiber, total fat, and alcohol were the final dietary components selected for the LASSO logistic regression model, as their contributions were statistically significant for the prediction model. Excessive consumption of carbohydrates and fats results in the accumulation of visceral adipose tissue and an increase in free fatty acids among individuals, which is believed to contribute significantly to liver damage in NAFLD due to the chronic low-grade inflammation it induces [47]. Interestingly, the model showed a negative association between carbohydrates and the prevalence of hepatic steatosis, which may be due to potential biases introduced by the bundled intake of multiple nutrients. A randomized controlled trial has provided evidence that a high-fiber diet is effective in mitigating NAFLD [48]. Furthermore, another animal study demonstrated that dietary fiber with high viscosity reduces obesity and diminishes hepatic steatosis in rats subjected to a high-fat diet [49]. This association may be attributed to the following potential mechanisms: diets abundant in dietary fiber can shape the diversity of the gut microbiome, which improves gut homeostasis and barrier function, thereby influencing metabolic and immune responses; additionally, they regulate energy expenditure and appetite through neural and humoral pathways, ultimately affecting body weight [50]. While alcohol is generally recognized as a risk factor for liver pathology [51], the prediction model in this study shows a negative association between alcohol intake and the prevalence of hepatic steatosis. This unexpected finding may suggest that alcohol is not a risk factor for liver steatosis at low levels of intake, which is consistent with previous research [51–53]. One potential explanation is that, at low doses, the anti-inflammatory effects of alcohol may outweigh its hepatotoxic effects [16]. Moderate alcohol consumption, particularly wine, has been widely recommended within the Mediterranean diet due to its anti-inflammatory properties [54]. Given that we have excluded statistically possible correspondence interactions, we still included alcohol as part of the DII in the predictive model. However, the precise impact of alcohol on liver health warrants further investigation in subsequent studies. Overall, the nomogram model demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in ROC curve analysis, and increasing dietary fiber intake could be an effective strategy for preventing hepatic steatosis. This highlights the utility of such models in assessing associations between dietary factors and hepatic steatosis in obese populations, underscoring the need to further explore the integration of body parameters and dietary patterns in future research.

Several limitations also warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, its cross-sectional nature enables association identification, but it cannot be used for causal inference. The nascent state of research on DII score and hepatic steatosis underscores the need for future longitudinal studies. Secondly, obtaining data on self-reported dietary intake and covariates via the NHANES questionnaire introduces potential subjective bias, compounded by the challenge of accurately reflecting long-term dietary patterns amidst daily dietary fluctuations. Thirdly, the study faces constraints regarding its applicability across diverse racial groups, due to variations in diet, genetic makeup, lipid metabolism, and susceptibility to liver disease, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation of its generalizability beyond the U.S. context. Fourthly, owing to the inherent limitations of cross-sectional studies, our sensitivity analyses were conducted as post-hoc analyses rather than strictly constrained during the collection of baseline data from cohorts, which may have led to a certain degree of bias. Fifthly, methodological limitations should be acknowledged. Many pathological conditions and biological effects are currently undetectable by traditional methods, and there is still a need for alternative indicators. However, these alternative indicators still struggle to perfectly explain the real-world situation. While the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) offers practical assessment (sensitivity 93.1% at cutoff < 30; specificity 92.4% at cutoff > 36) [22], its imperfect accuracy (AUC 0.81) may cause non-differential misclassification, potentially attenuating effect sizes. Moreover, although we propose plausible biological pathways linking dietary inflammation to steatosis (e.g., via insulin resistance and gut-liver axis mechanisms), these require validation through direct biomarkers (e.g., CRP, IL-6, adipokines). However, we were unable to conduct further examinations due to data limitations, thus our conclusions need to be interpreted with caution and subsequent studies are needed for further discussion. Lastly, while the LASSO-derived nomogram in this study identified key dietary factors associated with hepatic steatosis, several limitations should be noted. The model’s internal validation through bootstrapping may not fully address potential overfitting, and external validation in independent cohorts is needed to assess generalizability. The cross-sectional design lacks comprehensive diet-related information, which may affect its ability to evaluate associations with hepatic steatosis. To better understand these relationships, further cohort or case-control studies are needed to examine the potential utility of dietary and anthropometric data in hepatic steatosis assessment.

Conclusion

Our results indicate a notable association between DII score and hepatic steatosis. The developed nomogram demonstrates clinical utility for assessing steatosis risk using dietary and anthropometric parameters. These findings highlight the importance of dietary patterns and their inflammatory potential in hepatic steatosis development. However, given the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional study design, further longitudinal studies or intervention trials are necessary to establish the causal relationship underlying this association.
