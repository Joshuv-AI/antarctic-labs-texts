Virulence characteristics and antibiotic resistance analysis of multi-drug resistant Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli in healthy pigs from Zhejiang Province, China
Authors: Lei Lei, Sinan Wang, Jiajia Gong, Shuang Lu, Ruidong Zhai, Yue Han, Jiali Xu, Qin Wu, Jing Xia, Simin Deng, Mianmian Chen, Jing Wang, Zheng Nie, Dandan Fu, Houhui Song, Changyong Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) is a predominant diarrheal pathogen worldwide, employing adhesins and secreted enterotoxins to cause disease in mammalian hosts. The report about antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in ETEC have increased, detrimentally affecting both livestock industry and food safety. Despite this, there is a paucity of studies on the AMR genes, virulence factors and the correlation of them in ETEC.ResultsSix ETEC-positive samples collected from healthy pigs across three regions yielded 16 distinct ETEC isolates. Among these, 13 isolates (81.3%) exhibited multidrug resistance (MDR), while all 16 isolates demonstrated tolerance to working concentrations of sodium hypochlorite. 15 isolates harbored both heat-labile enterotoxin genes eltAB and heat-stable enterotoxin gene stb. One isolate uniquely carried eltAB, heat-stable enterotoxin genes sta, stb, and Shiga toxin gene stx2e. Galleria mellonella infection assays confirmed the virulence of representative strains in our study. Whole-genome sequencing of one isolate per sample (n = 6), integrated with NCBI porcine ETEC data, revealed broad distribution of sequence types (STs). A previously unreported ST7070 strain was identified as an enterotoxin gene carrier (eltAB + stb). The results of correlation analysis revealed that co-transfer in aminoglycoside resistance genes aph(3'')-Ib, aph(6')-Id, sulfonamide resistance gene sulIII and co-localized cluster of arr-3 (rifampicin resistance), aac(6')-Ib-cr (aminoglycoside resistance), catB (phenicol resistance), and blaOXA (β-lactam resistance). Significant co-transfer observed between disinfectant resistance gene sugE(p) and extended-spectrum β-lactamase gene blaCMY.ConclusionsETEC strains isolated from healthy animals exhibited both pathogenicity and MDR phenotypes. Besides, they harbored diverse AMR genes and disinfectant resistance determinants. Integrated analysis with NCBI-deposited ETEC genomic data further revealed co-transfer events of AMR and disinfectant resistance genes among ETEC populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05044-9.

Background

Diarrheal disease attributable to enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) causes substantial morbidity and mortality predominantly in mammalian hosts. ETEC strains adhere to intestinal epithelial cells with fimbrial adhesins, and produce one or more heat labile (LT) and/or heat stable (STaP, STb) enterotoxins to and caused watery diarrhea [1]. For fimbrial adhesins, F4 variants (F4ab, F4ac, F4ad) and F18 variants (F18ab and F18ac) are the most common fimbriae types among ETEC [2]. The prevalence of association of F18 ETEC with post-weaning diarrhea in different countries varied widely, from 15.4% in Australia, 35% in Slovakia, 39.3% in Denmark, and 53% in the United States to 61.9% in Poland [3]. Besides, ETEC usually caused diarrhea of children, the prevalence of ETEC in children which had acute diarrhea during 2015 to 2018 in Shanghai was 1.1% [4], as well as 29.7% of infants with diarrhea in peri-urban areas of Lusaka carried ETEC [5]. Previous study also displayed that ETEC were isolated from healthy and diarrhea samples from pig farm which had pigs with diarrhea [6].

Until now, antimicrobial agents are still the important means to prevent and control diarrhea caused by ETEC [7], though bacteria vaccines are considered as highly effective tools in combating antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and preventing infection of ETEC but the scope of use is limited [8]. However, with the usage of antimicrobial agents, the detection rate of antimicrobial resistant ETEC in livestock has become increased [9]. Previous studies indicated that antimicrobial resistant ETEC carried acquired genes such as blaTEM-1B, and showed high AMR rates of ampicillin, tetracycline, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid [10, 11]. Antimicrobial resistant ETEC made the poor therapeutic effect of antibiotics in clinical, so the persistence of these strains needs to be taken seriously. The investigation of ETEC revealed that ETEC can be isolated from food, food-producing animals and diarrhea patients, and food can act as a reservoir of ETEC with pathogenic potential [11, 12]. Besides ETEC in food chain can play a role in transferring antibiotic resistance and virulence genes from bacteria of animals to bacteria of human [13]. Nevertheless, there are few reports about the emergence of ETEC in healthy animals which is a potential threaten for public health, most of studies only focus on the diarrhea animals [6]. Also, there is lack of studies about correlation analysis of genotypes of antimicrobial resistance, virulence genes, mobile genetic elements and plasmid types in ETEC to find related genes. Therefore, we aimed at the epidemiological investigation of ETEC in healthy pigs, acquired the genotypes and phenotypes of AMR, virulence and transfer capacity, finally analyzed the correlation of AMR genes, virulence genes and plasmid genes using the database of strains in our study and National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), to report the molecular characterization of ETEC in healthy animals and the commonness of genotypes of ETEC in pig origins.

Methods

Bacterial isolation

Three hundred sixty fecal samples (180 piglets and 180 fattening pigs each) of healthy pigs were collected from 18 pig farms in Zhejiang of China during September to November, 2021, including 120 from six pig farms in Hangzhou, 140 from seven pig farms in Zhuji and 100 from five pig farms in Zhoushan (Table S1). All pig farms were large scale farming. Ten samples from piglets and ten samples from fattening pigs were randomly collected in each farm. The samples were cultured in modified MacConkey Broth overnight. Genomic DNA samples were extracted and purified for sequencing using boiling method. Heat labile enterotoxin gene eltA, and heat stable enterotoxin genes sta, stb which specific for ETEC were identified by multiplex PCR [14]. The colonies of Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli were purified on MacConkey Agar and subsequently confirmed by 16S rDNA sequencing.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

All isolates were examined in triplicate for susceptibility to 11 antimicrobial agents and 7 disinfectants using microdilution broth with quality control strain ATCC 25922. Antibiotics included gentamicin (GEN), ampicillin (AMP), florfenicol (FFC), cefotaxime (CTX), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (A/C), amikacin (AMK), colistin (CL), tigecycline(TGC), meropenem (MEM), doxycycline (DOX), ciprofloxacin (CIP). Disinfectants include ethyl alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, potassium peroxymonosulfate, benzalkonium bromide, chlorhexidine acetate, sodium hypochlorite, and triclosan. The powder of antimicrobial agents and disinfectants was purchased from the Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The AMR results were classified using the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 ED33: 2023 [15]and CLSI VET01S ED6: 2023 [16]. The disinfectant results were determined by component concentrations converted from commercial reagent working concentrations.

Detection of biofilm formation

The ability of biofilm formation was determined by Crystal Violet Assay (CV assay) [17]. All strains and negative control were performed in triplicate. Then absorbance detection at 570 nm with BioTek Synergy H1. The critical OD570nm (OD control, ODc) was calculated from the arithmetic mean of the absorbance of negative controls (without bacteria) with three times addition of SD. The following classification was applied for the determination of biofilm formation: no biofilm production (OD ≤ ODc), weak biofilm production (ODc < OD ≤ 2ODc), moderate biofilm production (2ODc < OD ≤ 4ODc), and strong biofilm production (4ODc < OD).

Virulence of ETEC to Galleria mellonella larvae

Larvaes with a body length of 3 cm were selected and stored in the dark at 4 °C. Each group containing ten G. mellonella. Every isolate had three groups in the same colony forming unit (CFU). The strain group was injected with 10 μL bacterial precipitation diluted by PBS for one G. mellonella. The negative control group was injected with the same volume of 10 mM PBS. During the following experimentation, G. mellonella were stored in Petri dishes at 37 °C for 72 h. G. mellonella were inspected every 8 h and were considered dead if they did not move in response to touch [18].

Bioinformatics analysis

DNA libraries of strains in this study were constructed and sequencing by Illumina NovaSeq PE150 at the Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. With Unicycler (version: 0.4.8) scaffolds were obtained (Bioproject: PRJNA1159457) and annotate the genome used Prokka (version: 1.14.6) and Islandviewer 4. We compared ETEC of pig origins downloaded from NCBI database, as well as identified antibiotic resistance genes, virulence genes, plasmid types, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) and Clermontype from database of Center for Epidemiology and Github. Correlation analysis was performed of the above data using Spearman method and visualized by R package corrplot. Phylogenetic tree was constructed by Snippy and IQ-tree. Data visualization used the tools of website iTOL, GBKviz, and CNSknowall.

Results

Isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility of ETEC

A total of 360 samples were subjected to detection in this study. Among these, only six samples from three pig farms were positive for enterotoxin genes. The herd-level prevalence was determined to be 16.7% (95%CI: 3.6%−41.4%), while the animal-level prevalence was 1.67% (95%CI: 0.6%−3.6%). The positive samples comprised one sample from a fattening pig in Hangzhou, one sample from a piglet in Zhuji, and four samples (one piglet sample and three fattening pig samples) from Zhoushan. A total of 16 enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) strains were isolated from these samples, including three from Hangzhou, one from Zhuji, and 12 from Zhoushan (piglet n = 9, fattening pig n = 3). All strains were sensitive to tigecycline and meropenem, but demonstrated highly resistant to florfenicol, ciprofloxacin (Fig. 1). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value of gentamycin, cefotaxime and colistin exhibited a phenomenon of numerical polarization, characterized by either high resistance or high susceptibility. Furthermore, strains isolated from one sample displayed different antimicrobial resistance (AMR) phenotypes. 13 isolates (except ZS03S8-2, ZS03S8-3, ZS03S8-4) in this study were resistant to three or more than three class of antimicrobial agents, which identified as multi-drug resistant (MDR). Biofilm formation, virulence potential and phylogenetic relationships analysis of ETEC

The biofilm formation capability of 16 isolates was evaluated. Only one isolate (HZ08S17-1) exhibited moderate biofilm production after 24 h, while the remaining isolates demonstrated weak biofilm production at 24, 48, and 72 h, including HZ08S17-1 at 48 and 72 h.

Sixteen strains in this study harbored enterotoxin genes eltAB and stb. Additionally, one strain (ZJ03S3) carried heat-stable gene sta and shiga toxin gene stx2e. 571 strains were retrieved from NCBI Pathogen Detection Database, and analyzed in conjunction with our isolates. 43 isolates which in one clade with different strains in our research were selected for phylogenetic tree construction (Fig. 2). The phylogenetic analysis revealed that four strains from our study (the grey dots marked in the tree) showed > 95% similarity with each other, including 3 from Zhoushan (ZS03S14, ZS03S8-1, ZS03S16) and 1 from Hangzhou (HZ08S17-1). They also demonstrated greater than 95% similarity with one ST1642 strain and two ST2526 strains from the United States. Furthermore, these strains exhibited over 90% similarity with twelve ST6234 strains and one ST9275 strain from the United States. The other two strains from our study were ST88 (marked by orange dot in the tree) from Zhuji (ZJ03S3) and ST7070 (marked by purple dot in the tree) from Zhoushan (ZS03S19). The ST88 strain showed greater than 95% similarity with 20 ST88 isolates from the United States, four ST88 isolates from China, and one untyped strain from the United States. The ST7070 strain displayed over 85% similarity with four ST5759 strains from Italy, Denmark, Belgium, and the United Kingdom. Notably, ST1642 and ST7070 in our study harbored different enterotoxin genes compared to strains with high similarity (Fig. 2). Moreover, the strains in our study exhibited distinct genotypes of resistance genes and mobile genetic elements compared to highly similar strains from the NCBI database. Strains from the NCBI database contained more aminoglycoside resistance genes, such as aac(3)-Iva, aadA, aph(3')-Ia, and aph(4)-Ia, as well as the amphenicol resistance gene cmlA1 and folate pathway antagonist genes dfrA, sulI, and sulIII. In contrast, only blaTEM and tet(A) were frequently observed in both our study and the NCBI database. Comparison of genotypes and correlation analysis

To investigate the molecular characteristics and co-occurrence patterns of virulence and resistance genes in ETEC of pig origins, a comparative and correlation analysis was conducted using both the strains analyzed in this study and those retrieved from the NCBI database (n = 571). The analysis encompassed resistance genotypes, virulence profiles, plasmid types, mobile genetic elements (MGEs), and strain metadata.

The vast majority of ETEC in pigs carried more than one enterotoxin gene. eltAB + stb were the Dominant enterotoxin group in NCBI database which accounts for 37.26% (n = 215). The remaining five enterotoxin combinations—eltAB, stb, eltAB + sta, sta + stb, and eltAB + sta + stb—were observed at frequencies of 3.12% (n = 18), 11.79% (n = 68), 6.24% (n = 36), 21.66% (n = 125), and 19.93% (n = 115), respectively. In our study, eltAB + stb was detected in 93.75% isolates. Among the 383 eltAB-positive strains, the heat-labile enterotoxin (LT) type was identified as LTIh-4, including six strains from our dataset, while one isolate expressed LTII-e. All heat-stable enterotoxins (STa and STb) were classified as the porcine variants STa1 and STb1.

Geographic variation in enterotoxin gene distribution was also observed (Fig. 5). In ETEC strains from North America (n = 341), Europe (n = 211), and Asia (n = 20), the majority carried multiple enterotoxin genes (polygenic), whereas strains from Oceania (n = 7) were more frequently monogenic.Fig. 5Distribution of enterotoxin genes in swine ETEC from different districts

Distribution of enterotoxin genes in swine ETEC from different districts

According to the virulence factor database (VFDB; https://www.mgc.ac.cn/cgi-bin/VFs/genus.cgi?Genus=Escherichia), several adherence and invasion-associated genes are linked to ETEC pathogenicity. All analyzed ETEC strains from both the NCBI database and our study carried at least one adherence-related gene (e.g., fimG, yagV/ecpE, yagW/ecpD, yagY/ecpB, yagZ/ecpA, yagX/ecpC, ykgK/ecpR) and at least one invasion-associated gene (e.g., ibeB, ibeC). Additionally, fimbrial adhesins such as K88 were commonly associated with ETEC. Among the 577 isolates, 313 (54.3%) carried the colonization factor F4 (K88), including 51 strains expressing F4 (K88) and 262 expressing F4ac (K88). Other colonization factors included F18ab (24 strains, 4.2%), F18ac (150 strains, 26.0%), F6 (987P) (2 strains, 0.3%), and F5 (K99) (1 strain, 0.2%). Two strains (0.3%) co-carried F4 (K88) and F18, while 87 strains (15.1%) lacked any of the above colonization factors. Notably, F4ac was frequently associated with the eltAB + stb genotype (Fig. 6). In our study, only strain ZJ03S3 carried the F18ac colonization factor.Fig. 6Relationships of colonization factors and enterotoxin groups

Over 80% of the NCBI-derived isolates and all strains in our study harbored at least one antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene. The most frequently detected resistance genes included tet(A) (tetracycline resistance), blaTEM (β-lactam resistance), and dfrA (trimethoprim resistance). Common plasmid types included Col(pHAD28), IncFII, and IncFIB, while prevalent mobile genetic elements comprised ISEsa1, IS421, and ISEc1. In contrast, the resistance genes oqxAB and bleO were uniquely identified in five strains from our study.

MLST, ST clonal complex (Cplx) and Clermont phylogenetic typing were performed for all strains. In our dataset, ST88 belonged to the ST23 complex, whereas ST1642 and ST7070 were not assigned to any known clonal complex. The NCBI-derived strains were distributed across 61 STs, with ST100 and ST10 being the most prevalent. Among these, 14 STs were grouped into nine clonal complexes, while the remainder were unassigned. The ST10, ST23, and ST165 complexes were the Dominant clonal groups, comprising 64, 55, and 256 strains, respectively. Clermontype A and B1 were predominant among NCBI strains. In our study, B1 (ZS03S14, HZ08S17, ZS03S16, ZS03S8), C (ZJ03S3), and E (ZS03S19) were identified. O-antigen serotyping showed that five isolates in our study belonged to O8, which was also the second most common O type in the NCBI dataset, following O149. Among O8 strains of ETEC in the NCBI database, Clermontypes B1 and C were predominant and frequently harbored the eltAB and stb virulence genes.

ETEC isolates were categorized into six distinct groups based on their enterotoxin gene profiles. Compared to the other five groups, isolates harboring eltAB + stb exhibited significantly weaker correlations (correlation coefficient < 0.3) with several genetic elements. These elements included the virulence genes astA and faeA (F4 (K88) fimbrial regulator), ISEc17 and ISEc18, aminoglycoside resistance genes aph(3'')-Ib and aph(6)-Id, and plasmid type IncFIC (Fig. 7). Discussion

ETEC is a major cause of post-weaning piglet diarrhea. A United States study covering 2010–2023 found that post-weaning colibacillosis stayed above 5%, and more than 95% of these cases were due to ETEC or mixed ETEC infections [19]. A recent nationwide study covering swine herds in five Chinese provinces identified ETEC in 9.02% of diarrheic samples [20]. In our survey of healthy pigs, only 1.67% carried ETEC. This low figure likely reflects the small number of farms and pigs we sampled; larger studies are needed for a more reliable estimate. ETEC mainly isolated from diarrheic piglets in previous works [19, 21, 22], whereas ETEC were found both piglets and fattening pigs in our study. With limited isolation, we cannot compare the importance of two groups in ETEC transmission directly. In our study, we found virulent strains from health animals, these findings align with previous report that a proportion of pigs can carry ETEC without exhibiting diarrhea [21]. Even so, these silent carriers shed ETEC and can spread it to others. Stress can trigger renewed growth of the bacteria and fresh outbreaks. Therefore, ETEC surveillance should include not only sick pigs but also healthy herds with a history of exposure.

The isolates from healthy pigs in our study, although exhibiting multi-drug resistance, were resistant to fewer antimicrobial agents compared to strains from diarrheic pigs [22]. In our research, high resistance rates were observed for chloramphenicols (florfenicol), quinolones (ciprofloxacin), cephalosporins (cefotaxime), and aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin). In contrast, previous studies have reported the highest resistance rates of ETEC for chloramphenicols, sulfonamides, tetracyclines, aminopenicillins, and streptomycin [23, 24]. The variation in antibiotic resistance profiles may be attributed to the differing use of antimicrobial agents across districts. Notably, antimicrobial agents from various classes were more frequently used in diarrheic pigs than in healthy pigs. Nevertheless, the isolation of ETEC in healthy pigs poses a public health risk in pig farms, contributing to the dissemination of ETEC and potentially causing diarrhea when other pathogens compromise the immune system of the swine population.

Previous studies have identified sta + stb as the most prevalent enterotoxin group in diarrheic pigs, particularly in piglets [25]. However, the major enterotoxin group identified in the NCBI database, which aligns with the isolates in our study, was eltAB + stb. The difference of the results because of the NCBI database did not contain all strains from the researches, also the strains in our study were too low. So there is a limitation for these results.

In this study, strain ZS03S19 exhibited the highest number of resistance genes but resulted in lower mortality of G. mellonella compared to strain ZS03S14, which harbored the same toxin genes. Conversely, strain ZJ03S3 possessed the greatest number of toxin genes and demonstrated higher mortality in G. mellonella than the other two strains. The expression of virulence genes was not assessed in this study; therefore, we cannot ascertain whether the antimicrobial resistance genes influenced the virulence of ETEC.

Although the strains carried acquired genes that may incur a fitness cost affecting growth, our analysis of other molecular characteristics of the strains in the NCBI database revealed that the ST and genotypes of ETEC were diverse. Resistance genes of aminoglycosides (aadA, aph(3')-Ia), tetracyclines (tet(A), tet(B)), sulfonamides (sulI, sulII), and cephalosporins (blaTEM) were predominantly observed in both our isolated strains and in previous studies [26, 27]. Furthermore, extended-spectrum β-lactamase gene blaCTX-M was found in both strains analyzed in this study and ETEC isolates from USA retrieved from NCBI database [28]. Additionally, prior literature has documented the presence of the tigecycline resistance gene tet(X4) in ETEC [29]. The co-occurrence of these genes in ETEC suggests not only an enhanced antimicrobial resistance profile but also poses a potential threat of highly virulent strains exhibiting co-resistance to multiple critical antimicrobial agents. Our own collection was small, so we combined it with publicly available genomes from NCBI to look for shared traits among ETEC. We found that several AMR genes often appear together, which can make bacteria harder to treat. Links between AMR genes and MGEs, such as blaCMY and Tn6092, point to ready movement of these genes between strains. Likewise, the tight pairing of qacEΔ1 with sulI, and qacF with sulIII, cmlA1, shows that resistance genes to antibiotics and disinfectants can transmit as a gene cassette. These patterns confirm that AMR genes, disinfectant resistance genes, and MGEs transfer together in ETEC and can spread quickly. Earlier work mainly focused on links between resistance and virulence genes [20], our study widened the lens by adding disinfectant resistance genes and MGEs, giving a fuller picture of how these genes move through strains from pig populations.

A new sequence type (ST7070) of ETEC was identified in this study, the same sequence types (ST88, ST1642) were also isolated in other regions of China and in other countries, such as Spain and the United States. Analysis of STs in our study and NCBI showed the host bacterium of enterotoxins were diverse, which enhance risk of dissemination. Previous studies have indicated a potential risk of ETEC transmission from animals to humans, particularly from samples within the food chain, highlighting the need for serious consideration of ETEC in healthy pigs [30]. Also, the new ST indicated that virulence genes eltAB and stb can transfer to other strains with MGEs. If they enter multidrug-resistant isolates, they could pose a public-health risk. The novel ST is extremely rare and has rarely been reported in other strains carrying AMR genes or virulence genes, suggesting that the virulence genes of ETEC may have been transferred to this isolate from other ETEC present within the same farm. Therefore, continuous surveillance of AMR and virulence profiles in ETEC is urgently required, and further research should be conducted to explore the relationship between virulence and AMR phenotypes in ETEC.

Conclusions

ETEC strains isolated from healthy animals exhibited both pathogenicity and MDR phenotypes. Besides, they harbored diverse AMR genes and disinfectant resistance determinants. Integrated analysis with NCBI-deposited ETEC genomic data further revealed co-transfer events of AMR and disinfectant resistance genes among ETEC populations.
