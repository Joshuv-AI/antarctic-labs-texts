Spillover of Riemerella anatipestifer to laying hens leads to a decrease in egg production and hatchability
Authors: Wanting Zhou, Xuezhi Cui, Shuning Zhou, Shuo Liu, Cheng Peng, Jizhe Yang, Jinping Li, Guangyu Hou, Wenming Jiang, Hualei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA strain of Riemerella anatipestifer,a bacterium that primarily infects ducks and geese, was isolated from a layer. Genetic analysis of the housekeeping genes (16S rRNA and rpoB), key virulence genes (prtC, hagA, and sspA), and dominant antigen gene (ompA) revealed that this isolate is closely related to strains commonly found in eastern China. Animal studies successfully replicated clinically similar cases, characterized by decreased egg production and reduced hatching rates, with the primary findings observed in the oviducts. Epidemiological investigations indicated that the disease has emerged in chicken farms across at least 5 provinces. Given these findings, enhanced surveillance is urgently needed to assess the potential impact on the poultry industry, particularly the layer sector, and to implement necessary measures to control the spread of the disease.Key words: Riemerella anatipestifer, Laying hens, Egg production, Hatchability, Decrease

Introduction

Riemerella anatipestifer (R. anatipestifer) is a crucial pathogen affecting domestic ducks and geese (Shousha et al., 2021). Its infection is likely the most economically significant infectious disease in the global duck farming industry (Abd El-Hamid, et al., 2019). Reported worldwide, this disease has severely hindered the sustainable development of the duck industry, incurring substantial economic losses due to high mortality rates, reduced growth rates, poor feed conversion efficiency, increased condemnations, and high treatment costs (Chang, et al., 2003; Sun et al., 2012).

This disease presents as acute or chronic septicemia in ducks, characterized by fibrinous pericarditis, perihepatitis, airsacculitis, caseous salpingitis, and meningitis (Leibovitz, 1972). Septicemia and infectious serositis are common in infected animals, manifesting as drooping of the head and neck, loss of appetite, respiratory distress, diarrhea, lethargy, and neurological symptoms (Kardos, et al., 2007).

To date, at least 21 serotypes of R. anatipestifer have been identified, with poor cross-protection among them (Pathanasophon, et al., 2002). Poor sanitation, inadequate nutrition, and sudden environmental or climatic changes often render poultry more susceptible to R. anatipestifer infection. Ducks aged between 2 and 8 weeks are particularly vulnerable, with high morbidity and mortality rates (Ren, et al., 2021). Once the disease infiltrates duck and goose flocks, it can become endemic, proving difficult to eradicate and prone to recurrent infections.

It also affects turkeys and wild birds, while chickens, quails, and pheasants are less frequently afflicted. In 2019, Chen et al. reported an outbreak of R. anatipestifer infection at a large chicken farm in China in which the adult chickens displayed neurological symptoms, a drop in egg production and an increase in dead embryos (Chen et al., 2024). Since then, several breeding-chicken farms and commercial egg farms in China have encountered an unusual clinical disease in laying hens. Affected hens exhibited salpingitis, maldevelopment of the oviducts, inflammation, cysts, or the presence of clear fluid in the oviducts, accompanied by a decline in egg production and hatchability.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was carried out in strict accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People´s Republic of China. The protocol was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the China Animal Health and Epidemiology Center (Protocol Number: DWFL-2024-02). All performances and efforts were made to minimize suffering.

Isolation and identification of bacteria

On November 30, 2023, infected hens on a breeding-chicken farm in eastern China began to show signs of declining egg production. The trachea, lungs, liver, and oviduct of diseased chickens were collected, homogenized, and used for DNA and RNA extraction. Polymerase chain reaction methods (in accordance with national or industry standards GB/T16550, GB/T 18936, GB/T 23197, GB/T 19167, GB/T 27527, NY/T 556, NY/T3233, and NY/T 551) were employed to test for Newcastle disease virus, avian influenza virus, egg drop syndrome virus, infectious bronchitis virus, infectious bursitis virus, infectious laryngotracheitis virus, Tambusu virus, and avian encephalomyelitis virus. All of these pathogens tested negative.

Fresh sections of tissues such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, trachea, ovary, and the uterine part of the oviduct from the tested chickens were inoculated onto blood agar plates and incubated at 37°C aerobically for 24 hours or anaerobically (anaerobic gas packs) for 72 hours. Eventually, no bacteria (e.g. Escherichia coli) were isolated under aerobic conditions. An anaerobic bacterial strain was isolated from the infected chickens. Appropriate colonies were later confirmed as Gram-negative organisms through Gram’s staining methodologies.

The hemolytic colonies on the blood agar plates were picked and boiled in 20 μL of ddH2O at 100°C for 10 minutes to obtain a DNA template. PCR amplification was carried out using primers for the bacterial 16S rRNA gene, following previously described methods (Han, et al., 2013; Nowaczek, et al., 2023).

Genetic and phylogenetic analyses

Subsequently, a dominant and specific antigen gene (ompA), a housekeeping gene (rpoB), and three virulence genes (prtC, hagA, and sspA) were amplified using species-specific primers, as described previously (Han, et al., 2013; Nowaczek, et al., 2023).

Animal experiment

Twenty-six 195-day-old Hyline laying hens (negative for R. anatipestifer by PCR) were divided into two groups (the infection group and the control group), with 13 hens in each group. The bacteria were inoculated into the vagina of chickens through the cloaca at a dose of 109 cfu/0.2 mL using a sterile syringe. At 2 days post-inoculation (dpi), 8 laying hens were added to the infection group as a contact group (housed in the same cage as infected hens). Egg production was continuously monitored for 30 days. At 7 dpi, 3 chickens from each of the infection, contact, and control groups were selected randomly and humanely sacrificed and the bacteria were isolated. The isolated bacteria were identified through Gram staining and PCR. The laid eggs were observed for hatching. Animal welfare has been fully taken into account in animal experiments.

Epidemiological survey

In 2024, we carried out an epidemiological survey of the disease in major poultry-producing areas across the country. We collected oropharyngeal swab and cloacal swab samples from egg farms, breeding farms and hatcheries, and conducted PCR tests to determine whether there was the presence of R. anatipestifer.

Results

Identification of bacteria

The bacterial colonies were transparent, shiny, and round in shape (Fig. 1a)). The bacteria were Gram-negative, short-rod-shaped (Fig. 1b), and were most easily isolated from the oviducts.

The bacterial strain was inoculated onto blood agar medium plates (a). The resulting bacterial colonies were transparent, shiny, round in shape. The bacteria were identified as Gram-negative and short-rod-shaped (b, Gram staining, 1,000 ×).

The sequencing results indicated that the homology between the amplified 16S rRNA gene of the CK-RA-SD24 strain and duck-originated R. anatipestifer strains RAf55, D-664, and HXb2 in GenBank was 99.9 %, suggesting that the isolates were R. anatipestifer. Phylogenetic analysis of the 16S rRNA sequences revealed that the CK-RA-SD24 isolate and duck-originated R. anatipestifer strains D-664, D-743, and RAf55 clustered on the same branch, indicating a close genetic relationship (Fig. 2a).

Phylogenetic trees of 16S rRNA (a), ompA (b), rpoB (c), prtC (d), hagA (e), and sspA (f) genes of the isolated Riemerella anatipestifer from chickens in China, 2024. These phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining method with the MAGE 11 software. The bacterial strain (CK-RA-SD24) from this study is marked with black circles. Scale bars represent branch length based on the number of nucleotide substitutions per site.

Genetic and phylogenetic analyses

The nucleotide sequence homology of ompA, rpoB, prtC, hagA, and sspA between the CK-RA-SD24 strain and duck-originated R. anatipestifer strains ranged from 99.9 % to 100.0 %. Phylogenetic analysis showed that ompA, rpoB, prtC, hagA, and sspA could be divided into two clades. The CK-RA-SD24 strain and duck-derived R. anatipestifer strains belonged to the same clade, demonstrating a close genetic relationship (Fig. 2b–2f).

Animal experiment

After cleaning the cloaca with sterilized saline solution, the bacteria were inoculated into the vagina of the 195-day-old Hy-Line laying hens chickens through the cloaca at a dose of 109 cfu/0.2 mL using a sterile syringe. At 2 days post-inoculation (dpi), 8 laying hens were added to the infection group as a contact group. Egg production was continuously monitored for 30 days. Egg production in the infection group began to decline at 6 dpi and did not recover throughout the observation period (Fig. 3a). The contact group also showed a decrease in egg production at 8 dpi. The infection group egg production dropped significantly from 84.6 % to 50 % (p < 0.01, t-tests) and the contact group egg production dropped significantly from 87.5 % to 40 % (p < 0.01, t-tests). Compared with the control group, their egg production decreased significantly (p < 0.01, t-tests).

Changes in the laying rate of laying hens after bacterial infection (a). After necropsy of chickens in infection group, the following gross changes were observed: oviductal gas distention (b), follicular regression, yolk collapse and discoloration (c), peritoneal serous effusion (d), atrophy of the oviductal uterus and isthmus (e), congestion of the oviductal mucosal surface, and luminal caseous material in the oviductal lumen (f). Coagulation of the egg yolk was found in some chicken eggs from the infection group and the contact group (g).

At 7 dpi, 3 chickens from each of the infection, contact, and control groups were sacrificed. Compared with the control group, the infection group exhibited the following gross changes: oviductal gas distention (Fig. 3b), follicular regression, yolk collapse and discoloration (Fig. 3c), peritoneal serous effusion (Fig. 3d), atrophy of the oviductal uterus and isthmus (Fig. 3e), congestion of the oviductal mucosal surface, and luminal caseous material in the oviductal lumen (Fig. 3f). Similar gross changes were observed in the contact group. R. anatipestifer was isolated from the oviducts of both the infection group and the contact group. These results indicated that R. anatipestifer was the primary pathogen causing the decline in egg production in laying hens.

After the eggs laid by the infection and the contact groups were disinfected, they were incubated according to a standard procedures. It was found that about 10 % chicken embryos in infection and contact groups did not develop, and their egg yolk coagulated (Fig. 3g). The same pathogen was isolated from these eggs and 70 % of chicks hatched from these eggs of infected hens tested positive for R. anatipestifer.

Epidemiological survey

The epidemiological survey revealed that similar cases had occurred in egg farms, breeding farms and hatcheries in Shandong, Liaoning, Shanxi, Hebei, and Henan provinces, indicating the spread of the disease. In egg farms, the cases usually manifest as a sudden drop of 5 % to 15 % in egg production rate, a decrease in egg quality (thin-shelled eggs, sandy-shelled eggs), and a 2-3 times increase in mortality and culling rate compared to normal levels. In breeding farms, although the mortality rate is relatively low, the fertilization rate of breeder eggs drops by 8 % to 12 %. In the hatcheries, the rate of dead embryos reaches 10 % to 15 %, and the proportion of weak chicks among the hatched chicks exceeds 20 %. Moreover, the typical symptoms of peritonitis in weak chicks usually appear within 7 days of age.

Discussion

Infections with R. anatipestifer affect most countries and regions worldwide and often result in substantial mortality and morbidity among ducklings (Gao et al., 2021). In 2019, a clinical infection case of R. anatipestifer in commercial broilers in Greece was reported (Tzora et al., 2021). In Australia, R. anatipestifer were also isolated from chickens (Omaleki et al., 2021). However, little is known about this disease in egg-laying chickens.

This disease is a newly emerged infectious disease in poultry in recent years. It has severely affected the production performance of laying hens, causing significant economic losses to the chicken industry. Clinically, young chickens infected with this bacterium often show no obvious symptoms, making it difficult to detect. Once the chickens start laying eggs, due to various stress factors, infected chickens may suddenly become ill, resulting in substantial economic losses.

In this study, R. anatipestifer was isolated from the oviducts of chickens with reduced egg production, indicating that the pathogen had crossed species from ducks to chickens and could colonize the reproductive system of chickens. Surveillance revealed that this bacterium could be detected from young chickens before they began laying eggs. The infection during the young stage might cause poor development of the oviducts. This bacterium was also isolated from the breeder flocks that failed to reach their peak egg-laying capacity. Animal regression tests further confirmed that the bacterium could cause a decline in egg production in chickens, consistent with natural infections.

The decline in egg production in both the infection and contact groups, and the isolation of the pathogen from hatched eggs of both groups suggested that the pathogen could be transmitted horizontally and vertically. Clinically, some breeders noticed a significant decrease in egg production or fertilization rates after 3–5 rounds of artificial insemination. In white-feather breeders, the decrease was mainly in fertilization rates, while in egg-laying breeders, it was mainly in egg production rates. Many of these laying hens failed to reach their peak egg-laying capacity. A certain amount of the pathogen was isolated from the oviducts of some flocks, and the oviducts of these chickens were underdeveloped or malformed (pseudo-hens), indicating that the main target organ of the bacterium is the reproductive system.

Artificial insemination technology in breeding chickens is an important method for improving the utilization rate of roosters, enhancing the fertilization rate of hens, reducing breeding costs, and increasing the economic benefits of chicken farming. In 2019, an outbreak of R. anatipestifer infection was reported at a large egg farm in Shandong Province, China. There the adult chickens displayed neurological symptoms, a drop in egg production by 5 to 8 %, and an increase in dead embryos in eggs by 8 to 10 %. The R. anatipestifer S63 isolate does not cause mortality in chickens or ducks, but infected adult chickens exhibit neurological symptoms, leading to reduced egg production and hatching rates. The findings indicated that administration of 1 × 107 CFU of S63 on 21 dpi resulted in 100 % mortality, while treatments with 1 × 105 CFU and 1 × 106 CFU of S63 resulted in survival rates of 50 % and 20 %, respectively. Surprisingly, all dead embryos displayed a jelly-like appearance, rather than the normal chick-shaped morphology (Chen et al., 2024).

The primary cause of infection associated with artificial insemination is procedural contamination. Reusing unsterilized semen-collection cups or insemination pipettes, or handlers coming into contact with contaminated surfaces, markedly facilitates the introduction of environmental R. anatipestifer. Data indicate that infection rates in artificially inseminated flocks are 30–40 % higher than in naturally mated ones. Therefore, attention should be paid to aseptic operations during sperm collection and insemination, as well as the presence of pathogens in breeding roosters. Comprehensive health management of chickens is a prerequisite for the application of this technology. Continuous and close surveillance of R. anatipestifer in poultry is necessary. Effective control measures, including vaccination, should also be evaluated to reduce the prevalence of the disease and mitigate its economic impact on the poultry industry. Currently, vaccines are used to prevent this disease in ducks. Whether the vaccines used on ducks are suitable for chickens mainly depends on whether their serum types are compatible, because cross-protective effects between different serum types are relatively poor. Detailed serotype analysis and experimental data are required from the clinical perspective.
