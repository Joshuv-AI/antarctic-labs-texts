Immediate or Early vs. Delayed Loading Protocols for Two‐Implant Mandibular Overdentures in Edentulous Population: A Comprehensive Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Morteza Sharifi, Asal Rezaeiyazdi, Mehdi Mohammadzadeh, Atiyeh Farahi, Majedeh Nami, Nazieh Abdollah Kookhi, Benika Abbasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsEdentulous patients often receive mandibular overdentures retained by dental implants; however, the optimal loading protocol (immediate/early vs. delayed loading) for minimizing marginal bone loss (MBL) remains uncertain. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to compare MBL between immediate or early loading protocols and delayed loading protocols in two‐implant mandibular overdentures.MethodsThis study was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing immediate and/or early loading with delayed loading in fully edentulous patients rehabilitated with two‐implant mandibular overdentures were included. Non‐randomized studies, studies involving partial edentulism, and those with insufficient sample sizes were excluded. A comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov from January 1990 to April 2025. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Data were synthesized using a random‐effects meta‐analysis, with subgroup analyses performed according to implant type, attachment system, and surface characteristics.ResultsTwenty‐two randomized controlled trials involving 580 patients (296 immediate/early loading; 284 delayed loading) and 1186 implants were included in the systematic review. Of these, 13 studies provided sufficient quantitative data for inclusion in the meta‐analysis. Pooled analysis demonstrated that immediate/early loading was associated with significantly lower marginal bone loss compared with delayed loading (WMD = –0.13 mm; 95% CI: –0.23 to –0.03; p = 0.04). However, this difference was small and likely not clinically meaningful and may fall within the range of radiographic measurement variability. Subgroup analysis showed that early loading was associated with greater reduction in MBL compared with delayed loading (WMD = –0.24 mm; 95% CI: –0.44 to –0.05), whereas immediate loading alone showed no significant difference. Bone‐level implants and Locator attachments were associated with more favorable outcomes, while effects of surface treatment were inconsistent. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies, and the certainty of evidence (GRADE) was low to moderate due to risk of bias, inconsistency, and potential publication bias.ConclusionImmediate and early loading protocols demonstrate similar clinical performance to delayed loading in terms of marginal bone preservation. Although a small statistically significant difference was observed, the effect size is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. The certainty of evidence is limited, and findings should be interpreted with caution due to heterogeneity and risk of bias across included studies.

Introduction

Currently, using two‐implant retained overdentures is considered a cost‐effective solution for mandibular edentulism [1, 2]. This approach provides flexibility, improved chewing quality, and greater efficiency compared to traditional removable complete dentures [3]. Unlike fixed full‐arch prostheses, this therapeutic approach is relatively straightforward, cost‐effective, and hygienic [4, 5]. Previously, it was believed that implants needed to be loaded for a minimum of 3 months to ensure successful osseointegration [6, 7]. However, the absence of a suitable temporary prosthesis may be unacceptable for some patients and can lead to decreased chewing performance. To address these challenges, immediate loading protocols have been introduced, enabling patients to receive a functional prosthesis immediately after implant surgery [7, 8]. Various loading protocols have been developed, with implant prosthesis loading defined within 1 week immediately and 2 months for delayed loading after placement [9, 10, 11]. Numerous clinical and histological studies have shown comparable results between immediately and delayed loaded implants for fixed prostheses [12].

In the 1990s, immediate loading became an option for mandibular overdentures. However, there is a need to gather data from similar studies to establish more reliable evidence. Different studies have shown varying outcomes, often with small sample sizes and no consensus on implant loading times [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Most previous systematic reviews have focused on marginal bone loss (MBL) or implant failure rates associated with different loading procedures [13, 14, 17, 18]. However, these reviews have only included a small number of studies that directly compare immediate vs. delayed loading, especially randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Furthermore, not enough research has been conducted to determine whether the number of implants may impact the results of immediate loading.

In 2020, a systematic review and meta‐analysis titled “Comparing Immediate and Delayed Loading Protocols for Two Submerged Dental Implants” was published. This was the first meta‐analysis in the field to analyze the findings of clinical trial studies and draw a unified conclusion. The results indicated that there was no statistically significant difference in peri‐implant bone loss between the two procedures [19]. Additionally, due to the higher fracture rate associated with delayed loading, immediate loading appears to be the preferred strategy. However, the main issue lies in the lack of sufficient information to compare these two approaches and determine the optimal method for performing submerged dental implants. Therefore, in this study, researchers decided to investigate both immediate/early and delayed loading protocols, considering all possible outcomes such as MBL. The results of this study have the potential to significantly improve clinical decision‑making and update current guidelines, potentially leading to improved treatment outcomes and patient satisfaction. By comparing MBL between immediate or early and delayed loading protocols for submerged dental implants, clinicians will have higher‑quality evidence to guide their treatment planning. The null hypothesis of this study was that there is no significant difference in marginal bone loss between immediate/early loading and delayed loading protocols for two‑implant mandibular overdentures.

Methods

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [20]. The protocol for the systematic review and meta‐analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42024522149.

Eligibility Criteria

In the systematic review and meta‐analysis titled “Immediate or early vs. delayed loading protocols for two‐implant mandibular overdentures in edentulous population,” the focus was on comparing immediate/early and delayed loading protocols for two‐implant mandibular overdentures. RCTs were included due to their capacity to provide robust evidence through randomization and control of confounding variables. The study targeted fully edentulous individuals in the mandibular arch, excluding research on partial edentulism or other dental arches. The comparison centered on immediate or early loading, where the prosthesis is attached to implants immediately after placement, vs. delayed loading, which involves a healing period before prosthesis attachment.

Key outcome measures included MBL and patient‐reported outcomes, rather than solely technical or laboratory outcomes. Only studies published in English were considered, with no restrictions on age or gender to ensure a broad participant representation. To maintain the reliability and validity of the findings, non‐randomized studies, non‐comparative studies, studies involving partially edentulous populations, and studies lacking sufficient outcome data for quantitative synthesis were excluded. No minimum sample size threshold was applied during study selection (Table 1).

Search Strategy and Screening Process

A comprehensive search strategy was developed to identify relevant studies based on the objectives of this review. Keywords were selected according to the research topic and combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The most common search phrases included “immediate loading,” “early loading,” “delayed loading,” “two‑implant mandibular overdentures,” “edentulous,” “implant‑supported overdentures,” “dental implants,” “oral rehabilitation,” “mandibular arch,” and “randomized controlled trial.” Synonyms were identified through controlled vocabularies in MeSH and Emtree to ensure inclusivity. The databases searched were PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library (including the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search period extended from January 1990 to April 2025. To minimize the risk of missing relevant studies, reference lists of included articles were also screened manually. Full details of the search strategy are provided in Supporting Materials File 1 (Table S1). All retrieved records were imported into EndNote version 8, where duplicates and irrelevant entries were removed. Screening was conducted independently by two reviewers (MSH and BA) in three stages: title, abstract, and full‑text review. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (BA).

Data Extraction

Data extraction focused on study characteristics and outcome measures relevant to the research question. Extracted variables included author name and publication year, study design and duration, sample size, population characteristics, implant type, and loading protocol. Information on randomization methods and diagnostic criteria for marginal bone loss (MBL) and other outcomes was also documented. Two reviewers (MSH and NK) independently performed the data extraction in three phases: study selection, qualitative assessment, and quantitative data extraction. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer (BA). All extracted data were cross‑verified for accuracy and completeness before inclusion in the meta‑analysis. Importantly, the reported sample sizes reflect the initial randomized populations of each trial. Patients lost to follow‑up or implants that failed were excluded from the pooled analysis of MBL outcomes, but these events were documented separately in Supporting Materials File 1 (Table S2). This ensured that only participants with complete outcome data contributed to the quantitative synthesis, while maintaining transparency regarding attrition and implant failures. Because MBL measurements require radiographic follow‐up data, implants that failed before outcome assessment could not contribute MBL values to the quantitative synthesis.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the selected studies was assessed using Version 2 of the Cochrane risk‐of‐bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [21] after the screening. The RoB 2 tool evaluates the risk of bias in five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. For each domain, the study was rated as low, high, or unclear risk of bias. By using the RoB 2 tool, the authors were able to assess the quality of the selected studies, ensuring that the study findings were reliable and accurate.

Data Analysis

The meta‐analysis was performed using the weighted mean difference (WMD) as the effect measure for continuous outcomes (marginal bone loss, MBL), as all included studies reported outcomes on the same measurement scale [22, 23, 24]. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of MBL for immediate/early and delayed loading groups were extracted from each study. When SDs were not directly reported, they were calculated from standard errors, confidence intervals, or p‐values according to Cochrane Handbook guidelines. A random‐effects model was applied using the inverse‐variance method based on the DerSimonian and Laird approach to account for both within‐study and between‐study variability. Between‐study variance (τ2) was estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird estimator, and study weights were calculated as the inverse of the sum of within‐study variance and τ2. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic. An I2 value > 50% was considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger's regression test, and where applicable, trim‐and‐fill analysis was performed to assess the potential impact of missing studies [25]. Subgroup analyses were conducted a priori based on implant type (bone‐level vs. tissue‐level), attachment system, and implant surface characteristics. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of results across studies with different risk of bias classifications. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 17), and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Included Studies

In this meta‑analysis, 22 clinical trials were selected after screening titles, abstracts, and full texts from international databases. These studies compared outcomes such as mean quality of life, marginal bone loss (MBL), and survival rates between two groups receiving either immediate/early or delayed implant protocols (Figure 1). Supporting Materials File 1 (Table S2) provides detailed study characteristics, including study type, total sample size in both groups, implant type, study population, and other relevant details. Across the selected trials, the total sample size was 296 patients (598 implants) in the immediate/early loading group and 284 patients (588 implants) in the delayed loading group, for a total of 580 patients and 1186 implants. Of the 22 trials, 10 specified the implant type: three used bone‑level implants, and seven used tissue‑level implants. Additionally, 19 studies reported attachment types—four used the Locator type, 11 used the Ball type, three used the Dolder bar type, and one used ITI retentive anchors. Regarding surface treatments, seven studies used TiUnite, four used SLActive, three used fluoride‑modified micro‑rough titanium, and one used Laser‑Lok, while the remaining studies did not report surface treatment details (Supporting Materials File 1, Table S2). Qualitative assessment of radiographic reporting across the included trials indicated that variability in imaging modality and reporting detail was present but did not appear to substantially influence the observed pattern of MBL. Most studies did not explicitly report the radiographic technique or calibration method used for MBL assessment. Where reported, studies using standardized periapical radiographs typically provided more consistent and lower‐variance MBL values, whereas studies with less detailed reporting or broader variability in measurements were more likely to have used panoramic radiography or non‐standardized imaging protocols. However, this interpretation is descriptive and cannot be confirmed due to limited methodological reporting in several trials. Supporting Materials File 1 (Table S2) and Supporting Materials File 2 (Screening file) provide comprehensive details on the complete screening and data extraction process.

Meta‐Analysis

Across the 22 included trials, the total randomized sample size was 580 patients (296 immediate/early loading; 284 delayed loading). After accounting for implant failures and losses to follow‑up, the effective sample size analyzed for MBL outcomes was slightly lower, as detailed in Supporting Materials File 1 (Table S2). Among these studies, five effect sizes compared mean MBL in early loading with delayed loading, eight studies compared mean MBL in immediate loading with delayed loading, and two studies compared mean MBL in immediate loading with early loading (the study by Reis, R., et al. did not report the mean difference in the early loading group). While most studies reported survival rates, they were not analyzable as all reported 100% survival in both groups, indicating no significant difference in survival rates. Additionally, the mean quality of life was reported only in the study by Alfadda SA and colleagues, and the mean pain score was reported only in the study by Reis, R., et al. Regarding the success rate or percentage, most selected studies reported a 100% success rate in both groups, so no analysis was conducted for this outcome.

Although 22 randomized controlled trials were included in the systematic review, only 13 provided sufficient quantitative data on MBL specifically mean values with standard deviations to be pooled in the meta‑analysis (Figure 2). The remaining nine studies were excluded from quantitative synthesis because they reported only survival or success rates, presented qualitative outcomes such as patient satisfaction, pain scores, or quality of life, or did not provide extractable numerical data for MBL. These studies were nevertheless retained in the qualitative synthesis to ensure a comprehensive overview of the evidence base. From the 13 studies included in the quantitative synthesis, 15 effect sizes were extracted: five compared early vs. delayed loading, eight compared immediate vs. delayed loading, and two compared immediate vs. early loading. The pooled analysis demonstrated that implants in the immediate/early loading group exhibited, on average, 0.13 mm less bone loss compared to the delayed loading group (95% CI: –0.23 to –0.03), a statistically significant finding. Overall heterogeneity was high (I2 = 80.63%, p = 0.04) (Figure 2). Although this difference was statistically significant, the magnitude of the effect (–0.13 mm) is small and is likely within the range of radiographic measurement error. Therefore, it should be interpreted with caution in terms of clinical relevance. Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the pooled effect size was robust. The weighted mean difference remained stable when each study was sequentially omitted, ranging from approximately −0.10 to −0.18 mm (Supporting Materials File 1, Figure S1).

The pooled weighted mean difference (WMD) of MBL between immediate or early vs. delayed loading protocols for two‐implant mandibular overdentures.

Results from studies comparing the bone loss around implants with immediate loading vs. implants with delayed loading showed that implants with immediate loading had, on average, 0.03 units less bone loss compared to implants with delayed loading (WMD: −0.03, 95% CI: −0.09 to 0.03, I2: 0.00%, P heterogeneity: 0.89). On the other hand, implants with early loading compared to implants with delayed loading showed a larger reduction in bone loss, with an average difference of 0.24 units (WMD: −0.24, 95% CI: −0.44 to −0.05, I2: 86.63%, P heterogeneity: 0.00).

To assess publication bias and heterogeneity, a funnel plot and Egger's test along with a Galbraith plot were used. The results are shown in Figure 3. Egger's test results indicated that there is publication bias in the combined studies (B: −2.23; SE: 0.857; P: 0.009). In this context, a trim and fill analysis was also performed, and the results showed that this bias did not significantly affect the results (WMD: −0.129; 95% CI: −0.233, −0.026).

The Galbraith and Funnel plots of MBL to assess heterogeneity and publication bias.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on the variables of type of implants, type of attachment, and surface treatment. The results of these subgroup analyses for comparing implants with immediate loading to implants with delayed loading on MBL are shown in Table 2. Overall, the mean MBL in implants with immediate loading compared to implants with delayed loading decreased by 0.55 units in the bone level subgroup (WMD: −0.55; 95% CI: −0.87, −0.23), while it decreased by 0.10 units in the tissue level subgroup (WMD: −0.10; 95% CI: −0.27, −0.01). Therefore, if the implant is placed at the bone level, the mean MBL in implants with immediate loading is significantly less than in implants with delayed loading (Q: 6.11; P: 0.01) (Table 2).

In the subgroup analysis based on the type of attachment, the results showed that the reduction in MBL was significantly greater in the Locator subgroup compared to the Ball subgroup. For the Ball & Equator and Bar‐clip subgroups, there were not enough studies to perform the analysis (each of these subgroups had only one study analyzed). Based on surface treatment, the results showed that the mean MBL in implants with immediate loading compared to implants with delayed loading decreased by 0.44 units in the fluoride‐modified microrough titanium subgroup (WMD: −0.44; 95% CI: −0.84, −0.05), while it decreased by 0.12 units in the SLActive/laser lock subgroup (WMD: −0.12; 95% CI: −1.06, −0.01). Subgroup analyses for comparing implants with early loading to implants with delayed loading on MBL based on these variables were not fundamentally conducted because all studies in this domain were on the bone level and ball attachments. No statistically significant difference was observed between imaging modality subgroups (Q = 2.88, p = 0.09), suggesting that radiographic method did not significantly modify the effect estimates (Table 2).

Risk of Bias Results

The quality assessment of the selected studies in this meta‐analysis was performed based on the Cochrane risk of bias tool, and the results are reported in Figure 4. According to this checklist, most of the selected studies had an appropriate quality for performing the analysis and drawing qualitative conclusions. However, in the dimensions of blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other biases, some studies had errors due to the lack of blinding of outcome assessors, evaluation or analysis of the outcome, and appropriate reporting of it. These were categorized as unclear or high‐risk (Figure 4). A subgroup analysis based on the risk of bias assessment was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect estimates. Studies with high risk of bias showed a pooled effect size of WMD = –0.17 mm (95% CI: –0.38 to –0.04), while studies with low risk of bias demonstrated a slightly larger effect size of WMD = –0.29 mm (95% CI: –0.54 to –0.04). Studies with unclear risk of bias showed a comparable effect (WMD = –0.28 mm; 95% CI: –0.57 to –0.10) (Table 2).

GRADE Results

The certainty of evidence for the primary outcome (MBL) was assessed using the GRADE approach. Overall, the certainty of evidence was rated as low to moderate. This rating was primarily downgraded due to serious concerns regarding risk of bias in several included randomized controlled trials, substantial inconsistency across studies (I2 ≈ 80%), and potential publication bias as indicated by Egger's test. Although the pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in marginal bone loss favoring immediate/early loading (WMD = –0.13 mm; 95% CI: –0.23 to –0.03), the certainty of evidence limits the confidence in the magnitude of this effect. No serious concerns were identified regarding indirectness, as the included studies directly addressed the population, intervention, and outcome of interest. Imprecision was considered low to borderline, given the narrow confidence interval but small absolute effect size, which is likely within the range of radiographic measurement variability. Overall, these findings indicate that the true effect of loading protocol on marginal bone loss is uncertain and should be interpreted cautiously in clinical decision‐making (Table 3).

Discussion

The present meta‐analysis provides valuable insights into the comparison of immediate/early loading and delayed loading of dental implants, with a particular focus on MBL. The overall pooled analysis demonstrated that implants with immediate/early loading exhibited statistically less MBL compared to those with delayed loading (WMD = –0.13 mm, 95% CI: –0.23 to –0.03, p = 0.04). Although the meta‐analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in marginal bone loss in favor of immediate/early loading, the observed effect size (–0.13 mm) is very small and is likely within the margin of radiographic measurement error reported for conventional implant radiographic assessments. Therefore, while statistically detectable, this difference is unlikely to be clinically meaningful in routine clinical practice. These findings suggest that both loading protocols provide comparable clinical performance in terms of marginal bone preservation. The results of the leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings, as exclusion of individual studies did not materially change the pooled effect size or its direction. When subgroup analyses were performed, the results differed. Immediate‐only loading vs. delayed loading showed no statistically significant difference, whereas early loading vs. delayed loading revealed a significant reduction in bone loss. These findings suggest that immediate/early loading protocols can be considered clinically comparable to delayed loading in terms of peri‐implant bone preservation. Importantly, immediate loading does not appear to increase the risk of bone loss, and early loading may, in certain contexts, confer a modest benefit. Nevertheless, these conclusions must be interpreted within the biomechanical prerequisites required for the safe application of immediate loading. Immediate loading is considered predictable only when high primary stability is achieved, as inadequate stability increases the risk of micromotion and early implant failure. Major consensus guidelines, including those from the International Team for Implantology (ITI) and the European Association for Osseointegration (EAO), recommend minimum thresholds of insertion torque (IT) > 35 Ncm or an Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) > 60 to ensure sufficient mechanical anchorage during the early healing phase. Many of the trials included in this review either explicitly required these stability criteria or excluded cases with compromised bone quality, which likely contributed to the favorable outcomes observed. Therefore, while the present findings support the non‐inferiority of immediate/early loading protocols, their clinical application should remain conditional on achieving adequate primary stability, and these results should not be extrapolated to implants placed in low‐density bone or situations where IT or ISQ values fall below recommended thresholds. Future clinical trials should report stability metrics more consistently to allow stratified analyses and to better define the boundaries of safe immediate loading. Taken together, these results support the interpretation of non‐inferiority: immediate/early loading protocols do not compromise peri‐implant bone levels when applied in appropriately selected patients who meet established stability criteria [26]. Furthermore, the analysis indicated that early loading resulted in a more pronounced reduction in mean MBL compared with delayed loading. This significant reduction, coupled with the considerable heterogeneity observed among studies, suggests that early loading may offer advantages in preserving peri‑implant bone levels. The substantial variability within this subgroup likely reflects differences in patient characteristics, implant macro‑ and micro‑designs, bone quality, and clinical protocols across the included trials. Early loading may provide an optimal balance between functional stimulation and biological healing, allowing controlled mechanical loading during a period when bone remodeling is active, which could contribute to its favorable effect on bone preservation. Additionally, some studies reported that greater bone loss in the delayed loading group may be attributable to inadequate oral hygiene maintenance during the healing period, highlighting the importance of patient compliance and peri‑implant care in influencing outcomes [27, 28].

The primary stability was necessary during surgical implant placement, while secondary stability achieved during function was essential after osseointegration, regardless of the loading protocol. Immediate loading requires sufficient primary stability to withstand occlusal forces during the initial healing period. Implant design features, such as conical connections, can help achieve the necessary stability for immediate loading without compromising bone levels [29, 30]. Bone quality was categorized into four types, ranging from dense to spongy bone. Implants placed in dense bone (types 1 and 2) show better initial stability than those placed in poorer quality bone (types 3 and 4) (2). Patients with adequate bone density and quality are better candidates for immediate loading, as they can provide the required primary stability. Poor bone conditions, such as significant bone loss or atrophy, may compromise osseointegration and increase the risk of bone loss with immediate loading [13, 31, 32]. The surgical technique plays a vital role in achieving primary stability and minimizing bone loss. Precise implant placement, avoiding excessive drilling, and maintaining the integrity of the extraction socket walls are important factors. Experienced clinicians are better equipped to handle immediate loading protocols [33, 34, 35, 36].

Delayed loading allows for a longer healing period before the implant is subjected to functional forces, potentially leading to better bone preservation. However, advancements in implant surfaces and surgical techniques have reduced the differences in bone loss between immediate and delayed loading protocols [37, 38, 39]. The mean MBL was significantly lower for immediate loading compared to delayed loading in the bone‐level implant subgroup, indicating that immediate loading might be particularly beneficial for this implant design. For tissue‐level implants, the reduction was less pronounced but still significant, suggesting that implant design plays a crucial role in the outcome.

In an in vitro study [40], it was discovered that ball attachments are more effective in reducing the movement of dentures and distributing stress in mandibular implant‐supported overdentures. They are also reported to be less expensive than other attachments and less sensitive to technique [41]. In cases where space is limited, locator attachments are preferred, as they offer good retention and higher patient satisfaction [42]. However, previous studies have not found significant differences between these attachment systems [43, 44]. The reduction in MBL was significantly greater in the Locator subgroup compared to the Ball subgroup, possibly due to the mechanical properties and stability provided by different attachment systems. Further investigation is needed to examine the effects of other attachment types. It seems that the success rate of implants is not affected by the attachment systems. Other factors, such as arch shape, bone quality, and quantity, seem to have a more significant influence on implant survival rates [43, 44].

The formation of an interface between connective tissue and the implant collar that is more like that of a natural tooth will improve the long‐term performance of dental implants [45]. With the help of improved surface characteristics, implants with diameters less than 3 mm could be placed with high primary stability in areas with minimal bone volume like in anterior ridges [46]. Implants with fluoride‐modified microrough titanium showed a significant reduction in MBL with immediate loading compared to delayed loading, suggesting that surface modifications enhancing osseointegration can positively impact bone preservation. For SLActive/laser lock implants, the reduction was minor but significant, highlighting the role of advanced surface treatments in implant success.

Radiographic variability across the included studies, including differences in imaging modalities and calibration methods for marginal bone loss assessment, represents a potential source of methodological heterogeneity. However, subgroup analysis based on imaging modality demonstrated no statistically significant impact on pooled effect estimates, suggesting that these methodological differences did not meaningfully influence the overall findings. Nevertheless, the limited reporting of radiographic protocols in several trials highlights the need for future randomized controlled studies to adopt standardized and well‐documented imaging and calibration procedures to improve comparability across studies.

In general, the results emphasize the significance of taking into account implant type, attachment type, and surface treatment when making clinical decisions. Despite the study's quality limitations and publication bias, the findings offer valuable insights for clinicians looking to improve implant loading protocols. With similar success rates between immediate and early loading, clinicians have increased flexibility in selecting the most appropriate method for individual patients. It is important to thoroughly assess the implant features, connection method, and surface treatment before making a choice. Additional well‐conducted studies with larger samples and extended follow‐up periods are required to enhance the evidence and establish clearer guidelines. Researchers must also investigate how certain implant design elements and surface treatments affect the effectiveness of immediate and early loading procedures.

This meta‐analysis has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although 22 randomized controlled trials were included in the systematic review, only 13 studies provided sufficient quantitative data for inclusion in the meta‐analysis, which may have reduced the precision of the pooled estimates. Second, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies, likely reflecting differences in implant systems, attachment mechanisms, surface characteristics, loading protocols, follow‐up durations, and patient‐related factors. Third, evidence of publication bias was detected by Egger's test, although trim‐and‐fill analysis suggested that the overall effect estimate remained relatively stable. Another important limitation relates to missing outcome data. Marginal bone loss measurements were unavailable for implants that failed before the scheduled follow‐up assessments; therefore, these implants could not contribute quantitative MBL data to the pooled analysis. Although implant failures and losses to follow‐up were documented separately and were relatively uncommon, the possibility of attrition bias cannot be completely excluded. In addition, several clinically relevant outcomes, including implant survival, implant success, prosthetic complications, pain, and patient‐reported satisfaction, could not be quantitatively synthesized because these outcomes were inconsistently reported across the included studies. Future well‐designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, longer follow‐up periods, standardized reporting of marginal bone loss, and comprehensive assessment of patient‐centered outcomes are needed. Consistent reporting of implant stability parameters, implant failures, and follow‐up data would also facilitate intention‐to‐treat analyses and strengthen the evidence regarding the comparative effectiveness of immediate, early, and delayed loading protocols.

Conclusion

This meta‐analysis indicates that immediate and early loading protocols for dental implants result in MBL outcomes that are generally comparable to those observed with delayed loading. Although the pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant difference favoring immediate/early loading, the absolute magnitude of this effect (–0.13 mm) is very small and is likely within the range of radiographic measurement variability, and therefore unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Subgroup analyses suggest that early loading may be associated with a modest reduction in MBL compared with delayed loading, whereas immediate loading alone shows no significant difference. Overall, the findings suggest that immediate and early loading protocols do not adversely affect peri‐implant marginal bone levels when appropriate case selection and clinical protocols are applied. However, given the small effect sizes and methodological heterogeneity across studies, these results should be interpreted with caution.
