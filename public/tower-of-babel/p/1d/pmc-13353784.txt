Non-absorbable antibiotics worsen alcohol-associated liver disease in gastric acid-suppressed mice

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric acid-suppressive medications, particularly proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), are commonly used in patients with alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) to prevent and manage upper gastrointestinal bleeding, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory/aspirin-induced gastroesophageal damage. By inhibiting the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, PPIs suppress acid secretion and impair bacterial killing, thereby promoting gut dysbiosis that disrupts barrier integrity and enhances bacterial translocation, ultimately exacerbating liver injury. PPIs are frequently co-administered with antibiotics for indications such as gastrointestinal bleeding, Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP), other infections, or hepatic encephalopathy prophylaxis, but the consequences of this combined therapy on gut microbial ecology and disease outcomes remain unclear. Our study addresses this gap by showing how PPI use, alone or with antibiotics, reshapes the gut microbiome and aggravates liver disease progression. In previous studies, we showed that PPIs promote dysbiosis and ALD progression in mice and humans by facilitating intestinal expansion and hepatic translocation of Gram-positive Enterococcus. Fecal cytolysin, an Enterococcus faecalis exotoxin that induces hepatocyte death, predicts mortality in patients with alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH). In this study, we have examined the mechanism by which PPIs alone and in combination with non-absorbable antibiotics targeting Gram-positive bacteria influence ALD, as well as the disease mechanisms associated with cytolytic Enterococcus faecalis and the development of therapeutic strategies. In mice, alcohol administration during gastric acid suppression promoted expansion of Gram-positive taxa, including cytolysin-producing Enterococcus. Similarly, PPI use in patients with AH was associated with increased fecal Enterococcus and higher 30-d mortality, underscoring the translational relevance of our findings. Unexpectedly, treatment of acid-suppressed mice with non-absorbable antibiotics designed to suppress Gram-positive bacteria worsened ethanol-induced steatohepatitis: while Enterococcus abundance decreased, Streptococcus and other potentially pathogenic taxa expanded, leading to increased bacterial translocation and aggravated liver injury. In patients with cirrhosis or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), PPIs did not promote Enterococcus expansion, indicating etiology-dependent microbiome responses. Finally, we identified dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine and Caspase-1 inhibitor as in vitro and in vivo modulators of cytolysin activity, highlighting potential therapeutic avenues. Collectively, our study demonstrates how PPIs and non-absorbable antibiotics targeting Gram-positive bacteria interact with the gut microbiome to drive ALD, underscoring the need for careful therapeutic management.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease causes over 2 million deaths annually (~4% of global mortality), with rising incidence driven by increasing alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) linked to global trends in obesity, diabetes, and alcohol use. 1 , 2 ALD encompasses a spectrum of liver injuries, from isolated steatosis to more severe forms such as alcohol-associated steatohepatitis (ASH) in 20%–40%, with or without fibrosis, progressing to cirrhosis in 8%–20% of ASH cases, and ultimately to portal hypertension, decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Some patients may develop alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH), which significantly worsens prognosis, with a 3-month mortality of 30%–40%. ALD prevalence is 3.5% in the general population, but it increases significantly among individuals with alcohol use disorder (AUD), reaching 55.1% for ALD and 12.9% for cirrhosis. 3 , 4 Alcohol is responsible for about 50% of cirrhosis-related deaths worldwide. 5 The gut-liver axis plays a crucial role in ALD pathogenesis; alcohol-induced gut barrier dysfunction allows bacterial translocation and their products to the liver, promoting inflammation and accelerating the progression of ALD. 6-11

Concurrently, the use of gastric acid-suppressive medications, especially PPIs, has risen globally over the past decade. 12-16 This increase persists despite the link to adverse outcomes in ALD. 11 , 17 , 18 Approximately 6%–23% of the general population is receiving acid suppression therapy 19-21 ; 32% of patients with MASLD 22 and 40%–72% of patients with cirrhosis take acid-reducing medications. 23-25 The global PPI market is projected to continue expanding. 26 These exponential trends underscore the need for ongoing evaluation of the interplay between acid-suppressive therapy and liver disease outcomes in clinical practice.

Gastric acid kills microorganisms, and its suppression significantly alters the composition of the intestinal microbiota. 27 Our previous work demonstrated that suppression of gastric acid secretion promotes intestinal dysbiosis and ALD progression in mice and humans by facilitating the expansion of intestinal Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) and translocation to the liver. 11 E. faecalis produces cytolysin, a two-subunit exotoxin, that causes hepatocyte death and liver injury. The presence of fecal cytolysin in patients with AH predicts mortality, while targeted bacteriophage therapy reduces ethanol-induced liver damage in humanized mice colonized with AH patient-derived microbiota. 28 With this evidence, we explored microbial dynamics in gastric acid suppression and the combined impact of non-absorbable antibiotics targeting Gram-positive taxa on ALD. We found that, in the context of ethanol consumption, gastric acid suppression promotes the abundance of Gram-positive taxa in both mice and humans. Strikingly, in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, which carry a point mutation in the gastric H+/K+-ATPase gene resulting in lifelong achlorhydria and are a well-established model of gastric acid suppression, 11 , 29 treatment with non-absorbable antibiotics targeting Gram-positive bacteria exacerbated ethanol-induced steatohepatitis. Although antibiotic treatment generally normalized the abundance of intestinal Gram-positive taxa, an increase in Streptococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, Parasutterella, and Helicobacter was observed. Notably, antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl mice exhibited increased bacterial translocation to the liver, suggesting a potential role in liver disease progression. PPI-induced Enterococcus expansion was observed in control individuals and patients with AH, but not in those with cirrhosis or MASLD, indicating that microbiome responses to PPIs depend on liver disease etiology. We explored in vitro and in vivo therapeutic strategies to neutralize cytolysin toxicity and found that dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine and Caspase-1/11 inhibitors reduced its cytolytic activity.

Material and methods

Mice

All experiments were performed using female mice, based on their increased susceptibility to ethanol-induced liver injury, which allows reproducible modeling of ALD. 30-34 C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River and used in Figures 3l–m, 6a–e, 6f–i, Figures S1 and S7a–e. Sublytic Atp4a Sl/Sl mice on a C57BL/6J background have been previously described. 11 , 35 This strain carries a T to C point mutation in the Atp4a gene, which encodes the α subunit of the gastric H+/K+-ATPase, resulting in impaired gastric acid secretion. Compared with pharmacological models using proton pump inhibitors, this genetic model of lifelong achlorhydria avoids potential off-target or secondary effects, allowing us to directly attribute observed changes in gut microbiota and liver pathology to impaired gastric acid secretion. 11 , 29 Breeding was performed using heterozygous mice, and experimental mice were either wild-type (WT) or Atp4a Sl/Sl littermates.

10-week-old female mice were fed a Lieber DeCarli diet for 9 weeks following a previously described protocol. 11 , 36 Ethanol content was gradually increased: 0% on day 1, 10% on days 2–3, 20% on days 4–5, 30% from day 6 to week 6, and 36% for the final 3 weeks to allow metabolic adaptation and maintain consistent caloric intake, minimizing acute toxicity and ensuring sustained ethanol consumption for reproducible induction of ethanol-induced liver injury. 37 , 38 Control animals received an isocaloric iso-maltose substitute.

Additionally, age-matched female mice underwent the chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding protocol (NIAAA model), 37 receiving Lieber DeCarli diet for 15 d with 0% ethanol for the first 5 d and 36% thereafter, and control groups were pair-fed with the isocaloric control diet. On day 16, mice were given a single ethanol gavage (5 g kg−1 body weight) or isocaloric maltose dextrin, respectively, in the morning and sacrificed 9 h later for hepatocyte isolation. Mice were pair-fed throughout. This model was selected as a well-established, efficient, and shorter alternative for inducing ethanol-related liver injury.

To model chronic liver injury and fibrosis, WT mice and their Atp4a Sl/Sl littermate mice were treated with CCl4 (4 µl/g body weight; 1:8 dilution with corn oil) or corn oil as a control (4 µl/g body weight) by intraperitoneal injections 3 times per week.

A subset of Atp4a Sl/Sl littermate mice was gavaged daily with either vehicle (water) or a combination of vancomycin hydrochloride (RPI, 100 mg kg−1) and bacitracin (Novaplus, 273 mg kg−1)—non-absorbable antibiotics primarily targeting Gram-positive bacteria 39-42 —during the last 4 weeks of a 9-week Lieber-DeCarli chronic feeding model. We used the non-absorbable antibiotics vancomycin hydrochloride and bacitracin to selectively counteract the PPI-driven expansion of Gram-positive bacteria, particularly Enterococcus, while minimizing systemic exposure and avoiding hepatic metabolism or toxicity. 42-44

To induce gastric acid suppression, a pharmacological model was used by incorporating omeprazole (Fagron) at 200 ppm into the liquid diet or vehicle (water) for 9 weeks of treatment as previously described 11 (Figure 3m).

An E. faecalis colonization model was used as previously described. 11 Female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River) received daily oral doses of polymyxin B (McKesson, 150 mg kg−1) and neomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, 200 mg kg−1) for 1 week to deplete commensal microbiota and facilitate colonization of oral gavaged cytolytic E. faecalis strain Sorialis (PRJNA318633; 5 × 109 CFUs) or water (vehicle) every third day. E. faecalis Sorialis was isolated from an ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mouse, and its identity was confirmed by 16S rRNA PCR and sequencing as previously described. 11 Similarly, an E. casseliflavus colonization model was established using live and heat-killed bacteria. Female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River) underwent the same antibiotic pretreatment, followed by oral gavage of live or dead E. casseliflavus (or vehicle; water) at the same dose (5 × 109 CFU) every third day following our established Enterococcus colonization protocol, to achieve intestinal Enterococcus levels comparable to those observed in achlorhydric mice. 11 E. casseliflavus was isolated from an ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mouse and identified by 16S rRNA PCR and sequence analyses. E. faecalis and E. casseliflavus were grown freshly in Bacto Brain-Heart infusion (BHI) medium (Becton Dickinson) for each gavage.

As described, Atp4a Sl/Sl mice naturally develop iron deficiency anemia at the ages of 4–6 weeks, as acid is crucial for efficient absorption of non-heme iron, 11 , 35 but 4 weeks of iron supplementation (2% carbonyl iron diet; 7012, Envigo) reversed anemia and iron deficiency for the length of the experimental procedures. 11 WT and Atp4a Sl/Sl littermates, as well as WT vehicle- and PPI-treated littermates, were placed on an iron supplementation diet for 4 weeks. 11 Mice were then maintained on a regular chow diet (RC) (5053; LabDiet) for 1 week before experiments.

For in vivo cytolysin activity inhibition studies, female C57BL/6 mice were pretreated for 1 week with non-absorbable polymyxin B (150 mg kg−1) and neomycin (200 mg kg−1) for 1 week as described above. Following microbiota depletion, mice were gavaged with 5 × 109 CFU cytolysin-positive E. faecalis Sorialis (or water as vehicle) every third day and subjected to chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding (NIAAA model). Four experimental groups were included: (1) ethanol + vehicle (water) (2) ethanol + E. faecalis Sorialis, (3) ethanol + E. faecalis Sorialis plus the irreversible Caspase-1 inhibitor Z-YVAD-FMK (10 mg kg−1, administered intraperitoneally every third day), and (4) ethanol + E. faecalis Sorialis plus dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC; 100 mg kg−1, administered intraperitoneally every third day, with additional daily dietary supplementation at the same dose).

All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of California, San Diego (protocol S24098, S09042). Euthanasia was performed either at study completion or upon reaching humane endpoints, following American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA0 and UC San Diego UC San Diego Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines. Humane endpoints included ≥20% body weight loss or a marked deterioration in overall clinical condition; clinical signs of distress such as hunched posture, piloerection, lethargy, or reduced food and water intake; and model-specific criteria, including diarrhea or signs of infection, as well as hypothermia, lethargy, or intoxication-related symptoms. Euthanasia was performed by CO₂ inhalation, either upon reaching humane endpoints or at study completion. Death was confirmed by a secondary method, including cervical dislocation or bilateral thoracotomy. For terminal tissue collection, euthanasia was conducted under deep anesthesia using ketamine/xylazine. All personnel involved in animal procedures were appropriately trained and authorized in accordance with institutional requirements.

Bacterial DNA isolation and 16S rRNA sequencing

DNA was isolated from the stool of mice using either the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN) or the previously described protocol, 11 and from the liver of mice using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN) protocol. 45 Fecal DNA was extracted following enzymatic digestion and mechanical lysis, then purified via phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. The V1–V3 regions of 16S rRNA genes were sequenced using 454 GS FLX Titanium, with Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) defined at 97% identity. Data are available under BioProject PRJNA294003 (BioSample IDs SAMN04032754–SAMN04032783). 11

Human stool samples collected from patients with AH from the InTeam Consortium, and DNA extraction from human stool samples or bacterial cultures were described before. 28 The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using barcoded Illumina-adapted primers. Amplicons were purified, quantified, normalized, and pooled for sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp, V2 chemistry). OTUs were identified using a MOTHUR-based pipeline. Raw reads are available under BioProject PRJNA525701. 28

Bacterial DNA isolation and 16S rRNA sequencing on stool samples from control individuals and cirrhotic patients were analyzed after DNA extraction as previously described. 27 Initial community profiling was performed using length heterogeneity PCR (LH-PCR) with fluorescently labeled 16S primers (27F/355R). Amplicons were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis, and peak areas were normalized with custom scripts. Taxonomic identification was then performed using Multitag Pyrosequencing (MTPS) with uniquely barcoded primers and sequencing on a Roche GS-FLX. Sequence reads were demultiplexed and normalized by barcode counts. Taxa were assigned using the RDP classifier (v10), and genera representing <1% relative abundance were excluded to focus on prevalent community members.

Bacterial DNA isolation and 16S rRNA sequencing on stool samples from patients with MASLD were analyzed as previously described. 46 Bacterial DNA from stool samples of MASLD patients was extracted using the RNeasy Power Microbiome Kit (Qiagen). The V2–V9 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified with the Ion 16S Metagenomics Kit (Thermo Fisher) and purified using the NucleoMag NGS Clean-up Kit. Libraries were prepared with the Ion Plus Fragment Library Kit, and sequencing was performed on the Ion Torrent S5 platform. Taxonomic assignment was based on MicroSEQ and Greengenes databases (97% genus, 99% species identity) using the Ion Reporter 16S workflow. Sequence data have been deposited in NCBI under BioProject PRJNA540738, with reads available under BioSample IDs SAMN11554417-SAMN11554433, SAMN11554446, SAMN11554451-SAMN11554484, and SAMN13895357-SAMN13895400. 46

Enterococcus cultures

Fecal samples were homogenized using a bead beater. For selective enrichment, samples were incubated in BBL Enterococcosel broth (Becton Dickinson) under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 72 h. Positive cultures were identified by a brown-black color due to esculin hydrolysis. Enterococcus strains were cultured statically or with shaking (~200 rpm) in BHI broth or on BHI agar at 37 °C. Cytolysin activity was assessed by culturing strains, including cytolysin-positive E. faecalis Sorialis, positive control FA2–2(pAM714), and negative control FA2–2(pAM771), 28 , 47 on blood agar to detect hemolysis.

Hemolytic assay

Hemolytic assay was performed as previously described. 48 E. faecalis Sorialis was cultured overnight in BHI at 37 °C with shaking (~200 rpm), diluted 1:100 in fresh BHI, and incubated for 4 h to OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.35. Cultures were centrifuged (5000 g, 5 min, 18 °C), and supernatants filtered (0.22 μm). Whole blood (2 mL; Hemostat #50-863-753) was diluted 1:10 in PBS, centrifuged (1000 g, 5 min, 4 °C), and washed 2–3 times until clear. In 96-well plates, 50 μL of 10% washed RBCs and 50 μL of bacterial supernatant were combined; PBS was used to adjust to 100 μL. After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, 40 μL was diluted in 180 μL PBS, centrifuged, and 100 μL of the supernatant was used to measure absorbance at 415 nm. Complete lysis was defined using 1% Triton X-100.

Real-time qPCR

RNA was extracted from mouse tissues, stool, or isolated bacterial strains (E. faecalis Sorialis, E. casseliflavus, or E. gallinarum). 11 , 28 , 49 Bacterial abundance was normalized to the total 16S in stool or the host Gapdh or Tbp gene in liver. The total bacterial load present in feces was quantified by multiplying a particular bacterial abundance by the total amount of DNA (μg) per mg of feces. Sybr Green (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and either ABI StepOnePlus or QuantStudio™ 5 real-time PCR system were used. Primer sequences targeting 16S rRNA and other genes were obtained from published sources 50 and PrimerBank. 51-53 All primers are listed in Table 1.

Cytolysin detection in patients with AH

Cytolysin detection in stool from patients with AH was performed as previously described. 28 , 68 DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN) with mechanical disruption via 0.5 mm zirconium oxide beads (Next Advance) in sterile 2 mL screw-cap tubes. Enterococcus was selectively enriched in Enterococcosel Broth (BD), then plated on blood agar (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and standard agar (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Colonies were homogenized with 4 mm glass beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and individual colonies were analyzed by qPCR for E. faecalis cylL S and cylL L. 21

Biochemical analysis

Plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were determined with the Infinity ALT kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the ALT/GPT liquid kit (Teco Diagnostics/Thermo Fisher Scientific). Hepatic triglyceride levels were measured using the Triglyceride Liquid Reagent kit (Pointe Scientific). Plasma lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels were quantified using the mouse LPS ELISA kit (Lifeome, E13066m-96). Plasma caspase-1 and caspase-11 concentrations were measured using the mouse Caspase-1 ELISA Kit (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-75014) and mouse Caspase-11 ELISA Kit (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-75019), respectively, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Staining procedures

Formalin-fixed liver tissues were embedded in paraffin (Paraplast Plus, McCornick) and sectioned at 5 μm for staining. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E Surgipath) or Sirius Red for fibrosis assessment. Sirius Red staining was performed using 0.1% Direct Red 80 in saturated picric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) to visualize collagen deposition. 69 Fibrosis was quantified by morphometric analysis using NIH ImageJ.

Immunoblot analyses

Protein extracts from liver tissue were prepared as previously described, 70 and expression of cleaved and pro-forms of Caspase-1 (1:1000; Novus Bio, NB100-56565) and Caspase-11 (1:1000; Novus Bio, NB120-10454) was evaluated by immunoblotting. β-Actin (1:5,000; Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a loading control. Immunoblots were imaged with a CCD camera in a luminescent image analyzer (Gel-Doc; Bio-Rad) and analyzed by densitometry using Image Lab v.6.1.0 (Bio-Rad).

Cytolysin expression and purification

Bioactive CylLL″ and CylLS″ peptides were produced using a previously established Escherichia coli expression system, 28 which co-expressed His₆-tagged CylLL or CylLS with CylM to mediate post-translational modification. The recombinant CylA (27–412), a soluble domain of the native peptidase used in cytolysin maturation, was used to remove the His-tag and leader peptide to produce bioactive CylLL″ or CylLS″. The core peptides were then purified by reversed-phase HPLC and confirmed by MALDI-TOF MS.

Primary mouse hepatocytes isolation

Mouse hepatocytes were isolated from C57BL/6 mice following chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding (NIAAA model) or control diet, as previously described. 11 Livers were perfused in situ with calcium-free EGTA buffer (0.5 mM), followed by 0.02% collagenase D (Roche). Digested livers were minced, filtered (70 μm), and hepatocytes were washed and centrifuged at 50 × g for 1 min. Cell viability (~85%) was assessed by Trypan Blue exclusion (Thermo Fisher). Hepatocytes (1.5 × 105) were seeded in collagen I-coated 12-well plates in DMEM-F12 with 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium, 40 ng/mL dexamethasone, 10% FBS (GeminiBio), and antibiotics. After 4 h, the media was replaced with the same formulation lacking FBS. 69 Cells were treated with vehicle, CylLS″ CylLl″, or a combination of CylLS″/CylLL″ at indicated concentrations. The concentrations of CylLS″ and CylLL″ were selected based on doses previously established in our laboratory and in published studies demonstrating cytolysin activity and biological relevance. 28 , 48 , 71 Hepatocyte viability and cytotoxicity assays were then performed.

Hepatocyte viability assay

Cell viability was assessed after 6 h of treatment using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5′-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, as previously described. 72 Hepatocytes (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. Following treatment with vehicle, CylLl″, or CylLS″/CylLL″, at indicated concentrations, 10 μL of MTT solution (0.3 mg mL−1) in DMEM-F12 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS was added per well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Medium was removed, formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 550 nm.

Hepatocyte cytotoxicity

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay was performed to assess cytotoxicity in primary hepatocytes isolated from WT mice fed an oral isocaloric (control) diet and incubated with vehicle, CylLS″ alone, or the combination of CylLS″ and CylLL″ as described above. Where indicated, cells were treated with Caspase-1 inhibitor Z-YVAD-FMK (10 μM; BioVision) or Caspase-11 inhibitor wedelolactone (40 μM; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 3 h. Cytotoxicity was quantified using Pierce LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Human study population

Inpatients with AH from the previously described InTeam Consortium Cohort 28 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02075918), from centers in the USA, Mexico, the United Kingdom, France, and Spain, were analyzed according to active PPI use vs. non-users, n = 39. Inclusion criteria included active alcohol abuse (>50 g/d for men and > 40 g/d for women) in the prior 3 months, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) > ALT, and total bilirubin > 3 mg dl−1 in the past 3 months, liver biopsy and/or clinical picture consistent with AH. Exclusion criteria were autoimmune liver disease (ANA > 1/320), chronic viral hepatitis, hepatocellular carcinoma, complete portal vein thrombosis, extrahepatic terminal disease, pregnancy, and lack of signed informed consent. In all patients, the clinical picture was consistent with AH, and in clinically indicated-patients who underwent liver biopsy, AH was confirmed by histology. Biospecimens were collected during their admission to the hospital. Biospecimens were collected during their admission to the hospital. The median time of specimen collection was 4 d following admission to the hospital (range 0–24, n = 82). The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 2. Patients with AH were analyzed exclusively within the AH cohort, regardless of fibrosis stage or presence of cirrhosis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. unless otherwise specified. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test prior to parametric analyses. For comparisons between two independent groups (e.g., WT vs. Atp4a Sl/Sl mice; −vs + conditioned E. faecalis media; vehicle-treated mice vs. antibiotic-treated), a two-sided unpaired Student's t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. For paired longitudinal comparisons within the same subjects (e.g., pre- vs. post-PPI treatment in control individuals and patients with cirrhosis), paired t-tests were used for normally distributed data, and Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests were used when normality was not met. For comparisons involving more than two groups (e.g., multi-group mouse experiments, cytolysin dose–response experiments, MASLD subgroup analyses), one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test or Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli (BKY) false discovery rate (FDR) post hoc tests were used for normally distributed data. Non-normally distributed multi-group comparisons were analyzed using the Kruskal–Walli's test followed by Dunn's test or BKY FDR-adjusted post hoc comparisons, as appropriate. Two-way ANOVA, for normally distributed data, was used for experiments involving two independent variables (e.g., genotype and treatment in each bacterial category), followed by Tukey's post hoc test or BKY FDR post hoc tests. Categorical variables such as cytolysin positivity rates were analyzed using Chi-square test, while other categorical variables (e.g., gender, death, 30-d mortality, antibiotics, steroids, lactulose use, Child-Pugh class, fibrosis stage, cirrhosis) were compared using Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI, albumin, ALP, ALT, AST, bilirubin, INR, platelets, Fibroscan, MELD–Na) were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests. Independent predictors of 30-d mortality were evaluated using multivariate Cox regression analysis to detect the association of PPI. The multivariate model was adjusted for MELD score. Hazard ratios (HRs) are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). p-values from the multivariate Cox regression were determined by the Wald test. Linear regression analyses were performed to assess associations between microbial taxa abundance and clinical parameters (e.g., AST or ALT levels in MASLD). The significance of the regression slope was assessed using an F-test (or equivalently, a t-test) to determine whether the slope differed significantly from zero. Results are reported as slope, intercept, 95% confidence intervals, R², and p-values. All statistical analyses were performed using R v3.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and GraphPad Prism v10. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Absence of gastric acid alters the composition of intestinal microbiota

We previously determined the role of gastric acid on ethanol-induced liver disease 11 in the Atp4a Sl/Sl mouse model lacking gastric acid (achlorhydria). 35 9 weeks of chronic ethanol feeding led to more severe steatohepatitis compared to WT mice. This was associated with greater intestinal overgrowth and dysbiosis compared to WT littermates. 11

Here, we studied intestinal microbial dynamics. 16S RNA sequencing revealed that in WT mice, 9 weeks of chronic ethanol feeding increased the proportions of Gram-negative taxa, while decreasing the proportion and absolute abundance of Gram-positive at the phylum level compared to control diet-fed mice (Figure 1a and b). In Atp4a Sl/Sl, mice ethanol consumption was associated with a reduction in the absolute abundance of Gram-positive OTUs (Figure 1b). In WT mice, ethanol-mediated expansion of Gram-negative taxa is driven primarily by an increase in Bacteroidetes (Figure 1c and e). Conversely, the reduction of Gram-positive taxa in both WT and Atp4a Sl/Sl mice was explained by a decrease in Firmicutes, a pattern that was also observed by qPCR, although the latter showed only a non-significant trend (Figure 1d and f).

Notably, under ethanol exposure, gastric acid suppression in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice resulted in an increased proportion and absolute abundance of Gram-positive taxa (Figure 1a and b), driven by an expansion of Firmicutes (Figure 1d and f). This was accompanied by a reduction in Gram-negative taxa (Figure 1a and b), attributable to a decrease in Bacteroidetes compared with WT mice (Figure 1c and e).

We further characterized ethanol-induced alterations at the class level within Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes. Ethanol exposure in WT mice increased the abundance of Bacteroidia (Bacteroidetes) and decreased Clostridia and Erysipelotrichia (Firmicutes) compared to control diet-fed mice (Figure 2a and b). In contrast, in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, ethanol selectively reduced Clostridia abundance, while other classes remained largely unchanged (Figure 2b).

Notably, achlorhydria in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice reduced the abundance of Bacteroidia in control diet-fed mice (Figure 2a). Conversely, it promoted expansion of Bacilli in control diet-fed and ethanol-fed mice and Erysipelotrichia in ethanol-fed mice relative to their respective diet-matched WT mice (Figure 2b).

At the genus level, ethanol exposure in WT mice decreased the abundance of Lactobacillus, compared to control-fed WT mice (Figure 2c–e). Within Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, ethanol exposure selectively increased Enterococcus abundance while reducing Lactobacillus within the Gram-positive Firmicutes phylum, compared to control-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, with no significant changes in other genera (Figure 2c–e).

Importantly, gastric acid suppression in ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice promoted a significant reduction in Parasutterella and Helicobacter (Proteobacteria), along with decreases in Parabacteroides and Bacteroides, and a trend toward reduced Prevotella (Bacteroidetes) compared to ethanol-fed WT mice (Figure 2c and d). In contrast, gastric acid suppression in ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice led to a significant expansion of Aerococcus, Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus compared to ethanol-fed WT mice (Figure 2e).

The expansion of intestinal Enterococcus in ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice was previously confirmed. 11 Despite similar intestinal permeability and endotoxin levels, these mice exhibited significantly higher translocation of live Enterococcus to the liver, suggesting a role in the progression of ethanol-induced liver disease. 11 Consistent with these observations, Tlr2 mRNA expression was elevated in ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl compared to WT mice (Figure 2f). These results indicate that in the absence of gastric acid, ethanol exposure promotes a specific expansion of the intestinal Gram-positive Firmicutes phylum, characterized by increased Aerococcus, Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus genera.

Progression of ethanol-induced liver disease by Enterococcus is specific to cytolytic E. faecalis

We previously demonstrated that intestinal overgrowth of E. faecalis strain derived from Atp4a Sl/Sl mice was sufficient to exacerbate ethanol-induced liver disease, using a model in which C57BL/6 mice were repeatedly gavaged with this strain. 11 E. faecalis Sorialis was selected because it was among the most abundant Enterococcus species identified in ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, as determined by 16S rRNA sequencing. 11 In the present study, we examined the contribution of another highly abundant Enterococcus species in these mice, E. casseliflavus. This bacterium was isolated from fecal samples of ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice and was gavaged with either live or heat-killed (dead) bacteria to C57BL/6 mice every third day, following the 9-week Lieber-DeCarli ethanol-induced liver disease model. In contrast to our findings with E. faecalis, increased intestinal colonization with E. casseliflavus did not exacerbate ethanol-induced liver injury, despite comparable ethanol intake in mice gavaged with dead bacteria and even higher intake in those receiving live E. casseliflavus (Figure S1a). Gavage with E. casseliflavus resulted in similar liver-to-body weight ratios (Figure S1b–d) but significantly lower plasma ALT levels (Figure S1e), indicating reduced hepatocellular injury.

We then studied the expression of cytolysin and its functionality in E. faecalis Sorialis. This strain harbors virulence genes in its gDNA and expresses transcripts of the cytolysin operon (cylL L , cylL S , cylL M ) and gelE, encoding gelatinase, and the esp gene, encoding the enterococcal surface protein Esp. On the contrary, E. casseliflavus or E. gallinarum (also derived from Atp4a Sl/Sl mice) only express the esp gene, with esp transcripts detected only in the latter (Figure 3a–­j). The genetic presence of cytolysin (cylL s ) was confirmed in fecal samples from ethanol-fed Atp4a Sl/Sl mice and mice gavaged with E. faecalis Sorialis (Figure 3k and l). We also confirmed cytolysin presence (cylL s ) in our previously established pharmacological model (Figure 3m), in which WT mice were treated with omeprazole (a PPI) during the 9-week Lieber-DeCarli ethanol-induced liver disease protocol. 11 In this model, consistent with the genetic model, mice developed more severe ethanol-induced liver injury, accompanied by increased fecal Enterococcus abundance and enhanced bacterial translocation to the liver. 11 The presence of cylLs was comparable across both the genetic and pharmacological models of achlorhydria, as well as in WT mice gavaged with E. faecalis Sorialis following ethanol administration (Figure 3m). The functionality of cytolysin in E. faecalis Sorialis was confirmed by its ability to lyse red blood cells in both liquid cultures and on blood agar plates (Figure 3n and o).

Non-absorbable antibiotics targeting gastric acid suppression-induced Gram-positive dysbiosis worsen alcohol-related steatohepatitis

We investigated whether non-absorbable antibiotics targeting Gram-positive bacteria involved in acid suppression-associated dysbiosis influence ALD. To this end, Atp4a Sl/Sl littermates were gavaged daily with either vehicle or a combination of vancomycin and bacitracin (antibiotics primarily targeting Gram-positive bacteria), 39-42 during the last 4 weeks of a 9-week Lieber-DeCarli chronic ethanol feeding model. As previously described, Atp4a Sl/Sl mice were more susceptible to ethanol-induced liver injury than WT littermates, 11 despite comparable food intake and ethanol metabolism (Figure S2a–h; Figure 4c). This heightened susceptibility was associated with increased intestinal bacterial load in stool and enhanced bacterial translocation to the liver 11 (Figure S2j and k). Interestingly, treatment with non-absorbable antibiotics further exacerbated ethanol-induced steatohepatitis in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, as evidenced by elevated plasma transaminase levels (Figure 4a), increased steatosis (Figure 4b and c), and upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory genes (Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Tnfa, Ccl5) (Figure 4d) and the profibrotic gene Tgfb1 (Figure 4e), while Sirius red staining showed trends toward increased collagen deposition (Figure S2e and f).

Paradoxically, despite targeting Gram-positive bacteria, antibiotic treatment led to increased hepatic Tlr2 expression (Figure 2f) and levels of secretory IgA (sIgA) in stool (Figure S2i), suggesting enhanced recognition of pathogen-associated patterns (PAMPs) in the liver, 74 and ongoing intestinal inflammation or pathogenic colonization. 75-80 These findings prompted us to investigate the dynamics of intestinal microbial composition.

Overall, bacterial overgrowth associated with gastric acid suppression was reduced in the stool of antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl (Figure 4g). Although antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl had a decline in relative abundance of Gram-positive A. viridans, Enterococcus, and Lactobacillus compared to vehicle-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl , an unexpected increase in the relative abundance of Gram-positive Streptococcus was found (Figure 4g). Additionally, we observed a relative expansion of Gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia coli), Parasutterella, and Helicobacter within the Proteobacteria, whereas Bacteroidetes were markedly depleted (Figure 4g).

When considering the absolute abundance of individual taxa, calculated as the product of bacterial quantities and the total DNA yield per fecal material, the overrepresentation of the mentioned Gram-positive genera within the Firmicutes was pronounced in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice (Figure S2j). Following antibiotic treatment, total bacterial load returned to WT levels, and so did these Gram-positive taxa, including Streptococcus, in terms of absolute abundance (Figure S2j). In contrast, within Proteobacteria, the absolute abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, including E. coli, remained elevated, Parasutterella levels remained unchanged, while Helicobacter showed a notable increase in antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl mice (Figure S2j). Interestingly, Bacteroidetes were markedly depleted (Figure S2j).

Intriguingly, antibiotic treatment to Atp4a Sl/Sl mice further increased bacterial translocation to the liver, exceeding the already elevated levels observed in untreated Atp4a Sl/Sl mice (Figure 4h; Figure S2k), suggesting a compromised gut barrier function. Indeed, the relative abundance of the studied taxa within the Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes was elevated upon antibiotic treatment (Figure 4h). Consistent with enhanced systemic exposure to bacterial products and increased translocation along the gut–liver axis, circulating LPS levels were also elevated in antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl mice (Figure 4i). Moreover, we found that cytolysin subunit cylL L was significantly more abundant in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice treated with antibiotics (Figure 4j), suggesting an enhanced cytolysin activity. 81

Pore-forming toxins are known to activate the inflammasome and Caspase-1/11 pathways. 82 , 83 We analyzed Caspase-1 and Caspase-11 processing in liver tissue. In liver samples, Atp4a Sl/Sl mice treated with non-absorbable antibiotics exhibited a significant increase in the ratios of cleaved Caspase-1 to pro–Caspase-1 and cleaved Caspase-11 to pro–Caspase-11 compared with Atp4a Sl/Sl mice without antibiotic treatment (Figure 4k). In contrast, plasma levels of Caspase-1 and Caspase-11 did not differ significantly between groups (Figure S4l and m). This observation is consistent with previous reports indicating that inflammasome activation and Caspase cleavage are predominantly tissue-localized events rather than reflected in the circulation. 84

Finally, we assessed the dynamics of intestinal fungal composition. Absence of gastric acid did not significantly alter the overall abundance of intestinal fungi or Candida albicans, but antibiotic treatment led to a reduction in total fungal abundance and C. albicans levels in the stool of antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/Sl mice (Figure S3a and b). Plasma levels of 1,3-β-D-glucan (structural component of the cell walls of fungi) and anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibodies (ASCA)-IgG remained unchanged, indicating that gastric acid suppression does not promote systemic fungal translocation (Figure S3a–d).

Despite reducing certain Gram-positive taxa, treatment with non-absorbable antibiotics resulted in enhanced bacterial translocation to the liver and exacerbated ethanol-induced liver injury in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice.

PPI use increases 30-d mortality risk in patients with AH, accompanied by Enterococcus overgrowth

We next examined the dynamics of intestinal microbial composition associated with PPI use in patients with AH. 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on fecal samples from patients with AH (n = 44), stratified by PPI use (PPI users; non-users, n = 39), from the multicenter InTeam cohort. The two groups' clinical characteristics and demographics are described in Table 2. Consistent with findings from the preclinical mouse model, gastric acid suppression in AH patients was associated with an increase in Enterococcus, cytolysin, and Streptococcus proportions, although Lactobacillus levels remained unchanged (Figure 5a–d). A downward trend in Bacteroides and Parabacteroides was observed (Figure 5e and f). Interestingly, PPI use in patients with AH was also associated with an increased abundance of genera historically linked to opportunistic infections, including Pseudomonas, Abiotrophia, Achromobacter, Acinetobacter, Aeromonas, Capnocytophaga, and Catonella 85-91 (Figure 5h). Antibiotic treatment in patients with AH using PPIs reduced the proportions of Enterococcus, cytolysin, Streptococcus, and the aforementioned opportunistic pathogens to control levels. However, a trend towards increased Escherichia-Shigella was noted (Figure 5a-b, d, g and h). Importantly, PPI use was associated with higher 30-d mortality in patients with AH (Figure 5i). Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by PPI use showed significantly lower 30-d survival in PPI-treated patients (log-rank p = 0.0047) (Figure 5j). Notably, in the multivariate Cox model including MELD and PPI use, PPI use remained independently associated with 30-d mortality (HR = 9.15, 95% CI = 1.07–78.32 p = 0.043) (Figure 5k).

We extended our analysis to a second, pre-existing longitudinal cohort 27 that included control individuals (n = 12–14) and patients with compensated cirrhosis (n = 10–12), assessed before and after PPI use, to examine changes in fecal microbial composition. 16S rRNA gene sequencing analyses revealed that PPI use was associated with a reduction of Gram-negative taxa and an increased proportion of Gram-positive taxa at the phylum level in control individuals (Figure S4a and b). This shift was primarily driven by a decrease in Bacteroidetes (Figure S4c) and an expansion of Firmicutes (Figure S4d). Notably, Enterococcus levels in control individuals increased following gastric acid suppression. 11 In contrast to healthy control individuals, patients with cirrhosis had a distinct microbial composition, characterized by an initial overrepresentation of Gram-positive taxa (Figure S4a and b, d). PPI treatment in these patients led to an increase in Gram-negative taxa and a reduction in Gram-positive taxa (Figure S4a–d), with no significant changes in Enterococcus (Figure S4e). In this cohort, consistently, PPI treatment did not modulate fungal abundance in control subjects (Figure S4f). These findings support the observation that gastric acid suppression does not affect fibrosis progression in the CCl₄-induced liver fibrosis model. Atp4a Sl/Sl mice showed no significant differences in liver weight-to-body weight ratio (Figure S5a), hepatic Col1a1 expression (Figure S5b), or collagen deposition compared to WT mice (Figure S5c and d).

We extended our analysis to a third, previously established cohort of patients with MASLD, 73 stratifying individuals into control subjects (n = 19) and patients with MASLD, further categorized based on PPI use (PPI users, n = 13; non-users, n = 83) (Table 3). 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed that PPI use in patients with MASLD was not associated with significant changes in the proportions of Gram-positive or Gram-negative taxa at the phylum level. This is likely attributable to a pre-existing microbial imbalance in MASLD, characterized by increased abundance of Gram-positive phyla (Firmicutes and Actinobacteria) and decreased abundance of the Gram-negative phylum Bacteroidetes. Furthermore, in PPI-treated MASLD patients, this dysbiosis was further exacerbated by a reduction in Bacteroidetes and an increase in Proteobacteria (Figure S6a–c). At the genus level, patients with MASLD exhibited an expansion of Lactobacillus and Streptococcus, with a trend towards increased Enterococcus, compared to controls. In PPI-treated MASLD patients, only Streptococcus was significantly expanded (Figure S6d–f), together with increased abundance of the Enterobacteriaceae family and Escherichia genus (Figure S6g and h). Additional genera expanded in PPI-treated MASLD patients included Coprobacter, Leuconostoc, Lactococcus, Clostridium hiranonis, Acetivibrio, Bulleidia, Faecalitalea, Mitsuokella, Veillonella, Leclercia, Salmonella, and Serratia (Figure S6i). Notably, PPI-treated MASLD patients showed significantly higher fibrosis stages, increased liver stiffness, and elevated serum AST levels compared to MASLD patients without PPI treatment, indicating more advanced liver injury (Figure S6j–l). Interestingly, Streptococcus proportions positively correlated with serum AST levels, whereas only a trend was observed between Enterobacteriaceae abundance and AST (Figure S6m and n).

The cirrhosis, AH, and MASLD cohorts were analyzed as independent, non-overlapping patient populations. These findings highlight context-dependent microbiome responses to gastric acid suppression across liver disease etiologies.

Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine and caspase-1/11 inhibitors reduced the cytotoxic effect of E. faecalis cytolysin

Previous work from our group demonstrated that the combined presence of recombinant large (CylLL″) and small (CylLS″) subunits of Enterococcus faecalis cytolysin induces cell death in primary mouse hepatocytes. 28 To explore potential therapeutic strategies to mitigate the hepatotoxicity of cytolysin, we isolated primary hepatocytes from mice subjected to either an ethanol or control diet, following the chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding paradigm (NIAAA model). We confirmed the hepatotoxic effects of purified, bioactive cytolysin peptides (CylLL″ and CylLS″) in this model 48 , 92 (Figure 6a). Cytolysin becomes functionally active only upon co-assembly of both subunits, resulting in overt hepatocyte lysis due to pore formation, 48 , 92 , 93 whereas CylLL″ alone does not form pores or induce cytotoxicity or hemolysis. 28 , 48 After 3 h of exposure, hepatocytes exhibited pronounced cytopathic changes indicative of pore-mediated cell death. Specifically, cells displayed marked swelling, loss of membrane integrity, and features of lytic rupture. These morphological characteristics are consistent with a pyroptosis-like form of cell death—an inflammatory, pore-dependent mechanism of programmed cell death characterized by membrane permeabilization, cellular swelling, and eventual lysis (Figure 6a). To confirm the hepatotoxic effect of cytolysin, here, we assessed hepatocyte survival. Incubation of primary mouse hepatocytes from ethanol- or control-fed mice with both cytolysin subunits resulted in a parallel dose-dependent decrease in cell viability (Figure 6b and c).

Given the observed pyroptosis-like features and the established role of Caspase-1 and Caspase-11 in mediating inflammatory cell death, we explored potential therapeutic strategies by testing selective Caspase-1 (Z-YVAD-FMK) and Caspase-11 (wedelolactone) inhibitors in vitro. Treatment with these inhibitors significantly reduced cytolysin-induced cytotoxicity in primary hepatocytes (Figure 6d). In parallel, based on previous findings that erythrocytes with higher membrane phosphatidylcholine content are more susceptible to cytolysin-mediated lysis and that exogenous phosphatidylcholine can inhibit cytolysin-mediated erythrocyte lysis, 93-95 we examined whether dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC)— a phospholipid consisting of two C16:0 palmitic acid chains esterified to a phosphatidylcholine backbone —could modulate cytolysin activity. These observations suggest that DPPC may function as a lipid “decoy”, sequestering cytolysin and thereby reducing its interaction with target membranes and subsequent pore formation. 93 , 95 , 96 DPPC was therefore used as a mechanistic probe to neutralize cytolysin. Using an RBC hemolysis assay, we found that DPPC partially reduced the hemolytic effect of cytolysin, suggesting a potential membrane-competitive interaction with the toxin (Figure 6e).

To validate the translational relevance of cytolysin-targeted interventions, we extended our in vitro findings to an in vivo chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding model (NIAAA). Female C57BL/6 mice were pretreated for one week with non-absorbable antibiotics (polymyxin B, 150 mg kg−1, and neomycin, 200 mg kg−1, orally once daily for a week) to deplete the microbiota and facilitate standardized Enterococcus colonization. Mice were then gavaged every third day with 5 × 109 CFU of cytolysin-positive E. faecalis Sorialis or vehicle control, while receiving ethanol feeding. To block the cytolysin mechanism of action, targeted interventions were tested by administering either the irreversible Caspase-1 inhibitor Z-YVAD-FMK (10 mg kg−1, intraperitoneally every third day) or DPPC (100 mg kg−1, intraperitoneally every third day, with additional daily dietary supplementation at the same dose). This design enabled direct assessment of the protective effects of cytolysin neutralization and Caspase-1 inhibition on ethanol-induced liver injury under controlled bacterial colonization conditions. In response to the chronic-plus-binge ethanol diet, mice gavaged with cytolysin-positive E. faecalis Sorialis showed increased plasma ALT levels and inflammatory markers in the liver, including Cxcl2, compared with vehicle-gavaged WT mice, indicating exacerbated liver injury (Figure 6f and h). Pharmacological inhibition of Caspase-1 resulted in a significant reduction in plasma ALT levels compared with mice receiving E. faecalis Sorialis (Figure 6f). DPPC treatment exerted a broader protective effect, reducing plasma ALT levels, hepatic triglyceride content, and inflammatory markers, including Cxcl2, compared with mice receiving E. faecalis Sorialis (Figures S6f–h and S7a). Furthermore, no significant differences in markers of hepatic fibrosis were observed among groups, suggesting that neither Caspase-1 inhibition nor DPPC treatment affected collagen deposition or fibrogenesis (Figure S7b). Notably, ethanol metabolism was comparable across all groups (Figure S7c).

Our approaches target cytolysin-mediated inflammation by inhibiting Caspase-1 or neutralizing cytolysin with DPPC as a lipid “decoy,” binding the toxin to block its interaction with host membranes, impeding pore formation and cytolysis, without anticipating overall intestinal E. faecalis abundance. 93 , 95 , 96 As expected, all groups of ethanol-fed mice gavaged with cytolysin-positive E. faecalis Sorialis exhibited increased fecal Enterococcus abundance compared with ethanol-fed mice gavaged with vehicle, regardless of DPPC or Caspase-1 treatments (Figure S7d). Interestingly, fecal levels of the cytolysin subunit cylL L mirrored Enterococcus abundance: E. faecalis Sorialis gavaged mice had increased fecal cytolysin subunit cylL L , as anticipated, treatment with Caspase-1 inhibition maintained elevated levels of cylL L , while DPPC treatment failed to reduce cylL L, levels, reflecting an ongoing production and local flux of cytolysin by E. faecalis Sorialis (Figure S7e). Consistently, relative to ethanol-fed mice gavaged with E. faecalis Sorialis alone, hepatic cylL L levels were reduced in DPPC-treated mice, reaching levels comparable to those observed in ethanol-fed mice gavaged with vehicle, suggesting that systemic cylL L is efficiently sequestered by DPPC, thereby limiting its activity. In contrast, Caspase-1 inhibition had no significant effect on hepatic cylL L abundance (Figure 6i). This suggests that while Caspase-1 inhibition protects hepatocytes by blocking downstream signaling, it does not reduce hepatic cytolysin levels and therefore provides only minimal protection against hepatocellular damage. In contrast, DPPC directly sequesters hepatic cytolysin, preventing its interaction with host cell membranes and thereby providing broader protection against hepatocellular injury, steatosis, and inflammation.

Discussion

Alterations in gut microbiota dynamics contribute to liver disease pathogenesis. 6-10 Building upon our previous breakthrough showing that gastric acid suppression promotes Enterococcus overgrowth and liver injury, 11 the present study substantially extends these findings by integrating clinical cohorts across multiple liver disease etiologies, uncovering unexpected consequences of microbiota-targeted therapies, and identifying actionable therapeutic strategies. By examining healthy controls, patients with AH, compensated cirrhosis, and MASLD, we demonstrate that PPI–associated microbial shifts are disease-specific and not uniformly driven by Enterococcus expansion. Moreover, we reveal that non-absorbable antibiotics paradoxically exacerbate ethanol-induced liver injury in gastric acid–suppressed mice, highlighting the risks of indiscriminate gut microbial depletion in this context. These unexpected effects include enhanced bacterial translocation to the liver, increased systemic exposure to bacterial products, and amplification of exposure to cytolysin in the liver despite reduced intestinal bacterial burden, underscoring the importance of microbial dynamics in different organs. Finally, we identify cytolysin as a direct driver of hepatocyte injury and introduce preclinical in vivo strategies to neutralize its activity, including Caspase-1 inhibition to block downstream activation and targeted toxin-neutralization to prevent pore formation in host membranes, such as with DPPC. Hence, these efforts shift the therapeutic paradigm from broad microbiota suppression to precision toxinomics strategies tailored to toxin neutralization and blocking of the mechanism of action.

Our study identifies gastric acid suppression as a key modulator of host–microbiome dynamics, driving an expansion of Gram-positive taxa, most notably Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Aerococcus, in gastric acid-suppressed mice under ethanol exposure. These findings align with our prior work in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, where the absence of gastric acid promotes the overgrowth and translocation of Enterococcus to the liver and demonstrated that E. faecalis Sorialis, isolated from Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, contributes to liver injury through TLR2-mediated inflammation and hepatocyte cytotoxicity. 11 , 28

We identified cytolysin, gelatinase, and enterococcal surface protein genes as E. faecalis Sorialis virulence factors. Gelatinase E is a secreted metalloprotease that degrades host extracellular matrix components and contributes to biofilm formation and tissue invasion, 97 while enterococcal surface protein F facilitates bacterial adherence to host tissues and promotes colonization and persistence. 98 Importantly, our previous clinical findings demonstrated that cytolysin, but not other enterococcal virulence factors, predicts mortality in AH patients, reinforcing cytolysin's role as a key determinant of pathogenicity. 28 Furthermore, suppression of cytolytic E. faecalis with targeted bacteriophage or IgY antibody therapy prevents ethanol-induced liver injury. 28 , 48 In contrast, cytolysin-negative E. casseliflavus isolated from Atp4a Sl/Sl mice, did not exacerbate hepatocellular injury. Additionally, we confirmed that intestinal cytolysin-positive E. faecalis expands in both Atp4a Sl/Sl mice and PPI-treated animals under ethanol exposure. We now show that antibiotic treatment is associated with increased expression of the cytolysin subunit cylL L in vivo. Enterococci use CylLL″ to actively probe the environment for target cells and, upon target recognition, upregulate cytolysin expression. 81 In the liver, target cells are copious; thus, elevated cylL L suggest an enhanced cytolysin activity and enhanced liver injury.

In control individuals, PPI use was also associated with a microbiome shift favoring Gram-positive taxa, with Enterococcus expansion. 11 Similarly, in patients with AH, PPI use promoted Enterococcus and Streptococcus expansion.

Notably, PPI use in patients with AH was associated with significantly increased 30-d mortality and reduced survival probability. Importantly, this association remained significant after adjusting for disease severity. However, the number of PPI-treated patients and mortality events was limited, which may have reduced statistical power and increased the risk of model overfitting, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals of the hazard ratio estimates. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than definitive. Expansion of Enterococcus and Streptococcus under PPI treatment could exacerbate hepatocyte injury through toxin-mediated mechanisms, including cytolysin production, thereby directly contributing to adverse outcomes. Although these observations suggest a possible association between PPI-associated microbiome alterations and mortality, larger prospective studies will be required to confirm this relationship. These findings suggest a previously underappreciated potential iatrogenic risk of PPI therapy. Limitations of this finding include the small number of AH patients documented as receiving PPIs, as well as the retrospective nature of the analysis. These results highlight the need for prospective studies to validate these associations. These findings are consistent with our previous observations, where detection of cytolysin in fecal samples correlated with worse outcomes and a higher mortality risk. 11 , 28

Patients with compensated cirrhosis exhibited a distinct response to PPI therapy: their baseline microbiome was already enriched in Gram-positive bacteria (81% vs. 58% in controls), and PPI use led to a reduction in Gram-positive abundance without affecting Enterococcus levels. Importantly, cirrhosis is not associated with cytolysin positivity or increased fecal E. faecalis abundance in patients with AH. 11 , 28 PPI use in patients with cirrhosis has been linked to an elevated risk of infections, 23 gut microbiota alterations—particularly increased Streptococcaceae 27 , higher 30- and 90-d hospital readmission rates, 17 and enrichment of Streptococcus phages in the gut virome, 99 but similar to our findings, no change in fungal dynamics. 100 Additionally, PPIs have been associated with hepatic encephalopathy, prolonged hospital stays, and increased ICU admissions. 101 However, these associations should be interpreted with caution, as PPIs are often initiated prophylactically in ICU settings, especially in patients with liver dysfunction, to prevent stress ulcer bleeding or are started without indication in predominantly decompensated patients, 102 although all patients studied here were compensated. 27 While some studies report a correlation between PPI use and increased liver disease severity or mortality in patients with cirrhosis, others do not. 18 In our murine model, gastric acid suppression did not exacerbate fibrosis in CCl4-treated mice. Although microbiome dynamics were not assessed in this model, CCl4 administration itself has been shown to induce specific microbiome alterations, even when delivered intraperitoneally. 103 These findings suggest that PPI-associated risks in patients with cirrhosis are unlikely to be mediated through cytolysin-dependent mechanisms.

Our data shows that in patients with MASLD, PPI use was not associated with a shift toward Gram-positive taxa or significant expansion of Enterococcus, suggesting disease-specific microbial dynamics potentially influenced by host factors or dietary context. Indeed, patients with MASLD had pre-existing microbial imbalance. Although Enterococcus expansion was observed in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) and in those on a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined (CDAA) diet—models of MASLD and MASH, respectively—clinical findings are consistent with previous clinical reports showing that neither E. faecalis nor cytolysin is elevated in MASLD or predictive of disease severity, 73 indicating species-specific microbial dynamics. In contrast, PPI-treated MASLD patients showed Streptococcus expansion along with significantly higher fibrosis stages, liver stiffness, and serum AST levels compared to PPI-untreated patients with MASLD. These features indicate more advanced liver injury and reflect a profile similar to that observed in cirrhosis. Intriguingly, Streptococcus abundance correlated positively with AST, suggesting a role in disease progression. Although the linear regression revealed a statistically significant association (p = 0.013), the model explained only 5.5% of the variance (R² = 0.055), indicating a weak relationship. This suggests that other factors, such as diet, genetics, epigenetics, among others, likely contribute to the observed variability. Microbiome responses to PPIs appear context-dependent across liver disease etiologies. Another caveat is confounding by indication: patients with more advanced disease are more likely to receive PPIs in an effort to prevent upper gastrointestinal bleeding, limiting causal inference. Therefore, although our study in healthy controls and patients with cirrhosis was prospective, there is a need for prospective studies in patients with AUD, AH, and MASLD, before and after PPI treatment, to better clarify these associations. It is worth noting that the experimental design in animal models differs from clinical practice, in that long-term antibiotic use, especially for SBP prophylaxis in patients with cirrhosis, typically targets Gram-negative organisms, 104 whereas the combination of interventions used here primarily targets Gram-positive bacteria. While this controlled setting provides valuable mechanistic insights, additional studies incorporating antibiotic regimens that target Gram-negative organisms are also needed to better capture the complexity of clinical treatments. This will be important for appropriately extrapolating findings to clinical contexts.

Importantly, treatment with non-absorbable antibiotics targeting Gram-positive taxa markedly exacerbated ethanol-induced liver pathology in Atp4a Sl/Sl mice. This was accompanied by an increased intestinal abundance of Streptococcus species and Gram-negative bacteria, including Enterobacteriaceae and Helicobacter, resulting in intestinal inflammation, enhanced bacterial translocation to the liver, and elevated circulating LPS levels, indicating enhanced systemic exposure to bacterial products and compromised gut–liver axis integrity. Notably, antibiotic-treated Atp4a Sl/S exhibited increased hepatic abundance of Enterococcus and cylL L, indicative of heightened cytolysin activity, alongside other microbial taxa. This microbial and toxin profile coincided with enhanced hepatic cleavage of Caspase-1 and Caspase-11, while circulating Caspase levels remained unchanged. These results indicate that selective depletion of the intestinal Gram-positive microbiota, particularly under gastric acid-suppressive conditions, disrupts gut microbial homeostasis, promotes bacterial translocation, and aggravates hepatic injury. This is consistent with clinical data demonstrating that long-term antibiotic and PPI use in cirrhosis patients increases the risk of infections at multiple sites, 105 significantly raises the incidence and recurrence of Clostridioides difficile infection, 106 and facilitates the spread of antibiotic resistance genes, promoting colonization by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae. 107

Collectively, these findings highlight a critical unmet clinical need for targeted strategies that selectively eliminate cytolysin-positive E. faecalis, such as bacteriophage therapy or neutralizing cytolysin-specific IgY antibodies. 21 , 52 Such approaches offer the advantage of preserving overall microbiota integrity while effectively neutralizing hepatotoxic cytolysin-positive E. faecalis. Mechanistically, cytolysin induces hepatocyte injury through membrane pore formation, presumably triggering pyroptosis-like cell death. We demonstrated that cytolytic activity is functional and contributes to hepatocyte injury via pore formation and a presumably pyroptosis-like cell death mechanism.

Accordingly, we explored therapeutic strategies targeting cytolysin-mediated hepatocellular damage. In vitro, selective inhibition of Caspase-1 (the effector protease of the canonical inflammasome pathway, which is known to be primed by TLR2 and pore-forming toxins 108 and Caspase-11 (effector of the noncanonical inflammasome pathway, which can be activated by increased epithelial barrier damage and endotoxin exposure 109 ) reduced hepatocyte death, implicating inflammatory cell death pathways in cytolysin-induced injury. However, the precise mechanism of cytolysin-mediated cell death warrants further investigation. In parallel, we explored DPPC as a lipid-based approach capable of partially neutralizing cytolysin's pore-forming activity, likely by acting as a membrane-competitive decoy. DPPC partially neutralized cytolysin hemolytic activity in vitro. Our in vivo analyses reveal a clear distinction between blocking Caspase-1 and directly neutralizing the toxin itself. Inhibition of Caspase-1 dampened inflammasome-driven hepatocellular damage, yet Enterococcus and cytolysin persisted in the liver, leaving hepatosteatosis and inflammation unresolved. By contrast, DPPC conferred broad protection, reducing hepatocellular damage, lipid accumulation, and inflammatory responses. These findings suggest that DPPC effectively neutralizes cytolysin in vivo, consistent with its proposed lipid “decoy” mechanism, 94 , 95 diverting cytolysin away from target membranes, thereby disarming its pore-forming capacity in vivo. Taken together, the data suggest that a dual approach, combining direct toxin neutralization with suppression of its downstream inflammatory signaling, may offer a comprehensive strategy to counteract microbial toxin–driven liver injury in ethanol-associated liver disease. A limitation of this study is the absence of a cell-type–specific Caspase-1 inhibitor, preventing definitive identification of the responsible hepatic cell population. Kupffer cells are the principal expressers of Caspase-1 and inflammasome components, with contributions from infiltrating monocytes and hepatic stellate cells. 110 , 111 While cytolysin exerts cytolytic effects on hepatocytes, it may non-selectively target multiple liver cell types, and immune–hepatocyte crosstalk likely amplifies hepatocellular injury. Caspase-1 inhibition mitigated hepatocellular injury in our model; however, future studies should employ cell-type–specific approaches and delineate the relative contributions of Enterococcus–TLR2–Caspase-1 signaling versus cytolysin–Caspase-1–mediated injury to fully understand hepatocellular pathogenesis.

Together, our findings establish inhibition of gastric acid secretion as a critical factor promoting the expansion of cytolytic Enterococcus faecalis and confirm cytolysin as a key driver of Enterococcus-mediated ethanol-induced liver injury in this context. We demonstrate that antibiotics and PPIs, commonly co-prescribed in clinical practice, can synergistically disrupt microbial homeostasis, exacerbate intestinal inflammation, and promote bacterial translocation, thereby accelerating liver disease progression. The prevalence of concomitant use of PPIs and antibiotics warrants careful evaluation, particularly in patients at risk for ALD. These insights underscore the need for more judicious use of acid-suppressing therapies in vulnerable populations and support the development of targeted interventions, such as strategies against cytolysin, to prevent disease progression. However, the observed association between PPI use and mortality in the AH cohort should be interpreted cautiously and requires validation in larger prospective studies.
