Type 2 Lepra Reaction Following Antituberculosis Treatment Initiation in an Elderly Male With Coexisting Hansen's Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Cary Amiel G Villanueva, Ysabel Regina H Ortiz, Winlove P Mojica, Albert B Albay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculosis and leprosy coinfection is increasingly rare even in endemic areas. We report a 70‐year‐old male presenting with fever and malaise. Pulmonary tuberculosis was suggested by chest computed tomography scan and confirmed on polymerase chain reaction testing of bronchial wash specimens. Skin punch biopsies were consistent with lepromatous Hansen's disease. After initiating isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, he developed new erythematous patches and plaques on the lower extremities and required supplemental oxygen. Erythema nodosum leprosum was diagnosed. Intravenous hydrocortisone, dapsone and clofazimine were started, and clinical improvement and decreased inflammatory markers followed. The patient was discharged with a 20‐week course of prednisone and multidrug treatment for drug‐susceptible pulmonary tuberculosis and leprosy. Concomitant Hansen's disease should be considered when treating patients for tuberculosis especially in areas where both are prevalent as different long‐term multidrug regimens are required.Keywords: case report, leprosy, mycobacterium infections, tuberculosis

Introduction

Tuberculosis and leprosy are chronic communicable diseases caused by pathogenic mycobacterial species and are both endemic in Southeast Asia. Despite coinfection becoming increasingly rare, prompt diagnosis is vital since different long‐term multidrug regimens are required.

Case Report

A 70‐year‐old Filipino male, nonsmoker, presented with pruritic skin lesions on the trunk and extremities, which were not triggered by sun exposure nor changes in temperature. No new products were applied nor new medications taken prior to the onset. The patient was evaluated by two dermatologists over a year and prescribed antihistamines and topical corticosteroids.

One week prior to admission, he developed exertional dyspnea on regular activity, malaise, and undocumented fever with non‐tender, non‐pruritic, non‐blanching, reddish purple patches and plaques on the forearms and knees. Neither hypoesthesia nor hypohidrosis was noted. He consulted a pulmonologist, was prescribed oral levofloxacin, and advised admission for further work‐up.

The patient received treatment for tuberculosis four decades earlier, had no history of asthma, and was not cognizant of exposure to Hansen's disease. He had a recent history of fall with a left‐sided pneumohydrothorax on chest computed tomography (CT) scan. He resided in a boarding house above a printing shop he operated.

On admission, decreased breath sounds and vocal fremitus were appreciated over the right lower chest. There were multiple, well‐defined, irregularly shaped, blanching erythematous patches and plaques observed on the upper extremities, trunk, buttocks and thighs (Figure 1A). Non‐blanching reddish‐purple macules, patches, and plaques on the upper and lower extremities (Figure 1B) were also noted. Baseline laboratory examinations revealed normocytic, normochromic anaemia (haemoglobin 86 g/L, normal range 135–180 g/L). White blood cell count (WBC 6.80 × 109/L, normal range 4.5–11 × 109/L) and serum creatinine (72 μmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate 95 mL/min) were normal. Nasopharyngeal swab was negative for SARS‐CoV‐2 via polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

(A) Multiple erythematous patches and plaques were noted on the bilateral upper extremities and trunk. (B) Non‐blanching reddish‐purple macules and patches, some coalescing into plaques, were also noted on the upper and lower extremities.

Chest CT scan findings including a tree‐in‐bud pattern and irregular apicopleural thickening bilaterally suggested pulmonary tuberculosis (Figure 2). Bronchial wash specimens demonstrated acid‐fast bacilli on smear microscopy (+8) and tested positive on PCR for Mycobacterium tuberculosis without isoniazid or rifampicin resistance. Endobronchial biopsy yielded benign bronchial tissue with focal chronic inflammation.

(A) Unenhanced cross‐sectional CT scan images of the chest demonstrated irregular pleural thickening at the apices (arrowheads), (B) cylindrical bronchiectasis (arrowhead) and tree‐in‐bud pattern (asterisk) in the left upper lobe, and (C) mass‐like consolidation with ground glass opacities in the right lower lobe (asterisk).

Isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol were initiated, and on the second day, the patient required supplemental oxygen, remained febrile (maximal temperature 39°C), and developed new erythematous nodules and plaques on the lower extremities, as well as bipedal edema. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, 114 mm/h, 8× upper limit of normal) and C‐reactive protein level (CRP, 128 mg/dL, 13× upper limit of normal) were elevated. A paradoxical reaction was considered, and intravenous hydrocortisone (200 mg/day) was started.

Two 4‐mm punch biopsies were obtained from the right upper arm and abdomen. Histopathology showed nodular aggregates of foamy histiocytes in the papillary and reticular dermis, surrounding blood vessels, adnexal structures and nerve twigs (Figure 3A) Focal nodular collection of neutrophils in the papillary dermis, likely a reactional change, were also noted. Fite Faraco stain of both specimens showed numerous acid‐fast bacilli in globi formation in the dermis and subcutaneous fat, consistent with Hansen's disease, lepromatous (Figure 3B). A slit skin smear indicated multibacillary leprosy (mean bacterial index 1.33). These along with new‐onset erythematous plaques, fever, and bipedal edema led to a consideration of type 2 lepra reaction or erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL). Adult multibacillary multidrug therapy with clofazimine (50 mg/day plus 300 mg/month), and dapsone (100 mg/day) was started alongside the rifampicin‐containing antituberculosis regimen. Inflammatory markers decreased after 5 days of steroids (ESR 107 mm/h and CRP 32.5 mg/dL).

(A) Punch biopsy of a plaque on the abdomen showed aggregates of foamy histiocytes in the dermis (H&E stain, ×40). (B) Fite Faraco stain showed an abundance of acid‐fast bacilli inside the histiocytes (×100).

The patient was discharged with a 20‐week course of prednisone (40 mg/day) and treatment for drug‐susceptible pulmonary tuberculosis and leprosy. He completed 6 months of antituberculosis treatment, remained free of respiratory symptoms, but developed cushingoid features. Most erythematous papules and plaques flattened with resolution of pruritus.

Discussion

Following the general global trend, incident tuberculosis and leprosy have declined due to public health efforts. Some hypothesize the possibility of cross‐immunity for tuberculosis and leprosy in synergism with social and ecological changes as another reason [1].

The absence of classic features of Hansen's disease, such as hypoesthesia, hypohidrosis, nerve enlargement and nerve tenderness, made the diagnosis unapparent in our reported case. Tuberculosis and leprosy require different regimens of long‐course multidrug treatment: a 6‐month course of daily isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol for most cases of drug‐susceptible pulmonary tuberculosis, and monthly rifampicin, daily clofazimine and dapsone for Hansen's disease.

Lepra reactions, the most significant cause of disability among persons with Hansen's disease, may be precipitated by treatment with antimicrobials active against M. leprae (e.g., rifampicin) and can mimic paradoxical reactions in tuberculosis [2] Type 1, or reversal, reactions are delayed‐type hypersensitivity phenomena due to an inflammatory response to the bacilli, while the Type 2 reactions—characterised by ENL—are immune complex‐mediated hypersensitivity reactions occurring in as many as half of patients with lepromatous leprosy [3] The risk of type 2 reaction is thought to be higher in those with lepromatous leprosy and positive bacterial index [4] ENL typically occurs within the first year of treatment, but can also occur in untreated patients, and is often managed with steroids [3].

A diagnosis of leprosy preceded tuberculosis in most coinfection cases in a review of 25 publications from 1968 to 2013 [5]. Among five reported lepra reactions, only one had ENL further highlighting the unusual presentation of our patient. Concomitant Hansen's disease should be considered when treating tuberculosis, especially in areas where both are prevalent and should be managed collaboratively.
