Clinician-in-the-loop screening saturation: predicting annotation yield for efficient EHR review
Authors: Assaf Landschaft, Leena Abdelmoity, Fatemeh Mohammad Alizadeh Chafjiri, Molly Ann Puckett, Jennifer Gettings, Tobias Loddenkemper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLabor- and cost-intensive manual chart review of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) remains a major bottleneck in retrospective studies, particularly when rare-disease cohorts require high specificity. Automated Natural Language Processing (NLP) rankers help, yet when trained on dated data, they leave teams guessing how long to keep reviewing charts. Therefore, this study presents a regression-based “screening-saturation” model that predicts residual yield at every point along the ranked list.MethodsUsing a previously validated Support Vector Machine (SVM) that ranks notes for pediatric status epilepticus, the authors first trained four predictive models: linear, polynomial, and support-vector regressions plus a lightweight neural net, on notes from 2013. Then, these models were tested on data from 2020. The target was to identify the proportion of true positives (ESE or RSE) expected below any score threshold.ResultsPolynomial regression offered the best balance of generalizability and interpretability, demonstrating a strong predictive performance even under temporal data shifts. On 2020 notes, a 20% yield threshold captured 78.1% of positives after reviewing 14.6% of records (1,118/7,636), which is equivalent to an 85.4% reduction in manual annotations (i.e., 6,518 fewer).ConclusionThe proposed scalable, model-agnostic framework turns AI scores into actionable staffing decisions in clinical workflows. This screening-saturation model integrates with clinician-in-the-loop (CITL) tools and readily adapts across medical domains requiring lean chart review.Keywords: Electronic health records, Natural language processing, Support vector machine, Retrospective cohort studies, Status epilepticus, Machine learning, Annotation efficiency, Yield prediction, Artificial intelligence, Clinician in the loop

Background

Manual chart-review bottleneck and limitations of NLP-only approaches

Retrospective cohort studies that analyze patient charts from electronic health records (EHRs) become increasingly critical to generate real-world evidence in neurology and other medical fields [1–3]. Since these patient charts often contain detailed clinical reasoning and symptom descriptions that are not captured in structured fields, they are crucial for the identification of complex or rare patient phenotypes. However, an accurate identification of the cases that meet strict inclusion criteria frequently requires extensive manual review of free-text notes, particularly when the goal is to ensure high specificity in cohort definition. Along with being time-consuming, this manual process is both costly and labor-intensive.

A potential solution to the problem of classifying clinical texts in retrospective research is to use advanced natural language processing (NLP) models [4–6]. However, these models’ limitations in sensitivity and specificity prevent them from replacing human judgment in high-stakes phenotyping tasks. This is particularly the case with rare conditions where research teams must rely on manual review as a validation step for AI/NLP-ranked candidate records and where misclassification can cause a significant bias in findings [7–8].

Furthermore, dependency on manual validation creates a bottleneck in large-scale studies, particularly when it is unclear how long the review should continue. In the absence of principled stopping criteria, researchers typically adopt heuristic approaches, such as reviewing a fixed number of top-ranked cases until a perceived plateau in positive cases is reached [9] or stopping because of time or personnel constraints [10]. However, an important limitation of these ad hoc strategies is that they are not only non-reproducible, but also can lead to inefficiencies, either prematurely halting the review and thus missing valuable cases, or unnecessarily extending the review into the low-yield tail of the ranking.

Temporal dataset shift and implications for planning

Another limitation of NLP models is that their performance is frequently evaluated under the implicit assumption that the data distribution remains stable over time, which rarely holds in clinical practice. Although previous studies validated models using random splits from a single dataset [11], such internal validation overlooks the reality that, due to changes in EHR systems, staff turnover, institutional policies, and population shifts, clinical documentation continuously evolves. Accordingly, when NLP models trained on earlier datasets are applied to newer records, these distributional shifts can lead to substantial performance degradation [12–13].

More specifically, when deployed on future datasets, standard classification metrics, such as sensitivity and specificity, calculated on historical data tend to fail to reflect a model’s actual performance. This concern is particularly relevant in clinical machine learning, where even small changes in documentation styles or coding practices can undermine model generalizability. For instance, in a recent systematic review, Guo et al. [14] found that temporal shifts frequently impact both calibration and discrimination of clinical prediction models, which highlights the need for regular re-evaluation and updating to maintain performance over time. In another study, the same research group demonstrated that a sepsis prediction model trained on the data from 2008 to 2010 experienced a marked decline in AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) when tested on the data from 2017 to 2019 [15], thus underscoring the risks associated with using static models in temporally dynamic environments.

These temporal distribution shifts bear important implications for applying NLP models in long-term cohort identification projects, where performance degradation over time can silently increase the number of missed cases, thereby reintroducing manual review burdens that these models intend to reduce. As a result, research teams face a fundamental planning challenge—namely, how to anticipate the actual utility of an NLP classifier trained on historical data when applied prospectively, and how to allocate manual review effort accordingly. This challenge is particularly salient with limited budgets for manual annotation or when the marginal yield of reviewing lower-ranked records is a priori unknown.

Indeed, when using EHR-based classifiers trained on historical data, it becomes increasingly challenging to estimate the amount of manual annotation required to achieve a desired level of case capture in newer datasets. Standard metrics such as precision, sensitivity, or AUROC calculated at the time of model development often fail to accurately reflect real-world utility at the time of deployment. As a result, long-term cohort identification projects must grapple with growing uncertainty around how much manual review is enough, and how much is too much. As a result, researchers may either overcommit effort to screening low-yield records ranked towards the bottom of the score distribution or prematurely terminate the review, which leads to missing a substantial fraction of true positive cases. Moreover, our data demonstrate strong front-loading of positives, with yields declining at lower ranks (see Fig. 2), and a yield-aware stopping rule capturing 80% of positives after reviewing only 16.6% of records (see Table 2; see also [16, 17] for a broader context on predictive modeling under imbalanced conditions and [18] for a non-NLP optimization-driven ML example).

Stopping rule performance

Research problem: Planning a reproducible stopping point under temporal drift

Large cohort-building projects frequently apply an NLP ranker trained on historical notes to a newer, temporally shifted dataset. Typically, investigators have to decide how far down the ranked list to continue manual review to reach a prespecified capture target (e.g., over 80% of true cases) without wasting effort on low-yield records. Formally, given a relevance score X and a current rank Y, the decision depends on the residual yield, i.e., the expected proportion of positive cases remaining below rank Y in the new dataset. However, the current available classifier-centric metrics (AUROC, sensitivity/specificity) computed at model development time do not answer this deployment-time question and can be misleading under temporal shift. Accordingly, the research problem addressed in this study is as follows: estimating residual yield along the ranked list in the prospective (shifted) data to select a reproducible, threshold-based stopping point that aligns review effort with a target capture rate and/or a fixed annotation budget.

Contribution of the present study

This study introduces and prospectively evaluates a classifier-agnostic regression framework that maps model scores to residual annotation yield in a real EHR use case (pediatric status epilepticus). Trained on 2013 notes and tested on 2020 notes, the proposed framework provides actionable stopping rules (e.g., at a 20% residual-yield threshold, review of 1,118/7,636 notes captured 78.1% of positives, reducing manual annotations by 85.4%). This directly operationalizes the planning problem above and makes the effort–yield trade-off explicit for study teams.

Overview of the proposed framework

This study develops and prospectively evaluates a classifier-agnostic regression framework that maps AI scores to residual annotation yield along a relevance-ranked list and supports data-driven stopping rules under temporal shift (train set: 2013; test set: 2020). Specifically, historical manual review data from 2013 are used to train regression models that predict the proportion of remaining positive cases, namely, Established Status Epilepticus (ESE) or Refractory Status Epilepticus (RSE) beneath any given record in a 2020 test set, sorted by classifier score. The proposed approach provides a quantitative, model-agnostic tool to forecast yield during manual review and supports more efficient stopping strategies under real-world, temporally shifted conditions.

Methods

Methodology overview and rationale

This study uses a classifier-agnostic regression framework that maps the existing model’s score to the residual annotation yield along a score-ranked list, producing an actionable, threshold-based stopping rule under temporal drift. The proposed method is intentionally simple (single predictor = base SVM score) to: (i) remain portable across sites and base models; (ii) produce smooth, calibratable curves amenable to thresholding; (iii) avoid metadata-specific overfitting; and (iv) emphasize prospective stability over in-sample accuracy. The strong score–yield correlation and temporal shift between 2013 and 2020 discussed earlier in this paper underlie the use of a regression-to-yield approach and prospective testing on 2020 data.The workflow has six phases (P0–P5), each with its specific inputs and outputs (see Fig. 1). Further detail on each of the phases is provided below.

Research methodology (P0–P5)

Data extraction (P0)

Inputs

The authors queried the Boston Children’s Hospital enterprise data warehouse (BCH360) for unstructured notes from the institutional EHR (Cerner PowerChart) and retrieved two non-consecutive, fully annotated years—2013 (training) and 2020 (prospective test)—to simulate real-world deployment under temporal shift. The pull was restricted to clinically relevant services and settings using neurology and ED (emergency department) filters and standard document-type constraints (e.g., “Emergency MD,” “Neurology Admission MD/Consultation/Inpatient MD,” ICU progress notes, and corresponding nursing and MSICU note types).

Process

The raw export comprised over 2 million notes across the period of 2013–2024. For the present study, the data from the years 2013 and 2020 were used because 2013 was the earliest year for which the data were available in the extract, while 2020 was the latest year with completed manual annotation for the status-epilepticus cohort, providing maximally separated datasets for prospective evaluation. Notes were filtered to the predefined neurology/ED document types to enrich for SE-relevant content prior to scoring and review. All data handling occurred in full compliance with the BCH governance; the protocol was IRB-approved with waiver of consent, and downstream sharing of raw notes was not permitted due to PHI restrictions.

Outputs

Outputs in P0 included the de-identified note corpus partitioned by year and limited to the target document types, with the following cohort sizes: 2013–362,525 total notes, of which 12,976 top-ranked records were manually reviewed; 2020–397,956 total notes, 7,636 of which were manually reviewed (top-ranked) records. These year-specific corpora fed phases P1 (label curation) and P2 (base ranking) in the workflow.

Rationale and safeguards

Selecting 2013→2020 enforced a meaningful temporal gap to expose distributional drift in documentation and case mix, reduce optimistic bias from temporal overlap, and better reflect prospective deployment conditions. To ensure label quality and comparability across years, identical operational definitions (International League Against Epilepsy/ILAE and Pediatric Status Epilepticus Research Group/pSERG) were applied in the subsequent phases and case-serial-number (CSN) propagated “automatic” labels were excluded from ground truth; only manually verified annotations were used in modeling and evaluation.

Label curation (P1)

Inputs

Inputs in P1 were de-identified notes from P0; clinician reviewers; a Clinician-in-the-Loop (CITL) annotation workflow implemented in the Document Review Tool (DrT) platform; and operational phenotype definitions for status epilepticus (SE) consistent with ILAE and pSERG practice. DrT supports CITL workflows where automated ranking triages records while clinicians perform final adjudication for high-specificity cohort construction [20]. Prior DrT deployments across diverse pediatric and safety-surveillance tasks demonstrated portability of this paradigm [21–25] and the platform documentation is publicly available [26]. In this study, the authors leveraged a previously validated SE-focused DrT pipeline that prioritized notes using an SVM n-gram model reported in previously published peer-reviewed work [27, 28], while SE phenotype definitions followed ILAE/pSERG conventions [29].

Process

Clinician annotators reviewed triaged notes in DrT and applied discrete labels on the note level according to ILAE/pSERG-aligned criteria for ESE (established SE) and RSE (refractory SE), with additional mutually exclusive categories for SE(NO) (convulsive seizures not meeting ESE/RSE criteria), NO (no SE event), Excluded (does not meet inclusion criteria), and Ineligible (outside the age range). To reduce redundant effort across documentation for the same clinical encounter, labels applied to the highest-scoring note within a case serial number (CSN) were propagated to other notes in that CSN; these propagated notes were explicitly marked “Automatic.” Only manually verified labels (i.e., not “Automatic”) were treated as ground truth for model development and evaluation in the subsequent phases. Ambiguities were escalated for expert adjudication to maintain consistency (see also Section “Base ranking (P2) for the scoring system used for triage) [20–26, 27–29].

Outputs

The output in P1 was a gold-standard, manually verified label set for each reviewed note, plus a study-level binary target for modeling: Category2 = Positive for notes labeled ESE or RSE, and Category2 = Negative for SE(NO), Excluded, or Ineligible. Notes marked “Automatic” (CSN-propagated) were excluded from ground-truth creation and from all modeling/evaluation steps, thus ensuring that regression targets and performance estimates reflected only clinician-adjudicated evidence. These outputs fed the next phase, P3 (Target construction), and the prospective evaluation pipeline [20–26, 27–29].

Notes on scope

Document-type filters used to prioritize SE-relevant content (neurology, ICU, ED) are specified in P0 (Data extraction) and were applied before label curation to enrich for clinically informative notes; label semantics and propagation logic above were independent of note type [27–29].

Base ranking (P2)

Inputs

Inputs in P2 included the DrT platform’s previously validated SVM-based, bag-of-n-grams ranker; de-identified notes from 2013 (training context) and 2020 (prospective context); and the manual label schema described in P1. As previously reported, DrT assigns each note a machine-learning (ML) score using an SVM trained on annotated data (2017–2019) with MRMR feature selection (top ~ 500 n-grams) [27, 28, 30]. The score was used exclusively as a single predictor for downstream yield modeling to maximize portability and avoid metadata-specific overfitting [27, 28, 30].

Process

Each note received a continuous score from the SVM classifier, with higher values indicating greater likelihood that the note contained evidence of SE (status epilepticus) [27, 28, 30]. Furthermore, clinician reviewers assigned mutually exclusive manual categories (Score 2) in DrT: ESE (established SE), RSE (refractory SE), SE(NO) (convulsive seizures not meeting ESE/RSE), NO (no SE), Excluded (did not meet inclusion criteria), Ineligible (outside age range). For efficiency, the label applied to the highest-scoring note within a CSN was propagated to other notes in that encounter and marked “Automatic.” Finally, for modeling, manual categories were mapped to Category2 as follows: Positive = {ESE, RSE}; Negative = {SE(NO), NO, Excluded, Ineligible}. Notes labeled Automatic (i.e., CSN-propagated, not manually reviewed) were excluded from ground-truth creation and from all modeling/evaluation steps. Ambiguities were escalated for expert adjudication [27, 28, 30].

Outputs

To each note, the following scores were assigned: (i) a raw SVM Score (continuous) that determined its position in the ranked list used for triage; (ii) Score2 (manual category) and (iii) Category2 (binary target, with Automatic excluded). These outputs fed the next phase, P3 (Target construction), and later prospective evaluation on 2020 data [27, 28, 30].

Front-loading and rank behavior

Categorical heat maps (2013 vs. 2020) in Fig. 2 visualize manual outcomes (Category2) over the score-sorted lists: green (ESE/RSE) concentrates near the top, confirming that the SVM score front-loads positives and supports rank-based review efficiency. The lower early-rank density of positives in 2020 illustrates the temporal shift.

To further characterize rank behavior, Fig. 3 showscumulative-gains (Precision@K) curves for 2013 vs. 2020; a permutation test on the first 1,000 records yielded p = 0.0457, indicating a significantly lower early sensitivity in 2020 [37, 38]. Operationally, acknowledging expected variability in real-world multi-year studies [39], 2013 and 2020 manual reviews were performed by different reviewer teams with expert adjudication for uncertainties to enhance external validity. In practice, many retrospective workflows rely on heuristic stopping (e.g., plateauing yield or fixed top-K [36]), and classifier-centric metrics can be poorly aligned with deployment-time utility under drift [31]. Accordingly, recent work in technology-assisted review and clinical NLP favoured saturation-aware heuristics and yield-guided planning [32, 33, 34–36], which motivated the regression-to-yield modelling formalized in phases P3–P5 in the present study.

Target construction (P3)

Inputs

Inputs in P3 included year-partitioned, score-ranked note lists from 2013 (training context) and 2020 (prospective context); manual labels as defined in P1 (with Automatic/CSN-propagated labels) were removed before target computation.

Process

The target variable for each record was computed as a positive ratio derived from the score-ranked list after excluding “automatic” notes. Specifically, for every position k in the ranked list, the authors calculated:

Positive_ratio_above: the ratio of positives among all higher-ranked records; and.

Positive_ratio_above: the ratio of positives among all higher-ranked records; and.

Positive_ratio_below: the ratio of positives among all lower-ranked records.

Positive_ratio_below: the ratio of positives among all lower-ranked records.

Following the study design, positive_ratio_below served as the regression target for subsequent modeling. Targets (positive_ratio_below/above), cumulative-gains curves, and all metrics were computed after removing “automatic” notes, using a custom function (Notebook Cell 5). This construction yielded a deployment-time quantity of interest, residual yield below rank k, that directly supported threshold-based stopping in P5.

Outputs

For each ranked record, the researchers obtained a paired set of yield targets (positive_ratio_above, positive_ratio_below) aligned to the manual ground truth, with positive_ratio_below designated as the primary regression target consumed by P4–P5.

The input feature used later for modelling was the raw SVM score (column score) produced by DrT. To maximize generalizability and maintain a classifier-agnostic design, predictors were intentionally restricted to the base model score; no metadata features (e.g., document length, note type, reviewer IDs) were included to minimize overfitting under temporal drift (see the next section for model families and training details).

Model training (2013) (P4)

Purpose

To map the base SVM Score to positive_ratio_below (residual yield), four regression families and hyperparameters were used. The goal was to compare bias–variance trade-offs affecting prospective stability under temporal drift, not to introduce novel architectures.

Inputs

Inputs in P4 included single predictor – the raw SVM score (column Score) assigned by the base ranker (see P2); regression target – positive_ratio_below computed in P3; training set − 2013 score-ranked notes with manually verified (non-Automatic) ground truth.

Process

The following four canonical regressors spanning a bias–variance and interpretability spectrum were trained using the single SVM score as input and positive_ratio_below as the target: (1) Linear regression [19] (sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression) as a transparent baseline; (2) Polynomial regression [19] (pipeline: PolynomialFeatures → LinearRegression), tuning degree (1–5) and intercept; (3) Support Vector Regression (SVR) [40] (pipeline: StandardScaler → SVR) with RBF and other kernels, tuning CCC (0.1–5.1), ϵ\epsilonϵ (0.01–0.21), γ\gammaγ (“scale”, “auto”, or sampled 0.001–0.101), and kernel type (“rbf”, “poly”, “sigmoid”); and (4) a lightweight neural network (NN) implemented in PyTorch and wrapped for scikit-learn compatibility (fully connected, feed-forward). NN hyperparameters included learning rate (0.0001, 0.001, 0.01) and hidden layers {(32,16), (64,32), (128,64), (32,16,8)}; training used Adam, MSE loss, 5 epochs, batch size 32. All inputs were standardized where appropriate. This design prioritized low-variance, well-behaved score→yield estimates that supported threshold-based stopping and minimized error inflation under the temporal drift (benchmarked prospectively on 2020).

Training protocol (2013)

Models were fit only on 2013 (N = 12,976 manually reviewed, top-ranked records). A 5-fold cross-validation on 2013 was used to estimate in-sample fit and to select hyperparameters for polynomial degree and SVR/NN configurations. The evaluation metrics computed on CV folds were MSE and R². To keep temporal testing separate from model training, all prospective 2020 results were reported in P5.

Outputs

Outputs in P4 included a set of fitted 2013 regressors (linear, polynomial, SVR, NN) with selected hyperparameters, along with their 2013 CV performance summaries (MSE, R²) for comparison. These trained models were then applied prospectively to 2020 in P5 to generate residual-yield curves and threshold-based stopping points.

Implementation details and reproducibility

Analyses used Python 3.10 with scikit-learn 1.3, PyTorch 2.0, pandas, numpy, matplotlib, and seaborn in a standard CPU environment (Intel i7, 16 GB RAM). Reproducibility seeds were set (e.g., np.random.seed(42), torch.manual_seed(42)). Code and de-identified example data are available (see Data Availability statement).

Prospective evaluation (2020) & stopping (P5)

Inputs

Inputs in P5 were fitted 2013 regressors from P4 (linear, polynomial, SVR, NN); the 2020 score-ranked notes with manually verified (non-Automatic) ground truth from P1/P3; and the SVM Score per note from P2.

Process

The authors simulated a prospective deployment by sorting 2020 notes in descending order of the SVM Score and applying each 2013-trained regressor to predict positive_ratio_below for every rank position (i.e., the residual yield below rank 𝑘). Next, the following yield-threshold stopping rule was operationalized: the stopping point was the first rank where the predicted residual yield fell below a predefined threshold. Following prior practice in technology-assisted review and clinical NLP [27, 32, 36], the authors evaluated 10%, 20%, and 30% thresholds to represent conservative, moderate, and exploratory operating points, respectively. For each threshold and model, the authors recorded: (i) reviewed records (N) and percent of the dataset at the stop; and (ii) captured positives (%) up to that stop, relative to all positives in 2020. Model-fit diagnostics on 2020 included MSE and R² between predicted and empirical residual yield; cross-validated 2013 metrics are listed in Table 1 (Results) to preserve a train-then-test separation.

Results

Model performance on training data (2013)

Each model’s ability was first evaluated to estimate annotation yield on the training data from 2013 using 5-fold cross-validation. To reflect precision and explained variance, the models were evaluated using MSE and the R² coefficient of determination. For linear regression, considering that no hyperparameter tuning was required, the standard implementation was used. Polynomial regression was evaluated with degree = 2 and intercept fitting enabled. SVR was evaluated with the best configuration identified during tuning: C = 2.16, epsilon = 0.016, gamma = 0.010, and kernel = ‘rbf’. The PyTorch Neural Network Regressor was evaluated with a three-layer architecture (32, 16, 8) and a learning rate of 0.01.

The results showed that SVR achieved the best overall performance, demonstrating the strongest ability to capture the underlying patterns in the data with high accuracy and generalization. The PyTorch Neural Network also performed strongly, effectively learning nonlinear relationships while maintaining robust generalization. Linear regression and polynomial regression (degree = 2) delivered identical results, both showing stable performance and capturing most of the variance in the data despite their relative simplicity. Interestingly, however, the superior performance of SVR came at the cost of a significantly higher computational expense during hyperparameter tuning. Prospective generalization to temporal test data (2020)

As a next step, to simulate real-world deployment under conditions reflecting potential shifts in population characteristics and workflow dynamics, all models were evaluated on held-out 2020 data. The results revealed that linear regression and polynomial regression (degree = 2) performed equivalently, achieving the lowest MSE and maintaining a high R², thus indicating strong robustness and resilience to temporal drift. These results suggest that the simpler linear models are better able to generalize under changing conditions, which could be attributed to their reduced risk of overfitting as compared to more complex models. While SVR showed a slightly higher MSE and a modestly lower R², reflecting some sensitivity to evolving data distributions, it still generalized reasonably well relative to the training data. By contrast, the PyTorch Neural Network exhibited a marked decline in performance, with substantially higher MSE and lower R², implying that its increased complexity and potential overfitting during training limited its adaptability to unseen, temporally shifted data. Taken together, these results highlight that, while all models experience some performance degradation under temporal drift, simpler models like linear and polynomial regression maintained more consistent generalization, whereas complex models require careful tuning or additional strategies to remain robust over time (see Table 1).

The authors also examined the fold-by-fold performance on test data, where linear regression and polynomial regression demonstrate nearly identical results. Specifically, the two models showed the lowest mean MSE and highest mean R², along with minimal variability across folds, thus indicating consistent and stable performance regardless of data partitioning. In its turn, support vector regression demonstrated a moderate performance, achieving a lower predictive accuracy than the linear models, but maintaining a relatively low variability, which reflects a consistent behavior across folds. By contrast, the PyTorch neural network exhibited the highest mean MSE and lowest mean R², along with substantially greater fold-to-fold variability in both metrics, suggesting a higher sensitivity to the specific training subsets and a less robust generalization on this dataset. Overall, the results revealed that the linear models emerged as the most reliable in terms of both accuracy and stability.

Furthermore, statistical testing revealed a significant difference in MSE across models (one-way ANOVA: F = 32.79, p = 4.58 × 10⁻⁷). The results of pairwise t-tests showed that linear regression significantly outperformed both support vector regression (t = − 4.84, p = 8.41 × 10⁻³) and the PyTorch neural network (t = − 7.14, p = 2.03 × 10⁻³), with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 2.0); yet, the performance of linear regression did not differ significantly from that of polynomial regression (p = 0.374). Confirming the superior and consistent performance of the linear models on the 2020 test data, this finding suggests that the added complexity of kernel-based or neural models does not translate into improved generalization—and may, in fact, hinder performance on unseen data.

Temporal drift impact analysis

The authors quantified temporal drift impact using multiple residual plots and diagnostic visualizations for each of the four regression models. In the Residuals vs. Predicted plots (Fig. 5), for the predicted values between ~ 0.4 and ~ 0.6, most models (especially linear and polynomial regression) showed residuals centered near zero in both 2013 and 2020 datasets. No strong upward or downward trend was observed, meaning the models were not systematically over or under predicting in this range. However, in the 2020 predictions, clear patterns emerged. For example, the SVR exhibited a strong upward trend in residuals, Similarly, PyTorch Neural Network showed increasingly positive residuals as predicted values rise, meaning it systematically underpredicted high outcomes. Taken together, these results indicate that models’ performance deteriorates for high predicted values. Concurrently, linear and polynomial regressions were found to possess the strongest stability between 2013 and 2020, meaning that they do not develop strong biases in the 2020 data. The authors also examined residuals vs. the SVM score—a critical input feature that indicates whether model errors depend on specific variables and whether that dependence changes over time. The plots (Fig. 6) indicate that, for high SVM scores, residuals drifted significantly away from zero. The neural network and SVR models, in particular, showed large positive residuals at high scores, meaning they underpredict when the SVM score is high. Furthermore, the MSE comparison bar chart (Fig. 7) clearly illustrates that all models exhibited higher errors when applied to the 2020 data. Notably, the SVR model—despite its strong training performance, experienced a significant 2488% increase in MSE (Fig. 7), indicating severe overfitting under data shift. The PyTorch neural network also showed a substantial deterioration, with an 80.5% rise in MSE and the largest absolute increase, highlighting poor generalization to newer data (Fig. 8). For linear and polynomial regression models, their MSE increase was moderate (378%); yet, considering that their baseline error was low, their absolute performance in 2020 remained acceptable.

Correlation of score with yield metrics

The authors computed Pearson correlation coefficients between the input score and both outcome metrics (positive_ratio_above and positive_ratio_below) on the 2020 data. The score-to-yield correlations amounted to 0.94; 95%-confidence-interval: [0.918, 0.955] and 0.98; 95%-confidence interval: [0.977, 0.982], respectively. This finding confirms a strong positive correlation: namely, with an increase in the score, the yield metrics also increase. This finding also supports our design rationale behind the score-ranked screening.

Saturation-guided review efficiency

To evaluate the practical utility of the four regression models, the authors simulated a stopping rule based on predicted positive_ratio_below values. The stopping point was defined as the first record in the score-ranked list where the predicted marginal yield (i.e., expected proportion of remaining positive cases) fell below a predefined threshold. In our analysis, the authors tested different thresholds ranging from 10% to 30%. Subsequently, the authors measured (i) the total number of records reviewed before reaching this point; and (ii) the total number of positive cases captured up to that point, relative to all positives in the dataset.

Furthermore, when polynomial was applied to the 2020 dataset, the stopping rule allowed review termination at different thresholds. For instance, using a 20% threshold, the review could be stopped after approximately 1,118 records. This approach captured 78.1% of all positive cases, effectively front-loading the high-yield segment of the record list. Furthermore, when a more conservative 10% threshold was applied, a total of 3,829 records (50.1% of the dataset) needed to be reviewed to capture 93.4% of positives. Likewise, when applying a 30% threshold, only 471 records (6.2% of the dataset) were sufficient, capturing 60.6% of positives (see Table 2).

These results demonstrate that the screening saturation model can significantly reduce manual workload. For instance, 20% threshold could save up to 85.4%of annotations (see Fig. 9). By improving retrospective cohort efficiency with explicit control over the effort–quality trade-off, this targeted stopping approach can be particularly useful in resource-constrained settings.

Discussion

Although retrospective cohort studies using EHRs are a cornerstone of real-world evidence generation, their manual review remains a major bottleneck, particularly when high specificity is required. To address this concern, the present study proposes a data-driven “screening saturation” framework that estimates the marginal annotation yield for AI-prioritized records. Regression models can accurately approximate the expected return, thereby enabling more efficient and transparent stopping decisions.

Across different model types, the authors observed strong correlations between model-assigned relevance scores and annotation yield, which confirmed feasibility of learning a predictive mapping for stopping rule optimization. However, the authors also found that the models’ ability to generalize across temporal shifts in the data substantially varied. Specifically, while the neural network achieved the highest performance during training, it exhibited a notable decline when tested on the temporally shifted 2020 data, which could have been due to overfitting. By contrast, the Polynomial Regression model demonstrated the most robust performance, achieving the best balance of generalization and accuracy in the test set.

These findings suggest that, in contrast to more complex architectures, their lightweight, interpretable counterparts may offer a more stable performance in real-world, non-stationary environments. Importantly, the authors also observed that yield-aware stopping rules based on these models can considerably reduce the manual annotation burden. Specifically, using a 20% yield threshold, review could be terminated after 1,118 records (equivalent to 14.6% of the dataset) while capturing 78.1% of all positives, thus substantially front-loading high-yield cases. This substantial efficiency gain underscores the value of saturation modeling in resource-constrained settings.

Therefore, compared to traditional stopping methods such as fixed quotas or yield plateaus, the proposed approach offers a principled, quantitative framework that aligns review effort with expected informational return. Overall, the proposed framework provides practical support for CITL cohort construction by enabling reproducible, yield-aware stopping decisions under temporal shift.

Finally, via prospectively validating the models on unseen temporal data, our results also highlighted the challenges posed by distributional drift in clinical AI pipelines. Specifically, changes in documentation styles, case mix, and underlying EHR systems can significantly alter score distributions and case density, thereby reinforcing the need for adaptive, yield-based tools that can accommodate such shifts. While the authors did not perform a dedicated linguistic change analysis, our framework is designed to remain useful without such an audit: it is classifier-agnostic and employs observed score-yield relationships to set reproducible stopping rules under temporal shift.

Estimated efficiency gains

If an 80% capture target is acceptable, the yield-based stopping rule captured 78.1% of positives upon review of 14.6% of notes (1,118/7,636), thus resulting in a 85.4% reduction (≈ 6,518 fewer annotations). Furthermore, assuming 2–5 min per note, roughly 217–543 person-hours can be economized. Of note, sites can map hours to local costs and thresholds remain tunable.

Comparison with existing screening and active-learning frameworks

Previous clinical NLP studies typically reported sensitivity/specificity (and sometimes AUROC) and relied on heuristic stopping rules (e.g., fixed top-K or plateau criteria) [41–42]. However, while these metrics are largely prevalence-insensitive and can appear stable under temporal drift, they do not quantify the residual yield that remains in the ranked list or the required review workload.

Likewise, while recent registry-building approaches such as focus on accurate extraction/classification from clinical notes report traditional accuracy metrics, they do not provide a calibrated, operational rule for reviewing documents (e.g [43]). By contrast, our framework yields an explicit, tunable stopping that the authors prospectively validated under drift (2013→2020). On the 2020 data, it captured 78.1% of positives after reviewing 14.6% of notes (1,118/7,636), making the effort–yield trade-off transparent and complementary to classifier-centric benchmarks.

Implications for practice and system design

The proposed screening saturation framework has several important practical implications for the development and deployment of CITL systems in clinical research and quality improvement initiatives. In contrast to traditional manual review pipelines that frequently rely on arbitrary quotas, fixed-time efforts, or plateau-based heuristics to determine when to stop reviewing AI-ranked records, the proposed method introduces a quantifiable and transparent mechanism to stop the review process based on predicted marginal yield, thereby aligning human effort more precisely with expected benefit.

Furthermore, embedding yield-aware stopping logic into clinical NLP systems, such as the DrT tool used in the present study, could allow researchers to visualize predicted annotation saturation in real time. For instance, users may receive system-generated alerts when a predefined low-yield threshold is reached or interactively adjust it based on evolving resource constraints. This would shift manual review from a fixed-scope task to a dynamically adaptive process, offering greater control over effort-quality trade-offs. By accelerating cohort construction and reducing annotation burden, such adaptive review workflows can reduce the time from data extraction to evidence generation, thereby enabling faster dissemination of research findings that will ultimately inform clinical practice and improve patient care.

Another important characteristic of the proposed regression-based framework that it is classifier-agnostic, meaning that, regardless of the specific architecture used, it can be deployed atop any base model that ranks records by relevance. While, in the present study, the authors used an SVM trained on n-gram features, the proposed saturation framework could easily integrate with more advanced transformer-based classifiers or hybrid rule-based systems. This compatibility promotes a broader applicability of the proposed framework across different clinical domains and annotation goals.

Furthermore, by helping low-resource teams achieve acceptable cohort quality without extensive manual labor, the proposed saturation-guided review could contribute to more equitable research practices. In global health contexts or small-scale clinical studies with limited staffing, such models could maximize the return on scarce annotation resources, thus democratizing access to high-quality retrospective research.

Finally, by allowing for user-defined trade-offs between effort and yield, the proposed approach supports tailored review strategies, for instance, stopping at a higher threshold (e.g., 30% remaining yield) in exploratory studies or at a lower threshold (e.g., 10%) in confirmatory analyses.

Limitations

The proposed screening saturation framework has several limitations. First, accuracy of the regression-based yield predictions inherently depends on quality, calibration, and stability of the underlying ranking model, in this case, the SVM-based classifier embedded in the DrT tool. If the base classifier assigns misleading scores due to miscalibration, distributional drift, or suboptimal feature representation, the saturation model’s predictions may become unreliable. Accordingly, it is essential to validate and update the base model regularly.

Second, while the proposed framework assumes that manually reviewed records, particularly those at the top of the ranking, are exhaustively and correctly labeled in real-world scenarios with multiple annotators or variable reviewer experience, label noise or inconsistencies in annotation completeness may compromise fidelity of the training signal and adversely affect accuracy of the yield prediction model. Inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen’s κ) was not computed because dual-annotation was infeasible at scale; to mitigate this limitation, standardized definitions and expert adjudication were used.

Third, single-institution scope and no cross-EHR external validation in this study may limit generalizability of the results. In the present study, the authors conducted a prospective evaluation using temporally shifted data (2013 and 2020) from the same institution. Yet, differences in documentation culture, clinical terminology, EHR platforms, and annotation protocols may introduce heterogeneity that could reduce generalizability. This warrants future research that would test the performance of the proposed framework in multi-institutional or multilingual settings, along with external validation across diverse sites and specialties.

Fourth, in the current implementation, only the raw model-assigned score was used as input to the regression model. While this simplification aids interpretability and ease of integration, it may limit predictive precision. Accordingly, including additional contextual features (e.g., note type, department, or reviewer-specific factors) in future studies could enhance model accuracy and robustness.

Fifth, our study focused solely on a binary positive-vs-negative yield prediction. Yet, in more nuanced annotation tasks, yield may not be adequately captured by simple binary outcomes. Accordingly, to better reflect clinical ambiguity and annotation effort, future implementations may need to incorporate richer label hierarchies or uncertainty measures.

Sixth, considering that the saturation framework is currently retrospective and offline, although the results support its integration into real-time or interactive systems, additional software engineering and user testing would be needed to validate its usability, interpretability, and impact on end-user decision-making in live annotation settings.

Finally, considering that the proposed framework focuses on yield prediction, rather than on classification performance, it does not produce standard metrics such as precision, recall, or specificity. This may limit its adoption in contexts where decision makers are more familiar with traditional model evaluation metrics. However, the present design choice reflects the framework’s major intent of guiding resource-efficient manual review, rather than automating case classification.

Future research directions

In further research, the proposed framework may be extended in several directions. First, regarding threshold optimization, future studies could use data-driven or Bayesian approaches to dynamically tune the stopping threshold based on cost-benefit trade-offs. Another meaningful future research path is multivariate modeling, where yield predictions could be further improved by incorporating metadata such as document type, reviewer experience, or visit context. Third, considering the novel opportunities for richer ranking functions afforded by large language models (LLMs), the proposed method could layer atop LLM outputs to guide a more efficient review. Fourth, to better reflect clinical ambiguity and support more nuanced annotation tasks, future implementations may extend beyond binary yield prediction to accommodate multi-class outcomes or incorporate uncertainty estimates. Finally, embedding saturation prediction into live annotation platforms could enable adaptive review workflows and immediate cost tracking.

In future studies, the yield-prediction framework can be complemented by fully probabilistic models that quantify uncertainty and enable decision-theoretic stopping. First, the number of positives below a score threshold can be modeled as Binomial with a Beta prior on the residual-yield parameter, producing posterior credible intervals and posterior probabilities that a chosen yield threshold is satisfied (e.g., for cost-aware stopping) [44]. Second, Bayesian nonparametric smoothing (e.g., Gaussian process regression over the score axis) can provide calibrated, uncertainty-aware yield curves [45]. These prospective research directions would preserve the classifier-agnostic interface while adding principled uncertainty quantification and utility-based threshold selection; a full development is left for future work.

Conclusion

Manual review of unstructured clinical notes remains a major barrier to scalable retrospective cohort identification, particularly in contexts requiring high specificity. While NLP models can rank candidate records for review, the actual cost and yield of manual validation remains difficult to forecast, particularly when models are applied to newer datasets. In the present study, the authors addressed this challenge by introducing a regression-based framework that predicts annotation yield at each point in a relevance-ranked list of EHR records. Our approach supports data-driven stopping rules that align review effort with expected benefit.

Using manually annotated notes from 2013 to train predictive models and prospectively evaluating performance of these models on the 2020 data, the authors found that yield prediction is feasible and strongly correlates with model-assigned scores, even in the presence of temporal distribution shifts. Among the tested models, polynomial regression was found to offer the best balance of generalization and interpretability, while the neural network, despite its strong in-sample performance, proved to be more vulnerable to temporal overfitting.

These findings highlight that the proposed screening saturation framework offers a practical way to manage the resource burden of manual review under conditions of model decay and uncertainty. By estimating the marginal return on continued review, the proposed framework enables tailored stopping decisions, for example, identifying ~ 78% of positive cases after reviewing ~ 15% of the dataset. The proposed approach moves beyond fixed or heuristic stopping rules, supporting a more transparent, reproducible, and resource-sensitive cohort construction. Finally, since the proposed method is classifier-agnostic, it can be integrated into various CITL NLP systems and applied meaningfully across multiple clinical domains. As retrospective studies increasingly rely on AI-assisted workflows, the proposed framework may prove essential for making the corresponding workflows more efficient, scalable, and responsive to real-world constraints.
