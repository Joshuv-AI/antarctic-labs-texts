Comparison of maternal and perinatal outcomes of multifetal pregnancy reduction versus conservative management in triplet pregnancies
Authors: Hale Cetin Arslan, Kadir Arslan, Tugce Arslanoglu, Alev Atis Aydin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increased use of assisted reproductive technologies (ART) has led to an increase in the frequency of multiple pregnancies. Multiple pregnancies are associated with higher maternal and fetal morbidity compared to singleton pregnancies. Multifetal pregnancy reduction (MFPR) can be performed to reduce these risks. This study aimed to compare the maternal and perinatal outcomes of triplet pregnancies undergoing MFPR with those followed without intervention. This retrospective cohort study was conducted in a tertiary obstetrics and gynecology clinic over 10 years (January 2014–January 2024). All triplet pregnancies received counseling regarding MFPR. Pregnancies reduced from triplets to twins constituted the MFPR group, while those followed without intervention constituted the conservative management group. Demographic data, obstetric history, and pregnancy outcomes were compared using appropriate statistical tests. A total of 187 triplet pregnancies (MFPR, n = 47; conservative management, n = 140) were examined. No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of maternal age, parity, history of miscarriage or preterm birth (P > .05). The gestational age at delivery was higher in the MFPR (35.2 vs 32.2 wk, P = .003). In triplet pregnancies, conservative treatment was an independent risk factor for delivery at <34 weeks (OR 2.4), cesarean delivery (OR 3.4), and need for NICU (OR 10.6) (all P < .05). Neonatal mortality was 5% in the conservative management and not in the MFPR. The MFPR procedure of triplets can significantly prolong gestational age, reduce the need for NICU, and improve perinatal prognosis. Because it provides these benefits without increasing maternal complications, MFPR should be offered as an option to patients and their families in the first trimester.

1. Introduction

Triplets and higher multiple pregnancies account for 3% of all live births.[1] The triplet pregnancy rate in 2009 was 153.4 per 100,000 births.[2] In 2018, the rate was 93 per 100,000 births, representing a 52.2% decrease in incidence compared to the peak years.[3] The widespread use of assisted reproductive technologies (ART) and advanced maternal age at conception are the 2 most important causes of multiple pregnancy occurrence. The main problem in multiple pregnancies is the severe fetal consequences, such as spontaneous preterm birth, related issues caused by prematurity, and mortality.[4] As the number of fetuses rises, the risk of adverse fetal outcomes also rises. Perinatal mortality is 4 times higher in twins and 6 times higher in triplets, and the risk of cerebral palsy is 5 to 10 times higher in multiple pregnancies compared to singleton pregnancies.[5,6] In addition, high multiple pregnancies are associated with adverse maternal outcomes such as hypertension, gestational diabetes, anemia, and the risks of complications of surgery.[7]

Studies show that monozygotic twinning occurs more frequently in ART treatments, with women under the age of 35 and those undergoing blastocyst transfer showing a higher propensity for monozygotic twinning.[8] Multiple embryo transfer methods have been employed in the past to increase pregnancy rates[9,10]; however, current guidelines recommend either selective embryo transfer or the transfer of a maximum of 2 embryos. Another method suggested to reduce the number of fetuses is the multifetal pregnancy reduction (MFPR) procedure, which has been in use since 1980.[11] The success of MFPR in improving obstetric outcomes, especially in quadruplet or higher pregnancies, has been reported in the literature.[12] The loss rate in 1000 consecutive MFPR cases was found to be 4.7%, and it has been shown that the highest preterm birth rates occur when pregnancies are reduced to singletons.[13]

Since MFPR involves several medical, sociopsychological, and ethical issues, explaining and applying the technique to couples undergoing ART treatment is complex. In societies where acceptance is difficult due to cultural beliefs, clinicians must offer effective counseling to couples, guided by current data.

This study investigated the maternal and fetal outcomes of triplet pregnancies managed with MFPR and conservative management at a tertiary center.

2. Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Istanbul Kanuni Sultan Süleyman Training and Research Hospital (date: July 11, 2024; KAEK/2024.07.132). Patients diagnosed with triplet pregnancies and followed until birth at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinic of Istanbul Kanuni Sultan Süleyman Training and Research Hospital, University of the Health Sciences, between January 2014 and January 2024, were included in the study.

Inclusion criteria: Age 18 years and older; triplet pregnancy diagnosis between 11 and 16 weeks of gestation. Exclusion criteria: Patients who were reduced from triplet to singleton pregnancies due to multiple anomalies; patients who were reduced from high-order pregnancies (quadruplets, quintuplets, etc) to triplets through MFPR; and missing data.

Patients’ age, gravida, parity, comorbid diseases, history of miscarriage and preterm birth, conception method, chorionicity, gestational age at birth, fetal malformations, twin–twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), preterm premature rupture of membranes, need for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), intrauterine fetal death, perinatal mortalities, and the number of live-born fetuses were documented. Patient data were obtained from follow-up forms and the hospital information system. Triplet pregnancies were divided into 2 groups: the no-intervention group (conservative management group) and the pregnancies that were reduced from triplets to twins (MFPR group).

In our clinic, all triplet pregnancies receive detailed counseling regarding MFPR upon their initial presentation. The week of pregnancy is determined by considering the current status of the pregnancy, additional diagnostic requirements, and patient preference. MFPR is generally performed between weeks 11 and 14 of pregnancy, following a first-trimester screening test or chorionic villus sampling. The literature indicates that this timeframe minimizes the risk of post-procedural pregnancy loss.[14] In select cases, the procedure can be postponed until the 23rd week for clinical reasons (patient late presentation, difficulties with genetic testing, etc); the literature also reports that interventions performed in this situation, also known as late reduction, may be associated with an increase in premature birth and adverse neonatal outcomes.[15] In our clinic, MFPR was performed after the first trimester screening test or chorionic villus sampling and nuchal translucency measurement between the 12th and 16th weeks of pregnancy. This was due to situations such as late application of patients or delay in decision-making by the patient and family due to cultural or religious reasons, after counseling. After performing antisepsis, an intracardiac or intrathoracic injection of 1 to 10 mL of 10% potassium chloride is administered using a 20 to 22 G needle under transabdominal ultrasonography guidance. The needle is removed once asystole is confirmed. After the procedure, the mother receives antibiotic therapy.

Triplet pregnancies are managed conservatively, and pregnancies undergoing MFPR are monitored until birth in the same manner, in our clinic. Fetal development and cervical length are tracked with serial ultrasounds every 2 weeks. If indicated, based on the evaluation of the biophysical profile, triplet pregnancies are delivered at or before the 32nd week; uncomplicated triplet pregnancies are delivered by the 36th week.

In the retrospective cohort study, which included triplet pregnancies over 10 years, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. An attempt was made to contact all patients during the study period.

2.1. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Inc., Chicago, (SPSS v26.0). The data’s conformity to a normal distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilks test, histogram, skewness, and kurtosis. Descriptive statistics were expressed as number of patients, percentage, mean ± standard deviation (minimum-maximum), median (interquartile range, Q1–Q3). The Mann–Whitney U test was used in comparisons between 2 groups that did not show normal distribution, and the independent sample t-test was used in cases where the distribution was normal. The Pearson chi-square test and Fisher exact test were used to determine the relationship between qualitative data. Variables determined to be significant in triplet pregnancies undergoing MFPR compared to those under conservative management were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis to determine independent risk factors. Significance was accepted at P < .05.

3. Results

A total of 195 pregnant women diagnosed with triplet pregnancy and who delivered between January 2014 and January 2024 were included in the study. 5 patients with missing data and 3 patients who were reduced from triplets to a singleton pregnancy due to multiple anomalies were excluded. A total of 187 patients were included in the study, comprising those managed conservatively (n = 140) and those who were reduced from triplet to twin pregnancy by MFPR (n = 47) (Fig. 1).

The median ages and parities of the MFPR and the conservative management were found to be similar (30, P = .910; 0.6 ± 0.8, P = .823, respectively). The prior abortion rate was higher in the MFPR than in the conservative management, but it was not statistically significant (42.6% vs 30.0%, P = .114). There was no significant difference in the history of preterm delivery between the groups (P = .733). Among the methods of conception, IVF/ICSI was the most common in the MFPR (36.2% vs 37.1%, P = .905); spontaneous pregnancy was the most common in the conservative management (42.1% vs 34.0%, P = .327). Trichorionic triamniotic (TT) chorionicity was observed at a significantly higher rate in the MFPR compared to the conservative management (78.7% vs 57.9%, P = .010). Fetal malformation rates were significantly higher in the MFPR (25.5% vs 7.1%, P = .001). Pregestational diabetes and chronic hypertension showed no significant difference between groups (P = 1.000) (Table 1).

The median gestational week at diagnosis in the MFPR was 12 (range, 11.2–13.2), while it was 13.6 (range, 11.3–15.3) in the conservative management; a significant difference was observed between the groups (P < .001). The gestational age at birth is notably greater in the MFPR (35.2 vs 32.2, P = .003). Only 25.5% (n = 12) of the MFPR and 53.6% (n = 75) of the conservative management delivered before 34 weeks of gestation (P = .001). There were no deliveries before the 20th and 30th weeks of gestation in the MFPR. Pregnancy outcomes like preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and postpartum hemorrhage showed no differences between the groups (P = .495, P = 1.000, P = 1.000, respectively). The conservative management experienced higher cesarean section rates (86.4% vs 74.5%, P = .056). IUGR and the need for NICU were significantly higher in the conservative management (P = .006, P < .001, respectively). Neonatal mortality was 5% in conservative management and was not observed in the MFPR (P < .001) (Table 2).

4. Discussion

This retrospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary center examined pregnancy and maternal and fetal outcomes in patients who underwent MFPR with conservative management in triplet pregnancies. No significant difference was found between the groups regarding conception methods. IVF/ICSI was the most common method of pregnancy in the MFPR, while spontaneous pregnancies were more common in the conservative management. Although this difference was not statistically significant, it is thought that the decision to undergo MFPR is more frequently considered in pregnancies conceived through assisted reproductive technologies due to the higher incidence of multiple pregnancies. In spontaneous pregnancies, the patient profile is generally younger, and different clinical approaches may be needed to determine the need for intervention. The gestational age at delivery was higher in the MFPR group, and intrauterine pregnancy loss was 2.1%. No neonatal mortality was observed in the MFPR group. Multivariate regression analysis of the study revealed that conservative management in triplet pregnancies was associated with a 2.4-fold increase in the likelihood of delivery before 34 weeks, a 3.4-fold increase in the likelihood of cesarean delivery, and a 10.6-fold increase in the need for NICU, all independent risk factors.

The rate of multiple pregnancies from ART and older maternal age has decreased due to specific measures. This may be because of limits on the number of embryos transferred and increased use of MFPR procedures.[16] However, the number of fertilized or implanted embryos cannot be controlled with the gonadotropins used for controlled ovarian hyperstimulation in fertility treatment.[17] Additionally, the literature reports that the spontaneous pregnancy rate in dichorionic triplet (DT) pregnancies is 44%.[18] In our study, the spontaneous pregnancy rate in all triplet pregnancies was found to be 40.1% (n = 75), which is similar across cases.

Chorionicity is another factor influencing perinatal outcomes in triplet pregnancies. Shared vascular anastomoses in monochorionic twins can result in adverse effects like TTTS, selective IUGR, and acute fetofetal hemorrhage. Early diagnosis and management of IUGR are essential in preventing morbidity and mortality.[19] When examining the relationship between chorionicity and perinatal outcome in triplet pregnancies, it was found that perinatal mortality was 2.6 times higher in monochorionic triplets (MT) compared to trichorionic triplets (TT), and prophylactic cerclage did not decrease this risk.[20] MFPR have been proposed as a way to lower the risks related to multiple pregnancies. The primary aim is to decrease the chance of early preterm birth.

Besides discussing conservative management or MFPR procedures in triplets, the topic of reducing the number to twins or singletons also piques interest. Studies indicate that the risk of early preterm birth drops by 10 to 18% when reduced to twins and by 5 to 8% when reduced to singleton pregnancies.[16,21] In triplet pregnancies with MT pairs, the MFPR procedure is either performed on the monochorionic pair, leaving the singleton pregnancy untouched, or on the other fetus, leaving the monochorionic twin intact. Separating from a monochorionic twin requires careful monitoring for potential complications. MFPR is performed through fetal intracardiac injection of KCl into fetuses of pregnancies with separate placentas, such as TT or non-monochorionic DT triplets. In DT triplet pregnancies with monochorionic fetuses and MT triplet pregnancies, the MFPR procedure is performed using bipolar cord coagulation or radiofrequency ablation of the umbilical cord. However, depending on the method used, the chance of a 5 to 15% decrease in the MFPR result should also be taken into account.[22] In a meta-analysis of 893 triplet pregnancies, MFPR was linked to a higher miscarriage rate (8.1% vs 4.4%) and a lower rate of early preterm birth (10.4% vs 26.7%) than conservative management.[23] In our study, no miscarriage occurred in the MFPR; however, the miscarriage rate was 7.9% in the conservative management.

It is effective for prognosis in triplet pregnancies, especially in DT, TTTS, anemia-polycythemia syndrome, and risks of selective IUGR. The rate of TTTS in triplet pregnancies ranged from 5.1 to 5.95% in one study, and this rate reached up to 24.0% in another study.[24,25] In our study, the TTTS rate was 6.4% in those who received conservative management and 6.3% in the MFPR, showing that MFPR had no preventive effect on TTTS. However, MFPR was found to significantly reduce the development of IUGR and the need for NICU, and it did not impact pregnancy outcomes such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, or postpartum hemorrhage.

Over the past 30 years, the average delivery week for triplet pregnancies has been around 32 to 33 weeks.[26] The importance of delivering TT triplets between 35 and 0/7 weeks and 35 and 6/7 weeks was emphasized to reduce perinatal morbidity caused by prematurity.[17] In our study, it was observed that none of the patients who underwent MFPR delivered before 20 or 30 weeks of gestation. It was found that MFPR patients delivered significantly later than 34 weeks compared to those who underwent conservative management, with an average gestational age at birth of 35.2 weeks.

Although MFPR can improve perinatal prognosis, it carries several risks. The rate of pregnancy loss after the procedure has been reported as 2 to 9% in the literature.[27] Other possible complications include amniotic fluid leakage, infection, hemorrhage, preterm labor, growth restriction in remaining fetuses, and, rarely, uterine irritability.[28,29] Furthermore, there is a risk of acute fetofetal transfusion due to vascular anastomoses, especially in triplet pregnancies involving monochorionic twins. Razaz et al concluded that perinatal mortality was 10% in triplet pregnancies, 5.6% when they were reduced from triplets to twins, and there was no mortality when reduced to singleton pregnancies.[30] In our study, intrauterine fetal death (>16 gw) was limited to 2.1% in the MFPR. Neonatal mortality was not observed in MFPR, but was 5.0% in conservative management.

The study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single-center study. Second, it lacks data on the sociodemographic background of the families, the number of fetuses undergoing reduction, timing of fetal reduction, indications for reduction, and the technique used. Additionally, we do not have enough information in the archives to assess how chorionicity influences the outcome of MFPR.

5. Conclusion

In our study, MFPR was found to extend gestational age in triplet pregnancies, reduce the need for NICU and perinatal mortality, and improve perinatal outcomes. The loss rate associated with MFPR was 2.1%. Although MFPR did not significantly affect maternal outcomes or TTTS, it did lower the risk of IUGR. The conservative management increased the likelihood of delivery before 34 weeks of gestation by 2.4 times, the chance of cesarean delivery by 3.4 times, and the need for NICU by 10.6 times. MFPR counseling should be recommended during the first trimester, considering the specific conditions of triplet pregnancies. Research should be conducted to evaluate how the techniques used in MFPR impact clinical outcomes, as this will also enhance the counseling services.
