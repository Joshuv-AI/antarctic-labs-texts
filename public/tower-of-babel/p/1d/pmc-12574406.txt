Effects of nature-based virtual reality interventions on physical and mental health symptoms in patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Surui Liang, Xiaojiao Wang, Jing Jing Su, Winnie Wai In Wong, Rick Yiu Cho Kwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of nature-based virtual reality (NBVR) interventions in alleviating physical symptoms (eg, pain, fatigue, nausea) and mental symptoms (eg, anxiety, depression, distress) in patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy.MethodsThis systematic review protocol is developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. We will conduct a comprehensive electronic search of healthcare databases for studies published in English from 2012 to February 2025. The databases to be searched include PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Allied and Complementary Medicine, CINAHL Complete and Cochrane Library. We will include randomised controlled trials investigating NBVR aimed at improving the physical and psychological outcomes for patients with cancer during chemotherapy, compared with waitlist controls or traditional methods. The risk of bias will be systematically evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool.ResultsAs this is a protocol, no results are available yet. The review will present quantitative and qualitative syntheses of NBVR effects on physical and psychological outcomes in chemotherapy patients.DiscussionBy evaluating rigorously designed trials, this review will clarify the potential of NBVR as a supportive intervention for patients with cancer during chemotherapy. Findings are expected to inform clinical practice and guide future research directions in integrating immersive natural environments into cancer care.ConclusionThis systematic review will provide comprehensive evidence on NBVR interventions for patients with cancer receiving chemotherapy, highlighting their potential benefits for physical symptom relief and mental health improvement.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251016213.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, posing significant challenges to healthcare systems and societies. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, cancer accounted for 14.57% of total deaths and 8.8% of total disability-adjusted life years globally.1 The age-standardised incidence rate was 790.33 per 100 000 people, while the age-standardised death rate was 116.49 per 100 000 people.1 Cancer affects body functions, limits activity and restricts participation, ultimately reducing quality of life and life expectancy.2 Common symptoms among patients with cancer include pain, fatigue, nausea, anxiety, depression and distress, which can persist even after treatment concludes.

Chemotherapy, used in over 60% of cancer cases, employs drugs to destroy cancer cells. While effective, it also damages healthy cells, leading to a range of side effects.3 Patients frequently experience physical symptoms (eg, nausea, fatigue, pain), which can negatively impact their mental health (eg, anxiety, depression).3

Nature-based therapies are interventions that use natural environments and elements, such as green spaces, water and outdoor activities, to promote physical and psychological well-being.4 These therapies have demonstrated effectiveness in improving both physical and mental health in patients with cancer. A scoping review demonstrated that nature-based interventions (eg, forest bathing) significantly improved physiological outcomes in cancer survivors, including a 17.7%–46.9% increase in natural killer cell activity, alongside mental health benefits such as 37% reduction in anxiety symptoms and 28% alleviation of depressive manifestations.4 Despite these benefits, patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy face significant barriers to accessing natural environments. Fatigue, weakened immunity and the demands of frequent medical visits often prevent them from spending time outdoors. Additionally, exposure to natural environments can increase the risk of infection.5

Virtual reality (VR) is defined as an advanced digital technology that generates interactive, computer-simulated environments, enabling users to experience immersive and realistic settings through visual, auditory and sometimes haptic feedback.6 Nature-based virtual reality (NBVR) applies this technology to recreate natural environments, providing individuals with nature exposure in a controlled and accessible manner, which has been shown to promote physical and mental health well-being.7 Clinical trials validate that combining VR with nature (eg, forest essential oils) synergistically reduces chemotherapy-induced fatigue by 42% and anticipatory nausea by 35% compared with VR alone.8 These benefits are supported by established theories, such as the Biophilia Hypothesis, which proposes an innate human connection to nature that fosters well-being9; Kaplan’s Attention Restoration Theory, which suggests that natural environments aid cognitive restoration by reducing mental distress and can alleviate physical symptoms like nausea and fatigue10 and Ulrich’s Stress Reduction Theory, which emphasises nature’s role in alleviating stress, promoting relaxation and reducing pain.11

Recent studies have investigated the use of VR with nature-based themes in cancer care, particularly during chemotherapy, to reduce pain, fatigue, anxiety and distress. A crossover design study exposed patients to nature-based VR during one chemotherapy session while serving as their own controls in a subsequent session without VR, leading to reduced distress symptoms.12 Chirico et al evaluated the impact of nature-based VR and music therapy as distraction interventions, finding that both were effective, although VR demonstrated superior efficacy in alleviating anxiety, depression and fatigue.13 A randomised controlled trial (RCT) involving patients with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy demonstrated that 12 weeks of non-immersive nature-based VR sessions significantly reduced distress and enhanced multiple dimensions of health-related quality of life.14 Despite promising findings, inconsistencies in study outcomes raise questions about the NBVR’s therapeutic effects. Differences in VR content, duration and frequency across studies make it difficult to establish standardised protocols for optimal implementation.

While previous systematic reviews have assessed the general use of VR in cancer care, research specifically focusing on NBVR interventions remains scarce.1518 The specific physical and mental challenges of chemotherapy patients have not been adequately addressed in existing reviews, which have primarily focused on patients with cancer in general.15 16 Moreover, the quality of existing reviews varies, with some including studies that have small sample sizes and high heterogeneity, which weakens the overall strength of the evidence.15 18 Given these gaps, a systematic review is necessary to synthesise existing evidence, identify knowledge gaps and develop standardised, evidence-based guidelines for implementing NBVR interventions in patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy. This review will provide insights into best practices and inform future research and clinical applications in oncology settings.

Objective

The study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of NBVR interventions in alleviating physical symptoms (eg, pain, fatigue, nausea) and mental symptoms (eg, anxiety, depression, distress) in patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy.

Methods

This systematic review will be conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols 2015 checklist (online supplemental material 1) to ensure methodological transparency and rigour.19 Online supplemental figure S1 presents the flow chart of the systematic review process.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible articles must be experimental studies that use NBVR interventions specifically designed for patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy. The Populations, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcomes and Study Designs (PICOS) framework will guide the inclusion of eligible studies.20 The eligibility criteria are discussed in the following sections.

Population (P)

The population of interest includes patients with cancer receiving chemotherapy, regardless of cancer stage, age or gender. The term ‘chemotherapy’ refers to the period of drug administration, such as infusion sessions, or within a chemotherapy cycle, such as the induction phase, whether conducted at home or in a hospital. Exclusion criteria include studies involving patients receiving only radiotherapy or those combining chemotherapy and radiotherapy where the effects of VR on chemotherapy-specific outcomes cannot be isolated. Additionally, studies involving VR interventions during prechemotherapy procedures, such as central venous catheter or peripherally inserted central catheter insertion, as well as studies focused on chemotherapy catheter-related topics, including maintenance or complications, will be excluded. These interventions primarily address procedural anxiety or catheter management rather than the broader therapeutic benefits of NBVR for physical and mental health symptom relief.

Intervention (I)

The intervention must incorporate both VR and nature-based themes, such as parks, oceans or forests. VR requirements include a device-generated immersive environment that engages at least the visual and auditory senses. Nature-based elements may include landscapes, natural sounds like waves or birdsong or simulated outdoor activities such as diving or stargazing. VR experiences that involve single-character interactions without a natural environment backdrop are excluded.

Comparator (C)

Control groups must not receive VR or nature-based therapy. Permitted comparators include routine care, such as pharmacological or non-pharmacological therapies, psychoeducation, relaxation techniques or other conventional non-VR interventions.

Outcomes (O)

The outcomes to be measured include physiological or psychological symptoms related to cancer or chemotherapy, such as pain, fatigue, nausea, anxiety or depression.

Study design (S)

Only RCTs will be included. Non-RCTs, other experimental studies, controlled pre-post studies, feasibility studies without control groups, study protocols, opinion pieces and conference papers will be excluded.

Information sources

Databases

PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Allied and Complementary Medicine, CINAHL Complete and Cochrane Library.

Literature search

We will systematically search the following electronic healthcare databases for studies published in English from 2012 to February 2025, with full texts available. The year 2012 was selected as it represents the advent of accessible, high-quality VR technology, which enabled more rigorous and widespread research into VR-based interventions.21

The following keywords and their synonyms will be used to develop the search strategy: “virtual reality”, “nature”, “cancer” and “chemotherapy”. A comprehensive search strategy has been developed and is provided in online supplemental material 2, detailing the exact search terms and Boolean operators used for each of the four key concepts. In addition, full search strings for each database are provided, incorporating both Medical Subject Headings terms (where applicable) and free-text terms to account for each database’s indexing and search functionalities.

Study records

The selection of studies will be conducted through three phases: screening, eligibility assessment and inclusion in meta-analysis. Two independent reviewers will conduct the initial screening by reviewing titles and abstracts based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies will be retrieved for a detailed assessment of eligibility. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to reach a consensus.

Data records and management will be meticulously conducted to ensure the accuracy and integrity of the extracted information. A data extraction form will be designed to collect relevant information from the included studies. The extracted data will encompass basic information (author, publication year, country/region), participant characteristics (sample size, grouping, mean age, gender), intervention characteristics (study design, specific intervention and control methods, duration of NBVR intervention) and outcome measures (result measurement methods, data type, statistical data and results). The Template for Intervention Description and Replication checklist will be used to ensure comprehensive extraction of intervention details.22 This checklist includes elements such as the intervention’s brief name, rationale, materials and procedures, the deliverer, delivery method, location, frequency and duration, tailoring, modifications and adherence. Data extraction will be independently conducted by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Extracted data will be input into Review Manager (RevMan) V.5.3 software for meta-analysis.

Risk of bias in individual studies

The risk of bias in included studies will be assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs. Disagreements between reviewers will be resolved by discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. The overall risk of bias for each study will be categorised as low, moderate or high based on the individual domain assessments.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis will initially be provided in the text and tables to summarise the characteristics and findings of the included studies. For the same study outcome, if at least three independent studies report relevant data (eg, means, SD, sample sizes), we will pool these data for meta-analysis using the RevMan software. For dichotomous outcomes, the effect sizes will be expressed as ORs with 95% CIs, while for continuous outcomes, standard mean differences (SMD) with 95% CI will be used. Statistical heterogeneity between studies will be assessed using the I2 statistic. A random-effects model will be applied when I2 >50%. Heterogeneity will be addressed using common approaches, including sensitivity analyses (eg, excluding studies at high risk of bias or applying alternative statistical models) and subgroup analyses (when sufficient studies are available, n ≥3 per subgroup).23 Publication bias will be examined using funnel plots and Egger’s test for meta-analyses that include at least 10 studies.

Certainty of findings

We will use the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations approach to assess the certainty of the evidence for each important outcome. The assessment will consider factors such as study limitations, inconsistency of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision and potential publication bias. The certainty of evidence will be categorised as high, moderate, low or very low. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion among reviewers.

Ethics and dissemination

This review does not require ethics approval. The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at relevant conferences.
