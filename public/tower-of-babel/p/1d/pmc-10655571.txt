Impact of the Hunga Tonga volcanic eruption on stratospheric composition
Authors: David M. Wilmouth, Freja F. Østerstrøm, Jessica B. Smith, James G. Anderson, Ross J. Salawitch
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai (HTHH) volcanic eruption on 15 January 2022 is unprecedented in the modern era in both the amount of water vapor injected into the stratosphere and the depth of penetration. The perturbation to water vapor and sulfate aerosols from this volcano significantly altered temperatures, dynamics, and chemistry in the stratosphere, including reductions in stratospheric column ozone, which is essential for protecting life on Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation. Here, the global evolution of the injected water vapor in latitude, altitude, and time is analyzed for the year following the eruption using satellite data. Significant changes in the concentrations of various chemical compounds in the stratosphere are identified and linked to the HTHH eruption.

The Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai (HTHH) volcanic eruption (20.54° S, 175.38° W) on 15 January 2022 produced a shock wave felt around the world (1) and released more water vapor into the stratosphere than any other volcano on record (2, 3). The powerful eruption sent aerosols, gases, and water in both vapor and condensed phases into the stratosphere with some material reaching over 56 km high into the lower mesosphere, altitudes never before reached from a volcanic eruption in the satellite era (4, 5). Observations from the Microwave Limb Sounder (MLS) aboard the NASA Aura satellite immediately following the eruption indicated that this single event increased water vapor in Earth’s normally dry stratosphere by approximately 10% (2). As a consequence of the HTHH eruption, water vapor levels in the stratosphere will likely stay elevated for years, potentially changing surface temperatures (2) and impacting stratospheric circulation (6) and chemistry (7).

A volcanic eruption of the character of HTHH was unexpected. Typically, the primary influence that an explosive volcanic eruption may have on the stratosphere is through injection of sulfur dioxide (SO2) and subsequent formation of sulfate aerosols. The June 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo (15.14° N, 120.35° E) in the Philippines, the largest and best-characterized explosive volcanic eruption of the satellite era, injected an estimated 14 to 23 Teragrams (Tg) of SO2 into the stratosphere (8–10). In comparison, the total amount of SO2 emitted into the stratosphere from HTHH was estimated to be more than an order of magnitude less at 0.41 Tg (2). The stratospheric SO2 from the Mount Pinatubo eruption produced a global sulfate aerosol veil that altered atmospheric circulation patterns (11–15), catalyzed heterogeneous chemical cycles (16–20), and contributed to widespread, years-long column ozone reductions (20–25). The vast majority of the impact was confined to the Northern Hemisphere (NH), where the eruption occurred.

The character of the HTHH eruption differs significantly from Mount Pinatubo in that there was less SO2 and dramatically more water vapor injected into the stratosphere, but the potential for widespread stratospheric changes, as seen in the years following the Mount Pinatubo eruption, is similar. It has recently been shown that the massive co-injection of water vapor with SO2 from the HTHH eruption led to substantially more stratospheric aerosol loading than would have otherwise been realized from the modest SO2 injection alone (7). Because HTHH was an undersea eruption, a large amount of seawater and associated chemical compounds such as sea salt may have also been injected into the atmosphere. The large perturbation to stratospheric water vapor has been shown to have decreased stratospheric temperatures (26), and the elevated sulfate and water vapor have the potential to accelerate heterogeneous and gas-phase chemical reactions and cause changes in stratospheric chemical composition for a number of compounds, including ozone. The focus of this study is to examine the evolution of the injected water vapor in the stratosphere over time, altitude, and latitude in the year following the HTHH eruption, to identify a number of observed changes to stratospheric chemical composition, and to examine these changes using a 3-dimensional chemistry–climate–aerosol model and observational tracer correlations.

Results and Discussion

Spatiotemporal Evolution of HTHH-Injected Water Vapor.

The explosive eruption of HTHH injected the majority of the water into the stratosphere between 20 and 40 hPa, peaking at 26 hPa (~26 km), in the Southern Hemisphere (SH) near 20° S latitude. The water vapor mixed into the background stratosphere and quickly spread longitudinally around the globe. To examine how the injected water vapor moved as a function of time, latitude, and altitude after the eruption, we first consider the evolution of elevated water vapor at the initial 26-hPa peak mixing ratio as measured by the MLS instrument onboard the NASA Aura satellite. Fig. 1 tracks the movement of the stratospheric water vapor anomaly with latitude, showing global maps (for all longitudes and 76° S to 76° N latitudes) over selected months following the HTHH eruption from February to December 2022. To better visualize the movement of the higher water vapor air, only mixing ratios 7 ppm and larger are shown; the background mixing ratio is normally 4 to 5 ppm at this pressure level.

Longitude is on the x axis and latitude is on the y axis, both in degrees. The mixing ratio is indicated by the color bar on the right side of each panel; note the changes in scaling.

As seen in the Upper Left panel of Fig. 1 for February 2022, elevated water vapor was uniformly present around the globe longitudinally soon after the eruption but was primarily confined to a relatively narrow band in latitude between approximately 30° S and 15° N. In March and continuing in April (Upper Right panel), the water vapor enhancement spread farther north and south. April was the month in 2022 with the most water vapor values in excess of 7 ppm at 26 hPa near 30° N latitude. Starting in May, the band of water vapor greater than 7 ppm was shifted southward in both hemispheres relative to April. By June (Middle Left panel), the water vapor values greater than 7 ppm in the NH were mostly near the equator, while in the SH, nearly the entire region from 0 to 60° S had elevated water vapor.

The maps for July and August as well as September (Fig. 1, Middle Right panel) do not vary dramatically, as the poleward movement of the elevated water vapor in the SH appears to stall at around 60° S. This transport barrier is significant and was due to the presence of the Antarctic vortex in the austral winter/spring in the polar region. In the NH, essentially no values of water vapor in excess of 7 ppm at 26 hPa were present by the end of the boreal summer. Nine months after the eruption, in October (Lower Left panel), the band of water vapor over 7 ppm at 26 hPa is entirely in the SH and moving below 60° S as penetration into the area of the vortex becomes significant. The poleward motion steadily continues in the austral spring and by December (Lower Right panel), nearly all of the water vapor over 7 ppm at 26 hPa is at mid- and high latitudes in the SH.

Fig. 1 represents a highly informative picture of the movement of elevated water vapor with latitude following the HTHH eruption. However, the details of this picture do vary with the threshold mixing ratio and the pressure level used in the analysis. For example, if 6 ppm were used as the threshold at 26 hPa rather than 7 ppm, water vapor penetration poleward of 60° S is seen a month earlier in September. If 22 hPa were used as the pressure level rather than 26 hPa, with a threshold of 6 ppm, penetration poleward of 60° S began in August. Immediately apparent in Fig. 2 are 1) the unprecedented water vapor enhancements in the MLS record in 2022, 2) the persistence of the enhancements in a latitude region once perturbed, and 3) the significant variation in the altitude of enhancement between latitude regions. Focusing first on Panel A, the water vapor profile shown includes the location of HTHH and is very close in time to the eruption. The February water vapor measurement for 2022 is greater than the 17-y mean over the entire vertical profile and is substantially greater between pressures of approximately 10 and 40 hPa. The peak value at 26 hPa is 11 ppm, which is about 2.8 times greater than the 17-y mean of 3.9 ppm. There is also a second, distinct and significant, lower altitude enhancement in the 68 to 83 hPa range, representing water vapor ~25% above the 17-y February mean.

Examination of the other latitude ranges shown in Fig. 2 (left column) reveals that the 0 to 30° N latitude (E) is the only other range that experienced substantially enhanced water vapor for February 2022. This finding is somewhat surprising since the 30 to 60° S region (I) is closer to the 20° S latitude of eruption. The enhancement in the 30 to 60° S region in April (J) is most similar to 0 to 30° N in February. Significant water vapor enhancement at the highest latitudes in the SH did not begin until October, consistent with Fig. 1, and was quite substantial by December (P). Complete monthly vertical profiles of MLS water vapor for 2005 to 2022 for all 4 latitude ranges are shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S4.

Considering the time evolution of the water vapor enhancements with altitude in Fig. 2, it is clear that the behavior in the tropics is distinct from mid- and high latitudes. Specifically, the elevated water vapor slowly moved upward for 0 to 30° S (A–D) and 0 to 30° N (E–H), appearing as a pronounced sharper peak beginning in December for the SH tropics and September for the NH tropics. The 30 to 60° S region (I–L) is very stable vertically with respect to the water vapor anomaly, having a relatively invariant peak at 21 to 26 hPa for the entire year. The water vapor enhancement in the 60 to 82° S range (M–P) was not present long enough to discern a trend, but the peak value in December at 26 hPa is present at substantially lower altitude than the peak at the tropical latitudes of 10 hPa. All of these vertical changes are qualitatively consistent with the large-scale Brewer–Dobson circulation of the stratosphere, which has rising motion in the tropics, followed by poleward movement of air and descent (27, 28). One year after the eruption, stratospheric water vapor levels are elevated across most of the globe.

To quantify the evolution of the elevated stratospheric water vapor from the HTHH eruption, we determined the mass of water vapor as a function of latitude and time. Specifically, we evaluated the mass of water vapor present from 1.2 to 100 hPa in 6 latitude bins covering the globe (0 to 30°, 30 to 60°, 60 to 82° in both hemispheres) for each month February to December 2022. This computation was accomplished using vertical profile data from MLS, similar to those shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S4, and is described in the Materials and Methods section. We find that the monthly mean data in 2021 show water vapor values larger than the 17-y mean, implying that the HTHH eruption injected water vapor into a background stratosphere that was already wetter than average, particularly in the lower stratosphere. Notably, our calculation of the water vapor enhancement for 2021 relative to the 17-y mean suggests that the background water vapor in the stratosphere was likely at least 40 Tg higher in early 2022 prior to the eruption of HTHH than the 2005 to 2021 average (SI Appendix, Table S1). To account for this difference, we utilize monthly 2021 data rather than the monthly 17-y mean values as the background when computing water vapor enhancements for 2022. It is necessary to compare monthly data rather than simply using Dec 2021 or early January 2022 as the reference value for this analysis due to the significant seasonal cycle of stratospheric water vapor.

The water vapor mass anomalies are shown in Table 1 for the six latitude ranges, along with the global sum of the individual monthly values. Fig. 3 shows the percentage of enhanced water vapor mass located in three latitude bins (one for NH and two for SH), with values calculated relative to the sums from Table 1. Several details are apparent: 1) the steady decrease over time in water vapor mass in the 0 to 30° S range where the eruption occurred, 2) the relative stability of the water vapor mass over the entire year in the 0 to 30° N range, and 3) the delayed then increasing impact on the other four latitude ranges given in Table 1. Specifically, the water vapor mass fell every month from February to December in the 0 to 30° S range, decreasing from 116 to 43 Tg (80 to 32% of the global total). At 0 to 30° N, the calculated mass was between 25 and 30 Tg every month, representing approximately 20% of the sum. Meanwhile, the mass of water vapor located in the 30 to 60° S range increased from 4 to 47 Tg between February and July and stayed relatively stable in the 46 to 50 Tg range (approximately 35% of the total) for the following 4 mo until falling in December as the 60 to 82° S mass increased. Again, the presence of the Antarctic vortex contributed to this apparent stalling of the water vapor in the 30 to 60° S range in the austral winter/spring. The impact of HTHH on the 30 to 60° N and 60 to 82° N ranges is small, noting that we exclude water vapor data at pressures >50 hPa for latitudes >30° N, which appear elevated throughout 2022 independent of the HTHH eruption. Overall, we find an average calculated water mass distribution between hemispheres of approximately 78% SH and 22% NH for 2022.

Based on the monthly sums, the total water vapor mass changed very little in the year following the eruption, meaning that the water vapor primarily stayed in the vapor phase and remained in the stratosphere. Using data from the months of February and March, the total water vapor mass injected from HTHH was 145 Tg. One source of uncertainty is the accurate identification of the background water vapor and whether anomalies were caused by the HTHH eruption or other factors. Second, the bimodal vertical structure of the water vapor enhancement as shown in Fig. 2 has the most dramatic perturbation in the upper stratosphere, but the second peak in the lower stratosphere contributes disproportionately to the uncertainty because the mixing ratio represents a larger mass at these pressures. Accounting for these factors using our described method with 2021 data as the reference for 2022, we report a total mass of water vapor injected from the HTHH eruption of 145 ± 7 Tg. This result is consistent with the initial mass estimate from Millán et al. (2) of at least 146 Tg and provides significantly greater constraint than an initial estimate from balloon measurements of at least 50 Tg (3).

Finally, we note that this analysis assumes that monthly water vapor masses in 2021 represent accurate background values for 2022 independent of the HTHH eruption. For reference, comparisons of 2021 to 2020 monthly water vapor masses show very good consistency between the 2 y for the 8 mo January to August; however, the global mass in 2021 was approximately 21 Tg higher than 2020 on average in the September to December time frame. Assuming this 1-y enhancement carried forward into early 2022, our estimate of the water vapor initially injected from the HTHH volcanic eruption would be 124 Tg rather than 145 Tg, a value in agreement with the recent estimate of HTHH-injected water vapor of 119 ± 6 Tg from Khaykin et al. (29). However, Khaykin et al. (29) also note that various higher initial mass estimates for injected water vapor are possible, up to 157 ± 7 Tg, depending on their methodology and which satellite data version is used. Uncertainty in the total injected mass of water is not expected to substantially alter the relative distribution of elevated water vapor in latitude and time as shown in Fig. 3.

Impacts of the HTHH Eruption on Stratospheric Chemical Composition.

To explore the impact of the HTHH eruption on stratospheric chemical composition, Fig. 4 shows MLS monthly mean vertical profiles of water vapor, temperature (T), chlorine monoxide (ClO), ozone (O3), nitric acid (HNO3), and hydrogen chloride (HCl) for the month of April from 2005 to 2022. Panels G–L display difference plots, calculated by subtracting the 17-y mean values from the individual vertical profiles. For reference, the analogous plot to Fig. 4 with all variables at 0 to 45° S is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S5. The blue lines in the lower panels represent ±2σ from the 17-y mean.

Fig. 4 demonstrates significant perturbations to stratospheric composition in 2022, unlike anything seen previously in the MLS record (Panels G–L, red vs. gray lines). At the 22 to 26 hPa water vapor peak, relative to the 17-y mean, temperatures are reduced by approximately 2 K, ClO is elevated by 18 ppt, O3 is lower by 160 ppb, HNO3 is higher by 0.7 ppb, and HCl is lower by 32 ppt. The deviations represent changes of +36% in ClO, –3.1% in O3 (considering either 0 to 30° S or 0 to 45° S), +18% in HNO3, and –3.1% in HCl. The ClO data are presented as combined day and night averages, consistent with the other variables; plotting only daytime values would further increase the calculated anomalies in ClO. The MLS retrieval of ClO is reported at half the number of pressure levels as H2O, T, and O3, so while the peak shapes cannot match exactly, correlations and anticorrelations are clear. Nitric acid in particular shows a broad vertical region of increase. Other than HCl, all of these anomalies lie more than 2σ outside the mean of previous years for each variable and, taken collectively, represent an unmistakable response to the HTHH volcanic eruption.

Analyses of the temperature changes have been conducted previously (6, 26). Here, we focus on investigation of the chemistry and composition by examining simulations conducted using the SOCOL-AERv2 chemistry–climate–aerosol model, as described in the Materials and Methods section. We simulated the HTHH eruption with an injection of 150 Tg water vapor and 0.41 Tg SO2 into the stratosphere (2) on 15 January 2022, tailoring the injection profile and height to match the zonally averaged, monthly mean MLS water vapor profile for February 2022. Fig. 5 shows anomaly plots for 0 to 30° S from the simulation, for the same six variables as in Fig. 4. In addition, several other modeled variables are shown: NOx (NO + NO2), N2O5, HOx (OH + HO2), and HOCl. [In this work, NOx, HOx, and ClOx (Cl + ClO) represent notations for chemical families capable of destroying ozone.] Here, the anomalies are defined as the differences between 30-member ensemble model runs for 2022 in which HTHH erupted and analogous ensemble runs for 2022 in which HTHH did not erupt.

Modeled zonal mean, monthly average vertical anomalies (A–J) for 0 to 30° S (as in Fig. 4) using the SOCOL-AERv2 chemistry–climate–aerosol model initiated with 150 Tg H2O and 0.41 Tg SO2 to simulate the impact of the HTHH volcanic eruption. The anomalies are calculated as the difference between a 30-member model ensemble for 2022 with the HTHH eruption and an ensemble excluding the eruption.

Apparent in Fig. 5 is that near the maximum HTHH perturbation as indicated by the water vapor peak, the model shows lower temperatures, elevated ClO, reduced O3 (with higher altitude increases), increased HNO3, and reduced HCl, all qualitatively consistent with the satellite observations in Fig. 4. Beyond these changes, the model shows decreases in NOx and N2O5 and increases in HOx and HOCl in response to the stratospheric perturbation from the HTHH eruption. The primary cause of each HTHH-induced anomaly in Fig. 5 was verified with H2O-only and SO2-only model runs as detailed in SI Appendix, Table S2. These simulations show that the anomalies in temperature and HCl soon after the HTHH eruption are primarily due to the addition of H2O, whereas the changes in HNO3, NOx, and N2O5 are primarily initiated by the addition of SO2. The anomalies in ClO, O3, HOx, and HOCl are significantly influenced by the volcanic injection of both SO2 and H2O. Following SI Appendix, Table S2 and its associated text are additional model-measurement comparison details for Figs. 4 and 5, notes on model performance, and model results for the distribution of sulfate in the year after the eruption (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Our focus below is identifying the reactions responsible for the HTHH-induced anomalies.

The N2O5 hydrolysis reaction is an important heterogeneous reaction that occurs on the surface of sulfate aerosols and forms two molecules of nitric acid, HNO3. The hydrolysis of N2O5 was shown to be very significant in perturbing stratospheric chemistry following the Mount Pinatubo eruption (16, 18). Of more minor importance, the heterogeneous BrONO2 hydrolysis reaction, which forms HOBr and HNO3, is also enhanced in the presence of elevated aerosol surface area density. Heterogeneous chlorine reactions, such as HCl + ClONO2, which instigate the majority of polar ozone losses, are highly sensitive to elevated water vapor and sulfate but are not significant here because the coincident temperatures are not cold enough (e.g., ~216 K near the HTHH-induced water vapor peak). One gas-phase reaction important in driving the measured and modeled chemical changes in the presence of additional water vapor is the reaction between an electronically excited oxygen atom, O(1D) (generated from ozone photolysis) and water vapor to form two hydroxyl radicals, OH. The modeled rate of the O(1D) + H2O reaction increases by approximately a factor of two at the water vapor peak relative to the runs with no volcanic eruption.

Substantial increases in OH (and therefore HO2 through HOx cycling) and HNO3 have wide-reaching impacts because the chemical cycles controlling stratospheric composition are highly interconnected. HOx directly participates in several cycles that deplete stratospheric ozone (30, 31), and the model predicts enhanced rates for the rate-limiting steps in these reactions, such as HO2 + O3, OH + O3, and O + HO2. Moreover, OH contributes to HNO3 production and NOx loss through reaction with NO2 and further contributes to both HCl loss and ClO production through reaction of OH with HCl. HNO3 is a reservoir for nitrogen oxides with a relatively long chemical lifetime on the order of weeks, so its production serves to remove NOx from the stratosphere. For example, at night NO2 and NO3 (formed from NO2) react to form N2O5, which is lost via the heterogeneous hydrolysis reaction. A reduction in NO2 leads to a decrease in NO because the two cycle rapidly in the daytime, on the order of a minute.

The decrease in modeled stratospheric NOx impacts ozone loss from both nitrogen and halogen cycles. Reduced NOx causes a slowing in the rate-limiting reaction rate of NO2 + O for NOx-driven ozone destruction, which in turn, leads to more ozone. However, reduced NOx simultaneously allows reactive ClO (and BrO) levels to increase by decreasing the importance of the ClONO2 (and BrONO2) reservoir formation reaction, which leads to greater halogen-mediated ozone loss. The modeled rate-limiting reaction rate of ClO + O is indeed significantly faster. Elevated ClO coupled with elevated HO2 leads to a more rapid modeled reaction rate for these two radicals and therefore higher HOCl mixing ratios, which also contribute to ozone loss by the ClO + HO2 cycle (32).

In short, the ozone-loss cycles involving HOx and ClOx are enhanced while the NOx-driven ozone loss slows. The complex interplay of these reactions leads to a net change in the ozone vertical profile that indicates net loss near the water vapor peak and net increase at slightly higher altitudes soon after the eruption. Both the MLS measurements and the model show these features (Figs. 4J and 5D). All the other modeled species considered show a unidirectional perturbation across the vertical profile.

Immediately apparent in Fig. 6 are the preponderance of reds in the ClO and HNO3 plots and blues in the O3 and HCl plots, as well as almost all anomalies being located at tropical and SH latitudes. Mixing ratios of ClO are consistently elevated following the HTHH eruption at tropical latitudes in both hemispheres and into the SH midlatitudes. ClO (day and night average) reached a maximum value 126% greater than its 17-y mean for the respective month and 15° latitude bin. Similarly, HNO3 is elevated at tropical latitudes and into SH midlatitudes with a maximum increase of 43%. Farther south, the higher altitude increase is replaced as the dominant feature in the vertical profile by a lower altitude HNO3 loss. Although most of the anomalies in Fig. 6 occur in the 20 to 40 hPa region, O3, HNO3, and HCl were also significantly perturbed at pressures <20 hPa at some latitudes. A positive HNO3 anomaly at pressures <20 hPa is responsible for the red and orange points in Fig. 6D at latitudes from 15° to 45° S for July to December, but it should be noted that the monthly vertical profiles for this latitude range and time frame show a large vertical region of HNO3 loss, which typically peaked at 31.6 hPa but did not always fall below –2σ from the 17-y mean. Significant changes in O3 are slightly delayed relative to ClO and HNO3 in Fig. 6, but broad decreases in the SH midlatitudes are observed in the second half of the year, with O3 loss peaking at –14%. The timing and location of changes in HCl are somewhat similar to those in O3, with the maximum decrease in HCl being –22%. Consistent with the data nearest the equator in Fig. 6, enhanced abundances of ClO were reported following the eruption of Mount Pinatubo without a substantial corresponding reduction in HCl (18, 33).

We emphasize that not every significant compositional change caused by the HTHH eruption is shown in Fig. 6. Only the largest perturbations in the vertical profiles are shown, and many real changes do not exceed the 2σ threshold, particularly when there is substantial natural variability in the 17-y MLS record. It is possible that using elevated water vapor as the marker for HTHH perturbation excludes some anomalies influenced only by elevated sulfate aerosols, which descended in altitude in 2022 relative to the peak water vapor due to sedimentation during transport (7); however, we note that Fig. 6 would be nearly identical if we expand the pressure range to 5 to 100 hPa. Finally, it is statistically possible that a few points meet the selection criteria without being caused by HTHH. Nevertheless, the broader picture in Fig. 6 is clear: There were widespread changes in stratospheric composition in 2022, of unprecedented magnitude and duration in the MLS record, following the HTHH eruption. Considering column ozone, the dominant anomalies in the 60° S to 60° N region, calculated as monthly averages in 15° latitude bins, are O3 losses in the SH midlatitudes and O3 increases near the equator, with the largest changes occurring in the final 5 mo of 2022. Specifically, the largest negative monthly anomalies in stratospheric column ozone calculated for the 1.2 to 100 hPa region are approximately –7% for 45 to 60° S (October) and –5% for 30 to 45° S (September), whereas the largest positive monthly anomalies are approximately +4% for 0 to 15° S (December) and +5% for 0 to 15° N (November). Note that the apparent inconsistency between the positive column O3 anomaly for 0 to 15° S in December 2022 and Fig. 6A is because the positive O3 anomalies cover a broad vertical region but do not exceed 2σ from the mean in this latitude bin.

Importance of Dynamics and Chemistry.

To investigate the importance of chemistry vs. dynamics in causing the observed concentration changes in the second half of 2022, we use observational tracer data (34). N2O is a long-lived tracer that correlates with large-scale transport in the atmosphere. The mixing ratio of N2O decreases as air photochemically ages, while the mixing ratio of HCl increases. The relationship between the mixing ratio of O3 vs. N2O is more complicated than for HCl vs. N2O, but consistent correlations (positive or negative) generally hold at given pressure levels over time. The presence or absence of deviations in the established relationships between HCl, O3, and N2O provides evidence as to whether observed mixing ratio changes are due to chemistry or dynamics, i.e., changing wind patterns causing faster or slower poleward transport of air.

In general, the MLS data show that at SH midlatitudes in the second half of 2022, low values of O3 and HCl were accompanied by high N2O, and conversely, high values of O3 and HCl near the equator were accompanied by low N2O. These correlations are apparent in SI Appendix, Figs. S9–S14, where monthly vertical profiles for HCl, O3, and N2O are shown for 30 to 45° S and 0 to 15° N, respectively, and suggest that changes in dynamics played an important role in determining the observed mixing ratio anomalies.

Chemical effects are also significant. In both panels of Fig. 7, the red and green points from June to December 2022 and 2020 are clear outliers relative to the other 16 y of data. Nearly all of the 2022 anomalies in HCl are more than 2σ below the 16-y mean. A greater fraction of the HTHH-induced anomalies in HCl and O3 for June to December 2022 lie below the 2σ line than the fraction of anomalies in 2020 from the ANY wildfires at these pressure levels (although, the ANY wildfires also perturbed HCl and O3 earlier in the year and at lower altitudes, with particularly large anomalies in HCl). In short, as shown in Fig. 7, the anomalies in HCl and O3 for 2022 are biased low and represent the lowest values on record at some values of δN2O. These deviations in the relationships of HCl and O3 with N2O represent signatures of the observed compositional changes being influenced by chemistry. The neighboring SH latitude ranges (45 to 60° S and 15 to 30° S), where significant HCl and O3 anomalies were also observed in the second half of 2022, show similar evidence supporting chemical influence. Importantly, the data from 2021 do not show significant deviations in Fig. 7, providing evidence that the anomalies for 2020 and 2022 are the consequence of real atmospheric changes and not the result of a bias over time in the N2O data from MLS (38).

The reductions in mixing ratio of stratospheric HCl at SH midlatitudes due to chemistry have different causes in 2022 and 2020. The chemical loss of HCl in 2020 was due to heterogeneous reactions on smoke particles (34–37), whereas chemical reductions of HCl in 2022 would have occurred primarily due to the gas-phase reaction of HCl with elevated OH, as well as slower HCl production from the Cl + CH4 reaction at anomalously colder temperatures. While deviations in the HCl and O3 vs. N2O relations suggest that chemical losses were significant, particularly for HCl, the aforementioned variations in dynamics driven by HTHH-induced reductions in SH temperature were likely the more important factor overall in determining mixing ratio anomalies in the second half of 2022. A significant change in stratospheric dynamics is evident in the fact that δN2O values had been negative for all of 2021 at 31.6 hPa and 46.4 hPa (Fig. 7B), as well as negative earlier in 2022 at these pressure levels, i.e., below –10 ppb for February to May (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), but became significantly positive in the second half of 2022 in correlation with lower O3 and lower HCl. The interplay between stratospheric compositional changes caused by both chemistry and by dynamics was similarly observed following the sulfate-rich eruption of Mount Pinatubo (15, 18).

While the focus of this study is primarily the tropics and midlatitudes, where the bulk of the stratospheric perturbations resulting from HTHH were located in 2022, the water vapor anomaly is expected to continue to increase in the SH polar region in 2023 due to the Brewer–Dobson circulation. This additional water vapor has the potential to significantly amplify ozone losses in the Antarctic polar vortex (7, 39) in austral winter/spring. Looking ahead, water vapor levels in the stratosphere will likely continue to be elevated due to the HTHH eruption for several years given the lack of an efficient sink of water vapor in the stratosphere, making continued observation essential.

Materials and Methods

Data from the MLS instrument onboard the NASA EOS Aura satellite were used in this analysis. Launched in July 2004, MLS measures microwave thermal emission from the limb of Earth’s atmosphere to obtain vertical profiles of various trace gases, temperature, and pressure with daily global coverage. The most recent MLS version 5, Level 2 data were utilized in this study, excluding January 2022 from the analyses due to the standard MLS data processing algorithm removing the largest H2O values (2). For each variable, all recommended data screenings from the MLS Version 5.0-1.1a Data Quality document (40) were applied. Because the MLS data are analyzed as monthly averages and considered over 15° or larger latitude bins in this study, the precision of the utilized data is greatly improved over the single profile precision. The ClO measurement has known biases at the lowest altitude pressure levels considered here (100 and 68 hPa), and the appropriate MLS-recommended bias corrections (40) were implemented. Importantly, because our study is focused on measurement anomalies and not absolute values, any systematic biases in the MLS measurements would tend to subtract out in the anomaly calculations (37). That the mixing ratio anomalies presented here exceed 2σ from the means for months at a time over a range of pressure levels provides compelling evidence that these anomalies are due to real atmospheric changes and not the result of MLS measurement error. The N2O data shown in Fig. 7 were not "detrended" for possible drifts in the MLS measurement with time [about –3%/decade (38)], as we strictly followed the data recommendations in the MLS Version 5.0-1.1a Data Quality document (40). However, we note that if we were to impose a linear fit adjustment to N2O with time (34), not unexpectedly there would be some visible changes in the tracer correlation plots like Fig. 7 (details vary with pressure level and latitude range), but the primary conclusions of our tracer correlation analysis would not change if N2O were detrended. Specifically, there is evidence for both dynamics and chemistry perturbing HCl and O3 mixing ratios in the SH in the second half of 2022, with the chemical perturbation to HCl significantly stronger than for O3.

To quantify the mass of water delivered to the stratosphere by HTHH, we convert the absolute difference in water vapor mixing ratio over the vertical column from 100 hPa to 1.2 hPa and over each specified latitude range to an integrated mass using the following equation:[Mass H2O Added to Column]100−1.2 hPa=∑100 hPa1.2 hPaA·1g·▵P·▵H2O·MWH2OMWair·(10−7) in [Tg].

Area (A) in km2 is determined for the latitude range of interest, e.g., 0 to 30° S, using the World Geodetic System of 1984 reference ellipsoid; g = 9.81 m/s2; ΔP is the difference in pressure between two successive MLS pressure levels defined in hPa; ΔH2O is the water vapor anomaly in ppm; molecular weights are MWH2O = 18.02 g/mol and MWair = 28.97 g/mol; and 10–7 is a scale factor to normalize the units. We linearly interpolate the water vapor mixing ratio between the MLS pressure levels. The additional water vapor mass in each layer is then summed from 100 hPa to the lowest pressure of 1.2 hPa.

A large-ensemble 3-D chemistry–climate–aerosol model was used to evaluate the stratospheric response to a volcanic perturbation of water vapor and sulfate aerosol comparable to the HTHH eruption. The model, SOCOL-AERv2 (SOCOL = modeling tools for studies of SOlar Climate Ozone Links, AER = 2-D aerosol model) (10, 41), has T42 spectral grid resolution (2.8° × 2.8° at the equator) and 39 pressure levels spanning the surface to 0.01 hPa. This model has previously been shown to represent the SO2 and aerosol from the Mount Pinatubo eruption well (42, 43). The model version implemented here is described by Feinberg et al. (41) with the following two modifications as detailed in Østerstrøm et al. (44): 1) The chemical boundary conditions were integrated with those of the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) scenarios. 2) Heat fluxes needed to implement an interactive mixed-layer ocean in the model were generated from a 10-member perturbed initial conditions model ensemble running from 2015 to 2100 following model spin-up of 20 y at 2015 conditions. Sea surface temperatures and sea ice thickness used in the ensemble were set to MIROC6 values obtained from the CMIP6 database (45). A 30-member model ensemble was used to simulate the volcanic eruption (and a non-volcanic background) under the SSP 3-7.0 scenario. Details of the simulations appear in the main text and SI Appendix.
