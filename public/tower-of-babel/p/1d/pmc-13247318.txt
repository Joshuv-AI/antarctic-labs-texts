Quadratus Femoris Muscle Pedicle Bone Grafting in Early-Stage Osteonecrosis of Femoral Head
Authors: Sumit Arora, Ojaswi Phaldessai, Arihant Kataria, Sabyasachi Chatterjee, Nitish Bansal, Abhishek Kashyap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeReports remain sparse on the outcomes of quadratus femoris muscle pedicle bone grafting (QF-MPBG) for osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) in the existing literature. This study reports on the clinico-radiological outcomes of this procedure in early-stage non-traumatic ONFH using a quadriceps-coxae sparing (QCS) modified posterior approach.Materials and MethodsThis prospective study included 22 symptomatic patients suffering from early-stage, non-traumatic ONFH. These patients underwent QF-MPBG surgery using a QCS modified posterior approach and were followed up for a minimum duration of 24 months.ResultsThe mean age of patients was 29 years (range, 20-47 years). As per the modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle, the distribution of patients was as follows: grade 1 (n=5); grade 2 (n=9); grade 3 (n=4); and grade 4 (n=4). All four patients belonging to grade 4 and two belonging to grade 3 required total hip arthroplasty (THA) within 24 months. As per the modified Ficat’s classification, 18 cases were in stage II and four in stage I. Six cases (out of the preoperative 18 cases classified as stage II) deteriorated to stage IV, and two deteriorated to stage III. The mean Harris hip score improved from 63 (range, 57-67) to 75 (range, 50-93) after surgery (P<0.05). Four out of six patients necessitating THA had steroid-induced ONFH.ConclusionThe QCS modified posterior approach provides sufficient exposure required for QF-MPBG. Steroid-induced ONFH and a modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle of >250° may be considered as potential risk factors for progression of this disease.Keywords: Osteonecrosis of femoral head, Non-traumatic, Quadratus femoris muscle pedicle bone grafting, Quadriceps-coxae sparing posterior approach, Avascular necrosis of femoral head

INTRODUCTION

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a progressive disorder resulting in compromised blood supply to the femoral head which may herald a cascade of bone cell death, articular surface collapse, and arthrosis1-3). The etiopathogenesis remains enigmatic. However, predominant risk factors include corticosteroid or alcohol abuse. There are various treatment options available for its management, including pharmaceutical management (e.g., bisphosphonates) or surgical options such as head salvage or hip arthroplasty. Among the surgical options, head salvage procedures are utilized in the early stages of ONFH, while total hip arthroplasty (THA) is the preferred option for advanced stages. Muscle pedicle bone grafting (MPBG) is one of the head-preserving surgical techniques for the management of ONFH4-10).

Widely used in orthopedic surgery, MPBG is a surgical technique which promotes bone vascularity, regeneration, and healing. Utilizing an autologous bone fragment with preserved muscle attachment having a known vascular pedicle, this technique enhances bone repair in various clinical scenarios, such as degenerative processes and femoral neck fractures. Muscle tissue utilized for the vascularized graft can be either anterior-based (rectus femoris [straight head]/sartorius/gluteus medius) or posterior-based (quadratus femoris [QF]/tensor fascia lata)4-10). The appropriate surgical approach is of paramount importance, as it can profoundly impact patient outcomes and complication risks associated with the procedure.

As the vascular supply to the femoral head is mainly derived from the posterior aspect, the posterior approach is more vulnerable to iatrogenic injury due to an already compromised blood supply. A future aim, to make this approach minimally invasive and reduce its propensity for iatrogenic vascular compromise to the femoral head, may be achieved by preserving the short external rotators during surgical dissection. The synergistic muscle-tendon unit, consisting of the piriformis, the superior gemellus, the obturator internus, and the inferior gemellus, is called the “quadriceps coxae” (QC). The quadriceps-coxae sparing (QCS) modified posterior approach has been reported on in the literature for hemiarthroplasty and THA11-13). Few researchers have reported on the outcome of quadratus femoris muscle pedicle bone grafting (QF-MPBG) via the standard posterior approach for non-traumatic ONFH4-7). However, to the best of our knowledge, none have used the QCS modified posterior approach for this purpose. Apart from its minimally invasive nature, this approach also helps improve prosthetic stability in the event of future hip arthroplasty13). In this study, we prospectively evaluated the QCS modified posterior approach for QF-MPBG in symptomatic patients suffering from early-stage, non-traumatic ONFH.

Following were the study questions:

• To study the primary clinical outcome and radiological outcomes of QF-MPBG performed via QCS modified posterior approach in symptomaticpatients suffering from early-stage, non-traumatic ONFH.

• Does the preservation of QC hamper the required visualization of the posterior femoral neck cortex?

• What factors predict progression to femoral head collapse after QF-MPBG in ONFH (and subsequent conversion to THA)?

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective observational study was conducted on patients with ONFH between August 2022 and July 2023 at a tertiary-care teaching referral institute attached to a reputed medical college. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Maulana Azad Medical College & Associated Lok Nayak Hospital, New Delhi, India (No. F.1/IEC/MAMC/MD/MS), and registered with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI). Patients gave written informed consent regarding the surgery, authorizing treatment, radiological examination, and photographic documentation. Patients gave further consent when they were informed that the data about their case would be submitted for publication in electronic and/or print journals. The sample size for this study was 22 individuals. This sample size was calculated at a 95% confidence level and taking a percentage of subjects with good to excellent outcomes after MPBG in ONFH patients (Wang et al.4)) as 79% and with a relative error of 20%.

ONFH patients presenting to the outpatient department were screened for possible inclusion in the study considering the following criteria: (1) age-group between 18-50 years; (2) symptomatic ONFH due to non-traumatic causes; and (3) early-stage disease, as per modified Ficat’s staging (stages I & II)14). Excluding cases secondary to previous hip fractures/surgeries (n=3) and posterior hip dislocation (n=1), we received 53 patients of ONFH. Patients in either advanced stages (stages III & IV; n=24) or the transition stage (n=2) were excluded. One patient was excluded from the study due to a contraindication for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) The remaining 22 patients were eligible for our study.

Using a QCS modified posterior approach, these patients underwent QF-MPBG according to the surgical technique described below.

1. Surgical Technique

An anesthetized patient is placed in a lateral decubitus position with the affected hip facing upwards and slightly abducted. The operative limb is kept at around 30°-40° flexion at the hip. With centering over the greater trochanter (GT) tip, an approximately 15 cm long straight skin incision is marked on the lateral aspect of hip. The subcutaneous tissue and fat are incised until the surgeon reaches the deep fascia (gluteal fascia [proximally] and iliotibial band [distally]). This layer is then opened in the distal to proximal direction, and gluteus maximus fibres are split by finger dissection. A Charney’s hip retractor is applied. Internal rotation of the hip helps identification of the short external rotators. The QF is identified. Using an ultrasonic bone scalpel, a bone section (approximate length 22-24 mm, width 12-14 mm, and thickness 8 mm) with attached QF is harvested from the posterior aspect of the femur (Fig. 1A). Now, a blunt hemostat is introduced in the distal to proximal direction from the inferior edge of the inferior gemellus muscle and between the QC and the posterior hip capsule. The obturator externus tendon is visualized under the QF and mobilized inferiorly and medially. The hip is abducted past neutral to de-tension the QC. A transverse posterior capsulotomy is performed to expose the posterior aspect of the femoral neck and head-neck junction. The right-angle retractor retracts the QC and the proximal half of posterior capsule superiorly. Using an ultrasonic bone scalpel, a trough (approximate length 20-22 mm, width 12-14 mm, and depth 8-10 mm) is created in the middle of the posterior femoral neck cortex, along the femoral neck (Fig. 1B). Should the ultrasonic bone scalpel not be available, multiple drill holes are made and then connected with fine osteotomes. This trough is extended underneath the head-neck junction by 3-5 mm towards the femoral head. Through this trough, multiple drill holes are made, using a 2.5 mm drill bit at a slow speed, towards the necrotic area of the femoral head (without penetrating femoral head articular cartilage). The harvested QF-MPBG is rotated and placed into the trough towards the femoral head beneath the head-neck junction (Fig. 1C). It is temporarily fixed near its lateral margin with a 1.5 mm Kirschner’s wire. A 3.5 mm titanium cortical screw is used to fix the MPBG at its center. It is placed in the postero-supero-lateral to antero-infero-medial direction according to the deadman theory15). Rotational stability of the graft is provided by anchoring this single screw in the femoral head. Finally, the wound is closed in layers over a suction drain, and an aseptic dressing is applied.

These patients underwent orthogonal radiographs of the pelvis with both hips (with 15° internal rotation) before and after surgery, then every three months for the first postoperative year. After the first year, patients underwent radiography every six months (Fig. 2, 3). As per the modified Ficat’s classification (stage I, II, transition, III, IV), radiographs were used to determine the stage of femoral head involvement. Before surgery, patients also underwent an MRI of both hips to determine femoral head involvement by Mitchell’s central region magnetic resonance (MR) intensity class (class A, B, C, and D)16), and the modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle (grade 1, ≤200°; grade 2, 200°-249°; grade 3, 250°-299°; and grade 4, ≥300°)17) (Fig. 4). Non-contrast computed tomography of the hip was done within the first postoperative week to visualize the attachment location of MPBG and the trough position. The DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) files of the scans were imported into RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software (ver. 2023.1; Medixant). Then, three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction was done to assess the size and position of the trough and graft. The dimensions and location of the trough were measured on the coronal section. In the 3D reconstructed image, the posterior end-on view of the femoral neck was selected, and the ratio of the remaining femoral neck proximal to the trough (FNprox) to the remaining femoral neck distal to the trough (FNdist) in the mid-transcervical area was calculated (Fig. 5).

All patients were provided with standard postoperative and nursing care after their procedures. They were encouraged to do ankle pumping, and isometric quadriceps exercises soon after spinal anaesthesia abated. All patients were mobilized after 24 hours with a walker/pair of crutches and non-weight bearing on the operated limb for the first three months. Patients were discharged from the hospital after they were confident in the independent activities of daily living, sitting, and walking with the aid of a walker.

The following variables were recorded: (1) etiology of ONFH; (2) duration of surgery (from skin incision to closure); (3) blood loss (intraoperative blood loss+collection in the postoperative drain); (4) measurement of the size and position of the femoral neck trough and graft; (5) screw trajectory on anteroposterior view radiograph; (6) modified Ficat’s classification; Mitchell’s classification; and modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle; (7) Harris hip score (HHS)18) graded as excellent (≥90), good (80-89), fair (70-79), and poor (<70) after adding the scores given for each criterion; and (8) the procedure-related complications (including the need for THA conversion within 2 years). Criteria for considering THA were progression to stage IV (as per the modified Ficat’s classification), hip arthritis, joint space narrowing, or osteophyte formation.

2. Statistical Analysis

The data were entered in Microsoft Excel (ver. 2016; Microsoft Corp.) and analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (ver. 25.0; IBM Corp.). Qualitative predictors were presented in terms of frequency (percentage). Quantitative predictors were presented in terms of range, mean±standard deviation, or median (interquartile range). The non-parametric binomial test determined the statistical significance of adverse outcomes. An unpaired t-test determined the statistical significance between quantitative factors and adverse clinical outcomes. The difference in means for preoperative HHS and postoperative HHS was analyzed by utilizing a paired t-test. A non-parametric Mann–Whitney test determined the statistical significance between the percent change of HHS before and after surgery and adverse clinical outcomes. The chi-square/Fisher’s exact test was utilized to determine the statistical significance of each independent predictor (etiology, modified Ficat’s classification, Mitchell’s classification, and modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle) with adverse outcomes. A two-tailed P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was done for the respective time to THA (or advised to undergo THA).

RESULTS

A total of 22 patients (20 males, 2 females; 22 hips) were evaluated in this study. All patients had good general condition with stable vital signs. Four patients (all males) had alcohol-induced ONFH, four (all males) had steroid-induced ONFH, and 14 (12 males, 2 females) were idiopathic. Seven patients had bilateral involvement. However, none of these patients underwent bilateral QF-MPBG surgery, as five had asymptomatic contralateral hip ONFH and two had already undergone contralateral THA in the recent past. As shown in Table 1, patients were followed for a mean duration of 26 months (range, 24-30 months). Table 2 summarizes the comparison of preoperative baseline parameters between steroid-induced ONFH and nonsteroid-induced ONFH. Patients in the steroid-group had more severe preoperative involvement, as per the modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle grades (P=0.01), and Mitchell’s classification (P=0.03). However, this group showed comparable involvement as per the modified Ficat’s classification (P=0.55).

The mean participant age was 29 years (range, 20-47 years). Mean surgical time was 69.5±8.5 minutes (range, 60-80 minutes). Mean blood loss was 180 mL (range, 150-240 mL).

The mean QF-MPBG length and width were 2.14±0.08 cm and 0.98±0.05 cm, respectively. The mean trough length and width in the femoral neck were 1.87±0.14 cm and 1.03±0.05 cm, respectively. The mean FNprox/FNdist ratio was 0.87±0.05 (range, 0.78-0.94). The screw was visualized in the postero-supero-lateral to antero-infero-medial direction (17 patients), and in the postero-superior to antero-inferior direction (five patients). None of the screws crossed the trajectory of femoral neck osteotomy (which might be required for possible future THA).

As per the Mitchell’s central region MR intensity class, patient distribution was as follows: class A (n=9); class B (n=12); class C (n=1); and class D (n=0). In terms of poor functional outcomes, none were observed in class A. Five poor functional outcomes were observed (out of 12 cases) in class B, and one was observed in class C. The association of Mitchell’s classes B & C with poor functional outcomes was statistically significant (P=0.03). Patient distribution, as per the modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle, was as follows: grade 1 (n=5); grade 2 (n=9); grade 3 (n=4); and grade 4 (n=4). THA was necessitated within 24 months of follow-up in all four patients belonging to grade 4 and two (out of four patients) belonging to grade 3 (P<0.001) (Fig. 3). As per the modified Ficat’s classification, 18 cases were in stage II and four in stage I. Six cases (out of the preoperative 18 cases belonging to stage II) deteriorated to stage IV, and two deteriorated to stage III. One case (out of the preoperative 4 cases belonging to stage I) deteriorated to stage II (Table 1). With the numbers available in this study, the association of the modified Ficat’s classification with poor outcomes couldn’t reach statistical significance (P=0.55).

The mean HHS improved from 63 (range, 57-67) to 75 (range, 50-93) postoperatively, and this difference was statistically significant (P<0.05) (Fig. 2). All cases had poor function before surgery, which improved to excellent outcomes in three cases, good in 12, fair in one, and poor in six cases. Deterioration was observed in the six cases, and those patients were advised to undergo THA (Table 1).

At the most recent follow-up, procedure-related complications were conversion to THA (n=4); need for pending THA (n=2); surgical site infection (n=0); bone graft fragmentation (n=0); need for blood transfusion (n=0); heterotropic ossification (n=0); nerve palsy (n=0); intra-articular penetration of screw (n=0); screw pull-out (n=0); and iatrogenic proximal femur fractures (n=0). Four out of six patients necessitating THA (within 24 months) had steroid-induced ONFH, while one patient belonged to the alcohol-induced ONFH and idiopathic ONFH groups each. These patients suffered disease progression beyond hip salvage, and four of them underwent THA at six months, 12 months, 13 months, and 15 months of follow-up, respectively. The remaining two patients were also advised to undergo THA at their 10-month and 12-month follow-ups, respectively, but are still awaiting surgery due to financial reasons. All four patients with steroid-induced ONFH had disease progression necessitating THA, and this etiological association was statistically significant (P=0.002).

The Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrated dips between six months and 15 months, indicating times when patients underwent/ necessitated THA (Fig. 6).

DISCUSSION

QF-MPBG offers a direct channel for vascularizing the compromised circulation to the femoral head. This technique is expected to maintain critical blood flow, facilitate revascularization of necrotic regions, promote healing, and potentially delay or avoid femoral head collapse10). This aligns with the minimally invasive and anatomy-preserving nature of the QCS-modified posterior approach11-13). The synergy between this minimally invasive approach and the vascular/structural support of QF-MPBG may create a comprehensive strategy for the surgical treatment of ONFH. This combination may address the ‘biology’ (revascularization/minimally invasive) and the ‘mechanics’ (preventing articular collapse) in early-stage ONFH.

However, despite these theoretical advantages, we identified several limitations of this study. Our study reported on a limited number of patients and, as such, there was no control group. A larger cohort would have further substantiated study outcomes. Considering the potential for late collapse of the femoral head in selected cases, an extended follow-up period would have been beneficial. Potential selection bias may have been introduced as the study was conducted at a single institution and involved only the Indian population. Regression analysis could not be performed as observations in the sample were not truly independent. As THA conversion is a time-dependent outcome, simple binary classification (THA vs. no THA) might lead to biased interpretation. Furthermore, different follow-up durations might have impacted the outcome.

Wang et al.4) suggested that a titanium mesh-cage containing bone graft, pedicled with the QF, can achieve satisfactory short-term outcomes for ONFH in ARCO (Association Research Circulation Osseous) stage IIC. This technique aimed to improve structural support in cases with extensive necrosis. Meng et al.5) concluded that the clinical satisfaction rate of core decompression (CD) alone is only about 30% as it relieves the pressure inside the femoral head. Thus, the support force is further reduced for the articular surface5). Li et al.6) summarized that, for early-stage non-traumatic ONFH, CD with bone-grafting is a reasonable option as it relieved pain and improved hip function with less surgical trauma as compared to QF-MPBG. The authors suggested that, generally, CD with bone grafting should be used in these early-stage cases. However, autologous iliac bone grafts after CD may be resorbed, heralding the onset of articular collapse and flattening of the femoral head. Popere et al.7) reported that QF-MPBG is a viable option to preserve the femoral head in young adults with early-stage ONFH or femoral neck fractures as good to excellent outcomes were reported in approximately 83% of their patients.

As reported in the literature, QF-MPBG has demonstrated significant benefits in early-stage non-traumatic ONFH (Table 3)4-7). However, the procedure is not without drawbacks. First, it is a technically demanding procedure and should be performed by an experienced hip surgeon. Second, its higher degree of surgical invasiveness (in comparison to CD) may be associated with longer surgical time, increased blood loss, and increased risk of complications. Finally, published articles report only short follow-up periods, therefore imposing limitations on our understanding of the procedure’s long-term efficacy.

The SPAIRE (Spare Piriformis and Internus, Repair Externus) approach was described by Hanly et al.11) as a method to preserve the integrity of the QC of the hip during hip arthroplasty. This minimally invasive approach may help prevent iatrogenic injury to the already compromised blood supply of the femoral head. This approach represents a significant advancement in hip surgical techniques. It aims to balance the need for adequate exposure with the preservation of critical soft tissues and vascular structures. While Hanly et al.’s results11) do not directly comment on the preservation of hip proprioception owing to spared QC, general principles of proprioception can be applied to this muscle group. Proprioception relies on mechanoreceptors in joint structures and muscle spindles, which act as length and velocity sensors. The QC likely contains many muscle spindles, similar to the rotator cuff in the shoulder. As such, these muscles would play a crucial role in sensing hip joint position and movement, especially during complex motions involving flexion, abduction, and external rotation. The length and activation state of these muscles would influence their proprioceptive sensitivity, with potential differences between active and passive states. Preserved attachments of the QC may have implications for the prosthetic stability in the event of future THA13). However, to confirm these extrapolations, specific studies on hip external rotators are needed. Despite several proposed advantages of QF-MPBG using the QCS modified posterior approach, we observed that six out of 22 patients subsequently necessitated THA. These failures may be attributed to the enigmatic biological behaviour of the disease as well as patient selection. Moreover, results from Table 2 suggest that the steroid group had a higher baseline combined necrotic angle (P=0.01). This elevated value may have possibly contributed to a higher propensity for disease progression and later THA.

Although these results are preliminary, they are, nevertheless, hypothesis-generating. Future research aims may focus on: (1) multi-institutional studies with larger patient cohorts to increase the statistical power and reliability of findings; (2) longer follow-up durations to assess long-term outcomes and potential complications; and, (3) comparative studies with other advanced techniques, such as vascularized fibular grafting/tantalum rod implantation/titanium mesh-cage containing bone graft.

CONCLUSION

QCS modified posterior approach provides sufficient exposure required for QF-MPBG. Furthermore, as observed trends in the small cohort of this study, steroid-induced ONFH and a modified Kerboul’s combined necrotic angle of >250° may be indicators of potential risk for disease progression.
