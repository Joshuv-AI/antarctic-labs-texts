Isolated Left Ventricular Apical Hypoplasia Presenting as Vasovagal Syncope in a Middle-Aged Male Patient
Authors: Lakshmi Siddhi Kolaparthi, Rajesh Thachathodiyl, Ramiah Rajesh Kannan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated left ventricular apical hypoplasia (ILVAH) is a rare congenital cardiomyopathy characterized by a truncated and spherical left ventricle with compensatory right ventricle elongation. Clinical presentation is variable and often delayed.Case SummaryWe report a 40-year-old man with no comorbidities who presented with a vasovagal episode. Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with incomplete left bundle branch block. Echocardiography suggested dilated cardiomyopathy; however, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) revealed the classic features of isolated left ventricular apical hypoplasia, including a truncated, spherical left ventricle with fatty apical infiltration, elongated right ventricle encasing the apex, and papillary muscle abnormalities. Despite preserved ejection fraction (53%), we initiated medical therapy with angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor, beta-blockers, SGLT2i, spironolactone, and aspirin. The patient declined coronary angiography and is under follow-up.DiscussionILVAH can mimic dilated cardiomyopathy on echocardiography. CMR is key to accurate diagnosis and management.Take Home MessagesSuspect ILVAH in a globular left ventricle with apical truncation. It may mimic dilated cardiomyopathy and requires CMR for confirmation.

History of Presentation

A 40-year-old man with no known comorbidities presented with an episode of transient loss of consciousness. The episode was brief, spontaneous in recovery, and without any postictal features. Vital signs were stable. Cardiovascular examination was unremarkable. No murmurs or additional heart sounds were noted.Take-Home Messages•Multimodality imaging, especially CMR, is essential for diagnosing isolated left ventricular apical hypoplasia, which can mimic dilated cardiomyopathy.•Recognition of this rare congenital anomaly allows early initiation of guideline-directed therapy even in the absence of severe systolic dysfunction or arrhythmia.

Past Medical History

The patient had no history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, or prior hospitalizations. He was not on any regular medications and had no family history of sudden cardiac death or cardiomyopathy.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included dilated cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, left ventricular noncompaction, and congenital left ventricular (LV) structural anomaly.

Investigations

A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 60 beats/min, a normal axis, and an incomplete left bundle branch block. Cardiac enzymes, including troponin, were within normal limits. A 72-hour Holter monitoring study revealed no significant arrhythmias (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated dilation of the left atrium, right atrium, and LV. The LV appeared globular and mildly dilated, with reduced systolic function, accompanied by jerky septal motion and mild mitral regurgitation (Figures 2 and 3; Videos 1 and 2). CMR revealed significant dilation of the left atrium, right atrium, and LV. The LV apex was noted to be thin, truncated, and globular in shape. The right ventricle (RV) was elongated and wrapped around the deficient LV apex (Figure 4). The apical LV cavity was hypertrabeculated, with the interventricular septum bulging into the RV. A dysplastic mitral valve apparatus with a complex papillary muscle network was visualized in the flattened anterior apex. Fatty infiltration was noted within the deficient LV apex (Figure 5). Native T1 mapping demonstrated focal elevation in the apical septum with a maximum value of 1,150 ms, and T2 mapping also showed focal elevation in the same region with a maximum value of 64 ms. No areas of late gadolinium enhancement were seen, ruling out infarction or myocardial fibrosis (Videos 3 and 4). The LV ejection fraction was preserved at 53%. These findings were diagnostic of isolated left ventricular apical hypoplasia (ILVAH).Figure 4Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (4-Chamber View)Truncated and spherical left ventricle with fatty infiltration of the apex. Management

The patient was initiated on optimal medical therapy for heart failure with dapagliflozin, sacubitril-valsartan, spironolactone, carvedilol, and aspirin. Coronary angiography was advised, but the patient declined. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement was not considered given preserved ejection fraction, absence of arrhythmias, and no family history.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged with close outpatient follow-up and lifestyle counseling. He remained asymptomatic at the 3-month follow-up, with good medication adherence. Repeat imaging is planned annually to monitor LV function.

Discussion

ILVAH is an uncommon congenital anomaly first described in 2004.1 It is characterized by a truncated LV apex, spherical LV shape, and compensatory elongation of the RV. The pathogenesis is likely due to abnormal ventricular septation or myocardial development in utero.

Most cases are detected incidentally or present in adulthood with dyspnea, palpitations, or, rarely, syncope. This patient's presentation with vasovagal syncope is atypical and emphasizes the need for comprehensive evaluation even in seemingly benign presentations.

Echocardiography often suggests nonspecific dilated cardiomyopathy, as in our case. CMR is essential for diagnosis, offering high-resolution assessment of morphology, tissue characterization (eg, fatty infiltration), and myocardial fibrosis. Our patient had no fibrosis on late gadolinium enhancement but had focal T1 and T2 elevation, suggestive of subclinical myocardial inflammation or edema.2

Bassareo et al3 reviewed 37 patients with ILVAH and found a variable phenotype: ∼35% asymptomatic, ∼40% dyspnea, with ∼56% demonstrating reduced ejection fraction; arrhythmias and congenital defects were also common features. Román et al4 reported an adult with classic ILVAH morphology and preserved function, closely mirroring our patient's profile.

Several differential diagnoses were considered. Dilated cardiomyopathy can present with a dilated, dysfunctional LV, but it typically lacks the apical truncation, fatty infiltration, and RV wrapping seen in ILVAH. Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy may show fatty changes and arrhythmias but usually involves the RV predominantly and does not feature the globular LV morphology. LV noncompaction shares features such as apical trabeculations, but the trabeculae are more prominent with deep intertrabecular recesses and a high noncompacted-to-compacted ratio, without associated RV elongation. Other congenital LV anomalies often present in childhood, are associated with additional structural defects, and do not exhibit the characteristic imaging findings of ILVAH.

No definitive guidelines exist for ILVAH management. Most patients with preserved ejection fraction are managed medically. Device therapy is reserved for arrhythmias or severe dysfunction.

Conclusions

This case highlights the utility of CMR in differentiating rare congenital cardiomyopathies from more common acquired forms. Early identification enables appropriate medical management and follow-up, even in the absence of significant systolic dysfunction.
