NLRP3 inflammasome partially mediates genotoxic E. coli infection in lean but not in genetically obese mice
Authors: Pauline Floch, Cécile Goursat, Lucas Lemmel, Alyssia Loriot, Julie Thevenin, Eric Oswald, Priscilla Branchu, Matteo Serino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGut microbiota dysbiosis in inflammatory bowel diseases and metabolic disorders is associated with increased Escherichia coli abundance, a condition exacerbated by a high-fat diet (HFD). However, the metabolic consequences of E. coli infection, particularly in relation to host metabolic state, remain poorly characterized. To this aim, we investigated the impact of commensal, NMEC genotoxic and AIEC proinflammatory E. coli on gut microbiota, inflammation and metabolic markers in wild-type (WT) lean, ob/ob, and HFD-fed WT mice. The gut microbiota of ob/ob mice showed minimal alterations whatever the strain. Only WT lean mice displayed an IL-18-associated genotoxic strain-induced colon inflammation, partially alleviated by NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition, with no significant effect in ob/ob mice. These findings reveal a divergent response to E. coli infection between lean and obese mice, with the potential involvement of NLRP3 inflammasome acting as a moderate regulator of colon inflammation during genotoxic E. coli infection in lean mice.

Introduction

Increased Proteobacteria (renamed as Pseudomonadota) is a common trait of gut microbiota dysbiosis in several pathologies, including metabolic diseases such as obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D).1,2 Among Proteobacteria are Enterobacteria such as Escherichia coli, which are described as opportunistic pathogens or pathobionts. E. coli strains belonging to the phylogenetic group B2 are capable of producing colibactin, a genotoxin responsible for double-strand breaks in host DNA,3 leading to colorectal cancer.4 We showed that a mother-to-pup transmission of the genotoxic neonatal meningitis-causing E. coli (NMEC) SP15 strain5 induces a gut microbiota change in the progeny, with an enrichment in microbial functions related to DNA repair.6 This result demonstrates that colibactin can also target microbial DNA.3 In this study, we investigated the gut microbial, inflammatory and metabolic effects of E. coli SP15 and the strain E. coli NRG857c, a Crohn’s disease-associated adherent-invasive E. coli (AIEC), also belonging to the B2 phylogroup, which induces chronic inflammation in mice.7 The prevalence of the B2 phylogroup is directly associated with urbanization8 and Enterobacteria infections are developing to a pandemic rate similar to that of metabolic diseases.9,10 Indeed, obese children have a higher E. coli abundance than lean ones,11 indicating E. coli infection as an additional aggravating risk factor for metabolic diseases in adulthood.

In addition, a high-fat diet (HFD), the strongest inducer of metabolic diseases and gut microbiota dysbiosis, was shown to favor AIEC infections in mice.1 Patients with obesity and/or diabetes are more susceptible to infections,12 though the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying frailty toward infections remain unknown. This evidence supports the rationale to study the effects of E. coli infection in both lean and obese mice. Hence, we hypothesized that differences in metabolic state shape the host response to bacterial challenge. To this aim, we realized a protocol of multiple infections in WT lean normal chow (NC)-fed mice, genetically obese ob/ob mice and HFD-fed WT mice with either a commensal E. coli HS (control strain), a genotoxic E. coli SP15 or a proinflammatory E. coli NRG857c strains. We studied markers of both systemic (blood) and tissue (colon and liver) inflammation as well as markers of glucose homeostasis, gut microbiota and inferred gut microbiome. To rapidly evaluate the systemic inflammation following E. coli infection in alive mice, we studied the reduction of blood platelet count, used as a rapid and reliable index of infection-induced systemic inflammation in clinical practice.13 Other cellular markers of systemic inflammation such as lymphocytes, eosinophils, monocyte precursors and granulocytes were studied. We show that the impact of E. coli infection on the above parameters depends on the metabolic state of the host, i.e., lean vs. obese and that the genotoxic E. coli-induced colon inflammation is partially NLRP3-dependent in WT lean but not in ob/ob mice.

Results

Gut microbial ecology following infection with commensal and pathobiont E. coli in WT lean, ob/ob and WT HFD-fed mice

To study whether the metabolic state of the host may modulate the effect of E. coli infection, we infected WT lean NC-fed, genetically obese (ob/ob) and HFD-fed WT mice with either commensal E. coli HS (control strain), genotoxic SP15 or proinflammatory NRG857c E. coli strains. In a fourth group of mice, a cocktail of antibiotics (ABx) was used before the infection to boost the capability of NRG857c E. coli to colonize the gut (Figures S1A and S1B). No antibiotic was used during infection with E. coli SP15 because this genotoxic strain is capable of creating its own intestinal niche via the production of colibactin [6]. The ABx cocktail was effective in boosting NRG857c E. coli strain in WT lean (Figure S1C) and in HFD-fed WT mice (Figure S1E), but not in ob/ob mice (Figure S1D). Two days after the first infection, the fecal bacterial load of the NRG857c E. coli strain boosted by the ABx was significantly higher compared to the HS strain in all of the three groups of mice (Figures S1F–S1H); however, the fecal bacterial load of the genotoxic and the NRG857c E. coli strains in the absence of ABx was significantly higher compared to the HS strain only in the WT lean mice (Figure S1F). All of the E. coli strains significantly affected the general gut microbial profile compared to the related PBS group in WT lean mice (Figures 1A, 1A′, and 1A″) and in HFD-fed WT mice (Figures 1C, 1C′, and 1C″) with the effect of NRG857c enhanced by ABx; by contrast, the gut microbiota of ob/ob was slightly affected by E. coli strains and by the ABx treatment (Figures 1B, 1B′, and 1B″), despite a change in the alpha diversity similar to the other groups of mice (Figures S1I–S1K). A specific taxonomic impact of each E. coli strain was observed depending on the metabolic state of the host (Figures S2A–S2C), with ob/ob mice sharing the less of the taxa with the other groups of mice (Figure S2D). As an exploratory approach to assess whether the E. coli strains reported above may induce potential functional changes in the gut microbiome, we applied a PICRUSt2-based analysis to 16S rRNA gene sequencing data. The general pathways-based inferred gut microbiome profile of ob/ob mice was slightly affected by whatever the E. coli strain, compared to the related PBS group and to the other groups of mice (Figures 2A–2C and 2E). Markers of systemic and tissue (colon and liver) inflammation following infection with commensal and pathobiont E. coli in WT lean, ob/ob and HFD-fed WT mice

To study whether the infections with the above reported E. coli strains may induce systemic inflammation in mice, we measured the reduction of blood platelet count. Platelets are key mediators of infection-induced systemic inflammation via the formation of leukocytes-platelets aggregates and the release of proinflammatory cytokines,14,15,16 two critical factors in the aggravation of infection as they promote a thrombo-inflammatory stage. Platelets are first sentinels during infection and a reduced platelet count, namely thrombocytopenia, is a rapid and reliable index of infection-induced systemic inflammation in clinical practice.13 Four hours after the first infection, the genotoxic E. coli SP15 strain significantly reduced the platelet count in WT lean mice (Figure 3A). In ob/ob mice, we observed a significant increase in the platelet count 4 h after the first infection with NRG857c strain (with and without ABx treatment; Figure 3E) and no significant changes in HFD-fed WT mice, compared to the commensal E. coli HS strain (Figure 3I). The genotoxic E. coli SP15 strain induced a general reduction in the lymphocytes count compared to the commensal E. coli HS strain (Figure 3B), slightly below the related physiological range from PBS-treated WT mice (Figure S8E); in ob/ob mice, the lymphocytes were significantly reduced 2 h after the first infection with E. coli NRG857c strain following ABx treatment (Figure 3F), slightly below the related physiological range from PBS-treated ob/ob mice (Figure S9E); in HFD-fed WT mice (Figure 3J), lymphocytes were increased regardless of the E. coli strain, within the related physiological range (Figure S10E).

The count of eosinophils, monocytes, and precursors was significantly reduced in WT lean mice by the E. coli NRG857c strain (Figure 3C), showed no significant modulation in ob/ob mice regardless of the infection (Figure 3G) and was increased in HFD-fed WT mice by all the E. coli strains (Figure 3K); all of the above reported values were higher than the related physiological range (Figures S8, S9, and S10F). The granulocytes count was significantly modulated by the E. coli NRG857c strain and to a different extent according to both the metabolic state of the mice (Figures 3D, 3H, and 3L) and to the related physiological range (Figures S8, S9, and S10G). At baseline, with no infection or treatment, these cellular markers were similar between WT and ob/ob mice (Figure S3A), suggesting that the low-grade chronic inflammation in ob/ob mice is not sufficient to affect these parameters. Systemically, plasma IL-6 levels did not significantly vary regardless of the group of mice or of the E. coli strain (Figures S3B–S3D), compared to E. coli HS.

In the colon, we observed a general significant trend toward increased markers of inflammation, i.e., TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-18, and Casp-1, only following the infection with the genotoxic E. coli SP15 in WT lean mice (Figure 4A), with no significant effect in ob/ob mice regardless of the infection (Figure 4B), compared to the E. coli HS group. By contrast, in HFD-fed WT mice, all of the E. coli strains and in particular the genotoxic E. coli SP15 induced a significant general reduction in markers of colon inflammation, majorly associated with reduced IL-18 gene expression, compared to the commensal E. coli HS strain (Figure 4C). Since the significant reduction in platelet count observed in WT lean mice occurred 4 h after the first infection with the genotoxic E. coli SP15 (Figure 3A), we further examined the effect of the E. coli strains used in this study in tissue samples collected at the same time point (Figure S4A). E. coli SP15 induced a rise in colonic IL-18 gene expression, compared to E. coli HS. We also studied whether the previous reported E. coli strains may induce anatomical colon remodeling. WT lean mice showed a significant reduction in colon thickness (Figure 5C) and ob/ob mice showed a significant increase in colon weight (Figure 5E) after NRG857c infection following ABx treatment, both compared to E. coli HS. No significant changes were observed in HFD-fed WT mice regardless of the strains (Figures 5G–5I).

In the liver, we observed a general significant trend toward increased markers of inflammation in WT lean mice which displayed a higher IL-18 gene expression during NRG857c infection following ABx treatment (Figure 4D), whereas ob/ob mice showed significantly higher IL-1β levels, in the same conditions as above (Figure 4E). As observed in the colon, the infections showed a general non-significant reduction of most of the previous reported inflammatory markers in the liver of HFD-fed WT mice (Figure 4F). Overall, these data suggest that nutritional obesity dampens genotoxic E. coli infection-induced inflammation in both colon and liver.

Glucometabolic response following infection with commensal and pathobiont E. coli in WT lean, ob/ob, and HFD-fed WT mice

Considering the role of inflammation in modulating insulin signaling and, consequently, glucose homeostasis,17 we studied whether the previous reported different gut microbial and inflammatory responses toward E. coli may have a diverse impact on glucose metabolism. Both WT lean and HFD-fed WT mice, showed higher oral glucose tolerance after NRG857c infection following ABx treatment (Figures S6A–6C), compared to commensal E. coli HS. By contrast, infection in ob/ob mice had a modest impact on the OGTT, regardless of the E. coli strain (Figure S6B). All of the groups of mice infected with E. coli NRG857c following ABx treatment showed significant lower fasting glycemia compared to commensal E. coli HS (Figures S6D–S6F). For all the three groups of mice, fasting glycemia values were below the pathological range of 7 mmol/L, typical of T2D. Fed glycemia was significantly affected only by the proinflammatory E. coli NRG857c in WT lean mice (Figure S6G). The body weight was significantly affected in WT lean but not in ob/ob or HFD-fed WT mice (Figure S7), although both groups of mice experienced a significant temporary reduction during the first week of ABx treatment (Figures S7B, S7C, S7E, and S7F). Of note, the ABx cocktail used in our study did not significantly affect per se any of the markers of systemic inflammation, glucose metabolism, body weight or colon anatomy as reported for WT lean mice (Figure S8), ob/ob (Figure S9) and HFD-fed WT mice (Figure S10). These results suggest a net effect of E. coli NRG857c strain, where applicable, once a niche is available within the colon environment, i.e., following ABx treatment.

Correlative associations of markers of glucose metabolism with gut microbial taxa in WT lean, ob/ob, and HFD-fed WT mice infected with pathobiont E. coli

Taxa significantly and specifically modulated by the E. coli strains used in this study were significantly correlated with markers of glucose metabolism in WT lean, ob/ob, and HFD-fed WT mice. In WT lean mice, the genus Blautia, higher following ABx + E. coli NRG857c infection, significantly and negatively correlated with OGTT area under the curve (AUC), fasting glycemia and with the species Roseburia faecis, higher following E. coli SP15 infection (Figure 5A); the two species reported above belong to the Bacillota (synonym Firmicutes) group. In ob/ob mice, the genus Alistipes, belonging to Bacteroidota (synonym Bacteroidetes) group and higher following E. coli NRG857c infection, significantly and negatively correlated with fed glycemia (Figure 5B). In HFD-fed WT mice, the order Desulfovibrionales (phylum Thermodesulfobacteriota), higher following infection with E. coli NRG857c, significantly and negatively correlated with fed glycemia and Clostridium cocleatum (reclassified as Thomasclavelia cocleata,18 phylum Bacillota) and significantly and positively correlated with genus Roseburia and species Lactobacillus reuteri (phylum Bacillota; Figure 5C). Inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome reduces markers of colon inflammation during genotoxic E. coli infection in WT lean but not in ob/ob mice

Following the trend toward an increase in IL-1β, IL-18, and caspase-1 (Figure 4A) and the increased gene expression of IL-18, in the colon of WT lean mice infected with genotoxic E. coli SP15 (Figure S4B), we performed an exploratory study on the role of NLRP3 inflammasome in the response to genotoxic E. coli SP15 infection in NC-fed WT lean mice. We chose NLRP3 inflammasome because it links genotoxic stress to colon inflammation. Genotoxic stress-induced DNA damage can drive mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation, along with potassium efflux, collectively acting as upstream signals that drive selective NLRP3 inflammasome activation.19,20,21 Both IL-1β and IL-18 are effector molecules of inflammasomes, including NLRP3, which is majorly involved in the sensing of pathogen associated molecular patterns (PAMPS) from bacteria such as E. coli SP15.22 IL-18 has a prominent expression in epithelial (non-immune) cells, whereas IL-1β is more restricted to inducible expression in professional immune cells.23 Hence, we pharmacologically inhibited the NLRP3 inflammasome using the specific inhibitor MCC950,24 according to the protocol shown in Figure 6A. A streptomycin pretreatment was performed to enhance the gut colonization by E. coli SP15 strain, compared to untreated WT mice (average of 7 × 106 CFU/g of feces ± 6 × 106; n = 7) and ob/ob mice (average of 6 × 104 CFU/g of feces ± 5 × 104; n = 5). We also challenged ob/ob mice with MCC950. Our rationale was to evaluate whether reducing the low-grade chronic inflammation characterizing the ob/ob mice could unmask the inflammatory capability of genotoxic E. coli SP15 observed in WT lean mice (Figure 4A), but slightly observed in ob/ob mice (Figure 4B). The treatment with MCC950 induced a general reduction of all of the inflammatory markers studied, with a significant reduction of the inflammatory index in the colon of WT lean mice (Figure 6B) but not in ob/ob mice (Figure 6C), during infection with genotoxic E. coli SP15. The treatment with MCC950 significantly changed the overall gut microbiota profile in WT lean (Figures 6D and 6D′; Figure S11A) and in ob/ob mice (Figures 6E and 6E′; Figure S11D), with no significant change in the diversity indices (Figures S11B–S11E) and in systemic inflammation evaluated as IL-6 plasma levels (Figures S11C–S11F). In WT lean and in ob/ob mice, cellular markers of systemic inflammation, body weight and fed glycemia did not significantly vary (Figures S13A–S13F and S13M-S13R, respectively), with platelets count below the physiological range from the related PBS-treated control groups (Figures S13A and S13M, respectively). We observed a MCC950-induced increase in oral glucose tolerance (Figure S13G) and a significant increase in colon weight (Figure S13J) in WT lean mice; treatment with MCC950 significantly but temporarily increased fecal E. coli SP15 count (Figure S13L) in WT lean mice; the previous reported parameters were not significantly affected in ob/ob mice (Figures S13S–S13X).

In naïve uninfected mice (Figure 7A), the treatment with MCC950 induced a non-significant reduction in colon inflammation in WT lean mice (Figure 7B), and a non-significant increase in ob/ob mice (Figure 7C); the overall gut microbiota profile was not significantly different in WT lean mice (Figures 7D and 7D′) though some taxa and some diversity indices were significantly modulated (Figures S14A and S14B, respectively); in ob/ob mice, the treatment with MCC950 induced significant changes in the overall gut microbiota profile (Figures 7E and 7E′), with some taxa, but no diversity indices significantly modulated (Figures S14D and S14E); no significant changes were observed in systemic inflammation evaluated as IL-6 plasma levels in both WT lean and ob mice (Figures S14C–S14F, respectively). The general inferred microbiome profile was not significantly affected by MCC950 treatment in naïve WT lean and in ob/ob mice, though some bacterial functions were significantly modulated (Figures S15A, S15B, S15C, and S15D, respectively). In WT lean and in ob/ob mice, cellular markers of systemic inflammation, body weight and fed glycemia did not significantly vary (Figures S16A–S16F and S16L–S16Q, respectively). We observed a modest MCC950-induced increase in oral glucose tolerance (Figure S16G) and a significant increase in colon thickness (Figure S16K) in WT lean mice. Discussion

Our study shows that WT lean, ob/ob, and HFD-fed WT mice exhibit distinct gut microbial, inflammatory and glucometabolic responses to infection with either commensal or pathobiont E. coli. As a primary conclusion, these findings indicate that the metabolic state of the host, which is reflected by differences in adiposity, insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis, critically shapes the responses to E. coli infection. The contrasting responses observed in metabolically healthy WT lean mice versus metabolically dysregulated ob/ob and HFD-fed WT mice suggest that obesity and glucose dysmetabolism modulate both microbial dynamics and host inflammatory and metabolic pathways during E. coli infection. We found that both genetic and nutritional obesity dampened most of the consequences of the E. coli infection. A plausible explanation for this result is the reduced gut colonization of obese mice by the E. coli strains used in this study, as supported by fecal bacterial load analysis.

Exploratory analyses about cellular markers of infection-induced systemic inflammation also displayed a divergent modulation depending on the metabolic state of the host. Ob/ob mice showed lesser changes in these markers, compared to WT lean and HFD-fed WT mice. However, given our short-term gavage-based infection model and taking into account the kinetics of the observed changes, these parameters should be considered supportive rather than definitive biomarkers.

The increase in IL-1β and IL-18 gene expression observed in the colon of WT lean mice infected with the genotoxic E. coli SP15 strain prompt us to perform an exploratory study on the NLRP3 inflammasome. When we pharmacologically inhibited the NLRP3 inflammasome in WT lean mice infected with the genotoxic E. coli SP15 strain, we observed a reduction in markers of colon inflammation and a change in both gut microbiota and inferred microbiome. We may hypothesize that, during infection, the sensing of pathobiont via NLRP3 may play a proinflammatory role in healthy mice. Following the inhibition of NLRP3, we observed a robust trend toward a decrease of IL-1β gene expression in all of the four mouse models herein studied (WT and ob mice, naïve and infected with E. coli SP15), as already described in colonic tissue explants from a mouse model of colitis, i.e., “Winnie” mice.25 This result suggests multiple and diverse pathways of cytokine production depending on the model of gut inflammation. By contrast, the trend toward the increase in IL-18, observed in naïve MCC950-treated WT and ob mice, suggests the contribute of other sources of IL-18, i.e., inflammasomes not inhibited by MCC950 and not activated by PAMPs. To note that the pks island responsible for colibactin production in the genotoxic E. coli SP15 strain also produces other molecules such as siderophore-microcin that act as antimicrobial peptides.26 Therefore, these molecules may also account for the herein observed effects ascribed to genotoxic E. coli SP15.

Inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome in ob/ob mice, showed a trend toward increased colonic inflammation, regardless of the genotoxic E. coli SP15 infection. Thus, we may hypothesize that, during infection, the sensing of pathogens via NLRP3 may play an anti-inflammatory role in ob/ob mice. By contrast, NLRP3 suppression was shown to improve metabolism and lifespan in HFD-induced obese mice.27 Previous work by Caesar and colleagues showed in E. coli-monocolonized axenic mice an LPS-induced impaired glucose and insulin tolerance.28 Of note, both LPS and NLRP3 signaling are linked since the LPS-induced activation of TLR4 promotes the synthesis of pro-IL-1β, which is then cleaved and activated to IL-1β by the NLRP3-activated caspase-1.

The most unexpected result of our study was the general reduction of proinflammatory markers, especially IL-18 in the colon, in infected HFD-fed WT mice, regardless of the E. coli strain. These observations are in accordance with the “obesity paradox”, which reports that patients with obesity resist diverse diseases compared to healthy subjects. Indeed, obesity may also be a beneficial condition in response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors in cancer therapy, during which patients with obesity respond to the treatment better than healthy subjects.29 Consistent with this paradox, an attenuated oxidative, inflammatory and apoptotic response was observed in obese mice infected with E. coli.30 In our study, the divergent response to E. coli infection may be explained, at least in part, by the ability of the E. coli strains to colonize the intestine of recipient mice. In fact, with no antibiotics, both E. coli SP15 and NRG857c did not significantly increase in the ob/ob nor in the HFD-fed WT mice, after the first infection. On the other hand, it is known that metabolic patients may display increased susceptibility to infections compared to healthy subjects.12 Therefore, identifying the molecular regulators operating at the host-to-microbiota interface will be essential for understanding how the host orchestrates a response to E. coli infection and for delineating the pathways that govern this response.

Limitations of the study

This study is subject to several limitations. The leptin-deficient ob/ob mouse model presents inherent constraints, as its immune and inflammatory alterations are driven by leptin deficiency rather than obesity itself, potentially affecting responses to E. coli infection. Wild-type (WT) mice were used as controls for ob/ob mice because ob/+ heterozygotes carry a single mutant leptin allele which has a significant metabolic impact31; using WT mice avoids the heterozygote effects of ob/+ mice and provides a clear baseline for comparison to the fully leptin-deficient ob/ob phenotype. However, WT mice originate from a different breeding colony and therefore do not share the same genetic and microbial background. In addition, obesity-related immune hypo-responsiveness and differences between genetic and diet-induced obesity models may restrict the broader applicability of the findings. PICRUSt2-based functional predictions should be interpreted cautiously, as they rely on computational inference rather than on a direct assessment of microbial activity. Also, the use of only male mice may restrict our conclusions. Finally, the sample size may occasionally limit the statistical power of more complex analyses such as ANOVA, post-hoc tests, or Pearson’s correlations, although the consistent trends observed across experimental groups support the robustness of our conclusions.

Experimental models and study participant details

Animal models, antibiotics (ABx) cocktail treatment and tissue collection

All mice used in this study are wild-type (WT) or ob/ob C57Bl/6 male mice from the same vendor (Janvi-er, France) and 12-week-old at the beginning of each treatment, as reported in the time-line of each protocol herein described (Figures 6A and 7A; Figures S1A, S1B, S4A, S8A, S9A, and S10A). All mice were acclimated for two weeks before any intervention. WT male mice were fed either an NC or a 60% energy HFD (D12492i, Research Diet32). During the acclimation period, we performed a bedding mixing, among all cages of a given protocol, to control and/or limit any starting microbiota difference and putative cage effect. Then, for each E. coli strain used, the mice were infected with 2 × 109 Colony Forming Unit (CFU)/mouse by intragastric gavage, once (Figures 6A and 7A; Figure S4A) or once per week for three weeks, according to the protocol (Figures S1A and S1B). Mice were sacrificed in a fed condition by cervical dislocation and the liver, colon and cecal content were collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. When applied, the antibiotics used were: ampicillin, neomycin, carbenicillin and metronidazole each at 0.5 g/L, for two weeks, in drinking water (from the animal facility) or streptomycin (20 mg/100 μL water/mouse, once by gavage, 24h before infection or PBS), according to the described protocol. Mice treated with antibiotics has “ABx” in the name of the belonging group. All of the E. coli strains used were human-derived streptomycin-resistant strains recovered from infected naïve mice, to avoid the impact of strain adaptation to the host intestine during our study. The ARRIVE guidelines have been followed for all of the animal experimental procedures, approved by the local ethics committee (authorization n° APAFIS#8506–2017012716362727 v4 on 02/11/2017 and APAFIS#8111–2016120716262061 v10 on 07/11/2017) of the Purpan University Hospital (Toulouse, France).

Strain cultivation and count

E. coli HS is a commensal strain33; E. coli SP15 is an extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC), serotype O18:K1, isolated from a patient with neonatal meningitis.5 E. coli SP15 harbors a pks island that produces colibactin.34 E. coli NRG857c is a proinflammatory strain isolated from the ileum of a patient with Crohn’s disease.7 For each strain, a preinoculum of 5 mL of Lysogeny Broth (LB) was incubated overnight at 37°C and 240 rpm. Then, the bacterial suspensions were diluted 1:100 in LB to a final volume of 25 mL and incubated for 2.5 h at 37°C and 240 rpm. The bacterial pellet was recovered by centrifugation at room temperature for 10 min at 5000 rpm and washed twice with sterile PBS. Optical density was measured at 600 nm. Mice were given 100 μL of a final bacterial suspension at 2 × 1010 CFU/mL, for a total of 2 × 109 CFU per gavage per mouse, once or once per week for three weeks, according to the protocol. Total Enterobacteria colonies were counted on sorbitol MacConkey agar plates (Oxoid, CM0813, England) at the related time point; E. coli SP15 and NRG857c colonies were counted on 50 μg/mL-Streptomycin-supplemented MacConkey agar plates, at the related time point and at least two days post infection to avoid overcounting of bacteria that did not stably colonize the gut.

Method details

Analysis of cellular markers of systemic inflammation

Cellular markers of systemic inflammation such as circulating platelets, lymphocytes, granulocytes and precursors of eosinophils and monocytes were monitored at baseline (basal), 2H or 4H following infection in tail-vein-collected total blood (9 μL) via a Cell-Dynn hematocrit analyzer (Abbott, France).

Metabolic analyses

OGTT was performed as previously reported35 at −30, 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated by the trapezoidal rule36 using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (84) for Windows 11 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and is shown as mmol/l × min. For OGTT following antibiotics cocktail and HFD feeding mice were given the same dose of glucose as the average dose calculated among all mice (on a basis of 0.5 mg/g of body weight), to avoid overdosage37 and, hence, misinterpretation due to respective treatments.

Gene expression analysis by qPCR

Liver and colon gene expression analyses were performed as previously described.35 Briefly, total RNA was extracted via the miRNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN, Courtaboeuf, France) and 1 μg was reverse transcribed using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR from BIO-RAD (Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Then, cDNA (50 ng) was used in duplicate in the qPCR assay using the SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix from Bio-Rad (Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Runs were obtained on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time System machine and analyzed with Bio-Rad CFX Maestro 2.3, version 5.3.022.1030. The following genes were analyzed, with abbreviation and Bio-Rad product number in brackets: Cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide (Camp, qMmuCED0004087), Trefoil factor 3 (Tff3, qMmuCID0008757), Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α, qMmuCED0004141), Interferon-γ (IFN-γ, qMmuCID0006268), Interleukine-1β (IL-1β, qMmuCID0005641), Interleukine-18 (IL-18, qMmuCID0005876), Caspase-1 (Casp-1, qMmuCID0026983), Interleukine-10 (IL-10, qMmuCID0015452), Mucin-2 (Muc-2, qMmuCID0019583), Tight junction protein-1 (Tjp-1, qMmuCID0005277), Occludin (Ocln, qMmuCID0005446), normalized by β-actin (qMmuCED0027505). The inflammatory index was calculated as the sum of delta percentages related to the inflammatory genes TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-18 and Casp-1.

Pharmacological treatment with MCC950

This shortened protocol, compared to that in Figure 1A, was conceived to boost genotoxic E. coli SP15 infection, to better appreciate the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Since body weight did not significantly vary in either interventional protocol (Figures S13E and S13Q), mice were intraperitoneally administered 50 mg/kg (calculated on the average body weight of all mice) of MCC950 (ADIPOGEN Life Sciences, Switzerland), a reversible NLRP3 inflammasome-specific inhibitor,24 in PBS, three times (−20h/-1h/0h before infection with E. coli SP15 strain) as reported in Figures 6A and 7A. MCC950 efficacy was validated in colon lysates from naïve uninfected 9-week-old C57Bl/6NRj female mice applying the same protocol reported in Figures 6A and 7A. In these mice, according to our experimental protocol, MCC950 treatment resulted in a reduction of IL-18 secretion exceeding 4-fold (489 ± 44 pg/mL/μg of protein, in MCC950 treated mice (n = 3) vs. ≥ 2000 pg/mL/μg of protein in control untreated mice, n = 2; Mouse IL-18 ELISA, Invitrogen). E. coli SP15 strain was prepared as previously described. A final bacterial suspension at 2 × 1010 CFU/mL was prepared and mice were given 100 μL of bacterial suspension for a total of 2 × 109 CFU/mL per gavage per mouse.

Taxonomic and inferred functional analysis of gut microbiota

Total DNA was extracted38 from feces or caecum content (for ob/ob only, due to reduced frequency of defecation and thus to avoid bias in the collection time). The 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 regions were targeted by the 357wf-785R primers and analyzed by MiSeq at RTLGenomics (Texas, USA). A complete description of Data Analysis Methodology for Microbial Diversity is available and can be found at the link https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5807c0ce579fb39e1dd6addd/t/6859bf8fde14344637dcf7b6/1750712208217/6.23.2025+Data_Analysis_Methodology.3.0.1.pdf. An average of 33784 sequences was generated per sample. LDA score graphs were drawn and predictive functional analysis of the gut microbiome was performed using the Huttenhower Galaxy web application via the LEfSe39 or PICRUSt2 algorithm,40 respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using either 2-ANOVA, the restricted maximum likelihood method followed by a two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger and Yekutieli to correct for multiple comparisons by controlling the False Discovery Rate (<0.05), Kruskal-Wallis test plus the two-stage step-up method described above, Mann-Whitney or One Sample t test, as indicated, using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (84) for Windows 11 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Values were considered significant when p < 0.05 or as reported after Bonferroni’s correction. For LDA score graphs, alpha value for factorial Kruskal-Wallis test among classes and alpha value for pairwise Wilcoxon test between subclasses were changed to p < 0.01 or as shown on figures. Principal component analyses (PCA) were performed and OTU-based diversity indices were calculated using PAST5.2.1 version. Axes on the PCAs related to the PICRUSt2 data are shown as Eigen values to reduce the graph size. Pearson’s correlation analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version as above).
