Inherited bone marrow failure with ERCC6L2 gene mutation: presentation of aplastic anemia in a 3-year-old child: a case report

Background

Aplastic anemia (AA) is a rare hematologic condition characterized by the combination of hypocellularity or aplasia of the bone marrow and cytopenia in at least two of the three main lines of blood cells: red blood cells (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs), and platelets. There is no clear association with race or ethnicity. AA affects both sexes equally, with a sex ratio of 1:1. Although AA can occur at any age, it has a bimodal peak age distribution, with peaks in young adults (20–25 years) and in older adults over the age of 60 years [1, 2].The decrease in mature blood cells in AA is attributed mainly to direct insults to the bone marrow, immune-mediated pathogenesis, and inherited or acquired bone marrow failure syndromes [4]. The most common etiology of AA is considered to be idiopathic, approximately 65% of cases. Direct insults to the bone marrow can result from drugs, chemicals, and ionizing radiation. Viral etiologies constitute a substantial share of cases. Among hereditary causes, Fanconi anemia (FA) is the most common accounting 25% of cases. Inherited bone marrow failure syndrome (IBMFS) is a rare cause of AA, with varying degrees of bone marrow failure. The four most common syndromes are FA, dyskeratosis congenita (DC) in 10–15% of cases, Diamond–Blackfan anemia (DBA) in 5–10% of cases, and Shwachman–Diamond syndrome (SDS) in 5–10% of cases. Another rare cause of bone marrow failure is ERCC6L2-related disease [3–6].The ERCC excision repair 6-like 2 (ERCC6L2) gene is located on chromosome 9 and encodes helicase-like proteins involved in transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair and cell proliferation. Germline homozygous mutations in children and adults predispose them to a unique form of bone marrow failure with a high risk of hematologic malignancies, especially acute myeloid leukemia (AML). ERCC6L2-related disease was first identified a decade ago in two patients, and since then, 31 additional cases have been reported, all with a high risk of AML and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). According to reports, patients with this disease present with cytopenias, with thrombocytopenia being the most common manifestation, followed by leukopenia and anemia. Patients may also present with short stature, developmental delay, intellectual disability, low-set prominent ears, a pointed prominent chin, deep-set eyes and café-au-lait spots. The uniqueness of our case report lies in the presence of an emerging ERCC6L2-related disease in a 3-year-old Ethiopian child. ERCC6L2-related disease has been reported in limited cases worldwide, mainly from Europe and North America, with emerging reports from Latin America and Eastern Europe. To date, reports from Africa are extermely rare. This condition has not yet been reported in our country so far. Our team believes this case report will allow fellow clinicians to gain a better understanding of IBMFS, enabling earlier diagnosis and improved treatment options for patients with the condition.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old Ethiopian female initially presented with a one-week history of dry, intermittent cough, low-grade fever, loss of appetite, and easy fatigability. She was evaluated at a local health center and was empirically treated for malaria and pneumonia for five days before presenting to our hospital. However, the abovementioned symptoms persisted, and she subsequently developed gum bleeding and worsening fatigue. A complete blood count (CBC) performed at the local health center revealed pancytopenia, prompting immediate referral to our hospital for further evaluation. On presentation to our facility, she appeared fatigued, although her vital signs were within normal limits (Table 1).

The child’s past medical history was unremarkable. She had no known chronic illnesses or history of long-term medication use. Her immunizations were up to date for her age. Family history could not be obtained, as she was raised in an orphanage. Her growth and developmental milestones were reportedly comparable to those of her peers, but she had delayed language development, speaking only one word at a time.

Physical examination revealed pale conjunctiva, palmar pallor, and distinct facial features suggestive of Fanconi anemia and other IBMFS, including a triangular face, microcephaly, and a prominent chin. She had mild gum bleeding, but no lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly was noted. Multiple hyperpigmented lesions, including café-au-lait spots, were observed on her back. Her chest was clear, with no added breath sounds (Fig. 1).

Café-au-lait spot

Upon presentation to our hospital, basic investigations, including CBC, electrolytes, liver function tests, renal function tests, coagulation profiles, and viral markers, were performed. Initial laboratory results confirmed pancytopenia. Serum electrolytes, liver and renal function tests, and coagulation profiles were all within the normal range. The direct Coombs test was negative. Viral markers were negative for HIV, HBsAg, HCV, CMV, EBV, and HSV. Serum vitamin B12 and folate levels were in the normal range. Autoimmune screening, including ANA and anti-double-stranded DNA, was negative.

The patient was immediately given blood products, including packed red blood cells, platelets, and fresh frozen plasma, after which her bleeding and fatigue subsided. Following bone marrow evaluation, a diagnosis of AA was confirmed. Owing to the early age of onset, unavailability of family history, physical features suggestive of FA, DC, DBA, and SDS, and the financial feasibility of further testing through her legal guardians (orphanage), genetic testing via whole exome sequencing (WES) was recommended to explore inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFS), including FA, DC, DBA, and SDS.

The patient was discharged and linked to our hematology clinic for outpatient follow-up after one week. A repeated CBC was performed at the hematology clinic, and she received packed red blood cells and platelets.

The genetic study results, which were obtained three weeks later, revealed a mutation in the ERCC6L2 gene.

Allogeneic stem cell transplantation is recommended as the optimal treatment option given the patient’s age and diagnosis. However, due to financial constraints, treatment abroad was not immediately possible. Over the following eight months, the patient was hospitalized twice for community-acquired pneumonia and severe pancytopenia, during which she was managed appropriately and discharged.

She has remained under regular follow-up at our hematology clinic and is currently scheduled for allogeneic stem cell transplantation abroad.

Discussion

AA is a hematologic condition characterized by pancytopenia and hypocellular bone marrow. Although acquired causes of AA are more common, the IBMFS represents a significant subset, especially in pediatric populations. These syndromes often present with early-onset pancytopenia and may be associated with congenital anomalies, growth retardation, and developmental delays. Correctly diagnosing the underlying etiology is crucial for appropriate management and prognostication [12–15].

IBMFS encompasses a diverse group of disorders, including Fanconi anemia (FA), dyskeratosis congenita (DC), Shwachman–Diamond syndrome (SDS), Diamond–Blackfan anemia (DBA), and, more recently, ERCC6L2-related disease. These conditions are typically inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and are characterized by hematologic abnormalities and various extra-medullary manifestations. FA, for instance, is associated with increased chromosomal breakage upon exposure to DNA cross-linking agents, a hallmark diagnostic feature [13, 15].

Owing to the patient’s age and physical findings, an inherited cause was strongly suspected. Chromosomal breakage testing for FA was not performed; however, the absence of typical features such as skeletal abnormalities, genitourinary malformations, or growth retardation coupled with normal chromosomal studies reduced the likelihood of FA [13, 15]. Consequently, whole-exome sequencing was performed, and the possibility of FA was ruled out.

In comparison with previously reported 31 cases of ERCC6L2-related bone marrow failure syndrome, our patient demonstrates several notable similarities and differences. While the disease was described as a predominantly adolescent or young adult onset (median age ~ 19 years) with generally mild bone marrow failure and trilineage cytopenia, our patient presented at a much earlier age (3 years) with severe pancytopenia and hypocellular marrow consistent with aplastic anemia. Additionally, the presence of syndromic features in our patient, including microcephaly, developmental delay, café-au-lait spots, and craniofacial dysmorphism, overlaps with reported extra-hematological manifestations in the previous cases [7].

Ultimately, whole-exome sequencing revealed compound heterozygous mutations in the ERCC6L2 gene, confirming the diagnosis of ERCC6L2-associated bone marrow failure syndrome. ERCC6L2 encodes a DNA helicase implicated in transcription coupled nucleotide excision repair and mitochondrial DNA maintenance. Mutations in this gene are associated with hematologic failure and increased susceptibility to myelodysplastic syndromes(MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia(AML) [9, 10, 16].

In our patient, in addition to cytopenias, microcephaly, delayed language development, and café-au-lait spots were noted—consistent with the phenotypic features reported in ERCC6L2 syndrome. These systemic effects are thought to arise from the gene’s role in both nuclear and mitochondrial genome integrity. Similar extra-medullary findings, including developmental delays, growth abnormalities, and neurocognitive impairments, have been reported in the literature, as documented by Tummala et al. and Ballew et al. [9, 10].

Importantly, there was no evidence of marrow dysplasia or increased blasts aligning with the known preleukemic state often observed in ERCC6L2-related disease. While patients are currently asymptomatic for myelodysplasia or leukemia, the identification of ERCC6L2 mutations necessitates long-term hematologic surveillance given the established risk of progression to MDS and AML [9–11].

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the rare presentation of the disease in Africa and addressing a clinical importance of having a high index of suspicion of IBMFSs in pediatric patients. The addition of ERCC6L2 to the IBMFS spectrum proves the evolving landscape of bone marrow failure etiologies and the necessity of personalized medicine in guiding therapy and long-term monitoring.
