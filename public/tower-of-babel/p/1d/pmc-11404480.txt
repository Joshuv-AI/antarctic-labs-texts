Superexchange Electron Transfer and Protein Matrix in the Charge-Separation Process of Photosynthetic Reaction Centers
Authors: Keisuke Saito, Hiroyuki Tamura, Hiroshi Ishikita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In type-II reaction centers, such as photosystem II (PSII) and reaction centers from purple bacteria (PbRC), light-induced charge separation involves electron transfer from pheophytin (PheoD1) to quinone (QA), occurring near a conserved tryptophan residue (D2-Trp253 in PSII and Trp-M252 in PbRC). This study investigates the route of the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer, focusing on the superexchange coupling (|HPheoD1···QA|) in the PSII protein environment. |HPheoD1···QA| is significantly larger for the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer via the unoccupied molecular orbitals of D2-Trp253 ([Trp]•–-like intermediate state, 0.73 meV) compared to direct electron transfer (0.13 meV), suggesting that superexchange is the dominant mechanism in the PSII protein environment. While the overall impact of the protein environment is limited, local interactions, particularly H-bonds, enhance superexchange electron transfer by directly affecting the delocalization of molecular orbitals. The D2-W253F mutation significantly decreases the electron transfer rate. The conservation of D2-Trp253/D1-Phe255 (Trp-M252/Phe-L216 in PbRC) in the two branches appears to differentiate superexchange coupling, contributing to the branches being either active or inactive in electron transfer.

Light-induced charge-separation is a fundamental process for energy conversion of photosynthetic reaction centers, including type-I (e.g., photosystem I) and type-II reaction centers (e.g., photosystem II: PSII and reaction centers from purple bacteria: PbRC). In type-II reaction centers, four (bacterio)chlorophylls (PD1, PD2, ChlD1, and ChlD2 in PSII and PA, PB, BA, and BB in PbRC), two (bacterio)pheophytins (PheoD1 and PheoD2 in PSII and HA and HB in PbRC), and two quinones (QA and QB) form a pair of electron transfer branches (D1 and D2 in PSII and A and B in PbRC) (Figure 1a,b). The electronic coupling between PA and PB is significantly larger in PbRC (114 meV) than that between PD1 and PD2 in PSII (13 meV) due to the difference in overlap of π-orbitals between the monomer pigments.1 Thus, PA and PB can specifically form the special pair, PAPB, with the lowest excitation energy in PbRC. In PSII, in addition to the small electronic coupling between PD1 and PD2, the presence of the water-splitting components, such as acidic and polar residues serving as proton-exit pathways or ligand residues of the Mn4CaO5 cluster, results in monomeric ChlD1 having the lowest excitation energy in PSII.1 Consequently, PAPB serves as the initial electron donor in the PbRC, whereas ChlD1 serves this role.

In PbRC, the initial electron acceptor BA in the active branch has a higher redox potential for one-electron reduction than BB in the inactive branch, facilitating the initial electron transfer.2 In PSII, the initial electron acceptor PheoD1 in the active branch has a higher redox potential than PheoD2 in the inactive branch.2,3 These redox potential values are directly associated with the driving force for electron transfer,4−6 a key factor differentiating the activity between the two branches.

Despite differences in the initial electron donor, these two type-II reaction centers share a common pathway for electron transfer from pheophytin (PheoD1 in PSII and HA in PbRC) to quinone (QA in PSII and PbRC) in the active branches (D1 branch in PSII and A branch in PbRC). These cofactors are conserved in the symmetrically located inactive branches, such as PheoD2 and QB in PSII and HB and QB in PbRC, but they do not participate in electron transfer.

For nonadiabatic electron transfer, the driving force (i.e., the difference in the redox potential), the outer-sphere reorganization energy, and the electronic coupling between the donor and acceptor groups are crucial.4 The electron transfer rate reaches its maximum when the driving force equals the outer-sphere reorganization energy. Among them, electronic coupling, characterized by the overlap of the molecular orbitals between electron donor and acceptor groups, is correlated with the edge-to-edge distances.5,6 In type-II reaction centers, the two electron transfer pathways are arranged along the pseudo-C2 axis. The edge-to-edge distances for the corresponding electron donor and acceptor pairs are slightly (∼10%) shorter in the active branch than in the inactive branches for the PheoD1···QA and PheoD2···QB pairs in PSII (8.3–8.6 and 9.3–9.4 Å7,8) and the HA···QA and HB···QB pairs in PbRC (8.8–9.5 and 10.3–10.7 Å9−11) (Table S1). Although not specifically investigated, the shorter distance is likely to contribute to slightly larger electronic coupling in the active branch compared with the inactive branch.

Besides the inherent cofactor arrangement, it is important to note that electronic coupling is also influenced by the protein matrix between electron donor and acceptor groups.5,6,12,13 Notably, D2-Trp253 is situated near the PheoD1 and QA moiety in the active branch of PSII, apparently forming a π–π stacking interaction with QA (Figure 1c). In contrast, the corresponding residue in the inactive D2 branch is a smaller aromatic residue, D1-Phe255, located near the PheoD2 and QB moiety. If this π–π stacking interaction is indeed relevant, it suggests that D2-Trp253 is more likely electronically coupled with the electron donor site, potentially mediating “superexchange electron transfer”12,14 from PheoD1 to QA. The D2-Trp253/D1-Phe255 pair is also conserved as the Trp-M252/Phe-L216 pair in PbRC, implying functional significance for unidirectional electron transfer in type-II reaction centers.

While the role of the protein environment in tuning the redox potential values (i.e., energetics) of cofactors and facilitating electron transfer is well-established (e.g., see refs (2 and 15−17)), little has been reported on the characteristics of superexchange electron transfer in the protein environment, particularly the contribution of the protein environment to superexchange electron transfer (with limited exceptions, e.g., ref (18)). To date, no experimental method exists to directly measure electronic coupling.12 As a result, electronic coupling for electron transfer from HA to QA in PbRC, analogous to electron transfer from PheoD1 to QA in PSII, was primarily investigated through theoretical approaches.19,20 However, these studies did not elucidate the role of the protein environment in electronic coupling (e.g., due to the absence of the protein environment19). Moreover, the calculated electronic coupling in these studies was significantly underestimated, which failed to reproduce the experimentally observed rapid electron transfer rate, even in scenarios where the electron transfer rate is expected to be maximized, i.e., when the driving force equals the outer-sphere reorganization energy.

Here, we investigate the electron transfer route from PheoD1 to QA in PSII, revealing detailed characteristics of the electron transfer mechanism in the presence of the entire PSII protein environment using a quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics/polarizable continuum model (QM/MM/PCM).

When D2-Trp253 serves as an electron transfer route from PheoD1 to QA, its superexchange intermediate (virtual) state can be characterized by tryptophan species. The species using unoccupied molecular orbitals resembles [Trp]•–, while the species using occupied molecular orbitals resembles [Trp]•+ (Figure 2).

In the scenario where electron transfer proceeds sequentially from PheoD1 to D2-Trp253 and then to QA (PheoD1 → Trp → QA), the [Trp]•–-like species is involved in the superexchange pathway (Figure 2a). The corresponding coupling can be calculated for unoccupied molecular orbitals between PheoD1 and QA via the molecular orbital of D2-Trp253 (i.e., superexchange coupling via the [Trp]•–-like intermediate state). The present QM/MM/PCM calculation yields a net superexchange coupling between PheoD1 and QA (|HPheoD1···QA|) of 0.73 meV (Table 1).

In contrast, when electron transfer to PheoD1 triggers subsequent electron transfer from D2-Trp253 to QA, followed by electron transfer from PheoD1 to D2-Trp253 (Trp → QA/PheoD1 → Trp), the [Trp]•+-like species is involved in the superexchange pathway (Figure 2b). The coupling in this case is calculated for occupied molecular orbitals between PheoD1 and QA via the molecular orbital of D2-Trp253 (i.e., superexchange coupling via the [Trp]•+-like intermediate state), resulting in |HPheoD1···QA| of 0.09 meV (Table 1).

Although |HPheoD1···QA| is significantly smaller for superexchange via the [Trp]•+-like intermediate state compared to the [Trp]•–-like intermediate state, it is important to note that the electronic coupling between PheoD1 and D2-Trp253 and that between D2-Trp253 and QA are nearly identical (double arrows, Figure 3). The substantially smaller |HPheoD1···QA| for the [Trp]•+-like intermediate is due to (i) the most contributing orbital in the [Trp]•+-like intermediate (the highest occupied molecular orbital, HOMO) being less stable (ΔEoccupied(1) = 9.95 eV, eq 4) than the corresponding orbital in the [Trp]•–-like intermediate (the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital, LUMO) (ΔEunoccupied(1) = 3.48 eV, eq 2) and (ii) the magnitude of each orbital’s contribution to |HPheoD1···QA| in the [Trp]•+-like intermediate being smaller than that in the [Trp]•–-like intermediate (Figure 3).

[Trp]•+ is observed at pH < 3 upon oxidation,22 and to the best of our knowledge, [Trp]•– has not been reported. However, the [Trp]•– and [Trp]•+-like intermediate states are virtual states for superexchange, existing only in the presence of substantial electronic coupling with either the electron donor or the electron acceptor moieties. These states are distinct from [Trp]•– and [Trp]•+ species in water and are not thermally accessible.21 Therefore, the superexchange from PheoD1 to QA via the [Trp]•–-like intermediate state, which is more favorable than the [Trp]•+-like intermediate state, is not directly related to the reported radical species of tryptophan in water. In fact, even though [Trp]•+ species can exist, they are only found at pH < 3, whereas the deprotonated neutral radical [Trp]• species is more relevant at pH > 3.22 Furthermore, if [Trp]•– or [Trp]•+ species were to form during the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer, the process would no longer be considered “superexchange via D2-Trp253”, but rather a process involving D2-Trp253 as a redox-active cofactor along with PheoD1 and QA. Below, we specifically focus on the superexchange mechanism via the [Trp]•–-like intermediate state.

To access the relevance of the superexchange electron transfer from PheoD1 to QA via D2-Trp253, the direct electronic coupling between PheoD1 and QA, without involving D2-Trp253, is also calculated. The QM/MM/PCM calculation shows that the resulting electronic coupling, |HPheoD1···QA|, decreases significantly to 0.13 meV compared to the case with the superexchange electron transfer via the [Trp]•–-like intermediate state (Table 1). Since electron transfer preferentially occurs along routes with larger electronic coupling (while still occurring along all available routes), the route involving D2-Trp253 is more dominant than the direct route, as long as D2-Trp253 is present in PSII. Therefore, D2-Trp253 plays a crucial role in facilitating the electron transfer from PheoD1 to QA.

Given that the actual electron transfer from PheoD1 to QA proceeds through all of the available routes, it is essential to use the overall electronic coupling to estimate the experimentally measured electron transfer rate. Using an overall electronic coupling of 0.51 meV [i.e., |H(direct ET) – {H(superexchange via [Trp]•–) + H(superexchange via [Trp]•+}| = |0.13 – (0.73 – 0.09)|, eq 1] and taking into account the experimentally measured rate constant for the electron transfer (e.g., 300 to 40023 or 350 ± 100 ps), along with the driving force of −0.36 to −0.4 eV (e.g., Em(PheoD1) = −0.5 V2,3,24 and Em(QA) = −0.125 to −0.14 V26,27), the reorganization energy for the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer is calculated to be 0.63 eV (Figure 4). This value falls within the range typically observed for electron transfer in protein environments.6 The slightly smaller driving force compared to the outer-sphere reorganization energy suggests that the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer in PSII remains within the normal region of Marcus theory,4 where an increase in driving force leads to an increase in electron transfer rate. Notably, the identification of the relevant values for electronic coupling and outer-sphere reorganization energy, as well as the elucidation that the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer occurs within the normal region of Marcus theory, was not apparent in previous estimations for the corresponding electronic coupling in PbRC.19,20 This discrepancy is due to their significantly underestimated electronic coupling values (e.g., an order of magnitude smaller19), which prevented them from numerically determining the outer-sphere reorganization energy required to reproduce the experimentally measured electron transfer rate.

At the PheoD1 and D2-Trp253 interface, the keto O site of PheoD1 provides the dominant electron transfer route to D1-Trp253 in the relevant electron transfer (Figure S1). The distance between the keto O site of PheoD1 and the Cη(2) site of D2-Trp253 is the shortest (OPheoD1···CD2-Trp253 = 3.2 Å). At the D2-Trp253 and QA interface, the significant overlap between the Cδ(1) site of D2-Trp253 and an aromatic C site of QA forms π–π interactions (CD2-Trp253···CQA = 3.3 Å), resulting in a significantly large electronic coupling between the LUMO of D2-Trp253 and the LUMO of QA (166 meV) compared to the electronic coupling between the LUMO of PheoD1 and the LUMO of D2-Trp253 (21 meV) (Figure 3a).

The larger electronic coupling between D2-Trp253 and QA compared to the coupling between PheoD1 and D2-Trp253 suggests that D2-Trp253 interacts more strongly with QA via hybridization of their molecular orbitals, forming the [D2-Trp253···QA] complex. Thus, D2-Trp253 plays a role in molecular extension of QA, making the entire [D2-Trp253···QA] complex serve as the electron acceptor of PheoD1. This example also provides implications for the architecture of the electron transfer pathway; to maximize the overlap of molecular orbitals between the electron donor and acceptor moieties, tryptophan should preferably be placed so that its long axis (Cη(2)/Cζ(3)···Cδ(1)) aligns with the electron transfer route (Figure 3a).

To examine the impact of the PSII protein environment on superexchange coupling, the superexchange coupling for PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer via D2-Trp253 is also calculated in water, removing the PSII protein environment. The calculated value of |HPheoD1···QA| decreases only slightly upon the removal of the PSII protein environment (Table 2, Figure 5). While the intermediate virtual state is more stable in water (e.g., ΔEunoccupied(1) = 3.10 eV) than in the PSII protein environment (e.g., ΔEunoccupied(1) = 3.47 eV), the resulting |HPheoD1···QA| is marginally larger in the PSII protein environment than in water (Table 2). This finding suggests that the protein environment enhances the electronic couplings between PheoD1 and D2-Trp253 and D2-Trp253 and QA, leading to a slight increase in the overall superexchange coupling |HPheoD1···QA| despite the destabilization of the intermediate state in the protein environment.

The contribution of the protein environment to superexchange electron transfer is primarily through local interactions that directly influence delocalization of the molecular orbitals of the electron donor and acceptor moieties. These interactions include H-bonds between PheoD1 and D1-Gln130 at the electron donor site and between QA and D2-His214 at the electron acceptor site. Specifically, D1-Gln130 donates an H-bond to the keto O site of PheoD1, the site that provides the primary route for electron transfer to D2-Trp253 (Figure S1). By donating this H-bond, D1-Gln130 not only raises the redox potential for the one-electron reduction of PheoD128 but also stabilizes and delocalizes the LUMO of PheoD1, thereby enhancing the electronic coupling between PheoD1 and D2-Trp253 in the PSII protein environment. Similarly, D2-His214, a ligand of the nonheme Fe complex, donates an H-bond to QA, stabilizing the LUMO of QA and increasing its redox potential.29 Removing the protein environment eliminates these local interactions, destabilizing the LUMOs of PheoD1 and QA and reducing their delocalization, which in turn diminishes the PheoD1···D2-Trp253 and D2-Trp253···QA couplings.

Thus, the primary contribution of the protein environment to superexchange coupling lies in local interactions, particularly H-bonds that directly affect the delocalization of the molecular orbitals of electron donor and acceptor moieties. However, the magnitude of this contribution is limited (|HPheoD1···QA| = 0.73 meV in protein and 0.71 meV in water, Tables 1 and 2), as typical H-bonds do not significantly alter the molecular orbitals of the interacting redox-active groups (for remarkable exceptions, see ref (18)). Therefore, the most significant role of the protein matrix in superexchange coupling lies in determining the cofactor arrangement, enhanced by its “preorganized protein environment”.30 The specific arrangement of cofactors in the protein environment, which cannot be achieved in bulk water, is crucial for effective superexchange coupling.

An exception to the limited impact of H-bonds on superexchange coupling occurs when significant changes in the size of the H-bond network occur during electron transfer. In PbRC, during the superexchange QA-to-QB electron transfer via the nonheme Fe complex, the formation of an H-bond between QB and Ser-L223 significantly extends the H-bond network from [QA···Fe···QB] to [QA···Fe···QB···Ser-L223···Asp-L213···Asp-M17].31 This results in a substantial increase in superexchange coupling from 1.1 to 3.5 meV, facilitating electron transfer from QA to QB.18 Such a change in the H-bond network size is not involved in the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer via D2-Trp253, explaining why the protein environment, specifically H-bonds, has a limited impact on typical superexchange electron transfer.

While D2-Trp253 adjacent to PheoD1 and QA in PSII is conserved as Trp-M252 adjacent to HA and QA in PbRC, the corresponding tryptophan is replaced with phenylalanine (Phe-L216 and D1-Phe255) in the inactive electron transfer branches of both PSII and PbRC. This raises the question of whether phenylalanine, an aromatic residue, would facilitate superexchange electron transfer between PheoD2 and QB in the inactive D2 branch if PheoD2•– were to form. To investigate this, D2-Trp253 is replaced with phenylalanine, and the superexchange electronic coupling for the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer via D2-Phe253 is calculated, assuming that phenylalanine serves as the intermediate (virtual) state. The calculated superexchange coupling of 0.22 meV (via the [Phe]•–-like intermediate state) in the D2-W253F mutant PSII is larger than the direct electronic coupling of 0.14 meV for PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer without involving D2-Phe253 (Table 1). Thus, in principle, D2-Phe253 facilitates superexchange electron transfer.

However, the smaller aromatic ring of phenylalanine in the D2-W253F mutant PSII leads to a decreased overlap of molecular orbitals between D2-253 and QA (Figure 6), significantly reducing the superexchange coupling for the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer (Table 1). Consequently, the rate constant for the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer is estimated to decrease to 1.0 ns at 300 K, ∼2.5 times slower than the rate constant of ∼400 ps in native PSII. The decrease in the π–π interaction between D2-253 and QA due to the D2-W253F mutation also destabilizes the LUMO of QA, lowering the QA redox potential and facilitating backward electron transfer from QA•–. Thus, the decrease in the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer shares the same origin as the observed increase in QA•– recombination yield upon the D2-W253F mutation.32,33

The present result also suggests that the PheoD2-to-QB electron transfer in the inactive D2-branch would have been 2.5 times less favorable than the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer in the active D1-branch in terms of electronic coupling, assuming the D1-Phe255 plane is parallel to the QB plane. In reality, the PSII crystal structure shows that the D1-Phe255 plane is almost perpendicular to the QB plane, as observed in the Phe-L216/QB pair in PbRC.34 Such changes in the orientation of the superexchange intermediate affect the electronic coupling by altering the overlap of the molecular orbitals. Thus, the superexchange coupling via D1-Phe255 is substantially absent in the PheoD2-to-QB electron transfer.

The difference in electronic coupling between electron donor and acceptor cofactors appears to contribute to the asymmetry in activity between the symmetrically arranged D1 and D2 branches, in addition to the difference in the redox potential values of the cofactors (e.g., see refs (1 and 2)). Notably, mutating His-M200 (the ligand of PB) to leucine, leading to the formation of a bacteriochlorophyll/bacteriopheophytin heterodimer special pair,35,36 or altering the Tyr-M210/Phe-L181 pair near BA/BB to phenylalanine/tyrosine37 could enhance initial charge separation via BB•– in PbRC. When one of these mutations was combined with an additional mutation of Phe-L216 to tryptophan, subsequent electron transfer along the B-branch was further facilitated.37 Although QB was removed in this reported multiple mutant PbRC for ultrafast transient absorption experiments,37 such mutations would likely facilitate electron transfer from HM to QB if QB were present.

In contrast, the presence of tryptophan even at the D1-255 position in the ancient D1 protein38 suggests that an ancestral PSII might have also facilitated superexchange electron transfer from PheoD2 to QB via D1-Trp255 in the D2 branch, resembling a homodimeric PSII, as proposed by Rutherford and Faller.39

Remarkably, for the superexchange electron transfer via D2-Phe253, the molecular orbital most contributing to |HPheoD1···QA| for superexchange via the [Phe]•–-like intermediate state (LUMO+3) involves its σ-orbitals at the H moieties, rather than π-orbitals at the aromatic C moieties (Figures 6 and S3). By using the σ-orbitals at the H moieties, phenylalanine can compensate for the spatial gap between PheoD1 and D2-Phe253, which is caused by its smaller aromatic ring size compared to that of tryptophan. This compensation maintains the electronic coupling with PheoD1 (30 meV, Figure 6a) at a level comparable to that between PheoD1 and D2-Trp253 (21 meV, Figure 3a). However, this most contributing orbital (LUMO+3) is significantly less stable (ΔEunoccupied(4) = 7.00 eV) than the LUMO (ΔEunoccupied(1) = 3.82 eV), resulting in a significant decrease in |HPheoD1···QA| to 0.22 meV compared to tryptophan (Figure 6).

Based on these observations, the following general outcomes for superexchange mechanisms can be deduced: (i) the intermediate state in superexchange electron transfer does not necessarily rely on π-orbitals but can also use σ-orbitals to maximize the overall electronic coupling between the electron donor and acceptor moieties; (ii) tryptophan appears to be the optimal amino acid residue for maximizing electronic coupling along its long axis, facilitating superexchange electron transfer, especially when bridging two redox-active cofactors. Although tryptophan is bulkier than phenylalanine, it is well accommodated and free from steric repulsion in the PSII protein environment, as indicated by the unaltered [PheoD1···D2-Trp253···QA] arrangement in the QM/MM-optimized geometry (see the Supporting Information for the QM/MM-optimized atomic coordinates). In fact, mutation of the corresponding tryptophan (Trp-M252) in PbRC to phenylalanine led to a 20% loss of QA,40 which suggests that evolutionary pressures have favored tryptophan over phenylalanine at this position in type-II reaction centers.

In summary, the superexchange coupling |HPheoD1···QA| for the PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer is significantly larger via unoccupied molecular orbitals of D2-Trp253 ([Trp]•–-like intermediate state, 0.73 meV) than via occupied molecular orbitals of D2-Trp253 ([Trp]•+-like intermediate state, 0.09 meV) (Figure 3). Furthermore, the direct electronic coupling between PheoD1 and QA, without involving D2-Trp253, is only 0.13 meV (Table 1). Consequently, PheoD1-to-QA electron transfer predominantly occurs via the [Trp]•–-like intermediate state with an outer-sphere reorganization energy of ∼0.6 eV (Figure 4). D2-Trp253 serves as an effective molecular extension of QA, making the entire [D2-Trp253···QA] complex the electron acceptor for PheoD1.

The primary contribution of the protein environment to superexchange coupling lies in local interactions, particularly H-bonds that directly affect the molecular orbitals of electron donor and acceptor moieties. However, the magnitude of this contribution is limited (Table 2 and Figure 5), except in specific cases.18 Instead, the role of the protein matrix is pronounced in determining the cofactor arrangement, which is most crucial for effective superexchange coupling.

Remarkably, the D2-W253F mutant PSII utilizes the σ-orbitals at the H moieties of D2-Phe253 rather than the π-orbitals at the aromatic C moieties, which suggests that superexchange electron transfer does not necessarily rely on π-orbitals but can use σ-orbitals to maximize the overall coupling (Figures 6 and S3). Nevertheless, tryptophan appears to be the optimal amino acid residue for maximizing electronic coupling along its long axis, facilitating superexchange electron transfer. This result may also explain the conservation of the D2-Trp253/D1-Phe255 and Trp-M252/Phe-L216 pairs in PSII and PbRC, respectively, highlighting their role in facilitating unidirectional electron transfer by differentiating the superexchange coupling between the two electron transfer branches.

While the importance of the driving force (redox potential) in electron transfer has been well studied in many systems, the present findings on superexchange coupling, an area that has not been extensively investigated and with few known characteristics, particularly for proteins,18,41 shed light on new aspects in protein electron transfer.

Computational Methods

The atomic coordinates were obtained from the X-ray structure of the PSII monomer unit (designated monomer A) of the PSII complexes from Thermosynechococcus vulcanus at a 1.9 Å resolution (PDB code: 3ARC).7 Atomic partial charges of amino acids were adopted from the all-atom CHARMM22 parameter set.42 D1-His337 was considered to be protonated.43 The electrostatic embedding QM/MM scheme was employed, explicitly considering the electrostatic and steric effects generated by the protein environment. To perform QM/MM calculations, the Qsite program44 was used, employing the restricted density functional theory (DFT) method with the B3LYP functional and 6-31G* basis sets. The QM region was defined as PheoD1 (excluding the phytyl tail), the side chain of D2-Trp253 in native PSII (D2-Phe253 in the D2-W253F mutant PSII), and QA (excluding the tail, which was H-capped at C11). The MM region was fixed at the original X-ray coordinates, while the H atoms in the MM region were optimized using the OPLS2005 force field.45 All atomic coordinates in the QM region were fully relaxed (i.e., not fixed) in the QM/MM calculations. See the Supporting Information for the QM/MM-optimized atomic coordinates.

The energetics and electronic couplings were calculated in the optimized geometry using the DFT method with 6-31G* basis sets and the CAM-B3LYP functional,46 as applied to chlorophyll and pheophytin in PSII.1,47 The range separation parameters used were μ = 0.14, α = 0.19, and β = 0.46. For calculations in the protein environment, a QM/MM/PCM approach1,41,47,48 was used. In this approach, electrostatic interactions with the protein environments were explicitly considered in the presence of bulk water with a dielectric constant of 78 and a solvent radius of 3.0 Å, using the QuanPol code49 implemented in the GAMESS code,50 as in previous studies.1,21,41,48 The CHARMM force field42 was used for the MM atoms.
