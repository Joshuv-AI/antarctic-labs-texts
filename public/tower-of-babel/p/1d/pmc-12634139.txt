De Novo Missense Variant in TP63 Gene: Insights on Clinical and Molecular Investigations
Authors: Jilong Chen, Xufeng Xi, Xiaoman Xu, Yao Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the phenotypes, mutation types, and loci associated with TP63‐related syndrome by focusing on an affected proband.MethodsEmploying a proband collection strategy, we identified a family presenting with TP63‐related syndrome and performed thorough clinical evaluations on the proband and family members. We subsequently documented the phenotype, mutation type, and locus of the TP63 gene, followed by Sanger DNA sequencing analysis.ResultsWe identified a family affected by TP63‐related syndrome. The proband, a young adult male, demonstrated congenital anodontia, hypohidrosis, sparse hair, and additional features characteristic of ectodermal dysplasia. Further clinical manifestations included left ear hearing impairment, cleft lip/palate, hypospadias, and syndactyly. Sequencing analysis revealed a missense nucleotide variant (c.184G>C, p.Val62Leu) in exon 2 of the TP63 gene. This variant was absent from established SNP databases and was not detected in other family members or unrelated healthy individuals.ConclusionsThe family exhibits significant symptoms consistent with TP63 syndrome. The identified missense mutation is preliminarily considered to be pathogenic.

Introduction

TP63 is a key member of the TP53 tumor suppressor gene family, with a genomic structure similar to that of TP53 and TP73. It consists of 15 exons and is characterized by a unique dual‐promoter regulatory system. The TP63 protein contains multiple functional domains, including the N‐terminal transactivation domain (TAD), central DNA‐binding domain (DBD), oligomerization domain (OD), C‐terminal sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain, and transactivation inhibitory domain (TID). These domains form a complex regulatory network that facilitates the transactivation of target genes and governs cellular development and differentiation [1, 2]. The transcription initiation sites of TP63 exhibit significant heterogeneity. Based on variations in the N‐terminal structure, TP63 isoforms are classified into two main types: TA (transactivation‐competent) and ΔN (N‐terminal truncated). TA isoforms initiate transcription at exon 1, while ΔN isoforms begin transcription at a specific site between exons 3 and 4. Through alternative splicing at the 3′ end, TP63 generates five C‐terminal variants (α, β, γ, δ, and ε), resulting in at least 10 distinct TP63 protein isoforms. Except for the α variant, all other isoforms lack the SAM domain due to alternative splicing [3].

During embryonic development and the formation of the nervous system, the TP63 protein serves a pivotal regulatory function. This protein is integral not only to the precise regulation of skin tissue stratification and terminal differentiation but also to limb morphogenesis. Deficiencies in TP63 protein function or genetic variations can result in a spectrum of complex multisystem developmental anomaly syndromes. The clinical manifestations of these syndromes predominantly involve developmental disorders of tissues originating from the three germ layers, including structural abnormalities in the orofacial region (such as cleft lip and palate malformations), developmental anomalies of ectodermal derivatives (such as ectodermal dysplasia), and limb developmental defects. Collectively, these conditions are referred to as TP63‐related syndromes (tumor protein 63‐related syndromes).

TP63‐related syndromes constitute a group of rare hereditary disorders that significantly impair patients' quality of life, primarily following an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Based on variations in the affected tissues and organs, these conditions are primarily categorized into six clinical subtypes: (1) ectrodactyly‐ectodermal dysplasia‐clefting syndrome (EEC; OMIM #604292), also referred to as cleft lip/palate‐ectrodactyly syndrome; (2) acro‐dermato‐ungual‐lacrimal‐dental syndrome (ADULT; OMIM #103285); (3) ankyloblepharon‐ectodermal defects‐cleft lip/palate syndrome (AEC; OMIM #106260); (4) limb‐mammary syndrome (LMS; OMIM #603543); (5) Rapp–Hodgkin syndrome (RHS; OMIM #129400); (6) split hand/foot malformation 4 (SHFM 4; OMIM #605289) [4, 5]. These syndromes exhibit similar clinical manifestations with overlapping symptoms, and some syndromes demonstrate overlapping gene mutations [6, 7]. Currently, diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for these conditions are limited, primarily focusing on symptom management, with a paucity of targeted treatments. The precision and comprehensiveness of genetic diagnosis require enhancement. Consequently, comprehensive research into the pathogenic mutations and phenotypic characteristics of TP63‐related syndromes is of substantial clinical significance.

In this study, we employed a systematic family collection methodology to focus on the proband, gathering familial samples associated with TP63‐related syndromes. We conducted comprehensive family structure surveys and clinical evaluations, culminating in a summary of the phenotypic characteristics observed within this family. Utilizing DNA sequencing technology, we identified pathogenic mutations in the TP63 gene and conducted predictive analyses with bioinformatics tools.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval Process

This study protocol strictly adheres to the ethical norms for biomedical research and has obtained official approval from the Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of Xiamen Medical College (Ethical Approval No.: HS20200312001). The research process complies with internationally recognized ethical guidelines.

Clinical Diagnostic Criteria

Strict case inclusion criteria were developed by referring to the disease phenotypic characteristics documented in the OMIM database and combining the typical clinical manifestations described in published literature [8].

Recruitment of Research Subjects and Data Collection

An informed consent protocol was implemented, whereby all participants, including the legal guardians of minors, voluntarily signed the family research consent form. Eligible probands were identified through the outpatient system of the Stomatological Hospital of Xiamen Medical College. Upon obtaining informed consent, clinical evaluations and diagnoses were performed. The research team comprised three experienced senior dentists. To ensure participant privacy, a standardized protocol was adhered to for family surveys, which included structured questionnaires, physical examinations, and biological sample collection.

General Survey

The developmental history and current medical history of all family members were thoroughly interviewed and recorded, with a focus on documenting the conditions of individuals affected by TP63‐related syndrome. Clinical data were filled in the oral genetic disease questionnaire and information form compiled by the research group.

Oral and Systemic Condition Examinations

Three senior dentists conducted rigorous oral and systemic condition examinations on family members, covering specialized oral examinations and comprehensive physical examinations. Special attention was paid to the developmental status of teeth, sweat glands, hair, skin, facial features, nipples, limbs, and external genitalia. For typical cases, frontal, lateral, intraoral photographs, and panoramic radiographs were taken. Each dentist performed independent examinations, followed by a joint discussion to reach a unified conclusion, which was then recorded.

Pedigree Mapping

The collected family data, information, and genetic samples were assigned strict anonymous genetic information codes in accordance with the unified standards formulated by the research group. These data were entered into the genetic resource database, and a complete pedigree chart was drawn (Figure 1).

Pedigree structures of the TP63‐related syndrome.

Sampling and DNA Sequencing

Sampling

A total of 10 mL of peripheral cubital venous blood was collected from each family member and subsequently divided into two separate tubes. These tubes were treated with EDTA‐2Na and heparin sodium anticoagulants, respectively. The EDTA‐2Na tube was designated for genomic DNA extraction, while the heparin sodium tube was utilized for lymphocyte culture. All samples were maintained at a temperature of 4°C.

DNA Extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood using a modified salting‐out technique, as described in reference [9].

PCR Amplification

PCR amplification targeted the exon‐intron junction regions, with specific primer parameters and amplified fragment lengths detailed in Table 2. The amplification process employed a standard 25 μL reaction system. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: (a) initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; (b) cyclic denaturation at 94°C for 30 s; and(c) gradient annealing between 53°C and 65°C for 30 s; (d) extension at 72°C for 30 s. Steps (b) through (d) were repeated for a total of 34 cycles. The process concluded with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min, followed by storage at 4°C.

Separation and Purification

The amplified products were separated and purified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target DNA fragments were then recovered.

Sequencing

Sequencing was outsourced to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. The quality of the raw data was assessed using Chromas software. The BLAST algorithm was employed to align the sequencing results with the human reference genome. For the identified sequence variants, authenticity was initially confirmed through sequencing maps. Subsequently, known single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were excluded using public databases, and the population frequency was verified using 250 randomly selected normal control samples to eliminate rare variants. All gene nomenclatures adhere to the standards set by the Human Genome Organization (HUGO) Gene Nomenclature Committee.

Results

Clinical Features

The family participating in this study originated from Zhangzhou City, Fujian Province, and encompassed four generations. Because of the extensive geographical distribution of family members, interviews were conducted selectively with key individuals. The proband (III:12) was a 26‐year‐old male patient who had experienced hypohidrosis since childhood and had a history of febrile convulsions during early childhood. The proband's mother (II:6) reported no history of miscarriage and denied the use of any medications during pregnancy. Clinical examinations indicated that the proband exhibited partial ectodermal dysplasia, characterized by the following features: (1) craniofacial and oral malformations, including a wide interocular distance, saddle nose, thick upper lip, post‐surgical scar from cleft lip repair, postsurgical status of intraoral cleft lip/palate and alveolar ridge cleft, severe oligodontia (with agenesis of teeth 12, 22, 23, and all mandibular teeth except 36 and 37, according to the FDI notation system), dental caries, maxillary and mandibular dentition defects. The dentition defects were further characterized by the remaining teeth exhibiting significant defects with most being residual roots or crowns, and notable alveolar bone resorption. (2) Hair abnormalities, such as sparse hair on the forehead, absence of the outer one‐third of the eyebrows, and sparse eyelashes; (3) rough skin with pronounced wrinkles; (4) mild ocular redness and photophobia; (5) narrow external auditory canal and hearing impairment in the left ear; (6) a history of urinary tract stones was noted, with congenital meatal stenosis identified during examination; (7) mammary gland dysplasia was observed, characterized by enlarged areolae and underdeveloped nipples; (8) limb and nail abnormalities included soft tissue syndactyly of the 3rd and 4th toes on the right foot and underdeveloped toenails with a dull gray appearance (Figure 2). No abnormalities were observed in other family members.

Inheritance Pattern

Neither of the proband's parents exhibited discernible clinical symptoms, and interviews with other family members similarly revealed no abnormal phenotypes. Because of the dispersed distribution of family members and the limited availability of samples, a precise evaluation of the family's genetic history is currently unattainable. Consequently, more comprehensive family surveys and genetic testing analyses are necessary to elucidate the inheritance pattern of this disease.

X‐Ray Examination

Panoramic radiographs of the proband revealed defects in the maxillary and mandibular dentitions. The remaining teeth exhibited significant defects, with most being residual roots or crowns. No images of successor permanent tooth germs were observed in the maxillary and mandibular regions where teeth had not erupted. Notable alveolar bone resorption was observed in the edentulous areas, characterized by reduced bone tissue height and a flattened morphology.

Sequencing Results

Genomic sequencing analysis (Figure 3) identified a single nucleotide variation (c.184G>C) in exon 2 of the TP63 gene, resulting in an amino acid substitution from valine to leucine at position 62 (p.Val62Leu). This genetic variation was absent in healthy family members and 250 normal control subjects, and it was confirmed to be unrecorded in several public databases, including dbSNP and gnomAD. Consequently, we postulate that this mutation may represent the pathogenic variant within this family.

Discussion

The TP63 gene, located on chromosome 3q27, is extensively expressed in various tissues, including those of the digestive, genitourinary, immune, and nervous systems. As a pivotal regulator of embryonic and neural development, TP63 is integral to processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Mutations in the TP63 gene can result in developmental anomalies, particularly affecting the skin, orofacial region, and limbs—phenotypes frequently observed in TP63‐related syndromes associated with ectodermal dysplasia, orofacial clefts, and acral malformations [10]. These syndromes predominantly exhibit an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, with comparable incidence rates in males and females, and are marked by considerable genetic heterogeneity and a high likelihood of overlapping clinical manifestations.

In the present study, the proband presented with syndactyly, distinct facial features, cleft lip/palate, and hearing loss, culminating in a diagnosis of Rapp–Hodgkin syndrome (RHS). Clinical evaluations indicate that the majority of patients initially seek consultation in the department of stomatology for edentulism restoration and frequently do not voluntarily disclose associated systemic abnormalities. This underscores the imperative for clinicians to perform thorough medical history assessments and systematic physical examinations, with particular attention to potential manifestations related to ectodermal dysplasia. These manifestations may include cutaneous abnormalities (such as sparse hair and skin lesions), dental anomalies (including microdontia, conical teeth, taurodontism, and enamel hypoplasia), nail dystrophy, and other systemic features (such as lentiginous pigmentation, limb malformations, lacrimal duct atresia, hypopigmentation, hypospadias, and mammary hypoplasia). It is noteworthy that subtle phenotypic features of organs, such as the mammary glands/nipples and genitalia, are frequently overlooked and thus warrant heightened attention.

In this study, Sanger sequencing revealed a single nucleotide variation (c.184G C) in exon 2 of the TP63 gene, resulting in an amino acid substitution from valine to leucine at position 62 (p.Val62Leu). TP63 is recognized for its role in modulating target genes within signaling pathways such as FGF and Wnt. This mutation is situated in the acidic TAD region of TP63 and may disrupt the transcriptional activation of specific target genes. In comparison to previously documented cases [11], the current case exhibited more extensive multisystem involvement, including particularly rare phenotypes such as genitourinary malformations and mammary hypoplasia. The patient harboring the p.Val62Leu mutation also manifested the ectodermal dysplasia triad (tooth agenesis, sparse hair, hypohidrosis), along with cleft lip/palate and hearing impairment. These findings suggest that this mutation may influence the dual regulatory function of TP63 in epidermal and neural crest development.

A thorough examination of families affected by TP63‐related syndromes in prior research indicates that approximately 40.3% of mutations are associated with EEC syndrome, followed by AEC syndrome at 26.4%. Mutations related to RHS and SHFM4 each constitute 9.3%, whereas those linked to LMS and ADULT syndrome account for 6.2% and 5.4%, respectively [11]. To date, researchers have identified at least 150 TP63 mutations associated with these syndromes, along with several mutation hotspots. Molecular genetic investigations have revealed that the majority of TP63 mutations in these syndromes are missense mutations, with notable differences in mutation loci among the various phenotypes (Figure 4) [1].

Domain structure of TP63 and distribution of mutations identified in different syndromes.

The majority of mutations associated with EEC syndrome are predominantly localized within exons 5–8, which encode the DBD, and these mutations are primarily of the missense type. These genetic alterations compromise the protein's capacity to bind to DNA, consequently diminishing its transcriptional activity. Notably, two mutations have been identified within the SAM domain: a frameshift mutation (c.1572 ins A, leading to premature termination) and a missense mutation (p.Leu563Pro). Given that the SAM domain facilitates protein–protein interactions during tissue development and differentiation, these mutations may interfere with critical molecular interaction mechanisms. In contrast, missense mutations in AEC syndrome are predominantly concentrated within the SAM domain, with a smaller number occurring in the DBD and the TID [11, 12, 13]. In EEC syndrome, TP63 mutations are primarily clustered at the following five hotspots: R204, R227, R279, R280, and R304, with four of these residues (excluding R227) situated within extended CpG islands [13, 14, 15]. Additional EEC syndrome hotspots reported subsequently (R243, R266, R318, R319, R343) are also located within the DBD [12]. This phenomenon echoes the presence of mutation hotspots at homologous sites of the TP53 gene in tumor development, suggesting that these nucleotide sequences may exhibit unique mutation susceptibility.

Hotspot mutations at various amino acid residues can have distinct impacts on clinical manifestations. For instance, mutations at residue R227 are frequently linked to renal and genitourinary disorders and hypohidrosis, whereas limb malformations are uncommon. Mutations at R279 are commonly associated with ectrodactyly. In contrast, R280 mutations are typically linked to skin erosion and syndactyly, without accompanying hypohidrosis, hearing loss, or renal disease. Individuals with R304 mutations exhibit a high prevalence of orofacial clefts, syndactyly, and hearing impairment. Similar to EEC syndrome, mutations in families with ADULT syndrome also demonstrate a clustered distribution, predominantly within the DBD of the TP63 gene. The most notable variation occurs in exon 8, specifically the R298Q missense mutation. This hotspot mutation is highly conserved across both syndromes, suggesting that this amino acid residue plays a crucial role in TP63‐mediated molecular pathways [4]. It is important to note that, due to overlapping and similar phenotypes, patients with the same mutation may be diagnosed with different syndromes. For example, a German female with the R343Q mutation was diagnosed with RHS, while her daughter, who also carried this mutation, was diagnosed with EEC [16]. Furthermore, Kosaki et al. [17] documented a case in which the father displayed the SHFM4 phenotype, whereas his son exhibited the EEC phenotype.

In summary, TP63‐related syndromes are characterized by symptom overlap and considerable genetic heterogeneity. A comprehensive and systematic collection of familial phenotypic characteristics, along with genotype–phenotype correlations, can enhance our understanding of the genetic diversity of these conditions and aid in their diagnosis and classification. In this study, a novel missense mutation in the TP63 gene was identified, associated with a multisystem developmental malformation phenotype, thereby expanding our understanding of genotype–phenotype relationships in TP63‐related syndromes. Despite limitations related to sample size and functional validation, these findings offer a new genetic marker for molecular diagnosis. Future research should aim to increase the sample size, undertake functional experiments, and conduct multiomics analyses to elucidate the pathogenic mechanism of this mutation. This approach will establish a foundation for genetic counseling and the development of targeted intervention strategies.
