Educational escape game in emergency medicine: what criteria are needed to increase satisfaction: a focus group study
Authors: Anne-Catherine Freyer, Alexandre Bosio, Garry Laudren, Christophe Goetz, Thierry Pelaccia, Laure Abensur Vuillaume
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ContextThe use of escape games in education is gaining in popularity, particularly in health teaching, and has already proved its effectiveness. However, the quality criteria for this type of format are not clearly defined.AimsThe main objective of our study was to identify the elements that promote or limit satisfaction with an educational escape game.MethodsOur team created a game for healthcare professionals and students, which was presented at an emergency medicine congress. Focus groups were conducted at the end of the sessions, then analyzed thematically to identify the determinants of learner satisfaction. Questionnaires were sent out 3 months later to study long term satisfaction.ResultsThe total recording time for the 19 interviews was 1h37min, i.e. an average of 5 min 06 s per interview (duration between 2 min 39 and 9 min 38). 43 people responded to the 3-month questionnaire. 17 out of 19 groups were very satisfied with the gamification experience Several quality criteria were identified for this type of tool, including playfulness, similarity to mainstream escape games, closeness of the experience to real life, and plausibility of the scenario.ConclusionsThe escape game was perceived as an engaging tool for training, but further studies are needed to confirm its impact on clinical performance Qualitative analysis has enabled us to identify quality criteria for this type of tool, aiding us to offer more suitable content in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08135-1.

Introduction

Today, most healthcare training use a competency-based approach. Educational models have evolved, and with them teaching methods [1]. The development of non-technical skills is essential to the day-to-day practice of healthcare professions, such as the ability to work as part of a team (inter-professional cooperation, taking into account the skills of each individual, communication, task-sharing, collegial decision-making), as well as the critical thinking and synthesis skills required for the diagnostic process [2]. Until recently in France, and in contrast to other countries [3], the most widespread teaching method was the objectives-based approach. This teaching method became widespread in the 1970 s and 1980 s, influenced by the behaviorist trend and the docimological perspective of evaluation [1]. The notion of pedagogical objectives was developed in response to the need to introduce greater rigor into training systems and was intended to make the aims of training explicit. However, this approach has shown certain limitations. It has sometimes led to the development of excessively detailed catalogs of specific objectives. This can lead to a fragmentation of learning and a lack of links between the various subjects covered, resulting in a loss of overall vision [1]. In higher education, and in health education in particular, the competency-based approach has gradually replaced the objective-based approach, in response to the problems and limitations identified in the latter [4].

The shift to a competency-based approach has further anchored training practices in the learning paradigm. In particular, in the learning of non-technical skills, medical simulation has taken on an increasingly important role in the training of healthcare students, as well as in the continuing education of professionals, particularly in emergency medicine [5]. Healthcare simulation corresponds to “the use of equipment (such as a mannequin or procedural simulator), virtual reality or a standardized patient, to reproduce healthcare situations or environments, to teach diagnostic and therapeutic procedures and to enable processes, clinical situations or decision-making to be rehearsed by a healthcare professional or team of professionals” [6]. Healthcare simulation responds to the principles of experiential learning and provides a safe environment for error and practice [7]. However, its implementation has some limitations. In the case of simulated patients (or standardized patients), there is the constraint of organizing and training specific personnel to embody patient roles. Besides, today’s medical simulation increasingly relies on the use of specific equipment (high-fidelity mannequins, computerized tools, etc.), which can be costly, and requires dedicated space and personnel [7].

It therefore seems essential to diversify skills learning through innovative approaches. Thus, we have proposed the educational escape game as a complementary tool to other teaching tools, enabling the training of students and professionals in soft skills, in a more accessible way thanks to its limited cost and ease of use [8]. It is also a collaborative, learner-centered, experience-based teaching tool in a protected environment. The game setting enables immersion in a real-life scenario, engaging the student in decision-making with a “felt” stake. The debriefing stage provides an opportunity to look back on the experience in order to reinforce the learning process [9]. The use of escape games in education is gradually gaining in popularity [10, 11], especially in healthcare training [12–18] and has already proved its effectiveness [12–14, 18, 19].The educational escape game offers experiential learning with the right to make mistakes [20, 21]. It is also based on cooperative learning, in small groups, to work on collaboration within a team [10, 11, 22, 23]. The format is suitable for all audiences and a wide range of teaching subjects. It is a particularly interesting training tool for learning non-technical skills such as communication or interprofessional collaboration [19, 24]. The proper use of this tool makes it possible to present situations that are realistic and close to professional experience, while stimulating engagement and motivation through play [10, 25, 26].

Our team has created a game for staff working in emergency departments [22]. This game was presented to healthcare professionals at an emergency medicine congress, in small groups of 4 to 6 players. The main objective of our study was to identify the elements that promote or limit learner satisfaction during this educational escape game in health education and to explore which aspects of the game’s design seem to help achieve the learning objectives of this type of training.

Course of the game

The game has been designed for a team of 4 to 6 players. At the start of the game, a facilitator, playing the role of a medical student, introduces them to the situation: they are a team of specialists in disaster medicine, and have been brought together at the request of the local authorities as part of an emergency plan following a suspected terrorist attack on an airliner. The suspected terrorist is in a critical condition and around twenty passengers are showing suspicious symptoms. The team has 30 min to study the situation and report back before the information is revealed to the public. They have access to the suspect’s personal belongings (her handbag and suitcase), as well as the clinical data of the sick passengers. At the same time, they must solve riddles to find clues in the suspected terrorist’s possessions and study the clinical symptoms of other people (in the form of clinical data sheets, with evolving information transmitted by the game master during the game) to come up with diagnostic hypotheses. The investigation part should enable them to learn about the suspect’s past and rule out the criminal hypothesis. Clinical information is used to progressively eliminate diagnostic hypotheses and arrive at the conclusion of a pulmonary embolism complicated by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. The patient’s clinical condition was aggravated by carbon monoxide intoxication, explaining the symptoms of the other passengers.

Players had to solve a complex clinical case involving several victims. In our game, learners played the role of an emergency medicine team. Specific roles were not assigned. Indeed, unlike medical simulation, in the escape game, the learner is in a game situation rather than in a professional setting; playing one’s own role is therefore not compulsory [8]. Roles had to be established quickly, either spontaneously or assigned by a leader [22]. The facilitator’s role-playing, the props, the padlocks to be opened and the search for clues served to enhance the immersion and playfulness of the experience, as in classic escape games. The facilitator remained in the room with the players during the game and could provide additional information at the players’ request. He also delivered clinical information about the patients (results of complementary examinations, evolution of the clinical state of the different passengers during their treatment) according to a predefined timing. Finally, he acted as a facilitator, providing clues for solving the puzzles when necessary.

The level of complexity and content are adapted to the participants’ level of knowledge and skill. Content is in line with the most recent recommendations and scientific consensus [27].

Game evaluation

To finalize the game, we tested it with two teams of five players from the healthcare sector. This enabled us to determine when the facilitator should intervene, and to improve the intelligibility of one of the puzzles.

The game was then proposed to voluntary healthcare professionals at an emergency medicine congress in 2019. 20 groups of 4 to 6 players participated in 60-minute sessions, including the briefing, the 30-minute game and the debriefing, in a dedicated 12 m² room.

This was a voluntary, non-probability sample. Participating healthcare professionals gave oral and written consent to take part in this evaluation. All data were collected in pseudonymized form (with the participants’ initials), analyzed and stored in anonymized form (with an anonymous number). This study did not require specific authorization according to the current French regulations because this is research that evaluates the value of teaching with a survey on healthcare professionals. This research is outside the scope of clinical research and in France does not require an ethics committee (French public health code: art.L1110-1 to L6441-1).

Thematic analysis

At the end of each of the 20 training sessions, the facilitator collected and recorded the participants’ opinions and remarks, during interviews inspired by the focus group model. The four facilitators had received a two-hour training course and were following an interview guide. They asked each group three questions: “How did you experience this teaching game?“, “What did you appreciate most?“, “What do you think could be improved?“. The length of the exchanges was not defined in advance, and the interview was interrupted when the participants had no more information to add. Time constraints and the organization of the medical congress limited the average length of the interviews.

The focus group recordings were transcribed in full using Sonal software and then analyzed by thematic and sub-thematic analysis [28] to determine which aspects of the educational escape game predicted satisfaction and fostered player engagement. A constructivist grounded theory approach was used to develop a theoretical explanation of the emerging themes using an iterative process of returning to qualitative data to refine theories. Grounded theory uses an inductive rather than deductive approach to data analysis and was selected as the subject has not previously been studied using in-depth qualitative analysis. Thematic analysis open and axial coding of the data was performed by one investigator (ACF). This investigator had not taken part in the creation of the game, nor in the recording of the focus groups. Open coding involved breaking transcripts into discrete data components and applying a descriptive label. Axial coding involved looking for connections between codes and condensing them into broader categories. A thematic framework was developed with a second author (LAV). All transcripts were re-coded using this framework by one author (ACF). During this process open and axial codes were iteratively refined then selective coding was used to determine overarching categories, identify thematic interconnections between the overarching category and other codes, and remove categories without sufficient supporting data. Our research team had expertise in emergency medicine (clinical and research), medical education, and qualitative research. Data saturation was verified using the data triangulation method.

Results

The participants were doctors and medical students, emergency physicians or general practitioners, as well as two emergency department nurses, who had voluntarily registered for the activity during a congress, and were divided into 20 groups of 6 players (120 participants). During one of the twenty sessions, the audio recording malfunctioned, which is why only 19 recordings could be studied. At the total, 114 participants were including in analysis. The total recording time for the 19 interviews was 1h37min, i.e. an average of 5 min 06 s per interview (duration between 2 min 39 and 9 min 38). Focus groups

Overall satisfaction (immediate assessment, Kirkpatrick level 1)

17 out of 19 groups were very satisfied with the gamification experience (“It was very pleasant, a great medical experience” (Group 02 - quote 1, G02-1)), and several groups were enthusiastic about taking part in further sessions (“Can’t wait for next year!” (G14-1)).

Groups 03 and 05 (2/19) were less positive. For these two groups, the focus groups were short, with many silences. Even when some players expressed satisfaction, it was with little enthusiasm and few arguments to back up their comments. In both cases, the analysis of the interviews showed that, for the players, the objectives of the experiment were not clear enough:

Members of Group 3 said they were “lost by the concept” (G03-1), with players concentrating on the fun part, partly forgetting to deal with the medical aspect. “We did more of a real-life escape than a medical escape.” (G03-1).

Group 5, on the other hand, expressed “[having] the impression of struggling the whole time” (G05-1). The players had a feeling of defeat even though they had reached the game’s objectives before the end of the allotted time. “We quickly found the pulmonary embolism, and afterwards we felt like idiots because we didn’t know what else to look for.” (G05-1).

The educational escape game tool

The players compared their experience with medical simulation on numerous occasions and found the escape game less stressful, as they did not feel observed or judged by another team team (often, in simulation, two groups are formed at the same time and the passing team is observed by the other team, then vice versa). This created a more relaxed and less competitive environment, which they felt facilitated learning (G09-1, G06-2).

On the contrary, some people think that adding a competitive aspect would improve training through greater engagement (G09-3).

Some players described a certain confusion between the two distinct aspects (fun/medical), sometimes linked to their expectations of a fun escape game, or to a lack of clarity about the objectives of the educational game (G03-1, G05-1, G10-1, G11-1, G20-1).

Finally, some players felt that the tool was better suited to a younger audience with previous public escape game experience (G06-1). Others, felt that the game’s fun aspect made it possible to bring together people of different ages, professions or levels of education (G11-4). Several suggested integrating this type of exercise into the curriculum of healthcare students, or into continuing education (G06-5).

Realism

Players noted that the escape game was somehow close to the healthcare professions, thanks to the time constraints and cooperation required for success (G01-1, G05-2, G09-2, 4.11). They also appreciated the handling of multiple victims (G01-1), with the gradual arrival of clinical information (G06-3). Managing stress and uncertainty, with a strong emphasis on critical thinking, were also cited as elements similar to their clinical practice.

Some even suggest adding other real-life-inspired elements, such as being frequently interrupted by unrelated problems (G20-2) or receiving oral rather than written patient transmissions.

Many players found carbon monoxide poisoning implausible in this context, which sometimes led to frustration (G04-1, G16-1, G20-3).

“If I may, perhaps insist on the fact that the plane is small, and not French […], in fact, we think CO intoxication on old buildings, […] and doctors always arrive after a fireman or the police, who will have detected CO. […] In fact, on blood gases, there’s now the CO fraction.” (G16-1).

Some players pointed out that there were too many clinical elements compared to a real situation, which others felt was justified to simplify case resolution. Finally, some players found the large number of clinical decompensations unrealistic.

Playfulness

Inspired by mainstream escape games, the similarity with these was often cited as a positive aspect of the experience (G11-3). Immersion, thanks to the setting and the game master, originality, puzzles (quality, difficulty, variety), accessories and search, were the elements most cited by players as having contributed to the fun of the activity. Some would have liked an even more immersive experience, along the lines of ludic escape games (G19-1), while being aware of the time and budget constraints. Most players liked the combination of training and games (G04-3), which helped motivate them to take part in the experience (G06-4, G13-1).

“It’s a great experience, it’s fun because it’s both educational and funny. It makes you want to immerse yourself in the scenario and try to solve the riddle, so it’s fun, it’s a good way to learn.” (G06-4).

Discussion

Based on focus groups conducted after the training sessions, our aim was to improve the tool and provide assistance to facilitators wishing to create this type of systemWe were able to identify quality criteria linked to participant satisfaction with 4 major criteria : 1-Aspects concerning the tool 2-Aspects of realism 3-Aspects of playfulness and 4-Training aspects.

To the best of our knowledge, no other study has attempted to precisely identify the determinants with satisfaction criteria of training quality in escape game format. Our results are significant and are based on a broad qualitative panel. which we have not observed in other authors. Indeed, the qualitative approach allows for a better study of the determinants.

However, some studies mention too news elements important to consider. We could therefore propose adding the following elements to our criteria, in line with the proposals of other authors : technical constraints, which we recommended in our previous article [22]: maximum number of players: 4 to 6 [30, 31], in 60 min maximum [30, 32]. Another point to consider could be the number of puzzles to solve [33].

Some studies mention too certain elements similar to those we have identified [11, 30].

several articles noted the importance of both briefing the participants before they enter the escape room and debriefing them after they complete the escape room experience [34, 35]. Playfulness appear as a central element of game-based learning, fostering engagement to training and motivation to learn [25]. In our study, players particularly appreciated all the playful elements that reminded them of a classic escape game: the riddles, the search, the props, the decor, etc. We observed that all this contributed to fun and immersion, and thus to engagement in training [36, 37].

In addition, the literature extensively describes satisfaction related to participation in educational escape games [30, 38, 39]. A systematic review on the use of escape games in education, published in 2020 by Veldkamp et al.. showed, in all the articles studied, that a vast majority of learners enjoyed the activity, and that learners were highly engaged and active during the activity [10]. With regard to learning, the authors found that a majority of participants felt that the escape game environment had helped them achieve their learning objectives and/or would be in favor of implementing this type of training in their curriculum.

In addition, the role of the facilitator is highlighted in our study, but also by other authors. This facilitator provides three essential elements: briefing, facilitation during the game, and debriefing [22, 30]. He has a major role to play in ensuring that the learning game runs smoothly [40]. In particular, groups for whom the objectives of the game were not sufficiently clear felt frustrated and enjoyed their experience less. Improving the briefing and adapting the facilitator’s support should help avoid this type of pitfall. Instructions need to be clear and reiterated if necessary during the game, so that learners don’t lose sight of the learning objectives [30]. Therefore, we believe it is essential that teachers receive specific training to facilitate educational escape games.

We also emphasized that theeducational content needs to be carefully thought out, with the plausibility of the scenario and the realism of the medical situations cited on numerous occasions as major elements of satisfaction or dissatisfaction. The scenario must be as plausible as possible to ensure that the players adhere to it: this was a remark made several times concerning the likelihood of carbon monoxide poisoning in an aircraft. This point is also cited by authors who have developed an escape game in intensive care [38]. In health education, realism is the key to quality training, in terms of both theoretical knowledge and technical and non-technical skills, through the notion of transferability of lessons [1]. However, the realism of playful situations is apparently less important. Nevertheless, one study notes the importance of scenario coherence in order to avoid cognitive dissonance (internal tension in a person’s system of thoughts, beliefs, emotions and attitudes when several of these are in contradiction with each other) and thus maintain immersion [10]. For example, in an emergency situation, we wouldn’t try to find the code of the suspected terrorist’s suitcase in her handbag, but we’d arm ourselves with a box cutter to open the suitcase and save time. This was mentioned in one of the groups, but more as a positive aspect enabling us to take a step back from situations that are no fun in real life. In any case, we believe this is an important point for teachers to bear in mind when creating their own educational escape games.

Finally, learners also appreciated the absence of observation and competition effects compared with simulation. It should be noted, however, that some authors have described the role of stress in learning situations as a positive factor [41] but this point has been poor evaluated in educational escape games [42].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, we are likely to suffer from selection bias. Indeed, all participants volunteered to take part in the educational experiment. However, the large number of participants and the consistency with the literature probably lend credibility to the results. Second, only the qualitative part was really a major asset for our study. In fact, the remote responses were not totally completed by the participants and remain self-declared elements, and most of the analysis material consists of audio recordings of the focus groups. It is therefore difficult to draw conclusions about the educational evaluation itself. However, other authors conclude that there have been significant improvements in teamwork in specialties similar to emergency medicine [38, 43]. Finally, the absence of a control group makes it difficult to compare the effectiveness of the escape game with other teaching methods. A structured study in a university context would make it possible to circumvent these limitations.

Conclusion

The educational escape game created by our team, tested on groups of professionals at an emergency medicine congress, won the satisfaction of the majority of participants. Analysis of their comments during focus groups enabled us to determine the quality criteria for this type of training device. We found that playfulness plays a key role in teaching through games, and must be carefully worked out, while maintaining the coherence and verisimilitude essential to maintaining immersion and the quality of the training. The escape game was perceived as an engaging tool for training, but further studies are needed to confirm its impact on clinical performance. Also, it will be important in future studies to conduct a structured study to validate the criteria we have proposed.
