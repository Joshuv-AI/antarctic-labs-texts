Ultra-sensitive optimized one-dimensional phononic crystal as a fluidic sensor to enhance the measurement of acetic acid concentration
Authors: Farhad Javanpour Heravi, Ali Hajjiah, Hussein A. Elsayed, Ahmed Mehaney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The current investigation theoretically presents a one-dimensional phononic crystal (PnC) as a fluidic sensor. The sensor under consideration aims to distinguish the concentration of acetic acid. The primary configuration of the proposed sensor is constructed with lead, epoxy, and a defect layer in the middle of the structure, that is filled with acetic acid (vinegar). As a result of the rise in density and decline in the speed of sound at a 100% concentration of acetic acid in comparison to pure water, the peak frequency of the output has shifted towards lower frequencies. Given that the maximum permissible concentration of acetic acid in water for vinegar is above 30%, sensor simulations were conducted within the concentration range of 25–35% with a step size of 1%. Interestingly, the sensitivity of the sensor exhibits a polynomial change in response to the concentration of acetic acid. Consequently, the highest level of sensitivity, which corresponds to the lowest concentration of vinegar, is recorded as 48.44 × 106 (Hz). The proposed system exhibits a remarkable value of the quality factor of 2802.91. Furthermore, the optimal figure of merit (FOM) is achieved when the concentration is at its lowest, with a value of 94.00. Furthermore, the temperature effects are taken into account for a wide range between 10 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{and}$$\end{document}and 60 °C. A pronouncing sensitivity is obtained for all temperatures changes and the highest one reached the value of 1.57 × 106 (Hz/°C) at a temperature of 25 °C. Considering the present circumstances, the suggested sensor configuration has the potential to cater to a diverse array of other fluids, specifically their concentration and temperature, thereby offering a broad scope of applications.

Introduction

Phononic crystals (PnC) also known as acoustic band gap materials are artificial periodic composites that can be utilized as a wave guide not only for acoustic waves but also for whole spectrum, from audible to thermal energy range, of mechanical waves1–4. This kind of crystals can be arranged in one, two or three-dimension lattices. Interestingly, PnCs can introduce some novel sensing platform that are capable of monitoring the properties of many different chemical, physical and biomedical materials5–7. In this regard, the one-dimensional phononic crystal (1D PnC) contains several unit cells, where every unit cell consists of two or more layers providing a higher contrast in their intrinsic physical properties like acoustic and elastic characteristics8,9. A common phenomenon in PnCs is the existence of Bragg gaps, which arise from the disruptive interference of waves scattered by their constituent layers. Consequently, within specific frequency ranges, the wave propagation can be impeded in all spatial orientations (band gaps) or solely in a particular direction (known as stop bands)10–12. These stop or inhibited frequency bands are known as phononic band gaps10,13,14. Therefore, band gaps have revealed due to Bragg’s scattering at the interfaces of the unit cells layers due to the inherent mechanical properties of PnC constituent materials. Specific properties of the band gaps pave the way to design theoretically and experimentally phononic structures in various application like, waveguides, liquid sensors, heat isolation system, filter multiplexing system, and so many other practical devices which is used in medical and industrial activities15–17. Fluidic phononic sensor is one of the most important branch in phononic-based sensors domain. Fluidic phononic sensors are being applied in a wide range of fields where the identification and observation of fluid characteristics are essential18,19. In the environmental monitoring sector, these sensors are utilized for the detection and monitoring of pollutants3, gases20, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs)21 in both air and water. Within the realm of biomedical diagnostics, fluidic sensors are instrumental in identifying biomarkers, pathogens, and saliva22. In the domain of industrial process control, Fluidic phononic sensors are employed for overseeing chemical processing and food production23. In the oil and gas industry, these sensors are used to detect hydrocarbon gases, identify leaks in pipelines and storage tanks, and evaluate fluid properties like viscosity and density17,24,25. In the automotive and aerospace sectors, they are utilized for monitoring fuel quality, exhaust emissions, and engine performance6,26. In chemical analysis and quality control, these sensors play a critical role in industries such as pharmaceuticals and cosmetics27. In the field of water quality, they are used to monitor various parameters of water quality such as pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, and turbidity in both freshwater and marine environments. In 2009, Ralf Lucklum et al. designed a 1D PnC sensor consisting of four Al plates and three water layers to discern liquid property17. Also in 2021, Ahmed Mehaney et al. introduced theoretically a novel type of a 1D PnC model as a fluidic sensor to distinguish between different components of gasoline28. The other noticeable theoretical investigation in fluidic sensors was prepared by Farhad Javanpour Heravi et al. in 2023 to specify the type of some dangerous metallic oxides3. The sensing mechanism in all mentioned fluidic sensors depends on the characteristic of the generated resonant mode through the acoustic band gaps. Therefore, 1D defective structure considered as a fundamental basis in the system of phononic detectors. Notably, the manifestation of the defect mode arises from the disrupted regularity of PnC structures. Meanwhile, in the current work a novel 1D defective PnC-based sensor is presented to discern the percentages of the acetic acid concentration. Hence, the primary step is to know well about the properties of acetic acid. Acetic acid is one of the simplest carboxylic acids that containing two carbons with antibacterial and antifungal properties29,30. It is well known that the corrosiveness of acetic acid depends mainly on its concentration31,32. Acetic acid has many uses in industry, for example it serves as a crucial chemical reagent and industrial compound employed in the manufacturing of plastic soft drink bottles33, photographic film34, as an antimicrobial food preservative in the food industry35,36, as a polar protic solvent, for recrystallization to purify organic compounds37,38. Moreover, it is producing polyvinyl acetate commonly known as wood glue39,40, and is widely available adhesive used for porous materials like wood, paper, and clothes. Pure acetic acid, often called glacial acetic acid, is a colorless, corrosive liquid (boiling point 117.9 °C [244.2 °F]; melting point 16.6 °C [61.9 °F]; glacial density 917(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{kg}/{\text{m}}^{3}$$\end{document}kg/m3)) that exhibits complete miscibility with water41. Household vinegar, a widely encountered form of acetic acid, is frequently encountered by consumers, which is naturally made from fermentable sources such as wine, potatoes, apples, grapes, berries, and grains. Vinegar typically consists of a transparent liquid solution comprising approximately 5 percent acetic acid and 95 percent of water. It is evident that the concentration of acetic acid plays a significant role in all its applications. The lowest concentration, which is 5%, is commonly found in household vinegar. On the other hand, the highest concentration, known as glacial acetic acid with a concentration of 99.5%, serves as a raw material and solvent in the manufacturing of various chemical products, including vinyl acetate. It must be considered; The FDA has established that the acceptable range for acetic acid concentration in water is between 4 and 30%. Any utilization of this substance beyond the approved limits set by the FDA will be deemed fraudulent in its production, posing a threat to human well-being. In light of this perspective, our design has employed this specific blend as the focal sample42. All liquids exhibit sound absorption to some extent across all frequencies. Therefore, altering the liquid's concentration can lead to changes in various parameters such as absorption rate and incident wave path length. An important observation in simulations is that the increase in density and decrease in speed of sound at 100% acetic acid concentration, as opposed to 0% acetic acid concentration, causes a shift in the output frequency peak towards lower frequencies. Given that the maximum permissible concentration of acetic acid in vinegar is above 30% in water, sensor simulations were conducted with incremental steps of 1% within the 25–35% concentration range42. Here, the proposed sensor is simulated in MATLAB program based on the transfer matrix method (TMM). Accordingly, the performance of the supposed design is scrutinized by evaluating the attained optimal values for relevant parameters such as the angle of the incident wave, number of periods, and layers thicknesses, to achieve the high-performance sensing system capable of accurately discerning different concentrations of acetic acid.

Theoretical treatment and modeling of PnC structure

Model design

In this section, the theory and schematic of the proposed sensor are illustrated in detail. The selection of appropriate methods is crucial in analyzing the propagation of waves in periodic structures. Two commonly used computational techniques for this purpose are the transfer matrix method (TMM) and the Green function method (GFM). While both methods serve the same purpose, they differ in their approach. The TMM is based on the principle of continuity of wave functions at interfaces between layers, making it suitable for structures with a finite number of layers43. It is especially effective in calculating transmission and reflection coefficients at various frequencies, and is widely utilized in the analysis of acoustic properties in phononic crystals and other periodic structures. On the other hand, the GFM involves solving the wave equation for the entire periodic structure using Green's functions, which describe the system's response to a point source44–47. This method is valuable for studying the detailed spatial distribution of waves within the structures and analyzing phenomena like wave localization and mode coupling. Here, the acoustic wave propagation through the supposed phononic structure is intended to be simulated. In order to achieve this goal, the calculation is set based on the TMM. Meanwhile, Fig. 1. describes a simplified schematic diagram of the considered 1D PnC structure. The selection of materials for phononic crystal unit cells is a critical aspect in achieving the desired acoustic properties efficiently. Factors such as cost and availability play a significant role in this decision-making process. Lead, with its high density, is often the preferred choice for phononic crystal unit cells. Its density enables lead to effectively impede the transmission of sound waves, establishing barriers within the crystal structure that restrict the passage of specific frequencies. On the other hand, epoxy is selected for its mechanical flexibility and adhesive properties. The ability to mold epoxy into various shapes and configurations provides versatility in designing the phononic crystal structure. It is important to comprehend and regulate the mismatch between sublayers in phononic crystals to optimize their performance in different applications. Additionally, there are alternative materials, including various types of polymers, metals, ceramics, and composite materials, that can be utilized in phononic crystal structures instead of epoxy and lead. The choice of these materials depends on the desired acoustic properties and specific requirements of the applications. Results and discussions

Detection of acetic acid concentrations

The proposed designed system is applied for detection and sensing purposes. Hence, the high performance in sensing process is one of the outstanding features that should be noticed. Therefore, the supposed crystal should be designed based on optimum values for effective parameters such as incident angle, thicknesses of layers, and the number of unit cells. The impact of the angle of incidence on sensor performance is a crucial factor to consider. The information provided in Fig. 3a illustrates that the sensitivity of the PnC sensor design rises as the angle of incidence increases as the angle of incidence goes from 0° to 18°. However, beyond 18 degrees, the resonance peak becomes unclear beyond the bandgap region. Therefore, an angle of incidence of θ = 18 is identified as the ideal angle for maximizing sensor performance. Aside from the angle of incidence, the number of periods is another significant parameter that influences sensor performance. As depicted in Fig. 3b, there is a correlation between the periodicity of the structure and its sensitivity. Increasing the number of periods from N = 4 to 7 actually decreases the sensitivity of the sensor. Furthermore, selecting a period lower or higher than N = 4 and N = 7 respectively results in the absence of discernible resonance peaks.Figure 3(a) The sensor's sensitivity in relation to the angle of the incident wave. After delineating the number of optimized unit cells, a distinction arises between the theoretical and optimization sections. It is important to note that infinite unit cells are not physically achievable, but are frequently employed in theoretical investigations and numerical simulations to probe the foundational characteristics of PnC structures, including band structures, dispersion relations, and wave propagation behavior. Conversely, finite unit cells commonly applied in practical aspect of the study. To sum up, while the initial analysis using infinite unit cells proved valuable for theoretical comprehension and modeling purposes, the transition to finite optimized unit cells reflects the practical considerations and objectives of the experimental aspect of the research.

Implementing the optimization process can be a crucial factor in attaining a high-performance system. Concerning the thicknesses of the layers, the optimization processes were conducted by assuming the epoxy layer thickness to be 6 μm. Figure 3d presents a graphical representation showing that the sensitivity gradually increases between the range of 1.4 μm and 2.6 μm, reaching its peak value when the thickness is 2.6 μm. However, for thicknesses exceeding 2.6 μm, the sensitivity experiences slight decrements. Hence, it is recommended to maintain the lead layer thickness at 2.6 μm for optimal results. Subsequently, the optimized value for the epoxy thickness was calculated while considering the lead layer thickness at 2.6 μm. The sensor performance

The importance of the sensors' efficacy is crucial to their utilization, and the examination of the sensor's functionality established the groundwork for comprehending the sensor's capacities and constraints. In this specific section, an in-depth analysis of sensor performance has been conducted, focusing on key parameters including sensitivity, Q-factor, and FOM that play a crucial role in determining the accuracy of the sensing process. The sensitivity of phononic sensors refers to the change in the position of the defect resonance mode caused by the variation of a specific parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi $$\end{document}ξ). In the context of sensors, sensitivity is typically quantified as the change in output signal per unit change in the input parameter. Essentially, having the highest sensitivity means that the sensor can detect even slight fluctuations in the measured parameter, thereby enhancing its efficiency in applications where precision and accuracy are of utmost importance. To attain the maximum sensitivity, it is often necessary to optimize a range of factors, including sensor design, material characteristics, signal processing algorithms, and environmental conditions, aiming to maximize the response of the sensor to variations in the input parameter. Among the essential parameters characterizing the performance of phononic crystals, the Quality Factor (Q) stands out as a pivotal metric. The Q factor acts as a quantitative measure of energy dissipation whit in the PnC structure, providing valuable insights into the efficiency of phonon confinement and manipulation. A higher Q factor signifies a lower energy loss, enhancing the potential for applications in various domains, such as acoustic waveguides, sensors, and phononic devices. The Q-factor refers to the ratio of the resonant peak's center frequency to its bandwidth at half maximum. Thus, Q values can be estimated based on the following relation:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q\, factor=\frac{{f}_{max}}{FWHM}$$\end{document}Qfactor=fmaxFWHM

In the realm of phononic crystals, the metric known as the figure of merit (FOM) is frequently employed to evaluate the effectiveness of a phononic crystal arrangement in the regulation and manipulation of sound waves. This metric typically takes into consideration various factors, including the band gap width, the efficiency of transmission, and other pertinent parameters 2,54.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FOM=\frac{S}{FWHM}$$\end{document}FOM=SFWHM

Experimental setup and fabrication

The fabrication techniques can be classified into two categories: single-step and multi-step fabrication methods61. The commonly utilized techniques for this purpose are 3D printing and machining 61, which are particularly suitable for generating designs incorporating Helmholtz resonators 62,63, periodic lattices 64,65, and labyrinthine structures 66–70. The fabrication of all types of PnCs cannot be adequately achieved using single-step techniques alone. In cases where the structures exhibit a high level of heterogeneity or consist of numerous intricate features on the micro or nanoscale, it becomes necessary to employ multiple processes in addition to printing and machining methods71–77. Conclusions

In this paper, a novel arrangement is proposed in a theoretical manner, serving as a discerning and receptive device relying on a one-dimensional defective PnC configuration. The primary basis for our calculations consists of the transfer matrix method and the acoustic properties of the utilized materials. Using these characteristics, the transmission spectra of the wave that propagates through the designed PnC sensor is calculated. The suggested design illustrates the capacity to differentiate between different levels of acetic acid concentration. Based on the acquired findings, the proposed sensor exhibits pronouncing sensitivity and FOM that exhibit an exponential variation in response to changes in acetic acid concentration. In contrast to previous PnC sensors, the sensor has excellent performance at low concentrations. The highest sensitivity achieved in this study was 48.44 × 106 (Hz) when the defective layer is filled with a 25% concentration of acetic acid. Additionally, the 25% concentration also exhibited the highest figure of merit (FOM) of 94.00. Another significant parameter, the Q-factor, has received a value of 2802.91 at 35% of acetic acid concentration. Moreover, it is evident that the temperature significantly influences the performance of any sensors. The highest sensitivity, reached to the value of 1.575 × 106 (Hz/°C), is achieved at T = 25 °C, and the Q-factor reached a superb result of 12,000 at 60 °C. Finally, the sensor design presents numerous benefits such as affordability, simplicity in assembly, and the capability to function effectively in the detection of both temperature and concentration of any other fluids.
