Analysis of heritability and environmental factors in preschool children with eczema: a case-control study
Authors: Qingfang Du, Lei Zhang, Chongzhi Ma, Li Yang, Jun Tian, Zijian Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Eczema is common in children, and its onset is affected by both genetic and environmental factors. We investigated the effects of genetic and environmental factors on the incidence of eczema in preschool children. 515 preschool children with eczema and 515 children participating in the physical examination were enrolled. The study included the incidence of childhood eczema, the child’s birth and feeding conditions, the history of eczema in the parents, and relevant environmental risk factors, and to comprehensively analyze the genetic and environmental factors influencing childhood eczema. Among 1030 children, 173 parents (8.4%) had eczema, with a heritability of 73.59% for boys’ parents and 58.59% for girls’ parents. Multivariate logistic regression results showed that premature infants, low birth weight, children who had used antibiotics before the age of 1 year the living environment between the first year of mother pregnancy and the first year of the child is humid, a father with a history of eczema, a mother with a history of eczema are risk factors for eczema in children. Actively preventing environmental factors related to eczema may be an effective means to reduce the risk of eczema in children.

Introduction

Eczema is a common inflammatory skin disease that often occurs in children1. It is reported that in the past 30 years, the global prevalence of childhood eczema has shown a rapid upward trend, with the prevalence of childhood eczema reaching as high as 20% in developed countries2,3. According to a survey conducted by Gu et al.4 and Guo et al.5, the prevalence of eczema among children aged 1 to 7 years in China was 3.07% in 2004, and increased to 12.94% in 2014. Zhang et al.6 conducted a multicenter cross-sectional survey of preschool children in 10 cities in China in 2010, indicating that 4.8–15.8% of children had eczema within the past 12 months of the survey, and 13.6–34.7% of children had eczema symptoms. Among them, about 8.2% of children aged 3–6 in northwest China were diagnosed with eczema within the past 12 months, and 29.0% of children had eczema related symptoms.

The etiology of eczema is complex and is influenced by both genetic7 and environmental factors8, making it prone to recurrent attacks. According to investigations, children with eczema have a higher risk of asthma and allergic rhinitis9, which not only affects the quality of life of children, but also brings a certain degree of disease burden to their families10. Therefore, it is of great practical significance to study the influencing factors of children’s eczema and further take targeted preventive measures to reduce the incidence rate of eczema.

Based on the above reasons, this study conducted a case-control study on eczema in preschool children in northwest China. By investigating the incidence of eczema and related environmental risk factors in the parents of the case group and control group children, a comprehensive analysis was performed to assess the association between genetic and environmental factors and childhood eczema. The aim of this study is to provide scientific and theoretical evidence for the prevention of childhood eczema.

Methods

Research object

A random cluster sampling method was adopted. Initially, we identified all preschool children aged 2–8 years who were hospitalized in The 63,600 Hospital of PLA from March 2021 to August 2021. From this population, 515 children diagnosed with eczema were randomly selected to form the eczema group. A 1:1 match was conducted based on gender and age, and healthy children who underwent physical examination in The 63,600 Hospital of PLA during the same period were selected as the control group. Additionally, we considered other potential confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status, parental education level, and environmental exposures, and ensured that these factors were evenly distributed between the two groups. This approach helped to control for potential differences in the population before extraction into the eczema and control groups. The selection process is illustrated in the flowchart below (Figure S1). Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The 63,600 hospital of PLA (2021-017). All stages of the study were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Parents of these children signed informed consent form.

Survey methods

A questionnaire survey was conducted on all children. The survey content refers to the questionnaire used by Zhang et al.6, and has been slightly revised accordingly. The survey content includes:1 Demographic characteristics;2 Feeding status;3 The prevalence of eczema among children and family members;4 The living environment;5 Smoking status of parents. The questionnaire was filled out by the parents, and trained investigators were arranged to answer questions about the content of the questionnaire. All questionnaires were reviewed by 2 trained members, and unqualified questionnaires were eliminated.

The relevant results had been determined1. Eczema: children who have been definitively diagnosed with eczema by a doctor, or those who present with itchy skin or recurrent rashes on the elbow, knee, ankle, hips, neck, ears, or around the eyes, and have been hospitalized with these symptoms for at least 6 consecutive months without a definitive diagnosis of eczema. If either of these criteria is met, the child is considered to have eczema;2 Premature infants and birth weight: Preterm infants are defined as newborns with a gestational age of less than 37 weeks. Low birth weight is defined as a birth weight of less than 2,500 g, normal birth weight is between 2,500 g and 4,000 g, and macrosomia is a birth weight greater than 4,000 g113. Smoking: Smoking equal to or more than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime12;4 Room cleaning frequency: often refers to cleaning at least twice a week, occasionally refers to cleaning < twice a week and cleaning > once a week, rarely refers to cleaning < once a week8;5 Dampness: obvious mildew, dampness, water flooding, or water leakage/seepage/damage on the floor, wall, or ceiling, as well as damp clothing and bedding13.

Statistical analysis

Epi Data3.1 software was used to establish a database, and SPSS27.0 software was used to process and analyze all data. Univariate analysis used the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, and univariate logistic regression analysis was initially conducted to identify variables that showed significant differences. Variables with significant differences in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were then included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The multivariate logistic regression enter method was used to analyze the influencing factors of childhood eczema, and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated for each variable. All statistical analyses were conducted using a two-tailed test with a significance level of 0.05. A difference was considered statistically significant when the p<0.05. The heritability calculation was performed using Falconer’s formula. Heritability h2(%)=b/r, where r represents the coefficient of relatedness [r = 1/2 for first-degree relatives (parents)], and b denotes the regression coefficient. b = qc(Xc-Xr)/ar, qc=1-pc, pc represents the disease prevalence in the control group, Xc is the standard deviation between the average susceptibility and threshold values in the control group relatives, Xr is the standard deviation between the average susceptibility and threshold values in the proband’s relatives, and ar is the standard deviation between the average susceptibility values in the proband’s relatives and the patients’ susceptibility values in the proband’s relatives. The values of Xc、Xr、ar can be obtained from Falconer’s table based on the disease prevalence.

Taking whether had eczema as the dependent variable (0 = no, 1 = yes), the significant variables of the single factor analysis results were introduced into the Logistic regression model. Finally, the six variables that entered the Main effect model were all related to the onset of eczema in preschool children (Table 3 and Table S1). After adjusted, premature infants (OR = 2.76, 95% CI = 1.66 ~ 4.61), low birth weight (OR = 1.58, 95% CI = 1.05 ~ 2.39), children has used antibiotics before the age of 1 year (OR = 1.50, 95% CI = 1.06 ~ 2.13), the living environment between the year before the mother became pregnant and the first year of the child’s life is dampness (OR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.03 ~ 1.92), father with a history of eczema (OR = 2.37, 95% CI = 1.40 ~ 4.02), and mother with a history of eczema (OR = 3.62, 95% CI = 2.19 ~ 5.99) are risk factors for childhood eczema.

Discussions

Many studies reported childhood eczema is closely related to genetic factors. Shi et al.8 conducted a survey on 8153 preschool children with eczema in Urumqi City, showing that children with a family history of eczema had a higher risk of eczema (OR = 3.53, 95% CI = 2.98–4.19). In consistent with this, we found parents with a history of eczema are risk factors for childhood eczema, and mothers with eczema are more likely to increase the risk. According to our results, children’s eczema has a very high heritability, and boys’ heritability is higher than girls’, indicating that boys are more susceptible to genetic influences.

We found that premature birth and low birth weight are risk factors for childhood eczema. Premature infants need to constantly respond to the complex external environment. Similarly, low birth weight children are more prone to symptoms such as growth retardation14. Premature infants have less maternal immunoglobulin G (IgG) in their blood circulation than full-term children15. Low birth weight is also one of the potential high-risk factors for developing food allergies16. We infer that the mechanism of premature delivery and low birth weight leading to an increased risk of eczema in children is similar to food allergy17.

The use of antibiotics in children aged 0–1 is a risk factor for eczema. Many studies have found that the use of antibiotics in infancy is a risk factor for childhood asthma18–20. More antibiotics used during infancy are related to more likely eczema in children21. Children have special reactivity and sensitivity to drugs due to their immature system. The above studies suggest that the abuse of antibiotics in infants and young children should be reduced to jointly promote the effectiveness and safety of drug use in children.

The presence of dampness in the living environment between the first year of pregnancy and the age of one year of the child is a risk factor for childhood eczema. Shi et al.8 found in a study that the humid living environment of a mother in the first year of pregnancy increases the risk of eczema in preschool children (OR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.17-2.00). Wang et al.22 reported that using humidifiers in homes increases the risk of eczema in children. Indoor humidity can lead to more dust mites in dwellings23,24, which may be the main reason for the increased risk of eczema in children caused by humidity.

Currently, there are few studies on building predictive models for the occurrence of eczema in preschool children25,26. This study constructed a nomogram prediction model for predicting the occurrence of eczema. The results show that the prediction calibration curve is close to the standard curve, indicating that the nomogram has a good prediction function.

This study analyzed the genetic and environmental risk factors for childhood eczema, which has certain practical significance for its prevention. However, several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, this study is a case-control study, which inherently has poor causal demonstration ability; a population-based cohort study is needed to establish causality more robustly. Second, although we calculated the heritability of eczema using Falconer’s method, which provided useful insights into the genetic factors associated with the disease, our approach did not include data from monozygotic and dizygotic twins. Calculating heritability using twin data would help minimize the effect of environmental factors and provide a more accurate estimation of genetic influence. The absence of twin data in our study might have led to an overestimation of heritability due to the inability to fully account for environmental effects. Third, not all factors related to atopic dermatitis have been examined. Specifically, the study did not collect detailed smoking histories at different time periods, such as current smoking status, secondhand smoke exposure, or smoking during pregnancy, which could potentially influence the assessment of smoking-related risk factors for eczema. Additionally, since many cases of atopic dermatitis develop early in infancy, the causal relationship between postnatal antibiotics and atopic dermatitis could not be assessed. Finally, there is a potential for recall bias as the study relied on parents recalling their children’s condition during the first year of life, which may affect the accuracy of the data regarding early-life exposures and conditions. Future research should involve population-based cohort studies, include twin data for more precise heritability calculations, and collect comprehensive histories of smoking and other relevant factors to provide a more thorough understanding of the risk factors for childhood eczema.

Conclusion

The parents of boys have a higher heritability. The prevalence of eczema in preschool children can be preliminarily predicted based on parents having eczema, premature delivery and low birth weight infants, antibiotic use, and humid living environments. Actively prevention of environmental factors related to eczema in preschool children may be an effective means to reduce the risk of eczema in children.
