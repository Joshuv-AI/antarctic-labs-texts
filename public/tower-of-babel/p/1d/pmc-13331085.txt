Cultivating health resilience in Singapore: a qualitative study from the perspectives of community-dwelling adults and care providers
Authors: Lixia Ge, Wan Fen Yip, Andy Hau Yan Ho, Eric Chua, Pann Pei Chieh, Ian Yi Onn Leong, Ringo Moon-Ho Ho, Woan Shin Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIndividuals vary in their capacity to adapt and respond to health adversities. Resilient people are better able to regain functioning following health adversity. This study explored the factors that support individuals in overcoming health-related challenges, drawing on perspectives from multiple stakeholders.MethodsAn interpretive systemic approach was adopted to understand the multidimensional nature of health resilience. Semistructured interviews were conducted with 65 community-dwelling adults (including caregivers), alongside 11 focus group discussions involving 53 healthcare and social care professionals. Thematic analysis, guided by the Resilience Framework and an interpretive-systemic lens, was used to identify key processes and resources shaping health resilience.ResultsWe identified seven overarching themes (with 18 subthemes) central to navigating health challenges: (1) personal dispositions and meaning-making (4 subthemes), (2) psychobehavioural adjustment (4), (3) health literacy for care navigation (2), (4) social support and relational anchors (2), (5) care affordability and financial security (2), (6) accessibility of and experience in healthcare systems (2) and (7) availability of and access to social support services (2). Findings indicate that participants cultivated health resilience by actively engaging personal, social and systemic resources. Meaning-making and psychobehavioural adjustment emerged as important internal mechanisms, which were themselves enabled or constrained by external relational and systemic factors.ConclusionsHealth resilience is a dynamic, multidimensional process influenced by interlinked intrapersonal, relational and systemic factors. Moving beyond trait-based understandings, our findings provide a culturally grounded perspective essential for designing holistic, person-centred health systems and public health strategies, particularly for ageing societies with rising chronic disease burdens.Keywords: Public Health, Community Health, Qualitative Research, Attitude, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

Resilience is increasingly recognised as a critical construct in health, referring to the capacity to maintain or regain psychological, social and functional well-being in the face of illness or adversity. Strengthening resilience is now a global public health priority, reflected in the WHO’s policy frameworks.1 The COVID-19 pandemic further underscored the importance of protective factors that enable individuals to adapt, cope and preserve well-being during health crises.2 3

Early research emphasised individual traits—such as hardiness, emotional regulation and social skills—that influence adaptive behaviours in response to stress.4 5 However, resilience is now widely understood as a dynamic, multifaceted process shaped not only by individual characteristics but also by family, community and systemic resources, including access to meaningful relationships, a sense of life purpose and supportive environments.6 7

The resilience literature has historically focused on children or trauma-exposed populations, with far less attention given to individuals managing acute or chronic health conditions.8 9 Furthermore, the existing evidence base is largely derived from Western, individualistic contexts, which may not adequately capture resilience processes shaped by different social, cultural and demographic realities. Notions of resilience are often culturally embedded, influenced by dimensions such as individualism-collectivism, which shape priorities around self-reliance, social harmony and help-seeking behaviour. As a result, it remains unclear whether resilience processes are distinct or overlapping across populations and cultures, and everyday resilience-building in the broader population remains underexplored.10

This gap highlights the need for evidence to inform approaches that strengthen adaptive capacity across diverse health contexts. For this study, we conceptualise health resilience as a dynamic and multidimensional process, shaped by individual capacities and relational and systemic context, through which individuals maintain or regain psychological, social and functional well-being when facing illness or health-related adversity.

In Singapore, a multiethnic Asian society characterised by a unique cultural blend of Eastern values and Western influences, collectivist traditions emphasising familial obligation and social harmony coexist with pragmatic self-reliance, personal responsibility and trust in formal institutions. These sociocultural orientations may shape resilience through both interdependence and proactive self-management, making Singapore a particularly informative context in which to examine health resilience. At the same time, the traditional hospital-centric and acute-care-oriented model of healthcare is increasingly insufficient for addressing population needs due to Singapore’s rapidly ageing population.11 12 Understanding local and culturally grounded resilience processes is therefore critical. Consequently, there is a growing need to prioritise preventive and wellness-focused care across the life course.13 Local initiatives have sought to enhance health literacy, promote self-care efficacy and improve access to neighbourhood resources and support, with some evidence suggesting these efforts may foster resilience.14,16 However, less is known about how individuals adapt within broader relational (eg, family, peer and community networks) and systemic (eg, healthcare, social and policy structures) contexts, particularly within Singapore’s unique sociocultural setting.

Research on how individuals recover and adapt to illness remains fragmented, often confined to narrow domains such as psychological resilience or condition-specific coping. In Singapore, resilience studies have predominantly centred on healthcare workers or pandemic-specific responses,17,19 leaving everyday resilience processes in the general population underexplored. Emerging qualitative research among people living with chronic illness suggests that resilience is often socially negotiated and grounded in meaning-making, agency and support from relational and community systems.20 Together, these gaps underscore the need for a more holistic conceptualisation of resilience that accounts for the dynamic interplay of individuals, families and society.

To our knowledge, no qualitative studies in Singapore have explored the factors and core processes that contribute to resilience in the face of health-related adversity from the integrated perspectives of adults, caregivers and care professionals. Specifically, it remains unknown how health resilience emerges from the dynamic interplay of individual, relational and systemic resources in Singapore’s unique sociocultural context, where collectivist values coexist with pragmatic self-reliance. Addressing this gap is important for understanding how health resilience is shaped not only by individual capacities but also by relational and systemic contexts. This study aimed to explore health resilience from these multiple perspectives and to identify how personal, interpersonal and systemic resources interact to support adaptation and recovery in response to health-related challenges. In doing so, we seek both to advance a culturally grounded conceptualisation of health resilience and to inform culturally responsive health promotion strategies and community-based interventions.

Materials and methods

This study adopted the Interpretive Systemic Framework (ISF)21 to guide the enquiry and subject recruitment. The ISF provides a multilevel framework of inquiry to elicit views and perspectives from diverse population groups that play critical roles in fostering health resilience.

Study population and sampling

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants from two key stakeholder groups central to understanding health resilience within the community: (1) community-dwelling adults, including family caregivers, for in-depth semi-structured interviews (ISIs) and (2) health and social care professionals for focus group discussions (FGDs). Our sampling strategy was rooted in theoretical sufficiency and informed by the concept of information power, whereby participants were purposively selected for their potential to illuminate the multidimensional and interconnected resilience processes under study. Guided by the ISF21 as a sensitising lens, sampling prioritised participants able to provide rich accounts of resilience-related experiences. Potential participants were approached by telephone, message or email. Data collection continued until theoretical sufficiency was judged to have been achieved, defined as the point at which no substantively new conceptual insights emerged across successive interviews and focus groups, and team consensus indicated that additional data were unlikely to materially alter thematic development.22 23 Eligibility criteria, sample sizes and participant characteristics for both ISIs and FGDs are detailed in table 1.

Data collection

Interview and FGDs

The interview guides (see online supplemental material) were developed based on a literature review on concepts of resilience and Bennett and Windle’s ‘The Resilience Framework’.24 The interview guide for the ISIs aimed to explore how participants or their care recipients prepared for, dealt with and overcame challenges of health adversities while the interview guide for FGDs covered three main topics: participants’ perspectives of how their patients or clients coped with health adversity; perceived factors contributing to individuals’ capacity to adapt; the perceived role of the health and social care sectors in supporting coping and recovery processes.25

Before the start of each ISI or FGD, each participant was asked to complete a brief survey capturing basic sociodemographic information and their experiences with health adversity, caregiving or providing care to individuals facing health challenges.

In-depth semistructured interviews

A total of 65 semistructured interviews were conducted with community-dwelling adults and caregivers between January 2020 and November 2021. Five interviews were conducted face-to-face in January 2020 within office facilities at a hospital, prior to the implementation of safe-distancing measures due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Subsequently, all interviews were conducted remotely to allow participants to join from the comfort and privacy of their own homes using a secure video-conferencing platform (Zoom). To mitigate limitations of remote interviewing, the research team contacted participants in advance to explain the study, orient them to the video-conferencing platform and troubleshoot potential technical issues. Video was used throughout interviews to support rapport-building and enable observation of non-verbal cues. Interviewers also minimised disruptions through scheduling guidance and maintained field notes to capture contextual observations that might otherwise be less visible in remote interactions.26 Each interview, lasting approximately 60 min, was facilitated by a trained researcher (LG or WFY) and recorded with participants’ consent. Field notes were taken during and/or after the interviews.

Focus group discussions

Eleven FGDs were conducted with healthcare and social care professionals between January and June 2021. The FGD format was chosen to encourage open discussions and interaction to capture diverse professional perspectives. Each group comprised four to six participants from various organisations and lasted 90–120 min and were recorded with participants’ consent. Participants were facilitated to connect from their home or work environments using the secure Zoom video-conferencing platform. The discussions were facilitated by an experienced mixed-methods researcher (WST), assisted by two co-facilitators (LG and WFY). Field notes were taken during and/or after the discussions. The facilitation team’s credentials and positionality are described in the Reflexivity section below.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in setting the research questions or study design. Community participants and care providers contributed through sharing lived experiences that informed interpretation of findings. Findings will be disseminated through community and professional networks involved in recruitment.

Reflexivity

The research team adopted a multidisciplinary approach, drawing on backgrounds in nursing, public health, health economics and implementation science. The three researchers who conducted the ISIs and FGDs and undertook data coding were senior health services researchers, all female, with doctoral or master’s level training and more than 10 years of research experience. This diversity of expertise enabled multiple analytic perspectives and helped to mitigate individual interpretive bias.

To ensure methodological rigour, we engaged in ongoing reflexivity throughout data collection and analysis. Regular team discussions were held to interrogate emerging codes and themes, challenge assumptions, and refine interpretations. Disagreements were resolved through iterative return to the verbatim transcripts and their contextual meanings. Feedback from the wider research team, including a senior clinician and two professors in psychology, provided additional perspectives that helped challenge assumptions and address potential gaps in interpretation. Final thematic decisions were reached through consensus.

We also considered how researchers’ seniority, sociocultural positioning, and institutional affiliations may have influenced both participant responses and interpretation of data, particularly in relation to culturally sensitive themes such as family, collectivism and self-reliance. These reflexive insights were used to enhance transparency and strengthen analytic credibility.

Data analysis

Audio recordings from the ISIs and FGDs were transcribed verbatim and audited for accuracy. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment to ensure confidentiality and minimise participant burden. To ensure a consistent coding approach, three members of the research team (LG, WFY and WST) independently double-coded three transcripts and had in-depth discussions to develop a shared coding approach. Discrepancies were resolved through in-depth discussions to ensure consistency and reliability of the research findings across different researchers.

Thematic analysis27 was employed using both inductive and deductive strategies. Framework analysis,21 a form of thematic analysis, was used to summarise and compare data across different cases using a matrix. Initial deductive coding was guided by Bennett and Windle’s ‘The Resilience Framework’, which outlines individual, community and contextual factors that interact to support resilience in the face of adversity. We also derived inductive codes from the data to allow emergent themes beyond the framework to refine and extend conceptual understanding. A working coding framework was developed and applied to all transcripts during line-by-line coding. As coding progressed, new codes were added as needed, and the team met regularly to review, discuss, and refine themes and subthemes. Final themes were validated through team consensus with input from our qualitative research consultant (AHYH).

To ensure trustworthiness and credibility, the study addressed the four criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba.28 Credibility was enhanced through independent double-coding of the first three transcripts, team consensus on theme development, and triangulation of findings across stakeholder groups (community-dwelling adults, caregivers and health and social care professionals). Dependability was supported by maintaining a clear audit trail that documented each stage of the analysis and subsequent revisions. Confirmability was strengthened through regular reflexive team discussions to bracket researchers’ assumptions and mitigate potential bias. Transferability was supported by the detailed description of participant characteristics, study context, and thematic findings to enable readers to assess applicability to other settings. Participants were not invited to review the findings. All analyses were performed using QSR NVivo software V.12 (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia, released in March 2018). This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.29

Results

Seven overarching themes were identified that together capture the multidimensional nature of health resilience. Each theme comprised two or four subthemes. Our findings affirmed that health resilience is a dynamic and interactive process shaped by intrapersonal capacities, relational supports and systemic factors. Participants described how they navigated, adapted to and recovered from health adversities, drawing on internal dispositions, meaningful social connections, psychological and behavioural adjustment, accessible health information and trusted care systems. Critically, these resources did not operate in isolation; rather, the interplay between personal agency and enabling environments underscored that resilience is both individually experienced and socially co-constructed. Each theme and its subthemes are elaborated below with selected supporting quotes provided in table 2 and online supplemental material. Although presented separately, these themes were often interconnected in participants’ narratives and collectively shaped experiences of health resilience.

Themes and subthemes of health resilience and respective quotations

Theme 1: personal dispositions and meaning-making (4 subthemes)

Participants’ internal attributes and meaning-making processes—rooted in personality traits, accumulated life experiences, and belief systems—appeared to support emotional regulation, adaptive thinking and sustained engagement with life despite illness. Four dispositions were described as supporting health resilience.

Persevering mindset

Perseverance appeared to reflect a cognitive commitment to recovery—not blind endurance but an active determination that sustained daily functioning even when outcomes were uncertain. Participants expressed an intrinsic refusal to give up despite uncertainty, illness or physical decline (see quote 1, table 2 and quote A1, online supplemental appendix 3). This mindset appeared to help participants face illness with courage and remain committed to recovery or stability, even in the face of uncertain or discouraging outcomes.

Optimistic outlook

Optimism appeared to function as a psychological buffer against despair, motivating self-care and reinforcing proactive coping in the face of health adversity. Maintaining a positive attitude—letting go of negative thoughts and cultivating hope and happiness—was seen as a vital psychological resource (quote 2, table 2 and quote A2, online supplemental appendix 3).

Faith through religiosity

In Singapore’s multicultural society, faith might function as an existential anchor that supported meaning-making and adaptive responses to suffering. Religious and spiritual beliefs across diverse faiths helped participants face illness with acceptance and inner strength. This reframing enabled adversity to be accepted as part of a larger plan without despair (quote 3, table 2) and reinforced gratitude that motivated recovery (quote A3, online supplemental appendix 3). Practices such as prayer, reading scripture and spiritual trust were described as emotionally sustaining, offering comfort and peace amid uncertainty while bringing reassurance that health adversities could be understood as part of a larger purpose.

Accumulated life wisdom

Past adversities appeared to contribute to transferable coping repertoires. Participants who had navigated previous hardships—including family separation, immigration, poverty or mental health struggles—drew on these experiences as cognitive and emotional resources, which participants described as helping reduce the perceived novelty and threat of current health challenges (quote 4, table 2 and quote A4, online supplemental appendix 3). These accumulated life lessons shaped participants’ resilience, ability to cope and sense of identity.

Theme 2: psychobehavioural adjustment (four subthemes)

Participants’ ability to psychologically adapt and adjust behaviours sustained well-being from diagnosis through long-term management. Four subthemes illuminate these adaptive processes.

Acceptance and reframing

Acceptance functioned as an important turning point, moving participants from resistance towards greater agency. Following initial denial or emotional resistance, participants described self-reflection that led to psychological adjustment and acceptance of the changes brought by health adversity. This adaptive shift enabled individuals to reframe their condition not as a defeat, but as a reality to be managed, and for some, as an opportunity for personal growth. Through this reframing, participants regained a sense of control and adopted more constructive ways of coping (quote 5, table 2 and quote A5, online supplemental appendix 3).

Motivation for self-care

Motivation for self-care was often relationally anchored, with the desire to remain well and present for loved ones strengthening engagement in self-care. Participants described internal and external motivators grounded in a sense of responsibility for both oneself and loved ones. Meaningful social connections, including family relationships, milestone events and supportive communities, often reinforced this motivation (quote 6, table 2). For some, illness also prompted renewed purpose, including re-engaging with previously overlooked aspects of life or giving back to society (quote A6, online supplemental appendix 3).

Action-oriented adaptation

Action-oriented adaptation reflected behavioural agency in practice. Participants described a range of self-initiated actions in response to health challenges, including healthier routines (diet modification, increased physical activity and medication adherence) and strategies for managing emotional stress (eg, hobbies, meditation, music, exercise and reading). These proactive adjustments supported symptom management, preserved daily function and contributed to psychological resilience (quote 7, table 2 and quote A7, online supplemental appendix 3).

Mental avoidance and concealment

Avoidance and concealment emerged as coping responses among some participants, who delayed disclosing diagnoses, concealed emotional struggles or avoided seeking support. These behaviours often stemmed from a desire to preserve independence, avoid appearing vulnerable or burdensome or protect loved ones from distress (quote 8, table 2 and quote A8, online supplemental appendix 3). While offering short-term relief, these strategies limited access to timely support, exacerbated emotional strain, deepened social isolation and potentially delayed recovery.

Theme 3: health literacy for care navigation (two subthemes)

Access to timely, accurate and understandable health information helped shape participants’ ability to make informed decisions and engage effectively with care. Importantly, health literacy may function not merely as a possession of knowledge but as an active resource: the capacity to seek, evaluate and apply information enabled participants to exercise agency within often complex or fragmented care environments.

Diverse information sources

Active information-seeking reflected resourcefulness in navigating health challenges. Several participants sought information from a wide array of sources, including care providers, online platforms, social networks and peer exchanges. Drawing on multiple sources helped participants better understand their conditions, evaluate treatment options and make tailored decisions (quote 9, table 2 and quote A9, online supplemental appendix 3), while enhancing confidence and agency. This active engagement with information represents one way resilience is enacted through sense-making under uncertainty, enabling participants to construct a workable understanding of their health situation even when professional guidance was incomplete or inconsistent.

Discerning ambiguous guidance

Insufficient, vague or conflicting information from healthcare providers or other sources sometimes led to confusion, frustration and reduced trust (quote 10, table 2). In response, participants evaluated information, sought second opinions and turned to alternative sources to reconcile uncertainty. This discernment emerged as an important skill supporting informed decision-making and fostering a greater sense of control. Healthcare professionals also observed broader systemic confusion among residents, linked to fragmented care pathways and limited patient education (quote A10, online supplemental appendix 3). These findings suggest resilience may be supported not only by access to information but by the capacity to critically interpret and navigate uncertainty within complex systems.

Theme 4: social support and relational anchors (two subthemes)

Supportive relationships within and beyond the family provided emotional grounding, practical assistance and a sense of continuity and belonging, helping individuals navigate health challenges with greater confidence.

Anchoring role of family support

Family members played a central role in emotional and practical caregiving, from offering encouragement and assisting with treatment decisions to providing round-the-clock support when needed. These supportive familial relationships provided emotional grounding and hands-on help that fostered hope, a sense of stability, belonging and shared resilience (quote 11, table 2; and quote A11, online supplemental appendix 3). Healthcare professionals echoed these observations, noting that engaged families could improve patients’ motivation, treatment adherence and recovery, whereas strained or unsupportive relationships could contribute to distress and hinder recovery (quotes A12 and A13, online supplemental appendix 3).

Supportive networks and enabling environments

Beyond family, participants also drew on support from friends, neighbours, workplaces and community networks, particularly when family support was unavailable or insufficient. Timely encouragement, colleague understanding, advice and logistical support sustained emotional well-being and navigated daily challenges (quotes 12, table 2 and quote A14, online supplemental appendix 3). The moral comfort and tangible assistance (eg, flexible work arrangements, peer mentorship, neighbour check-ins) reinforced confidence and motivation. Conversely, absence or refusal of such support was a barrier to recovery.

Theme 5: care affordability and financial security (two subthemes)

In Singapore’s co-payment healthcare financing system, affordability critically shaped care-seeking behaviour, treatment decisions and coping responses. Key influences included income, savings, insurance and family support. Together, these findings position financial security as a structural determinant of health resilience: rather than resilience residing solely in individual capacities, financial security can enable or constrain people’s ability to translate personal dispositions and motivations into effective health action.

Cost as a care barrier

Healthcare cost frequently influenced care decisions. Participants delayed or forwent medical appointments, therapies or medications due to affordability concerns, particularly for specialist services, unsubsidised treatments or long-term care (quote 13, table 2 and quote A15, online supplemental appendix 3). Financial barriers were shaped not only by limited resources but also by challenges navigating subsidies and support schemes and were more pronounced among those with limited savings, unclear eligibility for assistance or low financial literacy.

Managing care cost contingencies

Participants employed strategies to manage healthcare costs, drawing on structural resources and personal foresight. Access to health insurance (national, employer-based or private) and financial support alleviated financial anxiety and facilitated timely access to care (quote 14, table 2). Some showed financial foresight by purchasing insurance early, setting aside savings or actively seeking subsidies. However, financial strain persisted for those facing out-of-pocket costs, especially for outpatient, emergency services or long-term care (quote A16, online supplemental appendix 3).

Taken together, these subthemes illustrate how financial security functions as a structural enabler of resilience: financial buffers allowed participants to focus cognitive and emotional resources on recovery, whereas financial strain competed with illness management and constrained adaptive responses.

Theme 6: accessibility of and experience in healthcare systems (two subthemes)

Access to and experience within the healthcare system significantly influenced individuals’ resilience, shaped by trust, understanding, continuity and service responsiveness. These findings suggest that the healthcare system functions not merely as a site of treatment delivery but as a relational environment whose quality can support or hinder people’s capacity to cope.

Perceived accessibility and service navigation

Geographical distance, transport convenience and affordability, system inefficiencies and appointment scheduling shaped perceived access (quote 15, table 2). These logistical barriers sometimes delayed care, heightened stress or prompted reliance on informal alternatives (quote A17, online supplemental appendix 3). These findings suggest access functions not merely as a service issue but as a structural condition that can enable or constrain resilience through its effects on stress, continuity and resource mobilisation.

Relational trust in healthcare interactions

Communication and trust with healthcare professionals supported understanding, emotional adaptation and engagement in care. Respectful, affirming, person-centred interactions reinforced dignity, strengthened trust and motivated continued engagement in self-care (quote 16, table 2 and quote A18, online supplemental appendix 3). Clear, compassionate communication and continuity with familiar providers were valued. Conversely, impersonal, dismissive or rushed interactions undermined trust, created confusion and discouraged care-seeking and compliance (quote A19, online supplemental appendix 3). These findings highlight that relational quality within healthcare interactions is not merely a service satisfaction issue but a resilience-shaping mechanism: the degree to which patients feel seen, understood and respected may influence their capacity and willingness to engage in adaptive health behaviours.

Theme 7: availability of and access to social support services (two subthemes)

Social support services provided by social organisations, including emotional, spiritual and logistical aid, buffered the psychological and practical impact of illness. Availability, accessibility and coordination of these services influenced participants’ sense of connectedness, coping capacity and continuity of care. From a resilience perspective, these services represent the outermost layer of systemic scaffolding: they can compensate for deficits in personal, familial or healthcare system resources but only when they are reachable, navigable and responsive to diverse needs.

Psychoemotional and spiritual support services

Counselling, community support groups, religious care and peer sharing provided vital emotional anchoring amid health adversity. These services enabled individuals to process uncertainty, rediscover purpose and support coping and well-being (quote 17, table 2 and quote A20, online supplemental appendix 3). The significance of these services extended beyond symptom relief; they provided a relational and existential anchor that reinforced participants’ sense of meaning and capacity to persevere, functioning as a resilience resource that formal healthcare rarely provides.

Eligibility and accessibility barriers

Despite service availability, participants faced barriers navigating complex eligibility requirements, means testing and service awareness (quote 18, table 2 and quote A21, online supplemental appendix 3). Challenges were particularly pronounced among the sandwiched class (middle-income individuals ineligible for means-tested subsidies yet unable to comfortably afford full-cost services), caregivers and marginalised groups (eg, transnational families), whose needs were not always well captured by conventional support criteria. Providers believed that accessible, well-communicated and needs-adaptive services would facilitate timely relief and reduce stress, enabling focus on recovery and caregiving. These barriers to service access reveal a critical resilience equity issue: the individuals most in need of systemic support may be least able to access it, suggesting that eligibility criteria and service navigation pathways require redesign to reach underserved populations.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study offers a nuanced and contextualised understanding of health resilience as it unfolds in everyday life among community-dwelling adults, caregivers and healthcare and social care professionals in Singapore. By identifying seven interconnected themes, our findings illustrate that health resilience is not merely an individual trait but a dynamic, relational and systemic process that is continuously shaped by personal dispositions, interpersonal relationships and institutional structures. Beyond identifying protective resources, our findings contribute to socioecological understandings of resilience by conceptualising health resilience as an emergent process arising across these interdependent domains.

A central insight from this study is that resilience is expressed not through isolated protective factors but through the ways individual capacities such as optimism, agency and meaning-making operate through and are reinforced by social relationships, access to resources and enabling systems. Conversely, constraints at relational and structural levels may limit the enactment of personal resilience. Together, these findings support an interpretive systemic view of resilience as socially embedded and shaped across interacting levels.

To synthesise these insights, we propose a conceptual model of health resilience as a dynamic, multidimensional system (figure 1). This model illustrates how resilience emerges through interactions across three interconnected levels—intrapersonal, relational and systemic—with bidirectional influences shaping how individuals navigate and adapt to health adversity.

The multidimensional construct of health resilience.

Rather than representing discrete domains, the themes function as mutually reinforcing layers of resilience. For example, personal agency was often enabled by supportive relationships and trusted healthcare interactions, while financial insecurity or service barriers could undermine otherwise strong adaptive capacities. This interdependence underscores resilience as a dynamic systemic process rather than an individual attribute alone.

The following sections interpret our findings through this framework, focusing on the hybridised cultural nature of resilience in Singapore, the differential contributions of resources to well-being and the interplay between systemic factors and individual adaptation. The model may also serve as a heuristic for designing multilevel interventions that strengthen health resilience across personal, relational and structural domains.

Theoretical integration and cultural hybridity in resilience

Our findings align with and extend existing literature that conceptualises resilience as a multifaceted construct encompassing psychological, behavioural, relational and existential dimensions30 31 and as a dynamic process activated through life challenges and adversities.32 We demonstrate that resilience involves both reliance on internal resources, such as adaptive acceptance and reframing, as well as the capacity to recognise changing circumstances, mobilise available supports and engage in adaptive behaviours that sustain well-being.

A hybridised model of resilience: navigating collectivism and pragmatic self-reliance

Notably, our findings suggest a hybridised model of coping that reflects Singapore’s unique sociocultural positioning. Viewed through a socioecological and relational lens, these findings suggest that health resilience may be shaped through the coexistence of interdependence, family obligation and pragmatic self-reliance, rather than through a simple individualism–collectivism binary. In this context, self-reliance and interdependence may be mutually reinforcing, with self-reliance operating not in opposition to collectivist values but often in service of relational obligations and navigating institutional structures. This suggests resilience as a relationally embedded rather than purely individual process.

Our data reflect this synthesis. Collectivist values were evident in the emphasis on family support (theme 4.1), avoidance of being a burden (theme 2.4) and the framing of suffering within broader spiritual or familial narratives (themes 1.3, 1.4). This orientation is consistent with Eastern philosophies (eg, Buddhism, Confucianism) that frame hardship as existentially meaningful.33 34 Trust in healthcare professionals and systems (theme 6.2) further highlighted how relational and institutional contexts may support or constrain resilience.

Within this relational framework, a distinct form of pragmatic self-reliance emerged, manifested through persevering mindsets (theme 1.1), proactive information-seeking (theme 3) and behavioural adaptation (theme 2.3). This finding aligns with research highlighting the contextual shaping of resilience,35 particularly in Asian societies where social norms influence help-seeking behaviours, illness disclosure and caregiving roles.8 36 37 Moreover, participants often blended spiritual acceptance with proactive self-management, illustrating a culturally situated coping repertoire that underscores the need for resilience frameworks accommodating diverse worldviews and adaptive strategies.

Differential contributions to well-being domains

Guided by our definition of resilience as a dynamic and multidimensional process to maintain or regain well-being across multiple domains, we examined how the identified themes contribute differentially to psychological, social and functional outcomes.

Psychological well-being: Themes 1 (personal dispositions) and 2 (psychological adjustment) were most directly associated with sustaining mental and emotional health. Adaptive acceptance (theme 2.1), optimistic reframing (theme 1.2) and faith (theme 1.3) were core to maintaining psychological equilibrium, consistent with literature linking emotional regulation and adaptive processes to improve psychological adjustment in chronic illness.38

The interplay of systemic and relational support

A key insight from this study is that systemic factors can either facilitate or constrain the activation of personal resilience resources. Participants exhibited greater resilience when systems were navigable, inclusive, and emotionally supportive, often enabled by empathetic providers, coordinated services and community-based psychosocial support. Conversely, poor communication, rigid eligibility criteria and fragmented care exacerbated anxiety and delayed help-seeking, particularly among marginalised groups. Challenges such as unclear information (theme 3.2), financial barriers (theme 5.1) and complex service eligibility (theme 7.2) functioned as significant stressors that depleted cognitive and emotional resources necessary for adaptation.

Furthermore, supportive families (theme 4.1) and trusting patient-provider relationships (theme 6.2) fostered environments conducive to resilience, whereas strained relationships or impersonal care amplified vulnerability. This indicates that efforts to strengthen resilience should include enhancing relational competencies among healthcare professionals and developing alternative social supports (eg, peer groups, community networks) for those lacking strong familial ties.

Implications for policy and practice

Our findings suggest that strengthening health resilience requires moving beyond individual-level approaches to interventions that address relational, service and structural determinants. In practice, health services should integrate psycho-emotional and spiritual support into routine care, improve care coordination through navigational support and promote relational continuity to strengthen trust and engagement. Care planning should also better incorporate cultural beliefs, family context and patient-defined priorities to support more person-centred and responsive care.

At the policy level, findings highlight the need to expand access to support services for individuals who may fall outside conventional eligibility criteria and to strengthen community-based supports, particularly where familial support is limited. Investment in peer networks, social support infrastructure and caregiver-supportive policies may help buffer vulnerability and promote resilience. Overall, these findings suggest that fostering health resilience requires coordinated action across healthcare delivery, community systems and policy environments.

Strengths and limitations

This study draws on perspectives from community-dwelling adults, caregivers and health and social care professionals, enabling triangulated understanding of health resilience. The use of an interpretive-systemic approach allowed us to capture health resilience as shaped by interacting intrapersonal, relational and structural factors. The study offers a culturally grounded conceptual framework that contributes to broadening predominantly Western conceptualisations of resilience. However, several limitations should be considered. First, as a qualitative study conducted in Singapore, findings may not be directly transferable to other sociocultural or healthcare contexts. Second, participants willing to discuss resilience may have been more engaged or adaptive, potentially introducing self-selection bias. Although we sought multiple stakeholder perspectives, resilience processes may differ across underserved populations not fully represented here. Third, although remote interviews enabled participation during the pandemic, virtual data collection may have limited observation of some non-verbal cues and some dimensions of data depth and validation despite facilitation strategies to maintain rapport. Fourth, while reflexive discussions and coding consensus enhanced trustworthiness, participant member checking was not conducted and social desirability in discussing coping may have influenced responses. Finally, as an interpretive qualitative study, the proposed framework should be understood as a contextually grounded conceptual model that may warrant further examination and refinement in other populations and settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study delineates health resilience as a multidimensional and dynamic process shaped by personal, social and structural factors. Moving beyond trait-based explanations, we show that resilience is a process cultivated through access to resources, supportive relationships and responsive systems. These insights underscore the importance of developing integrated public health strategies that enhance personal control, emotional well-being and systemic accessibility, particularly in ageing societies facing rising chronic disease burdens.
