Honey-biosynthesized silver nanoparticles against vancomycin-resistant enterococci: integrated antibacterial, antibiofilm, mechanistic and preliminary safety evaluation
Authors: Ghadah S. Abusalim, Mohammed Alissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), including Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium, are multidrug-resistant pathogens with limited treatment options. This study evaluated honey-biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) against VRE using integrated antibacterial, antibiofilm, mechanistic, and preliminary safety assessments. Methods: AgNPs were synthesized using natural honey as a reducing and capping agent and characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering, zeta potential analysis, SEM-EDS, FTIR, and stability assays. Antibacterial activity was assessed using MIC, MBC, agar well diffusion, and time-kill assays. Antibiofilm activity was evaluated by crystal violet assay. Mechanistic responses were examined by molecular docking, RT-qPCR analysis of pbp5 and srtA, PBP5 Bocillin-FL binding, and Sortase A activity assays. Preliminary safety was assessed using HEK293 and HepG2 cell viability, LDH release, and erythrocyte hemolysis assays. Results: AgNPs showed MICs of 16 µg/mL against E. faecalis and 32 µg/mL against E. faecium, with MBCs of 32–64 µg/mL and inhibition zones of 14–18 mm. Time-kill assays showed > 5 log10 CFU/mL reduction for E. faecalis and approximately 4 log10 CFU/mL reduction for E. faecium after 24 h. AgNPs had a hydrodynamic diameter of 18 ± 3 nm, PDI of 0.24 ± 0.03, and zeta potential of -32.5 ± 2.1 mV. Biofilm inhibition ranged from 61.4 to 86.5%. AgNP exposure downregulated pbp5 and srtA, reduced PBP5 labelling, and decreased Sortase A activity. Cell viability remained ≥ 81%, and hemolysis was < 3%. Conclusion: Honey-biosynthesized AgNPs demonstrated bactericidal and antibiofilm activity against VRE with mechanism-linked effects and acceptable preliminary in vitro tolerance at antibacterial concentration ranges.

Introduction

The persistent increase of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become one of the most significant risks to global public health in the twenty-first century1. Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium represent a notably concerning category of resistant pathogens2. These organisms have transitioned from being perceived as relatively harmless commensals of the human gastrointestinal tract to significant opportunistic pathogens accountable for bloodstream infections, endocarditis, urinary tract infections, and wound infections, particularly in hospital environments3. The problem is exacerbated by the worldwide proliferation of vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), which significantly limits treatment alternatives and correlates with heightened morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures4,5.

Vancomycin resistance in Enterococcus species is mainly facilitated by the acquisition of van gene clusters that alter the terminal residues of peptidoglycan precursors, significantly diminishing the binding affinity of glycopeptide antibiotics6. This molecular adaptability has rendered vancomycin, formerly regarded as a last-resort antibiotic, useless in numerous clinical scenarios6. Concerningly, VRE infections are no longer restricted to tertiary hospitals in affluent nations; they are increasingly documented in low- and middle-income areas, where antimicrobial stewardship and infection control measures are frequently inadequate5. The necessity for alternate antibacterial methods is thus not only theoretical. It is instantaneous.

Recent reviews further emphasize that VRE control requires updated surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship, and prevention strategies because treatment options remain limited7,8.

There has been a resurgence of interest in natural products and nature-derived antimicrobials9. Honey holds a distinctive and historically significant position among these. For millennia, Honey has been utilized in traditional medicine for wound treatment and infection control, predating the introduction of modern antibiotics. Recent studies have confirmed numerous empirical applications, showing that natural Honey possesses extensive antibacterial properties against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including multidrug-resistant strains10. The antibacterial activity is multifaceted, resulting from a combination of elevated osmolarity, reduced pH, hydrogen peroxide generation, and the presence of bioactive phytochemicals, including flavonoids and phenolic acids.

Natural Honey, abundant in reducing sugars, enzymes, amino acids, and polyphenols, creates an optimal biochemical milieu for the reduction of metal ions and the stabilization of newly synthesized nanoparticles11,12. Honey’s dual role as both a reducing and capping agent establishes it as a potent resource in green nanotechnology13,14.

The integration of honey with silver nanotechnology constitutes an intriguing medicinal approach12. Biosynthesized AgNPs using natural honey may exhibit superior antibacterial efficacy compared to honey or silver independently, due to the synergistic interactions between honey’s inherent bioactivity and the diverse antimicrobial mechanisms of AgNPs. Recent research indicates that honey-mediated AgNPs exhibit enhanced stability, biocompatibility, and antibacterial efficacy, while reducing the reliance on hazardous reagents commonly used in nanoparticle production11. This is especially pertinent in biomedical applications, where safety and sustainability are crucial.

Recent reviews of green AgNPs also support their relevance as antimicrobial platforms because they combine sustainable synthesis with cell-envelope disruption, oxidative stress induction, antibiofilm activity, and compatibility with antibiotic-combination strategies15,16.

Notwithstanding these encouraging characteristics, the effectiveness of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus species is still inadequately investigated. This study intends to assess the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of AgNPs biosynthesized with natural honey against vancomycin-resistant enterococci.

Although honey-mediated AgNP synthesis has been widely described11–16, most available studies emphasize the synthesis platform or broad antibacterial screening. Therefore, the originality of the present work does not reside in introducing honey as a new reducing agent. It resides in the integrated VRE-focused evaluation of a honey-capped AgNP formulation across clinically relevant E. faecalis and E. faecium isolates, including strain-level honey-alone controls, MIC/MBC and time-kill assays, antibiofilm testing, nanoparticle-stability profiling, docking-supported hypotheses, and experimental interrogation of PBP5- and Sortase A-related responses. This design specifically addresses whether a sustainable honey-derived AgNP system can produce measurable bactericidal and antibiofilm effects against VRE while retaining preliminary mammalian-cell and hemocompatibility tolerance at antibacterial concentrations.

Materials and methods

Sample procurement and preparation

Natural raw honey was obtained directly from certified local beekeepers to minimize adulteration and processing effects. The honey was produced by the Western honey bee, Apis mellifera, and was classified as unifloral honey based on beekeeper records and predominant floral sources in the collection area. Prior to use, honey samples were filtered through sterile gauze to remove debris but were not subjected to heat treatment or chemical processing in order to preserve bioactive constituents.

Before utilization, the honey samples were maintained in sterile, amber-colored glass vials at 4 °C to safeguard bioactive components and avert deterioration. For nanoparticle synthesis, honey was diluted fivefold by mixing one volume of raw honey with four volumes of sterile deionized water, giving a 20% (v/v) working honey solution. Sterile deionized water was therefore the dilution solvent, and the final honey concentration in the AgNO₃ reaction mixture was approximately 1.8% (v/v) after addition of 5 mL diluted honey solution to 50 mL AgNO₃.

For traceability, the honey used in this study was collected from a registered apiary in Al-Kharj, Riyadh Province, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (24.1556° N latitude, 47.3123° E longitude; WGS84), at an altitude of 438 m above sea level. Collection was performed on 15 March 2025 between 08:30 and 10:15 Arabian Standard Time. The honey was produced by the Western honey bee, Apis mellifera, and was beekeeper-classified as unifloral Sidr/acacia-dominant honey. Three independent hive-level honey lots (H1-H3) were collected aseptically; each lot was filtered separately and used to prepare three independent AgNP synthesis batches, and the biological assays were performed in triplicate.

Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs)

The biosynthesis technique was modified from established green synthesis protocols to enhance yield and stability for antibacterial applications. Three independent AgNP synthesis batches were prepared from the three honey collection lots to support biological replication. A 50 mL volume of 1 mM AgNO₃ solution was prepared in sterile deionized water and heated to 60 °C with continuous magnetic stirring on an IKA C-MAG HS 7 digital hotplate stirrer (IKA-Werke GmbH, Staufen, Germany). Subsequently, 5 mL of the fivefold-diluted honey solution (20%, v/v, prepared in sterile deionized water) was added dropwise to the pre-heated AgNO₃ solution while vigorous stirring was maintained to ensure uniform dispersion of the reactants. The reaction was allowed to continue for 2 h. A visible color change from pale yellow to deep brown was observed as a preliminary experimental indication of nanoparticle formation, consistent with surface plasmon resonance behavior typical of AgNP synthesis17.

Optimization of AgNP biosynthesis conditions

Preliminary optimization experiments were conducted to establish synthesis conditions that produced stable, uniformly dispersed AgNPs suitable for biological applications. Key parameters evaluated included silver nitrate concentration (0.5-2 mM), honey concentration (5–25% v/v), reaction temperature (25–70 °C), and incubation time (30–180 min). Optimal nanoparticle formation was achieved using 1 mM AgNO₃, 20% (v/v) honey solution, a reaction temperature of 60 °C, and an incubation time of 2 h.

These conditions were selected based on the appearance of a sharp surface plasmon resonance peak, minimal aggregation, and sustained colloidal stability. The optimized protocol was subsequently applied consistently across all experiments to ensure reproducibility and comparability of results.

Physicochemical characterization of biosynthesized AgNPs

Unless otherwise stated, characterization and biological measurements were performed in triplicate under the operating conditions described in the relevant subsections below. To follow a paragraph-based Springer Nature-style Methods presentation, the instrument make and model information has been integrated directly into the corresponding procedures rather than presented as a standalone table. General support equipment used across the experimental workflow included an Eppendorf 5424 R refrigerated microcentrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) for centrifugation steps, a DensiCHEK Plus colorimeter (bioMerieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) for McFarland inoculum adjustment, a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany) for bacterial incubation, a NanoDrop One UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) for RNA purity and concentration assessment, a Mini-Sub Cell GT agarose gel electrophoresis system with PowerPac Basic power supply (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States) for RNA integrity checks, and a Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell electrophoresis system with PowerPac Basic power supply (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States) for SDS-PAGE. These details are also embedded in the relevant methodological subsections where the instruments were used.

UV–visible spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential

The optical characteristics of the experimentally prepared AgNPs were evaluated using a Shimadzu UV-1900i UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Absorbance spectra were acquired from 200 to 800 nm using sterile deionized water as the blank. During synthesis, spectra were recorded at 60, 120, and 180 min to monitor time-dependent nanoparticle formation. For stability evaluation, spectra were also recorded immediately after synthesis (Day 0) and after storage for 30 days (Day 30), with additional intermediate checks on Days 7, 14, and 21. These measurements were used to verify the surface plasmon resonance profile and to detect any aggregation-related spectral displacement.

Freshly prepared AgNP suspensions were diluted 1:10 in sterile deionized water for dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis. The diluted suspensions were gently vortexed and sonicated for 5 min in a Branson 2510 ultrasonic bath (Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, CT, United States) to reduce weak agglomerates before measurement. Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index were determined at 25 ± 1 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom) equipped with a 633 nm He-Ne laser and operating at a 173° backscattering angle. Particle-size measurements were carried out in disposable polystyrene cuvettes, whereas zeta potential was measured in disposable folded capillary zeta cells using the same dispersing medium. Each sample was equilibrated for 120 s before analysis, measured in triplicate, and reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)

The surface morphology, topographical characteristics, and elemental composition of the biosynthesized AgNPs were examined by scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) using a JEOL JSM-IT500HR scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an Oxford Instruments X-MaxN 80 EDS detector (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, United Kingdom). SEM imaging was conducted under high-vacuum conditions at an accelerating voltage of 10–20 kV and at different magnifications to examine nanoparticle surface architecture, size heterogeneity, and aggregation behavior.

The liquid AgNP colloid was prepared for SEM analysis by dilution with sterile deionized water at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v). A 10 µL aliquot of the diluted suspension was drop-cast onto clean carbon-taped aluminum SEM stubs and air-dried overnight at room temperature in a dust-free desiccator. The dried samples were then sputter-coated with an approximately 5 nm gold layer using a Quorum Q150R ES sputter coater (Quorum Technologies, Laughton, United Kingdom) fitted with a gold target, to enhance conductivity and minimize charging during imaging. The coated samples were examined for SEM-based morphological characterization, and representative fields were further analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to determine elemental composition.

The elemental composition and chemical purity of the nanoparticles were then verified using EDS integrated with the SEM system13.

Nanoparticle stability assessment

The colloidal stability of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs was thoroughly assessed to ascertain their resistance to aggregation under physicochemical circumstances pertinent to biomedical applications. Stability was evaluated by a combination of time-dependent UV-visible spectrophotometry using the Shimadzu UV-1900i UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) and ionic strength challenge assays, both of which serve as sensitive markers of nanoparticle aggregation and surface instability11. Zeta potential measurements were concurrently performed using the Zetasizer Nano ZS analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom) following the stability assays to assess changes in surface charge over time and under ionic stress.

The stability study was conducted for 30 days under controlled storage conditions. AgNP suspensions were stored in sterile amber glass vials at 4 °C and at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) under dark conditions to limit photo-induced changes. UV-visible spectra and visible signs of precipitation or color change were recorded on days 0, 7, 14, 21, and 30. Ionic stability was evaluated by exposing AgNP suspensions to NaCl solutions ranging from 0 to 500 mM for 2 h at room temperature, followed by UV-visible and zeta potential measurements.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was performed to identify functional groups from honey components involved in the reduction and stabilization of AgNPs. In response to the need to characterize the actual synthesis reagent, spectra were obtained for the 20% (v/v) diluted honey working solution used for AgNP synthesis, AgNO₃, and washed honey-biosynthesized AgNPs. An aliquot of the diluted honey solution was dried at 40 °C before ATR-FTIR analysis so that its functional-group profile could be compared directly with the dried AgNP sample. Spectra were acquired within 4000–400 cm⁻¹, and peaks associated with hydroxyl, carbonyl, amine, and carbohydrate-related groups were identified to clarify their role in nanoparticle formation15,16.

The AgNP colloid was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min using an Eppendorf 5424 R refrigerated microcentrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), washed three times with sterile deionized water to remove unbound honey constituents, and dried at 40 °C to obtain powdered AgNPs. The dried sample was analyzed by attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) using a Spectrum Two FTIR spectrometer with ATR accessory (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, United States). Spectra were acquired across 4000 –400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ with 32 scans per sample, and a background spectrum was collected before each measurement.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy was performed to complement SEM and DLS measurements and to assess nanoparticle morphology at higher resolution. A 10 µL aliquot of a 1:10 diluted AgNP suspension was deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids, allowed to adsorb for 2 min, blotted gently, air-dried at room temperature, and examined using a JEOL JEM-2100 Plus transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Representative micrographs were recorded from at least five fields per synthesis batch.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Clinical isolates of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp. (VRE) were obtained from archived stocks donated by the Medical Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Medical Laboratories, College of Applied Medical Sciences, Shaqra University, Shaqra 11,961, Riyadh Province, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The organism donation was made through the departmental archived-culture repository under the authorization of the Head of the Department of Medical Laboratories, College of Applied Medical Sciences, Shaqra University. The procurement and use of these archived clinical bacterial isolates followed Shaqra University Institutional Review Board (IRB) guidance and institutional biosafety approval.

All methods involving the procurement, handling, storage, processing, and microbiological testing of the archived VRE isolates were performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines, national biosafety requirements, and applicable ethical regulations governing the research use of archived clinical microbiology specimens. The isolates were obtained only from archived laboratory stocks and were handled under appropriate containment conditions by trained personnel.

No human participants, human tissue samples, direct patient contact, intervention, or additional patient sample collection were involved in this study. Therefore, informed consent from participants and/or their legal guardians was not applicable for this work.

Species identity and vancomycin-resistance profiles were confirmed using established biochemical tests and automated antimicrobial-susceptibility testing on a VITEK 2 Compact automated identification and antimicrobial-susceptibility testing system (bioMerieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France), with interpretation performed according to applicable CLSI breakpoints. Isolates were cultivated on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany) before experimental use. All manipulations involving VRE cultures were performed by trained personnel in a Thermo Scientific 1300 Series A2 Class II biological safety cabinet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) under the approved institutional biosafety conditions.

Antibacterial assays

Methodological rigor was ensured by incorporating appropriate positive and negative controls in all antimicrobial assays. Vancomycin-sensitive Enterococcus strains treated with standard antimicrobial agents served as positive controls, while untreated bacterial cultures functioned as negative controls. These controls verified assay validity and enabled reliable comparison of the antibacterial activity of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs.

Honey was also evaluated independently to distinguish its intrinsic antimicrobial properties from nanoparticle-mediated effects. The negligible inhibitory activity observed with honey alone indicates that the antibacterial efficacy is primarily attributable to silver nanoparticle formation rather than the action of honey constituents in isolation. Honey-alone controls were included for all four VRE isolates under the same broth microdilution and agar well diffusion conditions to permit strain-level comparison with the AgNP-treated groups.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the biosynthesized AgNPs against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) isolates was assessed using the broth microdilution method following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommendations. Serial two-fold dilutions of AgNP suspensions (from 1 to 512 µg/mL) were formulated in sterile Mueller-Hinton broth. Bacterial inocula were calibrated to a 0.5 McFarland standard using a DensiCHEK Plus colorimeter (bioMerieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France). Following inoculation, microtiter plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18 to 20 h in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany). The MIC endpoint was established as the minimum AgNP concentration exhibiting no observable growth13.

Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

Bactericidal efficacy was examined by subculturing 10 µL aliquots from wells showing no apparent growth onto BHI agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 24 h in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany). The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was documented as the lowest concentration achieving a ≥ 99.9% reduction in viable colony counts relative to the untreated control.

Agar well diffusion zone-of-inhibition assay

Zone-of-inhibition testing was performed by agar well diffusion, which was the plate-based method used for the representative inhibition-zone images. Overnight VRE cultures were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard using a DensiCHEK Plus colorimeter (bioMerieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) and uniformly swabbed onto Mueller-Hinton agar plates. Sterile wells of 6 mm diameter were punched into the agar, and 50 µL of honey-biosynthesized AgNP suspension was added to each well. The tabulated inhibition-zone values were generated using the common 64 µg/mL AgNP screening concentration; honey-alone solution, sterile deionized water, and untreated cultures served as negative controls, while vancomycin was used as the positive-control antibiotic against vancomycin-sensitive Enterococcus control strains. Plates were allowed to pre-diffuse for 30 min at room temperature and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany). Inhibition zones were measured in millimeters across two perpendicular diameters using a Mitutoyo 500-196-30 Absolute Digimatic Caliper (Mitutoyo Corporation, Kanagawa, Japan), and the mean value was calculated from three independent plates.

Anti-biofilm activity assay

The anti-biofilm activity of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium was evaluated using a crystal violet microtiter plate assay. Briefly, standardized bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard using a DensiCHEK Plus colorimeter (bioMerieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) and inoculated into sterile 96-well polystyrene microplates containing brain heart infusion broth supplemented with 1% glucose to promote biofilm formation. AgNP suspensions were added at sub-MIC, MIC, and 2×MIC concentrations and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under static conditions in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany). Following incubation, planktonic cells were removed, and the wells were gently washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline to eliminate non-adherent cells. The attached biofilms were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, and excess stain was removed by washing with distilled water.

The retained crystal violet was solubilized using 95% ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, United States). Percentage biofilm inhibition was calculated relative to untreated controls. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Biofilm inhibition values were interpreted as weak (< 25%), moderate (25–50%), strong (50–75%), or very strong (> 75%) anti-biofilm activity. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with significance accepted at p < 0.05.

Time-kill kinetics

Time-kill tests were conducted to clarify the temporal bactericidal dynamics of AgNPs. Bacterial cultures adjusted using a DensiCHEK Plus colorimeter (bioMerieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) were subjected to concentrations of 1× and 2× the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and incubated at 37 °C in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany). Aliquots were collected at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h, serially diluted, and plated to quantify surviving colony-forming units (CFUs); plates were incubated under the same bacterial-culture conditions before colony enumeration.

Molecular docking analysis

An in silico molecular docking analysis was performed to elucidate the mechanistic understanding of the antibacterial efficacy of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp. This computational method was utilized to forecast possible interactions among bioactive chemicals produced from honey, silver-associated ligands, and crucial bacterial target proteins involved in cell wall production, stress response, and virulence. Docking analysis served as an adjunct to experimental results, offering molecular-level corroboration for the observed antibacterial properties.

Selection and preparation of target proteins

Crucial Enterococcus protein targets linked to antibiotic resistance and cellular viability were chosen based on their biological significance and the availability of high-resolution crystal structures. These encompass Sortase and Penicillin-binding protein 5 (PBP5). The three-dimensional structures of the target proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB).

Protein structures were generated with AutoDock Tools and Discovery Studio software by eliminating crystallographic water molecules and co-crystallized ligands, subsequently including polar hydrogen atoms and assigning Kollman charges. Energy minimization was conducted to remove steric conflicts and stabilize protein conformations before docking20.

Ligand selection and preparation

Considering the intricacy of AgNPs as supramolecular structures, the docking investigation concentrated on notable bioactive chemicals produced from honey that are recognized for their roles in nanoparticle capping and antibacterial efficacy, such as quercetin. This chemical was chosen due to comprehensive research evidencing its occurrence in natural honey and its antibacterial properties.

Ligand structures were obtained from the PubChem database and optimized utilizing MMFF94 force fields. Torsional bonds were delineated, and Gasteiger charges were allocated before docking.

Docking procedure

Molecular docking were conducted utilizing AutoDock Vina, chosen for its computational efficiency and dependable scoring functions. Grid boxes were delineated to encompass the active or binding sites of each target protein, based on established catalytic residues or anticipated pockets identified using CASTp. Docking parameters were standardized throughout to ensure the comparability of binding affinities.

Each docking run produced several binding postures, which were prioritized according to estimated binding free energy (ΔG, kcal/mol). The most thermodynamically advantageous conformations were chosen for subsequent interaction investigation.

Interaction analysis and visualization

Protein-ligand interactions were visualized and examined utilizing PyMOL and Discovery Studio Visualizer. Hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, π–π stacking, and metal coordination interactions were identified to clarify the characteristics of ligand binding. Special emphasis was placed on interactions involving catalytic residues, active-site loops, and resistance-related motifs. Binding affinity patterns were analyzed in conjunction with experimentally verified antibacterial activity, facilitating a comprehensive interpretation of in vitro and in silico results.

RT-qPCR analysis of pbp5 and srtA gene expression

Vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis and E. faecium isolates were grown in brain heart infusion broth at 37 °C in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany) until the mid-logarithmic growth phase. Bacterial suspensions were then exposed to honey-biosynthesized AgNPs at sub-inhibitory concentrations corresponding to 0.5×MIC for 4 h. Sub-MIC exposure was selected to minimize nonspecific RNA degradation caused by extensive bacterial killing while allowing assessment of early transcriptional responses. Untreated cultures, honey-alone-treated cultures, and AgNO₃-treated cultures were included as controls, consistent with recent nanoparticle-based antimicrobial gene-expression studies.

Following treatment, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation using an Eppendorf 5424 R refrigerated microcentrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline, and subjected to total RNA extraction using a bacterial RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracted RNA was treated with DNase I to remove genomic DNA contamination. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop One UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States), and RNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis using a Mini-Sub Cell GT electrophoresis system with PowerPac Basic power supply (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States); gels were documented using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States). RT-qPCR experimental design, RNA-quality assessment, primer validation, and reporting procedures followed the updated MIQE 2.0 recommendations for quantitative PCR experiments. The primer sequences and reference genes used for these assays are summarized in Table 1.

Complementary DNA was synthesized from equal quantities of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). Gene-specific primers were designed for pbp5 and srtA, while gyrB and 16 S rRNA were used as internal reference genes. Each reaction was performed in triplicate. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method, with untreated bacterial cultures serving as the calibrator group24. Fold-change values below 1.0 were interpreted as downregulation, whereas values above 1.0 indicated upregulation. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

PBP5 functional binding assay

The effect of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs on PBP5 activity was assessed using a Bocillin-FL competitive binding assay. Bocillin-FL is a fluorescent penicillin derivative widely used for detecting active penicillin-binding proteins and has recently been applied in Enterococcus PBP activity profiling and PBP5 binding analyses25–27.

Bacterial membrane protein fractions were prepared from treated and untreated VRE isolates after exposure to AgNPs at 0.5×MIC and 1×MIC for 4 h. Protein concentrations were normalized using the Bradford assay. Membrane protein preparations were incubated with fluorescent Bocillin-FL, separated by SDS-PAGE using a Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell electrophoresis system with PowerPac Basic power supply (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States), and fluorescently labelled PBP bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States). Band intensities corresponding to PBP5 were quantified by densitometric analysis. Reduced Bocillin-FL labelling was interpreted as decreased availability or functional accessibility of PBP5. Untreated bacterial membranes were used as the 100% binding reference, while honey-alone and AgNO₃ controls were included to distinguish nanoparticle-mediated effects from honey or ionic silver alone.

Sortase A activity assay

Sortase A enzymatic activity was evaluated using a fluorogenic peptide substrate containing the LPXTG recognition motif. This approach was selected because Sortase A recognizes LPXTG-containing substrates and cleaves between threonine and glycine, and fluorogenic or FRET-based assays are commonly used for quantitative detection of Sortase A activity and inhibitor screening28–30.

Treated and untreated bacterial lysates were incubated with the fluorogenic LPXTG substrate under optimized assay conditions. AgNPs were tested at 0.5×MIC and 1×MIC, while honey alone, AgNO₃, and untreated lysates served as controls. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BioTek Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, United States) at the recommended excitation and emission wavelengths. Sortase A activity was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated control cultures. Decreased fluorescence intensity indicated inhibition of Sortase A-mediated substrate cleavage. All assays were performed in biological triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test.

In silico toxicity prediction platform

Toxicological profiling of quercetin was conducted using the ProTox-III web-based platform, an advanced in silico toxicity prediction tool that integrates machine learning models, molecular similarity analyses, and pharmacophore-based approaches. ProTox-III predicts a wide range of toxicity endpoints, including organ-specific toxicity, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, cytotoxicity, immunotoxicity, and interaction with nuclear receptors, stress response pathways, and metabolic enzymes.

Toxicity endpoint analysis

The toxicity prediction encompassed multiple biological endpoints relevant to drug safety and nanomedicine applications. These included hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, neurotoxicity, respiratory toxicity, blood–brain barrier permeability, and ecological toxicity. In addition, interactions with nuclear hormone receptors (e.g., estrogen receptor, androgen receptor, aryl hydrocarbon receptor, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma), stress response pathways (p53, NRF2/ARE, heat shock elements), and cytochrome P450 enzymes were evaluated. Predicted probabilities were reported as likelihood scores generated by the ProTox-III algorithm.

Network and radar visualization

ProTox-III outputs were visualized using two complementary graphical formats: a toxicity network map and a radar chart. The network map categorized predicted toxicity endpoints into active and inactive clusters, enabling qualitative assessment of quercetin’s toxicological interaction profile. The radar chart provided a quantitative comparison of predicted probabilities across all evaluated endpoints, facilitating identification of endpoints with relatively higher or lower predicted interaction likelihoods.

Toxicity data interpretation and limitations

Predicted toxicity outcomes were interpreted comparatively rather than as absolute measures of safety. Endpoints classified within inactive clusters or associated with low probability scores were considered indicative of reduced toxicological risk, whereas active endpoints were interpreted as areas of potential biological interaction rather than confirmed adverse effects. Given the inherent limitations of in silico prediction models particularly for complex biological systems and nanoparticle-associated compounds these results were used solely as supportive evidence to complement experimental antibacterial findings.

In vitro cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility assessment

The in vitro cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs were evaluated to assess their preliminary safety toward mammalian cells and human erythrocytes. HEK293 human embryonic kidney cells and HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells were used as representative non-target human cell models.

HEK293 and HepG2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified Thermo Scientific Heracell VIOS 160i CO₂ incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) containing 5% CO₂. Routine cell handling and AgNP exposure procedures were conducted in a Thermo Scientific 1300 Series A2 Class II biological safety cabinet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). For cytotoxicity analysis, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated for 24 h to allow cell attachment. The cells were then treated with honey-biosynthesized AgNPs at concentrations of 0, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 µg/mL for 24 h. Untreated cells served as the negative control.

Cell metabolic activity was determined using the MTT assay. Following AgNP exposure, MTT reagent was added to each well and incubated under standard culture conditions. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved using dimethyl sulfoxide, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, United States). Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to untreated control cells.

Membrane integrity was evaluated using the lactate dehydrogenase release assay. After 24 h of AgNP treatment, culture supernatants were collected and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Untreated cells were used as the spontaneous LDH-release control, while Triton X-100-treated cells served as the maximum LDH-release positive control. LDH release was quantified using a BioTek Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, United States) and expressed as a percentage of the maximum-release control.

Hemocompatibility was assessed using human erythrocytes obtained under the approved institutional procedure for erythrocyte-based compatibility testing. Erythrocytes were isolated by centrifugation using an Eppendorf 5424 R refrigerated microcentrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), washed repeatedly with phosphate-buffered saline, and resuspended to the desired concentration. The erythrocyte suspension was incubated with AgNPs at 0, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 µg/mL for 1 h at 37 °C in a Memmert IN55 microbiological incubator (Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany). Phosphate-buffered saline was used as the negative control, while Triton X-100 was used as the positive control representing 100% hemolysis. After incubation, samples were centrifuged using the same microcentrifuge, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a BioTek Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, United States). Percentage hemolysis was calculated relative to the negative and positive controls.

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Cell viability values of ≥ 80% and hemolysis values below 5% were considered indicative of acceptable preliminary in vitro biocompatibility at the tested concentrations. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by an appropriate post hoc test, with differences considered significant at p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

All wet-laboratory experiments were performed in biological triplicate, and data were reported as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated. The antibacterial, antibiofilm, nanoparticle characterization, mechanistic validation, cytotoxicity, LDH-release, and hemolysis outcomes reported in the Results were obtained from performed laboratory assays; computational docking and ProTox-III analyses were used only as supportive in silico analyses.

Zone-of-inhibition measurements, physicochemical characterization data, experimentally determined cell-viability results, LDH-release values, and hemolysis percentages were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple-comparison test when the assumptions for parametric analysis were satisfied. For datasets that did not meet parametric assumptions, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc multiple-comparison test was applied.

MIC and MBC values were treated as ordinal two-fold dilution endpoints and analyzed using appropriate non-parametric statistical tests. Time-kill assay data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with time and treatment or isolate group as the main factors, followed by Sidak’s multiple-comparison test. Stability-related outcomes measured at Day 0 and Day 30 were compared using paired t tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.

UV–visible spectroscopy

The optical characteristics of the honey-mediated reaction system were evaluated using UV–Vis spectroscopy over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm. As shown in Fig. 1A, the superimposed spectra recorded at 60, 120, and 180 min exhibited strong absorption in the UV region, followed by a sharp decline after approximately 300 nm and a low absorbance tail across the visible region. The inset highlights the 300–800 nm region, where slight differences among the spectra indicate time-dependent changes in the reaction medium during nanoparticle formation.

The control spectra confirmed the individual contributions of the precursor and reducing/stabilizing agent. AgNO₃ showed a strong absorption maximum at 206 nm with an absorbance of 10.000 (Fig. 1B), corresponding to the silver nitrate precursor signal. In contrast, pure honey displayed a characteristic absorption peak at 279 nm with an absorbance of 2.852 (Fig. 1C), which may be attributed to honey-derived organic compounds such as phenolics, flavonoids, sugars, and other reducing constituents.

Compared with the individual AgNO₃ and pure honey spectra, the time-dependent spectra in Fig. 1A indicate progressive spectral changes during the reaction, suggesting the interaction of silver ions with honey biomolecules and the subsequent formation/stabilization of silver-based nanoparticles.

The statistical comparison was performed to determine whether the minor spectral displacement represented a meaningful shift. The wavelength maxima obtained from replicate UV–Vis measurements were compared statistically. The surface plasmon resonance maximum changed only slightly from 430 nm to 433 nm, corresponding to a 3 nm red-shift. Statistical comparison of the two peak maxima showed that this difference was not significant (p > 0.05). Therefore, the observed 3 nm displacement was interpreted as a minor spectral variation rather than a statistically meaningful red-shift. This finding supports the colloidal stability of the honey-biosynthesized AgNPs and indicates that no substantial aggregation-related optical shift occurred during the observation period.

The polydispersity index (PDI) obtained from the DLS analysis was 0.24 ± 0.03, indicating a relatively narrow particle-size distribution and good colloidal homogeneity of the biosynthesized AgNP suspension. Stability assays

AgNPs preserved colloidal stability across a range of ionic strengths (0–300 mM NaCl) and environmental conditions. Partial aggregation was noted solely at NaCl values over 400 mM, suggesting appropriateness for biological applications (Table 2).

Stability was monitored by comparing Day 0 and Day 30 measurements under dark storage conditions. The UV-Vis SPR peak shifted from 430 to 433 nm, the normalized SPR absorbance decreased to 95% of baseline, the hydrodynamic diameter changed from 18 ± 3 nm to 20 ± 4 nm, and the zeta potential remained strongly negative at − 30.8 ± 2.4 mV after storage, indicating limited aggregation over the study period.

Commonly used colloidal-stability criteria classify ζ values of ± 0–10 mV as highly unstable, ± 10–20 mV as relatively stable, ± 20–30 mV as moderately stable, and values greater than ± 30 mV as highly stable. On this basis, the Day 0 ζ value of − 32.5 ± 2.1 mV and the Day 30 ζ value of − 30.8 ± 2.4 mV indicate that the honey-biosynthesized AgNP dispersion remained within the highly stable range [35,36].

Paired comparison of Day 0 and Day 30 stability parameters did not indicate a statistically significant loss of colloidal stability (p > 0.05). The small SPR shift, limited increase in hydrodynamic diameter, and preserved negative zeta potential support the physical stability of the AgNP suspension during the study period.

Antibacterial activity of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs

The antibacterial effectiveness of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs was assessed against four clinically significant Enterococcus strains, comprising vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis (VRE1, VRE2) and vancomycin-resistant E. faecium (VRE1, VRE2). The findings of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), and agar well diffusion zone of inhibition (ZOI) are presented in Table 4.

The zones of inhibition, assessed experimentally by the agar well diffusion approach at 64 µg/mL, corroborated these findings. AgNPs produced measured zones of inhibition of 18 ± 1.2 mm and 17 ± 1.0 mm against E. faecalis VRE1 and VRE2, respectively, while E. faecium strains showed slightly smaller but clear zones of 15 ± 1.1 mm and 14 ± 1.3 mm. Honey-alone controls showed negligible inhibition.

Honey-alone controls were reported for each VRE isolate in Table 4, including E. faecalis VRE2 and E. faecium VRE2, to maintain consistent control coverage across the AgNP-treated and honey-only groups.

Statistical analysis of zone-of-inhibition data showed a significant difference among AgNP-treated, honey-alone, vancomycin-positive-control, and untreated groups (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.05). Tukey’s post hoc analysis indicated that AgNP-treated isolates produced significantly larger inhibition zones than honey-alone and untreated controls (p < 0.05). The comparison also supported the observed strain-level trend, in which E. faecalis isolates showed larger inhibition zones than E. faecium isolates under equivalent AgNP exposure.

Time-kill kinetics of AgNPs

Time-kill experiments were conducted over 24 h using isolate-specific 1×MIC AgNP exposure: 16 µg/mL for E. faecalis isolates and 32 µg/mL for E. faecium isolates. Table 5 delineates viable-count changes at successive sampling intervals. The numerical outcomes in Table 5 represent viable bacterial counts expressed as log10 CFU/mL at each sampling time point.

AgNP treatment resulted in a swift and persistent decline in viable bacterial counts, with E. faecalis strains decreasing from 7.2 log CFU/mL to approximately 2.0 log CFU/mL after 24 h and E. faecium strains decreasing from 7.3 log CFU/mL to approximately 3.1 log CFU/mL. These experimentally measured reductions confirm time-dependent bactericidal activity under the tested conditions.

Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed a significant time-dependent reduction in viable counts for AgNP-treated VRE isolates compared with the untreated control across the 24 h exposure period (p < 0.05). Post hoc comparisons showed that the 24 h counts were significantly lower than baseline values for all AgNP-treated isolates (p < 0.05), confirming bactericidal activity over time.

Anti-biofilm activity of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs

The anti-biofilm activity of honey-biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) was evaluated against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium isolates using the crystal violet microtiter plate assay. The results showed that AgNPs produced a concentration-dependent reduction in biofilm biomass across all tested VRE isolates (Table 6).

At MIC concentrations, biofilm inhibition ranged from 61.4 ± 3.2% to 74.8 ± 2.9%. Increasing the treatment concentration to 2×MIC further enhanced biofilm inhibition, with values ranging from 76.3 ± 2.8% to 86.5 ± 2.1%. The highest anti-biofilm activity was observed against E. faecalis VRE2, with 74.8 ± 2.9% inhibition at MIC and 86.5 ± 2.1% inhibition at 2×MIC. In comparison, E. faecium isolates showed slightly lower inhibition, with maximum inhibition values of 76.3 ± 2.8% and 78.9 ± 2.6% at 2×MIC for VRE1 and VRE2, respectively.

Honey alone showed minimal anti-biofilm activity, with less than 15% inhibition across all tested isolates. This finding indicates that the observed biofilm inhibition was mainly associated with the AgNP formulation rather than honey alone. Untreated controls demonstrated dense and stable biofilm formation, confirming the intrinsic biofilm-forming capacity of the tested VRE strains.

Statistical analysis showed significant differences between AgNP-treated groups and untreated controls across all tested concentrations (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.05). In addition, E. faecalis isolates showed significantly greater susceptibility to AgNP-mediated biofilm inhibition than E. faecium isolates (p < 0.05). This pattern is consistent with the MIC and time-kill findings, where E. faecalis also showed higher sensitivity to honey-biosynthesized AgNPs.

Comparative molecular docking analysis

The molecular docking analysis demonstrated distinct binding characteristics of the ligand (Fig. 8) towards Sortase A (PDB: 2KID) (Fig. 9) and the penicillin-binding protein (PBP; PDB: 1MWS), indicating a greater anticipated affinity for PBP (Fig. 10). The ligand for Sortase A demonstrated binding energies between − 7.4 and − 6.5 kcal/mol across nine produced poses, with the highest-ranked conformation (Mode 0) showing a binding affinity of − 7.4 kcal/mol and an RMSD of 0.0 Å, signifying a stable and well-defined binding orientation. Conversely, docking with PBP yielded more advantageous binding energies, spanning from − 8.9 to − 8.4 kcal/mol, with the two highest-ranked poses (Modes 0 and 1) (Table 7) attaining a minimum binding affinity of − 8.9 kcal/mol. This evident energetic differentiation indicates a greater thermodynamic affinity of the ligand for the PBP active site in comparison to Sortase A.

A meticulous examination of the binding conformations corroborates these findings. For Sortase A, many conformations with moderate RMSD values (Modes 2, 3, 4, and 6; RMSD ≤ 5.1 Å) suggest stable ligand binding within the enzyme’s catalytic pocket. Figure 9 of the 3D interaction diagram demonstrates that the ligand is situated in the active-site groove, establishing stabilising hydrogen bonds and polar interactions with critical residues implicated in substrate recognition. The complementary 2D interaction study (Fig. 9) reveals several hydrogen bond interactions facilitated by the hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups of the ligand, in addition to hydrophobic contacts that enhance overall binding stability.

In silico toxicity profiling of honey-derived quercetin

In silico toxicity profiling of quercetin derived from honey was performed using the ProTox-III platform to predict potential organ toxicity, receptor interactions, and stress response pathways. The analysis revealed a clear distinction between predicted active and inactive toxicological endpoints, visualized through both network and radar representations.

The toxicity network analysis demonstrated that quercetin clustered predominantly within the inactive cluster, encompassing multiple toxicity endpoints including carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, cytotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, clinical toxicity, and nuclear receptor–mediated adverse effects (Fig. 11). These inactive associations suggest a low likelihood of quercetin eliciting broad systemic or chronic toxic responses. In contrast, a smaller active cluster was observed, primarily involving estrogen receptor–related pathways (ER and ER-LBD), aromatase interaction, and selected immunological and respiratory endpoints. This pattern indicates selective bioactivity rather than nonspecific toxicity, consistent with quercetin’s known phytoestrogenic and signaling-modulatory properties.

The radar chart analysis further corroborated these findings by quantitatively comparing predicted probabilities across a wide panel of toxicity and molecular interaction endpoints. Quercetin exhibited low to moderate predicted probabilities for most adverse toxicological categories, including hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, blood–brain barrier penetration, and cytotoxicity (Fig. 12). Notably, higher interaction probabilities were confined to hormone-related targets, particularly estrogen receptor subtypes and aromatase, while stress response pathways such as p53, NRF2/ARE, and heat shock elements remained weakly activated.

In vitro cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility results

The cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility of honey-biosynthesized AgNPs were evaluated in HEK293 and HepG2 cells, as well as in human erythrocytes. At concentrations corresponding to the antibacterial MIC range of 16–32 µg/mL, cell viability remained above 87% in HEK293 cells and above 81% in HepG2 cells. LDH release at these concentrations was modest, indicating limited membrane damage. Hemolysis also remained below 3%, suggesting good erythrocyte compatibility within the antibacterial concentration range. The complete concentration-response profile is presented in Table 10.

At higher AgNP concentrations of 64–128 µg/mL, cytotoxic effects became more pronounced, particularly in HepG2 cells, as shown by reduced cell viability and increased LDH release. Hemolysis also increased at 128 µg/mL, exceeding the commonly accepted 5% threshold. The half-maximal cytotoxic concentration was greater than 128 µg/mL for HEK293 cells and approximately 105–115 µg/mL for HepG2 cells.

Discussion

The emergence and worldwide spread of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium pose a significant challenge to modern antimicrobial therapy, especially in hospital settings where these pathogens lead to ongoing bloodstream, urinary tract, and wound infections. In this context, the current study presents robust evidence that honey-biosynthesized AgNPs exhibit significant and diverse antibacterial efficacy against VRE isolates.

The differential susceptibility noted between E. faecalis and E. faecium aligns with recognized biological differences between these species. Enterococcus faecium is well acknowledged for its heightened drug resistance and superior ability to endure antimicrobial stress, attributed to a combination of modified cell wall structure, diminished membrane permeability, and improved stress-response mechanisms37. The elevated MICs and reduced zones of inhibition observed for E. faecium in this investigation likely indicate these inherent defenses. Nonetheless, the achievement of bactericidal doses underscores the extensive and robust characteristics of AgNP-mediated antimicrobial efficacy.

The agar well diffusion results further corroborate the importance of these discoveries. The zones of inhibition nearing those of vancomycin-sensitive control isolates treated with vancomycin indicate that, at sufficient doses, AgNPs can provide antibacterial effects comparable to those of primary antibiotics. This observation is significant because honey alone had minimal activity, demonstrating that the antimicrobial effects are not due to honey’s osmotic or peroxide-mediated capabilities, but rather originate from the nanosilver formulation itself. Consequently, honey serves mainly as a reducing and capping agent that promotes nanoparticle formation and stability, rather than as a confounding antibacterial component.

Time-kill kinetics provide a more profound understanding of the bactericidal mechanisms of AgNPs. The swift and persistent decline in viable counts, particularly for E. faecalis, implies that these nanoparticles not only inhibit bacterial proliferation but also actively promote cell death. Reductions above 5 log CFU/mL within 24 h satisfy traditional criteria for bactericidal efficacy and are comparable to findings of AgNP-mediated lethality in other multidrug-resistant Gram-positive bacteria38–40. The prolonged lethality noted for E. faecium corresponds with its elevated MIC values. It reinforces the idea that AgNP effectiveness is influenced by species-specific structural and physiological characteristics rather than being uniformly applicable to all enterococci.

The physicochemical characteristics of the honey-biosynthesized AgNPs offer a credible rationale for their robust antibacterial efficacy. The nanoparticles had a narrow and uniform size distribution (15–20 nm), a distinct and sustained surface plasmon resonance peak, and a significantly negative zeta potential. These qualities collectively increase interactions between nanoparticles and bacteria. Smaller AgNPs exhibit a greater surface-area-to-volume ratio, enhancing their ability to attach to bacterial cell walls, traverse membrane barriers, and liberate biologically active silver ions (Ag⁺)41. The pronounced negative surface charge enhances colloidal stability and promotes electrostatic interactions with positively charged areas of bacterial membranes.

This interpretation agrees with recent evidence showing that AgNPs can act through multi-target antibacterial mechanisms, including membrane damage, reactive oxygen species generation, DNA and protein interference, and measurable changes in bacterial protein-expression profiles15,16.

This work’s significant addition is the combination of molecular docking analysis with empirical antibacterial data. The docking results demonstrate that quercetin, recognized as a significant honey-derived ligand, shows advantageous binding affinities for penicillin-binding protein (PBP) and Sortase A, two enzymes crucial to enterococcal survival and pathogenicity. Significantly, the affinity for PBP was energetically superior to that for Sortase A, a difference that reflects the pronounced bactericidal efficacy seen in vitro. Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) are crucial for peptidoglycan formation and maintaining cell wall integrity; their inhibition results in severe structural collapse and bacterial mortality42. The anticipated deep penetration of quercetin into the PBP active site, reinforced by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, elucidates the molecular rationale for the recorded MIC and time-kill outcomes43.

The interaction with Sortase A, albeit relatively weaker, is nevertheless biologically significant. Sortase A facilitates the attachment of surface proteins that play a role in adhesion, immunological evasion, and biofilm development44,45. Partial inhibition of this enzyme may not instantly eradicate bacterial cells, but it can diminish pathogenicity and render bacteria more susceptible to supplementary stressors, like oxidative damage caused by AgNPs. This dual-targeting direct interference with cell wall production, coupled with the disruption of virulence factor anchoring, provides a significant mechanistic insight that differentiates this study from previous publications that concentrated exclusively on nonspecific membrane damage or reactive oxygen species formation.

Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus remains a difficult clinical problem because it combines multidrug resistance, environmental persistence, biofilm formation, and limited therapeutic options. In this study, honey-biosynthesized silver nanoparticles showed antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis and E. faecium, with stronger activity against E. faecalis. This species-level difference is biologically plausible, given the greater intrinsic resistance, stress tolerance, and adaptive capacity commonly associated with hospital-adapted E. faecium lineages8.

The data support a multi-target antibacterial mechanism rather than a single-pathway effect. Silver nanoparticles are known to act through complementary mechanisms, including disruption of bacterial envelopes, oxidative stress, interference with proteins and DNA, and impairment of essential metabolic processes15,46. The observed bactericidal and antibiofilm effects are therefore most consistently explained by combined physicochemical injury and target-associated cellular stress, rather than by honey alone or by docking-predicted binding events in isolation.

The mechanistic validation assays strengthen this interpretation by moving the proposed mechanism beyond molecular docking. Reduced pbp5 expression and diminished PBP5 labelling would be consistent with interference in cell-wall biosynthesis, a central vulnerability in enterococci. PBP5 is a clinically important penicillin-binding protein in E. faecium; resistant PBP5 variants contribute to β-lactam resistance through altered protein dynamics and reduced β-lactam acylation, and Bocillin-FL binding has been used to quantify PBP5 active-site labelling26.

The reduction in srtA expression and Sortase A activity also provides a credible explanation for the antibiofilm phenotype. Sortase-dependent proteins contribute to adhesion, pilus assembly, persistence, immune evasion, and biofilm formation in Gram-positive bacteria. Recent work in E. faecalis showed that srtA and srtC deletion mutants had impaired biofilm formation and reduced persistence compared with wild-type strains, supporting the biological relevance of sortase-mediated surface-protein anchoring47.

The clinical and translational significance of these discoveries is considerable. VRE infections correlate with significant morbidity, extended hospitalizations, and elevated fatality rates, especially in immunocompromised individuals. Therapeutic alternatives are constrained and frequently depend on last-resort drugs like linezolid or daptomycin, which are also encountering rising resistance. The evidence that honey-biosynthesized AgNPs may efficiently eradicate VRE at low concentrations indicates that these nanomaterials may function as alternative or supplementary antibacterial agents. Possible applications encompass topical preparations, coatings for medical equipment, or combination medicines aimed at augmenting the effectiveness of current antibiotics.

Importantly, the present study also demonstrated that honey-biosynthesized AgNPs exert marked anti-biofilm activity against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus isolates. This finding is clinically relevant because Enterococcus species and other multidrug-resistant pathogens can persist within biofilms on host tissues, catheters, implants, and other medical devices, where the extracellular polymeric matrix and altered bacterial physiology reduce antimicrobial penetration and promote treatment tolerance48,49. The concentration-dependent reduction in biofilm biomass at both MIC and 2×MIC indicates that AgNPs may interfere with key stages of biofilm development, including initial bacterial adhesion, extracellular polymeric substance production, microcolony formation, and biofilm maturation. Recent studies have shown that AgNPs can inhibit biofilm formation by disrupting bacterial adhesion, damaging cell envelopes, inducing oxidative stress, impairing protein function, and weakening matrix-mediated protection15,50. The stronger inhibition observed against E. faecalis compared with E. faecium further supports the species-specific susceptibility pattern observed in the MIC and time-kill assays. Since honey alone produced only minimal biofilm inhibition, the anti-biofilm effect appears to be mainly associated with the nanoparticle formulation rather than the intrinsic activity of honey. These findings are consistent with recent reports showing that silver-based nanotherapeutics can reduce drug-resistant E. faecium biofilms and that AgNP-containing formulations exhibit antibiofilm activity against E. faecalis biofilms51,52.

The experimentally determined mammalian-cell cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility data provide an important biological safety context for the antibacterial activity of the honey-biosynthesized AgNPs. At concentrations corresponding to the antibacterial MIC range of 16–32 µg/mL, HEK293 and HepG2 cell viability remained above 80%, with only modest LDH release and hemolysis below 3%. These findings suggest that the AgNPs exert antibacterial effects within a concentration range that is comparatively well tolerated by the tested mammalian cells and human erythrocytes. This interpretation is consistent with ISO 10993-5 guidance, which uses mammalian-cell responses to assess in vitro cytotoxicity, and with established nanoparticle hemolysis interpretation criteria in which 2–5% hemolysis is considered slightly hemolytic and values above 5% are considered hemolytic53.

The concentration-dependent decline in viability and increase in LDH release at 64–128 µg/mL indicate that the safety margin of these AgNPs is dose limited. This pattern agrees with the broader nanotoxicology literature showing that AgNP toxicity is strongly influenced by concentration, particle size, surface chemistry, ion release, oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis54,55. The greater sensitivity of HepG2 cells compared with HEK293 cells is also biologically plausible, given the central role of hepatic cells in xenobiotic handling and nanoparticle-associated oxidative injury, although this should be interpreted as an in vitro signal rather than evidence of organ-level hepatotoxicity.

The low hemolysis observed at MIC-level concentrations is encouraging for preliminary biocompatibility, particularly for topical or localized antimicrobial applications where systemic exposure may be limited. However, AgNPs have been reported to interact with erythrocytes, plasma proteins, platelets, and immune cells in a manner dependent on coating, size, and exposure conditions; therefore, hemocompatibility should not be inferred from hemolysis alone56,57. Recent studies of green-synthesized AgNPs, including honey-mediated and biomolecule-stabilized formulations, support the potential of biologically capped AgNPs as antimicrobial materials, but also reinforce the need for rigorous safety profiling before biomedical translation58,59.

Molecular docking was conducted as a complementary, hypothesis-generating approach to explore potential interactions between honey-derived bioactive compounds associated with nanoparticle capping and key Enterococcus proteins involved in cell wall synthesis and virulence. Given the complex physicochemical nature of AgNPs, docking focused on representative honey-derived ligands rather than the nanoparticle core itself.

The docking analysis was not intended to provide definitive mechanistic proof but rather to offer supportive molecular-level insights that align with observed antibacterial activity. The limitations of docking, particularly in modeling metal nanoparticles and dynamic biological systems, are acknowledged. Accordingly, the antimicrobial efficacy reported in this study is primarily supported by experimental microbiological assays, with docking results serving as supplementary evidence.

The present study intentionally avoids overstating novelty in the green synthesis step. Honey-assisted reduction and capping of AgNPs are established in the literature, and silver-based antimicrobial effects against resistant bacteria have been reported previously. The contribution of this work is therefore defined as application-specific and evidence-integrative: it evaluates honey-biosynthesized AgNPs against clinically relevant VRE isolates using a coordinated set of physicochemical, microbiological, antibiofilm, mechanistic, and preliminary safety assays. In particular, the study combines strain-level honey-alone controls with MIC/MBC, disc-diffusion, and time-kill data; anti-biofilm measurements; stability and surface-characterization outcomes; docking-based target hypotheses; RT-qPCR analysis of pbp5 and srtA; PBP5 functional binding; Sortase A activity; and cytotoxicity/hemocompatibility testing. This combination clarifies that the manuscript’s novelty lies in the VRE-specific translational evaluation and multi-layered mechanism-linked evidence, not in the assertion of a previously unknown synthesis route or a general antibacterial property of silver.

Conclusion

Honey-biosynthesized AgNPs demonstrated reproducible, experimentally measured antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium. The performed assays showed bactericidal activity, reduced biofilm biomass, downregulation of pbp5 and srtA, decreased PBP5 Bocillin-FL labelling, reduced Sortase A activity, and acceptable preliminary in vitro tolerance at antibacterial concentration ranges. These findings support honey-biosynthesized AgNPs as a promising sustainable antimicrobial platform for further preclinical evaluation against VRE.
