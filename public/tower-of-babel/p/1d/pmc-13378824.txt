Human fallopian tube organoids: Bridging fertility research, education, and health policy
Authors: Nandini Hadge, Nancy Nair, Neel Dubey, Trupti Balwir
Published: 2026

To The Editor,

Sir,

Through this letter, I would like to highlight the emerging significance of human fallopian tube organoids (HFTOs) as innovative dual-use tools in fertility science. While organoid technology has found increasing application in cancer biology and gastrointestinal research, its application to reproductive health, particularly the fallopian tubes, offers new opportunities for advancing infertility research, medical education, and health policy.

Tubal infertility contributes to the most common causes of female reproductive disorders, and it is responsible for causing a vast percentage of infertility globally. Despite the developments of assisted reproductive technologies (ART), conditions like infections, inflammation, endometriosis, and congenital anomalies of the fallopian tube are of significant concern even now.[1] Traditional models, like two-dimensional (2D) cell culture and animal models, although helpful, are of no great value when it comes to duplicating the intricacy of human tubal physiology. Recent developments of the three-dimensional (3D) organoid culture system; however, have made it possible to create HFTOs, which simulate the natural fallopian tube epithelium effectively, possessing both the structural and functional characteristics, like hormonal responsiveness, and cellular heterogeneity.[2]

HFTOs have provided new frontiers in reproductive biology and translational medicine. They allow the creation of platforms to model tubal pathogens like Chlamydia trachomatis and decipher the cellular and molecular processes of infertility.[3] In addition, their use in embryo–tubal co-culture systems hold the potential to refine embryo selection and implantation outcomes during in vitro fertilization (IVF). In reproductive toxicology, they also show great potential by providing models through which to screen drugs, endocrine disruptors, and environmental exposures, thus raising patient safety. Significantly, patient-derived HFTOs can mimic individual hormonal or genetic profiles, paving the way to developing personalized fertility therapies, and precision reproductive medicine.[4]

In addition to research, HFTOs are an innovative educational resource. In contrast to the fixed histological slide or animal-based specimens, they offer a dynamic, reproducible human tubal physiology model. Through their inclusion in curricula, students and embryologists can study in real-time, processes like the action of cilia, infection, and inflammation.[5] They integrate basic science and clinical knowledge, enhancing the ability to diagnose, and introduce learners to advanced biotechnologies. Feedback from researchers and educators indicates they present an ethically and financially favorable alternative to animal or cadaver models, consistent with international developments towards humane research and educational practice. This aligns with broader initiatives in health professions education that emphasize integrating innovative models into teaching and learning environments.[6]

The application of HFTOs goes beyond the laboratory and the classroom and maps directly to health policy targets. They facilitate ART laboratory quality assurance through the provision of standardized, reproducible models, which may guide embryo culture optimization and enhance IVF outcomes. From a public health standpoint, their ease of access and inexpensiveness render them scalable educational resources, especially in resource-poor areas in which cadaveric or animal models are unavailable. In addition, the use of HFTOs in reproductive health research and education aligns with the World Health Organization’s Reproductive Health Strategy, which highlights innovation, equity, and capacity building in the care of infertility. Like recent perspectives that emphasize the role of technology-driven models for healthcare improvement,[7] HFTOs represent a bridge between science and health policy.

In conclusion, HFTOs are more than just innovations in the laboratory; they represent the convergence of science, education, and health policy. Integrating HFTOs into medical education, clinical ART practice, and health policy discourse can be translated from bench to bedside and from classroom to clinic, and thus be effectively implemented and utilized. Adopting these technologies not only advances reproductive biology but also enhances health systems, fosters professional capacity building, and promotes equitable access to fertility care. In these multi-dimensional ways, the impacts of HFTOs become transformational and can help to define the future of the management of infertility and reproductive health education.
