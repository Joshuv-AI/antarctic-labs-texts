Original Article--Intermittent Fasting in Chronic Heart Failure: A Systematic Review of Safety and Physiological Outcomes
Authors: Zulrahman Erlangga, Prenali D. Sattwika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesInterest in intermittent fasting regimens has expanded substantially due to its reported metabolic effects. However, whether such dietary patterns are safe for individuals living with chronic heart failure remains unclear. This issue is particularly relevant for patients who fast for religious observance, including Ramadan. We therefore performed a systematic review to evaluate the clinical safety and physiological implications of intermittent fasting regimens in stable heart failure populations.MethodsThe review protocol adhered to established reporting standards for systematic reviews. Multiple electronic databases were systematically searched from inception through December 2025 to identify studies evaluating intermittent fasting regimens in adults with chronic heart failure. Both randomized and observational designs were considered eligible. Key outcomes included clinical deterioration, cardiovascular events, New York Heart Association functional status, left ventricular systolic performance, and natriuretic peptide levels. Where data allowed, pooled analyses were conducted; otherwise, findings were summarized narratively.ResultsFive observational studies fulfilled inclusion criteria, three of which provided comparable data for quantitative synthesis, comprising 1345 participants. Across studies, fasting was not associated with increased rates of heart failure decompensation, major cardiovascular events, or mortality relative to non-fasting periods. Functional status remained generally stable, with a tendency toward improved symptom classification among fasting participants. Measures of systolic function and natriuretic peptides showed no clinically meaningful worsening.ConclusionIn clinically stable individuals with chronic heart failure, intermittent fasting regimens, including Ramadan fasting, were not linked to short-term adverse clinical outcomes based on currently available observational evidence.

Introduction

Heart failure represents a major and growing clinical challenge worldwide and is associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burden, within the broader context of cardiovascular disease affecting hundreds of millions of individuals globally, including over 300 million cases of ischemic heart disease and more than 9 million related deaths annually [1–4]. Although contemporary pharmacological and device- based therapies have improved outcomes, the overall prevalence of HF continues to rise, largely reflecting population aging and the increasing burden of cardiometabolic disease.

Patients are clinically stratified according to left ventricular systolic performance into reduced, mildly reduced, and preserved ejection fraction phenotypes. Symptom burden and functional limitation are commonly assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, which remains central to risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making [5]. Beyond impaired pump function, HF is increasingly recognized as a systemic syndrome characterized by metabolic dysregulation, insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation [6].

Intermittent fasting (IF) regimens have emerged as a lifestyle intervention with potential cardiometabolic effects. Experimental and clinical studies suggest that structured fasting regimens may improve metabolic flexibility, attenuate oxidative stress, modulate inflammatory signaling, and activate adaptive cellular stress pathways [7,8]. Recent clinical data have further indicated potential improvements in cardiac function associated with intermittent fasting [9]. However, these findings have largely been derived from populations without established HF, and their applicability to patients with chronic HF remains uncertain.

Intermittent fasting encompasses several distinct dietary patterns, including alternate-day fasting (complete or modified fasting days alternating with ad libitum intake), modified fasting regimens (e.g., the 5:2 diet, involving severe energy restriction on selected days), and time-restricted feeding (daily eating within a defined time window with prolonged fasting intervals) [10]. Fasting is also widely practiced for religious observance, particularly during Ramadan, when individuals abstain from food, drink, and smoking from dawn to sunset [11]. This form differs from most non-religious IF regimens due to the absence of hydration during fasting hours and its fixed diurnal pattern, potentially leading to distinct physiological effects. Despite variability in fasting protocols, beneficial metabolic effects have been reported across different strategies [12].

For patients with chronic HF, fasting presents a clinically relevant dilemma. Potential metabolic and anti-inflammatory benefits may theoretically support cardiovascular health. Concerns exist regarding hypoglycemia, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and hemodynamic instability, particularly in patients receiving complex pharmacological therapies such as diuretics, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. Clinicians are frequently asked to advise patients on the safety of fasting during Ramadan, yet evidence-based guidance remains limited.

Previous studies have evaluated Ramadan fasting in cardiovascular populations and generally suggest that fasting is well tolerated in stable cardiac patients [13,14]. However, these studies are heterogeneous, largely observational, and often include mixed cardiovascular populations without HF–specific outcome assessment. Consequently, the safety and physiological effects of intermittent fasting in patients with chronic HF remain incompletely defined, highlighting the need for a focused synthesis of the available evidence to better inform clinical decision-making in this population.

In this systematic review, we aim to evaluate the clinical safety and physiological effects of IF regimens, including Ramadan fasting, in adults with chronic HF, with a focus on clinical outcomes, functional status, and cardiac function.

Methods

2.1. Literature search

The review protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database prior to study initiation and was performed in line with established methodological standards for systematic reviews.

A structured electronic search was undertaken across major biomedical databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, and CENTRAL, from database inception through 9 December 2025. The objective was to identify studies evaluating IF regimens in adult populations with chronic heart failure. No language restrictions were imposed during the search process.

The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to heart failure (“heart failure,” “cardiac failure,” “HF*,” “CHF,” “cardiomyopathy,” “chronic heart disease,” “systolic dysfunction,” “left ventricular dysfunction,” as well as relevant MeSH terms) with terms related to intermittent fasting (“intermittent fasting,” “intermittent energy restriction,” “Islamic fasting,” “Ramadan fasting,” “religious fasting,” “time-restricted fasting,” “time-restricted feeding,” “alternate-day fasting,” “periodic fasting,” “meal skipping,” and related MeSH terms).

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH and Emtree terms) and free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND/OR). Search terms were adapted for each database to maximize sensitivity and specificity. The complete search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

All identified citations were exported to a reference management platform for de-duplication and screening. Title and abstract screening were conducted independently by two investigators (ZE and PDS), followed by full-text evaluation of potentially eligible studies (ZE and PDS). Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion among the review team.

2.2. Study selection

Study identification and selection followed a structured, multistage review process. After removal of duplicate citations, all remaining records underwent initial screening based on title and abstract to determine potential relevance to the research question. Publications clearly unrelated to chronic HF, conference abstracts without full reports, and case series were excluded at this stage.

Full manuscripts of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed by ZE and PDS in detail. Articles were excluded if HF–specific data could not be separated from mixed cardiovascular populations or if relevant outcomes were not reported for the predefined study population, even after attempts to obtain clarification from study authors. Studies focusing exclusively on unrelated metabolic or lipid parameters without clinical HF endpoints were also excluded.

Eligibility was determined according to pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria applied at the full-text stage. Differences in study assessment between reviewers were resolved through discussion until agreement was reached. The overall selection process, including reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage, is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram.

2.3. Data collection and extraction

Data extraction was performed using a structured framework developed specifically for this review. ZE and PDS of the research team independently reviewed each eligible study and recorded relevant information in a standardized database. In cases of disagreement, discrepancies were clarified through joint re-evaluation of the source article until agreement was achieved.

The extracted variables comprised bibliographic details (author and publication year), study design, geographic setting, sample size, baseline patient characteristics, and heart failure phenotype classification (HFrEF, HFmrEF, HFpEF). Information regarding fasting exposure, comparator groups, and duration of follow-up was also documented.

Clinical endpoints of interest included HF–related deterioration, hospital admission, acute decompensation, and major adverse cardiovascular events. In addition, measures of cardiac function such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), B-Type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels, and other relevant biochemical markers were collected. Functional status was captured using reported New York Heart Association classifications (NYHA).

2.4. Quality assessment and risk of bias

The internal validity of eligible observational studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. ZE and PDS independently performed the appraisal, focusing on key aspects of study design, group comparability, and outcome ascertainment.

For cohort designs, methodological rigor was examined with respect to participant selection, exposure definition, control group adequacy, adjustment for confounding, outcome assessment procedures, and follow-up integrity. Rather than detailing each checklist item individually, overall study quality was interpreted in the context of these domains.

Each study received a summary score reflecting methodological robustness. Based on cumulative scoring, studies were categorized into lower, intermediate, or higher risk of bias. Differences in scoring were resolved through joint reassessment and agreement between reviewers.

2.5. Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Given the small number of eligible studies and variability in reported endpoints, several outcomes were summarized descriptively rather than quantitatively pooled. These included HF–related deterioration, hospitalization, mortality, and broader cardiovascular events. For these endpoints, findings were presented narratively, with no formal meta-analytic aggregation undertaken.

Quantitative synthesis was feasible for NYHA functional status and LVEF, where sufficiently comparable data were available. For analytical consistency and improved interpretability, NYHA categories were consolidated into two clinically relevant groups: lower symptom burden (classes I–II) and higher symptom burden (classes III–IV), in line with established HF reporting practices [15]. Effect estimates were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) comparing fasting and non-fasting groups at follow-up.

When at least two studies provided compatible outcome data, pooled estimates were generated using inverse-variance methods within a random-effects framework to account for expected clinical and methodological variability, including differences in heart failure phenotype and duration of follow-up. Given the limited evidence base, pooled findings were interpreted cautiously.

For LVEF analyses, overall summary measures were derived when necessary by combining subgroup statistics using weighted calculations incorporating within-group variability and differences in subgroup means.

Between-study inconsistency was examined using both a chi-square–based heterogeneity test and the I2 statistic, with a threshold of 0.10 applied for statistical significance of heterogeneity. Funnel plot assessment was not performed due to the small number of included studies. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2) using the meta package.

Results

3.1. Study selection

The literature search initially yielded 2904 articles. After removing 778 duplicates, 2126 records remained for screening. Based on title and abstract assessment, 2112 records were excluded as they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. Of the remaining records, three were excluded because full texts were unavailable, leaving 11 articles for full-text review.

During full-text assessment, one study was excluded because it was a case series, and five studies were excluded due to mixed patient populations. Although the corresponding authors were contacted, these studies were ultimately excluded due to insufficient data for extraction. A detailed list of excluded studies is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

In total, five studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review. Of these, three studies provided patient-level comparisons between fasting and non-fasting heart failure HF populations and were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis of outcomes [16–18]. The remaining two studies were included to provide contextual observational evidence only and were not used for outcome synthesis due to the absence of individual-level fasting status and HF–specific outcome attribution [19,20]. The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1 (PRISMA flow diagram).

3.2. Study characteristics

Among the 5 studies included in this systematic review, 2 studies were included for background purposes only [19,20]. These comprised a prospective multicenter observational registry study and a retrospective registry-based analysis. The remaining 3 studies were prospective, cross-sectional observational studies [16–18].

In total, 1345 patients were included in the quantitative analysis, with 773 patients in the intervention group (IF regimens) and 572 patients in the control group. In addition, the 2 background studies comprised a total of 25,013 records [19,20].

Detailed characteristics of each included study are summarized in Table 1.

3.2.1. Quality of included studies

Risk of bias assessment using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale showed that 1 study was of poor quality, 3/9 [2,17] studies were of good quality, 7/9 [18] and 9/9 [16] (Fig. 2). Only studies included in the quantitative synthesis were assessed for risk of bias.

3.3. Clinical events (HF decompensation, hospitalization, mortality, and cardiovascular events)

Alaarag et al. (2025) [16] reported no significant differences in major cardiac events associated with fasting, including acute coronary syndrome (ACS) (3.0% vs. 2.9%), stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA) (3.3% vs. 1.5%), and sudden cardiac death (3.3% vs. 2.9%). Among 90 patients who observed fasting for one month, 8 patients (8.8%) developed acute heart failure, compared with 5 of 68 patients (7.4%) in the non-fasting group.

In another study, Salam et al. (2018) [19] analyzed hospitalizations for acute HF during Ramadan and non-Ramadan periods, assuming that patients in Gulf countries were predominantly fasting during Ramadan. The authors found no significant difference in hospitalization rates between Ramadan and non-Ramadan periods (171 vs. 242 hospitalizations, respectively).

Similarly, Suwadi et al. (2004) [20] evaluated 20,856 patients over a 10-year period and reported no significant difference in hospitalization rates for chronic HF during Ramadan compared with non-fasting periods.

3.4. NYHA functional class

Both Abazid et al. and Alam et al. demonstrated a shift toward improvement in subjective functional status, as reflected by changes in NYHA functional class following IF regimens. In the study by Abazid et al. (2018) [18], the proportion of patients classified as NYHA class I increased from 24.7% before intervention to 55.5% after intervention. Similarly, Alam et al. (2022) [17] reported an increase in the proportion of patients in NYHA class I from 35.57% to 43.64% following the intervention. In both studies, this improvement was accompanied by a reduction in the proportion of patients classified as NYHA class IV.

Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of NYHA functional classes for each study before and after the intervention.

In the dichotomous analysis, IF regimens were associated with improvement in NYHA functional class in both the Abazid and Alam studies, ORs of 0.06 and 0.71, respectively [17,18].

In the meta-analysis, both studies showed a directionally consistent association favoring IF regimens with respect to lower odds of severe symptoms (NYHA III–IV); however, effect sizes varied markedly between studies. The pooled analysis demonstrated a directionally favorable association between IF regimens and improvement in NYHA functional class (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.02–2.44); however, the estimate was imprecise and associated with substantial heterogeneity (I2= 95%), limiting the interpretability of the pooled effect (Fig. 4).

Echocardiographic outcomes

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was reported in 2 studies. In Alam et al. (2022) [17], mean LVEF remained within the preserved range at both baseline and follow-up in the fasting and non-fasting groups, with no clinically meaningful change observed. Accordingly, no significant difference in LVEF was detected between the intermittent fasting IF regimens and non-fasting groups (55% ± 4% vs. 53% ± 5%).

In Alaarag et al. (2025) [16], which included patients with reduced ejection fraction, mean LVEF values were comparable between fasting and non-fasting groups at baseline and follow-up, including analyses stratified by the occurrence of adverse events.

Across studies, pooled analysis showed no significant difference in LVEF between fasting and non-fasting groups (random-effects model mean difference −1.60, 95% CI −4.53 to 1.34), with substantial heterogeneity (I2= 95%) (Fig. 5).

4.1. B-Type natriuretic peptide

Similarly, BNP levels did not differ significantly between groups, with mean values of 139 ± 61 in the fasting group and 110 ± 42 in the non-fasting group [17].

Discussion

5.1. Principal findings

In this systematic review, the available evidence suggests that IF regimens, including Ramadan fasting, are not associated with clear short-term harm in selected, clinically stable adults with chronic HF. Across the included studies, fasting did not appear to precipitate worsening HF status, deterioration in cardiac function, or a consistent increase in major adverse cardiovascular events when compared with non-fasting periods. These findings are clinically relevant given concerns that prolonged fasting may exacerbate metabolic or hemodynamic vulnerability in HF. The absence of adverse signals suggests that IF regimens may be tolerated in clinically stable patients, including those who fast for religious or cultural reasons. Measures of cardiac function remained stable, supporting the absence of measurable short-term cardiovascular deterioration. Potential risks, including dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and impaired medication tolerance, are theoretically plausible, particularly in patients receiving diuretics or neurohormonal therapies. However, these risks were not reflected in consistent adverse clinical or biomarker signals in the available evidence, likely due to the inclusion of predominantly stable, ambulatory patients.

5.2. Functional status and symptom burden

Across the included studies, changes in NYHA functional class did not indicate clinically meaningful deterioration associated with intermittent or Ramadan fasting. No consistent shift toward more severe NYHA classes was observed.

Beyond the absence of harm, there was a tendency toward improvement in NYHA class among fasting patients, reflected by a shift in the distribution toward milder functional classes. Although NYHA classification is inherently subjective, this pattern was directionally consistent across studies and supported by dichotomous analyses, in which odds ratios favored IF regimens. Overall, the pooled estimate suggested a possible association between IF regimens and improvement in NYHA functional class; however, substantial heterogeneity and wide confidence intervals preclude definitive conclusions.

Notably, the magnitude of effect differed between studies. The improvement observed in the study by Abazid et al. was more pronounced, whereas the effect reported by Alam et al. was more modest. This discrepancy may be explained, at least in part, by differences in HF phenotype. Abazid et al. pre-dominantly included patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), in whom symptoms are often highly sensitive to short-term changes in preload and congestion, such that modest volume shifts during fasting may translate into measurable improvements in functional class [21]. In contrast, Alam et al. focused on patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a population in which functional limitation is largely driven by impaired diastolic reserve, ventricular-vascular stiffness, and chronotropic incompetence-mechanisms that are less likely to exhibit rapid improvement in response to short-term lifestyle interventions. From a clinical perspective, this pattern aligns with everyday practice, in which modest shifts in volume status may result in noticeable symptomatic relief in patients with HFrEF, whereas patients with HFpEF frequently report persistent exercise intolerance despite similar short-term interventions [22].

In patients with HFrEF, short-term symptomatic improvement observed during fasting is most plausibly mediated by changes in preload and congestion. Although IF regimens may induce metabolic adaptations, their independent contribution to symptomatic improvement cannot be established from the available short-term observational data.

In addition, intermittent fasting may induce metabolic adaptations, including improved insulin sensitivity and shifts in substrate utilization, as well as activation of cellular stress–response pathways such as autophagy. However, whether these mechanisms translate into clinically meaningful benefits in HF remains uncertain, particularly given the short duration and observational nature of the available studies. Evidence from non-HF populations should therefore be interpreted as indirect and not directly generalizable to patients with established heart failure [7,23–26].

From a clinical standpoint, these findings are reassuring. NYHA functional class represents a patient-centered outcome closely linked to daily functioning, symptom burden, and prognosis. Concerns that prolonged fasting might exacerbate fatigue, dyspnea, or exercise intolerance through volume depletion or metabolic stress were not supported by the available evidence. Instead, functional trajectories among fasting patients appeared broadly comparable to those observed during non-fasting periods, with some studies reporting modest improvements.

5.2.1. Cardiac function and biomarkers

Importantly, IF regimens were not associated with deterioration in objective measures of cardiac function. Across studies, LVEF remained broadly stable over time in both fasting and non-fasting groups, regardless of baseline HF phenotype. This observation is clinically important, as reductions in LVEF would be expected if fasting imposed significant hemodynamic stress, impaired myocardial energy supply, or precipitated recurrent decompensation. From a mechanistic perspective, concerns have been raised that prolonged fasting could negatively influence cardiac performance through volume depletion, electrolyte imbalance, or altered neurohormonal activation. However, the absence of consistent deterioration in LVEF suggests that such effects, if present, were not associated with measurable changes in systolic function over the observed follow-up periods in the studied populations. Notably, this finding was observed in both preserved and reduced ejection fraction cohorts, supporting a degree of generalizability across HF subtypes. Nevertheless, interpretation is limited by the observational nature of the data, short follow-up durations, and the likelihood that fasting patients were clinically stable and carefully selected. LVEF is also relatively insensitive to short-term functional or metabolic changes, and subtle effects on diastolic function or myocardial energetics may not have been captured.

Natriuretic peptide levels provide an objective marker of myocardial wall stress and are closely linked to congestion and prognosis in heart failure [27]. In the available evidence, IF regimens did not appear to be associated with worsening natriuretic peptide profiles when compared with non-fasting periods. This finding is clinically relevant, as elevations in BNP would be expected if fasting precipitated volume overload, increased filling pressures, or subclinical decompensation. This observation aligns with the absence of deterioration in functional status and systolic performance, supporting the interpretation that fasting did not impose excess hemodynamic stress in the studied populations. However, BNP levels can be influenced by multiple factors, including renal function, age, and body composition, and short-term fluctuations may not fully capture dynamic changes in congestion [28].

Generalizability and fasting patterns

An important consideration is that most included studies evaluated Ramadan fasting, a distinct form of IF regimens characterized by complete abstinence from both food and fluids during daylight hours. This differs from non-religious IF strategies, such as time-restricted feeding or alternate-day fasting, where hydration is typically maintained. In addition, variations in dietary composition, caloric intake during non-fasting periods, and environmental factors such as temperature and daylight duration may influence physiological responses. Therefore, extrapolation of these findings to other IF regimens patterns or geographic settings should be made with caution.

These findings are further supported by observational data from broader cardiovascular populations. Horne et al. reported that routine periodic fasting was associated with a lower risk of heart failure hospitalization among patients with COVID-19, suggesting a potential protective association between habitual fasting and heart failure–related outcomes [29]. However, this study reflects long-term habitual fasting behavior over several decades and was conducted in a specific population, which differs substantially from the short-term fasting patterns evaluated in the present review.

In addition, these associations may be influenced by lifestyle-related factors and residual confounding inherent to observational designs. Therefore, such findings should be interpreted as contextual rather than directly comparable evidence and should not be extrapolated to short-term intermittent or Ramadan fasting in patients with established heart failure. Similarly, Bartholomew et al. demonstrated that routine fasting practiced over approximately two-thirds of the lifespan was associated with improved survival following cardiac catheterization, an effect that may be partially mediated by a lower incidence of HF among individuals who routinely fasted [30]. In addition, studies focusing on religious fasting have reported reassuring safety signals. Chamsi-Pasha et al. showed that fasting during Ramadan had minimal effects in stable patients with cardiac disease, with the majority able to fast without significant adverse cardiovascular consequences [31]. Taken together, these data provide contextual support for our findings and suggest that intermittent or religious fasting, when undertaken by clinically stable patients, may be tolerated without clear evidence of harm, while potential benefits warrant further investigation.

6.1. Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, these findings provide cautious reassurance for patients with stable HF who choose to undertake intermittent or religious fasting. In appropriately selected patients, fasting does not appear to be associated with short-term adverse clinical outcomes when performed alongside standard medical therapy.

However, clinical decision-making should remain individualized. Factors such as HF severity, comorbidities, medication regimen, and overall clinical stability should be considered when advising patients. Shared decision-making between clinicians and patients is essential, particularly during periods of religious fasting such as Ramadan.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evidence base is limited and predominantly observational, restricting causal inference. Sample sizes were modest, event rates were low, and fasting regimens varied in duration, intensity, and context. Notably, the non-fasting group in the Abazid et al. study was substantially smaller than the fasting group, which may have influenced effect estimates. Second, patients with advanced HF, recent decompensation, or significant comorbidities were underrepresented, limiting generalizability to higher-risk populations. Third, follow-up durations were relatively short, and some outcomes (such as LVEF and BNP) may be insufficiently sensitive to detect subtle or longer-term physiological changes. Fourth, heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting precluded quantitative synthesis for most endpoints. Finally, self-selection bias is likely, as patients who chose to fast may have been healthier, more stable, or more adherent to medical therapy than those who did not fast. Residual confounding related to unmeasured clinical or behavioral factors cannot be excluded.

In addition, most included studies were conducted in specific cultural and geographic contexts during Ramadan, which may limit generalizability to other intermittent fasting patterns or populations with different dietary habits and environmental conditions. Moreover, important contextual factors specific to Ramadan fasting, including variability in fasting duration, seasonal variation, environmental temperature, sex differences, and associated changes in dietary intake, sleep patterns, and physical activity, were not consistently reported or adjusted for in the included studies. As a result, residual confounding related to these factors cannot be excluded and may have influenced the observed outcomes.

7.1. Future direction

Future prospective and randomized studies with standardized fasting protocols in diverse HF populations are needed, incorporating longer follow-up and comprehensive assessment of functional, hemodynamic, metabolic, and biomarker outcomes to better define the safety profile and potential benefits of IF regimens across the spectrum of HF.

Conclusion

The available evidence suggests that IF regimens, including fasting undertaken during Ramadan, are not associated with clear evidence of harm in selected, clinically stable adults with chronic HF, when undertaken alongside standard medical therapy. Across the included studies, fasting was not associated with consistent adverse effects on clinical outcomes, functional status, cardiac systolic function, or natriuretic peptide levels when compared with non-fasting periods.

While these findings provide reassurance for patients who choose to fast, they should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of studies, predominantly observational designs, and substantial heterogeneity. These findings are based on short-term observational data, and the long-term safety and potential physiological effects of IF regimens in patients with chronic HF remain uncertain. Higher-quality prospective and randomized studies are required to better define safety and potential phenotype-specific effects across different HF subtypes and disease severities, and to inform evidence-based clinical guidance.
