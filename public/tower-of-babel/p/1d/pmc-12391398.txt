Low-temperature plasma efficiently promotes blood coagulation with less thermal injury in porcine models
Authors: Yufan Wei, Yu Li, Hongye Zheng, Boya Zhang, Yan Li, Yiheng Zhang, Ziheng Xu, Aoxi Xu, Shanshan Jin, Zhi Fang, Li Zhang, Yajun Zhao, Li Zhu, Xiru Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
How to maintain the efficacy of blood coagulation while minimizing thermal damage has been a significant challenge in the optimization of clinical electrosurgical units (ESUs). Recent studies have reported that reactive species produced by low-temperature plasma (LTP) can promote blood coagulation. In this study, two forms of home-made atmospheric pressure plasma jets (APPJs) were compared to argon plasma coagulation (APC) and utilized in three different porcine bleeding models. The results indicated that APPJ can effectively generate reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), including OH radicals, excited nitrogen molecules and O atoms. Although no significant differences in coagulation rates were observed among the three devices, APPJs exhibited lower maximum operating temperatures. The results of H&E staining revealed that APPJs resulted in reduced thermal damage as well. In conclusion, two self-developed APPJs demonstrated reliable coagulation efficacy with reduced thermal damage in comparison to APC. The study provides valuable insights and empirical support for the future optimization of ESUs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-08016-z.

The advancement of electrosurgical units (ESUs) represents a significant milestone in the evolution of surgical practices, leading to notable enhancements in surgical efficiency and a decrease in complications associated with bleeding1. Nonetheless, the liquefaction of adipose tissue and the exacerbation of peripheral oozing, attributable to the elevated temperatures generated by conventional ESUs—such as high-frequency surgical units and argon plasma coagulation (APC)—have been identified as primary contributors to suboptimal wound healing and the formation of scars2,3. Consequently, the pursuit of alternative energy conversion methods that can mitigate temperature while maintaining effective coagulation has emerged as a critical challenge in improving patient outcomes.

Physical plasma represents an ionized, gas-like state of matter characterized by the coexistence of high-energy electrons, ions, and neutral particles, often referred to as the “fourth state” of matter4. Low-temperature plasma (LTP) is a specific variant of plasma that is characterized by a low macroscopic temperature while exhibiting significant biological effects5–8. Recent research has demonstrated that reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) produced by LTP play a crucial role in various physiological processes, including blood coagulation, wound healing, and anti-tumor activities9–12. LTP has the capacity to activate the oxidative stress response via RONS, leading to the formation of a membrane-like structure at the site of hemorrhage, commonly known as a “coagulated tissue.” This process can enhance vasoconstriction by promoting vascular contraction, activating coagulation factors, initiating platelet aggregation, facilitating fibrin polymerization, degrading fibrinolytic enzymes, and increasing blood viscosity13–15.

Temperature and biological effects are two critical factors in the coagulation process. The application of high-frequency current and elevated temperatures by APC primarily facilitates coagulation through the denaturation of proteins and the desiccation of blood16,17. As one of the most prevalent non-contact hemostatic devices utilized in clinical practice, APC is characterized by its high efficiency, lack of adhesion, and user-friendliness18. However, it operates at exceedingly high gas temperatures, reaching up to 200 °C or more. To address the challenges associated with these elevated temperatures while maintaining coagulation efficacy, we enhanced the argon and oxygen mixed gas by increasing the oxygen ratio in the working gas. Nitrogen species originated from ambient air entrainment into the plasma plume. This modification aimed to generate a greater quantity of RONS and to amplify the biological effects involved in the coagulation process, reducing heat flux and thereby mitigating thermal damage and reducing operational temperatures, with the same coagulation rate and effect.

The study involved the independent development of argon and oxygen mixed atmospheric pressure plasma jets (APPJs) featuring two distinct electrode configurations. These devices were compared to APC, to investigate the feasibility and coagulation effectiveness of this novel form of electrosurgical energy in porcine models. The study aims to provide evidence and optimization potential for future research in the field of ESUs.

Materials and methods

Plasma configuration and characteristics

In the present study, we utilized two home-made APPJs. The APPJs were composed of a high-frequency alternating current (AC) power supply, a jet reactor, a rotor flow meter, and a load gas. The high-frequency AC power developed by ourselves could generate sine waves with a voltage amplitude up to 15 kV, an output power up to 200 W, and a frequency ranging from 10 kHz to 40 kHz. Based on the electrode configuration of the jet reactor, we developed two distinct types of jet reactors: the APPJ with a slit electrode (sl-APPJ) and the APPJ with a single needle electrode (sn-APPJ). Both APPJ discharge structures employed the needle-grounding pad. The grounding pad is pasted on the surface of the animal, while the high-voltage electrodes in this study have two shapes. One is the traditional needle electrode; the other one is a blade-shaped electrode with dielectric coating on the surface while only the edge of the blade is exposed. The detailed structure is given in Fig. S1 B. The load gas mixture consisted of argon (99.9%) and oxygen (99.9%) (Ar + 0.25% O2) at a flow rate of 1.5 L per minute. The APC (VIO300S + APC2; Erbe, Germany) was operated in APC mode with a power output of 40–60 W, utilizing argon (99.9%) at a flow rate of 1.8 L per minute. Details on the experimental setup are provided in Fig. S1 and Table S1.

To measure the voltage-current waveforms of all three de reactive oxygen and nitrogen species vices, we employed an oscilloscope (ZDS3024; Guangzhou ZHIYUAN Electronics Co., LTD, China) with a sampling rate of 2 GS/s and a bandwidth of 200 MHz, in conjunction with a high voltage probe (P6015A; Tektronix, USA) and a current coil (PEARSON current monitor 6585; PEARSON, USA). Since the APC is a fully assembled commercial product, it is difficult to measure the actual output voltage directly. Therefore, we measured the voltage on the tissue, defined as Utissue, and Idis is defined as the discharge current. To make it comparable to the APC results, we also measured voltage and current waveforms on tissue for sn-APPJ and sl-APPJ. To identify the types and intensities of reactive species present in LTP, we utilized a spectrometer (FLAME-T-UV-VIS-ES, Ocean Insight, USA), which operates within a wavelength range of 178.7 to 885.0 nm. An optical fiber was positioned at a distance of 4.8 mm from the center of the tube and as close as possible to the outlet in vertical direction to collect the optical emission spectrum, with the spectrometer’s integration time set to 100 ms. Before discharge, the emission spectrum was recorded as the background, then it was subtracted from each recorded spectrum in experimental group. The measurement method for three devices is the same, and the obtained absolute value was used to compare. The experimental setup and the connections are given in Fig. S1A. The results were subsequently analyzed using OriginPro (version 2023; OriginLab Corporation, USA; https://www.originlab.com).

Experimental animals

Ethics statement

The protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center at the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (AMUWEC20230481). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All animal maintenance and procedures were in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental animals and anesthesia

In this study, six healthy female landrace pigs (Chongqing watson biological Technology Co., Ltd.), weighing between 46 and 54 kg and aged 4–5 months, were utilized. The animals underwent a fasting period of 12 h prior to the experiment, with water intake restricted for 4–6 h. Anesthesia was administered using Zoletil®50 (Virbac, China) and Sumianxin II (Dunhua Shengda animal drug Co., LTD, China), with Meloxicam (Qilu Animal Health, China) injected intramuscularly for analgesia. Routine intravenous rehydration was provided during the surgical procedure. The pigs were positioned supine for the surgery, with their limbs securely restrained. At the conclusion of the experiment, euthanasia was conducted using a high concentration CO2 method under deep anesthesia.

Model construction and treatment

An “L-shaped” incision, approximately 15 cm in length, was made in the abdominal wall. The incision was performed layer by layer. Upon entering the abdominal cavity, the liver was exposed and assessed for normalcy, ensuring the absence of significant lesions.

Hepatic surface resection model

A superficial square wound model was created on the liver surface to simulate the bleeding of superficial liver injury. First, a 1 cm × 1 cm square area was marked on the exposed liver surface using a sterile surgical marker. A scalpel blade was then used to make precise incisions along the marked boundaries to a uniform depth of 0.1 cm. The delineated tissue segment was subsequently excised using microsurgical scissors, removing the full 0.1 cm thickness of superficial parenchyma to create a standardized bleeding surface. All wound creations were performed by the same board-certified general surgeon to ensure maximum procedural consistency. This model produced active hemorrhage from the cut edges and base of the wound, with the square geometry facilitating accurate area measurement for subsequent coagulation rate calculations. To examine the differences in thermal damage, liver tissues were subjected to treatment induced by sn-APPJ or APC for durations of 5, 10, and 20 s at a consistent distance of 1 cm from the base of the wound.

Partial hepatic and splenic resection model

As performed in clinical settings, a wedge-shaped section of the liver was resected using a scalpel blade. Immediately following resection, the devices (APPJs and APC) were applied in alternating “Z”-shaped patterns across the raw surface, maintaining 1 cm distance between the probe tip and tissue. Each pass covered previously untreated areas. If persistent bleeding was observed in treated zones, repeated applications were permitted. For the partial splenectomy, a section was excised perpendicular to the long axis of the spleen, with subsequent steps mirroring those of the partial hepatectomy. Following hemostasis, the resected area was observed for a duration of 20 min. Successful coagulation was determined by the absence of blood seepage from the resection site.

Leg amputation model

Prior to the model preparation, a hemostatic bandage was applied to the proximal end of the pig’s foreleg joint to simulate surgical conditions. The foreleg was then amputated vertically along its long axis, avoiding the joint area, while ensuring that the hemostatic bandage remained intact post-amputation. The presence of blood seepage from the wound was considered indicative of successful model preparation. Coagulation treatment was administered using sn-APPJ, which was halted upon the cessation of active blood seepage, with the duration of treatment recorded. When addressing the vascular area of the amputation site, the device was employed to secure the upper portion of the vascular stump, facilitating targeted treatment until vascular contracture was observed. After a 10-minute treatment period, the hemostatic bandage was gradually loosened to assess the wound for any blood oozing. Successful coagulation was confirmed by the absence of active bleeding or oozing.

Coagulation assessment

The coagulation efficacy of APPJs was investigated using hepatic surface resection model, partial hepatectomy model and leg amputation model.

Coagulation rate

The clotting time (T) in seconds was measured from the initiation of coagulation until there was no visible blood oozing. Photographic documentation of the tissue treatment surface was conducted, and the area (S) in square centimeters was quantified using ImageJ Pro (Fiji, version 2.9.0; NIH, USA; https://imagej.net). The coagulation rate (v) was calculated using the formula v (cm2/s) = S/T.

Local maximum working temperature

The real-time tissue temperature induced by the device operation was measured using an infrared thermal camera (FLIR A600, ± 2 °C accuracy American FLIR Company, USA). The manufacturer specifies a standard temperature measurement range of – ​20 °C to 350 °C, with an accuracy of ± 2 °C or 2% of the measured value. The camera was positioned at a distance of 30–40 cm from the treatment surface, with the maximum temperature recorded.

Thermal damage

Histological specimen measuring 5 cm × 3 cm × 2 cm was excised from the area surrounding the treatment site, including the trauma surface, and were subsequently immersed in a 10% neutral formalin buffer for 24 h. Following this, the specimens were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Observations and photographs were acquired using the Pannoramic MIDI whole slide scanning system (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). To evaluate thermal damage, the entire area was categorized into three distinct regions: the carbonized area, the vacuolated area, and the sub-boiling coagulation area19. The thermal damage area (cm²) was quantified using ImageJ Pro software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were performed using the GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.2; GraphPad Software Inc., USA; https://www.graphpad.com). The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, one-way ANOVA was used to compare the sample means between multiple groups, and t-test was used to compare the means of two samples, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

The types and relative intensity of excited particles in LTP were determined by optical emission spectroscopy according to the spectra generated by the deexcitation of excited particles.

The results represented that some RONS, such as OH and O were were produced by all three devices. During the discharge process, ground state Ar atoms and ground state N2 can generate metastable Arm (reaction (1)), excited state Ar* (reaction (2, 11)), excited state N2 (C3Πu) (reaction (3, 4, 13)), excited state N2+ (B2Σu+)(reaction (6, 7)), through electron impact reaction and Penning ionization reaction, etc. OH radicals can be generated by the impact of high energy electrons on H2O (reaction (7)), or by the dissociation of H2O by Ar* excitation (reaction (14)), or by the reaction with metastable N2 (A3Σu) (reaction (15)). The generated OH radical can obtain the excited state energy of N2 (A3Σu) and be excited to OH (A2Σ) (reaction (16)). The collision of H2O molecules and O2 with electrons in the air (reaction (8,9)) and the collision dissociation of O2 itself with Ar* (reaction (17)) can produce O, which further collides with electrons to obtain energy (reaction (10, 11)) or Penning ionization with Arm to form an excited state (reaction (18))20,21. It is noted that the reaction processes in the atmospheric pressure Ar-N₂-O₂-H₂O mixed gas discharge plasma are indeed complex, including electron impact reactions, electron–ion recombination reactions, ion–ion, ion–neutral and neutral–neutral reactions. The reaction equations listed here mainly focus on the key paths of RONS generation. Thermal damage assessment

The resultant images of the liver post-treatment were presented in Fig. 2. The untreated control liver exhibited a reddish-brown coloration, while the liver exposed to sn-APPJ displayed a white appearance. In contrast, the liver treated with APC manifested a yellow hue accompanied by areas of carbonization, indicating a more significant extent of thermal damage (Fig. 2A). Histological analysis via H&E staining revealed that the thermally damaged regions could be categorized into three distinct areas, including carbonized, vacuolated, and sub-boiling coagulation zones. An increase in treatment duration correlated with an escalation in thermal damage for both APC and APPJ applications. However, no discernible carbonized regions were observed at the timepoint of 5 s. Notably, sn-APPJ exhibited a reduced level of thermal damage compared to APC, aligning with the general observational results (Fig. 2B and C).

Effective coagulation of the liver by APPJ

The coagulation efficacy of APPJs was investigated using partial hepatectomy model. The findings indicated that both sl-APPJ and sn-APPJ, along with APC, were capable of achieving effective coagulation. Notably, a dark red “coagulation clot” was observed following treatments with APC, sn-APPJ and sl-APPJ (Fig. 3A, B, and C). The average coagulation rates were recorded as 0.096 s/cm² for APC, 0.089 s/cm² for sl-APPJ, and 0.078 s/cm² for sn-APPJ, with no statistically significant difference noted (p = 0.416) (Fig. 3D). Similar trends were observed in the hepatic surface resection model, where the average coagulation rates of the three devices were 0.171 s/cm², 0.154 s/cm², and 0.170 s/cm², respectively (p = 0.678) (Fig. 3E and F).

Real-time monitoring of the local operating temperatures of the devices was conducted using infrared thermography (Fig. 4A). In the context of the partial hepatectomy model, sl-APPJ and sn-APPJ exhibited lower maximum operating temperatures of 87.2 °C (p < 0.01) and 102.0 °C (p < 0.05), respectively, in comparison to APC, which reached 120.9 °C (Fig. 4B). Similarly, in the hepatic surface resection model, the maximum temperatures recorded for sl-APPJ (103.1 °C, p < 0.05) and sn-APPJ (91.3 °C, p < 0.001) were significantly lower than that of APC (117.3 °C) (Fig. 4C). H&E staining was employed to evaluate thermal damage. The results revealed a distinct boundary within the sub-boiling zone, indicating the demarcation between thermally damaged tissue and normal liver parenchyma. The area of thermal damage following APC treatment was significantly greater than that observed with APPJs, encompassing both the carbonized region and the sub-boiling zone. Furthermore, minimal carbonization was noted following treatment with sn-APPJ (Fig. 4D).

Effective coagulation of spleen by APPJ

No instances of blood oozing were noted in the coagulation section following a 20-minute application of sl-APPJ, sn-APPJ, and APC in the partial splenectomy model. The coagulation process associated with APPJ was illustrated in Fig. 5A. The average coagulation rates for APC, sl-APPJ, and sn-APPJ were recorded at 0.047 s/cm², 0.086 s/cm², and 0.051 s/cm², respectively, with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.199) as depicted in Fig. 5B. The average maximum working temperatures for APC, sl-APPJ, and sn-APPJ were measured at 105 °C, 94.75 °C, and 94.20 °C, respectively, with no statistically significant difference observed (p = 0.743) as shown in Fig. 5C. H&E staining revealed that the area of thermal damage in the spleen tissue treated with APC was significantly larger compared to that of sl-APPJ and sn-APPJ, as illustrated in Fig. 5D.

Effective coagulation of porcine leg amputation sections by APPJs

At the site of amputation, bloody exudate was evident, accompanied by localized active bleeding, despite the application of a tourniquet (Fig. 6A). Following the amputation procedure, the site was treated with sn-APPJ at a consistent speed, with targeted reinforcement applied to the areas of active hemorrhage. The duration required for the cessation of significant bleeding and active hemorrhage was meticulously recorded, revealing an average treatment time of approximately 160 s, with a range of 125 to 200 s (Fig. 6B). After a waiting period of 10 min, only a minimal amount of blood exudate was noted at the amputation site, with no significant active bleeding observed (Fig. 6C). Notably, even after the tourniquet was removed, there was no evidence of significant active bleeding (Fig. 6D). These findings substantiate the efficacy of sn-APPJ in promoting coagulation and achieving effective hemostasis within a 10-minute timeframe, thereby fulfilling the criteria of the “Platinum Ten” (a benchmark for achieving hemostasis within 10 min to ensure the survival of wounded personnel and mitigation of side effects).

Discussion

This research validated the coagulation efficacy of two self-developed APPJs utilizing porcine models. The findings demonstrated that APPJs effectively facilitated coagulation. In comparison to APC, APPJs exhibited not only superior coagulation efficiency but also lower operational temperatures and reduced thermal injury. These attributes are conducive to enhancing wound healing and minimizing the incidence of postoperative complications in clinical settings.

APC is frequently employed for hemostasis in parenchymal organs, particularly during hepatic surgical procedures, and its application has progressively expanded to various endoscopic surgical interventions in recent years22–24. The operational mechanism of APC involves the transmission of electrosurgical energy, electric current, and heat through the excitation of argon ionic beams, facilitating coagulation primarily through thermal effects that induce vascular contraction25. Consequently, concerns regarding localized tissue hyperthermia and adjacent thermal injury associated with APC are pertinent. Elevated temperatures can result in tissue carbonization and impede the healing of wound scabs, presenting a significant challenge in clinical practice.

In this investigation, we designed two distinct types of APPJs featuring different electrode configurations: the sn-APPJ, characterized by a hollow needle-like electrode, and the sl-APPJ, which incorporates a slit electrode resembling a knife tip. Experimental findings indicated that the sn-APPJ exhibits the lowest operational temperature and the most rapid coagulation rate compared to the other devices, a phenomenon likely attributable to the enhanced plasma component generation facilitated by the hollow needle electrode. Additionally, our self-developed devices utilized a combination of argon and oxygen gases, which augmented the effective active components within the plasma beam and maximized the biological effects of RONS during the coagulation process. This strategy not only reduced the working temperature but also maintained coagulation efficacy, thereby minimizing thermal injury to the tissue.

The current research outcomes may inform the optimized design of future APPJ electrodes. Notably, it has been observed that plasma can form a membrane-like structure at the site of bleeding, referred to as a “coagulation clot,” which diminishes blood flow without inflicting thermal damage20,21. The formation of such a “coagulation clot” was distinctly noted during the coagulation processes involving both APPJs in our study. H&E staining results further corroborated that APPJs induced less thermal damage compared to APC. Moreover, we observed that the spectral intensity of RONS generated by APPJs was significantly greater than that produced by APC, suggesting a potential role of RONS in the coagulation mechanism of APPJs. However, the precise mechanisms underlying coagulation enhancement by LTP remain uncertain, with several hypotheses proposed, including the acceleration of blood vessel vasoconstriction, activation of coagulation factors, stimulation of platelet aggregation, and promotion of fibrin polymerization26–28.

Significantly, our observations indicated that both sn-APPJ and sl-APPJ exhibited a yellow flame-like reaction during the coagulation process, whereas such a reaction was nearly absent in the case of APC. Spectroscopic analysis revealed that the intensity of the [Na D] 589 nm emission spectra generated by sl-APPJ and sn-APPJ was comparable and substantially greater than that observed for APC. This finding suggests that the yellow flame-like reaction may be attributed to the [Na D] emissions resulting from the plasma excitation of the NaCl electrolyte solution present within the tissues.

Currently, the majority of research efforts are concentrated on the coagulation efficacy of small animal models, with a notable scarcity of validation studies utilizing large animal models29–32. Nomura et al. examined the coagulation effectiveness of APPJs in a porcine model of liver and gastric mucosal hemorrhage. However, their study employed a point hemorrhage model characterized by minimal bleeding, which limits its comparability to clinical intraoperative hemorrhage scenarios33. Kuo et al. investigated the efficacy and potential mechanisms of an air-excited LTP device in a porcine leg skin incision model and an amputation model34. Nonetheless, the air-excited plasma produced significant amounts of ozone and operated at elevated temperatures exceeding 100 °C within a brief timeframe, complicating the assessment of thermal damage due to the absence of tissue staining methods. Elmore et al. studied J-Plasma scalpel in porcine and rats models, demonstrating that the device could promote healing of surgical incisions while achieving efficient cutting and coagulation effects35. In addition, studies showed that not only APPJs, but also modified materials after plasma treatment had a great improvement in hemostatic efficiency. Ge et al. prepared an improved PVA/PLA fiber hemostatic film using electrospinning technology combined with air plasma modification, and showed that its hemostatic ability was increased by 79% through mouse liver hemostatic model36. In the present study, the coagulation effectiveness of LTP was evaluated using argon and oxygen mixtures across various porcine models. The APPJs employed in this investigation exhibited low-temperature characteristics and minimal thermal damage, aligning more closely with the requirements for clinical product translation. The ability of APPJs to achieve rapid hemostasis with minimal thermal injury makes them particularly suitable for delicate surgeries where precision is paramount. This may provide valuable insights for the optimization and enhancement of electrosurgical devices in future applications.

This research also has several limitations. Firstly, we represented a preliminary exploration of the efficacy of APPJs, with only speculative insights into the underlying mechanisms. The molecular mechanisms and potential pathways associated with the coagulation-promoting effects were not experimentally elucidated. Secondly, the apparatus utilized in this study was merely a preliminary prototype, and the types and ratios of the gases employed, as well as the voltage and current parameters, had not been optimized at this stage. It was plausible that an alternative parameter configuration could yield more favorable outcomes. Thirdly, in this study, APC primarily served as a comparative conventional ESUs. For safety compliance with device specifications, we exclusively utilized pure argon as the working medium without investigating potential effects of alternative on coagulation efficacy. The impact of different gas mixtures on the coagulation efficacy of APC requires further investigation.

Conclusions

The two home-made APPJs devices demonstrated significant coagulation efficacy with minimal thermal damage, particularly the sn-APPJ variant. In comparison to the clinically utilized APC, APPJs exhibited lower operational temperatures alongside enhanced biological effectiveness. Achieving an optimal balance between temperature and biological impact is essential in the coagulation process associated with LTP. The findings of this study offer valuable insights and technical parameters that may inform future clinical applications of LTP products for hemostatic purposes, suggesting a promising potential for LTP in the domain of surgical hemostasis.
