Factors contributing to the missed diagnosis of incidental gallbladder cancer (IGBC): an integrated analysis of clinical features and ultrasound radiomics
Authors: Zhiping Pan, Xu Sun, Zhenhua Shen, Feng Cen, Qiang Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncidental gallbladder cancer (IGBC) is often diagnosed only during or after cholecystectomy, and preoperative identification remains challenging. This study preliminarily explored factors contributing to IGBC missed diagnosis and attempted to develop an ultrasound radiomics‑based identification model.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 62 IGBC and 78 non‑incidental GBC (NIGBC) patients who were consecutively enrolled between 2016/01–2025/12 was analyzed. Clinical, laboratory, imaging, pathological, and immunohistochemical features were compared. From ultrasound images, 1220 radiomics features were extracted; after stability (ICC > 0.75), redundancy removal (Spearman |ρ|> 0.90), and LASSO regression, nine features were retained. Seven machine learning algorithms were used to exploratorily build radiomics‑only models. Potential clinical predictors were identified by logistic regression, and a clinical‑only and a combined model were attempted. Performance was preliminarily evaluated using area under the curve (AUC).ResultsIGBC showed higher gallstone prevalence (91.9% vs. 53.8%, P < 0.001) and a “benign masquerade” laboratory profile (higher albumin (ALB), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‑C); lower ratio of albumin to globulin (RAR); fewer elevated CA19‑9). Pathologically, IGBC was predominantly infiltrative (77.4% vs. 28.2%, P < 0.001), had earlier T stage compared to NIGBC (27.4% vs. 12.8%, P = 0.007), and exhibited lower Ki‑67 high expression (67.7% vs. 83.3%, P = 0.031) and weaker Topoisomerase II-alpha (Topo II) staining (P = 0.005). The extreme gradient boosting (XGB) radiomics model achieved a validation AUC of 0.865 (accuracy 0.833), suggesting potential discriminative ability. Gallstones (OR = 9.484) and growth pattern (OR = 0.230) might be independent clinical predictors. The clinical‑only model had AUC 0.830, and the combined model AUC 0.860, with no significant benefit over radiomics‑only.ConclusionPreoperative missed diagnosis of IGBC may be associated with gallstone‑related inflammation, infiltrative growth, early T stage, seemingly normal laboratory findings, and low proliferation marker expression. Although conventional ultrasound hardly identifies IGBC, its tumor heterogeneity might be quantified by radiomics. The XGB model showed preliminary ability to distinguish IGBC from NIGBC and holds potential as a non‑invasive tool for preoperative risk stratification, but findings require validation in larger multicenter cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16467-4.Keywords: Incidental gallbladder cancer, Missed diagnosis, Ultrasound, Radiomics, Machine learning, XGB

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is the most common malignant tumor of the biliary tract, accounting for approximately 1.2% of all cancer cases worldwide [1]. Its incidence exhibits significant geographical variation, with rates ranging from 0.8–1.3 per 100,000 in Europe, 1.1–8.5 per 100,000 in the United States, and approximately 2 per 100,000 in Asia [2–4]. The overall prognosis remains poor, with a median survival of less than one year and a 5-year survival rate below 10% [5, 6]. However, early accurate staging and standardized treatment can significantly improve the 5-year survival rate to approximately 75% [7].

Based on the timing of diagnosis, GBC is clinically classified into non-incidental GBC (NIGBC), which is diagnosed preoperatively by imaging, and incidental GBC (IGBC), which is unexpectedly identified during or after surgery through pathological examination. It is noteworthy that approximately 50%–70% of GBC cases are diagnosed only during or after cholecystectomy, whether elective or emergency procedures [8]. Previous studies have shown that even at comparable tumor stages, patients with IGBC have inferior survival outcomes compared to those with NIGBC [9, 10], highlighting the limitations of current preoperative diagnostic strategies.

Ultrasound (US), due to its convenience, accessibility, and good spatial resolution, is the primary imaging modality for gallbladder diseases. Suspicious findings such as wall thickening, irregular mucosa and submucosa, and abnormal echogenicity are valuable indicators of malignant lesions and play an important role in clinical decision-making [11]. Therefore, improving the ability of ultrasound to differentiate suspicious lesions and irregular gallbladder wall features is a key approach to reducing missed diagnoses and the occurrence of IGBC. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning have shown potential in enhancing diagnostic accuracy by capturing subtle imaging patterns suggestive of IGBC [12].

Existing research has largely focused on comparing clinical and prognostic aspects between IGBC and NIGBC [11, 13], while multidimensional systematic analysis of the reasons for missed diagnosis of IGBC remains relatively insufficient. Studies exploring radiomic features based on ultrasound imaging for discriminating IGBC are also scarce. In light of this, our study aims to systematically compare differences between IGBC and NIGBC across multiple dimensions—including clinical baseline data, hematological indicators, preoperative imaging findings, pathological characteristics, and immunohistochemical expression—to identify factors that may contribute to the underdiagnosis of IGBC. Furthermore, we intend to develop three types of predictive models — a radiomics‑only model, a clinical model, and a combined clinical‑radiomics model — based on ultrasound images to enhance the preoperative identification and discrimination of IGBC. This study is expected to provide methodological support and clinical reference for improving the preoperative diagnostic accuracy and reducing the missed diagnosis rate of IGBC.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This single‑center retrospective study consecutively enrolled all patients who underwent cholecystectomy at Huzhou Central Hospital between January 2016 and December 2025. A total of 62 consecutive patients were pathologically confirmed as IGBC after cholecystectomy for benign indications. During the same period, a total of 78 consecutive patients with a preoperative definitive diagnosis of NIGBC based on imaging and clinical evaluation were included as the control group.

Inclusion criteria: for the IGBC group, preoperative clinical and imaging findings suggestive of benign disease and postoperative pathological confirmation of GBC; for the NIGBC group, a definitive preoperative diagnosis of GBC followed by surgical intervention. Both groups were required to have complete key clinical and examination data and to have undergone preoperative ultrasound. Exclusion criteria included confirmed distant metastasis or extensive dissemination before surgery, absence of postoperative pathological results, and lack of essential clinical, imaging, surgical, or pathological records.

Data collection

Data collection was performed independently by two physicians. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, preoperative laboratory test results, imaging reports, surgical records, and pathological findings were extracted from outpatient and inpatient medical record systems. All available pathological blocks and slides were re-evaluated by specialized pathologists, focusing on tumor location, pathological type, gross appearance, differentiation, infiltration pattern, perineural and vascular invasion, and bile duct margin status. For pathological materials that could not be retrieved due to objective reasons, the original pathology reports were used. Tumor staging (TNM) was determined based on postoperative histopathological results combined with post-diagnosis imaging evaluation. Preoperative ultrasound diagnosis of GBC followed predefined criteria established by senior ultrasonographers at our institution [14].

Radiomics model construction

Image data collection

Preoperative ultrasound images of patients in both groups were collected for analysis. Ultrasound examinations were performed using Philips IU22 (C5‑2 probe) and GE Voluson E9 (C1‑6 probe) systems. To ensure consistency in subsequent quantitative analysis, all images underwent standardized preprocessing, including intensity normalization and region of interest (ROI) segmentation centered on the gallbladder lesion. Subsequently, 1220 radiomics features were extracted from the ultrasound images using the open‑source PyRadiomics software (version 1.6.0), covering first‑order statistics, shape features, texture features (including gray‑level co‑occurrence matrix [GLCM] and gray‑level run length matrix [GLRLM]), and wavelet‑transformed features. Data preprocessing

To minimize the impact of inter‑observer subjectivity on feature stability, 30 patients were randomly selected. Two independent radiologists (each with more than 5 years of experience in abdominal ultrasound) performed ROI delineation and feature extraction separately. Inter‑observer reproducibility of the extracted features was assessed using the intra‑class correlation coefficient (ICC), with a threshold of 0.75. Features with an ICC > 0.75 were retained, yielding 1,171 features. The correlation analysis results are shown in Fig. 2. To further mitigate the influence of inter‑scanner and inter‑institution variability on feature stability, as well as the effect of differences in feature scales on model construction, Z‑score normalization was applied to standardize all features to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Subsequently, the 140 patients were randomly divided into a training set (98 patients, 70%) and a validation set (42 patients, 30%) using a 7:3 ratio.

ICC closer to 1 indicates higher consistency between two segmentations; features below 0.75 were excluded

Feature selection

A hierarchical feature selection process was applied to the training set to reduce dimensionality and identify discriminative variables. First, each of the 1171 radiomics features was tested for differences between the IGBC and NIGBC groups. An independent samples t‑test was used when normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were met; otherwise, the Mann‑Whitney U test was applied. Using a two‑sided P < 0.05 threshold, 38 significant features were retained. Subsequently, Spearman's rank correlation was used to calculate pairwise correlations among the candidate features, with |ρ|> 0.90 defined as high collinearity. A greedy recursive strategy was then applied to iteratively remove the most redundant feature (i.e., the feature with the highest average correlation with other features) until no highly correlated feature pairs remained, resulting in 15 non‑redundant features. A feature correlation heatmap (Fig. 3) was generated to visualize the correlation structure, where color intensity represents the absolute correlation coefficient (diagonal = 1). This visualization facilitated the identification and removal of highly correlated feature pairs, thereby reducing overfitting risk and enhancing the robustness of subsequent modeling.

Spearman correlation heatmap of candidate features. Darker colors indicate higher absolute correlations (|ρ|). Pairs with |ρ|> 0.90 were removed via greedy recursive selection, yielding 15 non‑redundant features

After removing redundant features, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied for further feature selection and regularization. LASSO imposes an L1‑norm penalty on the regression coefficients, controlled by the regularization parameter λ, which shrinks the coefficients of features weakly associated with the outcome to zero, thereby achieving built‑in variable selection and mitigating overfitting. The optimal λ value was determined via tenfold cross‑validation to minimize the cross‑validation error (mean squared error). Under this optimal λ, features with non‑zero coefficients were retained to form the final radiomics feature set. Figure 4 shows the cross‑validation error curve (x‑axis: λ, y‑axis: mean squared error, with the dashed line indicating the optimal λ position). Figure 5 displays the coefficient path plot (each curve corresponds to one feature, showing its shrinkage trajectory as regularization increases). Ultimately, LASSO selected 9 features for subsequent model construction (Fig. 6). This process ensured model sparsity, enhanced generalizability and interpretability, and reduced the impact of multicollinearity on parameter estimation.

Radiomics model development and evaluation

Using the nine radiomics features selected by LASSO regression, seven machine learning algorithms—namely, Logistic Regression (LR), Naïve Bayes (NB), Multi‑Layer Perceptron (MLP), K‑Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, XGB)—were employed to construct predictive models. On the training set (98 patients, 70%), each model underwent fivefold cross‑validation combined with grid search to optimize hyperparameters. The models were then retrained using the optimal hyperparameters and evaluated on an independent validation set (42 patients, 30%).

Evaluation metrics included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Additionally, calibration curves were plotted to assess model goodness‑of‑fit, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to quantify the net clinical benefit across different threshold probabilities.

Clinical and combined model construction

Feature processing

Candidate clinical variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hepatitis, gallstones, common bile duct stones), laboratory parameters, and growth pattern. Univariate logistic regression was first performed to screen for variables significantly associated with IGBC (P < 0.05). Variables with P < 0.05 were then entered into multivariate logistic regression using backward stepwise selection (entry/removal threshold of 0.10) to identify independent clinical predictors.

The radiomics score (Rad-score) was calculated for each patient based on the nine radiomics features selected by LASSO (Fig. 5), as the linear combination of each feature value multiplied by its corresponding LASSO coefficient:

Rad-score = Σ (β_i × feature_i).

where the coefficients β_i were derived from the LASSO optimal λ value of 0.0285. The Rad-score reflects the overall radiomics characteristics of each gallbladder lesion.

The combined model features were constructed by integrating the statistically significant clinical factors identified through univariate and multivariate logistic regression with the Rad-score, forming a fusion model.

Model construction and evaluation

On the training set, we constructed a total of four models for clinical data and combined data using logistic regression and XGBoost — the latter having demonstrated excellent performance in the radiomics model development. For the clinical models, the statistically significant independent clinical predictors identified by univariate and multivariate logistic regression on the training set were used as modeling features. For the combined models, both the Rad‑score and the independent clinical predictors were included as modeling features. Each model was trained using fivefold cross‑validation and evaluated on the held‑out validation set (30% of the data).

Model visualization and post‑hoc power analysis

To elucidate the quantitative contribution of each feature to the model predictions, this study introduced the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework. To assess feature importance from a global perspective, we calculated the arithmetic mean of the absolute SHAP values for each feature across all samples, ranked the features in descending order, and plotted a feature importance bar chart. Furthermore, to reveal the specific influence of feature value variations on the direction of model decisions, we used SHAP summary plots to visualize the core features, intuitively displaying the mapping between the distribution of original feature values (red‑blue color mapping) and their positive or negative driving effects on the predicted phenotype.

To verify the adequacy of the sample size, a formal post‑hoc power analysis was conducted based on the classical Hanley‑McNeil framework. Statistical power was calculated using a two‑sided significance level of α = 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). The Shapiro‑Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous data were presented as mean (standard deviation), and comparisons between the IGBC and NIGBC groups were made using the t‑test or Mann‑Whitney U non‑parametric test, depending on the normality distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‑square test or Fisher's exact test and presented as proportions. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the maximum Youden index were applied to determine the optimal cut‑off values of continuous variables for predicting IGBC; these continuous variables were then converted into binary variables for subsequent regression analyses. Feature selection, model construction and evaluation, and model visualization were performed using Python (version 3.9.0). A two‑sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Histopathological features

The histopathological features are compared in Table 3. The growth pattern (Fig. 7) of the tumor differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.001). The infiltrating type was predominant in IGBC cases (77.4%), whereas the intraluminal growing type was more common in NIGBC cases (48.7%). T stage distribution also showed a significant difference between the groups (P = 0.007): the IGBC group had a higher proportion of early-stage (Tis-T1) cases (27.4% vs. 12.8%), while the NIGBC group had more advanced-stage (T3-T4) cases (67.9% vs. 50.0%). No significant differences were observed regarding tumor location, pathological type, differentiation grade, direction of invasion, presence of neurovascular invasion, or bile duct margin status (all P > 0.05).

Radiomics analysis

Using the nine radiomics features selected by LASSO regression, we constructed seven different classifiers: LR, NB, MLP, KNN, RF, SVM, and XGB. The optimal hyperparameter combinations for each algorithm were determined through hyperparameter optimization during the modeling process (Table 6). Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Overall, performance metrics on the training set were higher than those on the validation set, with differences within an acceptable range, and no obvious overfitting or underfitting was observed. Among them, the XGB model achieved the best discriminative performance on the training set, with an AUC of 0.917 (95% CI: 0.864–0.963), an accuracy of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.776–0.929), a sensitivity of 0.837, and a specificity of 0.855, demonstrating a good balance of metrics. On the validation set (Fig. 8 and Table 7), the XGB model maintained the optimal performance, with an AUC of 0.865, significantly outperforming other models: MLP (AUC = 0.840, accuracy = 0.786), RF (AUC = 0.810, accuracy = 0.810), SVM (AUC = 0.817, accuracy = 0.738), KNN (AUC = 0.805, accuracy = 0.738), LR (AUC = 0.741, accuracy = 0.762), and NB (AUC = 0.712, accuracy = 0.738), demonstrating good generalization ability. Detailed performance metrics for each model on the training and validation sets are summarized in Table 7.

Model evaluation and interpretability

This study evaluated feature importance based on SHAP values, revealing that nine core radiomic features significantly contributed to the predictive output of the model. The feature importance ranking and SHAP summary plots are presented in Figs. 14 and 15. Among these features, the logarithm-transformed first-order skewness (logarithm_firstorder_Skewness, Mean |SHAP|= 0.4094) and wavelet-LHH-based run length non-uniformity normalized (wavelet-LHH_glrlm_RunLengthNonUniformityNormalized, Mean |SHAP|= 0.3647) were the two most important features. High values of these features corresponded to positive SHAP values, suggesting that IGBC lesions exhibit greater asymmetry in intensity distribution in the logarithmic frequency domain and more pronounced local textural heterogeneity in the high-frequency wavelet subbands.

Furthermore, other texture heterogeneity metrics, including short-run low gray-level emphasis (exponential_glrlm_ShortRunLowGrayLevelEmphasis, Mean |SHAP|= 0.2526), cluster shade (logarithm_glcm_ClusterShade, Mean |SHAP|= 0.2387), and gray-level non-uniformity normalized (wavelet-HLH_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized, Mean |SHAP|= 0.2182), also demonstrated substantial predictive contributions. Sample size and statistical power analysis

The XGB classification model developed in this study demonstrated good discriminative performance in both the training and validation sets, with a combined area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9036, which was significantly higher than the random guessing level (AUC = 0.5). Considering the limited total sample size (140 cases), a post‑hoc power analysis based on the Hanley‑McNeil framework was performed to exclude potential sampling errors due to the sample size restriction. The results showed that, under a two‑sided significance level of α = 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 80%, the minimum true AUC that the current sample configuration could reliably detect was 0.637. Moreover, based on the observed strong effect size (AUC = 0.9036), the corresponding empirical power approached 100.00% (see Fig. 16). These quantitative analyses indicate that, although the absolute number of cases in this cohort was limited due to strict single‑center inclusion and exclusion criteria, the model effect was sufficiently strong. The existing 140 samples provide adequate statistical certainty, ruling out the risk of false‑positive or false‑negative errors due to insufficient sample size, thereby providing a robust mathematical foundation for the reliability of the predictive conclusions of this study.

Discussion

GBC typically follows a multi-step progression from normal epithelium through metaplasia, dysplasia, and carcinoma in situ, ultimately advancing to invasive carcinoma over approximately 15 years [15]. It has been reported that the incidence of IGBC in cholecystectomies performed for benign gallstone disease ranges from 0.2% to 2.9% [16]. Given that IGBC accounts for a non-negligible proportion of all GBC cases yet is often diagnosed only during or after surgery, this finding underscores the limitations inherent in current preoperative diagnostic pathways. Previous evidence indicates that even when stratified by stage, patients with IGBC have a poorer prognosis than those with NIGBC [9, 10]. This discrepancy may be attributed to factors such as intraoperative gallbladder perforation, residual tumor foci, and inflammatory adhesions associated with secondary surgeries. By systematically comparing the clinical, imaging, and pathological characteristics between the two cohorts, this study sought to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the preoperative missed diagnosis of IGBC from multiple perspectives.

First, the background of stone-associated chronic inflammation serves as a critical clue. We observed a significantly higher co-existence rate of gallstones in the IGBC group compared to the NIGBC group (91.9% vs. 53.8%, P < 0.001), which aligns with the reported association rate of 70%–98% in the literature [17]. While persistent inflammatory stimulation is known to increase carcinogenic risk [18], its "benign phenotype" on imaging and in symptomatology can obscure underlying malignancy. Specifically, cholecystitis often presents with diffuse, homogeneous wall thickening, whereas GBC tends to exhibit focal, irregular thickening [19]. This overlap in sonographic presentations complicates differentiation. Furthermore, clinical cues such as recurrent "stone-related" abdominal pain often steer the diagnostic focus towards benign conditions [20]. Our study also revealed more "occult" laboratory profiles in IGBC patients, including higher ALB levels, higher HDL-C, a lower RAR, and a lower proportion of elevated CA19-9. These seemingly benign serological signals further expand the blind spots for preoperative identification.

Second, the pathological morphology and stage distribution provide a framework for understanding preoperative missed diagnoses. Contrary to the conventional impression [21], IGBCs are not predominantly early-stage tumors: 72.6% of cases in our cohort were already at T2 stage or higher upon diagnosis, a finding consistent with previous reports [13]. Notably, the T-stage distribution differed significantly between the two groups (P = 0.007), with IGBC showing a higher proportion of early-stage cases and NIGBC more advanced-stage cases. This suggests that the relatively earlier stage and limited invasion depth of IGBC may contribute to its poor preoperative radiological conspicuity. More crucially, a disparity exists in the predominant growth patterns. The infiltrative type was most common in IGBC (77.4%), whereas the NIGBC group showed a higher prevalence of the intraluminal growing type (48.7%) or mixed type (23.1%). Infiltrative tumors often manifest as diffuse wall thickening on ultrasound, making them highly susceptible to being mistaken for cholecystitis [19]. In contrast, intraluminal growing types typically present as polypoid or mass-like protrusions, which are more readily detectable by imaging [17]. Consequently, the imaging recognizability dictated by the growth pattern [22], together with the superficial invasion of IGBC, collectively constitute the key pathological basis for its preoperative missed diagnosis.

Moreover, we observed differences in molecular pathology between the two groups. The IGBC group had a lower proportion of cases with high Ki-67 expression (≥ 30%) (67.7% vs. 83.3%, P = 0.031) and a less frequent occurrence of strong Topo II expression (2 +/3 +) (P = 0.005). Ki-67 is a marker of cellular proliferative activity, and Topo II is involved in DNA topology control and damage repair; their relatively reduced expression is often associated with weaker invasiveness and relatively enhanced genomic stability [23, 24]. It is tempting to speculate that this low-proliferation, low-activity profile might correspond radiologically to a lack of typical malignant features, such as obvious destructive borders or significant neovascularization [25], thereby reducing the detectability and raising fewer red flags on conventional ultrasound. It should be noted that the above P-values are not adjusted for multiple comparisons and the sample size is limited; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory.

From the perspective of diagnostic pathways and resource allocation, none of the IGBC cases (0/62) received a "malignant suspicion" on ultrasound, in stark contrast to 80.8% (63/78) in the NIGBC group. This highlights the insufficient sensitivity of routine ultrasound for IGBC. The IGBC group underwent MRCP more frequently (40.3% vs. 14.1%, P < 0.001); however, the strength of MRCP lies primarily in depicting the biliary tree, while its capability for resolving soft-tissue details of the gallbladder wall is limited [26]. Conversely, CE-CT/CE-MR was performed almost exclusively in the NIGBC group (94.9% vs. 19.4%, P < 0.001). These advanced modalities offer superior soft-tissue contrast [27] but are typically triggered by a "positive" or suspicious ultrasound finding, partly due to concerns regarding contrast-related risks [28] and cost-effectiveness. This ultrasound-first, stratified diagnostic pathway objectively exacerbates the risk of missing IGBC and underscores that enhancing the discriminative power at the ultrasound level is a crucial key to optimizing the clinical diagnostic pathway.

In this context, radiomics may offer a new avenue worth exploring for preoperative identification of this “occult malignant phenotype”. The XGB model developed based on ultrasound images in this study showed promising performance in the validation set (AUC = 0.865, accuracy = 0.833), and the decision curve analysis suggested a potentially high clinical net benefit. These findings imply that quantifying multi‑scale, multi‑dimensional texture and intensity features might help uncover subtle clues of micro‑heterogeneity from images presenting a “benign masquerade”.

In addition to radiomics, gallstones (OR = 9.484) and growth pattern (OR = 0.230) were identified as independent clinical predictors of IGBC. The clinical model based on these two factors achieved a validation AUC of 0.83. After combining the clinical predictors with the Rad‑score, the UNITE‑LR model showed an improved validation AUC of 0.860, outperforming the clinical‑only model but comparable to the radiomics‑only XGB model (AUC = 0.865), suggesting that radiomics features may have captured key discriminatory information that largely overlapped with the clinical variables. The combined model demonstrated good calibration (Brier score < 0.25) and a high net benefit. Collectively, the radiomics model may provide an important technical support to address the current diagnostic blind spots and potentially reduce missed diagnoses of IGBC.

At the feature level, the XGB-based radiomics model primarily relied on intensity distribution skewness in the logarithmic and wavelet domains (e.g., logarithm_firstorder_Skewness, wavelet-HHL_firstorder_Skewness), run-length non-uniformity (wavelet-LHH_glrlm_RunLengthNonUniformityNormalized), and zone-size gray-level non-uniformity (wavelet-HLH_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized). This suggests that complex edge information, asymmetry of gray-level distribution, and local scale heterogeneity in the frequency domain may serve as key spatial microstructural signatures for distinguishing IGBC from NIGBC, showing reasonable consistency with the infiltrative and asymmetric texture patterns of IGBC on ultrasound images.

SHAP analysis further revealed that higher feature values generally pushed predictions toward IGBC, whereas low-importance features (e.g., wavelet-LHH_glrlm_RunEntropy) had SHAP values close to zero, implying that LASSO selection may effectively capture high-contribution variables and reduce multi-dimensional noise, thereby enhancing model interpretability and generalizability. Notably, asymmetry of frequency-domain intensity distribution and micro-heterogeneity may be the core drivers of IGBC discrimination, while non-uniformity of local texture and zone size provides complementary spatial structural information. The quantifiable and visualizable nature of positive and negative evidence for individual predictions helps improve model transparency, offers verifiable quantitative support for image interpretation, and may facilitate clinical risk communication and individualized decision-making.

The clinical implication is that for patients with gallstones and a background of chronic inflammation who present with diffuse gallbladder wall thickening on ultrasound without typical malignant morphology, vigilance for IGBC should be heightened. Following initial ultrasound screening and after rigorous external validation, a radiomics model could be introduced for secondary risk stratification. High-risk individuals could then undergo advanced imaging (e.g., CE‑CT/CE‑MR) or enter intensive follow‑up protocols, aiming for a more balanced trade‑off between cost, risk, and benefit.

This study has several limitations. First, the modest sample size without a priori power calculation limits the detection of small effects and may lead to Type II errors; therefore, all non‑significant findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. Second, multiple comparisons were performed without adjustment for P‑values; consequently, the results should be considered hypothesis‑generating. Third, we used a single 70/30 data split without repeated cross‑validation, which may overestimate the generalizability of the model. Fourth, both ultrasound examination and ROI segmentation are subject to operator dependence, and radiomic features are influenced by variations in acquisition parameters and equipment. Fifth, the lack of an external validation cohort limits the assessment of model generalizability, and the reported performance requires further validation in an external dataset. Sixth, our radiomics model distinguishes IGBC from NIGBC rather than IGBC from benign gallbladder disease, which represents the real missed‑diagnosis scenario; this limits the direct clinical applicability of the current model.

Conclusion

Preoperative ultrasound missed diagnosis of incidental gallbladder cancer (IGBC) may be associated with its predominantly infiltrative growth pattern, high coexistence with gallstones, subtle serological changes, and low proliferative marker expression, with gallstones and growth pattern identified as potential independent clinical predictors. The ultrasound‑based XGB radiomics model demonstrated good discriminative performance for distinguishing IGBC from non‑incidental GBC (validation AUC = 0.865), whereas the combined clinical‑radiomics model did not significantly improve performance, suggesting that radiomics features may have captured most of the key information. This model might serve as a non‑invasive adjunctive tool to raise preoperative suspicion of IGBC; however, external multicenter validation is required, and future studies should directly compare IGBC with benign gallbladder disease to better reflect real‑world missed‑diagnosis scenarios.
