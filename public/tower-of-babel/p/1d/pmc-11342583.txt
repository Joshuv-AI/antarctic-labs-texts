The role of reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent on the evaluation of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia in pediatric cyanotic heart disease: a diagnostic study in Indonesia
Authors: William Cheng, Piprim Basarah Yanuarso, Pustika Amalia Wahidiyat, Syarif Rohimi, Partini Pudjiastuti Trihono, Muzal Kadim, Dina Muktiarti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn light of prolonged hypoxia, children with cyanotic heart disase (CHD) are at a high risk of developing iron deficiency iron deficiency (ID) and iron deficiency anemia (IDA). Reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent (Ret-He) is a novel and dependable indicator for assessing iron status. However, there has been no previous study regarding cut-off value in pediatric CHD group. The purpose of this study is to assess the role of Ret-He and to establish cut-off points in the diagnosis of iron deficiency and IDA in pediatric cyanotic heart disease.MethodThis study was conducted in two tertiary hospitals in Jakarta, Indonesia. 59 children with CHD, aged 3 months to 18 years, were enrolled consecutively. To determine iron status, hematological parameters (hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin) and biochemical parameters for iron status (serum ferritin, transferrin saturation) were analysed and compared to Ret-He levels. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed for the Ret-He cut-off points for ID and IDA. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value were calculated for each cut-off point.ResultNormal iron status was identified in 27 (45.8%) subjects, ID in 8 (13.5%) subjects, and IDA 24 (40.7%) subjects. The ID cut-off value for Ret-He is 28.8 pg (sensitivity 75%, specificity 85.2%, PPV 60%, NPV 92%, and AUC 0.828) and the Ret-He cut-off point for IDA is 28.15 pg (sensitivity 75%, specificity 88.9%, PPV 85.7%, NPV 80%, and AUC 0.824). Hemoglobin should be used in conjunction with Ret-He. ID might be detected in this cohort with Ret-He 28.8 pg and hemoglobin > 16,5 g/dL. While Ret-He 28.15 pg or Ret-He 28.15–28.8 pg with hemoglobin 16.5 g/dL could be used to diagnose IDA.ConclusionThe reticulocyte hemolgobin equivalent could be utilised as an iron status parameter in pediatric CHD, with a cut-off value of 28.8 pg for ID and 28.15 pg for IDA.Keywords: Pediatric, Iron deficiency, Iron deficiency anemia, Cyanotic heart disease, Reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent (Ret-He)

Background

Iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) are the most common micronutrient problem worldwide, especially in developing countries [1]. Pediatric are the high-risk group for iron deficiency due to an imbalance between the production rate of red blood cells and iron storage in the body, specifically during the growth spurt [2, 3]. Based on World Health Organization (WHO) 2015, South East Asia has a higher risk of IDA prevalence on toddler (53.8%). The IDA prevalence in pediatric population in South East Asia is higher than in Africa, around 66% versus 60% [4]. Its prevalence in Indonesia was 40.5% among children under 5 years old and 47.2% among school-aged children [1]. Iron deficiency has short and long-term effects on psychomotor and cognitive development, and many of them are irreversible [1, 4].

The impact of IDA on certain groups such as children with cyanotic heart disease (CHD) may be greater. In pediatric CHD, secondary erythropoiesis as a form of adaptation to chronic hypoxia increases iron requirement continuously. Additionally, hepcidin suppression due to hypoxia and a higher risk of erythrocyte destruction also increases the risk of iron loss. The previous study stated that iron deficiency in the pediatric CHD population was 47.5% [5] and IDA was 52.2% [6]. Therefore, early diagnosis is important to reduce morbidity and mortality of pediatric cyanotic heart disease [5].

American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommendation of IDA diagnosis with the measurement of (1) ferritin and CRP, or (2) reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent (Ret-He), as an additional examination for hemoglobin levels [7]. Since serum ferritin is an acute phase reactant, it is necessary to support the validity of the result with other inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) [7]. While Ret-He represents the synthesis of hemoglobin precursors in the bone marrow and is not affected by inflammation, infection, malignancy, or chronic disease and therefore it is a reliable parameter of iron status []. The usage of Ret-He in healthy children and neonates has been widely applied [8, 9]. However, its use in pediatric CHD is still limited [10]. The objective of this study is to evaluate the application of Ret-He in pediatric CHD population and to determine cut-off off points in detection of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia.

Method

Study design and setting

This is a prospective cross-sectional diagnostic test study of Ret-He as a diagnostic parameter for iron deficiency and IDA on children, aged of 3 months until 18 years old, with CHD, from December 2021 to March 2022, at Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital and Harapan Kita Women and Children’s Hospital, Jakarta. Both hospitals are tertiary referral hospitals located in the capital of Indonesia.

Participants

Individuals who met age criteria and diagnosed with cyanotic heart disease in both hospitals, were approached and given information of the study. Research team explained the literature using written information and gave opportunity for potential subjects to ask questions. Informed written consent was obtainted from the parents and assent was obtained from children and adolescents.

Cyanotic heart disease was confirmed using echocardiography by pediatric cardiologists, marked by a significant right-to-left shunt and the mean daily oxygen saturation of all subjects was lower than 90%. The reference growth parameter is 2006 WHO curves for less than 5 years old (Z score) and the 2000 CDC curve for over 5 years old (the percentage of ideal body weight according to the Waterlow criteria), based on gender. According to these criteria, subjects will be categorized as follows: normal (with a Z score between − 2 and 2 SD for WHO or > 90% for Waterlow criteria); mild-moderate malnutrition (with a Z-score between − 3 to -2 SD for WHO or 70–90% for Waterlow criteria); and severe malnutrition (with a Z score below − 3SD for WHO or less than 70% for Waterlow criteria) [11].

The blood sample was taken consecutively at the Clinical Pathology Laboratory of Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital and Harapan Kita Women and Children’s Hospital. Subjects’ absence on test series, received iron supplementation or therapy during the last 3 months, and CRP levels ≧ 5 mg/L were excluded. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Universitas Indonesia.

Study size

Sample size calculation was based on an expected sensitivity of 80%, with a margin of error of 15% and type I error of 5% [12]. Prevalences of ID and IDA were 47.5% for ID [5] and 52.2% [6] for IDA. Therefore, we aimed for 59 subjects for acquiring the diagnostic parameters for this study.

Variables

Iron deficiency was defined by:

Hemoglobin level ≥ 16.5 g/dL.

Hemoglobin level ≥ 16.5 g/dL.

Serum ferritin < 12 ng/mL for < 5 years old subjects or < 15 ng/mL for ≥ 5 years old subjects, or transferrin saturation < 15% [13].

Serum ferritin < 12 ng/mL for < 5 years old subjects or < 15 ng/mL for ≥ 5 years old subjects, or transferrin saturation < 15% [13].

Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) < 80 fL or mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) < 27 pg. [14, 15]

Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) < 80 fL or mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) < 27 pg. [14, 15]

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) was defined by hemoglobin levels < 16.5 g/dL [15] and met the criteria for iron deficiency.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) version 26 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA). Univariate analysis was used for frequency of data distribution. Normality study used Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The normal data distribution was presented by mean and standard deviation, whereas, abnormal data distribution by median, minimum, and maximum. One-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed numerical data and Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric test was used for abnormal data distribution. Statistic’s significance used p < 0.05. The ability of Ret-He discrimination in determining iron status was expressed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Iron profile of study subjects

The median Ret-He for all subjects was 28.7 pg (14.4 to 36.5 pg). The mean value of Ret-He in the group with normal iron status was 31.6 (SD 3.0) pg and decreased according to the level of iron status, 25.3 (NB 4.7) pg in the iron deficiency group, and 23.4 (SB 6.4) pg in the IDA group. Post hoc Mann-Whitney analysis found a statistically significant difference between the mean Ret-He in the normal iron status in iron deficiency group (p = 0.001) and normal iron status in the IDA group (p < 0.001). However, the mean difference between the iron deficiency and IDA group was not statistically significant (p = 0.428).

Receiver operating characteristic curve showed Ret-He cut-off of < 28.8 pg has an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.828 (95%CI 0.664–0.934) for iron deficiency without anemia as shown in Fig. 3. Discussion

Iron deficiency anemia is a significant risk factor for morbidity and mortality in pediatric CHD because of its relation to worsening hyperviscosity conditions [15, 16]. It increases the risk of cerebrovascular events, cyanotic spell, endothelial dysfunction, anorexia, and physical activity disturbance [5]. Pediatric CHD have a higher risk to develop iron deficiency and IDA due to chronic hypoxia could stimulate secondary erythrocytosis, suppress hepcidin, and increase erythrocyte destruction [9].

From this study, the proportion of pediatric CHD with iron deficiency without anemia and IDA were 54.2% (13.5% iron deficiency and 40.7% IDA). The prevalence of iron deficiency without anemia in pediatric CHD aged 6–48 months in the previous study was 63.6% [17]. Another study stated that the prevalence of iron deficiency was 47.06% in the age group of 0–12 months [16]. The most common type of CHD in this study was tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), around 32.8%, as also found in a previous study [14–16]. Our least common CHD in accordance with the rare incidence rate [18]. The proportion of gender in this study was equal, boys and girls ratio of 1.1:1. This was in accordance with the previous study stating that there was no gender predisposition to CHD [5, 18].

The majority of study subjects were 0–2 years old age group (49.2%). A study on the survival of CHD reported 90% of mortality occurred in the first five years of life [19]. Cohort studies in pediatrics and adults reported that the hazard ratio in CHD was 65.07 (95% CI 53.39–76.89) [20]. The relationship between age and iron deficiency and IDA could not be drawn in this study due to the wide range of age distribution. Preschool children and adolescents are prone to iron deficiency because of higher iron demand for growth [2, 21]. Prematurity profile on this study was less useful due to the wide range of age. It may serve as a risk factor during the first six months of life [22].

Children with CHD has a higher risk for malnutrition [5]. Stunting was a significant nutrition problem in this study. This result was in accordance with the previous study, with a prevalence of 60% [5], and 68% [23]. Chronic hypoxia was also a contributing factor to disrupting linear growth [24, 25]. Iron deficiency affects the synthesis of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein (IGFBP-1), which facilitates growth [25]. Children with congenital heart diseases mostly had mild to moderate malnutrition, and acute malnutrition significantly happened to CHD (45.83%) and all types of CHD (74.1%) [26]. The calories needed were increased to 20–50% normal need for age, inability to fulfill leads to acute malnutrition [27, 28]. The previous study showed a relationship between acute malnutrition with IDA (OR 2.89, 95%CI 1.45–5.76) [29].

The previous study stated that early increment of iron storage (compensated secondary erythropoiesis) was marked by increasing hemoglobin, hematocrit, and erythrocyte levels. This study demonstrated the presence of compensated secondary erythropoiesis, as seen by the higher mean hemoglobin level in the iron deficiency without anemia group (20.2 g/dL) compared to the normal iron status group (17.1 g/dL). Subsequently, as the progression occured, uncompensated secondary erythropoiesis was followed by decreased hemoglobin and hematocrit levels [30]. In this study, uncompensated secondary erythropoiesis seen on the mean hemoglobin level on IDA was 13.6 ± 1.88 g/dL and mean hematocrit 44.73 ± 7.92.

The utilization of erythrocyte index (MCV, MCH, MCHC, and RDW) to assess iron deficiency in pediatric CHD had conflicting results in previous studies. MCV and RDW are stable parameters on secondary polycythemia due to chronic hypoxia. MCV < 80 fL was used as a cut-off value for the progression of iron depletion in pediatric CHD [6, 15, 30]. In contrast to another study, MCV could be influenced by secondary erythropoiesis, which physiologically increased the production of young erythrocytes with a greater volume [14]. Therefore, their usage should be combined (MCV < 80 fL and MCH < 27 pg) [17, 30]. This study showed a lower mean value of MCV and MCH in all iron status groups, in accordance with a previous case-control study on pediatric CHD [5].

Serum ferritin is limited as a marker of iron deficiency as it is an acute phase reactant which will be increased in inflammation, chronic infection, and other diseases [31, 32]. Due to this reason, serum ferritin level may vary in IDA, necessitating the use of other hematological (hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH) and biochemistry (transferrin saturation) parameters [15]. Specifically, the application with transferrin saturation was suggested. The distinction between iron depletion and iron deficiency could be made based on the decrease in transferrin saturation, as observed in a previous study [10, 18]. Therefore, this study utilized both markers as indicator based on the fulfillment of one of the criteria.

The previous study stated that Ret-He is a stable parameter as it is not influenced by inflammation, malignancy, or chronic disease [2, 33]. Its usage on healthy neonates and pediatric showed a good diagnostic value [8, 9]. The cut-off value of Ret-He could be influenced by several factors, such as race and age [34]. Previous studies stated the Ret-He cut-off for iron deficiency without anemia differs between age groups, such as 29 pg for 3–4 months (AUC 0.86; sensitivity 85%; specificity 73%) [9], 28.55 pg for 6–24 months (AUC 0.819; sensitivity 76.6%; specificity 78.4%) [13], and 28.5 pg for 6–16 years (sensitivity 43.5%; specificity 91.1%) [2]. The majority of participants in this study were between the ages of 0 and 2 years (49.2%), so the ID cut-off of 28.8 pg without anemia was within range.

A study of Ret-He in Indonesia to determine iron status on healthy pediatric aged 6–18 years old had done with a cut-off value of 28.9 pg for ID without anemia (sensitivity 78.9%; specificity 56.2%) and 27 pg for IDA (sensitivity 75%; specificity 80%) [34]. The AUC for ID without anemia in this previous study was inferior with a cut-off of 28.9 pg (0.801) versus a cut-off of 28.8 pg (0.824) in this study. The current research exhibits greater sensitivity and specificity for ID without anemia in comparison to the previous study. Moreover, the AUC for IDA in the previous study was inferior with a cut-off of 27 pg (0.757) versus a cut-off of 28.15 pg (0.824), also the sensitivity and specificity due to the difference in anemia definition between studies. The hemoglobin levels in previous study used WHO criteria based on age, whereas in this study used hemoglobin levels on CHD (Hb < 16.5 g/dL) [15].

Although there are differences in the cut-off value of hemoglobin levels, this study found that the Ret-He values in the pediatric CHD population with lower hemoglobin levels (Hb < 16.5 g/dL) also had lower Ret-He values. The mean value of Ret-He in the normal iron status group was 31.6 pg and significantly lower in the IDA group at 23.4 pg. This mean difference was statistically and clinically significant. However, the mean value of Ret-He in the iron deficiency without anemia was 25.3 pg compared to the IDA group 23.4 pg, so that it was clinically and statistically not significant (p = 0.428). Therefore, Ret-He could not be used as a single parameter to differentiate pediatric CHD with iron deficiency without anemia and IDA; instead, it should be used in combination with hemoglobin. This result corroborates the prior research that Ret-He alone is insufficient to differentiate between IDA and iron deficiency without anemia [35]. Nevertheless, this outcome differed from another study that indicated that Ret-He could serve as a single parameter for IDA and iron deficiency without anemia, without taking into account other iron parameters [36].

Strength and limitation

There were several strengths of this study. To the best knowledge of the authors this was the first study to investigate the role of Ret-He in the evaluation of iron deficiency and IDA in pediatric CHD between the ages of 3 months and 18 years. Second, this study excluded some possible confounders in the evaluation of iron status, such as CRP ≧ 5 mg/dL and more than 3 months old.

There were also several limitations of this study. First, there was no universally recognized cut-off for hemoglobin levels in children with CHD across the globe. In this study, we applied a cut-off of hemoglobin less than 16.5 g/dL, which might be perceived relatively high [15]. Consequently, the use of a high cut-off level of hemoglobin may lead to overdiagnosis of IDA. Second, there was no peripheral blood smear for erythrocyte index confirmation, used to reduce the risk of error in automatic blood sample processing. Finally, as a cross-sectional diagnostic test, chronic hypoxia was evaluated on recruitment of the study subjects (daily oxygen saturation < 90%), and no differentiation of hypoxia severity. It would be beneficial to conduct a larger sample of diagnostic study in order to gather additional information regarding the use of Ret-He in this particular population.

Conclusion

Reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent is a good diagnostic parameter to evaluate iron deficiency and IDA in pediatric CHD. The usage of Ret-He should be accompanied by hemoglobin. In pediatric CHD, iron deficiency without anemia could be diagnosed with Ret-He < 28.8 pg with hemoglobin ≥ 16,5 g/dL. While IDA could be diagnosed with Ret-He < 28.15 pg or Ret-He 28.15-28,8 pg with hemoglobin < 16,5 g/dL. Further studies should be considered for determining the Ret-He cut-off in several age groups and different hypoxia severity.
