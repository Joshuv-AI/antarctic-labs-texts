The association of plasma testosterone level and progression of diabetic nephropathy in male

Published: 2025

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a common and serious complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), contributing significantly to the global burden of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. It remains one of the most severe long - term complications of DM and a major cause of mortality. DN affects approximately 20–40% of individuals with DM worldwide, highlighting its widespread impact and significance as a public health challenge. As one of the leading causes of CKD, DN poses a major challenge to healthcare systems worldwide and markedly impacts the patient’s quality of life [2, 3]. Although DM management has improved, the prevalence of DN is still increasing, and deaths attributed to diabetic kidney disease (DKD) have risen by 94% in people with DM over recent decades [4]. These findings highlight the importance of elucidating novel risk factors and mechanisms underlying its progression.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding the impact of sex differences on DN, particularly given the observed disparities in prevalence, progression, and outcomes between men and women. Numerous studies [5, 6] have revealed variations in the prevalence, progression, and outcomes of DN between men and women. Epidemiological evidence [7] suggests that men with diabetes are more likely to develop DN and experience faster disease progression than women. This gender disparity may be influenced by a combination of biological, hormonal, and lifestyle factors [8]. Testosterone, the primary male sex hormone, has been implicated in the pathophysiology of DN, potentially contributing to renal damage through mechanisms such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered glomerular hemodynamics [9].

Although several studies [9, 10] have investigated the role of sex hormones in CKD, the relationship between plasma testosterone levels and DN progression in men has not been clearly elucidated. Understanding this association could provide new insights into DN and identify testosterone as a potential biomarker to guide its clinical management and treatment. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the link between plasma testosterone levels and the progression of DN in male patients, focusing on clinical and biochemical outcomes. The findings may contribute to a deeper understanding of sex-specific factors influencing DN and offer directions for personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethic statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yuechi County People’s Hospital (KYLL-2023-025) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Despite its retrospective design, informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study participants selection

This retrospective cohort study collected electronic medical records between January 2021 and January 2024 from the People’s Hospital of Yuechi County and the Third Hospital of Mianyang, focusing on male patients with T2DM, with or without DN. In total, 347 participants met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, comprising 182 patients with DN and 165 patients without DN (Fig. 1).

Inclusion criteria: 1, Male patients aged 18 to 95 years. 2, Diagnosed with T2DM for at least 3 years. 3, Confirmed diagnosis of DN based on the histopathological evidence. 4, Plasma samples for testosterone measurement should be collected between 8:00 and 10:00 AM. Evidence indicates that serum testosterone levels peak in the morning and show relatively less variation across age groups at this time; thus, morning measurement is recommended to minimize the impact of diurnal fluctuations on clinical assessment [11]. 5, Availability of complete clinical data for key covariates, including age, BMI, blood pressure, and HbA1c.

Exclusion criteria: 1, Female patients. 2, Participants with primary renal diseases other than DN. 3, Patients with a history of malignancy, severe liver disease, or any other conditions that could significantly interfere with testosterone metabolism (e.g., thyroid dysfunction, Cushing’s syndrome, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or long-term use of glucocorticoids or opioids). 4, Patients receiving testosterone replacement therapy or medications that affect testosterone levels. 5, Participants with incomplete clinical data.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records and included demographic details such as age, sex, and diabetes duration; anthropometric measurements, including BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast to analyze biochemical parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and glucose levels. HbA1c levels were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), while the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was assessed from the first morning urine sample. All laboratory tests were performed in the hospital’s clinical laboratory following standardized protocols to minimize measurement errors.

Testosterone assay

Serum testosterone levels were measured using a chemiluminescence-based assay kit (Siemens, Germany; 10995707) on the Atellica fully automated chemiluminescent immunoassay analyzer, with probe wash solution 1 as an auxiliary reagent. All assays were performed in the standardized clinical laboratory of the Medical Examination Department of Yuechi County People’s Hospital by certified laboratory physicians in strict accordance with operational protocols. To ensure assay accuracy and reliability, chemiluminescent quality control materials (Shanghai Kunlai Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used to validate reagents and instruments prior to formal testing. All quality control indicators met the required standards, confirming the reliability of the results.

Outcomes and stages of DKD

Patients diagnosed with DN usually undergo routine outpatient reviews approximately every three months, although the exact timing may vary among individuals. For this study, prognostic outcomes were assessed using clinical and laboratory data collected 12 months after diagnosis. A favorable outcome was defined as stable disease without progression of laboratory parameters to a more advanced stage over the 12-month follow-up period. An adverse outcome was defined as disease-related laboratory progression to a more advanced stage or death occurring during the follow-up period. Laboratory parameters were classified according to the KDIGO [12](Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) guidelines for assessing and staging DKD, based on a combination of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and persistent albuminuria (urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, UACR). The classification was as follows. GFR Categories (Based on Kidney Function): (1) G1: GFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m² (normal or high) (2) G2: GFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m² (mild decrease) (3) G3a: GFR 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m² (mild to moderate decrease) (4) G3b: GFR 30–44 mL/min/1.73 m² (moderate to severe decrease) (5) G4: GFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m² (severe decrease) (6) G5: GFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² or dialysis (end-stage renal disease, ESRD). The persistent albuminuria categories are classified as A1(UACR ≤ 30 mg/g), A2(30–299 mg/g), and A3(≥ 300 mg/g).

Statistical analysis

SPSS statistical software (version 26.0; IBM, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between groups were compared using Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Skewed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range), and differences between groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are expressed as count (percentage), and the χ2 test was used to evaluate differences between groups. Logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the relationship between clinical parameters and renal dysfunction, with effect sizes expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The “RMS” package in R software (version 3.5) was used to construct the nomogram, and the “forestplot” package was used to draw forest plots.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 357 participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled in this study. Among these, 165 individuals were diagnosed with DN, while the remaining 182 were categorized as non-diabetic nephropathy (non-DN). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. The median ages were 62 years in the non-DN and 65 years in DN group. Consistent with previous reports [13], the serum cystatin C (Cyc) levels and UACR were significantly higher in DN patients compared with the non-DN group, whereas eGFR levels were markedly lower (p < 0.05 for all). Additionally, DBP was higher in DN patients than in the non-DN group, consistent with findings from other studies [14, 15] suggesting that DBP may serve as a risk factor for DN development or progression. However, no significant difference in plasma testosterone levels was observed between the DN and non-DN groups (p >0.05). This result suggests that plasma testosterone levels may have limited predictive value as a biomarker for DN progression, indicating only a minor role in disease prediction or progression monitoring.

Plasma testosterone and DKD stage

According to previous reports [9], male patients with DM are more likely to progress to DKD. However, in our study, no significant differences in plasma testosterone levels were observed between male patients with DN and those without DN. Therefore, we hypothesized that plasma testosterone levels might be associated with DKD stages and therefore conducted a correlation analysis. Table 2 summarizes the baseline characteristics of patients with DN. Among these patients, variables including age, DBP, SBP, plasma testosterone, TG, HDL, LDL, Cyc, UACR, eGFR, HbA1c, and BMI showed statistically significant differences across DN stages. In addition, we examined changes in testosterone levels with age in DN patients. Notably, testosterone levels did not exhibit a significant age-related decline, which may indicate the presence of sex hormone dysregulation in these patients (sFigure 1A).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of the relationship between DKD prognosis

Tables 3 and 4 summarize the results of the univariate and multivariate analyses examining the relationship between DKD prognosis and various clinical variables. To enhance the clarity, a forest plot was used to visually present the outcomes of both univariate and multivariate regression analyses (Fig. 2). Variables that reached statistical significance in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate regression analysis for further evaluation. In the univariate analysis, the odds ratios (ORs) for adverse outcomes varied across different clinical variables. Specifically, the ORs for adverse outcomes per one standard deviation (SD) increase in the following variables were as follows: Age (OR = 1.109, 95% CI: 1.058–1.16), SBP, (OR = 1.187, 95% CI: 1.067–1.348), DBP, (OR = 1.153, 95% CI: 1.105–1.258), testosterone (OR = 5.314, 95% CI: 2.958–10.115), Cyc, (OR = 4.122, 95% CI: 2.217–7.598), UACR, (OR = 1.029, 95% CI: 1.016–1.113), and eGFR, (OR = 0.909, 95% CI: 0.884–0.936). However, when multivariate regression analysis was performed, only a subset of these variables remained significant as independent predictors of adverse outcomes in patients with DN. Specifically, the following variables retained statistical significance: Age (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00–1.18), SBP (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.00–1.15), DBP (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.22), testosterone (OR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.25–8.87), Cyc (OR = 3.79, 95% CI: 1.04–10.79), and UACR (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03). These findings highlight that the identified variables are independent predictors of adverse outcomes and may play an important role in determining the prognosis of patients with DN. Since testosterone concentration is strongly age-dependent, we performed stratified analyses to minimize the potential confounding effect of age. The results showed that testosterone was consistently associated with increased risk across all age strata. Notably, higher ORs were observed in the Q1 and Q2 subgroups (sTable 1).

Nomogram prediction model

To facilitate practical application in clinical settings, we developed two nomogram prediction models to estimate the risk of adverse outcomes in male patients with DN. These models are presented in Fig. 3. Model 1(Fig. 3A), consists of the following variables: age, SBP, DBP, UACR, and Cyc. These factors were identified as significant risk factors contributing to adverse outcomes in male patients with DN [4, 15, 16]. The predictive performance of Model 1 was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), which showed high predictive accuracy with an AUC of 0.885 (95% CI: 0.852–0.999), as illustrated in Fig. 4A. Based on Model 1, Model 2 was developed with the addition of testosterone as an extra variable (Fig. 3B). This extended model showed improved predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.923 (95% CI: 0.891–0.999), as shown in Fig. 4B. In addition, the bootstrap method was applied for random resampling to reassess Model 2’s predictive performance. The results showed that, in the randomly selected validation cohort, the AUC was 0.910, confirming the strong clinical utility of the model for predicting adverse outcomes in DN (sFigure 1B). These findings suggest that testosterone may provide incremental predictive value beyond the existing risk factors and further improve the model’s ability to predict adverse outcomes in male patients with DN.

Discussion

In this study, we found that while plasma testosterone levels did not differ significantly between male patients with and without DN (Table 1), they varied significantly across the different stages of DKD (Table 2). This finding suggests that plasma testosterone may not directly determine the onset of DN but is likely associated with disease progression. To further evaluate its clinical significance, we performed univariate and multivariate regression analyses and constructed a nomogram model to assess its predictive value for adverse outcomes in DN patients. The results demonstrated that plasma testosterone levels are an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in DN and hold significant potential for clinical risk assessment and prognostic assessment.

Testosterone plays a role in the onset and progression of DKD. However, its role in the development and progression of DN is complex and not yet fully understood. Furthermore, its utility as a biomarker for DN remains inconclusive and requires further investigation. Zhao et al. [17] conducted a bi-directional Mendelian randomization study in men and women, which suggested that testosterone may contribute to worse kidney function in men. Sandeep Dhindsa et al. also suggest that patients with DKD tend to have lower testosterone levels [18]. In addition, free testosterone has been considered an independent predictor of the progression from severe proteinuria and early-stage DN to ESRD. Interestingly, a study published in a Science sub-journal reported that dihydrotestosterone may mediate oxidative stress and apoptosis in proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) under hyperglycemic conditions via the transcription factor HNF4A, thereby contributing to more severe disease manifestations [9]. Consistent with these findings, our study also indicates that plasma testosterone may hold potential clinical value in evaluating the prognosis of DN. Notably, a cross-sectional analysis evaluated the prevalence of low testosterone levels (LST) in male with diabetes and their association with microvascular complications, these results showed no significant difference in the prevalence of LST between patients with retinopathy and those with nephropathy. However, a significant association was found in patients with DN [19]. This finding suggests that LST may exert a pivotal role in the development of DN by disrupting the renal microvasculature, leading to impairment of the glomerular filtration barrier and alterations in the tubulointerstitial microenvironment, thereby accelerating disease progression. Conversely, another study [20] demonstrated that exogenous testosterone administration significantly increased TNF production during renal obstruction, promoted tubular cell apoptosis, and exacerbated tubulointerstitial fibrosis, thereby aggravating kidney injury. Therefore, the role of testosterone in renal damage remains controversial.

In our study, we found that there was no significant difference in testosterone levels between patients with DN and without DN. Interestingly, testosterone levels varied significantly across different stages of DN, with the highest levels observed in stage G5. Multivariate analysis and logistic regression further confirmed that testosterone was an independent predictor of poor prognosis in DN (OR = 3.13, 95%CI [1.25–8.87]). These findings appear to contradict previous studies, as several studies have indicated that testosterone deficiency may be associated with higher mortality in patients with CKD [21, 22]. However, some studies have suggested that mortality in CKD is not associated with sex hormones, and that neither LST nor testosterone replacement therapy shows a significant correlation with improved prognosis. Even in cases where LST has been linked to higher mortality, this association seems to be limited to specific stages of CKD [10, 23]. In fact, most studies identifying LST as a prognostic risk factor involve patients whose testosterone levels were within the hypogonadal range (< 2.3–3.0 ng/mL). Moreover, one study revealed that testosterone levels above 4 ng/ml may also pose risk [24]. In our cohort, patients in G1 and G2 had mean testosterone levels below those of age-matched healthy individuals (3 ng/ml), whereas only G5 patients exhibited elevated levels(>4 ng/ml). These findings suggest a U-shaped dynamic effect of testosterone in men with DN: early LST may contribute to DN onset and progression, while advanced DN may disrupt sex hormone balance, leading to subsequent rises in testosterone. In the mid-to-late stages of CKD, renal failure impairs the clearance of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), leading to elevated circulating levels of LH and FSH. This dysregulation disrupts the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, promoting testosterone production while simultaneously increasing SHBG levels, thereby contributing to the phenomenon of elevated serum testosterone [25]. In addition, because luteinizing hormone receptors (LHRs) are also expressed on testicular Leydig cells, LH can activate membrane-associated G proteins and enhance adenylate cyclase (cAMP) activity, further stimulating androgen synthesis [26]. Moreover, patients with CKD exhibit Leydig cell resistance to LST, resulting in suboptimal responsiveness to gonadotropins. This persistent stimulation may further upregulate SHBG synthesis, a relationship confirmed by a cohort study showing that SHBG levels increase in association with CKD progression, following a characteristic J-shaped curve. In advanced CKD, reduced renal clearance also favors accumulation of SHBG-bound testosterone [27], contributing to elevated measured testosterone levels. Collectively, these abnormalities in HPG axis regulation and impaired renal clearance may explain the paradoxical association between high testosterone levels and DN progression observed in our study, partly accounting for why higher testosterone appeared to be a risk factor. Furthermore, among previous studies, only one investigated the relationship between testosterone levels and CKD prognosis in the context of T2DM, and it found no association between testosterone levels and CKD outcomes [10]. Other studies primarily involved mixed CKD populations, which may also contribute to the paradoxical findings in our study. Additionally, whether the study participants were undergoing hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis could influence testosterone levels, as research has shown that patients on dialysis tend to have significantly lower testosterone levels compared with non-dialysis patients [28]. Our study differs from existing research that identifies LST as a risk factor for DKD in terms of cohort selection criteria. For example, the study by Wang et al. [29] did not exclude patients undergoing steroid hormone therapy, which may influence sex hormone levels, and the dialysis status of their participants was not specified. Their results also indicated variation in ORs across testosterone quartiles, with a decreased protective effect observed in Q4 compared with Q3. Similarly, Sandeep Dhindsa et al. [18] did not account for dialysis treatment among participants. In contrast, our research explicitly excluded individuals receiving steroid therapy and focused specifically on DN patients who had not undergone dialysis. The use of steroid therapy and dialysis history may represent significant confounding factors in evaluating the relationship between testosterone levels and CKD outcomes. Nevertheless, the underlying reasons for these differences remain unclear. Testosterone plays a multifaceted role [30, 31] in DM by influencing insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, inflammation, and pancreatic β-cell function. In DN, it contributes to oxidative stress, inflammation, proximal tubular epithelial cell dysfunction, and renal damage through androgen receptor signaling [9, 32]. The dual effects of testosterone underscore its complex involvement in the onset and progression of DM and DN and may also be one of the factors contributing to the observed differences in this study.

In this study, we observed findings consistent with previous reports, identifying in Age (OR = 1.10, [1.00-1.18]), SBP (OR = 1.07, [1.00-1.15]), DBP (1.15, [1.01–1.22]), Cyc (OR = 3.79, [1.04–10.79]), and UACR (OR = 1.02, [1.00-1.03]) as significant risk factors for DN progression in male patients with DN. However, we observed that testosterone was consistently associated with adverse outcomes of DN across all age groups, but the OR appeared to be higher in individuals younger than 65 years. This finding suggests that differences in endocrine sensitivity among populations may influence the predictive value of testosterone, and that in elderly patients, age-related physiological changes could attenuate its effect on adverse outcomes. Nevertheless, our results indicate that testosterone may serve as a risk factor for poor prognosis of DN across all age strata. Cyc, as a sensitive marker for eGFR [33], is elevated in cases of renal impairment and has emerged as the most significant predictor of poor prognosis in DN group. Previous research [34–36] has confirmed the association between Cyc and kidney injury, DN staging, and microalbuminuria. However, this study did not observe a direct correlation between testosterone and Cyc levels (data not shown). Notably, we developed a nomogram predictive model for poor prognosis in DN by combining Cyc with other risk factors, including testosterone. This model demonstrated excellent predictive value (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that Cyc, as a well-established risk factor for DN, can be integrated with other known risk factors to construct effective clinical predictive models, which could provide valuable guidance for the diagnosis and management of DN.

Moreover, our study population was predominantly male, even though sex differences in DN with T2DM have been extensively documented. Diabetes disrupts sex hormone homeostasis: compared to non-diabetic men, male diabetic patients exhibit lower testosterone and higher estradiol levels, whereas diabetic women show the opposite pattern, with elevated testosterone and reduced estradiol [32, 37]. These alterations may exacerbate proteinuria and renal tubular fibrosis, thereby increasing the risk of renal complications. SHBG has been identified by Gambineri and colleagues as a key factor contributing to circulating sex hormone disparities between men and women [38]. In the context of diabetes, endocrine and metabolic dysregulation alters SHBG concentrations in adipose tissue, and heightened oxidative stress may promote the conversion of testosterone and androstenedione into estradiol and estrone. These mechanisms likely contribute both to hypogonadism in diabetic patients and to the sex specific-hormonal differences observed in DN [39–44]. Therefore, clinical management and future research should closely monitor changes in testosterone, estradiol, and SHBG levels and their interrelationships in both male and female patients with DN.

This study highlights key baseline characteristics, risk factors, and predictive models for adverse outcomes in male patients with DN. The findings underscore the significance of SBP, DBP, Cyc, UACR, and testosterone in DN prognosis and offer practical tools for clinical application. Further research is warranted to validate these results and explore the mechanistic underpinnings of testosterone’s role in DKD progression.

Potential clinical value

In the nomogram developed in this study, patients were stratified into three risk categories based on total points and the predicted probability. Low-risk patients (total points < 100, predicted probability < 0.30) were typically younger (< 55 years), with normal or mildly elevated blood pressure (SBP < 130 mmHg), UACR < 300 mg/g, normal testosterone levels, and lower cystatin C levels. These patients had a low likelihood of disease progression and could be managed with routine follow-up. Intermediate-risk patients (total points 100–160, predicted probability 0.30–0.60) were commonly middle-aged to older individuals (55–70 years) with elevated blood pressure (SBP 130–150 mmHg or DBP ≥ 85 mmHg), UACR 300–1000 mg/g, slightly abnormal testosterone levels, and moderate cystatin C levels. These patients showed a substantially increased risk of progression, requiring shorter follow-up intervals and more intensive management of blood pressure, glycemic control, and proteinuria. High-risk patients (total points > 160, predicted probability ≥ 0.60) were predominantly elderly (> 70 years), with markedly elevated blood pressure (SBP ≥ 150 mmHg), UACR > 1000 mg/g, markedly abnormal testosterone levels, and high cystatin C. This group faced a very high risk of adverse renal outcomes, necessitating close monitoring, aggressive intervention, and, where appropriate, early planning for dialysis or kidney transplantation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the study cohort was relatively small, limiting the generalizability of the results. Larger, prospective studies are needed to validate the predictive models and confirm the identified risk factors. Secondly, this study did not employ a longitudinal design, which restricts the ability to explore causal relationships between testosterone and DN outcomes. Third, the lack of longitudinal data on testosterone dynamics further limits assessment of its role in disease progression. Fourth, the nomogram model was validated only with the bootstrap method and lacked external cohort validation, which may limit its robustness. Fifth, potential drug-related influences should be considered, as DN patients often require antihypertensive therapy with RAS inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors, which may interfere with the endocrine system and affect testosterone levels. Finally, this study did not include data on other relevant hormones such as SHBG, dihydrotestosterone, and E2, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of testosterone’s role in DN and its interactions with other sex hormones.

Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and investigating the mechanisms linking testosterone to DN. Incorporating emerging biomarkers and genetic data into predictive models could further enhance their accuracy and clinical utility. Moreover, since stepwise regression was used for variable selection—which carries a risk of overfitting—penalized regression approaches such as LASSO may provide more robust alternatives and should be considered in future studies.
