The effect of fatigue on kinetic and kinematic variables in individuals with flexible flatfoot during gait: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohamad Ali Biabangard, Reza Rajabi, Ali Asghar Maleki, Seyed Hamed Mousavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFlexible flatfoot is a prevalent musculoskeletal deformity characterized by partial or complete loss of the medial longitudinal arch. It can modify lower-limb kinematic and kinetic parameters during walking. Fatigue may further impair gait patterns, potentially aggravating the deformity and increasing injury susceptibility. However, despite substantial research on fatigue and flatfoot separately, integrative evidence on fatigue-related gait biomechanics in individuals with flexible flatfoot remains limited.MethodsThis study was conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis following PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was conducted from January 2000 to August 2025 in databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa checklist, and data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers. Statistical analyses were carried out using RevMan software version 5.4.1, and the I² test was used to assess data heterogeneity.ResultsA total of 14 studies were included. Meta-analysis results showed that fatigue had a significant effect on certain kinematic variables in individuals with flexible flatfoot. “Regarding kinematic outcomes, pooled analyses revealed that fatigue significantly affected several lower-limb joint angles. Specifically, significant reductions were observed in knee external rotation (SMD = − 0.79, 95% CI: −1.50 to − 0.08; p = 0.03) and ankle dorsiflexion (SMD = − 0.60, 95% CI: −0.97 to − 0.22; p = 0.002), while a borderline significant decrease was noted for knee internal rotation (SMD = − 0.48, 95% CI: −0.96 to − 0.00; p = 0.05). In contrast, changes in ankle inversion, ankle eversion, hip external rotation, and hip internal rotation were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). When all kinematic outcomes were pooled, fatigue was associated with an overall significant reduction in joint angles (SMD = − 0.40, 95% CI: −0.64 to − 0.17; p = 0.0008), with moderate heterogeneity across studies (I² = 44%). In contrast, The Meta-analysis of kinetic variables such as center of pressure (MD = 0.97, 95% CI: −0.92 to 2.87; p = 0.31; I² = 0%), peak pressure (MD = 1.19, 95% CI: −6.81 to 9.20; p = 0.77) and Peak force (MD = 0.07, 95% CI: −0.68 to 0.83; p = 0.85) showed no statistically significant differences before and after fatigue. However, some single studies reported significant changes in plantar pressure, joint moments, and ground reaction forces.ConclusionIn conclusion, fatigue appears to significantly disrupt gait kinematics in individuals with flexible flatfoot, potentially compromising postural stability and increasing the risk of musculoskeletal injury. While kinetic parameters remained largely unaffected in the meta-analysis, the findings underscore the importance of implementing targeted corrective interventions to mitigate the functional consequences of fatigue in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09898-4.

Introduction

Fatigue can substantially alter movement kinematics and kinetics [1, 2] and, in individuals with flatfoot, may aggravate biomechanical dysfunction and increase injury risk [3]. With increasing fatigue, muscle force production and joint mechanics may deteriorate, leading to reduced overall performance [4, 5]. Because flatfoot provides insufficient arch support, fatigue-related deficits may be amplified, further compromising movement efficiency and potentially increasing injury susceptibility [6–8]. This review considers four fatigue domains: local muscle fatigue (reduced performance in specific muscles after sustained/intense activity) [9],whole-body fatigue (systemic exhaustion following prolonged strenuous activity) [10], central fatigue (reduced central nervous system drive to activate muscles) [11] and cardiovascular fatigue (endurance-related exhaustion associated with elevated heart rate and aerobic demand) [12]. The findings of this review are also relevant for rehabilitation. Understanding how fatigue affects biomechanics can inform rehabilitation strategies, helping to develop targeted interventions for individuals with flatfoot to reduce injury risk and improve recovery outcomes.

Flexible flatfoot is a common condition characterized by flattening of the medial longitudinal arch during weight-bearing, which can alter gait and lower-limb alignment and increase the risk of injury due to biomechanical changes [13, 14]. This condition affects movement patterns during activities like walking and leads to discomfort and pain [15]. The prevalence of flatfoot is particularly high among children and older adults, but it can affect individuals of all ages [16–18]. The condition not only impacts an individual’s functional capacity but also affects their quality of life, making it a critical area of research in sports medicine and biomechanics [18, 19].

Gait is a fundamental, repetitive activity in daily life and sport. Owing to its coordinated muscle–joint demands, it is sensitive to both local and whole-body fatigue [2]. It provides a consistent framework to quantify fatigue-related changes in joint angles, spatiotemporal measures, and load distribution, especially in people with flatfoot [20].

Kinematic and kinetic variables are both crucial for understanding movement, particularly in individuals with flatfoot [21–23]. Kinematics refers to the study of movement without considering forces, including variables such as joint angles, speed, and displacement during motion. Kinetics, on the other hand, addresses the forces and moments that cause these movements [24–26]. In individuals with flatfoot, both kinematic and kinetic variables are often altered, which leads to changes in gait, posture, and an increased risk of injury [27–29].

Previous reviews have examined the biomechanical characteristics of individuals with flatfoot (e.g., [22]) and the effects of fatigue on gait (e.g., [30, 31]). However, no review has specifically synthesized the combined effects of fatigue on both kinetic and kinematic gait parameters in people with flatfoot. Accordingly, this systematic review integrates foot structure (flatfoot) and fatigue within a single framework to clarify how their interaction may worsen gait alterations, particularly in individuals who lack sufficient arch support. We aimed to evaluate the effects of fatigue on kinetic and kinematic variables during walking gait in individuals with flatfoot and to summarize evidence that can inform targeted preventive and rehabilitative strategies. Improving understanding of fatigue-related biomechanical dysfunction in this population may also help explain the etiology of overuse injuries and support the development of more effective rehabilitation interventions.

Study selection

The titles, abstracts, and full texts of the studies meeting the inclusion criteria were evaluated by MAB and AAM. In instances of disagreement, the two authors discussed the matter to reach a resolution. If they could not reach a consensus, a third reviewer (SHM) was consulted for assistance.

Data collection

Data collection involved a meticulous process where MAB extracted all relevant data from the included studies, and SHM verified the accuracy of these data. The extracted data focused on the effects of fatigue on the kinetic and kinematic variables of the lower limbs in individuals with flat feet. These variables included ground reaction forces, joint angles, moments, peak force, peak pressure, foot pressure, center of pressure, and spatiotemporal parameters. Additional information was also collected, including the study’s sample size, sex distribution, type of fatigue protocol used, and participants’ age. Furthermore, methods for measuring flatfoot, which include visual assessment, footprint analysis, and static or dynamic arch measurement methods, were used depending on study protocols. The Measurement Tools used to evaluate the condition of flat foot and related movement patterns included force plates, gait analysis systems, motion capture systems, and foot pressure sensors. These tools were consistently applied to ensure reliable data collection and to facilitate uniformity in the analysis across studies.

Synthesis of results

Results were pooled using a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting, and effect estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Where outcomes were measured on different scales, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated; where scales were comparable, mean differences (MDs) were used. Analyses were conducted in RevMan (version 5.4.1), and meta-analysis was performed only when at least two methodologically comparable studies reported a common variable. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using I² and p values, and interpreted as low (< 25%), moderate (25–50%), or substantial (> 50%) heterogeneity, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The results were interpreted according to the levels of evidence defined by van Tulder et al. [35]. This approach was adapted from Mousavi et al. (Table 1) [36, 37]. Due to a few studies, publication bias was not assessed, and no multiple-comparison correction was applied; results were interpreted cautiously, focusing on consistency and clinical relevance.

Results

Study selection

An initial literature search identified a total of 953 studies from various databases, including 384 from PubMed, 231 from Embase, 250 from Web of Science, and 85 from Scopus. After removing 468 duplicates, 485 articles remained. During title and abstract screening, 453 articles were excluded based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 14 articles were included in the systematic review, of which 9 met the criteria for meta-analysis. Among the 14 included studies, 10 investigated kinetic variables, 7 focused on kinematic variables, 10 studies were in English, and 4 studies in Persian. For missing data, we contacted the study’s corresponding authors by email. We deducted the studies from meta-analysis if we could not access data.

The studies were selected based on title, abstract, and full-text screening. Initially, the studies were imported into EndNote, and duplicates were removed. After that, the studies were imported into RevMan version 5.4.1 for the full-text screening process. This software was used to systematically screen studies based on titles, abstracts, and full-texts to ensure that all relevant studies were included in the review. Types of biomechanical variables

One study investigated the effect of fatigue on foot pressure in individuals with flat foot [38], whereas three studies investigated the effect of fatigue on ground reaction forces [41, 44, 47]. Two studies explored the effect of fatigue on the center of pressure [39, 40], and three studies explored the effect of fatigue on the peak force and peak pressure [41, 48, 49]. Three studies analyzed the effect of fatigue on moment [42, 45, 47], and two studies investigated the effect of fatigue on joint angle [50, 51]. One study focused on the effect of fatigue on power [45], whereas five studies investigated the effect of fatigue on spatiotemporal parameters [41–43, 46, 50].

Effects of fatigue on kinetic variables

Ground reaction forces

Three studies investigated the effects of fatigue on ground reaction force components and impulse in individuals with flat foot [41, 44, 47]. Analysis revealed that fatigue induced significant decrease in certain ground reaction force variables, specifically in the first peak anterior-posterior ground reaction force (MD = − 5.58, 95% CI: −9.18 to − 1.98; p = 0.002) and first peak vertical ground reaction force (MD = − 8.48, 95% CI: −16.68 to − 0.28; p = 0.04) [47], while other ground reaction force components did not show statistically significant differences [41, 44]. Additionally, analysis of impulse across various plantar regions indicated that fatigue had no significant effect on this variable (MD = − 1.54, 95% CI: −3.34 to 0.26; p = 0.09) [41]. (Figure S2, S3, S4).

Center of pressure

Two studies [39, 40] investigated the effect of fatigue on center of pressure during gait in individuals with flat foot. The meta-analysis indicated that fatigue did not produce a statistically significant change in center of pressure measures. There was no statistically significant difference between pre- and post-fatigue conditions (MD = 0.97, 95% CI: −0.92 to 2.87; p = 0.31). No heterogeneity was observed between studies (I² = 0%), indicating consistency in their findings. (Figure 2).

Foot pressure and plantar pressure

One study [38] investigated the effects of fatigue on foot pressure in individuals with flat foot. Foot pressure outcomes were analyzed across different plantar regions. In the medial Forefoot area, fatigue was associated with a non-significant reduction in pressure (MD = − 2.85, 95% CI: −5.92 to 0.22; p = 0.07). In the middle forefoot region, a statistically significant reduction in plantar pressure was observed following fatigue (MD = − 4.12, 95% CI: −6.49 to − 1.75; p = 0.0007).

For the lateral forefoot region, the reduction in pressure did not reach statistical significance (MD = − 0.78, 95% CI: −1.57 to 0.01; p = 0.05). Similarly, no significant changes were found in the midfoot lateral region (MD = − 1.07, 95% CI: −3.80 to 1.66; p = 0.44) or in the hallux region (MD = 0.39, 95% CI: −2.11 to 2.89; p = 0.76). Data for the hindfoot region were not estimable. Overall, pooled analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in total foot pressure following fatigue (MD = − 1.53, 95% CI: −2.98 to − 0.08; p = 0.04) (Figure S5). Another study [49] examined the effects of fatigue on plantar pressure. The results indicated that fatigue did not lead to any statistically significant changes in plantar pressure (MD = 0.03, 95% CI: −0.31 to 0.38; p = 0.86). Notably, a decrease of 0.10 units was observed in first metatarsal plantar pressure, but this change was not statistically significant (95% CI: − 2.04 to 0.18; p > 0.05). These findings suggest that while fatigue may cause slight increases in pressure in certain regions, such changes are not statistically reliable. )figure S6)

Peak force

Three studies [41, 48, 49] investigated the effects of fatigue on peak pressure and force in individuals with flat foot. The meta-analysis of three studies indicated that fatigue has no overall statistically significant effect on peak pressure (MD = 1.19, 95% CI: −6.81 to 9.20; p = 0.77) and peak force (MD = 0.07, 95% CI: −0.68 to 0.83; p = 0.85) across different plantar regions. However, a slight but statistically significant increase in peak force was observed specifically in the 1st toe region (MD = 2.61, 95% CI: 0.68 to 4.54; p = 0.008). These findings suggest that the effects of fatigue on these variables are localized rather than generalizable across the foot. (Figures 3 and 4)

Moment

Three studies [42, 45, 47] investigated the effects of fatigue on moments in individuals with flat foot. The findings revealed that fatigue did not lead to statistically significant changes in most moment parameters (MD = 0.00, 95% CI: −0.00 to 0.00; p = 0.11). Although one study [47] reported an increase in the first peak free moment following fatigue, the result was marginally non-significant (MD = 0.00, 95% CI: −0.00 to 0.00; p = 0.06). No other significant changes were observed in joint moment values across the lower limbs. Overall, these results suggest that fatigue does not have a significant effect on moment in this population. (Figures S7, S8, S9)

Power

One study [45] investigated the effects of fatigue on power in individuals with flat foot. The analysis showed that fatigue had no statistically significant effect on any of the measured power variables in the lower limbs (p > 0.05). The overall effect was also non-significant (MD = 0.02, 95% CI: −0.10 to 0.14; p = 0.78), indicating that fatigue did not produce any meaningful change in lower limb power in this population. (Figure S10)

Effects of fatigue on the kinematic variables

Spatiotemporals

Five studies [41–43, 46, 50] investigated the effects of fatigue on gait parameters in individuals with flat foot. The meta-analysis results indicate that fatigue has a significant effect on stride length in both the left and right limbs, with a statistically significant reduction observed in the left limb (MD = − 0.06, 95% CI: −0.11 to − 0.02; p = 0.004) and the right limb (MD = − 0.08, 95% CI: −0.13 to − 0.02; p = 0.005) following fatigue. This decrease in stride length reflects alterations in gait patterns post-fatigue. In contrast, the single support phase in both limbs did not show significant changes due to fatigue, with non-significant differences reported for the right limb (MD = 0.79, 95% CI: −0.52 to 2.11; p = 0.24) and the left limb (MD = − 0.12, 95% CI: −2.08 to 1.84; p = 0.90). These findings suggest that while fatigue substantially affects stride length, it does not significantly impact the stability during the single support phase. (Figures 5, 6, 7 and 8)

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies provides evidence on the effects of fatigue on kinematic and kinetic gait variables in individuals with flexible flatfoot. Our findings indicate significant changes in kinematic variables such as stride length and joint angles, while kinetic variables, including ground reaction forces and plantar pressure, showed more variable effects. This review integrates the effects of fatigue and flatfoot on gait mechanics, highlighting their interactive effects and the need for targeted rehabilitation strategies. Moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed for several pooled outcomes. This variability may be attributed to differences across studies in fatigue-induction protocols (e.g., treadmill walking, hopping, or repeated functional tasks), participant characteristics (including age, sex distribution, and severity of flexible flatfoot), biomechanical assessment tools, and outcome definitions. Given these sources of variability, random-effects models were applied, and pooled results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In interpreting the findings of this review, priority was given to results derived from meta-analyses, as these pooled estimates provide more robust and generalizable evidence. Outcomes reported by single studies were considered supplementary and were interpreted with caution due to the absence of pooled effect estimates.

Kinematic alterations in individuals with flatfoot following fatigue

Fatigue significantly reduces the range of motion in key lower limb joints (ankle, knee) in individuals with flexible flatfoot, disrupting movement patterns and challenging neuromuscular control [52, 53]. In addition, the observed decrease in ankle dorsiflexion (SMD = − 0.60, 95% CI: −0.97 to − 0.22; p = 0.002) likely reflects impaired function of the calf muscles and reduced shock absorption capacity during the stance phase of gait, which can shift abnormal loads to adjacent structures [54, 55].

At the knee joint, the reduction in both internal (SMD = − 0.48, 95% CI: −0.96 to − 0.00; p = 0.05) and external (SMD = − 0.79, 95% CI: −1.50 to − 0.08; p = 0.03) rotational range of motion after fatigue indicates altered movement strategies along the lower limb kinetic chain and reduced neuromuscular adaptability [56]. Over time, this loss of flexibility may increase the vulnerability to repetitive loading injuries and compensatory movement patterns. Similarly, a decrease in hip external rotation was observed, which may further disrupt overall gait mechanics, especially under conditions of muscular fatigue and reduced joint control [30]. These findings are consistent with previous studies that report individuals with flexible flatfoot are less able to maintain normal joint kinematics when fatigued, largely due to weakness in deep foot and calf muscles [21, 22]. Other researchers [3, 8] have also emphasized that muscle fatigue in this group increases. Overall, the findings are consistent with experimental work showing that fatigue can modify lower-limb kinematics, including ankle inversion/eversion and knee rotational behavior during gait [57].

Fatigue significantly reduces left stride length (MD = − 0.06, 95% CI: −0.11 to − 0.02; p = 0.004) limb and Right stride length (MD = − 0.08, 95% CI: −0.13 to − 0.02; p = 0.005) in individuals with flexible flatfoot, suggesting compensatory adjustments in gait mechanics to preserve stability [30]. This shortening of step length reflects a biomechanical adaptation to declining neuromuscular control and increased joint stiffness [58]. In contrast, studies of individuals with normal arches under similar conditions show less pronounced reductions in stride length, suggesting that normal-arch individuals rely less on compensatory gait strategies. This difference may be related to better neuromuscular control and joint flexibility in healthy individuals [22]. The reduction in stride length can also result in increased joint stiffness, particularly in the ankle and knee joints, as the body attempts to stabilize the lower limbs during each step [59].

Previous research is consistent with our findings. For instance, Sung et al. and Nourbakhsh et al. [22, 23] have reported that individuals with flatfoot, particularly when fatigued, tend to decrease their stride length and walking speed to compensate for compromised postural stability and arch support. Such adaptations, while momentarily protective, may increase ground contact time and concentrate loading on specific regions of the foot, thereby elevating the risk of overuse injuries [8, 26].

Moreover, single-study analyses in our review have shown that fatigue can cause a significant reduction in step time and an increase in the initial contact phase (MD = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.11 to 3.27; p = 0.04) [41, 50], reflecting further disruption in the timing and coordination of the gait cycle. These findings emphasize that spatiotemporal alterations in fatigued flatfoot individuals are not limited to step length alone but also affect the rhythm and efficiency of movement.

In summary, the present evidence demonstrates that fatigue results in widespread disruptions of lower limb joint kinematics in individuals with flexible flatfoot. These changes are not limited to a single joint, but reflect a complex, multi-joint impairment that affects the overall coordination and efficiency of gait. Therefore, addressing these kinematic alterations through comprehensive rehabilitation strategies—focused on neuromuscular endurance, proprioceptive training, and correction of abnormal joint motion—should be prioritized to support functional mobility and minimize the risk of musculoskeletal injuries in this population.

Kinetic alterations in individuals with flatfoot following fatigue

The results of the present meta-analysis indicate that fatigue, overall, does not have a significant effect on key kinetic variables, including peak force, peak pressure, and center of pressure in individuals with flexible flatfoot. In other words, pooled analyses showed no substantial differences in these measures before and after fatigue, suggesting a relative resistance of these indices to the acute effects of fatigue. However, a review of single studies revealed that, under certain conditions, parameters such as foot pressure [38], specific components of ground reaction forces [47], and impulse [41] may exhibit significant changes following fatigue, particularly in specific regions of the foot or during particular phases of the gait cycle. This discrepancy between meta-analytic and single-study findings highlights the need for more detailed, region-specific analysis of kinetic variables and careful consideration of participant characteristics and testing fatigue protocols.

Findings from single studies suggest that fatigue can reduce specific components of ground reaction forces, particularly the first vertical peak (MD = − 8.48, 95% CI: −16.68 to − 0.28; p = 0.04) and anterior-posterior peaks (MD = − 5.58, 95% CI: −9.18 to − 1.98; p = 0.002) in certain regions of the foot [47]. These changes may reflect compensatory strategies to redistribute load and maintain stability when muscular efficiency is reduced [60]. However, other components, such as the overall magnitude of forces and general patterns, appear more resistant to fatigue, with changes often not reaching statistical significance [41, 44]. This indicates that the body employs adaptive strategies to stabilize ground reaction forces, even if this results in increased localized loading, which could contribute to overuse injuries [31, 61]. From a biomechanical standpoint, this suggests the adaptive role of neuromuscular systems in maintaining the body’s dynamic equilibrium, utilizing alternative muscle groups or compensatory movement strategies to stabilize the ground reaction forces [62]. This also means that, while localized overloads may occur in certain regions of the foot, the global force distribution across the entire lower limb might still be maintained.

The results of this meta-analysis indicate that fatigue does not have a significant effect on the main parameters of center of pressure (MD = 0.97, 95% CI: −0.92 to 2.87; p = 0.31; I² = 0%) in individuals with flexible flatfoot. Even when other movement systems are influenced by fatigue, postural control and foot pressure regulation remain stable. This stability may be attributed to neuromuscular compensatory mechanisms or specific biomechanical characteristics in individuals with flexible flatfoot [26]. Therefore, muscle fatigue alone is not the primary cause of disturbances in center of pressure and balance, and assessment and rehabilitation should consider other movement indicators to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the effects of fatigue. From a mechanistic perspective, fatigue of the intrinsic foot muscles may compromise medial longitudinal arch stability and reduce the foot’s capacity for shock absorption, which can explain the observed reduction in ankle dorsiflexion and altered plantar loading patterns [63].

Single studies suggest that fatigue may lead to slight reductions in joint moments in individuals with flexible flatfoot, but these changes are often not statistically significant or consistent across participants [47]. Given the lack of meta-analysis, further research with larger sample sizes and more rigorous methods is needed to clarify the impact of fatigue on joint moments in this population.

The meta-analysis found that fatigue had no significant effect on peak force (MD = 0.07, 95% CI: −0.68 to 0.83; p = 0.85) or peak pressure (MD = 1.19, 95% CI: −6.81 to 9.20; p = 0.77) across the plantar surface in individuals with flexible flatfoot, with these parameters remaining stable before and after fatigue induction. This suggests that acute fatigue mechanisms do not disrupt force and pressure distribution. This stability may reflect the efficacy of neuromuscular compensation and the body’s ability to regulate loading through adaptive movement strategies or activation of alternative muscle groups. However, some studies reported region-specific increases after fatigue [31]. Differences from findings in healthy individuals, who often show clearer fatigue-related changes, may relate to the stabilizing role of a normal arch [3] and to variability in measurement methods and fatigue protocols. Future work should prioritise segmental (region-specific) force/pressure analyses alongside global metrics.

Single study evidence indicates that fatigue can significantly alter plantar pressure, particularly in the middle forefoot, where pressure decreases after fatigue, suggesting compensatory load redistribution in response to reduced muscular efficiency [38]. This reduction may reflect a protective mechanism to minimize stress on foot structures or a limitation in force generation under fatigue [64]. In contrast, fatigue did not significantly affect lower-limb joint power (MD = 0.02, 95% CI: −0.10 to 0.14; p = 0.79), implying that overall power generation and energy transfer at major joints are largely preserved despite plantar pressure shifts [65]. Collectively, these findings support the need for comprehensive biomechanical assessments of fatigue effects in clinical and rehabilitation contexts.

Clinical implications and rehabilitation strategies

The findings of this review highlight the multifactorial effects of fatigue on gait biomechanics in individuals with flexible flatfoot, emphasizing the need for comprehensive clinical evaluation and multidimensional interventions. Fatigue disrupts both kinematic variables (such as joint angles and stride length) and, to some extent, kinetic variables (including plantar pressure, ground reaction forces, and impulse). Rehabilitation programs should adopt a holistic approach, focusing on improving neuromuscular control and muscular endurance, particularly in the foot and lower limb muscles. Clinically, individuals with flexible flatfoot are at increased risk of gait instability and overuse injuries, especially during prolonged activities. Routine assessments should include dynamic tests of neuromuscular control and fatigue resistance. Early detection of abnormal movement patterns can enable timely interventions to prevent injury [66]. Rehabilitation should incorporate balance, proprioceptive exercises, and gait retraining with alignment correction, supported by foot orthoses. Progressive resistance training and functional drills can help improve muscle endurance and reduce injury risk [37, 67, 68]. These fatigue-induced biomechanical alterations are likely to promote compensatory movement strategies, such as reduced stride length, altered joint kinematics, and increased reliance on proximal joints, which may further elevate mechanical loading and injury risk over time.

Although meta-analytic pooling showed significant effects for selected spatiotemporal and kinematic outcomes, individual studies were not always consistent. This variability likely reflects differences in fatigue protocols, participant characteristics, measurement methods, and outcome definitions; moreover, some single-study effects did not remain significant after pooling, emphasizing the value of meta-analysis for more robust and generalizable conclusions.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Despite the comprehensive nature of this systematic review and meta-analysis, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, there was considerable heterogeneity in fatigue induction protocols, participant demographics, and biomechanical assessment techniques across the included studies, which limited the comparability of findings. Second, discrepancies in measurement tools, such as pressure-sensing platforms and motion capture systems, introduced potential measurement bias. Third, most studies had small sample sizes and lacked longitudinal follow-up, restricting the ability to assess long-term effects or causal relationships. Additionally, potential publication bias and language restrictions may have limited the inclusion of relevant data. Finally, the strict inclusion criteria, while necessary for methodological rigor, may have led to the exclusion of studies with incomplete but valuable data, narrowing the scope of the review. This study includes only articles published in English and Persian, which may introduce language bias and exclude relevant studies in other languages, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the review.

Recommendations for future studies

To address these limitations, future research should prioritize the standardization of fatigue protocols, including consistent definitions of fatigue thresholds, targeted muscle groups, and induction durations. Consensus guidelines for biomechanical assessments, such as high-resolution plantar pressure systems and 3D motion analysis, would improve cross-study comparability. Larger, longitudinal studies are needed to assess both immediate and chronic biomechanical effects of fatigue in individuals with flexible flatfoot. Future studies should also investigate how neuromuscular fatigue interacts with compensatory mechanisms during gait and its impact on injury risk and performance. Including diverse populations across age, sex, and activity levels will enhance generalizability. Finally, evaluating the effectiveness of targeted rehabilitation interventions to reduce fatigue-induced biomechanical changes should be a central focus of future clinical trials. Additionally, incorporating advanced computational approaches such as finite element analysis finite element analysis and musculoskeletal modeling can provide a clearer understanding of fatigue-related changes. Finite element analysis enables three-dimensional simulation of the foot to estimate internal stresses and vulnerable regions that cannot be captured through external measurements. In contrast, musculoskeletal modeling helps clarify how fatigue alters muscle activation patterns, joint loading, and compensatory strategies during gait [69]. Integrating experimental data with these models can offer a more mechanistic interpretation of fatigue-induced alterations in individuals with flexible flatfoot.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides evidences that fatigue significantly impairs kinematic parameters, especially stride length and joint angles in individuals with flexible flatfoot. Significant reductions were observed in ankle dorsiflexion, knee, and hip rotations, indicating compromised neuromuscular coordination. However, kinetic variables such as center of pressure, peak pressure, and peak force showed no significant changes, suggesting neuromuscular compensation. Individual studies revealed that fatigue can lead to localized changes in foot pressure and ground reaction forces. Overall, these findings highlight the complexity of fatigue-related gait adaptations and the need for targeted interventions to improve neuromuscular control and gait stability.
