Advances in the pathogenesis and treatment of pneumococcal meningitis
Authors: Yiyun Xu, Ji Wang, Xiaosong Qin, Jianhua Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus pneumoniae is a common pathogen associated with community-acquired bacterial meningitis, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates. While vaccination reduces the incidence of meningitis, many survivors experience severe brain damage and corresponding sequelae. The pathogenesis of pneumococcal meningitis has not been fully elucidated. Currently, meningitis requires bacterial disruption of the blood – brain barrier, a process that involves the interaction of bacterial surface components with host cells and various inflammatory responses. This review delineates the global prevalence, pathogenesis, and treatment strategies of pneumococcal meningitis. The objective is to enhance the thorough comprehension of the clinical manifestations and biological mechanisms of the disease, thereby enabling more efficient prevention, diagnosis, and therapeutic interventions.KEYWORDS: Pneumococcal meningitis, blood-brain barrier, epidemiology, risk factors, pathogenesis, adjuvant therapy

Introduction

Bacterial meningitis, an inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS), is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates. Globally, approximately 318,000 people succumb to this disease each year, and most survivors have severe neurological sequelae, including focal neurological impairment, hearing loss, cognitive impairment, and epilepsy [1,2]. Streptococcus pneumoniae is the leading cause of bacterial meningitis worldwide. Although some developed countries in Europe and the United States have adopted vaccination and antibiotic strategies to prevent invasive diseases caused by S. pneumoniae, the incidence of S. pneumoniae meningitis remains high.

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is composed of the basement membrane, cellular layer (including endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes), and glycocalyx. This barrier controls the flow of substances between the brain and the outside environment, permitting the selective transport of vital nutrients such as glucose and amino acids while preventing harmful substances such as neurotoxins and pathogens from passing through [3]. This selective permeability is crucial for maintaining the homoeostasis of the CNS. The blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB), which is formed by the choroid, meningeal microvasculature, and arachnoid barrier cells, is another important barrier in the central nervous system. It, along with the BBB, serves to protect the cerebrospinal fluid and brain parenchyma, respectively, from harmful substances in the blood. Although most blood-borne bacteria that cause infection cannot pass both barriers, some cross the BBB through infiltration, causing meningitis. The translocation of S. pneumoniae through the BCSFB is dependent on the TLR signalling pathway [4]. However, some researchers believe that S. pneumoniae preferentially adheres to subarachnoid blood vessels in mice and subsequently invades the veins, suggesting that it might not invade the brain via the BCSFB pathway [5,6]. Given these findings are based on animal models, the question of whether S. pneumoniae actually crosses the BCSFB in humans remains debated. Therefore, our review primarily discusses the mechanisms of S. pneumoniae crossing the BBB. To cross the barrier and reach the CNS, S. pneumoniae attaches to host cells via its own receptors and subsequently enters the brain, triggering a series of inflammatory cascades and producing virulence factors that interact with host cells to disrupt the integrity of the BBB [7]. The causes of meningitis are related not only to bacterial characteristics but also to the susceptibility of the host itself, as well as the immune system. Meningitis caused by S. pneumoniae can affect people of all ages, with a high prevalence among children under 5 years old and older people over 65 years old [8]. Although diagnostic techniques and antimicrobial treatment protocols for meningitis pathogens are relatively complete, severe neurological damage still pose a serious threat to the health and development of children and pose marked difficulties for adult patients returning to normal life after recovery [2]. Bacterial meningitis poses a significant socioeconomic burden in densely populated developing countries. Therefore, it is particularly important to explore the pathogenic mechanisms of meningitis caused by S. pneumoniae, identify and optimize the therapeutic measures to reduce brain injury, and maximize the quality of life of patients. This review summarizes the epidemiology, pathogenesis, and therapeutic measures for the prevention and treatment of pneumococcal meningitis.

Epidemiology and risk factors

Epidemiology

The incidence of pneumococcal meningitis (PM) varies significantly by geographical location. The confirmed PM proportion in Africa and other regions was 57%, nearly double that of Europe (29.8%) [9,10]. According to previous reports, there were approximately 83,900 PM cases in 2015 in China [11]. The widespread use of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) has led to a global decline in PM incidence. For example, the introduction of PCVs in the United States resulted in a 63% reduction in the incidence rate of PM between 1997 and 2014, with similar notable changes in low-income countries [12,13], though the rate of PM remained high. According to the World Health Organization disease surveillance report, S. pneumoniae ranked first in terms of paediatric bacterial meningitis deaths worldwide between 2014 and 2019, with a mortality proportion of 12.2% [10]. Economic factors have a significant impact on PM mortality, which has a significantly higher mortality rate in low-income countries with scarce medical resources than in developed countries. For example, in South Africa, the in-hospital mortality rate for PM is as high as 41%, with a median time to death of only 3 days [14], while in the United States, the mortality rate for PM is 9.7%, with a median survival time of 9.3 days [15]. It is crucial to acknowledge that the data from South Africa encompassed HIV-positive patients, potentially influencing the observed mortality rate.

Capsular polysaccharides (CPSs) are important virulence factors in S. pneumoniae. More than 100 serotypes have been identified based on genetic differences in capsule composition. The pathogenicity of some serotypes was particularly prominent among cases of PM across different regions (Table 1). In Africa, serotypes 1 and 14 are predominant for PM, both associated with increased mortality and outbreaks of meningitis [16,17] The main serotypes causing PM in Europe are highly dispersed. There are several reasons for the differences in the distribution of serotypes across countries, mainly related to differences in vaccine introduction time and vaccination rates, different antibiotic selection pressures caused by different antimicrobial drug use strategies, the spread of drug-resistant clones, and serotype replacement [10,30].

PCV, Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine.

To reduce the burden of invasive diseases caused by S. pneumoniae, PCVs and 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) have been developed for several common S. pneumoniae serotypes. Introduction of these vaccines has resulted in a remarkable reduction in PM morbidity and mortality. However, with the introduction of the vaccine for PM, including the selective pressure of antibiotics and fluctuations in the serotype of S. pneumoniae itself, there has been an increase in PM caused by serotypes not accounted for in the vaccine, conceptualizing “serotype replacement.” Since the implementation of PCV13, the number of non-vaccine serotypes causing meningitis, mainly types 8, 12F, and 22F, has substantially increased in the United Kingdom [35]. In France and Italy, the predominant serotype also shifted from the vaccine serotype 19A to the non-vaccine serotype 24F [30,36], posing a significant challenge for the prevention and treatment of PM. For example, from 2008 to 2014, an analysis of national inpatient samples in the United States found that the hospitalization rate for PM remarkably decreased following the introduction of PCV13; however, the case fatality rate, which was closely related to the change in serotype distribution after the introduction of the vaccine, did not improve [15]. The phenomenon of “serotype replacement” is pronounced in some low-income countries with a high incidence of S. pneumoniae, where approximately half the meningitis strains detected are non-vaccine serotypes [29]. In addition to serotype replacement, a considerable number of non-vaccine serotypes exhibit resistance to at least one antibiotic. Although the proportion of multidrug-resistant bacteria in non-vaccine serotypes is much lower than that in vaccine serotypes, the detection and monitoring of resistance in non-vaccine serotypes should not be overlooked [29,37].

Risk factors

PM remains a serious global health problem, with infants, children, older adults, and immunocompromised populations being more susceptible to invasive pneumococcal infections. Furthermore, individuals with diabetes, alcoholism, and cancer are also at a heightened risk for S. pneumoniae infection, the predominant pathogen associated with meningitis in these patient populations [38]. In some cohort studies, diabetes and alcoholism have been found to be strong independent risk factors for adverse PM outcomes and mortality [39,40]. Moreover, some underlying diseases, including asthma and severe mental illness, are considered risk factors for PM. Although not yet considered to be at a high risk for PM, these patients are still at a higher risk than is the general population [41,42].

The host’s innate immune response is an important line of defence against infection by external pathogens. Immune system damage and deficiencies in immune factors increase the risk of developing PM. Some genetically induced immune system defects, including altered NFKBIE polymorphisms in the gene encoding nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and deficiency of mannose-binding lectin (MBL), involved in complement activation, are associated with an increased risk of PM [43,44]. Moreover, patients infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) have a higher risk of PM mortality than does the general population [45]. Therefore, early prevention and interventions, including alcohol cessation and antiretroviral therapy, are necessary for patients with these high risk factors, and early vaccination against PCVs should be administered to improve population immunity and prevent the development of invasive pneumococcal disease.

Pathogenesis of pneumococcal meningitis

Pathogenic role of S. pneumoniae virulence factors among patients with meningitis

The pathogenesis of bacterial meningitis generally involves several basic steps: mucosal colonization, invasion of blood vessels, and proliferation to reach a high bacteraemia load and break through the BBB and BCSFB. Intranasal colonization is an important factor among patients with PM. After resisting the host’s innate immune response, including neutrophil phagocytosis and complement killing, the bacteria further penetrate the epithelium of the nasopharyngeal mucosa, enter the blood, and finally cross the BBB to invade the brain. Virulence factors play an important role in this process (Figure 1).

S. pneumoniae survives and proliferates in the blood and subsequently adheres to and invades the BBB with the help of virulence proteins, a process that involves bacterial NanA binding to sialic acid, RrgA binding to pIgR and PECAM-1, PCho binding to PAFR, and Ply contributing to the endocytosis of bacteria by astrocytes.

Survival of S. pneumoniae in blood

For bacteria to invade the CNS after colonizing the mucosal surface, they must survive and proliferate in the blood, and a certain level of bacteraemia is conducive to subsequent bacteria – host cell interactions and inflammatory reaction development. The host immune system activates defence mechanisms when it recognizes bacteria entering the bloodstream. Thereafter, virulence factors mediate the immune escape of S. pneumoniae. CPS is a key virulence factor for S. pneumoniae upon entry into the bloodstream and inhibits the activation of the classical complement pathway by preventing bacterial binding to IgG and C-reactive proteins [46]. Moreover, CPS inhibits non-receptor-mediated phagocytosis, which is advantageous for the survival of S. pneumoniae. [47] Second, S. pneumoniae surface proteins A and C can bind to the C4b binding protein in the host serum and limit complement C4b deposition on the bacterial surface, thus evading complement attacks and increasing bacterial survival in the serum [48].

Adhesion and invasion

After surviving in the blood against host immune defences, S. pneumoniae can translocate to the CNS by adhering to brain endothelial cells or choroid plexus epithelial cells, which involves the interaction of multiple adhesion factors with receptors. Phosphorylcholine on the surface of S. pneumoniae plays a critical role in the interactions between the bacteria and host cells. It binds to platelet-activating factor receptor (PAFR) of the host’s, forming a complex that enhances bacterial adhesion. When PAFR was blocked, the adhesion and invasion of S. pneumoniae were significantly reduced [49,50]. Docked to phosphorylcholine, choline-binding proteins (CBPs) form a large family that participates in bacterial adhesion and invasion. Choline-binding protein A (CbpA, also called PspC), a prominent member of this family, binds to laminin receptors and the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) on brain endothelial cells, enhancing the bacterium’s adhesion to the brain endothelium [51,52]. RrgA is a subunit of the type 1 heterotrimeric pilus in S. pneumoniae, and it is also a major adhesin that participates in the bacterial adhesion and invasion processes. Similar to CbpA, RrgA can binds to the pIgR. Additionally, it interacts with platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (PECAM-1), assisting S. pneumoniae in penetrating the BBB. Moreover, high expression levels of RrgA enhanced bacterial adhesion to the host and promoted the formation of individual spherical bacteria, resulting in greater BBB penetration capacity [52]. When RrgA was blocked with antibodies, the invasion of S. pneumoniae into the mouse brain was significantly reduced [53]. Neuraminidase A (NanA), also known as sialidase, is a surface-attached exonuclease that cleaves sialic acid from host cells and proteins, and its N-terminal lectin-like structural domain plays an essential role in promoting pneumococcal adhesion and invasion of brain microvascular endothelial cells [54]. Interestingly, certain strains of S. pneumoniae that lack a functional LPXTG anchor motif have been found to secrete NanA rather than express it on their surface. Gratz et al.’s research suggests that strains secreting NanA are more prevalent in clinical isolates obtained from blood and cerebrospinal fluid. Furthermore, it has been observed that secreted NanA may enhance the development of meningitis more effectively than the surface-anchored form [55]. Both the secreted and cell-bound variations of NanA play significant roles in the invasion of the BBB by S. pneumoniae; however, further investigation is required to ascertain which form has a more crucial impact on this process.

Endocytosis

After adhering to the host cell surface, S. pneumoniae interacts with the host cell through endocytosis and uses the host cell to cross the BBB. In the previous sections, we discussed how Phosphorylcholine binds to the PAFR, facilitating cellular adhesion and invasion. Following the binding of S. pneumoniae to PAFR, the bacterium is internalized into the cell via endocytosis with the assistance of β-arrestin and subsequent recruitment of clathrin [49]. Similarly, the binding of PspC to pIgR also requires the involvement of clathrin and caveolin, which are essential components of the dynamin-dependent endocytic pathway [56]. Once internalized, S. pneumoniae fuses with early endosomes and eventually with lysosomes. Pneumolysin (Ply), a major host pro-endocytic factor for S. pneumoniae, plays a critical role as a pathogenic factor in brain infections caused by this bacterium. It adheres to cholesterol in the host cell membrane, forming pores that lead to cell lysis. Experimental findings from the incubation of Ply with astrocytes and microglia demonstrated its capacity to enhance endocytosis in specific brain cells. Notably, in mice, intraperitoneal injections of the endocytosis inhibitor chlorpromazine effectively mitigated neuroinflammatory manifestations [57]. S. pneumoniae with low levels of Ply exhibits better intracellular survival and endocytosis, allowing the bacteria to enter the brain through the BBB, whereas S. pneumoniae with high levels of Ply exhibit lower host intracellular survival after infection, and the bacteria are more easily cleared via host cell ubiquitination [58]. Excessively high Ply levels are detrimental to bacterial survival. Ply can also promote neuronal death with RrgA and endocytosis. The increased exposure of β-actin after Ply acts on the neuronal plasma membrane, allows more RrgA to combine with it, and promotes pneumococcal infection of neurons [59].

In a meningitis mouse model, it was found that infection with S. pneumoniae disrupts the tight junction proteins between brain endothelial cells and promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. This further weakens the tight junctions between endothelial cells and the permeability of the BBB, demonstrating that S. pneumoniae can traverse the BBB through another mechanism, namely paracellular traversal [6].

Pathogenic signaling pathways

The host immune response is the first line of defence against bacterial invasion. However, while protecting the organism, it can also lead to a widespread inflammatory response that can damage the brain and induce adverse neurological sequelae. Once the bacteria breach the blood-brain barrier, their components or released immunogenic substances can bind to the host’s immune cells, such as microglia, macrophages, and astrocytes. This binding activates the host’s pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which in turn initiate diverse signalling pathways, leading to an immune response. The signalling pathways triggered by different pathogens that cross the BBB are different. Meningitis due to S. pneumoniae mainly involves the NF-κB, Notch, and P75NTR signalling pathways, which may be associated with the activation of the inflammatory response and damage of BBB, as well as sequelae (Figure 2).

Toll-like receptors (TLR) are critical receptors involved in innate immunity and recognize various cell wall components of microorganisms, including lipopolysaccharides, peptidoglycans, and lipoteichoic acids. When these cell wall components are recognized by TLRs, they activate a series of downstream signalling pathways, triggering strong immune responses. In PM, the lipoteichoic acid and lipoprotein in S. pneumoniae activate immune cells by binding to TLR2 on these cells, leading to the release of large amounts of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6. These inflammatory mediators can further damage BBB and exacerbate the clinical symptoms of meningitis, and the production of inflammatory factors depends on the stimulation of lipoproteins to a certain extent [60,61]. Furthermore, TLR stimulation enhances the phagocytosis and killing ability of microglia to S. pneumoniae, providing a protective effect on the brain [62]. Conversely, the absence of these TLRs results in increased BBB permeability, reduced survival rates, and higher risks of neurological sequelae in PM mice [63]. Endopeptidase O (PepO) and neuraminidase A (NanA), the virulence proteins of S. pneumoniae, interact with TLR2 and TLR4 to trigger a strong host immune response, whereas NanA-mediated desialylation interferes with the TLR2-associated signalling pathways [64]. Myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88) is a key adapter protein responsible for transmitting upstream information in the TLR signalling pathway, and its carboxy-terminus interacts with the structural domain of the Toll/IL-1 receptor to mediate NF-κB nuclear translocation and cytokine production. Picard C et al.’s study indicated that the presence of MyD88 increased resistance to S. pneumoniae in mice with PM and that its deficiency made patients more susceptible to bacterial meningitis [65]. Moreover, MyD88 also induces NF-κB to upregulate the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor by activating downstream signalling pathways, thereby increasing its protective effect on nerves [66]. However, MyD88 activation also upregulates other pro-inflammatory factors, including interleukin (IL)-1β, which triggers a severe inflammatory response and exacerbates cochlear and hearing damage caused by inflammation during PM [67].

The nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD) proteins belong to the NOD-like receptor family, widely found in human cells, and can activate the NF-κB and MAPK signalling pathways after recognizing the corresponding ligands. NOD2 plays a role in the pathogenesis of meningitis. Several hours after PM infection in rats, NOD2 expression was consistently elevated and activated NF-κB nuclear translocation to promote the release of inflammatory factors and expression of inflammatory cells. Owing to the mediation of inflammation, an increase in BBB permeability and brain water content aggravated brain oedema in rats [68]. NOD2 is also present in some CNS cells, including microglia and astrocytes, and is required to mediate NF-κB activation for the release of inflammatory factors after pneumococcal infection [69]. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are proteolytic enzymes known for their involvement in extracellular matrix degradation and modulation of immune responses via signalling pathway activation. High levels of MMP-8 and MMP-9 have been detected in the CSF of patients with bacterial meningitis. Furthermore, research conducted on rats has shown that MMPs play a significant role in compromising BBB, leading to brain damage [70,71]. NF-κB is one of the promoters of MMPs [72], and MMP9 enhances the invasion and metastasis of cancer cells via this pathway [73]. However, the role of this pathway in conjunction with MMPs in bacterial meningitis remains unclear.

The Notch signalling pathway is an important pathway that determines cell fate. Notch ligand – receptor interactions between neighbouring cells regulate cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis. Activation of the Notch pathway by the exogenous Notch pathway activator Jagged1 resulted in increased proliferation of the neural stem and progenitor cells among patients with PM. It also promoted astrocyte differentiation and inhibited neuronal differentiation, implying that it may mediate the development of neurological sequelae in patients with PM [74]. Moreover, P75NTR, a member of the tumour necrosis factor receptor family, interacts with nerve growth factor, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, and neurotrophic factor-3 to mediate neuronal survival and apoptosis. A recent study has shown that blocking the P75NTR-regulated signalling pathway ameliorates neuroinflammation and promotes hippocampal neurogenesis in rats with PM [75].

Inflammatory cells and cytokines

After evading the host immune defence system, S. pneumoniae often produces multiple inflammatory factors through the activation of various signalling pathways mediated by PRRs and recruits more immune cells to trigger secondary immune response dysregulation in the host, resulting in adverse consequences, including cellular damage, BBB dysfunction, and CNS complications. IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) are detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with early-stage PM [76]. Moreover, the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 were elevated in rat brains 12 h following PM infection. BBB breakdown in the hippocampus was observed several hours after elevation, suggesting a possible correlation between elevated cytokine levels and BBB integrity [66]. The excessive inflammatory responses caused by meningitis may also affect hearing. By measuring the levels of inflammatory factors in PM mice, researchers have found that elevated levels of TNF-α were closely associated with hearing thresholds in mice and that reducing the expression of TNF-α with inhibitors significantly reduced damage to cochlear neurons [77]. IFN-γ is secreted by natural killer cells and has been implicated in the regulation of bacterial clearance during bacterial infections. IFN-γ can contribute to the inflammatory response by enhancing nitric oxide synthase 2 production in microglia, affecting BBB function and causing neuronal damage. IFN-γ knockout mice have reduced BBB breakdown and increased survival [78].

As the bacterial component is recognized by the host immune system and triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses, leukocytes are rapidly recruited to the brain along with pro-inflammatory cytokines, damaging the BBB. Perivascular macrophages and meningeal macrophages kill bacteria during PM by promoting neutrophil recruitment to the brain [79], thereby initiating the inflammatory response during PM. CXCL16, an early-expressed chemokine in PM, is predominantly found on the surface of macrophages. Once shed from the cell surface, CXCL16 promotes the migration of leukocytes expressing its receptor, CXCR6, to the pneumococcal infection site [80]. Additionally, CXCL8 released by pericytes directly propels neutrophil migration and recruitment to the site of pneumococcal infection via transmigration across the endothelial barrier [81]. The formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), consisting of DNA networks covered with antimicrobial peptides and histones, occurs upon neutrophil death. While effective in degrading virulence factors and killing bacteria, an excess of NETs hinders the normal neutrophil antimicrobial function, making bacterial clearance more challenging; the increased bacterial load allows bacteria to escape through the BBB to other organs [82].

During the fight against infection, the production of various inflammatory factors stimulates the activation of polymorphonuclear cells in the host. This leads to the subsequent secretion of large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which aid in bacterial eradication. However, while favouring the defence against infection, these oxidizing substances can also damage the BBB and aggravate neuronal damage by causing mitochondrial damage, lipid peroxidation, and excessive inflammation [83]. Common oxidants include superoxide anions (O2−), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), and peroxynitrite (ONOO-). To ameliorate oxidative damage in the brain, numerous antioxidants have been used. Evaluating the concentrations of these substances in the cerebrospinal fluid provides insights into the course and prognosis of meningitis [84,85].

Complement system activation

In addition to the inflammation caused by bacterial components and interactions with host cells, activation of the complement system is also involved in PM-mediated inflammation. The complement system plays a critical role in the innate immune system. However, excessive immune responses may cause more harm than good throughout PM infection. Complement activation is mainly triggered by changes in the structural domains of proteases and directly kills bacteria by forming a membrane attack complex. There are three pathways for complement activation: classical, alternative, and lectin pathways. The impairment of either pathway affects the ability of the host to defend against and clear S. pneumoniae. In the classical complement pathway, patients with C2 complement deficiency have impaired bacterial host phagocytosis owing to the reduced deposition of C3b-iC3b on S. pneumoniae [86]. MBL and ficolins-2 are key molecules in the lectin pathway for complement activation, and their absence increases susceptibility to invasive pneumococcal disease [87,88]. Complement factor H (FH) is a critical active regulator of the alternative activation pathway and can be detected in the brain parenchyma and vascular lumen of patients with PM. Its deficiency increases complement C3 depletion and, consequently, aggravates the disease. However, exogenous FH supplementation does not reduce the mortality rate among mice with PM [89]. S. pneumoniae PepO exhibits complement regulatory activity by binding with complement C1q, significantly activating the complement system and consuming complement components, thereby facilitating immune evasion [90]. Additionally, S. pneumoniae can bind FH through its Hic protein, preventing the complement system from marking it for phagocytosis. This interaction also promotes the cleavage of C3b into iC3b, thereby altering the host’s immune response to the pathogen [91].

Although an activated complement system can increase the resistance to infection, an overactive complement system may lead to an uncontrolled inflammatory response. High complement levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with bacterial meningitis strongly indicate disease severity and poor prognosis. Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid samples from patients with PM revealed elevated levels of the common complement factors C1q, C3a, iC3b, C5a, and sC5b-9, with higher levels of complement factors C5a and sC5b-9 in the cerebrospinal fluid of deceased patients compared with those in survivors, implying that high levels of C5a and C5b-9 May be associated with mortality [92]. Therefore, the early initiation of complement inhibition may benefit patients. The outcomes of complement deficiency and activation among patients with PM are related to pathogen and patient differences.

Receptors and sensory neurons

Recent research indicates that following bacterial invasion into the CNS, the host’s receptors and central neurons also participate in the host’s immune response. During PM, Ply in S. pneumoniae can stimulate Nav1.8 nociceptors to release calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which is stored in dense core vesicles at nerve endings. CGRP release downregulates the expression of chemotactic factors by binding to receptor activity modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) on meningeal macrophages, thereby weakening the host’s antibacterial defence [93]. Additionally, when S. pneumoniae traverses the BBB and contacts with CSF, bacterial metabolites, such as bitter compounds, are exposed to CSF-contacting neurons (CSF-cNs)These neurons, known to express orphaned bitter taste receptors, are activated by these compounds, leading to abundant and sustained calcium transients. Furthermore, upon contact with S. pneumoniae, CSF-cNs, upregulate cell factors and complement components related to antibacterial and immune responses, thereby enhancing the host’s survival [94].

The interaction between host and pathogen is complex and multifaceted, involving not only the participation of immune cells and inflammatory mediators but also the intricate network of neural signal transmission. While some responses contribute to maintaining host survival, others may counteract the host’s immune defence. The body continually adjusts to achieve a balance in these interactions. Therefore, the mechanisms and effects of the nervous system on meningeal immune cells in host defence are still to be clarified.

Treatment methods

Acute bacterial meningitis has a high mortality rate, and delayed diagnosis is strongly associated with increased mortality. Therefore, prompt clinical evaluation of patients is imperative, and ancillary tests, including blood cultures and CSF examination (unless lumbar puncture is contraindicated), should be conducted to facilitate an accurate diagnosis. Empirical antibiotic therapy should be immediately administered when bacterial meningitis is suspected. An interval of >3 h between admission and administration of antibiotics may be an influencing factor on PM-induced mortality [95].

The choice of antibacterial drugs should be based on the age of the patient and resistance patterns of S. pneumoniae worldwide. Penicillin and beta-lactams are commonly used for the treatment of bacterial meningitis. Since the emergence of penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae, considerable attention has been directed towards understanding penicillin resistance in S. pneumoniae, with the establishment of drug susceptibility breakpoints based on both laboratory and clinical data. The Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) introduced breakpoints for meningitis and non-meningitis samples of S. pneumoniae in 2008 [96], which have been in use since then. This differentiation is because of the short distance between capillaries and alveoli in the lungs, resulting in a proximity of antibiotic concentrations between alveoli and the bloodstream. However, the BBB drastically reduces penicillin concentrations in the CSF compared to those in the lungs and bloodstream. Using the same penicillin MIC breakpoint for both scenarios may lead to treatment failure in sensitive strains isolated from patients with meningitis. According to the European Society for Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases guidelines (Figure 3), amoxicillin/ampicillin/penicillin combined with cefotaxime is recommended for neonates (less than one month), and third-generation cephalosporin antibiotics (cefotaxime or ceftriaxone) are recommended for children and adults. Therefore, penicillin-resistant pneumococcal strains should be treated with vancomycin or rifampicin [97]. In addition to antibiotic treatment, corticosteroids are commonly employed as an adjuvant therapy in patients with PM as they help reduce inflammation associated with infection and stabilize the BBB. The use of dexamethasone as adjuvant therapy has significantly improved the mortality and prognosis of patients with PM in many high-income countries [98,99]. However, dexamethasone adjuvant therapy did not provide a survival advantage for patients in countries with scarce medical resources, possibly due to the large proportion of patients with HIV infection and delayed access to care because of poor local health care [100].

Treatment of S. pneumoniae meningitis.

For neonates under one month of age, treatment is primarily amoxicillin/ampicillin/penicillin combined with cefotaxime or amoxicillin/ampicillin combined with aminoglycosides, regardless of penicillin sensitivity or resistance; while for children and adults, cefotaxime or ceftriaxone is administered if the isolate is penicillin-sensitive and vancomycin or rifampin is used if the isolate is resistant. Steroids such as dexamethasone can be used as adjuvant therapy within 4 h of antibiotic initiation according to the European Society for Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) recommendations, and emerging therapeutic agents such as C5 antibodies, receptor blockers, daptomycin, and edaravone are still not available for routine treatment. Because current antibiotic and corticosteroid treatments have limitations in terms of applicability and efficacy, investigators have explored novel adjuvant therapeutic modalities, including treatment with complement inhibitors, receptor blockers, novel antibiotics, and free radical eliminators.

C5, an essential component of the common complement pathway, is a novel therapeutic target for bacterial meningitis, and treating PM mice with monoclonal antibodies against C5 reduces mortality and brain damage in mice [101]. pIgR and PECAM-1 are receptors for S. pneumoniae to interact with host brain cells; blocking these receptors with antibodies can reduce the number of bacteria in the brains of PM mice and contribute to a favourable prognosis [102]. The non-bacteriolytic antibiotic daptomycin is a candidate for treating acute bacterial meningitis caused by gram-positive bacteria. The combination of daptomycin and ceftriaxone reduced PM-induced hearing loss and neuroinflammation in young rats [103,104]. The antioxidant edaravone exerts anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects by scavenging free oxygen radicals. Recent studies have shown that the survival rate of mice with severe PM treated with edaravone increased, that the number of leukocytes and TNF-α levels in the CSF of mice with mild PM were significantly reduced, and that neurological function also improved [105]. During inflammation, reactive oxygen species are produced, and treating rats with antioxidants such as combined with the antibiotic ceftriaxone significantly reduces intracranial hypertension in rats [106]. Research has shown that MMPs cause damage to the brain during the PM period. Administration of MMP inhibitors to PM rats reduced both the inflammatory response and brain injury [107]. Although these new adjunctive therapies show promise in animal experiments, their effectiveness lacks clinical trial and evaluation. Therefore, ESCMID recommends not using them as routine treatments for PM until their effects are confirmed.

Summary and outlook

S. pneumoniae is a common causative agent of acute bacterial meningitis. Although vaccines containing common causative serotypes have been introduced to control invasive pneumococcal infections and reduce disease burden, bacterial meningitis-related morbidity and mortality remain high worldwide owing to serotype replacement as well as other factors. To enter the CNS, S. pneumoniae must cross the BBB or BCSFB. When crossing these barriers, pneumococcal virulence factors interact with host cells, thereby aiding bacterial adhesion and invasion and causing signalling pathway activation and inflammatory cascade responses. However, the targets of bacterial action in host cells after crossing the barrier and how they further damage neuronal cells remain unclear. The current standard treatment for PM includes a combination of penicillin and third-generation cephalosporins. Although innovative research is investigating therapies that could prevent bacteria from crossing the BBB, these methods have not yet been implemented in clinical practice to treat patients with PM. Therefore, determining therapeutic agents targeting both the bacterial component and associated host response can provide novel therapeutic methods for treating patients with meningitis; further in-depth research on the pathogenesis and pathological changes in PM, as well as novel treatment and prevention strategies, is warranted.
