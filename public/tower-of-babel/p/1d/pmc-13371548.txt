Into the future: first robotic totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass in Southeast Asia
Authors: Dudy Arman Hanafy, Sudhir Prem Srivastava, Widya Trianita Suwatri, Jenni Pratita, Muhammad Eros Zulfikar Syafruddin, Bimo Kusumo, Dicky Aligheri Wartono, Sugisman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery bypass grafting remains the most commonly performed cardiac surgery worldwide, including in Indonesia. Robotic approach offers a minimally invasive alternative benefiting both patients and surgeons. Nonetheless, its adoption remains limited globally.Case presentationWe report Southeast Asia’s first robotic totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass performed at Harapan Kita National Heart Centre, Indonesia in November 2024 using SSI Mantra™ Surgical Robotic System, highlighting key aspects in robotic program initiation including structured team training, patient selection, operative workflow, and management of intraoperative challenges during early adoption.ConclusionThis case represents a milestone in the dissemination of robotic coronary revascularization in Southeast Asia. Beyond its regional significance, it provides practical technical insights relevant to centers initiating robotic coronary programs.

Background

Coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG) remains standard treatment for complex coronary artery disease and the most performed cardiac surgery in many countries, including Indonesia [1].

Robotic CABG, performed since 1998, offers less pain and bleeding, faster recovery, and improved cosmesis for patients. For surgeons, it offers improved dexterity and ergonomics, even surpassing minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass [2].

Nonetheless, globally, robotic CABG remains confined to limited centres [2]. In November 2024, Harapan Kita National Heart Centre in partnership with SSI Mantra™ successfully performed the first robotic off-pump totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass (TECAB) in Indonesia and Southeast Asia. Here we report the case and highlight key insights in establishing robotic CABG program.

Case

Training and preparation

Prior to the procedure, a structured preparation phase was undertaken. The SSI Mantra™ robotic system had been installed at our institution three months prior to the procedure, allowing the entire team to engage in repeated hands-on simulation and workflow familiarization. This preparation period was complemented by formal lectures delivered by Dr. Sudhir Prem Srivastava. No full procedural rehearsals were performed before the actual case, and none of the team members had prior experience with robotic cardiac surgery or other robotic platforms. Therefore, emphasis was placed on comprehensive team familiarization, involving not only the primary surgeons but also anaesthesiologists, bedside assistants (surgical residents), scrub nurses, and technicians. At the time of the procedure, Dr. Srivastava attended as proctor together with his team, including surgical, scrub, and technical personnel, providing on-site guidance and support. This structured preparation and proctorship model were essential in enabling safe implementation of the program despite the absence of prior robotic experience.

Patient selection

Through our instituion multidisciplinary heart team discussion and further review with the proctoring team, a 68-year-old male with non-obese body mass index (27.6 kg/m2), no prior cardiac or thoracic surgery, good left ventricular function (ejection fraction 56%) and normal valves was selected. Despite three-vessel disease (Fig. 1), distal lesions in left circumflex and right coronary artery favored a single left internal mammary artery (LIMA)-left anterior descending (LAD) anastomosis.

Surgical technique

Patient was positioned with left chest at table’s edge and left arm alongside his body below table level, ensuring unobstructed robotic arm movement. A roll was placed under left thorax and table was tilted right 10–15° (Fig. 2).

Following CO₂ insufflation (maintained at around 8 mmHg), exposure was optimized by single-lung ventilation. Ports were inserted under endoscopic guidance. Port placement was standardized to optimize triangulation and instrument reach. A three-arm robotic configuration was used. The ports were placed along the anterior axillary line, with camera port at the 5th intercostal spaces and instrument ports at the 3rd and 7th intercostal spaces, maintaining adequate spacing (10–20 cm) to prevent external arm collision (Fig. 2).

The SSI Mantra™ robotic system was docked from the patient’s right side (Fig. 3). Instruments (Fig. 4) were exchanged along the procedure steps by bedside assistants. Cautery spatula and forceps were first utilised for skeletonised LIMA harvesting. After systemic heparinization, a subxiphoid approach was used to introduce the endoscopic stabilizer. The pericardium was opened anteriorly to expose the LAD, and additional posterior pericardiotomy was performed to facilitate drainage and improve visualization.

LIMA distal end was prepared and partially transected using fine tip scissors while the proximal end was left attached to the chest wall. Endoscopic stabilizer was positioned to immobilize the target segment of the LAD, which was occluded both distally and proximally using silastic snares before arteriotomy with micro blade and fine tip scissors followed by insertion of intracoronary shunt. The anastomosis was performed with robotic needle driver using a double-armed 8 − 0 GORE-TEX suture (10 cm in length) in a continuous fashion. Initial attempts using 7 − 0 Pronova sutures were unsuccessful due to suture breakage, likely related to instrument handling and lack of haptic feedback in addition to the relatively tachycardic heart, necessitating repetition of the anastomosis. Due to the unavailability of an endoscopic transit-time flowmetry probe, intraoperative graft flow assessment was not performed. After hemostasis, chest tubes were inserted, lungs re-inflated ports removed, and incisions meticulously closed. Total operative time was 545 min. Patient received 250 ml packed red cell transfusion intraoperatively.

Postoperative course

Patient was extubated after 22 h, spent 24 h in ICU, and discharged on day 7. Predischarge transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved function. As per our institution protocol, patient was kept on dual antiplatelet therapy for at least a year after the procedure. He resumed normal activity in less than a month and remained symptom-free at six months. Patient was very satisfied and gave written informed consent for publication of the case.

Discussion

Robotic technology offers several advantages in coronary revascularization, particularly in enabling totally endoscopic approaches and facilitating minimally invasive arterial grafting [2]. In this report, we describe the successful implementation of robotic TECAB in a new center, with emphasis on early program initiation, team training, and technical considerations.

Patient selection remains a critical determinant of success, particularly during the initial phase of program adoption. In our institution, all patients undergoing coronary revascularization are routinely evaluated through a multidisciplinary heart team discussion involving cardiologists and cardiac surgeons. In this case, a patient with preserved ventricular function, no prior thoracic surgery, and favorable coronary anatomy was selected through this established process. The decision was further discussed with the proctoring team prior to surgery to ensure suitability for a first case. Although the patient had multivessel disease, the presence of distal lesions in non-LAD territories allowed a focused LIMA–LAD revascularization strategy, minimizing procedural complexity. With the introduction of robotic TECAB, future multidisciplinary discussions may incorporate additional strategies, including minimally invasive and hybrid approaches combining TECAB and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), enabling more individualized treatment planning.

A structured preparation phase was essential prior to undertaking the procedure. The robotic system was installed at our institution three months in advance, allowing repeated simulation and team familiarization. This was complemented by formal lectures and direct supervision from an experienced robotic surgeon. Notably, none of the team members had prior robotic cardiac surgery experience. Therefore, training extended beyond the primary surgeon to include anaesthesiologists, bedside assistants, scrub nurses, and technical staff. At the time of surgery, on-site proctorship further supported safe program initiation.

Despite these preparations, several intraoperative challenges were encountered, reflecting the early learning curve. The total operative time of 545 min exceeded established benchmarks, largely reflecting the initial phase of program adoption, including docking, instrument and supply exchanges, as well as technical challenge during anastomosis. The challenge arose from both technical and physiological factors. From a surgical standpoint, the absence of haptic feedback increased the difficulty of anastomosis, ultimately necessitating repeated anastomosis. This was compounded by the relatively tachycardic heart, attributed to the combined effects of CO₂ insufflation and inotropic support in the context of reduced venous return. From an anaesthetic perspective, maintaining hemodynamic stability during single-lung ventilation and CO₂ insufflation required careful balancing of preload, heart rate, and systemic perfusion. These challenges were mitigated through optimization of insufflation pressure, minimization of PEEP, adequate volume loading, judicious use of vasopressors, avoidance of hypercapnia, and pharmacological rate control using amiodarone. Technical adjustments, including the use of a double-armed 8 − 0 GORE-TEX suture as supported by previous report and experience of our proctor, also improved anastomotic reliability during robotic suturing [3].

The absence of intraoperative transit-time flowmetry also represented a limitation, as objective graft assessment could not be performed intraoperatively. In this context, graft evaluation relied on visual inspection and postoperative clinical outcomes, including hemodynamic stability, absence of ischemic symptoms, and preserved ventricular function on echocardiography. Ensuring the availability of such adjuncts may be beneficial for future cases, particularly during the early learning phase.

Moreover, the relatively prolonged extubation time and length of stay in our case reflect institutional protocols and the early phase of program implementation. At our center, routine extubation is not performed in the operating room, and patients undergo a standardized stepwise postoperative pathway, including a minimum ICU stay of 24 h followed by intermediate care prior to transfer to the general ward. In addition, discharge requires postoperative echocardiographic evaluation, which may be influenced by scheduling availability. A more cautious postoperative approach was also adopted given this was the first case. With increasing experience and protocol optimization, these parameters are expected to improve.

Taken together, these findings highlight several practical considerations for centers initiating a robotic coronary program. These include early system availability for simulation and team training, careful patient selection with external input when needed, strong coordination between surgical and anaesthesia team to manage the physiological effects of CO₂ insufflation, and familiarity with robotic-specific technical nuances such as lack of tactile feedback. Additionally, ensuring access to appropriate instruments and adjunct technologies may further enhance procedural safety and efficiency.

Looking forward, ongoing developments in robotic cardiac surgery, including the emergence of automated anastomotic technologies, make this an area of significant interest with potential to further streamline procedures and facilitate broader adoption [1, 4].

Conclusion

This case marks an important milestone for cardiac surgery in Southeast Asia, demonstrating the feasibility of robotic totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass in a new center. Successful program initiation requires careful patient selection, appropriate infrastructure, and the ability to address both technical challenges and adapt institution-specific workflows. Overcoming the learning curve through structured team training, early system familiarization, and dedicated proctorship is essential. These experience-based insights may help guide other centers seeking to establish a robotic coronary program.
