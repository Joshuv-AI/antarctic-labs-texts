Regulated cell death: a multidimensional regulatory network in the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis
Authors: Wen Li, Liang Zhou, Jinji Zhang, Jingying Sun, Qing Feng, Na Zhu, Kunyi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncreasing evidence indicates that regulated cell death (RCD), such as apoptosis, anoikis, autophagy, pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis and cuproptosis, plays crucial roles in renal tissue injury and the progression of fibrosis by promoting inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix deposition. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of different types of RCD is essential for treating patients with renal fibrosis. This review comprehensively presents the distinct and interactive roles of RCD in renal fibrosis pathogenesis and elucidates the crosstalk among different forms of RCD and core fibrotic signaling pathways. Moreover, we analyzed clinically promising RCD-related therapeutic targets and identified future research directions, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the clinical treatment of renal fibrosis patients. To mitigate the threat of compensatory crosstalk between RCD pathways during clinical translation, the development of drugs that simultaneously target multiple forms of RCD may offer a novel approach for precise renal fibrosis treatment.Keywords: Renal fibrosis, Regulated cell death, Apoptosis, Autophagy, Necroptosis, Pyroptosis, Ferroptosis, Cuproptosis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 10% of the global population and is the third leading cause of premature death worldwide [1, 2]. Renal fibrosis is the primary pathological process associated with CKD and the final common pathway in end-stage renal disease, participating in the loss of renal function and kidney failure in patients [1]. With the increasing prevalence of CKD, renal fibrosis has become an important public health concern in China. This progressive condition arises through complex pathophysiological processes, with major triggers including those secondary to diabetic kidney disease (DKD), obstructive nephropathy (ON), ischemia‒reperfusion injury (IRI) and aristolochic acid nephropathy (AAN) [3–6]. Additional contributing factors include hypertension, obesity, and environmental exposure [7–9].

The pathogenesis of renal fibrosis is multifactorial and complex and involves various cell types, molecular pathways and pathophysiologic changes. Poor postinjury repair of renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) contributes to abnormal lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, which in turn promotes fibrosis [10]. A hallmark of renal fibrosis is the deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), where resident fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, hematopoietic cells and mesenchymal stromal cells in the kidneys transform into myofibroblasts that secrete large amounts of ECM [11]. Without sufficient intervention, the balance between ECM production and degradation is disrupted, which leads to excessive ECM deposition and the secretion of large amounts of collagen. Further development causes enlargement of the renal interstitium, the loss of peritubular capillaries, and destruction of the normal structure of the renal parenchyma, culminating in tissue sclerosis, scarring, and ultimately the loss of filtration and reabsorption functions [1, 12]. The transformation of myofibroblasts is driven by several signaling pathways, including the classical profibrotic transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)/Smad signaling pathway, the phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (PKB/AKT) signaling pathway, and the wingless-related integration site (Wnt)/β-catenin pathway [2, 9, 13]. Additionally, the ubiquitin‒proteasome system and mitochondrial‒peroxisome dysfunction play crucial roles in this process [14–16].

Inflammation of renal TECs is a key factor in the initiation of fibrosis. Neutrophils, macrophages, and T cells can exacerbate renal tubular epithelial inflammation through the secretion of proinflammatory factors and cytotoxic substances, which in turn can activate myofibroblasts and collagen deposition [4]. Proinflammatory cytokines further amplify the vicious cycle of renal inflammation and fibrosis by promoting inflammatory cell recruitment, fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition [17]. Furthermore, oxidative stress is another major contributor to renal fibrosis. The overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in TECs leads to apoptosis, lipid accumulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately promoting fibrosis [10]. In accordance with the Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death (NCCD), cell death can be classified into accidental cell death (ACD) and regulated cell death (RCD) on the basis of cellular morphology, function and biochemistry [18]. ACD is activated by unexpected injury that exceeds cellular repair mechanisms, leading to the onset of nonprogrammed cell death, mainly necroptosis [18]. In contrast, RCD is a genetically regulated process that can be regulated by intervention [19]. In the absence of external factors, RCD occurs throughout the life cycle as an intrinsic driver that mediates organismal development or tissue turnover. RCD occurring under exclusively physiological conditions is also known as programmed cell death (PCD) [18]. RCD includes mainly apoptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis, necroptosis, lysosomal cell death (LCD), ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, among others. The main function of RCD is to remove nonfunctional, damaged or harmful cells from the body to maintain normal life activities. However, pathological cell death can be induced under disease conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, neurological disease, and renal disease [20–23]. In CKD and acute kidney disease (AKD) patients, RCD causes parenchymal cell loss and is involved in the regulation of renal inflammation, fibrosis, and immune responses [24]. Multiple patterns of cell death have been detected in the context of renal fibrosis, often occurring simultaneously and interacting through various signaling pathways during fibrosis progression. In-depth research on the mechanisms of RCD have focused on the regulation of these pathways, which may offer targets for therapeutic benefits. This review not only summarizes the molecular mechanisms of various RCD modalities but also highlights their crosstalk, therapeutic potential as targets, and challenges with clinical translation.

Classical apoptosis regulates renal fibrosis via multiplex mechanisms

We begin with apoptosis, the most extensively characterized form of RCD, which serves as the initial and primary cellular response to kidney injury. Apoptotic depletion of TECs not only reduces the mass of functional nephrons but also activates compensatory profibrotic signaling from the surviving parenchyma. This section establishes the foundational role of classical cysteinyl aspartate-specific proteinase (Caspase)-dependent cell death in the initiation of the fibrotic cascade. The subsequent sections will examine how resistance to death (anoikis), inflammatory lytic death (pyroptosis/necroptosis), and metabolic death (ferroptosis/cuproptosis) build upon or diverge from this baseline to orchestrate progressive renal scarring. Death at the cellular level was first detected by researchers in the nineteenth century and was officially named apoptosis in 1972 [25]. Apoptosis is the first discovered and most classical RCD pathway and plays a critical role in the maintenance of organismal development. Its main morphological properties include chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation, cell membrane blistering, cell crumpling, and the formation of apoptotic vesicles [25]. Apoptosis proceeds via two canonical pathways: intrinsic apoptosis triggered by cellular stress (e.g., oxidative damage and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress), leading to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release, and extrinsic apoptosis initiated by death receptor (DR) ligation. In the fibrotic kidney, however, these pathways are preferentially engaged by disease-specific stimuli. Intrinsic apoptosis of TECs is driven predominantly by metabolic dysregulation, including lipid accumulation secondary to defective fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial dysfunction, rather than by classical developmental cues [10, 26]. Conversely, extrinsic apoptosis is amplified by the proinflammatory cytokine milieu characteristic of the injured kidney, particularly TGF-β1 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α secreted by infiltrating macrophages and injured TECs [27, 28].

Sustained injury to and apoptosis of TECs are core contributors to renal fibrosis [29]. Apoptosis reduces the number of epithelial cells and increases the number of fibroblasts through the release of profibrotic factors (e.g., TGF-β) [26, 28]. Deficiency of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) significantly increases early renal macrophage apoptosis. Furthermore, the antiapoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) is significantly downregulated, whereas the level of caspase-3 is upregulated, exacerbating renal fibrosis. These alterations can also affect renal macrophage polarization through the Janus kinase (JAK) 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)1/3 pathway, exacerbating tubular injury, renal apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in murine models of ON [27]. The C3a receptor, a key G protein-coupled receptor in the immune system, may also reduce the severity of experimental chronic pyelonephritis by inhibiting apoptosis and inflammation and regulating macrophage polarization [30]. Graphene quantum dots (GQDs), as nanomaterials, modulate calcium-dependent apoptosis associated with mitochondrial damage in animal models of adriamycin-induced nephropathy and 5/6 subtotal nephrectomy. GQDs balance intracellular Ca2+ levels, increase the mitochondrial membrane potential and improve the morphology of mitochondrial cristae via transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily c member 5 (TRPC5). TRPC5 attenuates renal apoptosis, prevents podocytosis, and ameliorates renal injury during renal fibrosis development [31]. TRPC5 is predominantly expressed in renal podocytes and proximal tubular cells, where it serves as a mechanosensitive and calcium-permeable channel. Its upregulation in CKD patients is correlated with glomerulosclerosis severity, suggesting that TRPC5-mediated calcium dyshomeostasis is a kidney-enriched vulnerability amenable to therapeutic intervention. Sirtuins (SIRTs) are a family of deacetylases that are widely distributed in cells and play significant roles in apoptosis and fibrosis. In CKD patients and mouse models of fibrosis, SIRT4 exhibits significant accumulation in the nucleus. Researchers have reported that upon stimulation with TGF-β, SIRT4 translocates from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm through the Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax)/Bcl-2 Antagonist/Killer 1 (Bak) pore. TGF-β further activates the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway and induces SIRT4 phosphorylation for its subsequent nuclear translocation, which promotes the splicing of cellular communication network factor 2 (CCN2) precursor messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) in the nucleus and induces the protein expression of CCN2, accelerating fibrosis. However, overall deletion or renal TEC-specific knockout of SIRT4 was shown to attenuate tubulointerstitial fibrosis [32]. This TGF-β1 → ERK → SIRT4 nuclear translocation → CCN2 splicing axis appears to be unique to renal TECs and has not been observed in dermal or pulmonary fibroblasts under identical stimuli, underscoring the organ-specific nature of apoptotic–fibrotic crosstalk in the kidney.

Oxidative stress, inflammatory damage, and apoptosis induced by metabolic disorder contribute to the development of DKD and ultimately renal fibrosis [33]. Finerenone (FIN), a novel nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, has been shown to improve mitochondrial dynamics and aberrant mitochondrial autophagy and to suppress apoptotic cascades in high glucose-induced HK-2 cells. Therefore, finerenone ameliorates renal inflammation and fibrosis in the context of DKD [34]. Studies have shown that treatment with atglistatin, an adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) inhibitor, reduces the levels of oxidized fatty acids levels and mitochondrial function while exacerbating lipid accumulation and oxidative stress injury, apoptosis, and renal fibrosis in TECs [10]. Notably, renal proximal tubular cells are uniquely dependent on FAO for ATP generation because of their high mitochondrial density and limited glycolytic capacity. This metabolic vulnerability renders TECs more sensitive to ATGL deficiency than other renal cell types, such as glomerular endothelial cells or podocytes, which retain greater metabolic flexibility. ATGL overexpression restored dysregulated FAO and alleviated renal fibrosis [10]. In addition to triglyceride accumulation, dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism, particularly the accumulation of ceramides, is another lipid-driven mechanism that promotes apoptosis and fibrosis. Ceramides generated by ceramide synthase 6 (CerS6) suppress PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin-mediated mitophagy to drive tubulointerstitial fibrosis, while impaired sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase acid-like 3b (SMPDL3b) function in podocytes increases sphingosine-1-phosphate and ceramide-1-phosphate levels to disrupt glomerular integrity and acute kidney injury (AKI)-induced proximal tubule ceramide lipotoxicity [35–37]. Considering these data with the predictive value of serum ceramide levels, dysregulated sphingolipid pathways link cellular stress to structural kidney deterioration. Such ceramide-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and podocytopathy directly contribute to the initiation and progression of CKD and glomerulosclerosis. BX-001N, a synthetic polyethylene glycol-conjugated bilirubin 3α nanoparticle, can decrease oxidative stress in TECs, inhibit inflammatory cell infiltration, and reduce acute injury to renal tissues and renal tubular apoptosis, all of which are conducive to the alleviation of chronic fibrosis after acute injury [38].

Renal fibrosis is also among the main pathological features of hereditary nephropathy Alport syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in collagen type IV α3 (COL4α3), COL4α4, or COL4α5 [39]. The COL4α3 p.G799R mutation leads to the disruption of the proper assembly and secretion of heterotrimers, leading to the accumulation of COL4α3 chains in the ER. This activates myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, increasing inflammation and apoptosis in renal tissues of mice expressing this mutant protein and promoting renal injury and fibrosis [40]. Exosomes play a dual role in the progression of CKD and renal fibrosis, depending on the cell type of origin, the molecular content of the cell, and microenvironmental conditions. For example, exosomes can promote renal fibrosis by mediating communication between renal TECs and fibroblasts. Damaged or stressed TECs release exosomes that carry profibrotic factors such as miR-21 and TGF-β, which activate stromal fibroblasts and drive excessive ECM deposition [41]. Renal injury tends to increase the expression of tubule-derived exosomal-tumor necrosis factor-α-induced protein 8 (TNFAIP8), which promotes renal fibrosis by protecting fibroblasts from apoptosis and by targeting p53 for ubiquitination and degradation [42]. Furthermore, during the progression from AKI to CKD, macrophages release exosomes carrying miR-204-5p, which suppresses mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) expression in TECs, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of the inflammation‒fibrosis axis, and the promotion of fibrosis [43]. On the other hand, mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) carry a variety of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids and nucleic acids, and hold great potential for regulating inflammation, tissue regeneration, and repair. EVs derived from human umbilical cord MSCs (hucMSC-EVs) effectively suppress the expression of miR-146b-5p in glomerular mesangial cells under high-glucose conditions, upregulate Merlin expression, prevent the nuclear translocation of Yes-associated protein (YAP), improve renal function, and reduce collagen deposition, demonstrating a significant antifibrotic effect [44]. MSC-EVs also attenuate renal interstitial fibrosis in the context of DKD by inhibiting the NUAK family SNF1-like kinase 1 (NUAK1, a serine/threonine kinase involved in fibrotic signaling)/YAP axis [45]. In a mouse model of Alport syndrome, miR-146a-5p-rich MSC-EVs effectively alleviated renal dysfunction by promoting the polarization of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype [46]. The level of exosomal miR-122-5p is decreased in mice with unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO)-induced kidney fibrosis and TGF-β1-stimulated HK-2 cells. However, restoring its levels directly leads to hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α (HIF-1α)-mediated inhibition of TGF-β1/Smad signaling activation [47]. Therefore, the use of exosomes to modulate renal fibrosis through multiple mechanisms offers important directions for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. These findings suggest that apoptosis in renal TECs, podocytes, macrophages, and fibroblasts plays a key role in renal fibrosis, and controlling fibroblast apoptosis may be an important research direction for renal fibrosis treatment (Fig. 1).

Apoptosis in renal cells mediates the progression of renal fibrosis primarily through two major molecular mechanisms: the mitochondrial-mediated intrinsic pathway and the death receptor (DR)-mediated extrinsic pathway. Intrinsic stimuli induce B-cell lymphoma-2-associated X protein (Bax)/B-cell lymphoma-2 Antagonist/Killer 1 (Bak) to form oligomeric membrane pores on the mitochondrial outer membrane, leading to its permeabilization and the release of cytochrome C. Cytochrome C forms apoptotic bodies with apoptotic protease activating factor 1 (Apaf-1) and pro-caspase-9, activating caspase-9 and subsequently triggering the downstream effector caspases caspase-3 and caspase-7, thereby initiating the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) and B-cell lymphoma-extra large (Bcl-XL) inhibit the formation of oligomeric pores in the mitochondrial outer membrane. P53 participates in apoptosis regulation by promoting the transcription of proapoptotic factors such as Bax or inhibiting antiapoptotic factors such as Bcl-2. In the extrinsic pathway, Fas cell surface death receptor ligand (FasL) binds to Fas, recruiting Fas-associating protein with a novel death domain (FADD) and pro-caspase-8 to form a death-inducing signaling complex. Pro-caspase-8 is activated by autocatalytic cleavage, leading to downstream caspase-3/7 activation and promoting renal cell apoptosis and fibrosis. Moreover, caspase-8 cleaves and activates BH3 interacting-domain death agonist (Bid), which in turn activates Bax/Bak or directly translocates to mitochondria, causing permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane. The binding of FasL to Fas results in the recruitment of cellular FLICE-inhibitory protein (c-FLIP), which has an antiapoptotic effect. Another recruited protein, death domain associated protein (DAXX), activates the apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1)/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway to promote apoptosis. The binding of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) to TNF receptor (TNFR) results in the recruitment of tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated death domain (TRADD), which functions similarly to FADD but also activates the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) to protect cells from apoptosis. In the absence of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) on the surface of renal macrophages, the presence of transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) activates the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway, promoting macrophage apoptosis and the release of cellular contents or metabolites, thereby contributing to renal fibrosis [27]. In renal podocytes, or under conditions such as inflammation, calcium overload, oxidative stress, or mechanical stress, transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC) may disrupt intracellular calcium homeostasis. The resulting mitochondrial damage can induce podocyte apoptosis and promote renal fibrosis [31]. In renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs), TGF-β1 induces the nuclear translocation of Sirtuin 4 (SIRT4), promotes connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) expression and exacerbates fibrosis [32]. The overexpression of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) reduces lipid accumulation, reduces the levels of oxidized fatty acids, restores mitochondrial function, and alleviates oxidative stress and apoptosis, thereby mitigating renal fibrosis [10]

Anoikis contributes to renal fibrosis

Whereas apoptosis mediates the elimination of damaged epithelial cells after injury occurs, this section addresses a contrasting phenomenon: anoikis resistance. Paradoxically, fibrosis progression requires the survival and persistence of activated myofibroblasts to drive pathological ECM deposition. In the context of anoikis resistance, fibroblasts escape death signals, which promotes their accumulation in the renal interstitium. This section thus delineates the critical transition from cell loss (apoptosis) to pathological cell retention (anoikis resistance), explaining how the failure of detachment-induced death underpins irreversible scar formation.

Anoikis is a specialized form of RCD that occurs when cells detach from the ECM and prevents their reattachment to new substrates [48]. Like classical apoptosis, anoikis can be activated by both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. The intrinsic pathway is regulated primarily through the mitochondria and is triggered by the proapoptotic factor Bcl-2 interacting mediator of cell death (Bim), which promotes the formation of Bax/Bak oligomers on the outer mitochondrial membrane. This affects the function of the antiapoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma-extra large (Bcl-XL) [49, 50]. The extrinsic pathway is mediated by DR and leads to the activation of the caspase cascade [51]. While anoikis plays an essential role in tissue development and homeostasis maintenance, resistance to anoikis is often exploited under pathological conditions, such as cancer metastasis. Dyshomeostasis of cells and the ECM allows cells to survive, spread and acquire resistance to anoikis, which promotes tumorigenesis [52]. Recent studies have suggested that abnormalities in anoikis may also contribute to the fibrotic process. For example, fibroblasts in both SiO2-induced lung inflammation and nanomaterial-induced lung fibrosis models exhibit anoikis resistance. This resistance drives abnormal fibroblast proliferation and excessive collagen deposition, leading to persistent and irreversible pulmonary fibrosis [53, 54]. In the kidney, the concept of anoikis resistance is particularly relevant to two distinct cellular compartments: (i) myofibroblasts that evade detachment-induced death to persist in the fibrotic interstitium and (ii) TECs undergoing epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT), which acquire anoikis resistance as part of their phenotypic conversion. The following discussion addresses both aspects, highlighting the unique contribution of the renal ECM microenvironment, which is rich in collagen IV, laminin, and vitronectin, to the regulation of anoikis sensitivity.

Fibroblasts depend on transcription factors such as TGF-β/Smad for survival, and TGF-β1 has been shown to promote anoikis resistance in myofibroblasts via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [55]. Fei et al. explored the effect of TGF-β1 on HK-2 cells in an anoikis model by disrupting integrin–ECM interactions. Their results revealed that TGF-β1 treatment significantly induced anoikis resistance in HK-2 cells and that blocking PI3K/AKT signaling alleviated this effect. Additionally, the apoptosis-related gene encoding tumor-suppressing STF cDNA 3 (TSSC3) attenuated TGF-β1-induced anoikis resistance in myofibroblasts through the PI3K/AKT pathway [56]. Given that TGF-β1 can also induce SIRT4 phosphorylation, leading to its nuclear translocation, further research is needed to understand whether SIRT4 is involved in anoikis regulation [32]. Targeting integrins has been suggested as a strategy to inhibit anoikis resistance in the context of cancer [49]. In the context of renal fibrosis, macrophages play a crucial role in activating fibroblasts by creating a vitronectin-rich microenvironment. This process involves the integrin αvβ5 and steroid receptor coactivator (SRC) kinase signaling pathways, which may also be linked to escape from anoikis resistance [57].

While anoikis resistance in fibroblasts contributes directly to collagen deposition and fibrosis, epithelial cell anoikis is more closely regulated by EMT-related genes, which influence the initiation of fibrosis and cellular transformation. Mucin-1 (MUC-1) serves as part of the protective barrier in normal epithelial cells and regulates apoptosis, EMT, and tumor cell metabolism through the MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt pathways [58]. Jean-Baptiste Gibier et al. studied MUC-1 in a renal IRI model and reported that distal tubule-enriched MUC-1 prevents renal epithelial cell anoikis following acute renal ischemic injury and acute necrosis of renal tubules. However, sustained MUC-1 expression after severe ischemia is closely associated with EMT, which contributes to renal fibrosis. This segmental restriction of MUC-1 expression explains the differential susceptibilities of the proximal and distal tubules to ischemic injury and subsequent fibrotic remodeling. These findings suggest that MUC-1 may promote fibrosis by influencing the inflammatory response and that its late profibrotic effects may be linked to anoikis resistance [59].

Inflammation is a key driver of fibrosis, with monocytes playing important roles in renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Monocytes and myofibroblasts colocalize during early fibrosis, at which time monocytes express TGF-β1, stimulating myofibroblast accumulation and potentially mediating anoikis in the context of renal fibrosis [60]. Granzyme B has also been shown to mediate aberrant anoikis in inflammatory diseases, leading to ECM remodeling and fibrosis [61]. Studies on the crosstalk between renal fibrosis and anoikis are still in their early stages (Fig. 2). In the future, further studies that combine single-cell sequencing and organoid modeling are needed, which may provide a direction for the development of novel antifibrotic therapies.

Extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolic imbalance, immune cell-mediated abnormal ECM remodeling, and transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) and Mucin-1 (MUC-1) induce resistance to anoikis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through multiple key signaling pathways, collectively driving the progression of renal fibrosis. Cell detachment from the ECM disrupts intercellular connections, interrupting integrin-mediated ECM signaling. This induces the excessive activation of the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway, leading to the activation of factors such as BH3 interacting-domain death agonist (Bid) and B-cell lymphoma-2 interacting mediator of cell death (Bim). Consequently, the caspase cascade is triggered, ultimately resulting in anoikis. When renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) are stimulated, leading to an imbalance in ECM production and degradation, the focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) pathway becomes abnormally activated. This increases the expression of B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) and B-cell lymphoma-extra large (Bcl-XL), thereby inhibiting apoptosis and anoikis. This phenomenon, known as anoikis resistance, promotes aberrant cell survival and migration, ultimately contributing to renal fibrosis. Immune cells such as CD8+ T cells or NK cells secrete granzyme B, which cleaves the ECM, leading to abnormal ECM remodeling. This promotes anoikis resistance and the development of renal fibrosis [61]. The TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway can also promote renal fibrosis by inducing anoikis resistance through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [55, 56]. Following prolonged severe ischemic injury, MUC-1 in epithelial cells may promote EMT by regulating the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), PI3K/AKT, and wingless-related integration site (Wnt) signaling pathways, thereby contributing to the development of renal fibrosis [59]

Renal fibrosis and nonapoptotic RCD

Autophagy exerts dual effects on renal fibrosis

The preceding sections focused on cell-intrinsic suicide (apoptosis) and microenvironmental survival cues (anoikis resistance). We now examine autophagy, which operates not simply as a death pathway but also as a homeostatic rheostat with context-dependent dual functions. Basal autophagy preserves tubular integrity through organelle quality control, whereas sustained or excessive autophagic flux may lead to the transition from prosurvival signaling to profibrotic execution. This perspective serves as a bridge to the subsequent discussion of overtly lytic and inflammatory modes of RCD (pyroptosis and necroptosis), which are frequently triggered upon autophagic dyshomeostasis under chronic stress.

Autophagy is a conserved catabolic process involving the sequestration of cytoplasmic cargo within double-membrane autophagosomes for subsequent lysosomal degradation [62, 63]. In terms of macroautophagy, microautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy, scientific research has focused mainly on macroautophagy [64]. While its core machinery, which includes the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and adenosine 5’-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathways, is ubiquitous, the triggers, functional outcomes, and therapeutic implications of autophagy in renal fibrosis are highly context-dependent and kidney specific [65].

Autophagy plays a dual role in renal fibrosis, with both protective and pathological effects that may promote fibrosis progression. Mitochondrial autophagy can delay fibrosis by removing dysfunctional mitochondria, reducing oxidative stress, and preventing apoptosis caused by mitochondrial dysfunction [26]. Lycopene intervention has been shown to inhibit renal AKT signaling and promote the generation of autophagosomes, facilitating the removal of damaged mitochondria. Increasing autophagic flux inhibits the TGF-β/Smad pathway and EMT and ameliorates renal fibrosis [66]. Additionally, the activation of macrophage autophagy can attenuate inflammation after AKI and improve renal function by regulating mitochondrial biosynthesis in renal TECs via exosomal miR-195a-5p [67]. Transcription factor EB (TFEB), a key regulator of autophagy and lysosomal gene transcription, restores autophagosome‒lysosome fusion and enhances autophagic flux through binding with soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) [68]. In addition, TFEB overexpression or promoter demethylation using 5-azacytidine has been shown to mitigate renal fibrosis [68]. Furthermore, trehalose, a lysosomal activator, accumulates in lysosomes, inhibits mTORC1 activity, and triggers TFEB-mediated autophagy, thus reducing cisplatin-induced chronic kidney injury [69].

Autophagy-related gene (ATG) 5 expression is significantly increased in patients with CKD and mice with hyperuricemic nephropathy, AAN and unilateral renal ischemia‒reperfusion injury (UIRI). The expression of ATG5 leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and renal inflammation by regulating heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) activity and HIF-1ɑ-mediated glycolysis, and its sustained activation promotes renal fibrosis [70]. In TGF-β1-induced fibrosis, ATG5 reduces intracellular spermine levels by upregulating the expression of spermine oxidase, which contributes to ECM deposition and renal fibrosis [71]. Caloric restriction, which promotes autophagy through mTORC1 signaling, exacerbates renal insufficiency, tubular injury, and fibrosis in IRI mice. However, knockdown of ATG7 in renal tubules or the use of the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine alleviated caloric restriction-induced renal fibrosis [72].

In conclusion, the role of autophagy in renal fibrosis may vary with cell type, disease stage or the presence of predisposing factors (Fig. 3). For example, protective macrophage autophagy in the context of AKI may translate into profibrotic effects in the chronic phase [67, 73]. Future studies are needed to better define the specific contributions of different cell types and pathways to autophagy.

Mechanisms of the autophagy pathway and its bidirectional regulation of tubular damage and renal fibrosis during different stages of disease progression. Autophagy involves the transport of cytoplasmic material to lysosomes via double-membrane autophagosomes, which fuse with lysosomes to form autophagolysosomes. The signaling pathways associated with cellular autophagy involve complex interactions among multiple proteins and signaling molecules, including phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT), adenosine 5’-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK). When mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) activity is inhibited, the UNC-51-like kinase 1 (ULK) complex is activated, initiating the autophagy pathway. This process involves the following steps: Phagophore formation, phagophore expansion (the formation of the autophagy-related gene (ATG) 5/12/16L1 complex), the formation of autophagosomes (lipophilic microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3)-II binds to autophagosomes), the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes (mediated by soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) proteins) and autolysosome (the hydrolases within lysosomes, such as proteases and nucleases, degrade the inner membrane and contents of autophagosomes). Activated transcription factor EB (TFEB) promotes SNARE complex-mediated autophagosome‒lysosome fusion, maintaining the integrity of autophagic flux. mTORC1 inhibits TFEB nuclear translocation by increasing its phosphorylation. Under conditions of chronic stress, autophagy protects renal tubular epithelial cell (TECs) by clearing damaged organelles and protein aggregates while alleviating oxidative stress. Defects in autophagy increase cellular sensitivity to injury and accelerate fibrosis [26]. During the adaptive repair phase following AKI, sustained autophagy activation may promote fibrosis by facilitating lysosomal membrane damage, abnormal renal cell death, and inflammatory responses [70]

Pyroptosis regulates the progression of renal fibrosis

Building upon the concept of homeostatic collapse introduced with autophagy, we now turn to pyroptosis, an intrinsically proinflammatory and lytic RCD modality. Unlike the immunologically silent clearance of apoptotic bodies or the uncertain outcomes of autophagy, pyroptosis is defined by gasdermin (GSDM)-mediated membrane perforation and the release of potent damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and cytokines (interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18). The section of these factors highlights why pyroptosis functions as a central amplifier of sterile inflammation in the fibrotic kidney. The resulting inflammatory milieu directly activates quiescent fibroblasts, providing a mechanistic bridge linking innate immune activation to scar formation and chronic fibrosis progression.

Pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory RCD, has received much attention in recent years. It is a type of lysogenic cell death mediated by GSDM family proteins such as GSDMD and GSDME. Canonical pyroptosis relies on the activation of caspase-1 by inflammasomes such as NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3). Caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD, forming membrane pores that lead to cell swelling, lysis, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18, and high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1)) [74]. Extracellular HMGB1 acts as a potent DAMP, engaging toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on the surface of neighboring tubular cells and infiltrating macrophages. This HMGB1/TLR4 interaction subsequently activates the downstream MyD88/nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, thereby amplifying the inflammatory cascade and promoting the development of a profibrotic microenvironment [75, 76]. Noncanonical pyroptosis is mediated primarily by caspase-4/5 (in humans) or caspase-11 (in mice) and is typically triggered by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [77]. Unlike the canonical pathway, the noncanonical pathway does not depend on inflammasomes but still induces cell membrane perforation and pyroptosis through the cleavage of GSDMD. Perforin released by cytotoxic T cells and NK cells facilitates the entry of granzymes (such as granzyme A) into target cells, directly cleaving GSDMB to generate its N-terminal fragment (GSDMB-NT), leading to the formation of membrane pores and triggering pyroptosis [78]. Additionally, caspase-3 activated by the DR can cleave GSDME, generating GSDME-NT with perforin-secreting activity, thereby inducing pyroptosis [79].

Abnormal activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome can directly promote glomerular injury, tubulointerstitial inflammation, fibrosis, and vascular dysfunction. The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a role in the development of various kidney diseases, including AKI, DKD, and chronic renal fibrosis [80]. Notably, thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) has emerged as a critical sensor linking oxidative stress to NLRP3 inflammasome activation in the context of renal injury. The increase in ROS levels induced by mitochondrial oxidative stress enhances the binding of TXNIP and NLRP3, thereby activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, which plays a crucial role in ischemia‒reperfusion-induced AKI [81]. Nicotine drives the interaction between TXNIP and NLRP3 via the acid sphingomyelinase signaling pathway, increasing the colocalization of NLRP3/apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) and NLRP3/caspase-1, which leads to caspase-1 activation and IL-1β release, resulting in podocyte death and functional and structural damage. Treatment with the TXNIP inhibitors verapamil or SRI-37330 can block this process [82]. Additionally, omarigliptin alleviates pyroptotic kidney injury by increasing endogenous glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) activity, thereby indirectly inhibiting the TXNIP/NLRP3/GSDMD axis [83]. This mechanistic link underscores how metabolic dysregulation feeds into the inflammatory and cell death machinery within the renal filtration barrier.

In the context of renal fibrosis, pyroptosis is predominantly executed by two cell types: (i) renal TECs, where GSDMD/E cleavage directly compromises tubular integrity and induces DAMP release, and (ii) renal infiltrating macrophages, where GSDMD-mediated pore formation amplifies the local inflammatory milieu. In the kidneys of UUO mice, renal TECs pyroptosis mediated by the caspase-1/GSDMD and caspase-3/GSDME pathways increased in a time-dependent manner, which was accompanied by a sustained increase in the expression of the transcription factor Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4). KLF4 regulates the progression of AKI into CKD and renal fibrosis. Blocking the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of KLF4 initiates renal tubular cell pyroptosis to promote inflammation and renal fibrosis, and UUO-induced cell pyroptosis and renal fibrosis are attenuated by specific knockout of KLF4 [84]. Upon ureteral obstruction, TNFα/Caspase3/GSDME signaling is activated, leading to GSDME cleavage and subsequent pyroptosis in renal TECs. This results in the release of HMGB1, a crucial mediator of inflammation and tissue damage, which promotes tubular loss and fibrosis [85]. The knockdown of caspase3 in TECs improves renal function by reducing renal tubular cell pyroptosis and inflammatory cell recruitment [85]. GSDMD is significantly upregulated following renal IRI or folate-induced injury. Its active fragment, GSDMD-N, is primarily localized to CD68⁺ macrophages, and its expression is positively correlated with the degree of fibrosis in various forms of CKD. Macrophage-specific knockout of GSDMD ameliorates pyroptosis, inflammation, and renal fibrosis. This tubule–macrophage crosstalk is particularly relevant in the kidney because renal TECs and interstitial macrophages reside in close proximity within the tubulointerstitial compartment. Damaged TECs secrete IFN-α, which acts on neighboring macrophages via IFNαR1 to potentiate GSDMD cleavage—a paracrine amplification loop that is likely more pronounced in the spatially constrained renal interstitium than in other organs with looser tissue architectures [86]. Overall, the relative contribution of canonical (caspase-1-dependent) versus noncanonical (caspase-4/5/11-dependent) pathways varies by etiology. UUO-induced fibrosis relies heavily on NLRP3 inflammasome activation in TECs, whereas IRI-associated fibrosis involves both TEC-intrinsic and macrophage-derived pyroptotic signaling.

Mitochondrial damage is a key pathological feature of renal fibrosis, and pyroptosis contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion in TECs. Deficiency of family with sequence similarity 3 member A (FAM3A) leads to mitochondrial ROS accumulation through the PI3K/AKT/Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) signaling pathway. This accumulation activates NLRP3 and triggers caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis, which exacerbates renal fibrosis after IRI [87]. Pyroptosis is also associated with mitochondrial DNA leakage, which activates pyroptosis via the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. Restoring mitochondrial function can reduce mitochondrial DNA leakage, suppress pyroptosis, and mitigate renal injury [88]. In summary, inhibiting pyroptosis may emerge as a therapeutic strategy for treating renal fibrosis. The therapeutic potential of the NLRP3-specific inhibitor MCC950 has been extensively investigated. Animal studies have indicated that MCC950 reduces renal tissue inflammation, pyroptosis, and fibrosis by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby decreasing the cleavage and release of caspase-1, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18 [89, 90]. In addition to MCC950, other intervention strategies have demonstrated potential antipyroptotic and antifibrotic effects. For instance, berberine can alleviate inflammation and fibrosis in DKD models by inhibiting interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) expression and pyroptosis [91]. These findings suggest that in-depth investigations of the interaction networks between pyroptosis and other fibrotic pathways could provide safer and more effective strategies for the clinical treatment of renal fibrosis patients (Fig. 4).

Renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) or endothelial cells activate the release of proinflammatory factors through multiple pyroptotic pathways, thereby activating fibroblasts and promoting extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, which mediates the onset and progression of renal fibrosis. In the classic pyroptosis pathway, when renal TECs detect damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), or other stimuli, NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3) recruits pro-caspase-1 via the apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) adaptor protein to form the multiprotein complex known as the inflammasome. Upon inflammasome activation, pro-caspase-1 undergoes self-cleavage and is converted into its active form, caspase-1. Activated caspase-1 has dual functions: it cleaves pro-Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and pro-IL-18 to generate the mature proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 and cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), resulting in the production of gasdermin D N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-NT) with perforin-secreting activity. GSDMD-NT forms pores in the cell membrane, leading to osmotic imbalance, cell swelling, and membrane rupture. This releases inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and IL-18, ultimately triggering pyroptosis. Additionally, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) phosphorylation promotes the transcription of NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β/18, providing the molecular basis for pyroptosis. The noncanonical pyroptosis pathway is mediated by caspase-4/5/11 through the direct recognition of intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS). This pathway similarly relies on GSDMD-NT cleavage to form membrane pores, leading to cell lysis and inflammatory cytokine release. Other pyroptotic pathways also exist. Death receptor (DR)-mediated activation of caspase-3 induces the production of GSDME-NT with pore-forming activity. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)α/caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis promotes the release of inflammatory mediators such as high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1) from renal TECs, amplifying the inflammatory response and driving renal fibrosis. Perforin released by cytotoxic T cells and NK cells promotes the entry of granzyme into cells, thereby participating in the activation of other pyroptotic pathways. Furthermore, granzyme A can induce GSDMB-NT perforation, leading to the induction of pyroptosis. Inflammatory mediators released during renal TEC or endothelial cell pyroptosis (such as IL-1β/18 and HMGB1) create an inflammatory microenvironment. This environment activates fibroblasts and promotes ECM deposition by recruiting immune cells, upregulating the expression of profibrotic factors and promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby accelerating renal fibrosis [86–88]

Necroptosis is involved in renal fibrosis pathogenesis

Although it closely parallels pyroptosis in terms of lytic morphology and proinflammatory outcomes, necroptosis is driven by the distinct receptor-interacting protein kinase (RIPK)1/RIPK3/mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) signaling axis. Necroptosis acts as a “fail-safe” lytic pathway when caspase-8 activity is compromised, ensuring pathogen clearance but often inflicting collateral tissue damage under sterile fibrotic conditions. This section completes our survey of inflammatory cell death modalities and illustrates how the kinase RIPK1 functions as a critical signaling hub, integrating inputs from TNF receptors, TLRs, and metabolic stress to couple extracellular fibrotic cues with terminal cell fate decisions in the renal parenchyma.

Necroptosis is a form of RCD that mimics necrosis in terms of cell morphology, such as membrane rupture. Necroptosis is precisely regulated and triggered by DRs (such as TNF receptors), TLRs or pathogen receptors. TNFα binds to TNFR, recruiting adaptor proteins such as tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated death domain (TRADD), RIPK1, and the ubiquitin E3 ligases tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF2) and cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 1/2 (clAP1/2) to form Complex I [92]. RIPK1 undergoes ubiquitination while simultaneously mediating the recruitment and activation of TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and the inhibitor of nuclear factor κB kinase (IKK) complex. The IKK complex phosphorylates IκB, leading to its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This releases NF-κB in the nucleus, where it becomes activated to promote cell survival [93–95]. Deubiquitination of RIPK1 initiates downstream caspase cascade-mediated apoptosis or pyroptosis [96, 97]. Under conditions in which caspase-8 is inhibited or under other specific circumstances, RIPK1 dissociates from Complex IIa. Upon interacting with its receptor, it forms a complex with RIPK3, also known as the necrosome, and subsequently phosphorylates MLKL [98]. Activated MLKL oligomerizes and translocates to the cell membrane, disrupting membrane integrity and triggering the release of cellular contents and inflammatory responses, resulting in necroptosis [99]. In addition to RIPK1, RIPK3 can also be activated through interactions with other proteins, such as TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF) and Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1), thereby mediating events downstream of necroptosis [100].

TECs necroptosis can cause acute tubular necrosis, renal unit loss and renal fibrosis, resulting in sustained renal tissue damage [101]. RIPK1 is upregulated in glomerular podocytes in the context of DKD, promoting podocyte injury and proteinuria. The inhibition of RIPK3 reduces mitochondrial fission and dysfunction by decreasing the phosphorylation of MLKL, phosphoglycerate mutase 5 (PGAM5) and phosphorylated dynamin-related protein 1 (p-Drp1), ameliorating albuminuria and podocyte loss, and improving renal ultrastructure in DKD models [102]. Necroptosis is strongly proinflammatory and induces the release of DAMPs that recruit immune cells to exacerbate local inflammation and create a profibrotic microenvironment [103]. In cadmium-induced AKI, necroptosis drives an inflammatory cascade in renal TECs, triggering “necroinflammation”. This process is accompanied by significant mitochondrial dysfunction, exacerbating tubular injury and fibrosis [104]. Basophils can also promote myofibroblast activation through the secretion of proinflammatory factors [105]. Necroptosis of renal peritubular capillary endothelial cells leads to disruption of the microvascular network, a process that is particularly devastating in the kidney because peritubular capillaries are essential for tubular oxygenation and solute exchange. The resultant microvascular rarefaction is a hallmark of progressive CKD and directly contributes to chronic hypoxia-driven fibrosis. Activation of the Piezo1/Ca2⁺/HIF-1α axis—a mechanosensitive pathway enriched in renal endothelial cells—can interrupt pathological endothelial–fibroblast communication, restore microvascular integrity, and inhibit the development of renal fibrosis [106]. These findings suggest that endothelial cell necroptosis may also play a crucial role in renal fibrosis. Inhibitors of necroptosis may be used to suppress renal fibrosis. For example, mitogen-activated protein kinase 2 (MK2) and IKK can phosphorylate RIPK1 at Ser320 and Ser25, respectively, thereby inhibiting RIPK1 kinase activity. This suppresses necrosome assembly and RIPK1-dependent necrotic cell death, maintaining tissue homeostasis [107, 108]. Necrostatin-1 specifically inhibits RIPK1 kinase activity, thereby blocking RIPK1-mediated necroptosis [109].

Studies have shown that necroptosis can interact with other types of RCD to play complex roles in the process of renal fibrosis. For example, in a renal injury model associated with renal calcinosis, GSDMD-knockout mice exhibited severe renal tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis. Although pyroptosis signaling was not significantly altered, the number of necroptotic cells and the expression of necroptosis markers increased. The application of a specific RIPK1 inhibitor attenuated renal injury, interstitial fibrosis, macrophage infiltration, and renal calcinosis in these mice [110]. Dexmedetomidine attenuates lipopolysaccharide-induced AKI and reduces NLRP3 formation to prevent necroptosis and pyroptosis [111]. Considerable evidence suggests that there is a complex link between necroptosis and pyroptosis. In addition, studies have shown a close link between ferroptosis and necroptosis during the course of AKI [112]. In mice with folate-induced AKI, the levels of lipid peroxidation and glutathione (GSH)-metabolizing proteins are decreased, and the first form of cell death that occurs is primarily ferroptosis [113]. Necroptosis triggered by the activation of TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis/fibroblast growth factor-inducible 14 (TWEAK/Fn14) mediates secondary cell death in AKI [114]. These findings demonstrate the complexity of necroptosis in the progression of renal fibrosis and suggest that targeting necroptosis, inflammation, and other forms of RCD simultaneously may be a better clinical therapeutic approach (Fig. 5).

The tumor necrosis factor (TNF) α/toll-like receptor (TLR)/Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1) pathway activates the receptor-interacting protein kinase (RIPK)1/RIPK3/mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) necroptosis pathway, leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that induce inflammation and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby mediating the onset and progression of renal fibrosis. Following the binding of TNF to TNF receptor (TNFR)1, the cytoplasmic death domain recruits membrane-associated Complex I, which comprises tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated death domain (TRADD), RIPK1, and ubiquitin E3 ligases such as tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF2) and cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 1/2 (clAP1/2) [92]. RIPK1 undergoes ubiquitination while simultaneously mediating the recruitment and activation of transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and the inhibitor of nuclear factor κB kinase (IKK) complex. The inhibitor of nuclear factor κB (IκB) protein is subsequently phosphorylated and ubiquitinated, leading to nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation, which balances cell survival and death through transcriptional regulation and epigenetic modifications [93–95]. When clAP1/2 is inhibited or absent, RIPK1 is deubiquitinated, leading to the degradation of Complex I and the formation of cytoplasmic Complex IIa (TRADD, fas-associating protein with a novel death domain (FADD), pro-caspase-8 and RIPK1). Complex IIa can mediate the activation of caspase-8, thereby triggering the downstream caspase cascade, ultimately leading to renal cell death and the development of renal fibrosis [96, 97]. However, when caspase-8 activation is inhibited, RIPK1 is activated to initiate necrosome assembly, recruiting and activating RIPK3 to form Complex IIb. RIPK3 phosphorylates the pseudokinase domain of MLKL, triggering its oligomerization and translocation to the plasma membrane [98, 99]. This leads to membrane rupture and the release of DAMPs such as high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1), ATP, and nucleic acids, thereby initiating the inflammatory response. This further promotes the occurrence of EMT through the recruitment of inflammatory cells such as macrophages and neutrophils, which secrete profibrotic factors, including transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), ultimately leading to renal fibrosis. These events also promote renal fibrosis by disrupting microvascular integrity through endothelial cell necroptosis and inducing mitochondrial dysfunction. TLRs can promote RIPK3 activation via TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF), mediating events downstream of necroptosis [100]. After viral infection, RIPK3 can activate ZBP1 to induce necroptosis independent of RIPK1 [100]. Mitogen-activated protein kinase 2 (MK2) and IKK can phosphorylate RIPK1 at Ser320 and Ser25, respectively, thereby inhibiting RIPK1 activity. This suppresses necrosome assembly and RIPK1-dependent necroptosis, maintaining tissue homeostasis. Necrostatin-1 specifically inhibits RIPK1 activity, blocking RIPK1-mediated necroptosis [109]. Inhibiting necroptosis through the above mechanisms may prevent subsequent DAMPs-induced EMT and renal fibrosis

LCD cooperates with multiple forms of RCD to mediate renal fibrosis

Lysosomes are essential organelles that maintain cellular metabolism and remove harmful substances. LCD is a form of RCD characterized by lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP). This process causes the leakage of lysosomal contents, such as cathepsins, into the cytoplasm, triggering a cascade of reactions that ultimately leads to cell death [115]. Lipid peroxidation induced by the palmitoyl protein thioesterase 1 (PPT1) inhibitor DC661, lysosomal swelling due to nanoparticle aggregation or cationic drugs, translocation of pro-death proteins such as Bax/p-MLKL, and calcium leakage caused by damaged lysosomes contribute to lysosome membrane permeability [116–120].

LCD is a multifaceted process that can cooccur with apoptosis and autophagy and shares mechanisms with ferroptosis and necroptosis [121, 122]. In cisplatin-induced AKI, LMP-mediated cathepsin B release cleaves Bid, activating caspases to initiate apoptosis. Cathepsin B inhibitors significantly prevent cisplatin-induced apoptosis [121]. In addition, cisplatin-induced AKI can impair autophagic flux within renal tubular cells, inhibit mTOR activity and promote TFEB nuclear translocation. The enrichment of galectin-3 in lysosomes highlights the strong association between autophagy dysfunction and impaired lysosomal integrity. Lysosomal membrane repair, mediated by the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) III subunit charged multivesicular body protein 4A (CHMP4A), prevents cell death and increases autophagy in TECs, thus alleviating AKI [121]. Additionally, the accumulation of the autophagy activator trehalose in lysosomes impedes Rag GTPase-Ragulator complex formation, inhibits mTORC1 activity and dephosphorylates TFEB, attenuating mitochondrial dysfunction and renal fibrosis in AKI mice [69]. Moreover, intralysosomal lipid peroxidation can trigger the leakage of iron. Cellular ferroptosis is induced by increasing LMP and facilitating Fe2+ diffusion throughout the cell [123, 124]. Lysosome-targeted nanoparticles can induce LMP via the Fenton reaction, triggering ferroptosis and pyroptosis for cancer therapy [125]. Cathepsin B release can directly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby triggering caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis. This leads to the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, which may play a role in renal fibrosis [126]. LCD also promotes renal fibrosis by interfering with calcium signaling and reducing the activity of associated enzymes [127, 128]. In summary, LCD may serve as a common pathway linking multiple forms of RCD, and results from related mechanistic studies and targeted therapies show promise. Targeting lysosomal membrane stability may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for renal fibrosis patients, but further investigation is needed to elucidate its complex interactions with other pathways (Fig. 6).

Various pathological stimuli mediate lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP)-induced lysosomal cell death (LCD), and the combined actions of apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and abnormal autophagy collectively drive the progression of renal fibrosis. Oxidative stress, metabolic disorders, calcium overload, renal ischemia, proteinuria, lysosomal detergents, sphingosine, cisplatin, and proapoptotic protein translocation are among the various stimuli that can induce increased lysosomal membrane permeability, leading to LMP. Proteases (e.g., cathepsins) and other hydrolases within the lysosomes subsequently promote the leakage of Ca2⁺, H⁺, and Fe2⁺ from the lysosomes into the cytoplasm, triggering a cascade of reactions that ultimately leads to cell death [116–120]. LCD represents a multimechanism death pathway involving, cathepsin B cleavage of BH3 interacting-domain death agonist (Bid) via its cleavage effector, which activates caspase enzymes to initiate apoptosis [121]. LMP exacerbates cellular damage by promoting iron release and lipid peroxidation [123, 124]. The release of cathepsin B activates the NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, promoting pyroptosis and inflammatory cytokine release [126]. Additionally, LCD disrupts autophagy and calcium signaling and reduces the activity of related enzymes, collectively promoting renal fibrosis. Targeting lysosomal membrane stability may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for treating renal fibrosis. Inhibiting caspase-3-dependent apoptosis via Z-DEVD-FMK, suppressing lipid peroxidation with ferrostatin-1, and inhibiting pyroptosis with VX-765 or MCC950 all show promise in protecting cells from LCD-induced damage, thereby offering potential for the treatment of renal fibrosis

Metal-dependent cell death in renal fibrosis

Having systematically examined apoptosis, the resistance to survival, autophagy, and inflammatory lytic death, we now address an emerging frontier: metal-dependent cell death. The kidney is a central regulator of iron and copper homeostasis, and the metabolic dysregulation that occurs in the context of CKD frequently manifests as aberrant metal accumulation in tubular cells. This section focuses on ferroptosis and cuproptosis, which are RCD modalities uniquely driven by iron- and copper-mediated toxicity, respectively. Unlike receptor-proximal death pathways, these forms of cell death are intimately linked to cellular metabolism, mitochondrial integrity, and antioxidant defense. Given the high metabolic demand and oxidative burden inherent to the fibrotic kidney, metal-dependent cell death provides a crucial link between organelle dysfunction (including lipid peroxidation and disruption of the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) cycle) and cell death execution, underscoring metabolic and ionomic vulnerabilities as promising therapeutic targets.

Ferroptosis promotes lipid peroxidation to regulate renal fibrosis

Iron ions play roles in oxygen transport and energy metabolism that span all stages of mammalian life [129–131]. The abnormal accumulation of ferrous iron and lipid peroxides within cells leads to the loss of cellular function and cell death via a process termed ferroptosis by Scott J. Dixon in 2012 [132]. Ferroptosis is closely associated with glutathione depletion or the inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Ferroptosis serves not only as a convergent metabolic mediator in various kidney diseases (including AKI, DKD, and hyperuricemic nephropathy) but also as a pathological bridge linking acute injury, chronic fibrosis, and the development of renal cancer [133]. Ferroptosis has been established as a key pathological driver of renal fibrosis, with mechanisms involving the dysregulation of iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, the interaction of multiple signaling pathways, and cell type-specific susceptibility [134](Fig. 7).

Primary molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis (iron and lipid metabolism, System Xc−-glutathione (GSH)-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)-coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) and the mevalonate axis), their association with renal injury and fibrosis, and the effects of relevant inhibitors. Ferroptosis is caused primarily by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and is closely associated with glutathione depletion or GPX4 inactivation. The molecular mechanisms include the following. (1) Iron metabolism and lipid metabolism pathways: Increased iron absorption, reduced iron storage, and restricted iron efflux can lead to increased iron accumulation, which serves as a key signal initiating membrane oxidative damage. Circulating iron is absorbed in by ferroportin (FPN) the form of Fe3⁺ and then converted to Fe2⁺ by the iron reductase six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 (STEAP3) and the divalent metal transporter divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) before being released into the cytoplasm. Ferritin can be degraded through nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy, thereby increasing Fe2⁺ levels. Excess Fe2⁺ leads to the generation hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction, promoting the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) into phospholipids to form polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipid (PUFA-PLs). These PUFA-PLs are sensitive to lysyl oxidase (LOX)-mediated radical-induced oxidation, leading to the formation of PLs-PUFA-OOH. Peroxidized PUFAs incorporated into the plasma membrane promote cell membrane damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately leading to ferroptosis [132, 135–139]. (2) System Xc− -GSH-GPX4 pathway: System Xc−, which is composed of the subunits solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2), facilitates the exchange and transport of intracellular glutamate and extracellular cystine at a 1:1 ratio [140]. Cysteine enters renal cells for conversion into GSH, which exists in both a reduced (GSH) and oxidized (Oxidized glutathione (GSSG)) form. GSH and GSSG act as electron donors and acceptors, thereby maintaining the intracellular redox balance. GSSG is reduced to GSH by the action of glutathione reductase and the coenzyme NADPH. Fe2+ can generate large amounts of phospholipid hydroperoxides via the Fenton reaction. GPX4 utilizes GSH to reduce phospholipid hydroperoxides to lipid alcohols, thereby protecting cells from ferroptosis. When System Xc− is inhibited, intracellular GSH synthesis is blocked, leading to GPX4 inactivation and thereby promoting lipid peroxidation, which induces ferroptosis [139, 141]. (3) FSP1-CoQ10 pathway: FSP1 can catalyze the regeneration of CoQ10. The reduced form of CoQ10, ubiquinol, can scavenge lipid peroxyl radicals, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation and suppressing ferroptosis. System Xc−-GSH-GPX4 and FSP1-CoQ10 axis activation limits lipid peroxidation and subsequent ferroptosis. Additionally, Fe2⁺ efflux via the FPN1 transporter on the cell membrane can inhibit ferroptosis [141]. (4) Mevalonate pathway: 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) irreversibly reduces 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) to mevalonate. Mevalonate is converted to isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) through a series of enzymatic reactions. IPP can regulate amino acids in the active site of GPX4, affecting GPX4 synthesis and thereby modulating ferroptosis [142]. Ferroptosis, through iron overload, lipid peroxidation, inflammatory responses, and the activation of specific signaling pathways such as the transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)/Smad pathway, leads to renal damage and tubular epithelial cells (TECs) death, activates myofibroblasts, and results in extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and renal fibrosis. Ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, alleviates fibrosis by suppressing inflammation and lipid peroxidation, thereby improving renal injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrosis in mice [151, 152]. Dapagliflozin inhibits ferroptosis through multiple mechanisms and has shown promise for alleviating renal fibrosis [150]. Ferroptosis occurs in renal TECs during the progression of acute kidney injury (AKI) to chronic kidney disease (CKD) induced by aristolochic acid and high-dose folate. The strategy of protecting GPX4 using the histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) inhibitor RGFP966 may effectively reduce ferroptosis in TECs and slow the progression from AKI to CKD [147]

The core molecular mechanism in renal fibrosis involves primarily the following four key aspects. First, renal fibrosis is mediated by iron metabolism and lipid metabolism pathways. Increased iron uptake, reduced iron storage and restricted iron efflux serve as key signals that initiate membrane oxidative damage during ferroptosis. Circulating iron is converted to Fe2⁺ by six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 (STEAP3) and divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) and is released into the cytoplasm [135, 136]. Ferroportin is degraded via nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy, increasing Fe2⁺ levels [137]. The excess Fe2⁺ produced during the above process generates hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction [132, 138]. Polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids (PUFA-PLs) in cell membranes undergo lipid peroxidation in the presence of high levels of iron and ROS. The accumulation of lipid peroxides within the membrane disrupts its integrity, inducing ferroptosis [139]. Monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) may act as effective inhibitors of ferroptosis by competing with PUFAs for PL synthesis [139]. Second, ferroptosis is mediated by the System Xc−-GSH-GPX4 pathway. Extracellular cysteine enters renal cells via System Xc− and is converted into GSH, maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [140]. Oxidized glutathione (GSSG) is reduced to GSH by glutathione reductase (GR) in the presence of NADPH. GPX4 utilizes GSH to reduce PLs-PUFA-OOH to the lipid alcohol PL-PUFA-OH, thereby protecting cells from ferroptosis [139, 141]. When the cystine transporter is inhibited, intracellular GSH synthesis is blocked, leading to GPX4 inactivation and thereby promoting lipid peroxidation, which induces ferroptosis. Third, ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) can catalyze the regeneration of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10). The reduced form of CoQ10, ubiquinol, can scavenge lipid peroxyl radicals, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation and suppressing ferroptosis [141]. Fourth, isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP), which is generated through the conversion of mevalonate, can regulate the amino acid residues in the active site of GPX4, thereby influencing GPX4 synthesis and, consequently, ferroptosis [142]. In summary, ferroptosis is regulated by iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and glutathione metabolic pathways, all of which are crucial in understanding metabolic disorders [143]. Excessive iron ion accumulation leads to the production of ROS, cell death, tissue damage, and the development of various kidney diseases [144].

In the UUO model, TEC-mediated ferroptosis has been identified as a key driver of renal interstitial fibrosis. Persistent activation of ferroptosis exacerbates fibrosis via the TGF-β/Smad pathway, whereas inhibition of ferroptosis significantly alleviates renal fibrosis and preserves proximal tubular structure [145]. In clinical CKD patients and UUO mouse models, the downregulation of GPX4 expression promotes ferroptosis, which in turn leads to renal TECs injury and fibrosis. Smad3 mediates renal fibrosis by downregulating GPX4 expression [146]. RGFP966, a selective inhibitor of histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3), inhibits ferroptosis by restoring GPX4 expression, thereby attenuating the fibrosis-associated loss of renal function [147]. Ferroptosis is also involved in DKD-induced fibrosis owing to its disruption of iron homeostasis and lipid metabolism. SIRT1 can delay the onset and progression of DKD by promoting mitochondrial autophagy and inhibiting lipotoxicity-mediated ferroptosis in renal TECs [148]. Somapacitan activates the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling pathway by binding to the GLP-1 receptor, which phosphorylates protein kinase A (PKA) and cyclic AMP response binding protein (CREB) [149]. This causes an increase in the mRNA levels of the fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) major receptor β-klotho (KLB) and activates AMPK/acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC)/SIRT1/NRF2 signaling, reprogramming iron metabolism, fatty acid synthesis, and antioxidant enzymes to attenuate renal inflammation and fibrosis [149]. Dapagliflozin treatment inhibits ferroptosis markers, improves renal function, and reduces renal fibrosis in diabetic mice [150]. In the context of hyperuricemic nephropathy, characterized by renal tubular dysfunction, inflammation, and progressive fibrosis, uric acid oxidase-knockout mice exhibit marked renal impairment accompanied by increased iron deposition and reduced expression of GPX4 and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11). The ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 reduces iron overload and lipid oxidation and alleviates kidney injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrosis in these mice [151]. Renal tubular cells from aged mice and accelerated aging model mice exhibit extensive ferroptosis. The ferroptosis inducer erastin exacerbates age-induced renal interstitial fibrosis, but this effect is reversed by ferrostatin-1. These findings suggest that ferroptosis exacerbates renal tubular cell senescence and age-related renal fibrosis [152]. Targeting ferroptosis not only halts the progression of fibrosis but also offers new therapeutic approaches for the treatment of patients with conditions such as CKD and DKD.

During the progression of renal fibrosis, a complex interaction occurs between ferroptosis and autophagy, which may reduce fibrosis through the removal of damaged mitochondria (mitochondrial autophagy) or the inhibition of ferroptosis. SIRT3 inhibits ferroptosis by regulating mitochondrial homeostasis and GPX4 acetylation, alleviating cadmium-induced kidney injury [153]. Hypoxia/ischemia-induced autophagy of OTU deubiquitinase 5 results in the degradation of GPX4 during the transition from AKI to CKD, exacerbating ferroptosis in renal tubular cells and promoting fibrosis [154]. Ferritinophagy, the autophagic degradation of ferritin, promotes cellular iron accumulation through the release of free iron. PM2.5 activates autophagy through the AMPK/UNC-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) axis, promoting NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, which leads to ferroptosis [155]. In summary, ferroptosis and autophagy play independent and synergistic roles in renal fibrosis, and their dynamic balance is closely related to cell type, disease stage, and the microenvironment. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying their interaction will aid in the development of novel, precise therapies for individuals with kidney disease.

Cuproptosis may play a role in renal fibrosis

Studies have also shown that copper, as a cofactor for various enzymes, can disrupt renal microenvironment homeostasis when its metabolism is disturbed, thereby exacerbating tissue damage. Abnormal intracellular copper distribution—particularly mitochondrial copper accumulation—occurs during the progression of renal fibrosis, which can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular damage, and the promotion of fibrosis [156]. This form of cell death caused by copper accumulation within mitochondria is termed cuproptosis, a type of metal-ion-induced RCD that differs from other forms of RCD [157]. Excess copper ions bind to lipoylated components of the TCA cycle, causing the aggregation and dysfunction of these lipoylated proteins. This copper-dependent aberrant oligomerization of lipoylated proteins destabilizes iron‒sulfur cluster proteins, triggering proteotoxic stress and ultimately leading to cell death [157].

In a renal fibrosis model of UIRI with contralateral nephrectomy and UUO, compared with control mice, fibrosis mice exhibited elevated renal copper transporter 1 (CTR1) expression. This led to increased copper ion influx, promoting the binding of copper ions to lysyl oxidase (LOX), a key enzyme involved in ECM cross-linking, resulting in increased secretion of cross-linked elastin and collagen. The upregulation of CTR1 and LOX correlated positively with human tubulointerstitial fibrosis [158]. When tetrathiomolybdate was used to chelate intracellular copper ions, the protein level of LOX decreased. Furthermore, tetrathiomolybdate significantly attenuated the increases in the insoluble collagen content, the insoluble/soluble collagen ratio, and the protein levels of elastin and COL3α [158]. DKD is among the common causes of CKD. In a model of streptozotocin-induced diabetes, the kidneys of rats presented increased copper and zinc ion concentrations. The copper-selective chelator triethylenetetramine reduced copper levels and collagen and fibronectin deposition by inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, ultimately improving tissue structure and function in diabetic rats [159].

Copper ion chelators can also ameliorate hepatic fibrosis by alleviating copper ion deposition and oxidative stress in the liver [160]. The food additive trilobatin attenuates myocardial fibrosis and inhibits doxorubicin-induced cuproptosis by directly binding to ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), a key upstream regulator of the copper protein pathway [161]. In contrast, studies have detected the expression of genes related to ferroptosis in human and mouse models of lung fibrosis by single-cell RNA sequencing. Compared with those in the healthy controls, genes such as FDX1, pyruvate dehydrogenase E1-β (PDHB), dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and lipoyl synthase (LIAS), which promote cuproptosis, were significantly downregulated in the fibrosis group. The inhibition of the expression of genes related to cuproptosis followed the opposite trend [162]. Additionally, as fibroblasts differentiate, TCA cycle and the lipid metabolism activity decrease, whereas mitochondrial metabolism gradually increases, suggesting that cuproptosis may contribute to the alleviation of pulmonary fibrosis [162]. These findings highlight that whether organ differences in cuproptosis exist in fibrotic diseases remains to be explored, and the specific mechanism of cuproptosis in renal fibrosis needs to be further elucidated (Fig. 8).

The molecular mechanisms underlying cuproptosis and their association with renal fibrosis. Under normal conditions, intracellular Cu2⁺ uptake is mediated by solute carrier family 31 member 1 (SLC31A1), while ATPase copper transporting β (ATP7B) facilitates its efflux. When excessive Cu2⁺ accumulates, ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) catalyzes the reduction of Cu2⁺ to Cu⁺, disrupting lipoylated protein aggregation and inhibiting Fe–S cluster proteins. This induces protein toxicity stress, blocks the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) cycle, and triggers cuproptosis. Excessive Cu2⁺ accumulates in mitochondria and binds to dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), inducing abnormal DLAT oligomerization that increases cytotoxicity and triggers cuproptosis [157]. Excess Cu2⁺ also binds to lysyl oxidase (LOX), which promotes extracellular matrix (ECM) cross-linking, facilitating the secretion of cross-linked elastin and collagen and thereby induces fibrosis [157]. The inhibition of Cu2⁺ reduction is key for alleviating copper-induced death and renal fibrosis

Renal fibrosis and other novel forms of RCD

PANoptosis, a newly identified form of regulated inflammatory lysogenic cell death, was reported by Dr. Kanneganti et al. in 2019 [163]. PANoptosis is the synergistic activation of the key pathways of apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis that involves the formation of cytoplasmic multimeric protein complexes (PANoptosomes) composed of molecules such as caspase-8 and RIPK3 [164, 165]. PANoptosis is controlled by PANoptosomes, several types of which have been identified, including the ZBP1-PANoptosome, the absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2)-PANoptosome, the NLRP12-PANoptosome and the RIPK1-PANoptosome [166]. PANoptosis may synergize with multiple cell death pathways, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis and necroptosis, to promote fibrosis progression. Double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) released from neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) activates the RIPK1-PANoptosome in proximal renal TECs via the receptor TLR3, leading to the activation of PANoptosis signaling and exacerbating renal IRI. The inhibition of this process is expected to alleviate renal IRI and renal fibrosis [23]. The formation of mitochondrial permeability transition pores and Bax pores leads to the release of mitochondrial DNA into the cytoplasm, which activates the Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway and induces PANoptosis and inflammatory responses in renal tubular cells, which in turn promote fibrosis. In the kidney, this mtDNA-cGAS-STING-PANoptosis axis is particularly active in proximal tubular cells because of their high mitochondrial density and susceptibility to mitochondrial damage from ischemia, toxins, or metabolic stress. Lycopene can attenuate renal injury by inhibiting this pathway [167].

Research on PANoptosis in renal fibrosis is still in its early stages, and key targets in animal models need to be validated in human studies. Intervening in the PANoptosis process could present a potential therapeutic breakthrough. However, owing to the complexity of the PANoptosis mechanism, a single inhibitor is not sufficient to completely block its effects. The Salmonella effector SopF regulates PANoptosis through the phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase-1 (PDK1)-p90 ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK)-Caspase-8 axis, inhibits inflammation in and death (e.g., pyroptosis and apoptosis) of intestinal epithelial cells, and reduces clearance of infected cells while simultaneously promoting necroptosis, leading to increased tissue damage [168]. This study suggested that cotargeting PANoptosis with necrostatin-1 and caspase inhibitors might be more effective than using single agents, although further exploration of the underlying mechanisms and preclinical validation are needed.

Entotic cell death is a unique form of RCD that is characterized by the active invasion of internal cells into neighboring cells of the same type to form a “cell-in-cell” structure. This structure is then degraded through the lysosomal pathway, leading to death [169]. Although few studies have investigated entotic cell death in renal diseases, some have shown that p53 can regulate a variety of types of nonapoptotic cell death, including entotic cell death, suggesting that it may interact with other modes of RCD, which may represent a breakthrough in the study of entotic cell death in the context of renal fibrosis [170].

Conclusions and prospects

Renal fibrosis is the primary process and final common pathway through which CKD progresses to end-stage renal disease. Irreversible damage to renal substances contributes to the progressive loss of renal function and severe consequences. With the aging population in China, the incidence of renal fibrosis is expected to increase annually. Extensive research has indicated that RCD plays a crucial role in renal fibrosis (Fig. 9). This paper elucidates the diverse types of RCD involved in renal fibrosis and their intricate and connected mechanisms, the key factors of which are summarized in Table 1. Current RCD-targeted interventions for the treatment of renal fibrosis patients involve primarily the modulation of various cell death signaling pathways, the protection of mitochondrial function, the modulation of metabolic reprogramming, and the cross-modulation of multiple pathways. Many drugs and compounds have been developed to alleviate renal fibrosis, with a particular emphasis on ferroptosis. For example, rosiglitazone modulates iron metabolism and has shown potential therapeutic effects in a renal interstitial fibrosis model [171]. Ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, has been shown to significantly reduce kidney injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, and renal fibrosis in animal models [151]. Crocin can also alleviate renal fibrosis by protecting renal TECs fromferroptosis [172]. There are also a variety of drugs and compounds that target other forms of RCD, which are listed in Table 2. However, these approaches are still in the preclinical research stage. Clinical treatment also focuses on treating the primary disease and relieving symptoms, while specific, safe, and stable targeted drugs are lacking. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify effective therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of renal fibrosis. In this review, we provide insights into the mechanisms of different types of RCD and their roles in renal fibrosis, offer potential mechanisms for the treatment of this disease, and highlight possible new research directions for its effective control.

Limitations of current research and future perspectives

Although basic research on renal fibrosis and cell death is relatively well established, several limitations still exist. One major challenge lies in the profound cellular heterogeneity and the complex interplay among different forms of RCD during renal fibrosis progression. Proximal TECs with varying DNA content, particularly polyploid cells (> 4N), exhibit distinct profibrotic transcriptomic signatures. These cells exhibit dynamic regulation of senescence and apoptosis and are highly heterogeneous in the fibrotic microenvironment [190]. In the context of DKD, single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed a subpopulation of profibrotic TGF-β/Arginase-1 macrophages that promote the differentiation of tethered cells into myofibroblasts through activation of the TGF-β/Smad2/3/YAP signaling axis [15]. Death or dysfunction of these macrophages may exacerbate fibrosis through the release of profibrotic factors. Targeting specific cell death mechanisms or key molecules may offer a novel strategy for treating fibrosis. However, the cellular heterogeneity during fibrosis and the complexity of cell‒cell interactions make precise delivery of drugs to specific cell types extremely challenging. For example, different subpopulations of macrophages may drive or inhibit the fibrotic process. Specific knockdown of tumor protein 53-regulating kinase (TP53RK) in renal tubules or fibroblasts attenuates fibrosis, but delivery systems that distinguish between specific cell types remain elusive [29, 191]. Although existing delivery systems, such as biomimetic high-density lipoprotein nanoparticles, have demonstrated the ability to target injured renal tubules [192], the complex physiological barriers of the kidney (e.g., the presence of the basement membrane and cellular polarity) may impede the penetration of nanoparticles [193]. Overcoming these challenges in the future will require the combination of single-cell technology to resolve cell subpopulation-specific targets and multiomics approaches to validate the precision and safety of the delivery system.

A further limitation is the compensatory crosstalk among distinct RCD pathways. The mechanism of renal fibrosis involves multiple overlapping cell death modalities. When one pathway is inhibited, others may be activated to compensate for its function [194]. Apoptosis signaling may actively inhibit cell death by inhibiting ferroptosis [195]. Excess ROS can induce a variety of forms of RCD, but tumor cells may escape death by modulating ROS-mediated cell death pathways, suggesting that compensatory mechanisms may exist to promote cell survival [196]. These mechanisms play important roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis, escaping therapeutic resistance, and driving disease progression. However, they also allow cells to evade drug-induced cell death by activating alternative RCD pathways, leading to therapeutic failure.

Additional constraints stem from the inherent limitations of widely used preclinical animal models. The most common models include UUO, IRI, and drug-induced and genetic modifications. Taking the UUO model as an example, although it effectively replicates the key pathological processes of human renal fibrosis and has been widely used to elucidate renal fibrosis pathogenesis and screen for potential antifibrotic therapeutic agents, this model still struggles to replicate the complex clinical scenarios of human renal fibrosis. Initially, most studies focus on observations made at later stages of obstruction, which may obscure critical early pathological signals. The timing of observation may influence the results. The results of such studies are therefore subject to interference from multiple variables, including the duration and severity of obstruction, the compensatory capacity of the contralateral kidney and procedural details. These factors may cast doubt on the reproducibility of animal studies with small sample sizes. Ultimately, if variables such as the age of the animals are not strictly controlled in the experiments, this may interfere with an accurate assessment of the mechanisms underlying fibrosis.

Finally, a critical translational barrier is the lack of reliable, noninvasive biomarkers for real-time monitoring of fibrosis severity in the clinic. Although urine and blood markers may serve as indicators of fibrosis, the mechanisms linking them to the dynamic progression of fibrosis remain unclear, making determining the optimal timing for initiating treatment difficult. Once fibrosis progresses to the advanced stage, irreversible structural remodeling of the kidneys has already occurred. At this point, treatment is unlikely to restore renal function. The primary goal of current therapies is to delay or halt progression rather than reverse it. Furthermore, the activity of therapeutic targets may change as the disease progresses, and intervention at a single time point may fail to cover the entire pathological process, necessitating dynamic adjustments to treatment strategies. Therefore, the future development of effective drugs remains a promising yet highly challenging endeavor.

This section candidly discusses the multifaceted limitations hindering translational progress in renal fibrosis, spanning cellular heterogeneity and the compensatory interplay among regulated cell death pathways that complicate therapeutic targeting; the inherent inadequacies of current animal models in capturing early and clinically nuanced disease dynamics; and the critical absence of reliable noninvasive biomarkers to guide timely and adaptive intervention.
