Association between statin therapy and incident diabetes mellitus in patients with nephrotic syndrome: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yongsheng Qian, Wenyuan Gan, Yan Dong, Wenli Chen, Fan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of statins and other lipid-lowering therapies for nephrotic syndrome (NS) patients with dyslipidemia remains controversial. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of long-term statin therapy on the risk of developing diabetes mellitus (DM) in patients with NS.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted from January 2018 to December 2024. The association between statin use and the incidence of diabetes was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Restricted cubic splines were utilized to investigate potential nonlinear relationships between cumulative statin dose and diabetes risk. Subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, corticosteroid use, and other relevant clinical factors to assess possible effect modification.ResultsA total of 837 eligible NS patients were enrolled in this study, among whom 84 (10.03%) developed DM. Statin use significantly increased the risk of incident diabetes (HR 3.68, 95% CI: 1.95–6.94, p < 0.001), an association which remained significant after multivariable adjustment (HR 3.00, 95% CI: 1.40–6.43, p = 0.005). Higher risk was particularly evident among older patients (≥ 55 years), those with hypertension, corticosteroid non-users, and patients with BMI ≥ 28. A restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a nonlinear relationship between cumulative statin dose and diabetes risk, peaking at cumulative doses of approximately 8,000–13,000 mg and declining thereafter.ConclusionsStatin therapy independently predicts an elevated risk of diabetes among patients with nephrotic syndrome. This relationship exhibits dose-dependent nonlinear characteristics, suggesting careful consideration of cumulative statin dose is warranted when managing lipid disorders in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04424-5.

Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome (NS) is a clinical condition characterized by significant proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema, and hyperlipidemia [1, 2]. Among these, hyperlipidemia is a hallmark metabolic disturbance that contributes to the long-term complications of NS [3, 4]. Patients with NS often exhibit elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides, coupled with reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol [5]. This lipid imbalance not only increases the risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease but may also exacerbate renal damage through mechanisms such as lipid nephrotoxicity and endothelial dysfunction [6].

The potential risks associated with hyperlipidemia in NS underscore the need for effective therapeutic strategies to manage dyslipidemia [7]. Statins, well-established lipid-lowering agents, are frequently considered for this purpose. Their benefits in the general population, including significant reductions in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, have been extensively documented. In NS patients, statins have demonstrated efficacy in lowering LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides, contributing to short-term management of dyslipidemia [8]. Beyond their lipid-lowering effects, statins exhibit pleiotropic properties, including anti-inflammatory and endothelial-stabilizing effects, which may further benefit NS patients [9]. Some studies even suggest a modest reduction in proteinuria with statin therapy, though the clinical significance of this finding remains debated [10]. These supportive findings have led some guidelines, such as those for chronic kidney disease (CKD) management, to advocate for the use of statins in CKD stages 1–4, a population that overlaps with NS patients.

Despite these advantages, the use of statins in NS is not without controversy. Critics argue that the hyperlipidemia associated with NS is often transient and may resolve with remission of proteinuria, raising questions about the necessity of long-term statin therapy. Additionally, concerns regarding safety have been raised, particularly in NS patients, who may have altered drug metabolism and a higher risk of adverse effects. These include myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and potential liver dysfunction.

Moreover, recent studies have indicated that statins might affect glucose metabolism, potentially increasing the risk of developing diabetes [11–13]. In NS patients, who may already have a predisposition to metabolic disturbances due to chronic inflammation and altered lipid metabolism, the potential diabetogenic effects of statins warrant careful consideration. While the overall cardiovascular benefits of statins often outweigh this risk in high-risk populations, it remains a significant concern in patients requiring long-term therapy. This article aims to analyze the necessity of adding statins to the long-term treatment regimens of NS patients, focusing particularly on the potential risk of diabetes associated with statin use.

Method

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective, observational cohort study conducted at the Central Hospital of Wuhan, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, China. All data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system.

Study population

This study was a single-center investigation conducted in Central China, targeting all patients diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome (NS) between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024. As shown in Fig. 1, from an initial cohort of 11,354 patients diagnosed with chronic glomerulonephritis, 1,625 patients were diagnosed with NS. Among these, 824 patients had at least two records of kidney function data and a follow-up duration exceeding 90 days. All participants were of East Asian descent.

Variables and covariates collection

All demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were retrieved from the EMR and independently validated by two investigators. Statin exposure was defined as any prescription for atorvastatin calcium during the follow-up period, with cumulative dose calculated as the sum of all dispensed doses (in milligrams) over time. Corticosteroid use was similarly defined and quantified. Baseline comorbidities (hypertension, gout) were extracted based on ICD-10 codes and/or physician diagnosis. Laboratory variables included Hb, Scr, BUN, Alb, UA, lipid profiles, and others measured by standard automated clinical analyzers.

For all patients included in this study, only atorvastatin calcium was prescribed, and no other types of statins were used. The cumulative statin dose was calculated as the total amount of atorvastatin (in milligrams) administered throughout the follow-up period, taking into account both dosage and duration of use. This ensured that all statin exposure was standardized to atorvastatin-equivalent dosing.

Statin initiation criteria

Statin therapy was generally initiated based on clinicians’ assessment of overall cardiovascular risk, presence of nephrotic syndrome, and guideline recommendations for lipid-lowering in CKD and NS patients, rather than solely on baseline lipid values. In clinical practice at our center, statins may be prescribed for patients with NS to prevent atherosclerotic events, even when lipid levels are only modestly elevated, particularly if additional risk factors or comorbidities are present. The decision to start statin therapy was individualized and not standardized across all patients.

Assessment criteria

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2009 creatinine-based equation without race adjustment [14]. Hypertension was defined as an average systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medications, or self-reported history of hypertension. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was diagnosed according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria, based on the following glycemic thresholds: HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) after an 8-hour fast, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) accompanied by classical hyperglycemia symptoms or a hyperglycemic crisis. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²).

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome was new-onset diabetes mellitus, defined per ADA criteria (see Assessment Criteria section). Secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: myocardial infarction, stroke, unstable angina, coronary revascularization, cardiovascular death) and all-cause mortality. Follow-up time was calculated from cohort entry to the occurrence of the first event, last clinic visit, or end of the study period (December 31, 2024).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; normally distributed data were reported as mean ± SD and compared by Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared by Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared by chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Time-to-event analyses for diabetes incidence were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression. The proportional hazards assumption was tested by Schoenfeld residuals. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, ACEI/ARB use, corticosteroid use, gout); Model 3 (further adjusted for BMI, SBP, and laboratory variables).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to assess non-linear associations between cumulative statin dose and diabetes risk, with knots set at specified percentiles based on Harrell’s guidelines. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted for age, sex, hypertension, BMI, and corticosteroid use. Missing data were handled by complete case analysis. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The cut-off of age ≥ 55 years was selected based on the median or distribution of age in our cohort and reflects the relatively younger population affected by nephrotic syndrome in our setting. Similarly, the BMI cut-off of ≥ 28 kg/m² corresponds to the definition of obesity in Chinese adults, according to the guidelines of the Working Group on Obesity in China (WGOC).

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Table 2 presents Cox regression results showing a significant association between statin use and increased diabetes risk. The unadjusted Model 1 yielded an HR of 3.68 (95% CI: 1.95–6.94, p < 0.001). Adjustments in Model 2 (age, sex, ACEI/ARB use, corticosteroid use, gout) resulted in an HR of 3.29 (95% CI: 1.74–6.25, p < 0.001), and further adjustments in Model 3 (BMI, systolic blood pressure, laboratory parameters) slightly attenuated but maintained the significance of this association (HR 3.00, 95% CI: 1.40–6.43, p = 0.005).

Subgroup analyses

Statin use was associated with a significantly increased risk of incident diabetes in the total cohort (n = 837), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 3.68 (95% CI: 1.95–6.94; p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses (Fig. 2) were conducted to assess whether this relationship varied by sex, hypertension status, corticosteroid use, age, and BMI.

Fig. 2Subgroup analyses of the association between statin use and incident diabetes, stratified by age, sex, hypertension, corticosteroid use, and BMI, to assess potential effect modification

Subgroup analyses of the association between statin use and incident diabetes, stratified by age, sex, hypertension, corticosteroid use, and BMI, to assess potential effect modification

When stratified by sex, women showed a higher hazard (HR = 5.05; 95% CI: 1.77–14.40; p = 0.002) compared to men (HR = 2.88; 95% CI: 1.30–6.39; p = 0.009), although this interaction was not statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.413). Similarly, the association was stronger among patients with hypertension (HR = 4.14; 95% CI: 1.66–10.32; p = 0.002) compared to those without hypertension (HR = 2.30; 95% CI: 0.89–5.93; p = 0.085), but the interaction again was non-significant (p = 0.375).

In subgroup analysis by corticosteroid use, patients not receiving corticosteroids had a notably higher risk (HR = 5.26; 95% CI: 1.51–18.32; p = 0.009) compared to those receiving corticosteroids (HR = 1.89; 95% CI: 0.90–3.95; p = 0.092); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p for interaction = 0.169). For age-stratified analysis, older patients (≥ 55 years) exhibited a significantly higher hazard (HR = 8.29; 95% CI: 1.94–35.47; p = 0.004) compared to younger patients (< 55 years; HR = 2.35; 95% CI: 1.16–4.77; p = 0.018), although the interaction term was not statistically significant (p = 0.137).

Lastly, BMI stratification showed similar hazard ratios for both BMI < 28 (HR = 3.27; 95% CI: 1.72–6.20; p < 0.001) and BMI ≥ 28 (HR = 2.45; 95% CI: 0.62–3.43; p = 0.998), with no evidence of significant interaction by BMI category (p = 0.993).

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 3) evaluated the incidence of diabetes in different patient subgroups over time. In sex-stratified analysis, no significant difference was found between women and men (log-rank p = 0.307; HR = 1.257; 95% CI: 0.809–1.953). Age-stratified analysis revealed a significantly higher diabetes incidence in patients aged ≥ 55 compared to those younger than 55 (log-rank p = 0.001; HR = 2.167; 95% CI: 1.332–3.525). Patients with hypertension showed significantly increased diabetes risk compared to those without hypertension (log-rank p < 0.001; HR = 2.304; 95% CI: 1.406–3.776). Similarly, patients not using corticosteroids demonstrated a notably higher risk compared to those receiving corticosteroids (log-rank p < 0.001; HR = 3.509; 95% CI: 2.060–5.978). Additionally, BMI-stratified analysis indicated a significantly higher incidence of diabetes in patients with BMI ≥ 28 compared to those with BMI < 28 (log-rank p = 0.013; HR = 2.256; 95% CI: 1.163–4.375). Lastly, patients on statin therapy exhibited a significantly increased diabetes risk compared to those not receiving statins (log-rank p < 0.001; HR = 3.682; 95% CI: 1.953–6.944). Overall, age, hypertension, corticosteroid non-use, higher BMI, and statin use were significant factors associated with increased diabetes risk among these patients.

Relationship between cumulative statin dose and risk of diabetes mellitus

The relationship between cumulative statin dose and the risk of diabetes mellitus (DM) was analyzed using a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model within a Cox proportional hazards framework (Fig. 4). The x-axis in Fig. 4 reflects the total cumulative dose of atorvastatin (mg) administered to each patient during the follow-up period. The analysis demonstrated a statistically significant non-linear association (p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study comprehensively investigates the relationship between statin use and the risk of developing diabetes mellitus (DM) in nephrotic syndrome (NS) patients. The analysis identified statin use, corticosteroid use, a history of gout, and age as independent risk factors for DM. Patients using statins demonstrated a significantly elevated hazard ratio compared to non-users, indicating a strong association between statin therapy and DM development in NS patients. Additionally, the study explored the impact of statin use across different subgroups. Furthermore, this study validated the nonlinear relationship between cumulative statin dosage and DM occurrence, confirming a nonlinear relationship where the risk of DM increased disproportionately with higher cumulative doses of statins. These findings highlight the importance of individualized treatment strategies and careful monitoring of cumulative statin dosage in NS patients.

The relationship between statins and diabetes is a complex and highly debated topic [15, 16]. According to current research and evidence, statins have significant efficacy in reducing cardiovascular disease risk but may also increase the risk of new-onset diabetes. One study found that statin use might elevate diabetes risk by affecting insulin sensitivity and secretion [17]. Another study reported that high-dose statin users had an increased diabetes risk of 9–12% [18]. Furthermore, recent Mendelian randomization studies have shown an association between statin use and both type 1 and type 2 diabetes [19]. Despite the potential risk of diabetes, a meta-analysis involving 1,688 diabetic patients demonstrated that statin therapy significantly reduced all-cause mortality, vascular events, and major cardiac events [20]. Therefore, the use of statins should be carefully evaluated, weighing the benefits and risks for individual patients before initiation [20]. In NS patients, the use of statins significantly increases the risk of developing diabetes. Due to the heterogeneity of NS patients, different targeted treatment plans can be selected for different patients.

Statin use can certainly provide cardiovascular benefits, but in the NS population, the results are as follows. We further analyzed the cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in the enrolled population. We excluded individuals with Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE) at baseline (MACE includes myocardial infarction, stroke, unstable angina, coronary artery bypass grafting, and percutaneous coronary intervention), and then analyzed the occurrence of MACE and all-cause mortality during the follow-up (Table 3). We compared the two groups (statin use vs. non-use), and the p-value was 0.914 (> 0.05), concluding that in the NS population, statin use is not significantly associated with subsequent MACE events. Previous studies have also reached similar conclusions [21].

In the subgroup analysis of this study, the population was divided into different subgroups based on gender, age, history of hypertension, corticosteroid use, and BMI. This subgroup analysis reveals several important findings regarding risk stratification across different patient characteristics. Sex-based differences were notable, with female patients exhibiting a higher HR compared to males, suggesting that the risk of developing diabetes in female NS patients following statin use may be higher [22]A population-based case-control study also found that women aged 40–64 using statins had a higher risk of developing new-onset diabetes, highlighting the importance of considering gender in the selection of lipid-lowering drugs for NS patients. In the age subgroup analysis, both age subgroups showed a significant increase in the risk of developing diabetes after statin use (p < 0.05). Older patients (> 55 years) had a much higher HR compared to younger patients. The diabetes prevalence in the older group (21%) was significantly higher than in the 40–59 years (11.9%) and 20–39 years (3.3%) groups [23]. Aging leads to a decline in insulin secretion, metabolic rate, and muscle mass [24]further exacerbating insulin resistance [25, 26]. In the hypertension subgroup, the risk of developing diabetes after statin use did not increase significantly in non-hypertensive individuals, but in hypertensive individuals, the risk increased significantly, which may be related to endothelial dysfunction, sympathetic activation, and insulin resistance in the pathological state of hypertension [27, 28]. In the corticosteroid subgroup, both groups showed a significant increase in the risk of developing diabetes after statin use, which partially excludes the effect of corticosteroids themselves. Corticosteroid use is common in nephrotic syndrome patients, but long-term corticosteroid use can cause abnormal glucose metabolism [29]increase insulin resistance, and even induce steroid-induced diabetes [30]reminding clinicians to consider the risk of elevated blood glucose and subsequent management when developing treatment plans for NS patients. Statistical testing for interaction (corticosteroid use*statin use in Cox regression model) did not reach significance (p for interaction = 0.169), suggesting that concomitant corticosteroid use did not meaningfully modify the effect of statins on diabetes risk in our cohort. Mechanistically, both corticosteroids and statins can impair glucose metabolism through different, yet potentially additive pathways—corticosteroids mainly by increasing insulin resistance and hepatic glucose output, and statins by affecting insulin secretion and sensitivity. Although our data did not demonstrate a significant interaction, the possibility of additive or synergistic effects, particularly in susceptible individuals, cannot be fully excluded. Further mechanistic and prospective studies are needed to clarify these relationships.

Recent international guidelines, such as the 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [31] and 2019 European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society (ESC/EAS) [32] guidelines, advocate for the use of statins in adults with nephrotic syndrome or chronic kidney disease to reduce cardiovascular risk. However, these guidelines provide limited discussion on the potential diabetogenic effects of statin therapy in NS. Our findings suggest that, although statins should remain part of guideline-directed therapy for lipid control in NS, clinicians should maintain vigilance for new-onset diabetes, especially in high-risk individuals and those receiving moderate-to-high cumulative doses. Future guidelines may benefit from more explicit recommendations for glycemic monitoring and individualized risk–benefit assessment when prescribing statins in NS.

Additionally, regarding the specific dosage of statins and its relationship with the risk of diabetes in NS patients, this study plotted an RCS curve to reflect this relationship. Previous studies have considered the effect of statins on diabetes susceptibility to be a dose accumulation effect, meaning the risk of developing diabetes increases progressively with higher cumulative doses of statins [13, 33]. In this study, among the 837 patients, 565 used statins, which is a relatively large proportion. Unlike previous studies in the general population, the RCS curve showing the relationship between cumulative statin dosage and the risk of diabetes in this study showed an inverted U-shaped curve. Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a nonlinear association between cumulative statin dose and diabetes risk, with an apparent upward trend in risk observed between 8,000 and 13,000 mg. However, these findings are exploratory and observational, and the analysis does not establish a definitive threshold or causal inflection point. The observed pattern may reflect underlying heterogeneity in patient susceptibility or residual confounding. These findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between statin exposure and diabetes risk in the NS population and underscore the need for future studies to further elucidate these associations and to inform more individualized lipid management strategies.

Notably, we found a statistically significant difference in statin exposure duration between groups (as shown in Supplementary Table 2): the diabetes group had a shorter median cumulative statin exposure duration during follow-up compared to the non-diabetes group (600 days [Q1, Q3: 370, 1180] vs. 900 days [480, 1400], P = 0.023). This may reflect earlier onset of diabetes in susceptible individuals, leading to shorter statin exposure due to event occurrence or treatment modification. Alternatively, it may suggest that patients who developed diabetes did so after a relatively lower cumulative exposure, possibly reflecting underlying metabolic susceptibility. These findings highlight the complex interplay between statin therapy duration and diabetes risk in nephrotic syndrome patients and underscore the importance of individualized risk assessment and close glycemic monitoring during statin treatment.

While our study highlights an increased risk of new-onset diabetes associated with statin therapy in nephrotic syndrome, it is important to emphasize that statins remain a cornerstone of lipid-lowering therapy in this population. Hyperlipidemia is a hallmark feature of nephrotic syndrome, and current international guidelines (such as KDIGO 2024 and ESC/EAS 2019) recommend statin therapy for adults with NS and/or CKD to reduce cardiovascular risk. The overall benefit–risk profile of statins in NS favors their use, particularly in patients at high risk for atherosclerotic events. Our findings underscore the need for individualized risk assessment and glycemic monitoring but should not be interpreted as evidence to withhold statin therapy when clinically indicated.

Limitation

This study has several limitations.

First, the markedly smaller size of the diabetes group compared to the non-diabetes group may affect statistical power and the interpretation of between-group comparisons. This group imbalance is inherent to incidence-based outcome studies and should be considered when interpreting subgroup analyses and generalizability.

Second, statin exposure was not modeled as a time-dependent variable in the Cox regression analysis; instead, cumulative use and dose were calculated over the entire follow-up period, which may introduce immortal time bias or exposure misclassification and affect risk estimation accuracy.

Third, the cut-offs for age (≥ 55 years) and BMI (≥ 28 kg/m²) used in subgroup analyses do not correspond to conventional international standards (such as age ≥ 65 years and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), which may affect the comparability and generalizability of our findings to other populations.

Fourth, several important diabetes risk factors—such as family history, smoking status, and physical activity—were not available for most participants, and the use of other diabetogenic medications (thiazide diuretics and β-blockers) was uncommon, limiting our ability to fully adjust for these factors and possibly introducing residual confounding.

Finally, there remains potential for confounding by indication, as statin therapy was initiated based on clinicians’ judgment and not strictly on baseline lipid levels, and although we adjusted for available risk factors, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that statin therapy is independently associated with an increased risk of new-onset diabetes mellitus in patients with nephrotic syndrome, with a notable nonlinear and dose-dependent relationship. Clinicians should carefully weigh the benefits and risks of statin use in this population, especially in patients at high risk for glucose metabolism disturbances and should consider regular monitoring of glycemic parameters during treatment. Future prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings, elucidate the underlying mechanisms, and to define optimal lipid-lowering strategies that minimize diabetes risk while preserving cardiovascular protection in nephrotic syndrome patients.
