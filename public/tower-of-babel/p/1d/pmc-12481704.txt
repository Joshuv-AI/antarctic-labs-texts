Robotic-Assisted Triple Reaming Technique for Total Hip Arthroplasty in Protrusio Acetabuli
Authors: Narayan Hulse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a robotic-assisted triple reaming technique during total hip arthroplasty in protrusio acetabuli. This technique uses computerized tomography–based 3-dimensional preoperative planning and intraoperative dynamic referencing to prepare the acetabulum. Two separate surgical plans are created using the robotic workflow. Using the first plan, the protrusio defect is prepared utilizing a reamer that matches the size and location of the defect. Using the second plan, the true acetabulum is reamed, using a reamer that restores the hip center and provides an adequate rim fit. Thirdly, the impacted bone grafts are also reverse reamed using the second plan. The 3-step sequential reaming as well as final insertion of the acetabular component are performed with real-time robotic feedback. The robotic-assisted triple reaming technique can facilitate restoring the hip biomechanics in protrusio acetabuli.Keywords: Robotic assisted total hip arthroplasty, Protrusio acetabuli, Impaction bone grafting, Hip centre of rotation

Introduction

While performing total hip arthroplasty (THA) in a protrusio acetabuli (PA), recreating the center of rotation of the hip is important to restore the biomechanics of the hip joint and the abductor function [1,2]. Various techniques of acetabular reconstruction in PA include impaction bone grafting using autografts or allografts, metal cages, reinforcement rings, and structural bone grafts [2,3]. Potential difficulties encountered during the reconstruction of the PA are difficulty in dislocating the hip, lack of bone stock, deficient medial support for the acetabular component, and medialization of the joint center [2,4].

Impaction bone grafting has been successfully employed with good medium and long-term results using both cemented and uncemented components in PA [[2], [3], [4]]. Restoring the center of rotation by impaction bone grafting and achieving adequate peripheral rim fit depends on the precise reaming by the surgeon [5]. A lesser amount of graft may leave the cup unsupported, or an excessive graft may prevent proper seating of the implant [3]. Precise execution of the bone grafting process to restore the hip center can be difficult and may result in uncorrected deformity, leading to suboptimal placement of the acetabular cup [1].

Without enabling technologies like fluoroscopy, navigation, or robotics, it may be challenging to achieve the desired acetabular component position intraoperatively in PA [6]. Due to enhanced preoperative planning capabilities and real-time intraoperative dynamic referencing, robotic THA aims at improving the reproducibility and increasing the precision of component positioning, with promising radiological results [7,8]. Studies have indicated that robotic THA can provide more accurate cup placement and better restoration of the native hip anatomy compared to conventional THA, although these benefits need not translate into direct clinical benefits [9].

We describe a robotic-assisted triple reaming technique (RTR technique) to reconstruct the PA during THA using impaction bone grafting and an uncemented acetabular component. This method uses enhanced CT-based preoperative planning and intraoperative real-time dynamic referencing to prepare the acetabular component in PA.

Surgical technique

Step 1: preoperative planning

Patients are assessed clinically and radiographically (Fig. 1). The plain radiographs showed a moderate PA [10] with severe degenerative changes. A computerized tomography (CT) scan is also performed using the Mako hip protocol (Mako, Fort Lauderdale, Florida). The Mako segmentation process is utilized for preoperative planning in 2 stages to create 2 separate robotic plans: 1 for the protrusio defect and the second for the actual acetabular component.

Preoperative radiographs (a) anteroposterior view and (b) lateral view.

The surgical plans are created by the surgeon with the help of the Mako Technician, using the robotic protocol, usually just before the start of the surgery. It can also be done at any time before the surgery, once the back-end process of the Mako segmentation process is completed. Currently, the Mako system will not allow both these plans to be saved simultaneously, and hence, the second plan must be saved as a screenshot and then entered manually. The Mako system mandates a trained Mako Technician to assist you during the surgery, as the current software cannot be operated by the surgeon.

Planning for the acetabular protrusio: This step will determine the size and location of the acetabular reamer used to ream only the protrusio defect of the acetabulum. The protrusio part of the acetabulum is templated using different sizes of the acetabular components using the robotic protocol (Fig. 2). The reamer position is virtually adjusted in the sagittal, coronal, and transverse planes to fit the protrusio cavity (Fig. 2a-c). Any degree of anteversion and inclination is accepted for this reamer, as this determines only the best fit to prepare the bony defect and not the position of the final acetabular component. Leaving about 2 to 3 mm of medial bone is essential to prevent damaging the medial wall. The reamer used in this step will be smaller than the actual acetabular component. For example, size 42 is used for illustration in this report (Fig. 2). Once the size and depth of the reamer are satisfactory in all the planes, the surgical plan is saved as a screenshot.

Robotic planning for the acetabular protrusio.

Planning for the actual acetabular component: This step will determine the size, location, version, and inclination of the acetabular component and the corresponding reamer size. The acetabulum is templated using the Mako 4.0 protocol in standard fashion. The acetabular component's size and position are confirmed in the coronal, sagittal, and transverse planes (Fig. 3a-c). Typically, the reamer used in this step will be larger and placed laterally compared to the reamer used for reaming the protrusio defect. For example, size 48 is used for illustration in this report (Fig. 3). Once the templating of the acetabular component is satisfactory in terms of size and position, the plan is saved as a screenshot.

Robotic planning for the actual acetabular component.

Step 2: surgical approach and registration

The hip can be exposed using any surgical approach and technique of the surgeon’s choice. A direct anterior approach, with the patient positioned supine on a standard operating table, is used for illustration in this report. The registration process is completed according to the Mako 4.0 protocol.

Step 3: ‘the first reaming’ to prepare the acetabular protrusio

The Mako technician is asked to load plan 1(a) in the robotic system (Fig. 2) for reaming the protrusio. The protrusio part is reamed using the robotic arm (Fig. 4a) using real-time feedback displayed in the robotic monitor until the pretemplated depth and size are achieved (Fig. 4b). The acetabulum is dried and inspected to confirm it is satisfactory.

(a) Reaming the acetabular protrusio and (b) Robotic screen showing completion of the reaming as planned.

Step 4: ‘the second reaming’ to prepare for the actual acetabular component

Now, the Mako technician will load the plan for the actual acetabular component (Fig. 3) as planned in step 1b. Mako reamer is advanced under real-time feedback to achieve the correct center of hip, version, and inclination as displayed in the robotic monitor (Fig. 5a and b). In the example, we have used a cup size of 48, a version of 20°, and an inclination of 40°. Reaming is stopped when the preoperatively planned hip center is achieved. (Fig. 5 b).

(a) Reaming for the actual acetabular component and (b) Robotic screen showing completed reaming as planned.

Step 5: impaction bone grafting

Impaction grafting is performed using the autologous femoral head (Fig. 6). Grafting is performed in a standard fashion [11]. Impaction is performed until the reamed protrusio part is filled to reach the level of the actual position of the acetabular cup as determined in step 4.

(a) Autologous bone grafts, (b) Reamed acetabulum, (c) Bone grafts placed in the acetabulum, and (d) After impaction of the bone grafts.

Step 6: ‘the third reaming’ in reverse mode

Now, the Mako technician will load the plan for the actual acetabular component (Fig. 3) as planned in step 1 again. Reverse reaming is performed on top of the impacted bone grafts using the same reamer size used in step 3, which is the same size as the acetabular component (Fig. 7). Reverse reaming is done using the robotic arm to reach the desired depth as templated (Fig. 7b). This step has to be done carefully and gently with firm, continuous pressure on the reamer handle to prevent distortion of the impacted graft particles.

(a) Reverse reaming of the impacted bone grafts. (b) Robotic screen showing the completion of reverse reaming as planned.

Step 7. acetabular cup insertion

As preoperatively planned, the acetabular cup is impacted using the robotic arm, using real-time robotic feedback to achieve the pretemplated component position (Fig. 8a) Acetabular screws are inserted if indicated. Version and inclination are checked and confirmed using a robotic probe (Fig. 8b). The acetabular liner is then inserted (Fig. 8c).

(a) Real-time robotic screen confirming the position of the acetabular component insertion, (b) Final component position being confirmed by a robotic probe, (c) After insertion of the acetabular shell, and (d) After insertion of the acetabular liner.

The femur is prepared using the Mako 4.0 workflow (Fig. 9a). A trial reduction is performed. Final components are inserted (Fig. 9b), and the wound is closed in layers. Postoperative hip radiographs are obtained (Fig. 10).

(a) Robotic planning for the femoral component. (b) After insertion of the femoral component.

(a) Immediate postoperative radiograph, (b) and (c) follow-up radiographs. (Arrows point to the acetabular protrusio with impaction bone grafting).

Discussion

The robotic-assisted triple reaming technique described in this report enables surgeons to use the robotic technology to precisely prepare the acetabulum during a THA in PA. The RTR technique utilizes the Mako robotic technology for enhanced CT-based preoperative planning using a virtual 3D image. The triple reaming technique helps to prepare the acetabular protrusio, the true acetabulum, and the final impaction of the bone grafts using robotic precision and real-time intraoperative monitoring. Even though we have described the technique via the direct anterior approach, the RTR technique can be used via the Posterolateral and Anterolateral approaches using the Mako system.

Depending on the severity, PA can complicate the performance of THA, and successful clinical and radiological results may not always be achieved after conventional THA for treatment of PA [2,12]. However, with the appropriate technique, it may be possible to achieve satisfactory outcomes [1,3,4]. Ranawat et al. reported the results of THAs in PA in 35 rheumatoid patients at a follow-up of 4.3 years [13]. They observed acetabular radiolucency in 94% of patients if the postoperative hip center was migrated more than 1 cm medially or superiorly, compared to zero radiolucency when the hip center was within 5 mm of the anatomical position. In PA, restoring the center of rotation of the hip can be difficult using conventional methods. Without real-time verification of the cup position, it may not be possible to reliably achieve the desired component position, and hence, enabling technologies like fluoroscopy, navigation, and robotics may be required to facilitate this difficult task [6].

Callanan et al have reported that only about 50% of implants were within an acceptable safe range in conventional THA and hip resurfacing [14]. To improve the component positioning, several navigation systems have been developed. An image-based computer navigation system relies on information obtained from CT, magnetic resonance imaging, or fluoroscopy [[15], [16], [17]]. The imageless systems utilize a combination of kinematics and anatomical data collected during registration [15]. Although computer navigation systems offer 3D positioning of the surgical field, surgical tools, and implants, the operator may not place tools and implants accurately. Hence, robotic systems are developed that can be active or semiactive types [18]. Robotic-assisted technique allows accurate preoperative planning and its precise execution during the procedure [9].

Clinical evidence suggests that navigated THA and robotic THA help achieve accurate cup placement, even though patient-reported clinical outcomes and long-term outcomes superiority are not established [15,[18], [19], [20]]. Robotic and navigated THA have also been reported to be useful even in complex hip conditions, including dysplastic hips [16,21]. However, a specific reconstruction technique using these enabling technologies for THA in PA has not been described previously. The current method can only be performed using a CT-based robotic system because it involves preoperative planning and execution of a surgical plan using a CT-based, 3-dimensional virtual model.

The methods described by Ranawat et al. [13] or Pierchon et al. [22] are commonly used to determine the hip center using plane radiographs. These methods may be applied during the preoperative planning of hip arthroplasties, even though plane radiographic measurements can be challenging to reproduce during surgery. The technique used in our report utilizes a 3-dimensional virtual image for preoperative planning, and the plan is executed precisely using a robotic arm. Manual reaming of the medial wall in protrusion of the acetabulum could result in perforation, and some authors have advised gentle curettage only [3]. In the current technique, the medial wall is defined on a CT scan, reaming is performed safely using a robotic arm, and 2 mm of the medial wall is left intact.

Impaction bone grafting has shown reliable results in the medium and long term in PA [[2], [3], [4]]. However, achieving the precise hip center can be difficult, and impacting the bone satisfactorily in the 3D plane can be challenging. If the amount of the graft is even minimally inadequate, it can reduce the support to the acetabular component. If the graft is in excess, the component may not seat properly. Our technique performs the final reverse reaming of the impacted bone graft using the robotic arm, as precisely calculated in preoperative planning. However, vigorous reverse reaming can disrupt the integrity of already impacted bone grafts [23]. Hence, it should be done carefully and gently by applying steady pressure on the reamer. Reverse reaming should not be utilized for grossly changing the level of the impacted bone surface, but only for fine adjustments to match the preoperative planning.

There is a paucity of literature about robotic THA in PA. In a case report, Haniball et al. reported using robotic arm-assisted acetabular component placement in a post-traumatic PA [24]. These authors did not use robotic assistance to either preoperatively define the size of the protrusio defect or ream the acetabular protrusio using the robotic arm. However, the authors successfully used the robotic arm to ream and impact the acetabular component [24]. Several meta-analyses supported the benefit of robotic assistance in improved acetabular component placement within the Lewinnek safe zone in robotic THA [9,25,26], but not specific to PA.

The advantages of this technique are multiple. This technique not only avoids penetration of the medial wall but also helps to restore the hip center by accurately placing the bone grafts, reaming for the final position of the acetabular cup, and insertion of the acetabular cup under direct robotic control. These steps are traditionally accomplished using conventional techniques. However, it can be challenging to achieve accurate positioning of implants and assess the numerical value of implant positioning in THA using conventional methods [17].

Existing reconstruction techniques require manual preparation of the protrusio defect and impaction bone grafting [[1], [2], [3]]. Extrapolating the benefits of robotic THA, the RTR technique could be successfully utilized in the technically demanding reconstruction of the acetabular protrusion during THA. Robotic-assisted technique allows CT scan-based, accurate measurements at 3 critical steps of the reconstruction, and enables precise execution. However, long-term studies with an adequate sample size are required to confirm the efficacy of this technique, even though the biology of reconstruction is similar to established previous methods.

Conclusions

In PA, the RTR technique can facilitate the complex task of positioning the impaction grafting and implantation of the acetabular component to restore the hip biomechanics. This technique allows effective use of robotic technology for enhanced CT-based preoperative planning and reaming of PA with real-time intraoperative dynamic referencing.
