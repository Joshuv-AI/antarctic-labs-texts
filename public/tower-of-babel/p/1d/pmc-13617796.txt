How physical and sexual abuse impacts oral health: a cross-sectional study from a Norwegian Children’s House
Authors: Katrine Håkstad, Ewa Alicja Szyszko Hovden, Tove Irene Wigen, Liv Fegran, Unni Mette Stamnes Köpp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the association between neglect and an increased risk of oral disease is well established, the independent association between physical or sexual abuse and oral health has been less extensively studied.AimThis study explored oral health condition in children suspected of being exposed to physical or sexual abuse by examining the association between abuse type and caries experience and gingival condition. It also assessed whether these associations were modified by age, sex, and ethnicity.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 328 children, aged 1–18 years, who underwent clinical forensic dental examinations at a Norwegian Children’s House (NCH) from 2017 to 2021 (n = 328) due to suspected physical or sexual abuse. Data were collected from forensic dental and medical records at the NCH and included data from extra- and intra-oral examinations accompanied by conversations with the children. Negative binomial and logistic regression were performed for unadjusted, adjusted, and stratified analyses. Data are presented as ratios of means (RMs) and odds ratios (ORs), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSexually abused children had a higher mean number of decayed teeth (DT) (RM 1.99, 95%CI 0.99–4.02) compared with physically abused children. They also more frequently received toothbrushing assistance. When stratifying by sex and ethnicity, the results revealed that sexually abused males had a higher mean number of DT compared with physically abused males (RM 4.23, 95%CI 1.37–13.12). A similar association was observed in sexually abused Ethnic Norwegian children (RM 3.53, 95%CI 1.60–7.82). Gingival condition varied by age. Sexually abused children exhibited better gingival condition before the age of four compared with their physically abused peers, but these conditions deteriorated after the age of thirteen, where the results showed an opposite association.ConclusionsThe findings indicated that oral health condition varied according to abuse type, with poorer oral health conditions among sexually abused children than those physically abused. These results, underscore the crucial role of forensic and dental professionals in identifying oral health conditions that may signal possible abuse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09143-3.Keywords: Dental caries, Child abuse, Forensic dentistry, Oral health, Oral hygiene, Toothbrushing

Background

Child abuse refers to actions taken by a caregiver or responsible adult that directly harm or negatively affect a child’s well-being [1], even if harm is not intended [2]. Abuse is a part of the broader category of child maltreatment, which includes all forms of physical, emotional and sexual abuse, and neglect [3]. Child physical and sexual abuse represent particularly severe forms of harm involving direct bodily injury, coercion, or exploitation, often leaving severe immediate and long-term physical and psychological consequences [4]. These types of abuse are recognised as severe public health concerns [4]. The European Status Report on Preventing Child Maltreatment (2021) has estimated the prevalence of child physical abuse to be 22.9% and child sexual abuse to be 9.6% (13.4% in girls and 5.7% in boys) [5]. Children exposed to one type of abuse often experience other forms of abuse or neglect as well [6, 7]. Despite these concerning figures, many cases of child abuse may go undetected, allowing cycles of harm and trauma to persist. This can have serious implications for a child’s overall health, including oral health. These findings underscore the need to examine how child maltreatment impacts oral health, as the mouth often reveals signs of abuse.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines oral health as “the state of the mouth, teeth and orofacial structures that enable individuals to perform essential functions, such as eating, breathing and speaking, and encompasses psychosocial dimensions, such as self-confidence and the ability to socialise and work without pain, discomfort and embarrassment” [8]. Poor oral hygiene, such as inadequate brushing and flossing routines, increases the risk of dental diseases, infections, and inflammation in the mouth, negatively affecting gingival condition, which can lead to impaired oral health [9]. Poor oral health can adversely affect children’s physical and cognitive development and consequently diminish their overall quality of life [8, 9]. To secure lifelong good oral health, initiation of toothbrushing twice daily with fluoridated toothpaste is recommended from the eruption of the first tooth [10]. Parental involvement is necessary to establish and maintain proper oral hygiene routines for young children [11]. As children transition into adolescence, the responsibility for oral hygiene gradually shifts from parents to the adolescent. In cases of child abuse, oral hygiene assistance may be compromised, leading to neglect of the child’s oral health [12, 13]. Myran et al. [14] found that exposure to traumatic experiences, including abuse, was associated with less frequent toothbrushing, which may negatively impact oral hygiene behaviours [14].

Several studies have found that children who experience abuse or neglect exhibit a higher prevalence of dental caries and an increased risk of overall poor oral health [9, 15–19]. One study has found that sexually abused children may have poorer oral health outcomes than children experiencing other abuse [20]. Other studies have reported no significant differences in caries prevalence across different types of abuse [16, 18, 19, 21]. Severe, untreated caries may indicate neglect, but the relationship between abuse type and caries remains unclear [16, 22].

The mechanisms linking child abuse to oral health are multifactorial. Physical abuse often co-occurs with neglect and dysfunctional family environments, leading to inadequate oral hygiene and limited access to dental care [11]. In contrast, sexual abuse may trigger emotional distress and stress-related behaviours that reduce motivation for self-care and impair gingival conditions [30]. Moreover, chronic stress responses and social instability can aggravate inflammation and further deteriorate oral health [9].

In Norway, efforts are made to ensure that children suspected of being exposed to physical or sexual abuse or witnesses to violence undergo facilitated interrogations at a Norwegian Children’s House (NCH) [23]. Embracing the ‘Barnahus’ model [24] as standard practice, the NCH provides a child-friendly environment where forensic interviews, medical and dental examinations, psychological assessments, and child protection interventions are conducted under one roof with regular follow-up and referral to health and welfare agencies for ongoing support in suspected cases of abuse [23]. Since 2016, trained dentists have been part of the forensic medical team at the NCH. Forensic dental examinations are conducted in accordance with a NCH protocol at the request of the prosecution in cases involving suspected orofacial signs, such as findings or injuries to the head, neck, face, ears, lips, or inside the mouth.

Toft et al. [20] analysed public dental records but did not account for key confounders [20]. In contrast, the present study utilizes forensic clinical data to provide a more detailed assessment of oral health according to abuse type. The study therefore aimed to examine the associations between abuse type and caries experience and gingival condition in children suspected of physical or sexual abuse and further assess whether these associations were modified by age, sex, and ethnicity. Based on previous research, we hypothesised that child abuse negatively affects oral health and that oral health outcomes vary according to the type of abuse experienced.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study has been prepared in accordance with the STROBE checklist [25] (Appendix B).

Participants

Between 2017 and 2021, 2159 children and adolescents (aged 1–18 years old) attended the Norwegian Children’s House (NCH) in Southern Norway on suspicion of physical or sexual abuse occurring within close relationships. Of these, 408 were referred to and underwent a forensic dental examination due to suspected abuse involving the orofacial region (head, face, neck, ears, lips and oral cavity Eighty cases with incomplete dental or forensic reports, missing data, or duplicate records were excluded from the analysis. The final study sample comprised 328 participants (Fig. 1).

The inclusion and exclusion of participants in the final study sample (n = 328)

Data collection

Data were collected from forensic dental and medical records at the NCH. The reports included the forensic assessment protocol and the dental records (OPUS). They were based on the clinical forensic examinations as well as the forensic referral documents, which including the police report, any concern reports, and the request for forensic dental examination from the prosecution authority. Based on the dentist’s conversations with the child, self-perceived information on oral hygiene routines was retrieved from notes in the dental records.

Clinical examinations, including extra-oral and intra-oral regions, were thoroughly conducted and documented. The extra-oral examination included inspection for objective signs, findings and injuries of the head, neck, ears, face, and lips, while the intra-oral clinical dental examination was conducted using a visual-tactile approach in accordance with an NCH protocol (Appendix A). Four trained dentists performed the forensic dental examinations in a modified dental office at the NCH, which was equipped with a dental chair (HEKA Dental, Unic Line), dental mirror (CE 4 Top Dent, DAB, S-Upplands Väsby, LOT 34184), sharp probe (28775 Nordenta, Stainless CE), and a dental operating light (HEKA Dental, Unic Line) for adequate illumination. No X-rays, air-syringe or cotton rolls were available.

The dental examination collected information about the prevalence of caries, caries experience, the presence and amount of plaque accumulation, and the presence and severity of gingivitis. The visual aspect allowed the clinician to identify obvious signs of dental decay, discolouration, or structural anomalies, while the tactile component aided in detecting less visible issues such as subgingival calculus or surface irregularities on the teeth [26]. Although formal calibration procedures were not conducted, inter-examiner consistency was maintained through discussion and consensus to resolve diagnostic uncertainties.

Variables

Dependent (outcome) variables

Dental caries and gingival condition were the oral health outcomes and were used as dependent variables in the analyses.

Caries was recorded in both dentitions using the tooth surfaces as unit measurements. Teeth were coded based on their condition: sound, decayed (D), missing due to caries (M), or filled (F). In the analysis, caries experience (DMFT for permanent teeth and dmft for primary teeth) and untreated caries (DT) were used as continuous (COUNT) variables. All tooth surfaces were thoroughly examined, and each tooth was classified according to its most severely affected surface using a five-graded caries scoring system, where grades 1 to 2 indicated caries confined to enamel and grades 3 to 5 indicated caries extending to dentin [27]. This five-grade visual–tactile system was summarised as DT and DMFT within the WHO framework. If visual and tactile examination revealed dentine or secondary caries, surfaces were registered as decayed.

The assessment of gingival condition included the presence of visible plaque and bleeding on probing. Plaque and gingivitis were recorded as either absent or present, and further combined into gingival condition and dichotomised into “poor” and “good”. Poor gingival condition was defined as moderate to severe amounts of visible dental plaque and/or bleeding on probing. Good gingival condition was defined as having no or little visible plaque, callus, or no bleeding on probing (gingivitis). Gingival condition was assessed using a simplified dichotomous measure.

Independent (exposures) variable

The type of abuse, whether suspected or confirmed, was categorised as the exposure variable, with children being classified as victims of either physical or sexual abuse the forensic reports. Sexual abuse included but was not restricted to oral sexual abuse. Information on overlapping or co-occurring abuse types was not available, as the authorities only recorded the primary abuse type that formed the basis for prosecution. Clinical forensic dental examinations were requested by the prosecutor only when the police investigation revealed suspicion of inflicted acts involving the orofacial region [27].

Covariates

The potential confounding factors included the demographic factors of age, sex, and parental national origin. Age was recorded as a continuous variable and divided into age groups (i.e., 1–5 years old, 6–11 years old, 12–18 years old). Sex was recorded as male or female. Parental national origin was classified as Norwegian or non-Norwegian, with non-Norwegian referring to children with one or both parents of foreign origin, regardless of their geographical background.

Mediators

Toothbrushing frequency, fluoride supplements and toothbrushing assistance (for children under 12 years) were self-reported oral health behaviours collected through conversations with the child during the dental examination. Toothbrushing frequency was reported as morning and evening, once a day, or seldom/never, and dichotomised as twice a day (morning and evening) and once a day or less. The use of fluoride supplements and toothbrushing assistance were reported as yes, no, or seldom/never, and dichotomised as yes and no (no and seldom/never).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, Texas, USA) [28]. Descriptive statistics in the form of frequencies (percentages) were used to summarise categorical data, while means and standard deviations (SD) were used to summarise continuous data. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

The difference in mean values between children who were sexually and physically abused was assessed using independent samples t-test. Associations between categorical variables were analysed using the Chi-square test if the expected cell count was > 5. Otherwise, a Fisher’s exact test was used.

Negative binomial regression (NBR) models were chosen to investigate the association between abuse and count of caries experience and untreated caries (DT) as these variables exhibited overdispersion, making NBR more appropriate than any contrasts. Estimates of the ratio of means (RM) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were obtained from the NBR models, showing an increase or decrease in the count of caries experience and untreated caries among the children who were sexually abused relative to those who were physically abused. Further, a binary logistic regression was used to assess the likelihood of having poor gingival condition among the children who were sexually abused compared with children who were physically abused. All statistical models were adjusted for age, sex and ethnicity, and the statistical significance level was set at a = 0.05.

Furthermore, possible interactions between abuse types and sex or ethnicity were checked. The likelihood ratio (LR) test in STATA was used to assess effect modification on models with two-way interactions to assess the associations between abuse type and caries in men separately from women and in Norwegians separately from non-Norwegians.

The analytical strategy and the relationship between the variables and potential mediating pathways are described in the Directed acyclic graph (DAG; Fig. 2). In the multivariable analyses, guided by the DAG, physical and sexual abuse were considered exposure variables, while caries experience, untreated caries, and gingival condition were treated as outcome variables. Age, sex, and ethnicity were included as confounding factors. Although the DAG illustrated possible mediation pathways, we did not adjust for mediators in the models, as the goal was to assess the total effect of the relationship between the exposure and the outcomes. The DAG served as a framework to guide the data analysis strategy and to reduce the risk of bias [29].

Sex-specific associations between type of abuse and caries experience

The LR test indicated effect modification by sex. An association between caries experience and child sexual abuse compared with physical abuse was only found in males. Results showed that sexually abused males were associated with more than four times higher mean decayed teeth (RM 4.23, 95% CI 1.37–13.12) and nearly three times higher caries experience (RM 2.81, 95% CI 1.00–7.86) compared with physically abused males; however, the CIs were wide.

Ethnicity-specific associations of sexual and physical abuse with caries experience

Results from the NBR analysis showed that when stratified by ethnicity, Norwegian children exposed to sexual abuse had three and a half times more decayed teeth (RM 3.53, 95% CI 1.60–7.82) compared with those who experienced physical abuse, and almost three times higher caries experience (RM 2.95, 95% CI 1.51–5.75; Table 3).

By contrast, non-Norwegian children who were physically abused had a significantly higher mean number of decayed, missing, and filled teeth (RM 0.27, 95% CI 0.09–0.80) compared with sexually abused non-Norwegians (Table 3).

Figure 3 and 4 shows that the predicted mean number of decayed teeth and caries experience in children who were physically abused was higher among non-Norwegian children compared with Norwegian children. Discussion

This study examined the impact of physical and sexual abuse on oral health in children at a Norwegian Children’s House. The findings support the hypothesis that child abuse negatively affects oral health with outcomes varying by abuse type.

The results showed that children who had a history of sexual abuse demonstrated higher mean DT and DMFT scores and more often received toothbrushing assistance compared with children who had experienced physical abuse. Differences in caries prevalence between children suspected of sexual versus physical abuse may stem from variations in caregiving environments and stress-related behaviours [9, 30]. The abuse in this cohort occurred within close relationships, and in most cases the suspected perpetrator was the primary caregiver or a person close to the family. This relational proximity may contribute to both emotional distress and reduced attention to daily oral hygiene routines. Several studies confirmed an association between poor oral health, particularly caries prevalence [14, 16, 18], and child maltreatment [9, 15, 16, 18, 21] or neglect [17, 31]. Nakamura et al. [16] described a difference in caries prevalence among abused children, with an odds ratio of 4.1, but only an association with neglect was specified and not the specific abuse types [16].

To our knowledge, only one study has investigated the association between oral health in sexually abused children versus physically abused children using data from dental records of children referred to an NCH [20]. Our findings align with results from that study, which reported four times higher caries prevalence among children suspected of experiencing sexual abuse compared with those suspected of experiencing physical abuse [20]. However, unlike that study, the present study provided a more accurate assessment of the relationship between abuse type and oral health by controlling for key confounders, such as age, sex, and ethnicity. This finding suggests that the present study may shed light on a less-examined aspect of the relationship between abuse and oral health, particularly the higher mean number of decayed teeth among males, warranting further investigation. Moreover, our results on the specific association between oral health outcomes and cases of physical abuse versus sexual abuse remain relatively underexplored, with limited research on this comparison.

An unexpected finding was the higher levels of decayed teeth in sexually abused Norwegian children compared with their physically abused counterparts, alongside a greater caries prevalence in physically abused non-Norwegian children compared with physically abused Norwegian children. The increased caries prevalence in children with parents of non-Norwegian origin aligns with the results of a scoping review by Folayan et al. [31] that examined childhood caries in individuals with a migrant background [31]. However, they did not differ between abuse types. Our findings may indicate that ethnicity plays an important moderating role in the relationship between the type of abuse and oral health outcomes. This observation may be further explained by cultural barriers to healthcare, stigma surrounding sexual abuse, or health-related factors unique to these populations [19, 31]. These patterns may indicate complex interactions between ethnicity, type of abuse, and oral health, meriting further investigation and potentially providing valuable insights into the relationship between child abuse and health disparities.

This study revealed age-specific variations in gingival condition. Sexually abused children exhibited better gingival condition before the age of four, followed by a decline after the age of eight, with gingival health worsened by adolescence in sexually abused children compared with their physically abused counterparts. To our knowledge, these associations have not been previously documented. The age-related decline in gingival health may partly reflect the transition from caregiver-assisted toothbrushing in early childhood to increasing self-responsibility in adolescence, when motivation and oral hygiene routines often weaken [11, 12]. The observed association between abuse type and gingival condition may arise through several mechanisms. Physical abuse could directly affect orofacial regions or indirectly lead to poor oral hygiene through dental neglect [7], while sexual abuse might influence gingival conditions via stress-mediated pathways or reduced motivation for self-care [17]. Moreover, a proportion of the included children had parents originating from countries where corporal punishment is not legally prohibited, and such cultural differences in caregiving norms and health behaviours may have influenced both oral hygiene practices and stress responses [31, 32]. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design and relatively small subgroups limit inference, and the observed differences may partly be due to random variation. Further research is needed to confirm these associations.

Parental involvement is essential for young children’s oral health outcomes [10–13]. Compromised caregiving can lead to insufficient supervision and reduced toothbrushing frequency among abused children, thereby increasing the risk of adverse oral health outcomes [10]. Within the NCH cohort, among children under twelve years who received toothbrushing assistance, those who had experienced sexual abuse received assistance more frequently than those who had experienced physical abuse—a somewhat surprising finding [32]. The distinction between oral and non-oral sexual abuse may be relevant in this context, as oral abuse could have a more direct impact on oral health behaviours. This possible association contrasts with previous research in children aged 2–18 years old, which has generally shown that abused children typically receive less preventive dental care [16]. Additionally, Matsuyama et al. [11] demonstrated that insufficient parental involvement and lack of supervision were associated with a higher prevalence of caries and unhealthy oral health behaviours [11]. Beyond caregiving factors, psychosocial mechanisms such as fear, avoidance, and disrupted daily routines may also contribute to poorer oral hygiene among abused children by reducing motivation for self-care and regular dental attendance [14, 30]. These findings contribute to understanding the complex relationship between oral hygiene, toothbrushing behaviour, and parental involvement.

Childhood sexual abuse has been linked to psychosocial and behavioural problems in adulthood, resulting in increased emotional distress and challenges in maintaining self-care routines, including oral hygiene [14, 30]. In contrast, although childhood sexual abuse is often linked to re-traumatisation and dental anxiety in adulthood, such responses are less commonly observed in children [30]. This discrepancy may result from children’s ability to conceal psychological distress and stigma, making abuse more challenging to identify through behaviour or verbal communication alone [30]. Their ability to mask symptoms emphasises the need for comprehensive health assessments, including dental examinations, to identify potential abuse [30].

All studies have some strengths and limitations. A major strength of this study is the use of data from clinical examinations of children with police-reported abuse. Unlike most existing studies comparing abused with non-abused children, this research provides valuable insights into comparisons between abuse types. Moreover, the study uniquely controlled for confounding factors like age, sex, and ethnicity in this demographic. However, the study’s findings should be interpreted considering some limitations. Abuse types were categorised based on the primary reason for the criminal investigation as either physical or sexual abuse. However, the data did not include information on whether oral sexual abuse had occurred. Furthermore, different types of abuse often overlap and should be considered in cases where multiple types coexist. Ethnicity was categorised differently in this study than in others, which may have influenced the observed variations related to ethnic differences. Data were collected from a single Children’s House in one region of Norway, and information on parental education and socioeconomic status was not available. The absence of these covariates may contribute to residual confounding and limits the generalisability of the findings. Dentists had access to the forensic referral, including the police report, concern reports, and requests for forensic dental examination from the prosecution authority, but external dental records were not available; therefore, comparison with previous or external dental treatment data was not possible.

Although orofacial injuries are frequently observed in abused children and were assessed within this research project, these findings were not included in the present analysis, as they are being examined in detail in a separate study based on the same forensic dataset.

The protocol was based on national guidance and WHO/DMFT standards and was internally piloted but not formally validated [27]. Furthermore, using visual-tactile dental examinations without X-rays restrict the ability to detect interproximal, subgingival, or early caries and periapical conditions, increasing the risk of underdiagnosing oral health disease. This study evaluated DMFT for both primary and permanent dentitions as a measure of total caries experience and does not account for differences in dentitions type or stages of dental development. Lastly while dental caries and gingival condition were assessed, intra-oral soft tissue signs of abuse were not evaluated in this study. Furthermore, gingival condition was assessed using a simplified dichotomous measure based on visible plaque and bleeding on probing rather than a standardized clinical index, which may limit measurement validity and comparability with other studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated distinct oral health disparities in sexually abused children compared with those physically abused, emphasising the importance of incorporating oral health assessments into forensic evaluations of suspected abuse. The results reinforce the important role of forensic and dental professionals in recognising oral health conditions that may signal child abuse, including, but not limited to, neglect. Future research should further explore the differential impact of different types of abuse on oral health. Moreover, enhancing awareness and knowledge among dental professionals and healthcare personnel through strengthened protocols for detection, documentation, and follow-up is important to improve the identification, appropriate referral and management of child abuse.
