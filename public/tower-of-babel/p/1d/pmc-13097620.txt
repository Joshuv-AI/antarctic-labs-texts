Optimal Wound pH and Related Factors in the Healing Process of Diabetes‐Related Foot Ulcer: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Sukarni Sukarni, Suriadi Jais, Haryanto Haryanto, Muhammad Aminuddin, Kanae Mukai, Mayumi Okuwa, Makoto Oe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDelayed healing in diabetes‐related foot ulcer (DFU) remains a clinical challenge, and regulating wound pH may offer a promising solution due to its significant role in healing. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between wound pH and healing, and identify factors influencing wound pH at each healing phase.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 187 people with diabetes‐related ulcer for up to 8 weeks or until healed. Wound pH was measured at the wound bed, wound edge, and peri‐wound. Healing status and phase transition from inflammation to proliferation were assessed using DMIST. Receiver operating characteristic and multivariate regression were used to determine optimal pH cut‐off and influencing factors.ResultsHealing within 3 days was significantly associated with wound bed pH < 6.7 and wound edge pH < 6.8, while wound edge pH > 7.7 indicated transition to the proliferative phase. In the inflammatory phase, higher wound edge pH was associated with inflammation, absence of systemic infection, and necrotic tissue color. In the proliferative phase, lower pH was associated with higher albumin, absence of maceration, use of hydrofiber dressing at both the wound bed and edge, no stingless bee honey on the wound bed, and use of dialkyl carbamoyl chloride and calcium alginate on the wound edge.ConclusionsThis study provides novel evidence on specific wound pH thresholds associated with healing outcomes and phase transition in DFU highlighting the importance of controlling pH‐related factors to prevent delayed healing and promote healing.Keywords: acidic, alkaline, DMIST, inflammatory phase, proliferative phase

Introduction

Diabetes‐related foot ulcer (DFU) is a foot ulcer in a person with current or previously diagnosed diabetes mellitus and is usually accompanied by peripheral neuropathy and/or peripheral artery disease in the lower extremities [1]. Affecting an estimated 6.3% of the global diabetes population, DFU constitutes one of the disease's most severe complications, often progressing to infection, persistent inflammation, or gangrene, thereby necessitating hospitalization or lower‐extremity amputation [2, 3, 4, 5], with a 5‐year mortality rate of approximately 30%, while mortality exceeds 70% among patients who undergo major lower‐extremity amputation [6]. Delayed and inappropriate treatment of DFUs significantly increases the risk of developing severe complications [7]. Therefore, early and appropriate DFU treatment is essential to prevent these severe complications.

DFU management includes debridement, pressure off‐loading, vascular evaluation, infection control, and glycemic regulation [8, 9, 10]. Topical and adjuvant therapies further optimize the wound environment, including antimicrobial dressings, bioactive products, and advanced options such as negative pressure or hyperbaric oxygen therapy [8, 11, 12, 13]. Despite the development of various treatments, delayed healing of DFU can still occur.

One of the solutions to these issues is the control of wound pH, which plays a critical role in modulating the wound‐healing environment. Wound pH is increasingly recognized as an important component of the local wound microenvironment that may influence key biological processes involved in healing, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and tissue regeneration. Experimental and translational studies have shown that inflammatory and oxidative stress responses are regulated by intracellular signaling pathways, such as the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway, which plays a central role in cell survival, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and tissue repair [14]. In addition, evidence from studies on bioactive compounds, nanomaterial‐based therapies, and regenerative approaches indicates that modulation of the local physicochemical environment can affect apoptotic and regenerative signaling pathways, further influencing tissue repair processes [15, 16, 17]. Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic framework supporting the relevance of wound pH as part of the wound microenvironment that may modulate inflammatory and regenerative responses during wound healing.

Previous studies have demonstrated that wound pH is an important determinant of the healing process in DFU. An alkaline wound environment is associated with increased bacterial colonization, elevated protease activity, impaired tissue regeneration, and delayed healing [18, 19, 20], whereas a mildly acidic environment promotes keratinocyte migration, fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and re‐epithelialization [21, 22, 23]. A gradual decrease in wound pH during healing is recognized as an indicator of clinical improvement, whereas wounds that remain alkaline tend to exhibit delayed healing [19, 24]. These findings suggest that the optimal pH varies during the wound healing process and in the various sites, such as the wound bed, wound edge, or peri‐wound area, although the details remain unclear. Furthermore, although it has been reported that some topical treatments, such as acid‐oxidizing solutions and cold atmospheric plasma jets, affect the wound pH and healing [23, 25], the relationship between most topical treatments and wound pH remains unclear. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between wound pH and wound healing and to clarify the factors that affect wound pH at each wound phase. If these can be clarified, this could make it possible to prevent delayed healing by targeting the optimal pH for each stage, such as the inflammatory and proliferative phases, and the individual sites such as the wound bed, wound edge, and peri‐wound area, by controlling the associated factors for each.

Methods

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [26].

Study Population and Design

This study had a prospective observational design. Inclusion criteria for the DFU patients were subjects over 20 years, and those classified as Wagner grades 1 to 4. Exclusion criteria included patients with critical conditions and subjects who did not attend routine follow‐up visits during the study period. The sample size was calculated using Slovin's formula, with the total population being the number of patients examined over 3 months (135 people) and the acceptable margin of error being 95%. The minimum sample was 99, with the added censored rate of 47% [27]. The total number of samples was 187. This study was conducted at the Pembinaan Kesejahteraan Umat Muhammadiyah Kitamura Wound Clinic in Pontianak, Indonesia. The recruitment period was between October 2023 and October 2024, with all participants followed for 8 weeks or until they were healed. Discontinuation criteria were amputation and death.

Data Collection

Characteristic Participants and Wounds

Characteristic participants and wounds were collected from medical records at the first visit. Wound status, infection severity, and topical treatments were assessed and recorded at every clinical visit.

Measurement of pH

Wound pH was measured before and after cleansing at the wound bed, edge, and peri‐wound using a Skin‐pH‐Meter PH 905 (Courage+Khazaka electronic GmbH, Köln, Germany). The wound bed, wound edge, and peri‐wound were measured at one site each. The pH of the wound bed was measured at the center of the wound bed. The pH of the wound edge was measured at 0 o'clock. The peri‐wound pH was measured within 4 cm from the wound edge at the 0 o'clock position. The site was measured three times, with the average of these three readings then used as the final pH value for that site. After cleansing, pH measurements followed the same procedure as before cleansing.

Calibration was performed using standard buffer solutions (pH 4.01 and 7.00) and routine recalibration every 4 weeks. This ensures consistent reliability over measurement sessions. The measurement accuracy was ±0.1 pH units, and reliability was confirmed by an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.976. pH measurements were performed by one trained wound expert (researcher) and conducted at every visit throughout the observation period.

Wound cleansing was performed using sterile 0.9% sodium chloride solution, which is non‐buffered and commonly used in clinical practice to avoid chemical interference with the wound microenvironment. No antiseptic or buffered solutions were applied before pH measurement.

Outcome Measures

Primary Outcomes: Healing Status and Phase Transition from Inflammatory to Proliferative

Wound healing progression was assessed at every clinical visit using the DMIST tool [28]. Complete healing was defined as a total DMIST score of 0. The inflammatory phase was defined as a DMIST inflammation/infection of ≥ 1 point. The proliferative phase was defined as a DMIST inflammation/infection of 0 points and tissue type of the wound bed of ≤ 1 point.

Secondary Outcomes: Factors Affecting Wound pH

Factors that affect wound pH include participants' characteristics, wound characteristics, and topical treatment. Characteristics of the participants evaluated in the study included smoking, antibiotic agents, hemoglobin, albumin, and ankle‐brachial index.

Regarding wound characteristics, the superficial layer was defined as a DMIST depth score of 1, while the deep layer was defined as a score of 2 or more. Maceration was defined as the presence of maceration when a score of 1 or more was met on the DMIST maceration item. Inflammation was defined as a DMIST inflammation/infection score of 1, while infection was defined as a DMIST inflammation/infection score of 2 or more. Osteomyelitis was defined as a DMIST inflammation/infection score of 3 or 4, while no osteomyelitis was defined as a score of 1, 2, or 5. Systemic infection was defined as a DMIST inflammation/infection score of 5, while no systemic infection was defined as a score of 1 to 4. Small size was defined as a DMIST size score of 2 or less, while large size was defined as a score of 3 or more. Granulation was defined as a DMIST tissue type of a wound bed score of 1, while no granulation was defined as a score of 2 or more. White, yellow, and/or grey necrotic tissue was defined as a DMIST tissue type of wound bed score of 2, while no white, yellow, and/or grey necrotic tissue was defined as a score of 1, 3, or 4. Black necrotic tissue was defined as a DMIST tissue type of a wound bed score of 3, while no black necrotic tissue was defined as a score of 1, 2, or 4. Gangrene was defined as a DMIST tissue type of wound bed score of 4, while no gangrene was defined as a score of 1 to 3. Hyperkeratosis/lining/epibole was defined as a DMIST type of wound edge score of 2, while no hyperkeratosis/lining/epibole was defined as a score of 1, 3, or 4. The red ring was defined as a DMIST type of wound edge score of 3, while no red ring was defined as a score of 1, 2, or 4. Ill‐defined or unable to assess due to infection, etc., was defined as a DMIST type of wound edge score of 4, while no Ill‐defined or unable to assess due to infection, etc., was defined as a score of 1 to 3. Tunneling or undermining was defined as the presence of tunneling or undermining when a score of 1 or more was met on the DMIST tunneling or undermining item. Data on DMIST and topical treatment were collected at every visit.

The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kanazawa University, Japan (111104‐1), and the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Technology and Health Muhammadiyah Indonesia (369/II.I.AU/KET.ETIK/X/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were fully informed about the study, including its objectives, procedures, and potential risks. Participants were also informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Patients were not disadvantaged in any way if they decided to decline participation in the study.

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics of Participants and Wounds

Continuous data were presented as the means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Characteristics of the participants and wounds were analyzed using χ 2 or Fisher's exact test and t‐test.

Optimal Cut‐Off pH Values

The relationship between wound pH and healing within 3 days was examined using a t‐test. A period of 3 days or less was selected based on the rationale that pH values during this period reflect a wound environment most conducive to healing. Healing within 3 days refers to a condition in which the wound was confirmed as healed at a clinical visit conducted within ≤ 3 days from the previous visit. This definition did not imply that the entire healing process had occurred within 3 days from the wound onset, but rather that clinical healing was verified within this interval. A similar approach was used to assess the association between the wound pH and the transition from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase, utilizing the same 3‐day predictive interval preceding the observed onset of the proliferative phase. Subsequently, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to identify the most appropriate pH cut‐off values for predicting wound healing and the transition from inflammation to proliferation.

Factors Associated With Wound pH

To examine factors related to the pH, a Mann–Whitney analysis was used for categorical data, while Spearman's correlation coefficient was used for continuous data. Data that showed statistically significant differences in these tests were included as independent variables in a multiple regression analysis using the stepwise method, with the pH used as the dependent variable. At that time, the relationship between the independent variables was confirmed using Spearman's correlation coefficient to identify the multicollinearity among the candidate independent variables, and if there was a correlation coefficient of 0.8 or higher that indicated correlation, the variables with these coefficients were not selecting them. In the multiple regression analysis, the independent variable categorical data were converted into dummy variables. Because the number of data points per participant varied, ID was included as an adjustment factor. All statistical tests were two‐sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study Population

Of the 235 participants initially enrolled, 187 were included in the final analysis. Individuals excluded were those with critical conditions (n = 8) and patients who did not continue to return (n = 40). A total of 142 (75.9%) participants were censored for various reasons, including unknown reasons (n = 136), death (n = 4), and amputation (n = 2) (Figure 1). Of the 1531 total data points obtained from 187 participants, 621 (40.6%) were in the inflammatory phase, 758 (49.5%) were in the proliferative phase, and 152 (9.9%) did not fit either definition.

Wound pH and Healing Outcomes

Wound Healing Within 3 Days

Of the 1531 data sets, 1286 (83.9%) with a known 3‐day prognosis were used in this analysis. There were 33 (2.6%) wounds that healed within 3 days, and 1253 (97.4%) wounds that did not heal within 3 days. Before cleansing, a t‐test showed that the pHs of the wound bed (6.28 ± 0.70) and wound edges (6.47 ± 0.78) of the wounds that healed within 3 days were significantly lower than the pHs of the wound bed (7.10 ± 0.86) and wound edges (7.48 ± 0.84) of the wounds that did not heal within 3 days (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively). In contrast, the peri‐wound pH did not differ significantly between the healed and unhealed within 3 days (6.13 ± 0.57, 6.26 ± 0.78, respectively, p = 0.33). After cleansing, the wound bed pH remained significantly lower in the healed vs. the unhealed (6.46 ± 0.57, 7.20 ± 0.76, respectively), and similarly at the wound edge (6.55 ± 0.65, 7.68 ± 0.73, respectively, p < 0.001) (Figure 2). However, there was no significant difference for the peri‐wound pH between the groups (6.10 ± 0.45, 6.31 ± 0.63, respectively, p = 0.07).

Distribution of the wound bed pH and wound edge pH wound healing within 3 days.

ROC analysis results showed that the area under the curves (AUC) of the wound bed and wound edge before cleansing were 0.776 (95% CI: 706–846) and 0.813 (95% CI: 745–881), respectively (p < 0.001), and the AUCs of the wound bed and wound edge after cleansing were 0.784 (95% CI: 728–843) and 0.870 (95% CI: 812–928), respectively (p < 0.001) (Figure 3). Since the AUC after cleansing was higher than the AUC before cleansing, we performed a subsequent analysis using the pH after cleansing.

ROC curve healing within 3 days in the wound bed and wound edge.

The optimal cut‐off point for the wound bed was selected at pH 6.7 (sensitivity: 75.7%, specificity: 72.7%), which indicated that the wound exhibited good progress if the wound bed pH was less than 6.7. At the wound edge, the optimal cut‐off was pH 6.8 (sensitivity: 88.0%, specificity: 75.8%), which indicated that a wound edge pH less than 6.8 supported wound healing within 3 days (Table 2A).

Transition From Inflammatory to Proliferative Phase Within 3 Days

Of 1286 data sets with a clear 3‐day prognosis, 621 data sets from the inflammatory phase were used in this analysis, with 78 (12.6%) wounds progressing to the proliferative phase within 3 days, while 543 (87.4%) wounds did not progress. The wound bed pH did not vary significantly between the groups (7.17 ± 0.88 vs. 6.96 ± 0.78, p = 0.06). In contrast, the wound edge pH significantly differed between the groups. The mean wound edge pH in the transitioning group was significantly higher compared to the non‐transitioning group (7.89 ± 0.61 vs. 7.42 ± 0.76, p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 4). The AUC for the wound edge pH was 0.703 (p < 0.001) (Figure 5). The optimal cut‐off point was identified at pH 7.7 (sensitivity: 71.8%, specificity: 61.0%), which indicated that the transition occurred from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase within 3 days if the wound edge pH was more than 7.7 (Table 2B).

Discussion

The present study is the first to reveal that the pHs of the wound beds and wound edges that heal within 3 days are significantly lower than the pHs of those that do not heal, with cut‐off values of 6.7 and 6.8, respectively. In contrast, the pH of the wound edges that transitioned to the proliferative phase within 3 days was significantly higher than the pH of those that did not, with a cut‐off value of 7.7. Furthermore, the wound pH in the inflammatory phase was primarily related to the morphological characteristics of the wound, while the wound pH in the proliferative phase was mainly related to the topical treatment. These results suggest that controlling these factors may be a way to help maintain the optimal pH and prevent delayed healing.

Wound pH is generally alkaline at the onset of healing and decreases as repair progresses. An alkaline wound environment is known to increase the activity of proteases, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), elastase, and plasmin, which reach their optimal activity at neutral to alkaline pH levels. When sustained, excessive protease activity accelerates extracellular matrix degradation and may prolong the inflammatory phase, thereby delaying healing progression [20, 29]. In this study, a wound edge pH > 7.7 signified transition to the proliferative phase, indicating readiness for tissue repair—contrary to the conventional view that alkalinity reflects poor healing—and aligns with previous evidence that keratinocyte migration and proliferation, as well as fibroblast proliferation, occur optimally under neutral to mildly alkaline conditions (approximately pH 7.5), whereas excessively acidic or highly alkaline environments impair these cellular processes that are essential for re‐epithelialization and new tissue formation [30].

The wound pH cut‐off points that were identified in the present study were, a pH < 6.7 at the wound bed and a pH < 6.8 at the wound edge for the purpose of predicting healing within 3 days. These findings are consistent with the physiological mechanisms of wound healing. Changes in wound pH play a direct role in regulating multiple biochemical pathways that are critical throughout the wound healing process, and these effects are highly phase dependent. Under acidic conditions (pH 6–7), the activity of proteolytic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases, elastase, and plasmin is reduced, limiting extracellular matrix degradation and supporting tissue regeneration [20, 31]. Acidic pH also enhances oxygen release through the Bohr effect, promotes collagen synthesis and angiogenesis, and inhibits microbial growth, thereby facilitating the proliferative phase [20, 32, 33]. At the cellular level, pH also critically influences skin cell behavior, particularly keratinocyte and fibroblast migration and proliferation [30]. However, the present study contributes further by specifying more precise threshold values, whereby patients with a pH < 6.7 at the wound bed and < 6.8 at the wound edge exhibited higher healing rates than those with pH values above these thresholds. Establishing these specific cut‐off points offers essential clinical implications, as these may serve as objective indicators for guiding more accurate and targeted wound care decisions.

Collectively, these findings indicate that wound pH modulates healing through distinct, phase‐specific biochemical mechanisms, thereby providing a biological explanation for the apparent paradox of differing pH requirements during the inflammatory and proliferative phases of wound healing and underscoring the importance of interpreting wound pH in a phase‐ and site‐specific manner rather than as a single universal target.

Factors associated with phase transition in this study were identified by a higher pH at the wound edge, particularly in cases with inflammation, absence of systemic infection, and absence of white, yellow, and/or grey necrotic tissue. Although infection is generally associated with elevated wound pH, the present findings indicate that the presence of systemic infection and necrotic tissue was correlated with a lower pH at the wound edge. This observation is consistent with previous reports that have shown that necrotic tissue, pus, and fibrin slough are commonly associated with more acidic conditions, with wound pH values averaging 6.1 ± 0.6 [32]. A low pH in infected wounds may indicate the coexistence of extensive necrosis, and following debridement in which necrotic tissue is removed, the pH levels have been shown to significantly increase [32]. Supporting this, wounds clinically diagnosed with infection but without necrotic tissue have been reported to have a higher mean pH of 7.2, as compared to 6.5 in the non‐infected wounds [34]. In the present study, most infected wounds were accompanied by necrotic tissue, which likely contributed to the observed reduction in wound edge pH.

Albumin functions as a pH buffer by stabilizing wound pH through ion exchange, maintaining an optimal microenvironment for enzymatic activity and tissue regeneration [35]. In contrast, maceration from excessive exudate reflects an imbalanced, alkaline wound environment caused by protein‐ and ammonia‐rich fluids, hindering the shift to an acidic state. This condition promotes microbial growth and delays healing due to excess moisture and tissue instability [36, 37].

Hydrofiber dressings help lower wound pH during the proliferative phase by maintaining moisture balance and providing antimicrobial effects through silver ion release. Although prior studies emphasized their roles in infection control and accelerated healing [38, 39, 40, 41], the present findings indicate an additional function in optimizing pH conditions for tissue regeneration. DACC‐coated dressings lowered wound edge pH by reducing microbial load through hydrophobic binding, thereby stabilizing the wound and supporting an acidic environment [42, 43, 44]. Similarly, calcium alginate reduced pH by absorbing exudate, maintaining moisture balance, and promoting fibroblast activity for tissue regeneration [45, 46, 47, 48]. In contrast, stingless bee honey increased wound bed pH during the proliferative phase, suggesting cautious use in wounds with unstable microenvironments despite its antimicrobial benefits [49, 50, 51].

In addition to conventional clinical factors, emerging evidence from regenerative medicine and advanced wound biomaterials highlights the importance of optimizing the local wound microenvironment in supporting tissue repair. Studies on nanocomposite‐based wound materials have demonstrated that advanced dressings can modulate inflammatory responses and promote regenerative processes through microenvironmental regulation [52, 53]. Similarly, regenerative approaches using mesenchymal stem cells have shown that paracrine signalling can influence inflammation control, oxidative stress regulation, angiogenesis, and tissue regeneration, underscoring the sensitivity of cellular responses to local physicochemical conditions [54]. Collectively, these findings support the clinical relevance of optimizing the local wound microenvironment in influencing inflammatory and regenerative processes that are critical for wound healing.

Cultural background influences healthcare‐seeking behaviors, preferences for topical or traditional remedies, and dietary choices, all of which may affect wound characteristics and the local wound microenvironment, including pH [55]. Dietary habits and nutritional status are closely associated with skin integrity and tissue repair, and deficiencies—often shaped by socio‐economic factors—may compromise wound healing, particularly in vulnerable populations [56]. In addition, wound treatment practices, including the selection of dressings, topical agents, and individualized nutritional support, can further modulate the biochemical milieu of the wound and influence pH regulation, thereby affecting healing outcomes [23, 25, 32, 35]. Collectively, these cultural, dietary, and treatment‐related factors highlight the need for caution when interpreting and generalizing wound pH findings across different clinical and population settings.

Based on the results of the present study, the following can be suggested with regard to the management of DFU. If the DMIST inflammation/infection score is 1 or higher and the wound edge pH is 7.7 or higher, there is a high possibility of transitioning to the proliferative phase within 3 days. If the wound edge pH is less than 7.6, it is necessary to control the systemic infection and remove necrotic tissue. If the wound bed and wound edge pH are less than 6.7 and 6.8, respectively, there is a high possibility of healing within 3 days. In particular, if the DMIST inflammation/infection score is 0, the wound bed tissue type score is 1 or higher, and the wound bed pH is 6.7 or higher, serum albumin control, wound maceration control, use of hydrofiber dressing, and discontinuation of stingless bee honey should be considered. If the wound edge pH is 6.8 or higher, serum albumin control, wound maceration control, hydrofiber dressing, DACC‐coated dressing, and calcium alginate should be considered.

There are several limitations that should be acknowledged for this study. First, this work was conducted in wound clinic, and the topical treatments used were limited to those that have been approved in this wound clinic. Furthermore, the relationship between some topical treatments and wound pH has already been reported, and the results of the present study do not refute any of the previous findings. Second, the underlying reason for the high proportion of patients classified as censored in this study remains unclear. It is plausible that their clinical status could have deteriorated, thereby necessitating referral to hospitals with more comprehensive facilities, which limited the data collection to their last recorded visit and prevented observation across the full study period. Additionally, this study was conducted on wounds undergoing treatment at a wound clinic, and it is believed that the wound pH could have been unexpectedly controlled. The distribution of wound pH in this study suggests that this might be the case. In addition, there may be an optimal range for wound pH, and not just a cut‐off point. However, this study did not evaluate this possibility. In other words, wound pH that is too high during the inflammatory phase does not necessarily affect the proliferative phase, and thus, attention must be paid to the optimal range of the wound pH. As this was an observational study, the observed associations between wound pH dynamics, topical treatments, and healing outcomes should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than causal, and prospective interventional trials are needed to confirm the clinical utility of pH‐guided wound management.

In future research, it will be necessary to establish a protocol to prevent delayed wound healing through intervention for the factors identified in this study. This will help to ensure that the wound pH is maintained at an optimal level, and then make it possible to confirm the effectiveness of this protocol in a randomized controlled trial setting. These wound pH cut‐off values may support wound monitoring and risk assessment, but interventional studies are needed to validate their role in improving clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The present study showed that there was a significant association between the wound pH and healing and the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase within 3 days. Cut‐off points for the wound bed pH of < 6.7 and the wound edge pH of < 6.8 were associated with healing, while a wound edge pH of > 7.7 was associated with the transition from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase.

In the inflammatory phase, factors associated with a higher wound edge pH included the presence of inflammation, absence of systemic infection, and absence of white, yellow, and/or grey necrotic tissue. In the proliferative phase, factors associated with a lower wound bed pH included higher serum albumin levels, absence of maceration, non‐use of stingless bee honey, and the application of hydrofiber dressings. Likewise, a lower wound edge pH in this phase was associated with higher albumin levels, the absence of maceration, and the use of hydrofiber dressings, DACC‐coated dressings, and calcium alginate. Use of the wound pH cut‐off values clarified in this study to intervene in these factors at each stage may help to prevent delayed healing of DFUs.
