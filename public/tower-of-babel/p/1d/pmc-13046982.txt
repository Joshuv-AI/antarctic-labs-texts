Real-World Burden of Generalized Pustular Psoriasis in a French Observational Study: Prevalence, Incidence, Healthcare Resource Utilization, Comorbidities, Treatment Use, and Mortality
Authors: Manuelle Viguier, Denis Jullien, Laurent Misery, Christian Kromer, Maja Mockenhaupt, Bhargav Lakshminarasimhan, Marie Najean, Nichiren Pillai, Shah Alam Khan, Alice B Gottlieb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeneralized pustular psoriasis (GPP) is rare, chronic, and associated with life-threatening complications. We investigated the burden of GPP in France.MethodsUsing data from 2010 to 2021 in the Système National des Données de Santé database, healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), costs, comorbidities, mortality, and treatments were compared among GPP (N = 4351), plaque psoriasis (N = 12,945), and general population (N = 12,981) cohorts, matched for sex, age, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and region. GPP prevalence and incidence were also investigated.ResultsAnnually, there were 0.5–0.8 new GPP cases per 100,000 people. Across the cohorts, 54.5–54.7% of people were male, with mean age 58.7–59.5 years and mean CCI score 1.98–2.06. The GPP cohort incurred significantly greater HCRU and costs versus the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts, including greater proportions of patients receiving emergency care (78% vs 63% and 55%) and intensive care (28% vs 17% and 14%), longer hospitalizations (mean 38.5 vs 26.2 and 22.4 days per patient), and higher medication costs (€4360 vs €1991 and €1543 per patient-year), respectively. Despite similar CCI scores, GPP was associated with more cardiometabolic and psychological comorbidities versus the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts, e.g., hypertension (37% vs 21% and 20%), obesity (21% vs 9% and 6%), depression (13% vs 4% and 4%), alcohol abuse (16% vs 3% and 3%), and sleep disorders (8% vs 4% and 3%), respectively. Treatments in the GPP cohort were those used for plaque psoriasis, including topical steroids (77%), systemic steroids (50%), and biologics (23%). Twelve-month survival was 86.9% (GPP), 97.5% (plaque psoriasis), and 90.0% (the general population).ConclusionHCRU, costs, and comorbidities with GPP were often double those for comparator cohorts, and mortality was higher. These findings highlight the need to use GPP-targeted treatments that improve patient outcomes and may reduce the burden on healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01691-4.Keywords: Comorbidity, Generalized pustular psoriasis, Healthcare resource utilization, Incidence, Mortality, Plaque psoriasis, Prevalence, Treatment use, France

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) is associated with substantially higher healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) than plaque psoriasis in the USA, Japan, the UK, and Sweden, although published data on the burden of GPP are lacking for France.In this French real-world study, we investigated HCRU and costs, comorbidities, mortality, and treatment use between 2012 and 2020 in matched cohorts of patients with GPP (N = 4351) versus plaque psoriasis (N = 12,945) and the general population (N = 12,981). What was learned from the study? The GPP cohort incurred significantly greater HCRU and costs, which were often double those in both comparator cohorts, including longer hospitalizations, more common emergency and intensive care, and higher medication costs.GPP was associated with substantially more comorbidities, including cardiometabolic and psychological conditions, and significantly greater mortality than in both comparator cohorts.Given that the study predates the recent availability of targeted therapy for GPP, treatments in the GPP cohort were mainly those used for plaque psoriasis, despite fundamental differences in pathophysiology and lack of evidence for efficacy in GPP. Therefore, the observed treatment patterns should not be interpreted as currently optimal or appropriate GPP management strategies.

Introduction

Generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) is a rare, serious, and chronic neutrophilic inflammatory skin disease, which follows a heterogeneous and unpredictable clinical course, potentially resulting in life-threatening complications such as sepsis [1–4]. Most people with GPP fear flares, which are painful and debilitating, and commonly triggered by emotional stress, steroid withdrawal, or infection [1, 2, 4–7]. Patients may experience multiple GPP flares per year, often encompassing systemic symptoms including fever and fatigue [1, 2, 4–8], and typically lasting 1–5 weeks [5, 6].

Recent publications from the USA, Japan, the UK, Germany, and Sweden show that GPP is associated with substantially higher healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and/or greater numbers of comorbidities compared with cohorts of patients with plaque psoriasis or without a diagnosis of psoriasis [9–13]. In France, cardiometabolic comorbidities were reported in patients with GPP in an uncontrolled chart review, and a national database study (2012–2015) reported greater occurrence of common cardiometabolic comorbidities versus patients with other forms of severe psoriasis (5% with plaque psoriasis) [14, 15]. A small patient survey in France has also suggested substantial impact on quality of life and the occurrence of fatigue, stress, skin pain, and joint pain [16].

However, a need remains for recent, comprehensive studies on the burden of GPP in France, particularly in terms of HCRU, costs, treatment use, psychological comorbidities, and mortality, compared with plaque psoriasis and the general population. Here, our aim was to report these outcomes and to provide additional information on GPP prevalence, incidence, and cardiometabolic comorbidities using the French national health insurance database, Système National des Données de Santé (SNDS), covering the years 2010 to 2021. The study population included thousands of patients diagnosed with GPP, which is larger than in several previous studies that generally included hundreds of patients with GPP per study [9–16].

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This was an observational, non-interventional study, conducted with secondary data obtained from the SNDS national health insurance database, which covers almost the entire population of France [15, 17].

GPP incidence was calculated per year based on data extracted across the study period, between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2021. Cumulative prevalence of GPP cases was also calculated across the study period, using the number of all alive patients with GPP as the numerator and the French population size as the denominator.

All other outcomes were compared in three cohorts—GPP versus plaque psoriasis and the general population—which were matched for sex, age, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and region of residence. Patient data were collected until the end of the study period (December 31, 2021) or death (if earlier).

For the cohorts, the enrollment period was between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2020 (Fig. S1), with the enrollment date defined as the earliest date with the following: GPP cohort: ≥ 1 hospitalization or long-term disease with the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision (ICD-10) code L40.1; plaque psoriasis cohort: ≥ 1 hospitalization or long-term disease with ICD-10 code L40.0 or ≥ 2 prescriptions for topical vitamin D derivatives (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification code D05AX) in a 2-year period; general population cohort: any other medical diagnosis. In these inclusion criteria, ‘hospitalization’ included home-based care (i.e., ‘hospitalisation à domicile’) and inpatient HCRU.

Outcomes

The co-primary outcomes, GPP prevalence and incidence, were assessed between 2010 and 2021. Other co-primary outcomes (HCRU and associated costs, comorbidities, treatment use) were assessed in the matched cohorts between 2010 and 2021. Total direct costs were computed as the sum of the following costs: outpatient visits to physicians, outpatient medical imaging, hospitalizations in short-stay institutions, and medication. Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes. A secondary outcome, all-cause mortality rate, was also assessed in the matched cohorts.

Ethical Approval

Given the use of anonymized patient data from SNDS, no individual informed consent and no institutional review boards were required for this study. Permission to access the database was granted for this project.

Statistical Analysis

Propensity score (PS) matching was performed as follows. Step 1: PS were calculated for each individual in each cohort using a logistic regression model. The logistic model included covariates with a significance level ≤ 10%. Step 2: Cases and controls were matched 1:3 by PS, with calipers of 0.2 of standard deviation of the PS, using a greedy matching algorithm. Step 3: The balance was checked among the three cohorts before and after PS matching and assessed using the standardized difference method. An absolute standardized difference of 0 for a covariate indicated no between-group imbalance for that covariate, and values < 0.1 indicated an inconsequential imbalance.

Data were analyzed descriptively and using Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney, Chi-squared, and Fisher’s exact tests. Due to the non-normal (skewed) distribution of cost variables and the potential inflated zero values, generalized linear models were applied to patients with non-zero cost data. All analyses were performed using SAS®, version 9.4.

Results

Patient Characteristics in the Matched Cohorts

The GPP, plaque psoriasis, and general population cohorts included 4351, 12,945, and 12,981 people, respectively. The GPP cohort was well matched with the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts in terms of sex, age, CCI, and region of residence (Table S1). Across the cohorts, 54.5–54.7% of people were male, with a mean age of 58.7–59.5 years and a mean CCI score of 1.98–2.06. In the GPP, plaque psoriasis, and general population cohorts, respectively, mean (standard deviation) follow-up duration was 4.4 (2.9), 5.2 (2.7), and 4.3 (2.8) years.

GPP Incidence and Cumulative Prevalence

GPP was rare, with an annual incidence of 0.5–0.8 cases per 100,000 people (370–522 new cases per year) between 2010 and 2021. The cumulative prevalence of GPP was 6.4 cases per 100,000 people (4329 patients were diagnosed with GPP) between 2010 and 2021 (Fig. 1).

HCRU and Associated Costs

Inpatient HCRU was significantly greater in the GPP cohorts than in the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts. This included higher proportions of patients receiving emergency room care (78% vs 63% and 55%) and approximately twofold more frequent use of intensive care (28% vs 17% and 14%), aftercare/rehabilitation (32% vs 15% and 13%), and psychiatry care (5% vs 2% and 1%), respectively (Fig. 2a). Mean (standard deviation) duration of hospital stays was 38.5 (68.2) vs 26.2 (96.9) and 22.4 (71.4) days per patient in the GPP, plaque psoriasis, and general population cohorts, respectively (Fig. 2b). Dermatology outpatient care was also almost two-fold more frequent in the GPP versus the general population cohort (Fig. 3).

Comorbidities

In the GPP cohort, 69% of patients had comorbid plaque psoriasis. Notably, although mean CCI score was similar per matched cohort, differences in the types of comorbidities were observed. Cardiometabolic disorders were more common in patients with GPP than in the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts, including greater proportions of patients with hypertension (37% vs 21% and 20%), obesity (21% vs 9% and 6%), and type 2 diabetes (21% vs 15% and 12%), respectively (Fig. 5). Psychological comorbidities were also more common in patients with GPP versus the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts, including greater percentages of patients with anxiety (8% vs 3% and 3%), depression (13% vs 4% and 4%), alcohol abuse (16% vs 3% and 3%), and sleep disorders (8% vs 4% and 3%), respectively (Fig. 6).

Treatments

Many patients with GPP received topical steroids (77%), systemic steroids (50%), and biologics (23%); none of these treatments were approved for GPP at the time of the study (Fig. 7). Biologics (23% vs 2% and 0.3%), methotrexate (19% vs 4% and 0.9%), and acitretin (20% vs 5% and 0.02%) were used more frequently in the GPP cohort than in either the plaque psoriasis or the general population cohort, respectively (Fig. 7). In the GPP cohort, the most common biologics used were ustekinumab (used by 10% of patients), adalimumab (8%), secukinumab (7%), etanercept (5%), guselkumab (4%), and ixekizumab (3%) (Table S2). Other biologics (brodalumab, certolizumab pegol, tildrakizumab, and infliximab) were each used by < 1.4% of patients in the GPP cohort.

Mortality

The 12-month survival rate was 86.9% for the GPP cohort, 97.5% for the plaque psoriasis cohort, and 90.0% for the general population cohort (p < 0.0001 for GPP vs both comparator cohorts; Fig. 8).

Discussion

In this nationwide SNDS health insurance database study, GPP was associated with significantly greater HCRU, associated costs, treatment use, comorbidities, and mortality than plaque psoriasis and for the general population in France. Patients with GPP experienced significantly more and longer hospitalizations, including double the proportions of patients using intensive and psychiatry care and more than double the medication costs. These findings are compatible with reports of greater HCRU and costs, comorbidity, and mortality for GPP than for plaque psoriasis in other countries [9–13, 18, 19].

Annual incidence of GPP in France remained stable at 0.5–0.8 cases per 100,000 people (370–522 new cases per year) between 2010 and 2021 in France. During the 11-year study period, 64 new cases of GPP were recorded per million people (4329 patients between 2010 and 2021). These estimates are compatible with other recent estimates of GPP incidence [12, 19, 20], including 0.28–0.82 and 0.25 per 100,000 in Sweden and England, respectively, and are within the wide prevalence range of 1.76–124 cases per million in previous studies [21]. This wide range may be due to various factors, including misdiagnosis [4] and geographic differences in GPP definitions and diagnostic criteria that the International Psoriasis Council recently attempted to standardize [22]. Notably, our cumulative prevalence estimate of 64 cases per million between 2010 and 2021 is consistent with previously reported estimates in France (45.2 cases per million, based on data from 2010 to 2017), England (30 cases per million, based on data from 2008 to 2019), and Germany (50–100 cases per million, based on data from 2016 to 2020) [12, 15, 23]. This rarity can hamper clinical investigations. In a recent phase 2 trial of imsidolimab, an interleukin (IL)-36 receptor monoclonal antibody (mAb), only eight patients with GPP were enrolled [24], although two phase 2 studies of another IL-36 receptor mAb, spesolimab, did successfully enroll 53 and 123 patients [25, 26].

Our analysis of treatment use was conducted before the 2022 approval of spesolimab, a targeted therapy for GPP, in France. Therefore, the observed treatment patterns reflect the pre-targeted therapy era and should not be interpreted as currently optimal or appropriate GPP management strategies. Patients with GPP mainly received treatments used for plaque psoriasis, despite fundamental differences in the clinical presentation and pathophysiology of the two conditions [3, 19, 27] and unsatisfactory outcomes [4–6, 8, 28]. Importantly, common treatments included steroids, which have a reported risk of triggering flares upon withdrawal [4–6], and biologics that were not approved for GPP because of limited evidence of efficacy. Although systemic steroids were used by similar proportions of patients in the GPP versus plaque psoriasis cohorts, other systemic treatments were more commonly used in the GPP cohort, including biologics (23% vs 2%), methotrexate (19% vs 4%), and acitretin (20% vs 5%). Previous studies suggested that disease severity may be inferred by the proportion of patients receiving systemic treatments [9, 11]; thus, our data may indicate that disease severity was milder in the plaque psoriasis cohort than in the GPP cohort. Comorbidities may also influence treatment (69% of patients in the GPP cohort had comorbid plaque psoriasis). A recently published US database study suggested that 21.5% of patients in a GPP-only cohort were treated with biologics, rising to 39.6% in patients with GPP and comorbid plaque psoriasis [29].

GPP was associated with substantially more cardiometabolic comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, obesity, type 2 diabetes) and psychological comorbidities (e.g., depression, alcohol abuse, sleep disorders) than the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts. We speculate that the detrimental psychological impact of GPP may be related to various factors, including the fear and uncertainty about flares, the lack of effective GPP-targeted treatments at the time of the study, and patients feeling isolated because of the rarity of the disease. An online US survey, conducted in 2020, reported that 71% of 66 patients with GPP were afraid of flares and 59% felt hopeless and depressed [4]. In France, another recent survey found that 83% of 41 patients with GPP were suffering from moderate-to-severe stress [16].

In the current French study, we demonstrated that patients in the GPP cohort were at greater risk of mortality (12-month survival was 86.9%) than people in the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts (97.5% and 90.0%, respectively). This reaffirms the burden beyond the physical symptoms of GPP and comorbidities. This finding is consistent with data from other countries. A recent US database study reported a 1.5 times higher mortality risk for patients with GPP versus plaque psoriasis [30]. A cohort study in England reported higher mortality with GPP than for both the plaque psoriasis and palmoplantar pustulosis cohorts in the presence of comorbidities [12].

We speculate that in our study the higher 12-month survival rate for patients with plaque psoriasis (97.5%) than for the general population (90.0%) may be related to the inclusion criterion that patients in the general population cohort were required to have “any other medical diagnosis.” Therefore, the general population cohort might have a greater proportion of patients with life-threatening diseases than the plaque psoriasis cohort and may not be representative of the background population in France.

Given the use of secondary data from a claims database to investigate a rare disease, this real-world study has limitations. First, databases may contain inaccurate data, including diagnostic miscoding; the rarity of GPP may result in misdiagnoses and underreporting. A US survey highlighted that most patients with GPP waited for months (36%) or years (38%) before receiving a GPP diagnosis, and a common reason for the delay was misdiagnosis (59%) [4]. However, it is hoped that errors have been minimized by the widespread use of SNDS codes across France [15]. Second, some mutations associated with GPP may be specific to particular ethnic populations and, as collection of information on ethnicity is prohibited in France, no analyses by ethnic background were possible [15, 21, 31]. Third, as in previous database studies of GPP [9–13], no analyses were conducted according to disease severity. Disease severity was likely milder in the plaque psoriasis cohort than in the GPP cohort, which may have affected the findings. A study in the USA, using electronic health records, reported greater HCRU in patients with more severe GPP [32]. This could be an area for further research in France and other countries. Fourth, the GPP, plaque psoriasis, and general population cohorts were matched for demographics and CCI scores (calculated from an extensive range of ICD-10 codes) in order to isolate GPP as the main variable, although potentially with residual confounding on outcomes, such as HCRU. Despite the matching, the GPP cohort was still associated with higher levels of specific cardiometabolic and psychological comorbidities than the plaque psoriasis and general population cohorts. Finally, no analyses were conducted according to the occurrence of GPP flares. However, as expected, patients who experienced GPP flares incurred greater HCRU and associated costs than patients without flares in the USA [32].

Our study also has notable strengths. GPP burden was investigated using the SNDS claims database, which covers almost the entire French population. In addition, the number of patients included in the GPP cohort (N = 4351) is substantially larger than in several previous investigations of GPP-associated HCRU in other countries (9–13).

Conclusion

Patients with GPP in France encountered a significantly higher burden of disease versus patients with plaque psoriasis and the general population. HCRU, costs, and comorbidities associated with GPP were often twice as high as in both comparator cohorts. Mortality was also higher, and off-label treatments were used that lack evidence for efficacy in GPP. These findings highlight the need to use GPP-targeted treatments that improve patient outcomes and may reduce the burden on healthcare systems.
