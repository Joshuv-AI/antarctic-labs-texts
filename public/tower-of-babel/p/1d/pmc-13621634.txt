MINERVA: a public XAI-powered platform advancing multi-target discovery in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Nicola Gambacorta, Daniela Trisciuzzi, Fabrizio Mastrolorito, Francesco Leonetti, Cosimo D Altomare, Modesto de Candia, Marco Catto, Nicola Amoroso, Fulvio Ciriaco, Orazio Nicolotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder involving a complex interplay of interconnected pharmacological targets, a feature that limits the success of traditional single-target medicinal chemistry approaches. Here, we present MINERVA (Multi-target Interactive Network for Explainable Research and Visualization in Alzheimer’s disease), a public web-based platform designed to support multi-target drug discovery assisted by eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) strategies. MINERVA integrates large-scale disease-focused high-quality data comprising as many as 70,960 small molecules annotated across 33 AD relevant targets, taken from ChEMBL and CADRO databases. To capture different levels of pharmacological relevance, four distinct pharmacological thresholds (i.e., 10 μM, 1 μM, 100 nM, and 10 nM) were set to enable the parallel exploration of weak to high-affinity ligand spaces. Independent Balanced Random Forest (BRF) classifiers were trained for each AD target threshold combination using an extended core-substituent fingerprint, which ensures robustness against class imbalance and chemical heterogeneity. MINERVA incorporates a probability binning based domain of applicability (DoA) to quantify prediction reliability and a SHAP-based explainability framework to fairly map fragment-level contributions directly onto chemical structures. MINERVA is freely accessible at https://prometheus.farmacia.uniba.it/minerva/. Scientific contribution Herein we introduce MINERVA, the first freely accessible platform that combines large-scale AD-related data curation, multi-target prediction, and multi-threshold bioactivity modeling within a fully explainable and user-friendly environment. By enabling transparent, ligand-based exploration of chemical space across multiple AD pathways, MINERVA provides a unique and practical resource for accelerating multi-target drug discovery in the neurodegenerative research area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13321-026-01253-y.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and a pressing global health concern, affecting more than 55 million people worldwide. By 2050, this number is expected to rise to 130 million, with significant socioeconomic and medical burdens on individuals and healthcare systems [1, 2]. AD manifests clinically through progressive memory loss, cognitive decline, language impairment, and behavioral disturbances [3]. The pathology is characterized by a complex interplay of neurodegenerative mechanisms, including extracellular accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques, intracellular aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein forming neurofibrillary tangles, mitochondrial dysfunction, cholinergic deficits, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress [3–6]. Over the last two decades, substantial efforts have been devoted to unraveling the molecular bases of AD [7, 8]. Nonetheless, therapeutic development has been met with repeated clinical failures, with more than 90% of drug candidate compounds failing during phase II and III clinical trials. Among the primary reasons for this attrition there are the limited understanding of disease heterogeneity, poor target validation, and insufficient predictability of preclinical models [9, 10]. These shortcomings have fueled interest in developing predictive approaches that can properly guide early stage drug discovery by integrating bioinformatics, cheminformatics, and systems biology data [11–14]. Recent advances based on artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have facilitated the derivation of highly reliable predictive models for pharmacological profiling, target engagement, toxicity prediction, and multi-target design [15–22]. While such approaches have been widely applied in oncology [23], cardiology [24], and infectious diseases [25, 26], their application to neurodegenerative disorders remains relatively underexplored. In particular, existing predictive strategies often focus on single or a limited number of targets (e.g., AChE, BACE1) [27–30], thereby overlooking the multifactorial and polypharmacological nature of AD, which involves a wide network of tightly interconnected biological pathways and molecular targets. To the best of our knowledge AD involves a broad spectrum of functional classes, contributing to the onset and progression of the disorder: these span cholinergic modulation, amyloidogenic enzymes, tau-associated regulators, neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress mediators, neurotransmitter transporters and receptors, hormonal and nuclear signaling pathways, growth factor and stem cell receptors, intracellular kinases, matrix remodeling enzymes, and proteins involved in lipid and glucose metabolism [31].

In the present study, we identified 33 AD-related targets based on their functional classifications and the availability of high-quality data. Experimental data for these targets were collected from two main sources: (i) the ChEMBL database [32], one of the most comprehensive repositories of chemical and pharmacological information on about three million of bioactive compounds, by employing a Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) ID driven retrieval strategy to systematically extract AD associated bioactivity data; and (ii) the Common Alzheimer's and Related Dementias Research Ontology (CADRO) database [33], a specialized resource for neurodegeneration related targets. Leveraging this comprehensive body of solid pharmacological data, we introduce MINERVA (Multi-target Interactive Network for Explainable Research and Visualization in Alzheimer's Disease), a free web-based predictive technological platform to drive the multi-target design of AD therapeutic small molecules by predicting a comprehensive interaction profile. MINERVA employs independent Balanced Random Forest classifiers trained on the entire pool of AD targets defined on the basis of their functional categories. MINERVA employs the extended core substituent fingerprint (CSFP) [34], which is optimized for medicinal chemistry purposes and provides a robust representation of fragment-level features. Noteworthy, to improve the understanding and transparency of the results, MINERVA is also empowered with an eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) framework, based on SHAP [35] (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to give users the chance of visualizing the bioactivity contribution of single molecular fragments across the 33 explored AD-related targets. This level of interpretability is essential for rational drug design, as it allows medicinal chemists to pinpoint chemotypes and pharmacophores that may drive AD target engagement. To further enhance the reliability of predictions, MINERVA incorporates an innovative domain of applicability (DoA) strategy based on probability binning. By integrating disease-specific target curation, robust ensemble modeling, fragment-based explainability, and a stringent DoA, MINERVA fills a significant gap in the neurodegenerative drug discovery landscape by allowing hypothesis-driven exploration of chemical space for multi-target AD drug candidates. It is freely accessible via an intuitive web interface available at https://prometheus.farmacia.uniba.it/minerva/, thereby allowing access to cutting-edge predictive tools for researchers in academia and industry.

Methods

Data gathering

MINERVA comprises a large volume of high-quality experimental data on 70,960 small molecules with associated bioactivity values across 33 well-established AD-related targets. Specifically, ChEMBL database was employed to retrieve data, while CADRO was used exclusively as a curated source for AD target identification. AD targets have been selected on the basis of their documented involvement in AD pathology. Nevertheless, some among them (e.g., sigma 1 and sigma 2 receptors) [36, 37] have not been enrolled in MINERVA due to the paucity of quantitative data currently available. With respect to ChEMBL, a MeSH ID driven approach was employed to associate specific AD ontologies with protein targets. The MeSH IDs are unique identifiers corresponding to standardized biomedical terms within the MeSH vocabulary maintained by the U.S. National Library of Medicine (https://www.nlm.nih.gov/mesh) and are extremely effective in data mining by avoiding ambiguity while ensuring consistency. The MeSH ID for AD is D000544. Within ChEMBL, the corresponding AD-related target identifiers (the so called TIDs) were thus selected by first intersecting “drug mechanism” and “drug indication” tables and then filtering the entries labeled with D000544 as MeSH ID (details are provided at https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/chembl/ChEMBLdb/releases/chembl_35/schema_documentation.html). Overall, this explorative analysis yielded 29 AD-related targets from ChEMBL while other four were taken from CADRO, as detailed in Table 1. [38–41]. To ensure homogeneous and consistent data retrieval, ChEMBL was used as the sole source for mining chemical and pharmacological information related to the 33 AD associated targets, as shown in Fig. 1.

MINERVA data gathering scheme

For the sake of clarity, we grouped all the retrieved AD-related targets within their specific functional categories, as reported in Table 1.

Experimental IC₅₀ values were generally used to pharmacologically flag AD-related targeting compounds. The only exception was PPARγ, for which EC₅₀ values were employed, as only agonists are relevant to AD-related therapeutic mechanisms. Bioactivity data were encoded as binary variables using four thresholds, thereby enabling users to explore varying and more stringent classification levels. More specifically, four bioactivity cutoffs were set: 10 μM, 1 μM, 100 nM, and 10 nM. For each AD-related target, compounds with bioactivity data equal to or below each given threshold were thus labeled as active, the counterpart as inactive. However, the number of classification levels also depends on the number of compounds available for each AD-related target. In this respect, a number of at least 100 active and inactive compounds was required at each bioactivity threshold to ensure statistical validity. Consequently, models were successfully derived for all bioactivity cutoffs for 20 of the 33 AD-related targets. For those remaining, complete model derivation across all cutoffs was not carried out because of limited data availability. For the sake of completeness, additional information is reported in Table S1 of the Supporting Information.

The compounds reported for the AD-related targets were represented by using their canonical SMILES, target names, and associated bioactivity data. To ensure data consistency and standardization, only entries explicitly reported with IC₅₀ (or EC₅₀ in the case of PPARγ) as the standard_type were retained. Our multiple classification strategy enabled exploration of a broader range of bioactivity definitions across 33 AD-related targets, thereby providing a more robust foundation for training and validating structure–activity relationship (SAR) models. In total, 70,960 compounds were collected across 33 AD-related targets. Data curation

To minimize discrepancies during the data collection from ChEMBL, a set of filtering criteria was employed as described elsewhere [15, 72]: (i) target filter: “target_type: Single protein”; (ii) ligand filter: “molecule_type: Small_molecule”; “prodrug: not 1”; (iii) activity record filters: “confidence_score: > 5”; “standard_relation: = ”; “standard_type: IC50|EC50”; “standard_units: nM”; (iv) when multiple activity experimental values were available for the same compound, values in the upper and lower quintiles were discarded to mitigate the influence of outliers when estimating the average.

Molecular representation

We employed an extended version of the CSFP, a fragment-based molecular representation specifically tailored for medicinal chemistry applications, for representing AD-related targeting compounds. The original CSFP framework was developed through the systematic decomposition of over 67,000 biologically active compounds from ChEMBL, yielding a comprehensive library of molecular cores and substituents. From this collection, the most frequent 250 single-ring fragments, 250 fused-ring fragments, and 500 common substituents were selected based on their prevalence in known bioactive scaffolds, resulting in a 1000-bit fingerprint encoding system. For the present investigation, the fingerprint dictionary was further expanded by incorporating 13 additional fragments to better reflect the structural diversity present in AD-related chemical space. These supplemental bits were specifically designed to encode functional groups and ring systems frequently encountered in drug-like molecules but underrepresented in the original CSFP. The additions include several heterocyclic and saturated ring motifs (e.g., tetrahydropyridine, hexahydropyrimidine, 2H-pyrrole, aziridine), conjugated systems such as 1,4-cyclohexadiene, and macrocyclic or large-ring features with heteroatoms (e.g., > 8-membered rings containing oxygen or nitrogen). Additional descriptors were also added for key functional moieties like carbonyls and oxepane, as well as for atoms embedded within medium- to large-sized heterocycles (e.g., sulfur, nitrogen, or oxygen in 7- to 8-membered rings) [24]. This fingerprint enhancement allowed for a more representative and chemically diverse encoding, ultimately improving the ability of ML models to discern SAR across many AD-related targets.

Features reduction

To mitigate informational redundancy and improve computational efficiency, a two-step feature selection strategy was carried out. Initially, pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between all feature pairs. For each pair with a correlation coefficient > 0.9, one of the two features was removed to reduce multicollinearity, thereby limiting noise in the predictive models and bias in downstream interpretability analyses. In the subsequent step, Balanced Random Forest (BRF) based feature importance was computed on the remaining variables, retaining only those with an importance score exceeding 0.001. This approach allowed us to focus on features with substantial predictive values, effectively reducing dimensionality without compromising model performance. Importantly, the removal of redundant features also plays a critical role in the context of model explainability: high feature correlation can alter SHAP value distributions, leading to misleading attributions. By implementing this stringent reduction pipeline, we enhanced both the efficiency and the interpretive clarity of our models, facilitating more reliable chemical insight in the multi-target drug discovery framework for AD. Model building

The BRF algorithm was employed to develop all the predictive models. This ensemble method extends the traditional Random Forest by performing random undersampling of the majority class within each bootstrap iteration, thereby mitigating class imbalance and improving predictive performance for minority classes. To assess model performance, ten independent random splits were performed, each allocating 20% of the experimental data to external test sets. The remaining 80% of the data served as the training set and was subjected to fivefold stratified cross-validation (CV), maintaining the ration of actives and inactive unchanged in each fold. This was iterated ten times to ensure statistical robustness. The overall summary concerning the training and derivation of the models is detailed in Fig. 3.

Domain of applicability

The reliability prediction of the in silico strategies represents a critical aspect of drug discovery, especially when employing multiple level classifiers across heterogeneous biological mechanisms as those involved in AD. To evaluate the predictive reliability, we analyzed the distribution of correctly and incorrectly classified samples across probability bins ranging from 0 to 1, with a bin width of 0.1. For each AD-related target, the ratio between correct and total predictions was computed within each bin as an estimate of predictive reliability. Because the different AD datasets were very often highly imbalanced, a balancing correction was applied to adjust the relative contribution of low- and high-probability bins. The balancing coefficients c0 and c1, were equal to the total number of true active and true inactive compounds, respectively, across the entire bin range.

This analysis shows that model confidence is highest at the boundaries of the predicted score range. Predictions associated with high scores (e.g., > 0.9) consistently exhibit high reliability and are therefore considered within the DoA, whereas predictions with scores close to 0.5 display near-random reliability and should be regarded as outside the DoA and interpreted with caution. This approach allows us to evaluate the prediction accuracy within each bin using CV. The resulting DoA histograms provide users with an intuitive, probabilistically anchored assessment of confidence, by improving traditional distance-to-model or leverage-based DoA techniques.

Model explanation

In this study, model interpretability was addressed through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, which are grounded in cooperative game theory and quantify the individual contribution of each feature to a given prediction. For each of the 33 AD-related targets, explainability analysis was performed using a tree-based SHAP explainer implemented via the SHAP Python package (v0.44.0). This allowed for both global and local interpretation of model behavior across structurally diverse compounds. To facilitate chemical insight, the SHAP derived fragment importance scores were mapped directly onto molecular structures, where each bit of the extended CSFP contributed to the final classification output. These fragments were flagged with color-coded highlights identifying molecular substructures associated with predicted activity. This fragment-level transparency enhances the interpretability of predictions, enabling researchers to identify molecular moieties likely to drive or prevent bioactivity across many AD-related targets.

Results

Data distribution

Following the data curation protocol outlined in the Methods section, a total of 70,960 compounds were collected across the 33 AD-related targets, each fulfilling the criteria for IC₅₀ (or EC50) based classification. The final dataset encompassed many protein targets with sufficient bioactivity data to support robust SAR analyses. For each retained AD-related target, compound distributions were evaluated across the four bioactivity thresholds (10 μM, 1 μM, 100 nM, and 10 nM), thereby providing multiple level classifications. Within each bioactivity threshold, we observed heterogeneous yet informative distributions: some AD-related targets exhibited relatively balanced ratios between active and inactive compounds, whereas others displayed strong imbalances, possibly reflecting the biological relevance or assay coverage. This variability underscores the importance of adopting target- and threshold-specific modeling strategies in conceiving ML workflows.

Model evaluation

Multiple level classifiers based on BRF were successfully trained at each bioactivity threshold considering all the AD-related targets. Performance evaluation across the ten independent data splits demonstrated consistent and reliable predictive behaviors. For each split, the external test sets, comprising 20% of the initial data, provided an unbiased measure of generalization performance, while the 5-fold stratified CV iterated ten times on the remaining 80% offered insights into model stability and robustness. Across all AD-related targets, the models achieved generally fair to high balanced accuracy (with mean values in the range from 0.65 to 0.91 irrespective of the bioactivity cutoffs), with particularly strong performance observed on AD-related targets with more balanced distributions. Sensitivity and specificity values varied (with mean values in the range from 0.64 to 0.91 and from 0.59 to 0.91 respectively, irrespective of the bioactivity cutoffs) depending on class imbalance, yet remained within acceptable ranges, indicating that the models were capable of correctly identifying both active and inactive compounds. Notably, as detailed in Table S3 of the Supporting Information, MCC values ranging from 0.26 to 0.82 further confirmed the model predictive power, especially for those AD-related targets with highly unbalanced datasets. In this respect, we also compared our BRF models against alternative imbalanced-learning strategies, such as SMOTE and cost-sensitive methods [73, 74]. Overall, the ten-fold repetition of both CV and data splitting allowed for a thorough statistical characterization of model performance, mitigating the impact of data partitioning bias. For clarity, the CV and test set MCC values for each AD-related target, computed at each bioactivity threshold, are shown in Table S3 of the Supporting Information. To better challenge the reliability of our models, a scaffold split-based validation was performed at each bioactivity threshold. Specifically, compounds were clustered according to their Bemis-Murcko scaffolds [75], and a group-fivefold cross-validation scheme was applied. This analysis showed a slight deterioration of the average MCC values due to the structural diversity of the scaffolds held out in each validation fold but without compromising the robustness of the predictive power across all models at each cut-off, as shown in Table S5 of the Supporting Information.

Global explainability results across AD-related targets

The CSFP was preferred as fingerprint as it is acknowledged to ensure a straight link between model performance and unequivocal chemical patterns, thereby enhancing model transparency [76, 77]. Consequently, SHAP based interpretability analyses provided a comprehensive overview of the fragment-level determinants driving model predictions across the 33 AD-related targets. For each AD model, global SHAP summary plots were generated with the aim of ranking the CSFP encoded fragments according to their overall contribution to the classification result. As representative examples, we show here a pair of AD-related targets taken from distinct functional categories (Figs. 4, 5), by reporting the global SHAP summary plot corresponding to the most stringent bioactivity threshold. For the remaining AD-related targets, all global SHAP visualizations are freely available for download in the Data section of the MINERVA website.

As far as AChE is concerned, the two most influential features driving the predicted activity class were the 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroacridine and piperidine rings. Notably, the 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroacridine scaffold constitutes the core of tacrine, a well-established AChE inhibitor and the structural core of several highly potent analogues [78–81]. Similarly, the piperidine ring is a moiety of the approved drug donepezil, where it plays a key role in mediating π–π and cation–π interactions with the critical Trp86 residue within anionic subsite of the catalytic site (CAS) [82]. For BACE1, the pyridine ring emerged as the most informative fragment, in line with structural and computational evidence showing that its nitrogen atom often forms hydrogen bond or electrostatic interactions with Trp76 in the S2′ subsite and can also engage in stabilizing contacts with Arg235 [83, 84]. Moreover, extensive fluorine scan studies have demonstrated that strategic fluorination of aromatic rings can affect both binding affinity and ADME properties across many BACE1 inhibitors, highlighting its relevance in the optimization of drug-like profiles [85]. Interestingly, similar patterns of features for AChE and BACE- are consistently observed across all bioactivity cutoffs, further highlighting the importance of these features in distinguishing active compounds.

For PPARγ, two structural features emerged as the most relevant drivers of the active class prediction: the oxazole ring and the methoxy/ethoxy substituted benzene moiety. The oxazole heterocycle is a well-established scaffold in PPARγ ligand design, largely due to its dual capability to act as a hydrogen bond acceptor and to engage in aromatic interactions within the ligand binding pocket. The combination of methoxy and ethoxy groups increases the electron density of the aromatic ring, important for high affinity binding in the predominantly hydrophobic cavity of PPARγ [86, 87].

With regard to HDAC1, the most informative fragment was the long aliphatic chain, which is specifically designed to occupy the narrow hydrophobic slot leading to the catalytic Zn2⁺ ion, a region naturally engaged by the acetylated lysine side chain of histone substrates. In addition, the thienyl group frequently appears in potent HDAC inhibitors because of its ability to accommodate within the so called foot pocket, reinforcing hydrophobic interactions and contributing to optimal positioning of the zinc binding group [88–90]. In line with the previous case study, the recurrence of specific and discussed feature patterns for PPARγ and HDAC1 across all activity thresholds further supports and strengthens the explainability analysis performed by MINERVA. Satisfactorily, across all examined targets, the features highlighted by the SHAP analysis strongly align with their experimentally known interaction patterns. For transparency, Figures S1-S6 show the global SHAP analysis for 10 ﻿μM, 1 ﻿μM and 100 nM cutoffs, respectively.

To illustrate the complete prediction workflow, we evaluated MINERVA using XYY-CP1106, an investigational drug candidate for AD treatment exhibiting an inhibitory activity (IC50) of 14.7 nM against Monoamine Oxidase B (MAO-B) [91]. Results are summarized in Fig. 7.

Target-level MINERVA output page for predicted active compounds towards MAO-B target, including color map based SHAP explanation and reliability assessment

As shown in Fig. 7, MINERVA correctly identified XYY-CP1106 as an active compound across all four bioactivity thresholds for MAO-B, with DoA scores of 0.94, 0.71, 0.61, and 0.61 at the 10 µM, 1 µM, 100 nM, and 10 nM thresholds, respectively. The explainability analysis further highlighted the coumarin core as chemotype widely occurring in potent MAO-B inhibitors, as a key contributor to activity [92]. In particular, the combination of the coumarin ring with an ethoxy linker connecting to a second aromatic moiety was consistently ranked among the most influential structural elements. This arrangement aligns with established SARs for MAO-B ligands, where planar aromatic systems favor π–π stacking interactions within the aromatic cage (e.g., residues Tyr398 , Tyr435 and FAD cofactor), while flexible linkers help position substituents toward the hydrophobic binding cavity. For the sake of clarity, only the 10 nM model, the most informative threshold due to its ability to capture the highest affinity ligand subset, was included in the figure. In line with MINERVA multi-target design framework, XYY-CP1106 was also predicted to display reliable activity against additional AD relevant targets, including AChE and BACE1, supporting its potential suitability as a multi-target agent. As described in Fig. 8, the explainability maps for AChE again emphasized the coumarin core linked to an additional aromatic moiety, in agreement with structural features frequently observed in dual AChE and MAO-B inhibitors, as reported elsewhere [92]. Conversely, the model for BACE1 assigned elevated importance to the fluorine atoms linked to aromatic ring and to the dihydropyridine fragment, both of which are consistent with known medicinal chemistry determinants for BACE1 binding.

Additional target-level output returned from XYY-CP1106 prediction

In agreement with this multi-target profile, XYY-CP1106 was also predicted to be active against COX-2 and MMP-13. In this context, the presence of a the hydroxypyridone moiety (i.e., the pharmacophore of the iron-chelator drug deferiprone) introduces an additional disease-oriented mechanism through its well-established Fe3⁺-chelating capability. While this fragment is not expected to directly interact with enzymatic metal centers, metal chelation may modulate redox- and inflammation-driven pathways implicated in AD. This aspect is particularly relevant for the heme-dependent COX-2 and for inflammation-associated metalloproteinases such as MMP-13, whose expression and functional regulation are influenced by oxidative stress. Altogether, the predicted activity on these targets, combined with the deferiprone fragment, further supports a multi-mechanistic and system-level mode of action. For completeness, Figure S7 of Supporting Information reports the corresponding results.

Conclusion

In this work, we present MINERVA, a dedicated predictive platform for AD that integrates high quality bioactivity data, robust machine learning models, and fragment-level explainability. By collecting 70,960 compounds across 33 AD relevant targets and applying an enhanced CSFP fingerprint, BRF classifiers, and a probability-binning DoA, we conceived a framework capable of reliable activity prediction across heterogeneous and imbalanced datasets. Furthermore, the ability to explore four different bioactivity thresholds provides a customizable level of analysis, making MINERVA suitable for both expert and non-expert users according to their specific goals. Moreover, a key strength of MINERVA is the use of SHAP-based interpretability, which maps molecular fragments directly to their contributions in target specific predictions with the aim of better driving the rational design of multi-target ligands. The MINERVA web-based interface also demonstrates its ability to recognize known patterns and provide transparent target-level explanations. Overall, MINERVA fills a critical gap in computational resources for neurodegenerative drug discovery. By combining disease-specific target curation, high performance predictive modeling, and interpretable output within an accessible framework, it offers a practical and free-to-use tool to guide the exploration of chemical space in AD. Future extensions may incorporate additional targets, endpoints, and modeling strategies, further expanding the platform purpose in accelerating multi-target AD drug design. Last not least, MINERVA can screen large volumes of data on demand, ensuring both scalability and flexibility in its application. MINERVA is freely available at https://prometheus.farmacia.uniba.it/minerva/ website.
