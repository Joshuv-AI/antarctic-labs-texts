When Cancer Erupts: Recognizing the Volcano Lesion in Gastric Metastases
Authors: Siri A. Urquhart, Seth Sweetser
Published: 2025

CASE REPORT

A 77-year-old man with liposarcoma of the left thigh requiring chemoradiation and surgical resection several years prior presented with acute on chronic angina and microcytic anemia concerning for occult gastrointestinal bleeding with a hemoglobin of 6.1 g/dL. He had a similar presentation the year prior, although with overt gastrointestinal bleeding in the form of melena. At that time, upper endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) demonstrated multiple cratered gastric ulcers in the cardia, fundus, and greater curvature of the stomach with the largest lesion measuring 10 mm in size (Figure 1A). Histopathology showed findings of active gastritis, ulceration, and regenerative changes with negative stains for Helicobacter pylori, cytomegalovirus, S100, smooth muscle actin, and AE1/AE3.

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) demonstrating multiple cratered gastric ulcers in the cardia, fundus, and greater curvature of the stomach with the largest measuring 10 mm in size (A). Repeat EGD with several large, ulcerated masses in the stomach (B) and histopathology demonstrating high-grade spindle cell lesion consistent with known liposarcoma (C).

During his current hospitalization, he required 5 packed red blood cell transfusions. He underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy, which revealed several large, ulcerated masses in the stomach (Figure 1B). Biopsies of the lesions demonstrated high-grade spindle cell lesion consistent with known liposarcoma (Figure 1C). He is receiving palliative radiotherapy.

Gastric metastases are rare, accounting for a small proportion of secondary tumors in the stomach. The most common primary sources are lung, breast, and melanoma. Endoscopically, these metastases frequently appear as elevated lesions with or without central ulceration, sometimes described as “volcano-like” lesions. The “volcano lesion” refers to a raised lesion with central ulcer, a classic but not pathognomonic appearance for metastatic involvement.1 The combination of endoscopic appearance and histology at initial endoscopy yields a sensitivity up to 98% and specificity of 84%–99% for detecting malignancy in gastric ulcers when both are benign. Most malignancies are identified at initial endoscopy, and the incremental diagnostic yield of follow-up endoscopy is very low if the ulcer appears benign and initial biopsies are negative. Diagnostic accuracy is maximized by obtaining multiple biopsies—at least 7 from the ulcer edges and base is recommended for suspicious lesions. Biopsies from both the margin and base increase detection rates, particularly for early gastric cancer.2–6

Routine surveillance endoscopy is not recommended for ulcers with benign endoscopic appearance and negative biopsies. Surveillance is reserved for ulcers with high-risk features including incomplete healing, high-risk histology, or clinical risk factors such as older age, large size, corpus/cardia location, background atrophy, or family history of gastric cancer. The American College of Gastroenterology and American Gastroenterological Association recommend systematic biopsy protocols (eg, the Sydney system with 1–2 biopsies from each of 5 sites) for patients at increased risk or with suspicious lesions. For ulcers with benign appearance and negative histology, further endoscopic follow-up does not improve malignancy detection.2,4,7–9

This case highlights the classic endoscopic features of gastric metastases as submucosal lesions with elevation and central ulceration (ie, “volcano lesions”) and documents the evolution of these malignant lesions. Furthermore, this case underscores the importance of having a high index of suspicion for metastatic malignancy when multiple volcano-appearing lesions are present in the stomach despite initial negative biopsies for malignancy. Earlier diagnosis of gastric metastases from liposarcoma in this case could have enabled more timely initiation of systemic therapy and radiotherapy, potentially improving disease control and more effective systemic disease management, rather than significant impact on overall survival.
