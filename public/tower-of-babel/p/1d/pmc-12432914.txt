Efficacy of stereotactic body radiotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma with adrenal metastases: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Xi Cheng, Jing Li, Yong Li, Fenghua Liu, Tiehua Li, Yan Meng, Miaomiao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with adrenal metastasis is associated with poor prognosis and lacks standardized treatment strategies. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) is a promising non-invasive option, yet its efficacy in this setting remains underexplored. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of SBRT in managing HCC patients with adrenal metastasis, focusing on factors influencing local control (LC) and overall survival (OS).MethodsThis retrospective study included 52 HCC patients with adrenal metastases treated with SBRT using the CyberKnife system between February 2016 and March 2022. Gross tumor volume (GTV) and biologically effective dose (BED) were calculated, and LC and OS were analyzed. Prognostic factors were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression.ResultsAt a median follow-up of 12.5 months, the overall response rate was 86.0%, with LC rates of 100%, 81.7%, and 75.8% at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years, respectively. The median OS was 22 months. Intrahepatic tumor control and absence of extra-organ metastases were independently associated with improved OS (P=0.01 and P=0.001, respectively). No grade ≥2 adverse reactions were observed.ConclusionsCyberKnife-based SBRT provides high LC rates and acceptable survival outcomes in HCC patients with adrenal metastases. Intrahepatic lesion control and extrahepatic disease burden are key factors influencing prognosis. SBRT should be considered as a local treatment strategy in multidisciplinary management of this population.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), adrenal metastasis, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), local control rate (LC rate), overall survival (OS)

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study shows that stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) using the CyberKnife platform achieves high local control (LC) and favorable overall survival (OS) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients with adrenal metastases. Intrahepatic tumor control and the absence of extra-organ metastases were identified as key predictors of OS.

What is known and what is new?

• Adrenal metastases from HCC are rare but clinically challenging. While SBRT has emerged as a promising modality for treating adrenal metastases, there is limited evidence in HCC populations.

• This study retrospectively evaluated CyberKnife-based SBRT in 52 HCC patients with adrenal metastases and revealed its safety, efficacy, and prognostic factors for OS.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• SBRT should be considered an effective local therapy for adrenal metastases from HCC, especially in patients with controlled intrahepatic disease. Integration with systemic therapy may further enhance survival benefits.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide, with particularly high incidence and mortality rates in regions with endemic hepatitis B and C, such as East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (1,2). Its development is closely associated with chronic viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. While early-stage HCC may be curable through resection, transplantation, or local ablation (3,4), most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages with distant metastases, where treatment options remain limited (5).

The adrenal gland is a relatively common site of extrahepatic metastasis in HCC, with an incidence ranging from 4% to 27% (6,7). Adrenal metastasis is typically associated with aggressive disease and poor prognosis (8). Its deep anatomical location and proximity to critical organs increase the complexity of management and the risk of endocrine dysfunction (9). Current treatment data are limited to small retrospective series, with no standardized approach established, particularly regarding the timing and modality of intervention in asymptomatic cases (10,11).

In recent years, systemic therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., lenvatinib, donafenib) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., pembrolizumab) have improved survival in advanced HCC. The IMbrave150 trial demonstrated that atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab achieved a median overall survival (OS) of 19.2 months (12). However, patients with adrenal metastases are often excluded from pivotal clinical trials due to anatomical complexity or multiorgan involvement [e.g., HIMALAYA trial exclusion criteria (13)], leaving a lack of high-level evidence for this subgroup. Moreover, the objective response rate (ORR) of systemic therapy remains limited to 20–30% (14), and approximately 40% of patients require dose reduction or treatment discontinuation due to toxicity (15). Therefore, identifying effective and well-tolerated local treatment strategies for this population is significant clinical importance.

Conventional treatments for adrenal metastases, including surgery, radiofrequency ablation, and conventional radiotherapy, are often limited by tumor size, deep location, and comorbidities, with relatively high complication rates (10,11,16-18). Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), with its high precision, non-invasiveness, and favorable dose conformity, has emerged as an alternative for patients unsuitable for surgery or other local interventions (19,20).

CyberKnife, a robotic SBRT platform with real-time image guidance and submillimeter tracking accuracy, is particularly suited for treating anatomically complex lesions such as adrenal metastases (21). Compared to conventional radiotherapy, SBRT enables high-precision treatment in fewer sessions with reduced margins and improved local control (LC) (20). In HCC, SBRT has demonstrated favorable outcomes in patients with inoperable or refractory disease (22), and meta-analyses have shown superior LC and survival rates compared to conventional radiotherapy, with lower toxicity (23). As such, SBRT is increasingly recognized as an essential modality in the multidisciplinary management of HCC. Nevertheless, data on the application of SBRT in HCC patients with adrenal metastases remain limited, and prospective large-scale studies are lacking. Further investigation is warranted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of SBRT in this specific metastatic setting.

This study aims to retrospectively evaluate the outcomes of CyberKnife-based SBRT in patients with HCC and adrenal metastases, focusing on LC and OS. The findings are expected to provide evidence for clinical decision-making and support the role of SBRT in managing this underrepresented patient population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jgo.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jgo-2024-1011/rc).

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective study included patients with primary HCC and adrenal metastases who underwent SBRT between February 2016 and March 2022. A total of the 70 patients initially screened, 52 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Inclusion criteria were: (I) confirmed primary HCC with ≤5 extrahepatic metastases, among which only adrenal lesions received SBRT; other metastatic sites (e.g., lung, bone) were either asymptomatic and <1 cm, previously treated with local therapy, or scheduled for systemic treatment after SBRT; (II) ≥1 cm distance between the lesion and gastrointestinal structures; (III) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2; and (IV) Child-Pugh class A or B liver function.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) active gastrointestinal bleeding within two weeks before treatment; (II) severe cardiopulmonary or renal dysfunction; (III) concurrent malignancies; and (IV) incomplete clinical or follow-up data.

Although adrenal lesions were asymptomatic in all patients, SBRT was indicated based on the following: oligometastatic disease status, large lesion burden (GTV ≥10 mL), radiologic signs of progression risk (e.g., vascular involvement), uncontrolled adrenal progression despite stable intrahepatic disease under systemic therapy, or multidisciplinary consensus that LC could delay systemic progression and improve survival.

Patient grouping and clinical intervention

Patients were stratified into subgroups based on the following criteria: (I) tumor laterality: left, right, or bilateral adrenal metastases; (II) tumor size: ≥4.2 vs. <4.2 cm, with the cutoff based on prior evidence linking tumor size >4 cm to reduced LC following SBRT (24), and ROC analysis in this cohort identifying 4.2 cm as a sensitive threshold for local recurrence; (III) timing of metastasis: synchronous (present at initial HCC diagnosis) or metachronous (developed during disease course); and (IV) treatment: all patients received SBRT.

All patients also received systemic therapy during or within 3 months of SBRT, including (I) first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sorafenib 400 mg bid or lenvatinib 8–12 mg·qd; 68.3%); (II) immune checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab 200 mg·q3w or atezolizumab 1,200 mg·q3w; 42.1%); and (III) combination therapy with TKIs and ICIs (29.6%). Regimens were adjusted based on Child-Pugh liver function status and individual tolerance.

SBRT radiotherapy

Computed tomography (CT) localization and imaging: all patients were positioned supine on a vacuum cushion with spinal tracking assistance and underwent contrast-enhanced CT and four-dimensional CT (4DCT) to assess adrenal tumor motion. Scans were acquired with a slice thickness of 1.5 mm, covering 10 cm above and below the lesion. SBRT was delivered using the CyberKnife system (Accuray Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The SBRT protocol used in this study followed international best practices and institutional standards.

Target delineation and treatment planning: (I) target delineation: physicians delineated the gross tumor volume (GTV) and organs at risk (OARs) on CT images, including the normal liver, kidneys, stomach, and duodenum; (II) target expansion: based on 4DCT motion assessment, the planning target volume (PTV) was expanded by 3–9 mm in all directions from the GTV; (III) treatment planning: physicists developed a treatment plan based on dose constraints and prescription dose, ensuring that over 95% of the PTV received the prescribed dose; (IV) radiotherapy dose: the prescription dose ranged from 30 to 56 Gy, delivered in 3 to 7 fractions, with isodose levels between 55% and 76%. The biologically effective dose (BED) was calculated using the formula: BED = nd[1 + d/(α/β)], with α/β set at 10, and a median BED of 59.5 Gy for treatment.

Follow-up and efficacy evaluation

All patients underwent regular imaging and laboratory evaluations during follow-up to assess disease progression and treatment response.

Follow-up schedule: patients were followed every 3 months after treatment. Assessments included complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), cortisol levels, and imaging studies (chest CT and abdominal contrast-enhanced MRI).

Efficacy evaluation: treatment response was assessed according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST), and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). LC and OS were defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to lesion progression or death, respectively.

Adverse reactions monitoring

Adverse reactions are classified according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE 4.0). The primary monitored reactions include gastrointestinal symptoms caused by radiotherapy (such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea), renal function impairment (such as elevated serum creatinine and renal insufficiency), local pain, and other related adverse reactions. For each adverse reaction, detailed records of the onset time, duration, and severity are maintained to comprehensively assess the patient’s tolerance and the safety of the treatment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 and R software (with the survminer package). LC rates were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and group differences were assessed by the log-rank test in univariate analysis. Multivariate analysis used the Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent prognostic factors for LC and OS. The BED10 was dichotomized at 75 Gy, and GTV was stratified at a threshold of 150 mL. Systemic therapy was defined as targeted or immunotherapy administered within 3 months before or after SBRT. A time trend plot was constructed to visualize the temporal trends in LC across subgroups, with time points (1 and 2 years) on the x-axis and LC rate (%) on the y-axis; each line represented a clinical subgroup. Figures were generated using Python 3.11 with the Matplotlib library. All statistical tests were two-sided; a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Clinical Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University (Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital) (No. EHBHKY-H024-P001). All patients participated voluntarily with full informed consent and signed consent forms, agreeing to receive SBRT radiotherapy and authorizing the use of their clinical data for scientific research. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and related ethical guidelines, ensuring that the research design and implementation meet ethical standards and maximize patient rights protection. Patients’ personal information was carefully protected during data collection, with all data anonymized to prevent identification. Sensitive patient information is accessible only to the research team, with technical measures in place to ensure data security and prevent unauthorized access or disclosure.

Results

Follow-up status

A total of 52 eligible patients were included in the study. Baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. All patients were asymptomatic at diagnosis, and all metastatic lesions were identified by imaging (chest CT and abdominal contrast-enhanced MRI). A total of 57 evaluable adrenal lesions were identified, including 11 on the left, 36 on the right, and 5 bilateral. The median maximum lesion diameter was 4.2 cm (1.4–10.0 cm). Fifteen patients had adrenal metastases at initial diagnosis, while 37 developed metastases during HCC treatment. The median interval from initial HCC diagnosis to adrenal metastasis was 9 months (0–146 months). As of May 10, 2022, the median follow-up duration was 12.5 months (2–63 months). Twenty-three patients (44.2%) presented with additional extrahepatic metastases, including lung (n=12), bone (n=6), and lymph nodes (n=5). Among them, 18 (78.3%) received local treatment (ablation or surgery), and 5 (21.7%) did not undergo intervention due to stable disease. The median time to progression of non-adrenal metastases was 8.2 months (3–19 months).

LC rate

All patients underwent regular imaging and laboratory tests during follow-up to monitor disease progression and treatment response. By the last follow-up, of the 57 evaluable lesions, 21 (36.8%) achieved CR, 23 (40.4%) achieved PR, 5 lesions (8.8%) maintained SD, and 8 lesions (14.0%) showed PD. The LC rates were 100% at 6 months, 81.7% at 1 year, and 75.8% at 2 years (Figure 2). Eight progressive lesions in six patients had LC durations ranging from 8 to 24 months. Two patients received additional SBRT after progression and achieved renewed LC.

LC rate curve of patients with primary HCC with adrenal metastasis treated with SBRT. HCC, hepatocellular carcinoma; LC, local control; SBRT, stereotactic body radiotherapy.

Survival status

By the last follow-up, 33 patients (63.5%) had died, while 19 patients (36.5%) were still alive. The median OS was 22 months, ranging from 2 to 62 months. The OS rates were 89.6% at six months, 69.0% at one year, 41.4% at two years, 29.5% at three years, and 7.4% at five years (Figure 3). Among the 33 deceased patients, 23 (69.7%) died from liver failure due to intrahepatic tumor progression, 4 (12.1%) from lung metastasis, 3 (9.1%) from gastrointestinal bleeding caused by esophageal and gastric varices, 1 (3.0%) from hemoptysis, and 2 (6.1%) from cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events.

Kaplan-Meier survival curve for HCC patients with adrenal metastasis. HCC, hepatocellular carcinoma.

Disease progression

During follow-up, 44 patients (84.6%) experienced disease progression, primarily presenting as intrahepatic recurrence and distant metastasis involving multiple sites, such as the lungs, brain, bones, and lymph nodes. Following disease progression, patients received various subsequent treatment strategies: 31 patients (70.5%) received systemic therapies (such as targeted or immunotherapy), 15 patients (34.1%) underwent interventional treatments, and 10 patients (22.7%) received additional SBRT.

Univariate and multivariate analysis

Univariate analysis showed that lesion location (P=0.02) and GTV (P=0.01) were significantly associated with LC. However, these factors did not retain significance in multivariate analysis (lesion location: P=0.14; GTV: P=0.95) (Table 2, Figure 4). Regarding OS, the univariate analysis identified significant associations with vascular invasion at initial diagnosis (P=0.049), intrahepatic lesion control at the time of SBRT (P=0.004), and the presence of extra-organ metastases (P<0.001). These variables were subsequently included in multivariate analysis to assess their independent prognostic value. Multivariate analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model identified intrahepatic lesion control (P=0.01) and the presence of extra-organ metastases (P=0.001) as independent predictors of OS (Table 3). Vascular invasion of the initial intrahepatic lesion, lesion count, adrenal control status, and post-SBRT systemic therapy were not statistically significant in the multivariate model. Patients with controlled intrahepatic lesions had 1-year and 2-year OS rates of 85.9% and 52.5%, respectively, significantly higher than those without intrahepatic control. Similarly, patients without extra-organ metastases showed superior survival, particularly in 2-year OS.

Adverse reactions

Among all patients receiving SBRT radiotherapy, no radiotherapy-related deaths were observed, demonstrating the high safety of this treatment. In terms of adverse reactions, 7 patients (13.5%) reported grade 1 acute gastrointestinal reactions, including 4 cases of nausea and 3 cases of vomiting. Additionally, 1 patient reported grade 1 fatigue. All of these mild adverse reactions gradually resolved after the completion of radiotherapy and did not have a lasting impact on the patient’s daily life. Notably, no grade 2 or higher acute adverse reactions were observed throughout the treatment period, and no late radiotherapy-related adverse reactions were detected during follow-up.

Discussion

Clinical efficacy of SBRT radiotherapy in HCC with adrenal metastasis

Adrenal metastases from HCC are typically observed in the advanced stages of the disease. Traditional treatments such as surgical resection and chemotherapy offer limited efficacy and are highly invasive (25-29). With the advancement of precision radiotherapy, SBRT has emerged as a preferred option for inoperable patients due to its ability to deliver high-dose radiation in a few fractions with high precision. In this study, a retrospective analysis of 52 HCC patients with adrenal metastases treated with CyberKnife SBRT demonstrated favorable short- and mid-term outcomes: a 6-month LC rate of 100%, 1- and 2-year LC rates of 81.7% and 75.8%, respectively, and median OS of 22 months, with a 5-year survival rate of 7.4%. Although long-term survival remains suboptimal, SBRT offers meaningful clinical benefits by delaying disease progression through effective LC, enhancing the efficacy of systemic therapies (e.g., targeted or immunotherapy), and potentially preventing endocrine complications such as adrenal crisis (14).

Technical features of CyberKnife and platform selection strategy

CyberKnife, a robotic-assisted SBRT system, offers real-time image guidance and six-dimensional tracking capabilities, allowing submillimeter accuracy by dynamically correcting tumor motion due to respiration (30). CyberKnife’s Synchrony respiratory tracking reduces the required PTV margin to as little as 3–5 mm for anatomically mobile targets such as adrenal glands, which are significantly affected by diaphragmatic motion. In contrast, conventional LINACs lack real-time motion compensation and typically require larger PTV expansions of 8–10 mm to account for motion uncertainty (31).

Dosimetric comparisons have demonstrated that CyberKnife provides superior target conformity index (CI =1.12±0.08 vs. LINAC 1.25±0.11) and steeper dose gradients index (GI =2.8±0.4 vs. 3.5±0.6) (P<0.05), resulting in lower radiation exposure to adjacent organs, particularly in anatomically complex regions such as near the bowel or kidneys—for instance, duodenal Dmax was reduced by 15–20% (31). However, LINAC-based SBRT is more time-efficient, with a typical treatment time of 15–25 minutes, compared to 30–60 minutes for CyberKnife due to frequent image acquisition and robotic adjustments (32). Therefore, for patients with poor performance status (e.g., ECOG ≥2) or relatively fixed tumor locations, LINAC may be a more suitable option. Notably, clinical outcomes across SBRT platforms appear consistent. A study using LINAC-based SBRT reported 1-year LC rates of 78–85% in HCC patients with adrenal metastases (33), comparable to our findings (81.7%). It suggests that platform selection should be guided by individual patient factors such as respiratory motion tolerance, performance status, and tumor location, rather than the perceived superiority of a specific technology.

Clinical value and multidimensional mechanisms of SBRT

SBRT exerts its antitumor effects by delivering high BED, inducing direct tumor cell death, endothelial damage, and remodeling of the immune microenvironment. In this study, despite a median BED10 of only 59.5 Gy (range, 45–100.8 Gy), the 1-year LC rate reached 81.7%, which is comparable to outcomes reported in adrenal metastases from lung cancer treated with BED10 ≥72 Gy (33). It may be attributable to the unique biological characteristics of HCC metastases, including rich vascularization that enhances sensitivity to radiation-induced vascular occlusion, and relatively small lesion volumes (median GTV: 98.5 mL in this study), facilitating adequate dose coverage.

Multivariate Cox regression identified intrahepatic tumor control [hazard ratio (HR) =3.17, P=0.01] and the presence of extrahepatic metastases (HR =1.89, P=0.001) as independent predictors of OS. These findings align with established progression patterns in HCC, where uncontrolled intrahepatic disease often leads to hepatic failure and multiple extrahepatic lesions reflect more aggressive tumor behavior (28). As such, LC of adrenal metastases via SBRT should be integrated with active management of intrahepatic disease using transarterial chemoembolization, ablation, or systemic therapies. In this study, patients receiving SBRT in combination with sorafenib or lenvatinib had a median OS of 25 months, compared to 19 months in those without systemic therapy.

Although all patients in this cohort were asymptomatic at baseline, early intervention with SBRT may reduce the incidence of endocrine complications such as adrenal crisis (<5%) and tumor-related pain (<10%) (34). Additionally, SBRT may potentiate systemic immune responses by promoting tumor antigen release and enhancing the abscopal effects of programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors. One study reported an ORR of 54% for patients with oligometastatic HCC treated with SBRT plus pembrolizumab, significantly higher than the 32% achieved with monotherapy (32).

Currently, there is no consensus on the optimal SBRT dose-fractionation regimen for adrenal lesions. Some data suggest that a BED10 >100 Gy (e.g., 3×18 Gy) may provide the best LC (33). In contrast, this study achieved a 1-year LC rate of 81.7% with a more conservative regimen (median BED10 of 59.5 Gy, equivalent to 5×7 Gy), potentially due to the radiosensitivity of HCC metastases, the high conformality of the CyberKnife platform, and the synergistic effects of systemic therapy. Dose escalation must be balanced against the tolerance of OARs. A meta-analysis indicated that duodenal Dmax >32 Gy was associated with a 2.3-fold increased risk of gastrointestinal ulceration (14). In this study, all lesions adjacent to the bowel were planned with a duodenal Dmax ≤32 Gy, and only 13.5% of patients experienced grade 1 gastrointestinal toxicity (e.g., nausea), with no ≥ grade 2 events observed. For lesions distant from critical organs, escalated doses (e.g., BED10 ≥80 Gy) may be feasible, whereas more conservative regimens (e.g., 5×7 Gy) are recommended for tumors abutting high-risk structures.

In treating HCC with adrenal metastases, SBRT demonstrates substantial clinical utility. This study shows that SBRT can achieve a high level of LC with minimal toxicity, making it a suitable option for patients not candidates for surgery or systemic therapy. By delaying tumor progression, SBRT contributes to improved mid-term survival and, when combined with targeted therapies (such as sorafenib or lenvatinib) or immunotherapy, may further extend OS. These findings support the role of SBRT as a key component of local therapy for adrenal metastases from HCC and provide a foundation for optimizing individualized treatment strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study was a single-center retrospective analysis with a limited sample size, which may introduce selection bias and affect the generalizability of the findings. The wide range of follow-up durations also presents limitations: short-term follow-up may underestimate long-term efficacy, while extended follow-up may be influenced by confounding variables, compromising the reliability of survival analysis. The study primarily evaluated LC and OS, but lacked detailed analysis of key treatment variables such as radiotherapy dose, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, limiting insights into optimal treatment strategies. In addition, dosimetric parameters (e.g., bowel V30 Gy) and their correlation with toxicity were not assessed; future studies should establish dose-toxicity constraint models to refine treatment planning. Furthermore, the absence of biomarker testing and genomic analysis restricted the exploration of molecular heterogeneity and limited the potential for individualized therapeutic approaches.

Future perspectives

Prospective, multi-center studies with larger cohorts are warranted to reduce bias and enhance the external validity of findings. Extended follow-up duration and increased follow-up frequency will be important for more accurately evaluating the long-term efficacy and safety of SBRT. Further investigations should focus on integrating SBRT with systemic therapies, including targeted agents and immunotherapies, to improve survival outcomes and quality of life in patients with adrenal metastases from HCC. Molecular profiling through techniques such as genomics and transcriptomics will also be essential for identifying predictive biomarkers of radiotherapy response and prognosis, thereby advancing precision and personalized radiotherapy strategies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SBRT can achieve high LC rates and favorable long-term survival in patients with HCC and adrenal metastases, with LC rates of 100%, 81.7%, and 75.8% at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years, respectively. The treatment was well tolerated, with no significant adverse events observed. Intrahepatic tumor control and the presence of other organ metastases were identified as key factors influencing OS, suggesting that local therapy should be combined with systemic treatment to improve outcomes. Compared to conventional radiotherapy platforms, SBRT offers greater precision in anatomically complex regions and is suitable for patients who are not candidates for surgery or have poor treatment tolerance. Future efforts should focus on dose optimization, biomarker-guided treatment, and multimodal strategies to further enhance therapeutic outcomes in patients with metastatic HCC.
