Target FADS1-arachidonic acid-ferroptosis axis: A metabolic bridge linking matrix stiffness to PDAC stemness
Authors: Tiancheng Gong, Jiashuai Yan, Hongyu Zhu, Xiaoqi Guan, Haowei Xing, Zhen Wang, Di Wu, Biwen Zhu, Xue Zhang, Yidan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The elevated stiffness of extracellular matrix (ECM), one of the typical physical hallmarks of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which was proved to promote stemness and leads to adverse prognosis. Therefore, to dissect the underlying mechanisms between matrix stiffness and stemness is important. To this end, stiffness tunable hydrogel based on polyacrylamide (PA) was employed to simulate the stiffness of PDAC microenvironment. Our results illustrated that stiff matrix enhanced PDAC (MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells) stemness by inhibiting ferroptosis. Lipidomic analyses further revealed that matrix stiffness reprogrammed medium and long chain fatty acid metabolism, with particularly prominent arachidonic acid (AA) depletion. Intriguingly, AA supplementation restored ferroptosis sensitivity and reversed stiffness-induced stemness enhancement. Mechanistically, matrix stiffness inhibits AA content by downregulating FADS1 (fatty acid desaturase 1), a key biosynthesis enzyme. Simultaneously, the ferroptosis was suppressed accompanied by the AA reduction, which was validated via decreased lipid peroxidation and preserved mitochondrial morphology. Besides, FADS1 knockdown recapitulated the stiff matrix effects, causing ferroptosis inhibition and stemness enhancement, which could be rescued by AA supplementation. Collectively, our data shed light on the novel mechanism: matrix stiffness-suppressed FADS1 drives AA-replied ferroptosis inhibition, ultimately promoting PDAC stemness. Targeting the FADS1-AA-ferroptosis axis may provide practicable opportunities for the therapy of PDAC.

Introduction

As one of the mortal solid tumors, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) with a low five-year survival rate [1]. The characteristic tumor microenvironment (TME) with dense desmoplasia (abundant fibrotic stroma) lead to high malignancy of PDAC [2]. This aberrant extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling not only constitutes a physical barrier that hinders drug delivery, and more critically, the resultant alterations in its mechanical properties-particularly the pronounced increased matrix stiffness-serve as crucial biophysical cue that persistently drives tumor occurrence and progression by activating downstream signal transduction networks [3,4]. Indeed, a number of studies have shown that higher matrix stiffness enhances several tumor biological progress such as invasiveness [5], angiogenesis [6], epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [7] and immunosuppression [8]. Prior studies have indicated that the ECM of PDAC tissue exhibits increased stiffness [9]. Meanwhile, our preliminary research demonstrated that higher matrix stiffness upregulated the stemness level of PDAC based on stiffness tunable GelMA hydrogels [10].

The PDAC stemness with the self-renewal capacity, infinite proliferation and differentiation, correlating with unfavorable patient prognoses, is fundamental to the tumorigenesis and progression. [11,12]. Furthermore, stemness is not only regarded as a critical trigger of tumor refractoriness to standard radiotherapy and chemotherapy, also identified as the primary source of metastasis and recurrence [13,14]. Despite contemporary radiotherapy and chemotherapy have made considerable progress in tumor debulking, they exhibit inferior efficacy in targeting tumor stemness, especially in PDAC [15]. Therefore, profound exploring the mechanisms of matrix stiffness governing PDAC stemness is of significant scientific importance and clinical value for advancing therapeutic strategies and improving patient prognosis.

As a novel pattern of cell death, ferroptosis is distinct from canonical programmed cell death, such as apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy, in terms of genetic, biochemical, and morphological characteristics [16]. From the biochemical perspective, ferroptosis results from an oxidative disturbance within the intracellular microenvironment, a process primarily governed by Fe2+ (ferrous iron), lipid peroxidation [17]. Recent studies shown that cells perceive and response matrix stiffness to modulate the extent of ferroptosis. For instance, matrix stiffness has been shown to suppress ferroptosis via inducing PD-L2, thereby promoting hepatocellular carcinoma progression and sorafenib resistance [18]. Furthermore, ferroptosis exerted essential role in mediating the stemness of malignant gastric, breast and colorectal cancers [[19], [20], [21]]. Recent evidence indicates that SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis drives the inhibition of PDAC stemness and chemoresistance [22]. Our previous study demonstrated that 3D printed PDAC mechanical microenvironment based GelMA and HAMA hydrogels boosted chemoresistance through NRF2-supressed ferroptosis [23]. However, the regulatory relationship among matrix stiffness, ferroptosis and stemness of PDAC remains unclear.

Metabolic networks critically regulate cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis, with lipid metabolism serving as a central determinant [24]. Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) possess bis-allylic hydrogen structure, which facilitates lipid peroxidation and indispensable for ferroptosis [25]. Consequently, both the abundance and localization of PUFAs determine the magnitude of lipid peroxidation, thereby ultimately dictating the extent of ferroptosis. Arachidonic acid (AA) is an essential n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid that is a major component of cellular membrane phospholipid bilayers. Because of its highly polyunsaturated, AA is exquisitely vulnerable to lipid peroxidation, a property that lies at the heart of its role as a key molecular substrate for propagation of ferroptosis [26]. In addition, extensive researches have established that AA involved in the tumorigenesis and progression of lung cancer, triple-negative breast cancer and liver cancer et al. [[27], [28], [29]]. EPHX1-mediated AA metabolism inhibition to promote PDAC growth and gemcitabine resistance [30]. Although our previous work has clarified that SCD1-dependent lipid metabolic reprogramming participated in the process of matrix stiffness triggered PDAC chemoresistance, whether it regulates ferroptosis via modulation of AA remains to be elucidated [31].

The intracellular level of AA is primarily determined by its biosynthetic pathway, a cascade of reactions involving a series of desaturases and elongases. Specifically, the essential fatty acid linoleic acid (LA, the primary precursor of arachidonic acid) is modified into gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) through catalysis by Δ6-desaturase (encoded by FADS2) [32]. Subsequently, GLA undergoes carbon chain elongation facilitated by elongases (such as ELOVL family) to form dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (DGLA) [33,34]. Finally, Δ5-desaturase (encoded by FADS1) converts DGLA into arachidonic acid (AA) [35]. FADS1/2 and ELOVL family elaborated momentous role in several malignancy phenotypes, such as proliferation, invasion, migration and ferroptosis, via controlling PUFA metabolism [[36], [37], [38]]. Consequently, elucidation of the precise mechanism by which matrix stiffness modulates the biosynthesis of AA is crucial for understanding its role in PDAC ferroptosis.

Taken together, based on stiffness tunable Polyacrylamide (PA) hydrogel platform, we demonstrated that AA-reduction originated from matrix stiffness-sensitive FADS1 deactivation, drove the inhibition of ferroptosis, which stimulated PDAC stemness. This work underscores the role of tumor biomechanics in AA metabolism, ferroptosis and stemness, also provides practicable potential strategy for the therapeutic of PDAC.

Materials and methods

Polyacrylamide (PA) hydrogel

For the synthesize of PA hydrogels with varying stiffness, polymerizing acrylamide and bis-acrylamide in specific ratios with N,N,N′,N′-Tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) and ammonium persulfate (APS) was performed (S-Table.1). Then the surfaces were covered with sulfo-SANPAH (0.5 mg/mL, MCE, China) under 365 nm UV for a period of 10 min, followed by incubation with Collagen type I (0.1 mg/mL, Solarbio, China) overnight. After rinsing with PBS, the prepared hydrogel samples were transferred to 6-well plates for subsequent assays.

Cell culture

Human cell lines of PDAC (MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1) were gained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cultivate atmosphere was as follows: PANC-1 cells in DMEM (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cytiva, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2, whereas for MIA PaCa-2 cells required additional 2.5% horse serum (HS). For passaging, cells were dissociated with collagenase I and subsequently washed twice with PBS.

Reverse-transcription and q-PCR

Total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, 343903, USA) as per the manufacturer's instruction. Then cDNA was reversed transcription with commercialized kit (Thermo Scientific, K1622, USA), and amplified with the Fast SYBR Green qPCR Kit (Thermo Scientific, 1176202K, USA). The quantification of relative gene expression was calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt method. The detailed primer sequences were listed in S-Table 2.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence assay, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was utilized to fix cells and rinse with PBS, while tissue sections from the previous study's paraffin blocks were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval [39]. Then, in turn blocked with 10% BSA at 37 °C for 2 h, incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, washed with PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20), incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h, DAPI counterstaining for 15 min. Finally, the images were obtained with a fluorescence microscope (LEICA, Germany). The detailed primary and secondary antibodies were provided in a list in S-Table 3.

Sphere formation assay

500 cells (MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1) per well were digested and plated in Ultra Low Attachment 96-well plates (Corning, USA) for 10 days. The images were collected with the bioluminescence microscope (Olympus, BX41, Japan).

RNA-seq

Trizol reagent was applied to lyse MIA PaCa-2 cells cultured on PA hydrogels. The purification of RNA, reverse transcription process, the construction of library, as well as sequencing were performed at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, and prognostic Cox proportional hazards (PCPH) model were applied to analyze, according to differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05 and |log2FC| >0.15. R programming language were leveraged to analyze and visualize the results.

BODIPY 581/591C11 analysis

Cells were treated and incubated with BODIPY 581/591C11 dye (MCE, HY-D1301, USA) for 25 min, then rinsed with serum-free DMEM. Subsequently, the nuclei were counterstained with hochest33342. Images were observed and visualized through confocal microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Intracellular LPO and MDA

The levels of cellular LPO (lipid hydroperoxide) and MDA (malondialdehyde) were evaluated with relative assay kit (JianCheng, A106-1, China) and (JianCheng, A003-4-1, China), respectively according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells were harvested and suspended in extracting solution, then crushing cells with ultrasonic cell crusher (SONICS, VCX150, USA). The LPO and MDA contents were determined as per OD value at 586 nm and 532 nm, respectively, using microplate reader (Synergy HT, BioTek).

ROS assay

5 μM DCFH-DA (Solarbio, CA1410, China) in serum-free DMEM was utilized to incubate MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells. Imaged with fluorescence microscope (LEICA, Germany) after washed with PBS thrice. The positive areas were quantified via Image J software.

TEM observation

2.5% glutaraldehyde was applied to fix MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, and the cells with dehydrated with gradient ethanol and embedded in Spurr's resin. Subsequently, uranyl acetate and lead citrate were used to stain, the samples were visualized and imaged with TEM HT7700 (Japan).

Targeted medium and long chain fatty acid analysis

MIA-PaCa2 cells were digested and harvested from PA hydrogels. Medium and long chain fatty acids were extracted with dichloromethane/methanol solution (v/v = 1:1), dried under nitrogen, methylated with 0.5 M hydroxide methanol solution and re-extracted with n-hexane, according to manufacturer's protocol. Targeted medium and long chain fatty acid analysis based on Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was performed at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Furthermore, KEGG analysis were performed on differentially fatty acids with Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05 and |log2FC| >0.15. R Programming Language were utilized to analyze and visualize the results.

Quantification of arachidonic acid (AA)

Detached and collected MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells for AA ELISA assays, performed as per the manufacturer's instructions with AA assay kit (Solarbio, SEKSM-0032, China). The AA contents were determined using microplate reader (Synergy HT, BioTek) according to OD value at 450 nm.

RNA interference

The siRNAs were obtained from G&Jbio, and the sequences were displayed asfollows: Scrambled siRNAs served as a negative control. FADS1 siRNA#1, 5′- UCGACACAAUUACCACAAATT UUUGUGGUAAUUGUGUCGATT -3'; FADS1 siRNA#2, 5′- UUGUGAAGAAGUAUAUGAATT UUCAUAUACUUCUUCACAATT -3'; FADS1 siRNA#3, 5′- GACAUCAUCCACUCACUAATT UUAGUGAGUGGAUGAUGUCTT -3'. And siRNAs were transfected with riboFECT CP Transfection Kit (Ribo, China).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded blocks from the previous study were sectioned (5-μm thickness) [39]. Sections were blocked with peroxidase blocking agent (10 min) and BSA (60 min), incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (10 min). Finally, slides were developed with diaminobenzidine solution and counterstained with hematoxylin. The detailed primary and secondary antibodies were provided in a list in S-Table 4.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in three independent replicates (n ≥ 3). Data were displayed with Mean ± standard deviation. Normality was confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk test. The group differences were evaluated by T-test of students and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was indicated statistical significances.

Results and discussion

Stiff matrix reduces the ferroptosis of PDAC cells

The tissue stiffness of advanced PDAC patients presented a significant elevated compared to healthy individuals. Previous studies indicate that for PDAC tissues the Young's modulus exceeds 40 kPa, whereas moduli of normal tissues exhibit under 15 kPa [40]. To mimic the stiffness of normal pancreatic and PDAC tissues, according to literature and our prior study, 2.55 kPa (Soft group) and 40.40 kPa (Stiff group) were established based on PA hydrogels coated with collagen [39,41]. To eliminate cell line-specific events, two human MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cell lines were applied and cultured on both matrices for 5 days. To unravel the mechanisms underlying the matrix stiffness regulate the PDAC cell phenotypes, RNA-Seq of MIA PaCa-2 cells cultivated on both soft and stiff substrates was performed. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 [42]. The Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant on differential expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 1A). KEGG analysis unraveled a principal enrichment of the “Ferroptosis” and “Fatty acid metabolism” (Fig. 1B left). GO enrichment analysis revealed that a multitude of biological processes were influenced by matrix stiffness, such as “cellular response to reactive oxygen species”, “cellular response to mechanical stimulus” and “iron ion homeostasis” (Fig. 1B right). To better visualize the relationship between the key pathways and DEGs, the chord diagram integrating KEGG/GO pathway and log2FC values of DEGs was constructed. The results showed that multiple genes involved in “iron ion homeostasis”, “programmed cell death in response to reactive oxygen species”, “fatty acid metabolism” and “ferroptosis” were significantly differentially expressed in cells cultured on soft versus stiff matrix (Fig. 1C). Therefore, in this research, ferroptosis would be the primary focus. Initially, based on RNA-Seq analysis, all DEGs involved in “ferroptosis” enrichment within KEGG were chosen. The heatmap indicated notable variations in gene expression levels influenced by matrix stiffness regulation (Fig. 1D). Intriguingly, the expression of these genes was also extremely different between PDAC tumor and normal tissues based on the TCGA database (Fig. 1E). To explore their clinical significance, PCPH model was established. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified the ferroptosis core genes, GPX4 (a ferroptosis suppress factor via glutathione-catalyzing) and STEAP3 (an Fe3+ transporter), as independent prognostic factors in PDAC, with their expression levels being significantly correlated with patient overall survival (Fig. S1) [43,44]. Furthermore, the curves for the high and low GPX4 and STEAP3 expression groups were nearly parallel according to Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 1F). It suggested a time-invariant effect of their expression levels on patient survival risk, thereby substantiating their role as reliable independent prognostic indicators for PDAC. Consequently, ferroptosis is not merely a cellular phenomenon in vitro, but a pivotal biological process that determines clinical outcomes in patients with PDAC. However, despite PTEN-induced ferroptosis has been reported to suppress chemoresistance of PDAC, the regulatory relationship between matrix stiffness and PDAC ferroptosis remains unclear [45].

Due to the key ferroptosis pathways are governed by negative feedback loops and compensatory mechanisms, the expression of the ferroptosis-related genes sometimes reflects a dynamic, compensatory cellular response to stress rather than the net rate of ferroptosis. For example, in response to ferroptosis induced by the AZD1775 (a ferroptosis activator via inhibiting WEE1), lung cancer cells adaptively upregulate the expression of SLC7A11 (a ferroptosis inhibition gene) as a compensatory mechanism to resist ferroptosis [46]. Thus, to accurately evaluate the ferroptosis level of cells cultured on matrix with different stiffness, BODIPY-C11 was employed. This fluorescent probe enables the detection and quantification of lipid peroxidation within living cells, the hallmark of ferroptosis. It operates via a spectral transition from red (580 nm) to green (510 nm) fluorescence triggered by oxidative damage. The results indicated the green fluorescence in the stiff group was distinctly attenuated relative to soft group (Fig. 2A B). And the quantification of total LPO level showed a great decrease in the stiff group, which consistent with the BODIPY-C11 trend (Fig. 2C). As the final metabolite of lipid peroxidation, malondialdehyde (MDA) strongly correlates with ferroptosis and makes it a reliable biomarker for detection of ferroptosis [47]. Quantitative analysis also showed significantly decreased MDA levels on stiff matrix compared with soft matrix in both PDAC cell lines (Fig. 2D). These findings indicated that stiff matrix may effectively antagonize intracellular lipid peroxidation and, in this way, suppress ferroptosis. Via DCFH-DA (cell-permeable fluorescent ROS probe), we also observed that the stiff group showed downregulation of total ROS level (Fig. 2E F). This suggested that the inhibition of ferroptosis by matrix stiffness was mediated not only via the lipid metabolism pathway but also potentially through the regulation of oxidative stress homeostasis. Next, to obtain the gold standard morphological evidence for ferroptosis, TEM was employed to examine the alterations of mitochondrial architecture within PDAC cells. The characteristic mitochondrial morphological alterations in ferroptotic cells include shrinkage, membrane density elevation, cristae disruption, and adventitia rupture [48]. The TEM images displayed that more apparent mitochondrial hallmark of ferroptosis was observed with the cells in soft group, including mitochondria shrinkage and thickened membranes (Fig. 2G). Correspondingly, the mitochondria of cells cultured on a stiff substrate exhibited relatively intact structures, maintaining a normal morphology and cristae architecture. Finally, since ferroptosis is an iron-dependent cell death form, iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO, 75 μM 48H) was applied to treat cells on soft matrix [49]. BODIPY-C11 results showed that DFO significantly downregulated the lipid peroxidation (Fig. S2 A B). It shows that the observed lipid peroxidation in our study is iron-dependent, a defining feature of ferroptosis. It is notable that ferroptosis has been established as a pivotal mechanism governing stemness [50]. Our prior study based on GelMA hydrogels demonstrated that matrix stiffness can promote PDAC stemness [10]. It was reported that the SLC7A11-relied inhibition of ferroptosis can induce PDAC stemness [22]. Interestingly, our present RNA-seq data and validation experiments led us to notice that matrix stiffness starkly inhibits PDAC ferroptosis. However, this convergence implies a non-random correlation whereby stiffness can boost stemness and inhibit a negative regulator of stemness (ferroptosis). Driven by this ‘coincidence’ and existing literature, we hypothesized that matrix stiffness induces PDAC cell stemness by limiting ferroptosis. Further research will focus on understanding the mechanistic relationship between matrix stiffness, ferroptosis and stemness.

Matrix stiffness enhances PDAC stemness

Stemness is an important phenotype that affects the behavior of PDAC. In our previous work, we use GelMA hydrogel to mimic the stiffness of ECM and proved that matrix stiffness promotes PDAC stemness. To establish that this biological phenomenon is intrinsic to the mechanical cue rather than just an artefact due to the chemical composition of the hydrogel, we reproduced this effect using PA hydrogels in this study. Stemness level of PDAC was evaluated by stemness markers, SOX2, OCT4 and NANOG. They are canonical markers of tumor stemness, and can maintain the stemness of PDAC cells and also relate to the survival outcomes of patients with pancreatic cancer [51,52]. qPCR and immunofluorescence results of above stemness marker expression were analyzed. Our results show that compared with the soft matrix, stiff matrix increased mRNA expression of stem cell markers SOX2, OCT4 and NANOG (Fig. 3A). Concordantly, immunofluorescence findings demonstrated a consistent trend at the protein level (Fig. 3B). A sphere formation assay was also carried out further, which is also considered to be a gold standard to determine tumor cell stemness. It shown that MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells formed spheres of larger diameter cultured on stiff matrix compared to soft matrix, implying a stronger sphere-forming ability (Fig. 3C D). Thus, we found that stiff matrix could increase the stemness of PDAC cells at the gene, protein and functional levels, in agreement with GelMA hydrogels [10]. It indicated that the promotional effect of substrate stiffness on PDAC stemness is reproducible and stable, independent of the biomaterial hydrogels used. Matrix stiffness promotes stemness via inhibiting ferroptosis

To elucidate the interplay between ferroptosis and stemness modulated by matrix stiffness of PDAC, the rescue experiment utilizing erastin (ERA) was performed. Studies have demonstrated that Erastin exerts its effect by blocking cystine uptake via the cystine/glutamate antiporter, known as system xc-. This system xc-transporter is critical for the biosynthesis of glutathione, the major intracellular antioxidant. By inhibiting this process, Erastin compromises the cell's antioxidant defense system, ultimately triggering iron-dependent oxidative cell death. [16,53]. Due to cellular toxicity of dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), less than 1‰(v/v) was utilized to solube liposoluble erastin [54]. Consequently, an equivalent amount of DMSO was setted as a control. In the stiff group, MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells were cultured on 40 kPa PA hydrogels for 3 days, followed with 10 μM erastin treatment for 2 days. Enhanced green fluorescence intensity of BODIPY-C11 was noticed after the treatment with erastin (Fig. 4A B). Consistent with this, biochemical quantification showcased that the content of LPO and MDA were also significantly increased in the erastin-treated group compared with control group (Fig. 4C D). Interestingly, DCFH-DA confirmed that erastin treatment also resulted in a large increase in overall intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 4E F). One reason for this phenomenon might be the mechanism of erastin: opening voltage-dependent anion channels to depress mitochondrial membrane potential and produce mitochondrial ROS [55]. Furthermore, compare to control cells with intact mitochondrial structures, erastin-treated cells displayed mitochondrial constriction, augmented membrane electron density, and the ablation of mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 4G). Together, these data demonstrated that erastin rescued the suppression of ferroptosis caused by matrix stiffness. Stemness evaluation revealed a decrease in the expression of stemness markers (SOX2, OCT4, NANOG) at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5A B). Matrix stiffness reprograms lipid metabolism profiles and suppresses arachidonic acid biosynthesis

The abundant accumulation of membrane lipid peroxides is the hallmark of ferroptosis [56]. The susceptibility to this process is primarily governed by the abundance of peroxidizable polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and related lipid metabolic enzymes [57]. Consequently, to elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying stiffness-modulated ferroptosis, targeted lipidomics analysis for medium and long chain fatty acid was performed on the PDAC cells cultured on both soft and stiff substrates. Distinct separation in the lipidomic profiles between soft and stiff groups was observed through the PCA and PLS-DA analysis, which indicated that matrix stiffness profoundly reprograms the fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 6A B). The volcano plot also revealed a major shift in fatty acid abundance, with many differentially accumulated lipids between the two groups (Fig. 6C). Moreover, to characterize the specific metabolic pathways involved, enrichment of KEGG pathway suggested very prominent enrichment of “Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids”, “Fatty acid biosynthesis” and “Linoleic acid metabolism” (Fig. 6D E). Notably, the enrichment of KEGG pathways related to fatty acid metabolism was anticipated, given that our targeted metabolomics approach selectively profiled medium and long chain fatty acids. However, the results singled out the unsaturated fatty acids biosynthesis and specific fatty acids (Linoleic acid) metabolism, rather than fatty acid uptake or oxidation in a purely mode. Also, “Fatty acid biosynthesis” enrichment is consistent with our previous study pointing to reprogramming of SCD1-dependent lipid metabolism in PDAC regulated by matrix stiffness [31]. This convergence of evidence suggested that lipidomic remodeling was mediated by specific transcriptional regulation rather than by an overall metabolic disturbance. Intriguingly, the heatmap showed that compared to soft matrix, PDAC cells seeded on stiff matrix have accumulation of linoleic acid (LA, C18:2n6c) compared with soft matrix, whereas its downstream metabolite arachidonic acid (AA, C20:4n6) was substantially decreased (Fig. 6F). This metabolic pattern, characterized by precursor accumulation coupled with product depletion, suggested that matrix stiffness may specifically impede the metabolic conversion of LA into AA, resulting in the insufficient synthesis of ferroptosis-inducing AA, thereby suppressing ferroptosis. And the quantification of AA level showed a great decrease in the stiff group, which consistented with lipidomics data (Fig. 6G). To validate the specificity of AA-induced cell death and rule out non-specific oxidative damage, we conducted pharmacological rescue experiments using Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 5 μM 48H), a specific ferroptosis inhibitor. The results showed that Fer-1 treatment effectively reversed the AA-induced lipid peroxidation on stiff matrix, confirming the ferroptosis nature of the process (Fig. S2 C D). Furthermore, since ACSL4 is essential for the esterification of PUFAs into ferroptosis-susceptible phospholipids, we treated PDAC cells with PRGL493 (a specific ACSL4 inhibitor, 10 μM 48H) [58]. BODIPY-C11 results demonstrated a marked decrease in lipid peroxidation by PRGL493 (Fig. S2 E F). It indicated that AA must be esterified into membrane phospholipids by ACSL4 to trigger ferroptosis, providing strong mechanistic evidence for the specificity of our model. Collectively, these data indicated that exogenous AA supplementation effectively activates ferroptosis in PDAC cells residing on stiff matrix.

Given the established link between ferroptosis and stemness, we next investigated whether the activation of ferroptosis by AA could reverse the stiffness-induced stemness enhancement. Compared to the control, the mRNA of SOX2, OCT4 and NANOG were significantly downregulated in the AA treated group (Fig. S5 B). This suppression was further confirmed at the protein level via immunofluorescence, which showed weakened fluorescence intensity of these stemness markers upon AA treatment (Fig. S5 C). Functionally, sphere-forming assay demonstrated that AA treatment significantly reduced the diameter of PDAC cell spheres (Fig. S5 D). In summary, the findings presented demonstrated that matrix stiffness-induced AA inhibition boosts PDAC stemness via impeding ferroptosis. It established AA as a pivotal metabolic mediator linking the physical properties of ECM to the biological behavior of PDAC cells.

It strongly suggested that the metabolic conversion from LA to AA is impeded under stiff matrix conditions. The biosynthesis of AA from LA is a multi-step enzymatic process involving desaturation and elongation, primarily mediated by fatty acid desaturases (FADS1, FADS2) and elongation of very long-chain fatty acids proteins (ELOVL2, ELOVL5) [59,60]. Consequently, we investigated the levels of these four key genes.

The mRNA expression levels of FADS1, FADS2, ELOVL5, and ELOVL2 were all downward in stiff group compared to soft group (Fig. 8A). Among them, FADS1 exhibited the most significant reduction (Fig. 8A). Combining in vivo and in vitro results, it suggested simply targeting stemness would not be sufficient if the mechanical microenvironment continuously reprograms cell metabolism to thwart ferroptosis. Instead, combination therapy designed to disrupt key metabolism nodes in the signal transduction cascade of mechanical forces might be particularly fruitful in PDAC. In clinical studies, oral ω-3 PUFA supplementation in patients with sporadic colorectal adenomas resulted in a 13% to 70% reduction in the mucosal epithelial cell proliferation index [64]. And, a higher intake of PUFA was found a risk reduction of gastric cancer in individuals of the Vietnamese population, regardless of sex or age [65]. Therefore, restoring the sensitivity to ferroptosis by correcting the lipid metabolic deficit (FADS1-AA) could potentially block PDAC progression and improve patient prognosis. It provides a mechanistic rationale for the development of FADS1-targeted agents and their evaluation in future clinical trials for PDAC.

Noteworthy, beyond the FADS1-AA-ferroptosis axis, emerging evidence underscores various metabolic plasticity of PDAC in maintaining malignant phenotype. For instance, SERPINB3–MAPK axis-mediated cuproptosis resistance was shown to enhance the response to antitumor immunotherapy [66]. Separately, the circRNA cFAM124A has been identified to drive chemoresistance by enhancing CTSL-mediated tRXRα generation [67]. Alongside our present work, these findings collectively delineate a paradigm where PDAC cells strategically reprogram different metabolic nodes (AA biosynthesis, copper homeostasis, and lysosomal proteolysis) in response to diverse stressors (matrix stiffness, copper overload, and chemotherapy) to maintain malignant phenotypes (stemness, immune escape, and drug-resistant). This multiplicity of metabolic escape routes emphasizes the challenge of monotherapies and reinforces the rationale for combination strategies that simultaneously disrupt multiple key nodes.

In addition, implementing the FADS1-AA axis as a biomarker for patient stratification faces several practical hurdles. First, assessing matrix stiffness in vivo requires advanced imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance elastography (MRE), which is not universally available in all clinical centers. Second, detecting FADS1 expression or intracellular AA levels requires standardized immunohistochemical or metabolomic assays, which are currently lacking. Therefore, in future clinical trials, a companion diagnostic workflow (potentially combining stiffness imaging with FADS1 expression profiling) would need to identify the patient subset most likely to benefit.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that matrix stiffness promotes PDAC stemness by suppressing ferroptosis via the downregulation of FADS1 and subsequent AA depletion (Fig. 12). This work not only deepens our understanding of PDAC biomechanics but also identifies the FADS1-AA-ferroptosis axis as a potential therapeutic vulnerability.
