Antimicrobial Susceptibility Pattern of Staphylococcus aureus with Special Reference to Inducible Clindamycin-resistant Isolates from Various Clinical Samples at a Tertiary Care Teaching Hospital
Authors: Yash Khandelwal, Jigna Karia, Govind Ninama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Staphylococcus aureus is a major pathogenic organism responsible for various infections. The emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and inducible clindamycin resistance (ICR) complicates treatment and highlights the importance of accurate detection of Macrolide-Lincosamide-Streptogramin B (MLSB) resistance mechanisms.Aims/Objectives:This study aimed to assess the antimicrobial susceptibility pattern of S. aureus, detect ICR using the D-zone test, and evaluate its association with MRSA.Methodology:A total of 212 nonduplicate S. aureus isolates were obtained from various clinical samples at Medical College Baroda. Identification was performed using standard microbiological techniques. Antimicrobial susceptibility was tested using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method and interpreted as per the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. MRSA was identified using cefoxitin (30 µg), and ICR was detected via the D-zone test.Results:Among 212 isolates, 124 (58.49%) were male and 88 (41.51%) were female. MRSA was detected in 40 (18.87%) isolates. ICR (inducible MLSB [iMLSB]) was seen in 48 (22.64%) isolates, MS phenotype in 21 (9.90%) and constitutive MLSB in 9 (4.24%). A significant association was found between MRSA and iMLSB (P < 0.05).Conclusion:Routine D-zone testing is essential to detect inducible resistance. Continued surveillance and rational antibiotic use are necessary to prevent resistance spread.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a significant bacterial pathogenic organism known for its ability to cause a wide range of infections from superficial skin lesions to severe systemic illnesses. These infections include bacteremia, infective endocarditis, skin and soft-tissue infection (SSTI) (impetigo, folliculitis, furuncles, carbuncles, cellulitis, etc.), osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, pulmonary infections (pneumonia and empyema), gastroenteritis, meningitis, toxic shock syndrome, and urinary tract infections.[12] Its prevalence in both community and healthcare settings makes it a persistent challenge for clinicians and microbiologists.

The emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has significantly complicated treatment protocols due to its resistance to multiple antibiotic classes.[3] In India, MRSA prevalence varies widely from 7% to 60%, highlighting the importance of localized surveillance to guide empirical therapy effectively.[4]

Clindamycin, a lincosamide antibiotic, is often favored for treating MRSA-related SSTIs due to its excellent tissue penetration, ability to suppress toxin production, and oral availability. However, its efficacy is threatened by inducible clindamycin resistance (ICR), typically mediated by erythromycin ribosome methylation (erm) genes. These genes may not be detected through routine susceptibility testing, leading to potential therapeutic failure when clindamycin is prescribed.[5]

To address this diagnostic gap, the D-test is recommended to identify the inducible macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin B (iMLSB) resistance phenotype.[6] The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) advocates for routine D-testing in erythromycin-resistant, clindamycin-sensitive isolates to ensure accurate detection of ICR.[7]

This study aims to assess the antimicrobial susceptibility profile of S. aureus isolates from diverse clinical specimens, with particular emphasis on MRSA and ICR, at a tertiary care hospital.

Methodology

Study design and duration

This cross-sectional study was carried out over 6 months, spanning from April 2024 to September 2024, within Bacteriology Section of the Diagnostic Laboratory at the Department of Microbiology in Medical College Baroda and S.S.G. Hospital, Vadodara after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee. The approval letter number was IECBHR/35-2024.

Sample size

A total of 212 isolates required for the study calculated based on 16.5% prevalence of ICR from institutional previous data, 5% precision or absolute error with 95% confidence interval.

Study population

Inclusion criteria

All the patients having S. aureus infection and those samples which were collected and labeled properly with no sign of gross contamination were included in this study.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients who were already on antibiotic and repeated samples were excluded from this study.

Sample processing

A total of 9198 clinical specimens, including pus, blood, urine, sputum, body fluids, and high vaginal swabs, were collected aseptically. The samples were processed using standard procedures such as gross examination, direct smear from clinical samples, and culture. Identification of isolates was based on conventional methods, including colony morphology, Gram staining, catalase, slide and tube coagulase tests, and standard biochemical assays.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, along with the Broth microdilution method, in accordance with the CLSI guidelines.[7] The antibiotics tested included Penicillin G (10 units), Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (20/10 µg), Cefoxitin (30 µg), Erythromycin (15 µg), Clindamycin (2 µg), Gentamicin (10 µg), Linezolid (30 µg), and Vancomycin (minimum inhibitory concentration determined via the broth microdilution method).

MRSA detection was determined by assessing cefoxitin resistance, specifically a zone of inhibition of 21 mm or less.[7] Rest isolates were considered as MSSA. Erythromycin resistant isolates were tested using the D-test, which involved placing erythromycin and clindamycin disks 15–26 mm apart on the inoculated MHA plate. S. aureus ATCC 25923 and ATCC 43300 were utilized as quality control strains.

Statistical analysis

The data gathered were organized and analyzed in Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Additional computation was conducted utilizing relevant statistical analyses such as frequency tables accompanied by corresponding percentages.

Results

Among 212 S. aureus isolates, the majority were recovered from pus/wound swabs [Table 1]. Males accounted for 58.49% of patients, and the most affected age group was 0–10 years (20.75%), indicating pediatric vulnerability. MRSA was detected in 40 isolates, accounting for 18.87% of the total. The majority of MRSA isolates were obtained from inpatients (72.50%) compared to outpatients (27.50%) [Table 2].

All MRSA strains demonstrated resistance to Penicillin and Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. However, susceptibility rates for Vancomycin and Linezolid were 100% and 97.5%, respectively. In addition, the resistance rates for Erythromycin and Clindamycin were higher in MRSA compared to MSSA, with rates of 62.5% versus 31.4% and 37.5% versus 21.7%, respectively [Table 3].

Discussion

S. aureus continues to be a major contributor to both community-acquired and nosocomial infections. Its pathogenic versatility and increasing resistance to antibiotics necessitate ongoing surveillance and tailored treatment strategies. In this study, the organism was most frequently isolated from pus samples, underscoring its role in SSTIs, surgical site infections, and abscesses.

The antimicrobial susceptibility profile demonstrated a high rate of resistance to Penicillin (91.98%), which is comparable to the finding of Duran et al.[8] and Bouchiat et al.[9] who reported resistance rates of 92.8% and 91.3%, respectively. The high level of resistance may be attributed to widespread production of beta-lactamase among staphylococcal isolates. In contrast, all isolates in the present study susceptible to vancomycin. Similar susceptibility pattern have been also reported by Datta et al.,[10] Vidhani et al.,[11] and Anupurba et al.[12] indicating its excellent activity against S. aureus.

The prevalence of MRSA in the present study was18.87%, which is lower than several recent Indian studies included in our comparison. Difference in MRSA prevalence across geographical regions may be influenced by variation in antimicrobial stewardship policies, infection control practices, healthcare setting and local antibiotic prescribing patterns. Isolation of more MRSA from wards and ICUs may be due to the presence of vulnerability of patient, higher frequency of medical procedures, prolonged hospital stay, and increased opportunities of transmission through healthcare workers. These strains exhibited complete resistance to Penicillin and Amoxicillin-clavulanate, while remaining fully susceptible to Vancomycin and nearly all to linezolid. This pattern reinforces the critical role of these agents as last-resort therapies and the need for their judicious use to prevent resistance development.

ICR was detected in 22.64% of all S. aureus isolates. Similar finding reported by Mahalakshmi et al.[14] and Pradhan et al.,[16] who observed iMLSB rate of 24.5% and 22.8%, respectively. ICR poses a hidden threat to treatment efficacy. These strains may appear clindamycin-sensitive in standard testing but can rapidly develop resistance during therapy, leading to clinical failure. Routine implementation of the D-test is essential to uncover this resistance phenotype, especially when clindamycin is considered for empirical use.

The study revealed a higher prevalence of ICR among MRSA isolates (30%) compared to MSSA (20.93%). Similar finding also reported in several recent Indian studies [Table 5]. These observations indicate that MRSA strains are more likely to harbor iMLSB determinants, which suggests a possible genetic linkage between methicillin resistance and erm-mediated clindamycin resistance. However, since molecular characterization was not performed in the present study, the underlying genetic mechanism could not be confirmed. The co-existence of these resistance traits significantly narrows therapeutic options and highlights the importance of comprehensive phenotypic and genotypic profiling.

The findings of this study support the routine use of D-test routinely to prevent therapeutic failure where clindamycin is frequently prescribed for SSTIs. The comparatively lower MRSA prevalence with significant proportion of ICR provides important local epidemiological data that may assist clinicians in selecting appropriate empirical therapy and guide antimicrobial stewardship initiatives.

Strengths of the study

The present study includes the considerable number of clinical isolates obtained from the diverse clinical samples. It provided comparative analysis of MRSA and MSSA isolates and specifically evaluated ICR using the D-test, thereby generating local antimicrobial resistance data relevant to clinical practice.

Limitations of the study

The study was conducted at a single tertiary care center over a 6-month period, which may limit the generalizability of the result to other healthcare settings. The present study did not include molecular characterization of resistance genes (e.g., ermA, ermC, and msrA); therefore, genotypic confirmation of inducible resistance could not be established. In addition, clinical exposure, prior antibiotic exposure, and patient comorbidities were not assessed, limiting the evaluation of the direct clinical impact of ICR.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that S. aureus remains an important pathogen in clinical infections, with MRSA accounting for 18.87% of isolates. ICR was detected in 22.64% of isolates and was more frequently observed among MRSA strains than MSSA strains. These findings emphasize the importance of performing the D-test in erythromycin-resistant and clindamycin-sensitive isolates to ensure accurate susceptibility reporting and appropriate antibiotic selection.

The continued susceptibility of isolates to vancomycin and linezolid indicates that these agents remain effective therapeutic options. The study provides valuable local epidemiological data regarding antimicrobial resistance patterns and ICR in the tertiary care setting. Further multicenter studies incorporating molecular characterization and clinical outcome assessment are required to better understand the epidemiology and clinical significance of ICR.
