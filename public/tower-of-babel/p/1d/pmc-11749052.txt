Extrafollicular Adenomatoid Odontogenic Tumors: A Series of Five Rare Cases with an Insight into Its Clinicopathological Aspects
Authors: Debarati Ray, Snehanjan Sarangi, Tathagata Bhattacharjee, Jay Gopal Ray
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Adenomatoid odontogenic tumors (AOT), first described by Steensland in 1905, are benign, slowly enlarging, nonaggressive, odontogenic epithelial neoplasms comprising 3%–7% of all odontogenic tumors. They tend to originate from the dental lamina remnants or the reduced enamel epithelium. Mutation at codon 12 of KRAS oncogene (Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog) plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis. AOTs demonstrate three clinicopathological spectrums, namely follicular, extrafollicular, and peripheral. The extrafollicular AOTs are hypothesized to originate from a stimulus triggering the dental lamina remnants. While intraosseous follicular AOTs are fairly common (70.8%), the extrafollicular counterparts are infrequent (24%), and novel entities that may mimic other lesions due to the absence of tooth inside. Herein, we elucidate a series of five unique extrafollicular AOT cases with a relevant emphasis on its pathogenesis and clinico-radiological aspects. This report aims to add another significant record to the literature pertaining to the compilation of extrafollicular AOTs.

Introduction

Adenomatoid odontogenic tumor (AOT) is a benign, odontogenic neoplasm, elucidated first by Steensland as epithelioma adamantinum.[12] Philipsen and Birn, in 1969, adopted the presently used term AOT, which was later used by the WHO.[3]

The possible origin of AOT includes dental lamina remnants, reduced enamel epithelium, or cell rests of Malassez. Most of the cases tend to occur in females, involving the maxillary canine region.[34]

Based on the location and origin, AOT shows three distinct clinicopathologic spectrums– intraosseous follicular (70.8%), intraosseous extra-follicular (24%), and peripheral (2.3%). The intraosseous follicular variant appears as a well-defined radiolucency involving the crown and partially the root of a tooth. The extrafollicular type is an intrabony radiolucent lesion without any intralesional tooth. The association of permanent cuspids accounts for 60% of all follicular types. Likewise, extrafollicular AOTs (89%) also show an affinity for the same anatomical site. Radiographically, the extrafollicular type is located in between or above the roots of erupted teeth. The peripheral variant is extraosseous and attached to the gingival tissues as sessile masses. It may cause superficial bony erosion.[3456]

Herein, we elucidate five cases of the rare extrafollicular variant with its relevant clinicopathological aspects.

Case Reports

Case 1

A 67-year-old-female complained of a hard swelling in the left upper anterior jaw region for the past 1 year. Intraorally, a nontender, buccal swelling was noted, along with missing 23. Orthopantomogram (OPG) showed a unilocular radiolucency. Histopathology revealed the presence of a monotonous population of cuboidal to columnar cells with hyperchromatic nuclei in the rosette and ductular pattern. Prominent cystifications with mineralization foci were noted [Figure 1].

(a) Intraoral view showing buccal swelling, in relation to 22, 23, and 24 regions, with missing 23, (b) Maxillary occlusal radiograph revealed cortical plate expansion with divergence of 22 and 24, (c) Orthopantomogram showed a well-circumscribed unilocular radiolucency in between 22 and 24, (d) H and E (×10 view) sections revealed the presence of a monotonous population of hyperchromatic cuboidal to columnar cells in rosette and ductular patterns jutting out toward the lumen of the cystic cavity

Case 2

A 22-year-old female with pain and swelling in the left upper jaw revealed a large, smooth surfaced, fluctuating swelling from 22 to 26 regions, causing bicortical expansion. The standard upper occlusal radiograph exhibited a unilocular radiolucency in the palatal region, causing a root deviation of 25. H and E stained sections showed odontogenic epithelium of spindle and cuboidal cells arranged in ductal and rosette pattern within the fibrous stroma [Figure 2].

(a) Extraoral swelling involving the left maxilla (white arrow) with facial asymmetry, (b) Intraoral view showing swelling from 22 to 26 regions, with bicortical expansion, (c) Standard upper occlusal radiograph exhibiting a unilocular radiolucency causing deviation of the regional tooth roots, (d) H and E (×10) stained sections showed odontogenic epithelium of spindle and cuboidal cells arranged in prominent rosette patterns within the fibrous stroma

Case 3

A 18-year-old female reported with mild discomfort involving the right maxillary anterior region. OPG revealed a small, fairly circumscribed radiolucency between the roots of 12 and 13, causing displacement. Incisional biopsy showed prominent rosette-like areas composed of spindle-shaped epithelial cells with intermittent calcification [Figure 3].

(a) Intraoral view showing anterior palatal swelling from 11 to 13 regions, (b) Orthopantomogram showed a small, fairly well-circumscribed radiolucency between the roots of 12 and 13, (c-e) H and E (×10) stained sections showing prominent rosette-like areas composed of spindle-shaped epithelial cells, microcystic spaces, and intermittent calcification foci

Case 4

A 16-year-old male reported with mandibular swelling in relation to the left premolar region. OPG exhibited a well-corticated radiolucency, possesing multiple patchy radiodensities, from 33 to 35 regions, with displaced 33, 34. Incisional biopsy showed odontogenic epithelial cells arrayed in ducts, cords, and rosettes, with calcific zones [Figure 4].

(a) Intraoral view showing swelling in left mandibular premolar region along with mild buccal cortical plate expansion, (b) Orthopantomogram exhibited a well-corticated radiolucency, with patchy radio densities and root divergence of 33, 34, (c) Axial computed tomography scan showing an expansile radiolucency with buccal cortical plate expansion, (d) H and E (×10) stained sections showed odontogenic epithelial cells arrayed in islands and rosette patterns with intermittent calcification

Case 5

A 16-year-old female reported with mild pain involving upper anterior teeth. OPG revealed a well-circumscribed sclerotic radiolucency with patchy calcific foci, and root divergence of 13, 14, 15. Histopathological evaluation revealed multiple duct-like structures and solid areas composed of hyperchromatic spindled to cuboidal odontogenic cells with multiple mineralized foci [Figure 5].

(a) Extraoral swelling causing obliteration of right nasolabial fold, (b) Intraoral swelling showing bicortical right maxillary expansion, (c) Fairly defined sclerotic radiolucency causing divergence of 13, 14, 15 along with patchy radio-opacity, (d) H and E stained section (×10) showing multiple duct-like structures, solid islands, and foci of calcification

All five cases were histopathologically confirmed as AOT and radiologically identified as its extrafollicular variant.

Discussion

AOT is a benign, noninvasive, slowly enlarging neoplasm, first elucidated by Steensland et al.[2] In 1915, Harbitz described it as “cystic adamantoma.” The WHO incorporated the term “Adenomatoid odontogenic tumor,” in 1971.[36] It has been interpreted as “adenomatoid odontogenic cyst” (AOC),[7] by many clinicians due to its variable cystic components. About 62.3% of intraosseous AOT variants involve the maxilla, whereas 35.3% occur in the mandible.[3]

AOT generally affects young women during the second to third decades, being associated with an unerupted tooth, mostly canine.[48] Here, all the cases of extrafollicular AOTs affected the females, complying with the conventional finding, except the fourth case, which affected the mandible of a young male. Strikingly, all the cases showed anatomical predilection for the canine-premolar region of the jaws.

Most AOTs are relatively asymptomatic. Larger growths can cause cortical expansions, tooth displacement, and root resorption.[46] Likewise, in the first and third cases, prominent buccal expansion was seen, while the second case showed pronounced bucco-palatal expansion. All five cases showed mild-to-severe degree of root divergence of the regional teeth.

Radiographically, multiple radiopaque flecks were noted in the third, fourth, and fifth cases.

Considering the radiographic interpretation of the intraosseous extrafollicular variant, Philipsen further subdivided them into four types (E1–E4), depending on the location of radiolucency. In the first type (E1), radiolucency is not related to tooth structures. In the second type (E2), radiolucency is interradicular in position, and tumor extension causes regional apical root divergence. The third type (E3) has radiolucency superimposing a root apex, and in the fourth type (E4), the radiolucency is superimposed at the midroot level.[3]

Interestingly, in this series, all five cases radiologically corroborated to the E2 variant. The first case featured replacement of 23 with the lesion; while the other four cases showed presence of all the complements of teeth, including canine.

Grossly, AOTs are encapsulated variably solid growths, with interspersed cystifications. Microscopically, AOTs show spindle-shaped, columnar, or cuboidal epithelial cells. They show glandular patterns, whorled masses or sheets of cells in a fibrous stroma. Occasional amyloid deposition might be evident. Scattered foci of mineralization might represent dysplastic dentinoid or cementoid.[479] Similar findings were noted in our instances, with cystic changes in the first case, very prominent rosette formation in the fourth case, and calcifications in the third, fourth, and fifth cases.

Extrafollicular AOTs need to be differentiated from lesions like central giant cell granuloma, which exhibits the presence of multinucleated giant cells histologically, early cemento-ossifying fibroma that shows prominent osteo-cementoid trabeculae, rosette and ductular pattern al periodontal cyst which is signified by luminal nodular concrescences in odontogenic cystic lining. Small odontogenic keratocyst and calcifying odontogenic cyst can be differentiated by the presence of basal columnar palisaded cells with superficial corrugated parakeratin layer and the presence of ghost cells in the cystic lining, respectively. Other differential diagnoses may include a radicular cyst, which is characteristically associated with a nonvital tooth with an arcading pattern histologically, dentigerous cyst, which typically shows an intracystic impacted tooth, and a thin 2–3 cell-layered odontogenic cystic lining. Lesions which fall under remote differential diagnoses include unicystic ameloblastoma, which shows ameloblastomatous lining, ameloblastic fibroma, which is associated with a primitive ectomesenchyme-like connective tissue, early ameloblastic fibro-odontoma, odontogenic fibroma, and calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor, which all shows odontogenic strands cords, variable calcifications, and Liesegang rings, respectively.[310]

Since AOTs are well-encapsulated growths, surgical enucleation is the treatment of choice, and recurrences are infrequent.[3]

Considering the pathogenesis, AOTs might originate from the residual enamel epithelium and grow as a cystic lesion or as a solid neoplastic mass, arising from the epithelial remnants in the gubernacular dentis. Recently, activation of MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, along with mutation at codon 12 of KRAS oncogene, has been hypothesized in the pathogenesis of AOT. The extrafollicular type of the AOT is thought to occur as a response to an unknown stimulus triggering the dental lamina residues outside the eruptive path.[68]

Extrafollicular AOTs are relatively rare entities and possess distinct clinico-radiological diagnostic features. Herein, we have elucidated five such unique instances, with an emphasis on the diagnostic aspects. However, further studies are needed to better understand their pathogenesis, behavior and biology.
