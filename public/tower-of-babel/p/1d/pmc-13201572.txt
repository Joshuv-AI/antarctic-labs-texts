Gelatin production from red tilapia skin fish using high-pressure processing and its characterisations
Authors: Irwandi Jaswir, Nurlina Yusof, Yuliana, Miftahul Khair, Minda Azhar, Rahadian Zainul, Widya Lestari, Ridar Hendri, Anton Komaini, Kazuo Miyashita, Ahmad Muhammad Salamatullah, Mustofa Ahda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGelatin is a key ingredient widely used in the food industry. In recent years, alternative sources of gelatin, such as fish skin, have been explored. However, traditional extraction methods are time-consuming and environmentally harmful. High-Pressure Processing (HPP) has emerged as a novel and eco-friendly approach for gelatin extraction. Using HPP can reduce processing time and chemical waste, while also enhancing the properties and yield of gelatin. Gelatin produced through HPP method yields 1.24 times more than the conventional method (yields: 32.1 ± 1.81% and 25.8 ± 1.24%, respectively). Besides, gelatin concentration of both methods is not different significanly. Moreover, the functional groups and protein composition of gelatin obtained by the conventional and HPP methods are identical. This study found that HPP produces gelatin with a spongy microstructure and excellent gel strength. Notably, gelatin extracted from red tilapia using HPP exhibited a 1.45-fold higher gel strength than that extracted by the conventional method (gel strength 184 g and 128 g, respectively). However, applying high pressure (350 MPa) reduces the amino acid content of the extracted gelatin. This study determined that a pressure of 250 MPa is optimal for producing gelatin with desirable characteristics using the HPP method. To achieve high-quality gelatin from fish skin, further optimisation of the HPP process is necessary.Keywords: Amino acid levels, Eco-friendly extraction, Fish gelatin, Gel strengthSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

The integrity of halal products extends throughout the supply chain, from farm to table, including the ingredients, processing, handling, and product distribution. Assurance of halal ingredient integrity is important because it is the first step in ensuring a product’s halal status. Malaysia leads the global halal industry1. The country possesses knowledge and capabilities in this area, and it provides training and guidance to other nations on the halal and haram issues2. Even though, International Islamic University Malaysia (IIUM) also contributes expertise and conducts halal food research3.

Currently, gelatin is the most commonly used food ingredient. Gelatin is an essential ingredient in the industry. The use gelatin for food industry and pharmaceutical industry increased significantly4. Rather et al.,5 reported that markets of gelatin will reach 5.0 billion USD in 2025 because the food and pharmaceutical industries also increase. Nonetheless, concerns about religion and health have prompted researchers to seek alternatives to gelatin derived from pigs and unslaughtered cattle. It leads to the discovery of the latest source of gelatin including fish skins, scales, and bones.

Fish gelatin has a longer production time and lower quality when compared to mammalian gelatin6. Since then, extensive research has been conducted to solve the deficiencies and improve the gelatin structure and properties. To produce one batch of fish protein, at least 24 h are required. Furthermore, fish gelatin has weaker properties than mammalian gelatin, such as lower gel strength and melting point5. These challenges minimize the application of fish gelatin in industries. Chemical treatment is frequently used to improve the properties of fish gelatin7, but residues may remain in the gelatin extract8. It is critical to develop a new procedure that reduces operation time while improving the properties of fish gelatin.

In this study, gels were extracted using high pressure processing (HPP). This technique has numerous advantages, including the ability to shorten processing times. Jaswir et al.9 successfully produced fish gelatin from various local fish. Until recently, several food products and ingredients prepared with HPP technology were commercialised, including milk, fruit jams, and fruit juices, as well as liposome nanoencapsulation10. However, the use of HPP as a new method for isolating active components from raw materials is growing11. The high-pressure technique was recently introduced as a novel method for extracting gelatin from fish skin. According to the literature, applying HPP to the process increased gelatin yield and improved gelatin protein structure12.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fishes were bought from market in Gombak, Selangor, Malaysia. They were washed, dissected and deboned while the skins were removed manually by sharp knives before being cut into small pieces (1 cm x 1 cm). The skins were kept in ~ 80 °C overnight and was dried using freeze dryer machine to remove excess of water. Three solutions, 0.2% NaOH (CAS: 1310-73-2), 0.2% sulphuric acid (CAS: 7664-93-9), and 1.0% citric acid (CAS: 77-92-9) were prepared and was kept in 4 °C for at least overnight. All the chemicals used were analytical grade.

High-pressure processing parameters

Gelatin extraction

Acid-based extraction

The extraction procedure was carried out according to Ahmed et al.,13 with slight modification. Fish skin was thawed and cleaned. For pre-treatment process, the skins were soaked in 0.2% (w/v) NaOH, 0.2% (v/v) sulfuric acid, and 1.0% (v/v) citric acid, respectively. Each soaking period lasted for 40 min at 4 °C. The skins were washed thoroughly using distilled water after every soaking. The ratio for the skin and all solution was 1:14. Later, treated fish skin was extracted in distilled water for 18 h at 45 °C.

HPP-based extraction

Procedure for gelatin extraction assisted by HPP in pre-treatment process and during extraction was performed according to Yusof et al.,14 with several adjustments. Fish skin was soaked in NaOH and acetic acid as the previous method. For swelling procedure, fish skin with citric acid were sealed in a polyethylene bag. The bag was placed inside the pressure chamber and the lid was closed. HPP test was run at various pressure and pressure holding time, followed by thermal extraction in distilled water as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Gelatin content

The biuret test was done according to Gornall et al.19. UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Brand: Biochrom, Model: LIBRA S12) were used to obtain the absorbance reading for each concentration at 540 nm wavelength.

Gel strength

Bloom test for gelatin was done according to GMIA20[ and British Standards Institution21 with some modification. Dissolved 7.50 ± 0.01 g of gelatin powder in 105 ± 0.2 mL of distilled water and stand still at room temperature for 30 min. The solution were dissolved in 50 mL beaker. Then, the beaker was placed in water bath at 60 °C for 10 ~ 15 min until it fully dissolved. The liquid gelatin was cooled down before and were kept at ~ 10 °C for 16 ~ 18 h for gel maturation. The gel strength was tested using texture analyzer (CT3 Pro Texture Analyzer) equipped with a load cell of 5 kg, cross-head speed 1 mm/s and equipped with a 0.5 inch (27 mm) in diameter, flat bottomed plunger. The sample beaker was placed centrally under the plunger and the penetration test was then performed. The maximum force (g) was determined when the probe proceeded to penetrate into the gel to a depth of 4 mm. The measurements were done in triplicate. Bloom value of gelatin treated with HPP were compared with gelatin from traditional acid base extraction.

Amino acid content

Amino acid composition was performed using a High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). For acid hydrolysis, 0.1 g of each sample was hydrolyzed with 5 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid (HCl) in a closed test tube and then kept in the oven for 24 h at 110 °C. The samples for cysteine analysis were oxidized with performic acid before hydrolysis.

SDS-PAGE analysis

Sample preparation

5 mg of dried gelatin were dissolved in 1 mL of distilled water at 60 °C. Once dissolved, the sample was removed from the water bath and left to cool at RT. Mixtures of 2x Laemmli sample buffer and 30% (v/v) Acrylamide/Bis solution were prepared with the ratio 19 to 1. After that, 100 µL of gelatin solution was mixed with 100 µL of Laemli buffer. The mixture was kept at -20 °C until further used.

SDS-page test

4% (v/v) stacking gel and 12% (v/v) resolving gel were prepared accordingly. 10 µL of marker and 15 µL of sample were loaded into the well. The SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis was run at 120 V for 70 min. Once finished, the gels were soaked in coomassie blue (CB) (0.1% (w/v) CB in 40% (v/v) methanol, 10% (v/v) acetic acid, and 50% (v/v) water) for 60 min. After that, the gel was washed with distilled water before soaked in the destaining solution several times until the gel to become clear. The bands on the gel were recorded.

Identification of functional groups from extracted gelatin

The structural changes in fish gelatin extracted using conventional and HPP methods were confirmed using attenuated total reflectance infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) (Shimadzu Prestige 21, Japan). The ATR-FTIR analysis was performed at 20 degrees Celsius, covering a range of 650–4000 cm− 1 with 32 scans22.

Morphological evaluation

Gelatin extracted using conventional and HPP methods was evaluated using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Phenom Pro X) as reported by Ahda et al.,23 with slight modifications. The morphology was observed at an acceleration voltage of 5.0 kV.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data is expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The statistical comparisons were carried out using ANOVA Post-hoc Tukey’s test (SPSS Version 20). The statistic test using p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Furthermore, gel analysis of extracted gelatin from red tilapia skins is shown in Table 2. Red tilapia was extracted using conventional and HPP methods, resulting in firm gels. Furthermore, the HPP method for extracting gelatin from fish skin results in a weak gel. It could indicate that the gelatin strength is of poor quality. Yields and gelatin concentration

Gelatin yield is an important factor in industry because it is proportional to industrial profit. Unfortunately, it was typically calculated by dividing the dry gelatin extract by the weight of wet fish skins. However, it is not very reliable because water content influences the entire extraction process (freezing, salting, scraping, draining, etc.)27. Therefore, this study calculates gelatin yield as the weight of dry gelatin divided by the total amount of dry matter in the skin, as proposed by Arnesen and Gildberg29.

Figure 2 demonstrates that the HPP method produced more yield and gelatin from red tilapia fish skin than the conventional method. In this case, conventional and HPP methods increase gelatin yield from red tilapia skin by 258 mg/g and 321 mg/g, respectively. Pressurization causes high-pressure-induced protein denaturation by destabilizing inter and intra-molecular bonds. Liqing et al.30 found that thermal extraction can boost gelatin yield. According to mass transfer theory, the rate of mass transfer is proportional to pressure/resistance, so higher pressure increases the amount of solvent that enters the cell membrane, allowing more acid to permeate. During HPP extraction, the pressure differential between the cell interior and the cell membrane exterior is so high that permeation occurs quickly31. However, grouper and threadfin bream yields are comparable in terms of gelatin content. It demonstrates that conventional and HPP methods have no discernible effect on gelatin yields in this case.

Furthermore, higher gelatin concentrations produced by the HPP method due to pressurization did not destroy the amino acid composition of the gelatin, but they did affect the amino acid composition and molecular weight distribution of the gelatin32. The gelatin arrangement was slightly altered during the HPP process, resulting in a higher concentration of gelatin protein extract. According to the findings of this study, HPP-assisted pretreatment has a significant impact on the yield and concentration of gelatin extracted from fish skin compared to conventional extraction. Sockalingam et al.33 state in their study that the pretreatment method has a significant effect on gelatin yields.

Functional groups of fish gelatin

The extracted gelatin from red tilapia fish skins using different methods has different gelation form. It was affected significantly by protein compositions of gelatin. Therefore, the identification of functional groups of gelatin using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy is essentially performed. This technique can also be used to determine the secondary structure of gelatin34. Generally, Gelatin has high peak regions in several wavenumbers such as 3600 –2700 cm− 1, 1900 –900 cm− 1, and 400–900 cm−1g representing regions 1, 2, and 3, respectively35. Gelatin protein consists of five amide bands, which were amide A, Amide B, Amide I, Amide II, Amide III, resulting from NH stretching vibration, asymmetry stretching CH, CO stretching, NH bending and CH stretching, respectively36. Spectra of gelatin showed the major peaks in the amide region. To compare the absorption intensity among spectra, the peak height of all amide bands were quantitatively measured and compared37. Gelatin extracted from poultry has several functional groups detected by the FTIR bands were at 3410–3448 cm− 1, 1635 cm− 1, 1527–334 cm− 1, and 1242–871 cm− 1 representing amide-A, amide-I, amide-II, and amide-III bands, respectively38. Furthermore, amide-B will be detected in wavenumbers around 3075 cm− 1.

Figure 3 reports the wavenumber of gelatin protein from fish skin that has been undergo pressurization produced similar peak to the gelatin extracted using traditional acid-base extraction. The functional groups and secondary structure of gelatin extracted from red tilapia skin assisted by HPP shows the presence of all important amide peaks in the amide region which were Amide A (3296.92), Amide B (2926.56), Amide I (1633.08), Amide II (1543.74) and Amide III (1235.95). The extracted gelatin using HPP pretreatment has higher amides compared to the conventional gelatin. It is indicating the relationship with increased molecular order and increased gel strength37.

FTIR spectrum of gelatin extracted from: (a) red tilapia fish skin using traditional acid-base extraction; (b) red tilapia fish skin assisted by HPP.

Amide I, with a wavenumber range of 1600 to 1700, exhibits a C = O stretching vibration that bonds with C-N, C-C-N, and N-H bending modes. The region of 1640 cm− 1 indicates C = O vibration or hydrogen binding with COO−39. The wavenumber peak in the amide I region is the most important data for analyzing the secondary structure of proteins and characterizing the properties of gelatin’s triple helix structure due to the presence of hydrogen bonding37. The wavenumber for gelatin extracted by HPP is 1633.08 cm− 1, similar to the study done by Mattulessy et al.40, who emphasized the peak at 1633 cm− 1 as characteristic of the structure of gelatin. Furthermore, the findings are similar to gelatin extracted from farm giant catfish, seabass, silver carp, and tilapia zilli41. On the other hand, the wavenumber of amide I for conventional gelatin is 1629.13 cm− 1, which is slightly lower than the gelatin obtained through the HPP procedure. High pressure affects protein denaturation, particularly the primary, secondary, and tertiary gelatin structures42. Furthermore, the higher peak on the graph indicated a greater loss of triple helix due to increased disruption of hydrogen bond interaction caused by high pressure35. As a result, mechanical properties can be influenced, as previously explained. Weng et al.43 found that extracting gelatin by denaturing triple helical collagen influenced its mechanical properties.

Gelatin extracted with HPP showed amide II bands at 1543.74 cm− 1. The presence of the amide II band is due to the combination of C-N stretching vibrations and NH deformation modes of the peptide group41. It is also indicative of changes in gelatin’s secondary structure, though this region is typically more sensitive to hydration than structural changes in protein37. The amide III region is responsible for the coil or disordered structure in proteins and is located between 1200 cm− 1 and 1300 cm− 1 wavenumber37, with the peak representing the combination of amino group and alpha carbon from amide linkages41. The amide III band of gelatin after HPP is slightly lower compared to conventional (1237.66 cm− 1), indicating that high pressure caused changes from an α-helical to an irregular arrangement of the protein structure, and was associated with loss of triple helix state during thermal extraction35.

Amide A band in the wavenumber range 3400–3440 cm− 1 associated with the N-H stretching vibration revealed the presence of hydrogen bonds38. Amide A band from this study measured 3296.92 cm-1. When a peptide’s N-H group participates in hydrogen bonding, the position shifts to a lower frequency41. The amide A of gelatin with HPP pretreatment had higher frequencies than commercial gelatin (3280.55 cm− 1), indicating that it contained fewer hydrogen bonds than untreated collagen due to the effect of ultrahigh-pressure on hydrogen bonds6. Amide B represented CH antisymmetric and symmetric stretching for -CH3 and -CH2 in aliphatic compounds and is typically found at wavenumber 2936 –2928 cm− 136. Aside from amide I, II, III, A, and B, the graph revealed a peak at wavenumber 1337.07 cm− 1 caused by stretching vibrations of -CH2 groups in proline acid residues of gelatin fragments44.

Because of intramolecular interactions between amino acid functional groups, gelatin has a generally smooth structure with hollow cells45,46. Another study discovered that when examined with a SEM at magnification of 50, gelatin structure resembled spongy pores47.

According to this study, the HPP method produces holes in gelatin structures, as shown in Fig. 4. However, gelatin’s microstructure is an important parameter because it describes the material’s physical characteristics. This finding is consistent with the gel strength, as gelatin treated with HPP demonstrated a high bloom strength. High pressure disrupted the covalent bond during the extraction process, influencing gel formation and gelation arrangements6,48. Gel strength, moisture content, and Ash content from red tilapia

Other characteristics of gelatin red tilapia fish skin including ash content, moisture content, and gel strength. However, the quality of gelatin produced also been determine by the ash content. Ash is an inorganic material remaining after complete oxidation (ignition) of organic compound in food materials50. Gelatin with ash less than 1% is acceptable for pharmaceutical application51, while maximum 2% ash (2 g/ 100) g can be accepted in food applications52. Gelatin shows the ash content results for gelatin produced with HPP treatment and traditional acid-base extraction process. The traditional acid-base extraction produces low ash contents of gelatin which is 1.16% whereas gelatin treated with HPP has 2.1%. This study reported that the extraction process influences the ash content of gelatin. Khirzin et al.53 also reported that the ash content depends on the origin of raw materials and the treatment process. From the results, the acid pretreatment can dissolve and remove unwanted minerals. However, Haug & Draget26 in their report explained that it is possible to have ash content up to 3% at this stage before applying the ion-exchanger process to remove access salt. Besides, to reduce ash contents of the extracted gelatin can be used the deionization method54. This is the reason why commercial gelatin has low ash content.

Table 3 shows the moisture content results for gelatin extract assisted by HPP and conventional methods. The moisture content of fish gelatin treated with HPP is 5.53% while 1.2% moisture was recorded for the standard. The new method produced higher moisture content as compared to conventional method. The differences observed could be due to the degree of drying, humidity during storage, and absorbency of moisture by packaging material55. Moisture content is important for longer shelf life. Bougatef et al.,52 stated that low moisture content presenting the efficient elimination of some macromolecules (fat and minerals) from the skin. However, the moisture content for both methods are acceptable because it was below the maximum acceptable moisture percentage of 15%54. High range of moisture for red tilapia skin gelatin was recorded by See et al.55 found that moisture for gelatin was 10.98% and another study reported that moisture content of fish gelatin which are 7.11%, and 7.51%, depending on the extraction pH41.

Furthermore, the evaluation gel strength of fish gelatin extracted using conventional method and HPP-treated method. Bloom for conventional method is 128 g, which is similar to study done by Jamilah & Harvinder56. In contrast, HPP method produces gel strength of 184 g bloom. It shows that the pressurization during pre-treatment has significant impact on the gel structure. Gel strength is one of the most important parameters to determine the quality of the gelatin57. Its value is affected by concentration, conditions of preparation, and the presence of other hydrocolloids8. Usually, the range for gelatin strength is between 100 and 300 g, depending on their sources, extraction method, and their amino acid content58. Higher bloom displays high gel strength and various bloom values applied for different functions. The strength of the gelatin structure is linked with the presence of α-chains, β components, and higher molecular weight molecules58. This study reported that gel strength for gelatin treated with HPP is higher as compared to those extracted using the conventional acid-base method. Liqing et al.24 in their works found that the gel strength of the pig gelatin increased from 320 g to 395 g after being treated with HPP due to high amount of subunit presence. However, HPP might increase the denaturation activity by disturbing the cross-link bond during pre-treatment. In addition, high pressure could promote molecular polymerization through coagulants of the protein component, which makes the content of the high molecular weight components of the gelatin increase30. Gel strength is not only used to determine the quality of the gelatin, it is also important to prepare gelatin for different purposes. Although the high of ash content of gelatin did not affect gel strength and transparency59.

Protein composition of red tilapia skin gelatin

Protein separation can be accomplished using Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate - Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). It is a molecular weight-based protein separation technique60. The higher band represents a higher molecular weight, while the lower band represents the opposite. Figure 5 depicts SDS-Page band images of gelatin treated with HPP and the conventional method, which produce comparable results. Gelatin treated with HPP and acid extraction methods showed three important fragments (β, α1, and α2), while commercial gelatin only showed a clear β band.

The result of protein separation using SDS-Page.

Native collagen protein is primarily in the form of a long linear triple-helix structure with three polypeptide chains linked together. It is composed of primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structural elements. During the extraction process, acid pre-treatment was used to weaken the chains’ bonds, allowing the collagen protein to be converted into a single peptide chain during thermal hydrolysis. Mild acid is sufficient to stabilize the cross-linkages found in the skin matrix of gelatin protein61. Furthermore, Zhang et al.6 found that HPP increased the molecular weight of extracted gelatin. Figure 4 shows that the high-pressure treatment produces a high molecular weight of protein in the gelatin (22 kDa) which is smaller molecular weight than the conventional method. The HPP produces more molecular weight of protein above 32 kDa. This material can be applied as food functional62 and a halal substitute in food packaging, preservation, and processing63.

Amino levels of gelatin from red tilapia

The physical properties of gelatin were significantly influenced by the amino acid types used to form polypeptide chains and extracellular matrix compositions. Wu et al.,57 state that they influence the behavior and physical properties of gelatin. Collagen contains more 18 different amino acids64, including glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline. Table 4 shows amino acid compositions for gelatin treated with various extraction methods. Gelatin treated with HPP has a total amino acid content of 601 mg/g, which is higher than the traditional acid base (535 mg/g). However, amino acids have a significant influence on gelatin gel strength65. A high concentration of amino acids resulted in a harder gel, which explains why HPP-treated gelatin has a higher gel strength (Table 3) than traditional acid-base gelatin. High pressure caused protein denaturation by disrupting secondary and tertiary bonds, resulting in an increase in the number of amino acids released6. Zhang et al.27 discovered that the number of proteins in basa fish skin remained constant after being subjected to high pressure, demonstrating that optimal pressure has no negative impact on protein structure.

Glycine has been identified as the most abundant amino acid in gelatin, accounting for 130 mg/g or 22% of total amino acid content. However, the glycine content of extracted gelatin from HPP is lower than in previous studies (Table 4). Therefore, gelatin with a high amino acid content has higher gel strength57. Furthermore, the hardness of gelatin is strongly associated with proline and hydroxyproline. A high concentration of both amino acids results in a high bloom strength of the gelatin66. The proline value for HPP-treated gelatin was 73.45 mg/g. Whereas no proline component was detected from previous study67. Therefore, gel strength for HPP-treated gelatin increased significantly when compared to traditional acid-base gelatin and the reference (Table 4).

The effects of high pressure on amino acid content in gelatin have also been investigated. The increase in pressure during the extraction process also reduces glycine levels and total amino acid contents. Total amino acid for gelatin treated at 250 MPa was 601.60 mg/g, whereas only 546.08 mg/g was recorded for the sample treated at 350 MPa. Furthermore, all individual amino acids, including glycine, proline, cysteine, and glutamine, decreased as pressure increased. Gamlath and Wakeling68 stated in their report that excessive pressure can cause damage to the amino acid structure, resulting in a reduction in total amino acids. Thus, the amino acid compositions (mg/g) of gelatin extracted from red tilapia skin decreased, assisted by high pressure (350 MPa) of HPP method. A previous study by Kim et al.69 found that the superheated steam method produced gelatin with low gel strength and melt point. It indicates that the amino acid reduction observed in this study may have an effect on the physical properties of gelatin, including gel strength.

Conclusions

Gelatin extraction using an HPP machine has shown promising potential for future research. Overall, the HPP method is effective as a pre-treatment, as it expands the skin’s capillaries and enables more efficient gelatin penetration and extraction. The results of this study indicate that red tilapia skin is an excellent source for gelatin production due to its high yield and gelatin concentration. Additionally, the HPP method produces gelatin with high gel strength, which is important for many industrial applications. However, this study also found that applying high pressure (350 MPa) can reduce the amino acid content of gelatin extracted from red tilapia skin.
