Pressure Injury Incidence and Risk Factors in Neonates Undergoing Surgical Intervention: A Prospective Study
Authors: Aynur Kocakap, Gülten Sucu Dag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aim of this study is to determine the incidence of pressure injuries (PI) and risk factors in neonatal patients followed up in the intensive care unit undergoing surgical intervention. Neonates are recognised as a patient population at high risk of pressure injury. Although the incidence of PI in hospitalised neonates is high, epidemiological studies on postoperative PI and affecting factors in hospitalised infants are scarce. A prospective, descriptive study. This study report follows the STROBE checklist. This study was conducted with 105 patients who received postoperative care in the neonatal intensive care unit of the gynaecology and obstetrics hospital of a province in XXX between November 2023 and January 2024. The Neonatal Descriptive Characteristics Form, Neonatal Q Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment Scale, and the NPUAP Pressure Injury Classification System were used to collect data. The mean Neonatal Q Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment Scale risk score of patients included in the research was 18.42, and 87% of those who developed PI had a risk of skin disorders. 21.9% of the neonatal patients developed PI, and 14.3% of them had Stage II PI. The majority of PI developed in the back region, and the rate of pressure injury was higher in those who underwent cardiopulmonary surgery longer than 3 h. It was determined that the use of medical equipment such as a central venous catheter, urinary catheter, drainage tube, and vasoactive drugs affected the rate of postoperative pressure injury development in neonates. The neonatals admitted in intensive care unit undergoing surgery suffered PIs. In the case of intensive care units, the incidence is even higher. The risk increases with cardiopulmonary surgery while the presence of medical devices is the main risk factor.Keywords: intensive care, neonates, pressure injury, risk factors, surgical intervention

Introduction

Pressure injury (PI) is defined as localised damage to the skin and/or underlying tissue as a result of pressure or pressure in combination with shear [1]. Pressure injuries (PI) constitute a health problem that prolongs hospitalisation, increases the risk of various complications, and increases mortality, morbidity and costs during the treatment process [2, 3].

In the latest guidelines for the prevention and treatment of PI, neonates and children are recognised as a patient population at high risk of pressure injury [1]. In studies that have included paediatric and neonatal patients, the incidence of pressure injury has been found to vary between 1.6% and 48% [4, 5, 6, 7]. In a study in which pressure injury and risk factors in paediatric and neonates were examined, it was found that the prevalence was 3.4%, that at least two of the patients used medical treatment devices for more than 4 h, and that this was an important risk factor leading to the development of pressure injury. In the same study, it was reported that repositioning every two to 4 h had a 13‐fold effect in preventing pressure injury [5]. However, in a study conducted in China, a high incidence of neonatal PI of up to 16.1% was reported [8].

Neonates have some specific risk factors for pressure injury in addition to those for an adult patient. These additional risk factors include skin maturity, perfusion and oxygenation status, presence of medical equipment, disease severity, decreased neurologic response, humidity, decreased movement and length of stay in intensive care units [4, 9, 10]. In neonates, the risk factors associated with the development of PI are higher due to their characteristics. The most important risk factors for pressure injury in neonates are therapeutic and diagnostic device use, which has an incidence of pressure injury of 50%–90% [11]. Factors such as the presence of an endotracheal tube, use of noninvasive mechanical ventilation, hypotension, hypoxemia, prolonged stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), low birth weight (< 2500 g) and preterm delivery (< 37 weeks GA) also increase the risk [4, 5, 12].

Pressure injury is common in childhood and is more likely to occur in the epidermis and dermis layers, especially in the neonatal period ([13, 14, 15]). The anatomical region of PI in children and newborns differs from those in adults, and occipital PI, which are less common in adults, are seen at a higher rate in this patient group [16]. In neonates, the most common body regions in which pressure injury is seen are the occiput (16.7%), sacrum (15.6%), ear (9.9%), heel (9.4%), ankle (7.5%), nose (7.3%) and ischial tuberosities (5.3%) [4, 17].

Although the incidence and prevalence of PI in hospitalised neonates is high, specific studies in this population are limited and studies on the incidence of postoperative PI in neonates are very limited [18, 19, 20]. Therefore, the level of evidence for recommendations for the prevention and treatment of PI is also low [21, 22]. Since the anatomical and physiological characteristics of neonates are different from those of children and adults and surgical intervention is an important risk factor for pressure injury, there is a need for studies that will contribute to the adaptation of recommendations for prevention in the neonatal population [4]. Since studies on the development rates and risk factors of pressure injury in neonates after surgical intervention are limited in our country, it is thought that this study will provide scientific data for future studies on this population, establish care protocols for neonates by determining the risk factors, and contribute to raising awareness and planning interventions in nurses about the risk factors of pressure injury in neonates undergoing surgical intervention.

Design and Methods

Design

This was a prospective descriptive study.

Study Setting

This study was carried out in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit of a state hospital. The clinic has 72 incubators with a capacity of 45 patients in the third stage and 27 patients in the second stage. The general neonatal intensive care unit includes an isolation room, sepsis room, room for premature patients, ventilator room and room for cardiovascular patients.

Sampling

The study population consisted of newborns who received care after surgical intervention in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit of an Obstetrics and Gynaecology Hospital in XXX. According to hospital data, the number of these patients was 10–20 patients per month in 2020 and the total number of patients per year was 150. The study sample was determined using the sampling method for a known population, and the sample was determined as 108 patients with a confidence level of 95% and a confidence interval of 5% by taking a population of 150 patients. A total of 105 patients were included in the study. Three patients were excluded because their parents refused to participate in the study.

Inclusion Criteria

Neonates aged between 0 and 28 days.

Neonates undergoing surgical intervention.

Neonates without a previous and current PI.

Neonates who were hospitalised for at least 72 h after surgery [23].

Data Collection Tools

Neonatal Descriptive Characteristics Form

This form was prepared by the researchers based on the literature regarding the descriptive and clinical characteristics of neonates and risk factors for pressure injury [4, 17]. Descriptive characteristics consist of questions regarding neonatal age (days), gender, weight, gestational week and birth weight. Clinical characteristics and Pressure Injury Risk Factors include the reason for neonatal hospitalisation, the area where the neonate was monitored (general intensive care, cardiovascular intensive care), surgical intervention, type and duration of anaesthesia, use of vasoconstrictor drugs, use of vasopressor drugs, intraoperative hypotension status, blood transfusion status, postoperative respiratory support, medical tools used (oxygen saturation probe, ECG, IV cannula, NG tube, endotracheal tube, PEG, CPAP mask, tracheostomy, chest tube), intervals of postoperative positions given to the patient, type of nutrition, length of hospital stay, pressure injury development status and pressure injury development time.

Neonatal Q Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment Scale (NBQPRA)

In this study, the risk of pressure injury in neonates was evaluated using the NBQPRA. This scale was chosen because it has better sensitivity and specificity than the Braden QD scale [15] and the Turkish validity and reliability of the NBQPRA has been established [24].

The Neonatal Q Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment (NBQPRA) Scale for Newborns was developed by Wacek and Ecklund [25]. Its development was based on the Braden Q Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment Scale for adults and the Modified Braden Q Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment Scale for paediatric patients. The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale were established by Baltacı et al. [24] and the Cronbach alpha coefficient was found to be 0.896 [24]. In this study, the Cronbach alpha coefficient of NBQPRA was 0.71. In NBQPRA, there are 32 parameters, including eight parameters: gestational week, mobility, activity, sensory perception, moisture, friction‐shear, nutrition, and tissue perfusion/oxygenation and four sub‐parameters under each parameter. Each parameter is scored from 1 to 4, and the minimum and maximum total scores are 8 and 32, respectively. In the evaluation of NBQPRA, a total scale score of < 20 indicates a Skin Disorder Risk [24].

Pressure Injury Follow‐Up and Classification Form

The International NPUAP Pressure Injury Classification System was used for staging PI [26]. The site(s) of pressure injury in the patients were also recorded.

Data Collection

In the collection of research data, the data in the Descriptive and Clinical Characteristics Form and intraoperative data were collected from the patients' files. The risk of pressure injury in operated patients was determined using NBQPRA after the patient was stabilised postoperatively. According to the Association of Perioperative Registered Nurses (AORN), surgery‐related PIs develop in the first 48–72 postoperative hours therefore the study follow‐up period ended after 72 h ([23]. Patients were followed up for the development of pressure injury with the Pressure Injury Follow‐up and Classification Form. Data collection and follow‐up of newborns were performed by the first researcher.

Ethical Consideration

For the ethical dimension of the study, written approval was taken from the ethics committee of the university (ET00‐2022‐0024) and written consent was received from the parents of the neonates included in the study using the Informed Voluntary Consent Form.

Data Analysis

The data obtained in the study were analysed in SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) for Windows 25.0 program. Descriptive statistical methods (number, percentage, min–max values, mean and standard deviation) were used for data analysis. The data were tested for conformity to normal distribution. Conformity to normal distribution can be examined by Q–Q Plots [27]. In addition, the normal distribution of data used depends on the skewness and kurtosis values of ±3 [28]. For the comparison of non‐normally distributed quantitative data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for the difference between two independent groups. Chi‐square analysis was used to test the relationship between categorical variables.

Factors Affecting Pressure Injury in Neonates

In the comparison of the descriptive characteristics of neonates according to the development of PI, it was found that there was a statistically significant difference between the type of ventilation and neonatal PI (p < 0.05) (Table 1). The difference in the Neonatal Braden Q Scale classification according to the development of PI was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Pressure injury developed in 87% of neonates with skin disorder risk and in 13% of those without skin disorder risk. The relationship between gestational age, gender, clinic of hospitalisation, chronic diseases and feeding type and the development of PI was statistically not significant (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Among the characteristics related to the intraoperative period, it was found that 56.3% of patients who underwent cardiopulmonary surgery and whose operation time was > 181 min developed PI (p < 0.05) (Table 2). When some postoperative clinical characteristics of neonates were compared according to the development of PI, the difference between PI in neonates with a central venous catheter, urinary catheter, and drainage tube, and those who used vasoactive drugs postoperatively was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

The incidence of PI associated with medical devices was assessed. PI developed in 27.1% (n = 23) of patients with a central venous catheter, 30.8% (n = 16) of patients with a drainage tube, 31.4% (n = 16) of patients with a urinary catheter and 30.4% (n = 21) of patients receiving noninvasive mechanical ventilation.

Discussion

In this study, the incidence of pressure injury in patients undergoing surgical intervention in the neonatal intensive care unit and the affecting factors were determined.

Numerous factors increase the risk of developing PI in hospitalised neonates. Prevention of hospital‐acquired PI in this patient population requires early risk identification [25]. It is necessary to identify risk factors based on a comprehensive clinical examination using risk assessment tools to guide nursing interventions in the prevention of PI [29]. Risk classification will guide nursing care in planning and implementing nursing interventions for the prevention of PI.

In this study, it was determined that 69.1% of the neonates had a risk of pressure injury. The incidence of PI was 21.9% and the rate of stage II pressure injury was 14.3%. It was observed that the results of this study were similar to the literature. In the literature, the incidence of pressure injury in neonatal intensive care has been reported to range from 2.3% [5] to 23% [30, 31, 32]. In the study conducted by de Lima et al. [31] in Brazil to test the validity and reliability of the Neonatal Braden Q risk scale, the incidence of pressure injury was found to be 13% [31]. In the study conducted by Kulik et al. [20] in patients aged 39 weeks to 21 weeks who underwent paediatric cardiac surgery and were monitored in the intensive care unit, it was reported that 70% of PI were caused by medical tools and equipment, that 30% were caused by immobility, that 40% of PI were Stage I, and that 26.7% were Stage 2 [20]. In the study conducted by Curley et al. [18] on paediatric intensive care patients with a diagnosis of congenital heart disease who were aged between 21 days and 8 years, it was determined that 27% of the patients developed PI, that 70% of the PI were Stage I, and that 27% were Stage II [18]. In another study conducted by Curley et al. [19] on intensive care unit patients undergoing paediatric cardiac surgery, it was determined that 2% of patients developed PI due to immobility [19].

Neonates have very little subcutaneous fat until a gestational age of 32 weeks and therefore PI can occur anywhere on the body [33]. In critical, immobile and sedated neonates, PI can often be seen in the occiput and ears [34]. In the study conducted by Marufu et al. [5] on the incidence of PI in neonatal intensive care units, it was found that PI were seen in the nasal septum and tracheostomy area [5]. Curley et al. [19] reported PI to the head, chest, and sacrum in patients due to immobility in intensive care patients undergoing paediatric cardiac surgery [19]. In the literature, it has been observed in the facial region in general [5, 19, 34]. In this study, it was found that PI were mostly seen in the back region (19%). It was observed that pressure injury developed in the shoulder and scapula as well as the back region.

Although there are studies on pressure injury and the factors affecting its development in neonatal and paediatric patients in the literature, studies on the risk factors of pressure injury in neonates in the postoperative period are very limited [18, 19, 20]. Considering that the studies have been mostly conducted in neonates who underwent cardiac surgical interventions, it is thought that this study will contribute to the literature by including other surgical interventions. In this study, a predominance of PI in the back region was noted, which may be explained by the fact that 69.6% of the patients who developed PI had cardiopulmonary surgery and 56.3% of the patients who developed PI and underwent cardiopulmonary surgery had an operation time of more than 3 h. In the quality improvement study conducted by Ciprandi et al. [35] to prevent intraoperative PI, surgeries lasting 1 or ½ h in patients with comorbidities such as prematurity, low birth weight, and skin disorder and surgeries lasting > 3 h in patients aged 0–3 years were considered as criteria for prolonged surgical intervention [35]. As a result, it can be stated that at least the duration of surgery is important in the postoperative pressure injury in neonates and it is mostly seen in the back region due to operation time and prolonged immobility under anaesthesia.

It was remarkable that 10.5% of PI in this study developed on postoperative day 0. It was observed that the majority of neonates who underwent cardiopulmonary surgery developed PI in the early postoperative period on postoperative day 0. This was thought to be due to the lack of protective skin care measures among the primary care practices in neonatal intensive care units in the early postoperative period. At the same time, the risk of pressure injury increases in surgical patients due to prolonged immobility under anaesthesia and time spent in the operating room [36].

On the other hand, PI may not be detected because the position of the child cannot be changed during surgery, as well as prolonged surgeries and postoperative skin controls cannot be performed due to the protection of the sterile site [35]. In cardiac surgery patients, specific risk factors include postoperative use of vasopressors [29, 37]. In some studies, it has been reported that vasoactive drug use is among the risk factors for the development of pressure injury in neonates as in adults [4, 18, 38, 39]. In addition, the administration of vasoactive drugs in patients undergoing cardiac surgery and monitored in the intensive care unit is one of the factors that increase the risk of PI [37]. In this study, more than half of the patients underwent cardiopulmonary surgery and were monitored in the intensive care unit, which explains that vasoactive drug use is an important risk factor for developing pressure injury.

Evidence has shown that medical device‐associated PI are a growing health concern. PI caused by medical devices are iatrogenic, meaning that they are caused by treatment and can worsen due to lack of assessment and care [40]. It has been reported that PI in neonatal intensive care patients are mostly associated with medical equipment [4]. In studies conducted in the neonatal intensive care unit, it has been reported that 79.6% of PI developed due to medical tools and equipment [16, 18]. In the study conducted by García Molina et al. [4] in the neonatal intensive care unit, it was found that the use of non‐invasive mechanical ventilation was one of the main causes of PI. In the study of Stellar et al. [41], in which pressure injury due to medical equipment in neonates was examined, it was determined that respiratory support devices were the most common factor causing PI, followed by immobilisers, gastric tubes, and monitors [41]. In this study, it was found that the rate of pressure injury was higher in patients who were on non‐invasive mechanical ventilation, who had central venous catheter, urinary catheter, and drainage tube compared to those who were not on non‐invasive mechanical ventilation and there was a statistically significant difference. This finding was found to be consistent with the literature. The awareness of nurses that medical equipment is an important risk factor for pressure injury in neonates and adopting appropriate measures may be an important intervention to reduce the incidence of PI.

Risk factors for PI after surgical intervention in neonates and children have not been defined as in adults. However, according to the pressure injury prevention and management guideline of the Royal Children's Hospital, risk factors in patients undergoing surgical intervention include the duration of the surgical intervention, hypotension during surgery, hypothermia, limited mobility on the first postoperative day, and prolonged placement of medical equipment after surgery [39]. In this study, some operative and postoperative characteristics were compared with the development of pressure injury, and no statistically significant difference was detected except for postoperative use of vasoactive drugs and length of intensive care stay.

Study Limitations

Our study has limitations to consider. The study was conducted in the neonatal intensive care unit of the gynaecology and obstetrics hospital of a province in XXX so that the findings cannot be generalizable. The study did not assess the use of preventive measures in the operating room or later in the ICU and how this could have influenced the results of the study.

Conclusion

In this study, the Neonatal Braden Q Pressure Injury Risk score was 18.42 in patients undergoing surgical intervention in the neonatal intensive care unit, and 87% of those who developed PI were at risk of skin disorders. It was found that 21.9% of the neonates developed PI and 14.3% had stage II PI. It was also determined that the majority of PI developed in the back region and that the rate of pressure injury was higher in those who underwent cardiopulmonary surgery for more than 3 h. The rate of developing pressure injury postoperatively in neonates was found to be affected by non‐invasive mechanical ventilation, medical equipment such as central venous catheter, urinary catheter, and drainage tube and the use of vasoactive drugs.

According to these results, it is a priority for nurses and other intensive care health professionals who provide postoperative care to neonates undergoing surgical intervention to develop and implement preventive care strategies regarding the characteristics and risk factors of PI and accurate risk assessment for the prevention of PI. In clinical practice, it is recommended that nurses increase awareness and develop clinical protocols for the implementation of preventive interventions starting from the intraoperative period and continuing during the stay in the intensive care unit, considering that the risk of pressure injury is high in neonates undergoing cardiopulmonary surgery and those undergoing cardiopulmonary surgery for more than 3 h. Considering that the rate of PI due to medical tools and equipment during intensive care unit follow‐up in neonates undergoing surgical intervention is high, similar to other intensive care patients, it is recommended that prevention of PI due to medical devices and equipment in neonates be among the essential priority care practices.
