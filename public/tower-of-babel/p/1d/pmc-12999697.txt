Patient and Physician Experiences in Immune Thrombocytopenia
Authors: Andrew Hsu, Tammy Fassett, Steve Pallagi, Sandhya R Panch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is a rare autoimmune disease that results in low platelet counts and an increased risk of spontaneous bleeding due to impaired blood clotting. Several therapeutic approaches can be used to treat patients with ITP. However, many patients either lose response in the long term or are unable to maintain a response after treatment discontinuation, necessitating chronic treatment and multiple lines of therapy. Here, two patients with ITP share their experience, each providing a firsthand description of their ITP diagnosis, symptoms, management, and perspectives on the future. These stories are complemented by a clinical review of ITP pathophysiology, symptoms, and treatments presented by two expert hematologists who care for patients with ITP. The physician perspective reinforces the challenges faced by patients in everyday life and highlights the remaining areas of concern regarding the treatment of chronic ITP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03438-7.Keywords: Chronic ITP; Health-related quality of life; Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP); Patient perspective, refractory ITP

Key Summary Points

The objective of this article is to describe two patients’ experiences with chronic immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and provide a physician perspective to contextualize these patient experiences.This article was co-authored by two individuals with ITP and two hematologists who care for patients with ITP.Each patient describes their journey to diagnosis, their experience with trying many different treatments while trying to manage their disease symptoms, and the impact of ITP on their personal and professional lives.The physician perspective is incorporated to contextualize the patient experience and to provide a clinical review of ITP pathophysiology, presentation, management guidelines, and emerging treatments.Both the patient and physician perspectives emphasize the importance of health-related quality of life and the need for new treatments that offer long-term remission for patients with chronic ITP.

My Journey with Immune Thrombocytopenia: Tammy Fassett

Diagnosis and First-Line Treatment

My experience with immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) began in July 2006 after a blood transfusion following a hysterectomy. A week later, I developed petechiae and had a huge bruise on my back from sitting in a chair, and my mouth was black with blood blisters. At the emergency room, my platelet count was undetectable, and I was diagnosed with ITP. While at the hospital, it was discovered that I have platelets that are negative for certain antigens. This is extremely rare and not routinely tested. During my initial blood transfusion, I unknowingly received blood that was not matched for this rare antigen type, causing my body to start attacking my platelets. My doctors and I believe this condition caused post-transfusion purpura, which may have triggered my ITP.

I was rushed to the intensive care unit (ICU) and given intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), prednisone, additional blood transfusions, and platelet transfusions. The hospital had a few people who could donate matched platelets, so they came in, but it was going to take too much time; in the short-term, I had to get more unmatched platelets. My hematocrit was 12%, and I developed a life-threatening lung infection. Thankfully, I recovered and was released from the ICU about 5 days later.

At home, I received prednisone 40 mg and had an appointment with a local hematologist. I didn’t fully understand my diagnosis of newly discovered ITP, and my main concern was function at my job as a second-grade teacher when I could barely walk and needed help with the stairs. I stayed on prednisone 40 mg from August 2006 until January 2007. I put on lots of weight and had “moon face.” I got mad easily, did not sleep well, and felt bad for my students. I was able to wean off prednisone and spent a short period without any treatment before restarting treatment again due to low platelet counts (Fig. 1A).

Second-Line Treatments

After prednisone, I was treated with rituximab, which gave me 6- to 18-month remissions but would cause fatigue, hair loss, and nausea. It took me a month to recover from each infusion.

After rituximab, I was given mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and was happy it was an oral medication, but had to adjust to its strict dosing schedule of twice a day without food. After 2 years, I was forced to wean off MMF because of safety concerns.

I next tried eltrombopag (oral), which controlled my ITP and kept my platelet counts at a safe level for 2 years; however, the side effects of muscle weakness and headaches were intense. After eltrombopag, I tried romiplostim (injection) for 1.5 years. My platelet counts would sometimes drop into single digits and I was getting headaches after each injection, so it didn’t seem worthwhile to stay on a medication that didn’t stabilize my platelets.

Other treatments included dapsone (caused anemia), sirolimus (no platelet response), fostamatinib (intense side effects and failed to control platelet counts), avatrombopag (initially denied by insurance), and a clinical trial (ineffective). I used WinRho as a rescue therapy and to bridge time between treatments but now avoid it because of its impact on reducing red blood cells to low levels. I only use WinRho now when needed (Fig. 1A).

I am thankful to have had excellent doctors who always considered my opinion when recommending treatments. It’s a delicate balance between platelet counts, symptoms, and treatment side effects. Eventually, my doctor was able to get my insurance to approve avatrombopag, which I have been on for the last 5 years (Fig. 1A). I tend to bruise easily but the side effects are minimal. My platelet levels are stable and under control. It’s been a relatively peaceful 5 years regarding my ITP. This summer was the first time in years my legs weren’t covered in bruises, and I could wear shorts without looking like I was in a fight!

The Impact of ITP and ITP Treatment

I’ve been taking prednisone almost continuously since I was diagnosed with ITP (Fig. 1A). I’ve been on a low dose (≈5 mg), but it still causes significant problems. I have osteopenia and have had cataract surgery and problems with my thyroid medication. Prednisone improves my platelet counts but at the expense of my overall health.

Currently, the side effects from prednisone bother me more than my ITP symptoms. Even when my platelet counts are high, osteopenia limits my activities. I no longer ride my bike or motorcycles with my husband, and I have stopped playing softball for fear of getting hit by the ball. I avoid going outside if it is icy. I know I’m not young, but most people in their mid-50s can do these things.

I increase the prednisone only when needed to get through vaccinations and illnesses. I’m very cautious to avoid getting sick. I would love to stop taking the prednisone, but no single medication alone has ever improved my platelet count. Ideally, I would like it if my platelets were 90 to 100 × 109/l because that is the range where brain surgery is safe. I would like to know if I could undergo brain surgery if needed.

I usually have fatigue. It’s hard to know if the fatigue is from the ITP, my thyroid medication, or the prednisone. On days with bad fatigue, I use up all my energy at work and have nothing left for when I get home. I have reduced foods that cause inflammation (i.e., most meat), which helps increase my energy levels. I can tell when my platelet levels start to drop because I bruise more easily, and I get blood blisters in my mouth from eating harder foods like pizza crust or crackers.

In recent years, I’ve started to change my mindset. Focusing on all the things I’ve lost was difficult and depressing. Instead, I’ve started asking myself, “What can I do?” I can’t play softball, but I can play golf with my friends. I can’t ride a motorcycle with my husband, but we can go off-roading in the Jeep. I listen to my body and rest when needed. If I’m feeling well, I get outside and do things!

Challenges and Future Perspectives

Throughout my journey with ITP, the Platelet Disorder Support Association (PDSA) has been an invaluable resource where I receive almost all my updated information on ITP. The PDSA has regional meetings and a conference. Having a community of people who understand ITP is incredible.

I’ve learned through the PDSA that not everyone has the same access to care. There is a need for more healthcare provider education on ITP. There are some doctors still recommending early splenectomy. I also feel insurance companies shouldn’t dictate access to medication. Many doctors don’t have time to submit letters to insurance companies just so their patients can switch to approved medication.

My Journey With Immune Thrombocytopenia: Steve Pallagi

Diagnosis and First-Line Treatment

At the end of 2016, I was in my best shape since college. After being diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, I committed to improving my health—I worked out, changed my diet, lost weight, and eventually stopped all of my medications. I looked and felt great! At the time, I owned a successful business that provided onsite drug testing services to businesses, corporations, and professional athletes.

In June 2017, I noticed petechiae on my arm while moving my daughter from college. I didn’t think much of it since it didn’t hurt or itch. In preparation for my annual physical, I had a blood draw. That afternoon, my doctor called me and told me to go to the nearest hospital as my life was in immediate danger. My platelet count was 9 × 109/l. I was admitted to my local hospital later that afternoon, and my platelet count dropped to 7 × 109/l. Looking back, I believe a live, first-generation shingles vaccine I received in 2017 had something to do with my low platelet counts and may have triggered my ITP. However, at the time, the reason for my low platelet counts was unknown.

Diagnosis of ITP is a process of elimination. They test you for everything and may start treatment before a diagnosis is made. In the hospital, they gave me a platelet transfusion and IVIG, but my platelet count was not stable and rarely > 20 × 109/l. I convinced my hematologist to release me from the hospital after a few days so I could go back to work to support my family.

Over the next 3 months, we tried many different tests and therapies, including weekly platelet transfusions and IVIG. Steroids and rituximab had little to no effect on my platelet count. I had a low tolerance for rituximab, so the infusions had to be slow and required constant monitoring by a nurse. I also developed an adverse reaction to rituximab, where my heart rate would go from normal to > 200 bpm instantaneously for unknown reasons. My cardiologist determined this was the result of a drug-drug interaction. During this time, I went to an oncology center 3 to 4 days a week to receive platelet transfusions and IVIG. With the infusions, my platelet count would rise to the high teens but within 1 to 2 days dropped back down to < 10 × 109/l.

On my diagnostic journey, I received a bone marrow biopsy. It was the most uncomfortable process I’ve ever gone through, worse than a root canal, and was the last test they did to eliminate the possibility of a leukemia-related disease. After this test, I was finally diagnosed with ITP, as it was the only disease left on the list.

When treatments failed, my hematologist recommended a splenectomy. I did not support this, believing that my spleen was functional and a splenectomy was premature; I did more research and found that splenectomy generally wasn’t recommended for patients this early in their disease progression. I turned to the PDSA for advice and spoke with the local chapter president who referred me to a hematologist who specialized in ITP. My new hematologist was fantastic and was also not in favor of splenectomy for my situation (Fig. 1B).

Second-Line Treatments

Under the guidance of my new hematologist, I started treatment with eltrombopag. Eltrombopag controlled my ITP, although my platelet count would crash every few months, requiring IVIG. It was only after my platelet count recovered to > 50 × 109/l that IVIG infusions became effective, which would increase my count to > 60 × 109/l within days. At the same time, my dosage of eltrombopag would be increased. During the 2 years I was receiving eltrombopag, I experienced many side effects from the medications I was taking; I was back on therapy for type 2 diabetes, I gained weight, I experienced hair loss, I developed cataracts, and a fatty mass grew in my chest that had to be surgically removed.

In June 2019, I was feeling “off” and could tell my platelet levels were low. I had petechiae and blood blisters in my mouth and felt very fatigued. I sent an email to my hematologist, explaining my symptoms and was advised to come in immediately. Upon arriving at my doctor’s office, I was tested, and my platelet count was 1 × 109/l. I was hemorrhaging internally, with bleeding into my eyes and bruising all over my body. They gave me emergency IVIG as soon as I arrived. The staff stayed until 10:00 pm to complete the infusion. I was then transported to the hospital, where I received additional IVIG infusions. By the third day of care, my platelet count had reached 50 × 109/l.

It was determined that the eltrombopag was no longer effective. My doctor wanted me to switch to avatrombopag; however, my insurance company denied the treatment. My hematologist was in contact with my insurance company with repeated denials and appeals to get approval for avatrombopag, which took more than 6 weeks. During this process, I received IVIG infusions every 7 to 10 days to keep my platelet count safe.

Since then, I’ve been stable on a combination of avatrombopag and MMF, which we added to my treatment regimen in 2020. Over the next year, the dosages of the two medications were monitored and adjusted, which allowed me to reduce the avatrombopag dose from two pills a day to one pill every other day. By combining the MMF and the avatrombopag, my platelet count has been stable, rarely dropping to < 100 × 109/l. I have only required two emergency IVIG infusions in the past 4 years.

The Impact of ITP and ITP Treatment

All the testing, new medications, and time at the oncology center had a significant effect on my employment and standard of living. I eventually had to close my business as I was unable to fulfill my contracts. I’m now semiretired. We are living off Social Security and our savings. In 2019, we sold our house so that we could downsize and reduce our cost of living. Thankfully, my daughter was in college when this started; otherwise, the impact on her and my wife would have been much more significant.

I’ve experienced many symptoms due to low platelet counts. Before we got my platelet count under control, I couldn’t exercise. Doing just a few push-ups would cause my shoulders and arms to turn blue. Due to bleeding in my eyes from when my platelets dropped to 1 × 109/l, I went through an approximately 2-year process to repair my retina. I also must constantly monitor my kidney function for side effects of MMF treatment.

My platelets tend to drop when I get sick or stressed or get a vaccine. My wife and I took the COVID-19 pandemic very seriously, but I still got COVID-19 in 2022; thankfully, it was a mild case. As soon as I feel any cold symptoms, I take zinc and plenty of fluids. This may not work for everyone but for me it seems to help. To protect myself, I stay up to date on my vaccinations and carry tranexamic acid with me when I travel, which is used to stop sudden excessive bleeding, especially when I cannot check my platelet counts.

Challenges and Future Perspectives

My original hematologist saw hundreds of patients, and I was his only patient with ITP. I’ve done a lot of research on ITP and felt like I knew as much about ITP as he did. It’s not surprising, as I only accounted for a small proportion of his time. I feel that many patients with ITP are in this situation. My advice for anyone with this disease is to find a specialist who treats ITP. It is also important to learn how to communicate with medical insurance companies; the frontline people are kind and want to help. They are as frustrated as we are with the administrative hurdles.

The PDSA is an amazing resource—they have people who can answer almost any question regarding ITP and are knowledgeable on the latest research. I wouldn’t have found my new hematologist without the PDSA. I don’t want to think about what would have happened if I never found the right doctor.

Physician Perspective

Disease Background and Clinical Presentation

ITP is a rare autoimmune disease characterized by platelet counts < 100 × 109/l [1, 2]. ITP can affect patients of all ages and sexes, with peak incidence in children under 5 years of age and adults aged > 65 years [3, 4]. Patients with ITP have an increased risk of spontaneous bleeding, especially when platelet levels are < 30 × 109/l [1, 2, 5]. A diagnosis of ITP is typically made by excluding other known causes of thrombocytopenia, including post-transfusion purpura (PTP), a delayed transfusion reaction wherein a patient develops dramatic, sudden, and self-limiting thrombocytopenia expected to last approximately 2 weeks [6]. There is no gold standard test to reliably diagnose ITP, which can lead to diagnostic delays, most frequently due to waiting for additional tests to rule out other conditions or for a referral to see a specialist [7–9].

ITP is caused by both the destruction and the insufficient production of platelets [10, 11]. In patients with ITP, antiplatelet antibodies are produced by B cells [10, 11]. These antiplatelet antibodies mark platelets for destruction by macrophages in the spleen [11]. Antiplatelet antibodies can also bind to and damage megakaryocytes, leading to insufficient platelet production in the bone marrow [11]. Multiple T-cell abnormalities also contribute to platelet destruction and immune dysregulation. Cytotoxic CD8 + T cells induce platelet lysis and apoptosis, leading to accelerated clearance by macrophages and hepatocytes. Regulatory CD4 + T cells are diminished in ITP, correlating with lower platelet counts [12, 13]. Most cases of ITP are idiopathic; however, viral infections and, less commonly, vaccines against viruses can precede ITP [14]. It is important to note that the mention of a possible link between vaccine administration and ITP onset, as described by patient Steve Pallagi, is a patient-reported experience and is not based on evidence.

The clinical presentation of ITP varies widely [7, 15]. Some patients may be asymptomatic, while others can experience spontaneous life-threatening bleeding [15, 16]. More commonly, mucocutaneous bleeding may appear without an obvious precipitating event, often presenting as petechiae, bruising, or purpura. Many patients with ITP report nosebleeds, heavy menstruation, and/or bleeding gums [15]. These symptoms are often the first sign of ITP and can prompt patients to seek medical care [9]. Severe and potentially life-threatening gastrointestinal or intracranial hemorrhage can also occur, although less frequently [7, 17]. ITP is also associated with an increased risk of both venous and arterial thrombotic events [18, 19].

Beyond physical symptoms, ITP can also impact health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [20]. The ITP World Impact Survey (I-WISh), an international, online survey completed by 1507 adult patients, found that ITP has a multidimensional impact on HRQoL [20]. More than 60% of patients reported that ITP impacted their daily life with the past month at least “sometimes,” 49% reported that ITP negatively impacted their psychological and emotional well-being, and > 33% who were employed felt that ITP negatively impacted their work productivity [20]. Contributing to reduced HRQoL, severe fatigue is present in many patients with ITP and tends to remain debilitating throughout the course of the disease [9, 21]. There is no convincing evidence that any of the existing ITP treatments improve fatigue, and the relationship between fatigue and platelet counts remains unclear [9, 22]. Almost half of patients report fatigue as the top symptom they would like resolved [9].

Treatment Guidelines and Recommendations

ITP can be acute or chronic and is classified according to the time from diagnosis and response to treatment [7, 8]. Newly diagnosed ITP occurs ≤ 3 months from diagnosis. Persistent ITP is ITP with a duration of 3 to 12 months, including patients not reaching spontaneous remission or failing to maintain a complete response to therapy. Chronic ITP is defined as ITP with a duration of > 12 months from diagnosis [7, 8]. Acute ITP (newly diagnosed ITP) is more frequent in children, with many experiencing spontaneous recovery within 3 months of diagnosis. Most adults with ITP develop chronic disease [7, 11].

ITP treatment should be individualized to the patient and the disease phase. Overall, treatment should prevent severe bleeding, maintain a target platelet level of > 20 to 30 × 109/l, minimize toxicity, and optimize HRQoL [8]. Treatment options include therapies that reduce the destruction of platelets by the immune system (corticosteroids, rituximab, spleen tyrosine kinase [Syk] inhibitors such as fostamatinib, Bruton tyrosine kinase [BTK] inhibitors such as rilzabrutinib) and agents that stimulate platelet production (thrombopoietin receptor agonists [TPO-RAs]) [7, 8, 11, 23].

In 2019, both the American Society of Hematology (ASH) and an international consensus group updated guidelines for the treatment of ITP [7, 8]. According to these guidelines, treatment should be initiated in adults with newly diagnosed ITP and a platelet count of < 30 × 109/l, regardless of symptoms [7, 8]. Both sets of guidelines recommend corticosteroids with or without IVIG as first-line treatment for newly diagnosed ITP [7, 8]. The duration of corticosteroid treatment should be limited to ≤ 6 weeks and patients should be closely monitored for potential side effects [7].

Adults with persistent or chronic ITP who are dependent on or unresponsive to corticosteroids should proceed to second-line treatment, for which the current guidelines are less direct [7, 8, 24]. The 2019 ASH guidelines recommend TPO-RAs (i.e., eltrombopag or romiplostim), rituximab, and splenectomy as second-line treatment options for adults with ITP [7]. Splenectomy should be delayed for at least 1 year after diagnosis because of the potential for spontaneous remission in the first year, and patients who value avoidance of long-term medication may prefer splenectomy or rituximab [7]. The 2019 international consensus report and a 2022 review of the 2019 ASH guidelines added fostamatinib to the list of second-line treatments supported by robust clinical evidence (Table 1) [8, 24]. Complicating matters, the recent approval of rilzabrutinib as a second-line treatment for adults with ITP adds another potential treatment option that is not in any of the current guidelines [25]. While a precise second-line treatment algorithm does not exist, the guidelines do compare second-line treatment options and suggest that the choice of treatment should be individualized based on the duration of disease, goals for treatment, and shared decision-making [7, 8].

Emerging Treatment Options and Ongoing Phase 3 Clinical Trials

There are currently six therapies being investigated in multicenter phase 3 clinical trials for adults with ITP. These include novel Syk and BTK inhibitors as well as agents with unique mechanisms for the treatment of ITP, such as a neonatal Fc receptor inhibitor, an anti-CD38 antibody, and a B-cell activating factor receptor inhibitor (Supplementary Table S1).

Challenges and Future Perspectives

Despite advancements in the management of ITP, areas for improvement remain. Corticosteroids are the standard first-line treatment for patients with ITP [7, 8]. While most patients initially respond to corticosteroids, many experience treatment failure or become steroid dependent. Prolonged exposure to corticosteroids can cause significant morbidity, and steroid dependency is a problem for many patients with ITP [26]. Therefore, most adults with ITP require second-line therapy. Current treatment guidelines recommend six different second-line treatment options [7, 8, 24]. However, the absence of a discrete treatment algorithm can make second-line treatment decisions very complicated. Fundamental questions such as when to stop first-line treatment or which second-line treatment to start first remain unanswered [27].

Many patients lose response in the long-term to the currently available treatment options and are unable to achieve sustained remission upon treatment discontinuation [26, 28, 29]. Loss of response causes patients to consistently try new medications, and the lack of treatment options that provide sustained remission necessitates chronic treatment [26, 28]. As illustrated by the firsthand patient accounts presented here, this can lead to a constant cycle of treatment failure, trials of new second-line treatments, and uncertainty for many patients with ITP. This process is highly burdensome and can negatively impact HRQoL.

Conclusion

Both patients and physicians recognize that managing ITP extends beyond achieving platelet stability and must also address the broader impact of the disease on HRQoL. Systemic barriers such as limited access to specialists, challenges with insurance coverage, and physician awareness of newer treatment options can further complicate disease management. Identifying treatment strategies that provide long-term sustained remission, preferably in the absence of treatment, is one of the greatest needs for patients with chronic ITP.
