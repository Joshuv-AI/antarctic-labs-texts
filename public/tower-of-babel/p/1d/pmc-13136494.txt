Impact of γ-aminobutyric acid and 1-methylcyclopropene on qualitative attributes of apples (Malus domestica Borkh. cv. Fuji) during cold storage
Authors: Yanqing Dang, Yanju Xiang, Adila Abudula, Xujie Hou, Yunfeng Pu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the efficacy of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA, 1.0 mM) and 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP, 1 µL L− 1), individually and in combination, in mitigating watercore disappearance, flesh browning, and postharvest quality in apples during 180 days storage. The results showed that the combined GABA + 1-MCP treatment outperformed individual applications, significantly delaying watercore disappearance by preserving sucrose and sorbitol while moderating glucose and fructose accumulation. Furthermore, the combined treatment significantly reduced flesh browning compared to the control, likely attributed to suppressed polyphenol oxidase activity and enhanced antioxidant capacity. This approach also minimized weight loss and maintained firmness and titratable acidity. Although total phenolics and total flavonoids increased progressively during storage, their levels were effectively regulated by the GABA + 1-MCP treatment. These results indicate that the combined exogenous application of GABA and 1-MCP is a potentially effective method to improve postharvest quality, and enhance storage efficiency of apple fruit.

Introduction

The environmental and climatic conditions in Xinjiang are favorable for apple growth, creating a unique quality of watercore apples, which has become a symbol product, and selected as the recommended fruit for the Beijing Olympic Games in 20081. Although watercore is a physiological disorder of apple characterized by glassy or translucent tissue in the flesh2, and visible only when the fruit is cut in half3, watercore apples are safe and edible4. In some countries, watercore appearance is a desirable trait for apples, as an indicator of full ripeness5,6. However, watercore tissues tend to disappear during storage, leading to a significant loss of unique identity and market value6. Additionally, long-term storage often results in flesh browning4. Hence, these challenges highlight the need for effective postharvest treatments to maintain post-harvest quality in watercore apples.

γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA), a non-proteinogenic amino acid, can be rapidly accumulated in response to biotic and abiotic stresses and involves in signaling or regulatory mechanisms, nitrogen and carbon metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis7,8. Postharvest GABA treatments have been shown to delay senescence, enhance chilling tolerance, and improve the quality of various horticultural products during cold storage9–11. Additionally, Aydin Shekari12 found that GABA treatment could be employed for retarding cap browning and maintaining the sensory and nutritional quality of button mushrooms during cold storage. Conversely, 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP), a synthetic compound that works by competitively binding to ethylene receptors and inhibits ethylene action, has been widely used to delay ripening and senescence in climacteric fruits such as apples13–15. Studies confirm its efficacy in delaying softening by modifying cell wall pectin16 and in inhibiting degreening by suppressing the ethylene climacteric and related chlorophyll catabolism17. While both compounds are effective individually, research on their combined application remains limited and has mainly focused on other climacteric fruit species. For example, in avocado and tomato, combined GABA and 1-MCP treatment has demonstrated synergistic effects in reducing chilling injury, decay, and weight loss, while better preserving firmness, color, chlorophyll, flavonoids, and membrane integrity during storage18,19. Preliminary evidence also suggests that even low concentrations of GABA (1–5 mM) can complement 1-MCP in maintaining color and membrane stability20.

Nevertheless, the combined effect of GABA and 1-MCP on apple fruit has not been systematically examined, especially regarding its efficacy in maintaining watercore and overall quality throughout extended cold storage. This gap is notable given the commercial significance of apples and the need for advanced preservation techniques. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the individual and combined effects of GABA and 1-MCP, individually and in combination, in postharvest quality of apples during long-term storage. The results are expected to provide novel insights for prolonging the marketability of watercore-affected apples.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

Apple fruits (Malus domestica Borkh. cv. Fuji) at commercial maturity were hand harvested from an orchard in Wensu, Xinjiang, with permission obtained from the orchard owner, and transported to the laboratory at the same day with cartons. The fruit with good texture, uniform size and maturity, free of physical injuries and pest were randomly divided into four groups. The treatments were as follows: (GABA) immersion for 20 min in 1.0 mmol/L GABA solution (≥ 99%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd, Shanghai), a concentration selected based on previously established efficacy in postharvest fruit studies10,21, followed by 24 h of air exposure; (1-MCP) immersion in distilled water for 20 min followed by 24 h of fumigation with 1 µL L⁻¹ 1-MCP(Xiqin Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Xi’an), a dosage chosen in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendation and previous studies22,23; (G + M) sequential treatment involving GABA immersion (as for group GABA), followed by air-drying at room temperature and subsequent 1-MCP exposure; and (CK) immersion in distilled water for 20 min followed by 24 h of air exposure as a control. To prevent mechanical damage during storage, all treated apples were individually sleeved with commercial foam nets and packed in corrugated boxes (48 cm × 32 cm × 27 cm; 30 fruits per box). All boxes were stored at 0 ± 1 °C. Postharvest quality was evaluated by periodically analyzing samples that were randomly collected at 30-day intervals.

Watercore incidence (WI) and internal browning (IB)

The WI and IB were assessed as follows. For each treatment, 15 apples were sampled—five fruits randomly selected from each of three replicate corrugated boxes. The WI was evaluated according to the method described by Li24. After weighing, each fruit was cut transversely at the equatorial region. Apples exhibiting glassy or translucent tissue in the cross‑section were recorded as watercore‑affected, and the percentage of WI was calculated.

The IB was determined following the procedure of Wood25. Similarly, fruits were cut equatorially and examined for browning symptoms. The number of apples showing internal browning was recorded, and the IB percentage was calculated accordingly.

Weight loss, firmness, and titratable acidity (TA)

Fruit weight loss was measured by the difference of final weight from initial weight and expressed as percentage weight loss. The firmness was evaluated following the method reported by Yuan26 on a Texture Analyzer (GY-4; Zhejiang Top Instrument Co., Ltd., China) equipped with a 3.5 mm diameter cylindrical probe. Five apples were measured for each treatment, and the results were expressed as kg/cm2.

TA was determined using the method described by Pu27. Briefly, a 5.0 g sample was homogenized with 50 mL of distilled water. The homogenate was then titrated with a standardized 0.01 mol/L sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution, using phenolphthalein as an indicator. TA was calculated based on the volume of NaOH consumed and expressed as a percentage of malic acid (%).

Glucose, sorbitol, fructose and sucrose

The contents of individual sugars (glucose, sorbitol, fructose, and sucrose) were determined according to the method of Pu27, with minor modifications, using an LC-20 A HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a RID-10 A refractive index detector and a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Briefly, a 10 g sample was extracted with distilled water by ultrasonication at room temperature for 30 min. After centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter prior to injection. The analysis was performed under isocratic elution with 75% acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. Sugar contents were quantified using external standards and expressed as g per kg fresh weight (g/kg FW).

Total phenolics content (TPC) and total flavonoids content (TFC)

The TPC was measured according to the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method27, Briefly, a 10.0 g sample was homogenized and extracted with 80% ethanol using ultrasonication for 30 min at room temperature. The homogenate was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, a 0.2 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 10 mL of distilled water and 0.3 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After reacting for 6 min at room temperature, 1 mL of a 15% Na₂CO₃ solution was added. The mixture was then incubated in the dark for 2 h at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV-2450 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The TPC was calculated using a gallic acid standard curve and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of fresh weight (mg GAE/g FW).

The TFC was determined following the modified colorimetric assay27 with the same sample pretreatment as described for TPC. Briefly, a 5.0 mL aliquot of the prepared extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of a 5% NaNO₂ solution. After standing for 6 min at room temperature, 0.5 mL of a 10% Al(NO₃)₃ solution was added and mixed, followed by another 6-min incubation. Subsequently, 4 mL of a 4% NaOH solution was added. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and allowed to stand for 20 min at room temperature. The absorbance was then measured at 502 nm using a UV-2550 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). Rutin was used as the standard for quantification, and the results are expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalent per gram of fresh weight (mg RE/g FW).

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicate independently, and three samples were analyzed for each group. The data was subjected to one-way analysis of variance using the SPSS statistical software (Version 20.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), and the significant differences were compared by the Duncan’s test (P < 0.05). Furthermore, principal composition analysis (PCA) was performed using Unscrambler 10.1 (CAMO AS, Trondheim, Norway) software.

Results and discussion

Watercore incidence (WI) and Internal browning(IB)

As shown in Fig. 1A, the WI progressively declined throughout refrigerated storage. At the beginning of storage, all fruit displayed pronounced watercore (WI > 90%), primarily localized around vascular bundles (Fig. 1). As storage progressed, watercore gradually diminished and eventually disappeared, a pattern consistent with previous reports in apples24,28. Treatment effects became evident by 60 days of storage, when GABA, 1‑MCP, and their combination (G + M) maintained significantly higher WI than the control (CK) (P < 0.05), with distinct spatial distributions visible in imaging analysis (Figs. 1, 2 A). After 90 days, the CK and 1‑MCP (M) groups showed accelerated watercore remission (WI < 20%), whereas GABA‑treated fruit (G and G + M) retained WI values above 50% (G: 56.67%; G + M: 63.33%), preserving the characteristic translucent parenchyma. By 120 days, watercore had completely disappeared in CK and M fruit, while GABA‑treated groups still exhibited detectable watercore (G: 6.67%; G + M: 13.33%) until 150 days.

The differential persistence of watercore among treatments can be attributed to distinct effects on sorbitol metabolism, membrane integrity, and fruit ripening. Watercore fundamentally arises from apoplastic sorbitol accumulation, which acts as an osmolyte, drawing water into intercellular spaces and causing tissue translucency29–31. Normally, phloem-derived sorbitol is transported into the cytoplasm and metabolized to fructose by sorbitol dehydrogenase (SDH). Disruptions in unloading, transport, or metabolism can lead to its accumulation in the apoplast29,32. Our results indicate that both 1‑MCP and GABA treatments delayed the remission of this sorbitol-mediated watercore through distinct mechanisms. 1‑MCP primarily acts by inhibiting ethylene action, a key regulator of ripening and senescence33. By slowing ripening progression, it likely retards the metabolic signals that mobilize and convert apoplastic sorbitol, potentially via modulation of SDH or sugar transporters (e.g., MdSOTs) whose expression increases during maturation34,35. This delays the clearance of osmotically active solutes, resulting in slower remission—consistent with reports that 1‑MCP reduces disorder severity36. However, its effect was less sustained than that of GABA. In contrast, GABA exerted a stronger and more prolonged effect, likely due to its role in maintaining membrane integrity and reducing solute leakage21. Senescence-associated membrane deterioration, indicated by elevated electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde accumulation, promotes solute and water equilibration across compartments4. GABA mitigates membrane lipid peroxidation, thereby preserving symplast-apoplast compartmentalization. This maintained barrier directly slows water redistribution and the retrieval or metabolism of apoplastic sorbitol—key processes in watercore resolution. The prolonged efficacy of the combined treatment (G + M) points to a synergistic mechanism: 1‑MCP slows ripening‑related sugar catabolism, while GABA strengthens membrane integrity, thereby reducing solute leakage and redistribution. This dual inhibition effectively extends the duration of the watercore state.

The IB of apples during cold storage was significantly affected by postharvest treatments (P < 0.05, Figs. 1 and 2B). Control (CK) group manifested initial flesh browning (IB = 3.33%) at 90 days postharvest, progressing to severe discoloration (IB = 73.33%) by storage terminus. 1-MCP-treated (M) group exhibited delayed onset, with first browning symptoms (IB = 3.33%) emerging at 120 days, contrasting with complete absence in GABA-treated groups (Fig. 3). Additionally, the GABA treated groups did not exhibit fruit flesh browning throughout the entire storage period. Li37 demonstrated that GABA application enhances the mitochondrial antioxidant system and reduces peel browning in pears following removal from cold storage. Similarly, Shekari12 reported that GABA-treated mushrooms exhibited elevated activity and gene expression of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) alongside reduced polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity, collectively resulting in retarded cap browning. Consistent with these findings, other studies have shown that exogenous GABA treatment effectively protects membrane integrity in various fruits, as reflected by reduced electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, thereby delaying flesh browning during cold storage12. In contrast, Jung38 observed that late-harvested fruit treated with 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) were more susceptible to flesh browning during prolonged storage, primarily due to a compromised antioxidant defense mechanism under stress, along with elevated polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity and electrolyte leakage rates. Collectively, these results demonstrate that GABA treatments not only delay watercore dissipation but also effectively inhibit flesh browning.

Weight loss

The weight loss of apples progressively increased during 180-day cold storage across all treatments. The highest weight loss (9.56%) was observed in control fruit. In this study, 1-MCP and GABA treatments alone or in combination significantly (P < 0.05) reduced weight loss of apple fruit compared to control treatment and the lowest weight loss (7.57%) was found in fruit treated with 1-MCP+GABA (Fig. 3). The increase in weight loss during storage is likely attributable to water loss resulting from respiration, transpiration and metabolic activity39. 1‑MCP limits ripening‑related changes by blocking ethylene receptors, thereby protecting fruit from both internal and external ethylene and leading to reduced weight loss40. This mechanism aligns with observations in tomato, where 1‑MCP has been shown to reduce postharvest water loss by regulating epidermal cuticle composition and morphology41. In apples specifically, 1‑MCP may further preserve quality during cold storage by delaying changes in cuticular wax density and inhibiting wax crystal melting, potentially through downregulation of MdCER642. GABA, on the other hand, might mitigate weight loss by reducing membrane lipid peroxidation via modulation of lipoxygenase enzyme activity, thereby enhancing cell wall stability43. Additionally, GABA treatment could decrease weight loss by reducing membrane damage, preserving cell membrane integrity, and suppressing malondialdehyde (MDA) production8, collectively limiting cellular water loss.

Firmness

Firmness serves as a critical determinant of edible quality, consumer acceptability, and commercial value in apples. As illustrated in Fig. 4, both treatment type and storage duration exerted significant effects (p ≤ 0.05) on fruit firmness. All treatments exhibited progressive firmness loss during storage, with the most pronounced decline observed in control fruit. This gradual softening is primarily attributed to the degradation of cell wall polysaccharides, particularly pectin depolymerization and solubilization, mediated by enzymes such as polygalacturonase and pectin methylesterase, a well-documented process during apple postharvest senescence44,45.After 180 days, the combined 1-MCP+GABA treatment maintained the highest firmness (6.69 kg/cm²), significantly surpassing individual GABA (6.03 kg/cm²), 1-MCP (5.83 kg/cm²), and control (5.01 kg/cm²) groups (P < 0.05). The superior performance of the combined treatment can be explained by complementary physiological mechanisms. 1-MCP, an ethylene action inhibitor, delays softening by blocking ethylene receptors and thus suppressing the expression of cell wall–degrading enzymes17,46. GABA may contribute to firmness retention through enhanced antioxidant capacity, membrane stabilization, and mitigation of oxidative stress, which indirectly helps maintain cell wall integrity21. The synergistic effect observed in the 1-MCP+GABA treatment likely results from the simultaneous inhibition of ethylene-driven senescence and the reinforcement of cellular structures under prolonged storage, aligning with recent studies reporting enhanced quality preservation using combined postharvest strategies18,19. Therefore, the combined application not only alleviates ethylene-mediated softening but also enhances fruit resistance to aging-related metabolic changes, leading to better texture preservation.

Titratable acid (TA)

The TA constitutes a critical determinant of apple flavor quality, with both treatment and storage duration significantly influencing its retention (p ≤ 0.05; Fig. 5). All treatments exhibited a gradual decline in organic acid content throughout storage, indicative of their catabolism serving as respiratory substrates within senescence-associated metabolic pathways47,48. This reduction in TA is primarily driven by the characteristic increase in respiration rate during storage. Under these conditions, organic acids are metabolized via the TCA cycle to generate cellular energy, leading to the observed decrease in TA49. Compared with the control, both 1‑MCP and GABA treatments significantly better preserved TA levels. Furthermore, the combined GABA + 1‑MCP treatment consistently maintained the highest TA throughout storage, indicating a more pronounced delay in acid metabolism than either treatment alone. By day 180, TA under the combined treatment (0.19%) remained higher than in fruit treated with 1‑MCP alone (0.16%) or GABA alone (0.17%). The effect of 1‑MCP can be attributed to its inhibition of ethylene‑mediated senescence and reduction in respiratory metabolism, which helps conserve organic acids33. GABA, on the other hand, enhances cellular stability and mitigates stress‑induced acid degradation21. The superior acid retention under the combined treatment suggests a synergistic effect between 1‑MCP and GABA in maintaining organic acid reserves, a phenomenon supported by recent studies on combined postharvest treatments18,20.

Glucose, fructose, sorbitol and sucrose

As shown in Fig. 6, the composition of these sugars changed markedly during storage(P ≤ 0.05). Sucrose (Fig. 6D) and sorbitol (Fig. 6C) contents declined consistently across all treatment groups, whereas glucose and fructose levels progressively increased (Fig. 6A, B). In the control fruit, sucrose content decreased sharply within the first 90 days, largely due to its hydrolysis by invertase into glucose and fructose50. Sorbitol content also declined steadily as it was metabolized to fructose via the sorbitol dehydrogenase pathway51. These catabolic processes led to the most pronounced accumulation of glucose and fructose in the control group, reflecting higher corresponding enzymatic activities. Treatments with 1‑MCP and GABA, either alone or in combination, effectively moderated these metabolic shifts. 1‑MCP slowed the degradation of both sucrose and sorbitol, thereby delaying the rise in glucose and fructose. This aligns with its known role in inhibiting ethylene‑mediated senescence, which reduces overall metabolic rates and associated enzymatic activities33. GABA similarly helped preserve sucrose and sorbitol while attenuating hexose accumulation, likely through enhanced cellular integrity and reduced stress‑induced metabolic activity52. Notably, the combined 1‑MCP + GABA treatment exhibited the strongest stabilizing effect on sugar metabolism. It best maintained sucrose and sorbitol levels and resulted in the slowest increase in glucose and fructose, indicating a synergistic interaction between the two treatments in regulating postharvest sugar dynamics.

Total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoids content (TFC)

The changes in TPC and TFC during storage were significantly influenced by both storage duration and postharvest treatments (Fig. 7). The TPC increased progressively throughout the storage period in all treatment groups. The control group exhibited the highest phenolic accumulation rate. This increase may be attributed to the more advanced ripening and senescence stage of untreated fruit, during which phenolic metabolism is actively remodeled. Cell wall modifications during postharvest ripening both induce phenolic release and increase the bioaccessibility of plums53. Furthermore, enhanced phenolic synthesis can occur in advanced ripening stages as part of senescence-related physiological and stress responses, including the oxidative stress that frequently accompanies late ripening54. The 1-MCP and GABA treatments showed a slower but steady increase in total phenolics. The combined treatment of 1-MCP and GABA resulted in the lowest rate of phenolic accumulation. In contrast to TPC, the TFC exhibited a dynamic trend during storage, characterized by an initial decline followed by a subsequent increase. In the control group, the TFC decreased significantly during the first 60 days of storage. However, after 60 days, the TFC began to increase. Similar trends were observed in the 1-MCP and GABA treatment groups, although the extent of the initial decline was less pronounced compared to the control. The combined treatment of 1-MCP and GABA maintained the highest stability in TFC throughout storage, exhibiting the smallest initial decline and the most gradual increase during later stages. While water loss during storage may partially contribute to the observed increases in TPC and TFC of apple fruit, the differences among treatments strongly suggest active physiological regulation. Wang8 demonstrated that GABA treatment promoted the accumulation of total phenolics and proline and enhanced antioxidant defense system of banana fruit during storage. Similarly, Ali11 reported that the GABA application increased phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity, suppressed polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity, and consequently elevated total phenol levels. Further supporting this, Ge21 reported that GABA treatment enhanced the accumulation of total phenolics and flavonoids in blueberry fruit by regulating phenylpropanoid pathway. These results suggests that the combined treatment effectively mitigated oxidative stress and preserved flavonoid and phenolic compounds stability, likely through the synergistic effects of ethylene inhibition and enhanced antioxidant activity.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to comparatively evaluate the effects of different treatments on the postharvest quality of apple fruits. This multivariate approach visually represents relationships between the treatments and measured quality indices. The PCA loading plot extracted two principal components (PC-1 and PC-2) accounting for 99% of total variance, revealing distinct clustering patterns among apples from different treatments. The correlation loadings plot (Fig. 8) showed samples and indices segregated into three distinct groups: Group 1 (Right plane) clustered sucrose, sorbitol, firmness, TA, and WI; Group 2 grouped all samples centrally; Group 3 (Left plane) clustered glucose, fructose, TPC, and weight loss (WL). Group 1 exhibited high levels of its constituent indices during early storage, while Group 3 showed high levels in the final storage stage. Based on their positions and associations, sucrose, sorbitol, firmness, TA, and WI are characteristic of fresh apple fruit, whereas glucose, fructose, TPC, and WL are characteristics of apples after long-term storage.

Conclusion

In summary, this study shows that the combined application of 1-MCP and GABA effectively maintains the postharvest quality of cold-stored apples. The treatment influenced key physicochemical and market attributes, notably delaying the disappearance of watercore. This effect may be attributed to the complementary actions of 1-MCP—which suppresses ethylene-mediated senescence—and GABA—which enhances membrane integrity—together contributing to slower metabolic shifts and water redistribution during storage. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the delayed watercore loss, particularly those involving ethylene signaling, sorbitol metabolism, and cellular compartmentation, remain to be clarified. While the results point to a possible synergistic interaction between 1-MCP and GABA, further mechanistic studies are needed to confirm this relationship. A better understanding of these processes will be important for refining treatment protocols and supporting commercial application. Overall, this work suggests that the combined treatment is a promising approach for preserving apple quality, while also underscoring the need for more detailed investigation and validation under varied storage conditions.
