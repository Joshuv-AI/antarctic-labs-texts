Tracking environmental antimicrobial resistance: Isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of E. coli across hospital, livestock, and community settings in Gampaha, Sri Lanka
Authors: Randeni Arachchige Sachini Bhagya Randeni, Chamini Nadisha Badanasinghe, Sunil-Chandra Naranapity Pathirannahalage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) are multifactorial, necessitating a One Health approach for its mitigation. Indiscriminate and unnecessary antimicrobial use drives AMR within hospitals and livestock farms, hypothesized as key hotspots. This descriptive cross-sectional study was carried out to investigate environmental contamination by non-susceptible Escherichia coli (E. coli) in hospitals, livestock farms, and a community in close proximity to a hospital in the Gampaha District, Sri Lanka.ResultsA total of 134 environmental samples were collected, comprising wastewater (n = 74), soil (n = 30), and groundwater (n = 30). Of these, 54 samples were obtained from five hospitals, 50 samples from six livestock farms, and 30 groundwater samples from the surrounding community. A total of 49 (36.56%) E. coli isolates were recovered (hospitals = 15/54, 27.77%, livestock farms = 31/50, 62%, community = 3/30, 10%). Of the total, 27 (55.1%) isolates (hospitals = 11/15, 73.33%, livestock farms = 16/31, 51.61%, community = 0) were non-susceptible to at least 1 of 7 antibiotics tested. Highest rate of non-susceptibility was observed for ampicillin (24/49, 48.97%), followed by co-trimoxazole (16/49, 32.65%), ciprofloxacin (13/49, 26.53%), co-amoxiclav (9/49, 18.36%), cefotaxime (7/49, 14.28%), gentamicin (2/49, 4.08%), and meropenem (1/49, 2.04%). Of the total isolates, 16.32% (8/49) were multidrug-resistant (MDR).ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that hospital and livestock environments may act as important reservoirs of AMR E. coli, underscoring the urgent need to integrate environmental surveillance, wastewater management, and antimicrobial stewardship into Sri Lanka’s One Health AMR control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05392-y.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an increasing global public health concern with significant clinical and economic implications. Antimicrobial resistant pathogens continue to compromise clinical outcomes, resulting in prolonged hospitalisation, escalated healthcare costs, and treatment failures. In 2021, bacterial AMR was associated with an estimated 4.71 million deaths worldwide, including 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to resistance. Projections have suggested that, if current trends continue, the global burden of AMR will further increase, with an estimated 8.22 million deaths annually associated with AMR by 2050, of which approximately 1.91 million may be directly attributable to resistant infections. The six leading bacterial pathogens associated with AMR—E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa—collectively accounted for an estimated 929,000 deaths directly attributable to AMR (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 660,000–1,270,000) and 3.57 million deaths associated with AMR (95% UI: 2.62–4.78 million) globally in 2019 [1, 2].

Although the emergence of AMR in microorganisms is a natural evolutionary response to antimicrobial exposure, its selection and spread are driven by multiple factors, with indiscriminate and excessive use of antimicrobials in humans and animals being a major contributor [3, 4]. In this context, environment plays a pivotal role in its emergence and dissemination. Antibiotic residues are released into the environment via several pathways, such as from clinical settings, human or animal waste, the use of products containing antibacterials, and through food and fodder supplemented with antibiotics which are given to animals. The exposure to these antibiotic residues exerts selective pressure on microbial communities, contributing to the emergence and dissemination of AMR [5–12]. Such AMR-contaminated environmental compartments pose a direct threat to public health, as these determinants can be transmitted to humans through multiple exposure pathways, including contaminated water, food chains, and direct environmental contact. The key hotspots for antibiotic resistant bacteria (ARB) include wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), pharmaceutical effluents, food and animal production sites in agriculture and aquaculture, and clinical settings such as hospitals where antibiotic exposure is particularly intense [3, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13].

To understand AMR from a One Health perspective, comprehensive surveillance across human, animal, and environmental sectors is essential. However, current surveillance programs primarily focus on AMR in human clinical cases and livestock, with comparatively limited integration of environmental monitoring [14]. This gap is particularly pronounced in developing countries such as Sri Lanka, where resource constraints, inadequate wastewater treatment infrastructure, and the use of antimicrobials in humans and animals is often unregulated, with inadequate antibiotic stewardship programs in hospitals and a lack of legislation governing antibiotic use in animal husbandry [4, 15]. This study aimed to address this gap by investigating the environmental contamination by antimicrobial non-susceptible E. coli, with a specific focus on key hotspots such as hospitals and livestock farms. Since some of these environmental samples (e.g. effluents from WWTPs of hospitals, wastewater from farms) are discharged into the community, there is a potential to contaminate both surface water and groundwater sources in the community with an impact on both human and animal health. Escherichia coli was selected as the indicator organism because it is a well-established marker of faecal contamination and is widely used as a sentinel organism for monitoring environmental AMR owing to its ubiquitous distribution, ability to acquire resistance determinants, and relevance to both human and animal health [14].

Methods

The aim, design and setting of the study

This cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted in the Gampaha district of Sri Lanka, from January to August 2023. The study aimed to assess environmental contamination with E. coli and its antimicrobial susceptibility patterns across hospital, livestock farm and community settings using a One-Health approach. The study was conducted in the Gampaha District because it is the most populous district in Sri Lanka with a high density of healthcare facilities, and intensive livestock production providing an ideal setting to investigate environmental AMR across interconnected human, animal, and environmental sectors. In addition, limited environmental AMR surveillance data are available from this region.

Environmental samples were collected from five hospitals, six livestock farms and 30 household wells located within a 500 m radius of a teaching hospital (H1). The hospital settings included: one teaching hospital (H1) two specialized hospitals (respiratory [H2] and rheumatology [H3]), one District General Hospital (H4), and one Divisional Hospital (H5). The six livestock farms comprised mixed and single-production farms, including pig (n = 2), poultry (n = 3), and dairy farms (n = 2). Laboratory analysis was performed at the Department of Medical Microbiology, Faculty of Medicine, University of Kelaniya. Sample collection and sampling strategy

A total of 134 environmental samples were collected using a purposive sampling approach, targeting sites considered to be at high risk for antimicrobial contamination. These included wastewater discharge points, manure-contaminated soil, and groundwater sources used for domestic purposes.

The samples consisted of wastewater (n = 74), soil (n = 30), and groundwater (n = 30), distributed across the study settings as follows:

Hospital settings: 54 samples (wastewater, n = 42; soil, n = 12).Livestock farms: 50 samples (wastewater, n = 32; soil, n = 18).Community (groundwater): 30 samples collected from household wells.

Wastewater sampling

Wastewater samples from hospitals were collected from open drains of hospital buildings, influent and effluent points of on-site WWTPs, and effluent discharge outlets into community drainage systems. In livestock farms, wastewater samples were obtained from drainage channels and effluent outlets.

At each sampling location, composite samples were prepared by combining ten subsamples collected from spatially distributed points along the open drainage system or effluent channel. Approximately 50 mL of the composite sample was collected aseptically into sterile screw-capped centrifuge tubes using an extension-sampling pole.

Soil sampling

Soil samples were collected from hospital open waste disposal sites and manure-contaminated areas within livestock farms. At each site, ten subsamples (~ 10 g each) were collected from the top 10 cm of soil at spatially distributed points to ensure representativeness. The subsamples were combined to homogenize, and transferred into sterile, resealable plastic bags to obtain a composite sample (~ 100 g).

Groundwater sampling

Groundwater samples were collected from household wells located within a 500 m radius of the teaching hospital (H1), with one sample obtained per well. Approximately 500 mL of water was collected from 1 to 2 feet below the surface using a clean collection device and transferred aseptically into sterile glass bottles.

All samples were transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions (4 °C) and processed within 4–6 h of collection.

Isolation and identification of E. coli

The samples were processed using standard microbiological techniques appropriate for each sample type in accordance with standard operating procedures by Sri Lanka College of Microbiologists’ laboratory manual and previously published methods [16, 17].

Wastewater samples were centrifuged (3,000 rpm for 10 min) and the resulting sediment was inoculated onto MacConkey and Salmonella-Shigella agar plates.

Soil samples were air-dried and sieved through a 2–4 mm mesh to remove debris. A 10 g portion of soil was suspended in 100 mL of sterile peptone water and vortexed to obtain a homogenized suspension. After settling, an aliquot of the supernatant was inoculated onto MacConkey and Salmonella-Shigella agar plates.

Groundwater samples were processed using the membrane filtration technique. Approximately 500 mL of each water sample was filtered through a sterile 0.45 μm membrane filter under vacuum conditions. The membrane filter was then aseptically placed onto a MacConkey agar plate.

All inoculated plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Presumptive E. coli colonies were identified based on lactose fermentation on MacConkey agar and further confirmed using Gram staining and standard biochemical tests [16, 17].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method according to the CLSI guidelines, 2023 [18].

A panel of seven antibiotic discs was used: ampicillin (AMP), co-trimoxazole (SXT), ciprofloxacin (CIP), cefotaxime (CTX), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AMC), gentamicin (CN), and meropenem (MEM). These seven antimicrobial agents were selected because they represent commonly prescribed antibiotics in human and veterinary medicine in Sri Lanka and include agents from different antimicrobial classes recommended for surveillance of E. coli.

After incubation, inhibition zone diameters were measured and interpreted according to CLSI breakpoints. Isolates categorized as intermediate or resistant were considered non-susceptible for analysis.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more different antimicrobial classes. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as a quality control strain.

Statistical analysis

Data were recorded and the results were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. Results are presented as absolute numbers (N) and percentage values (%). Fisher’s exact test and Pearson chi square test were used to compare the isolation of E. coli and non-susceptible E. coli across the hospitals and the livestock farms. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The complete dataset used for analysis is provided in Additional file 1 and detailed statistical outputs are provided in Additional file 2.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study involved the collection and analysis of environmental samples, including wastewater, soil, and groundwater, and did not involve human participants, human biological materials, or identifiable personal data. Therefore, informed consent from study participants was not applicable. Permission for sample collection was obtained from the relevant hospital authorities, the Provincial Department of Animal Production and Health (Western Province), farm owners, and household well owners prior to sampling.

Results

The overall rate of isolation and antibiotic non-susceptibility of E. coli

The overall rate of E. coli isolation was 36.56%. A total of 49 E. coli isolates were yielded (hospitals = 15/54, 27.77%, livestock farms = 31/50, 62%, community = 3/30, 10%) from 134 environmental samples (wastewater [n = 74], soil [n = 30], groundwater [n = 30]). Comparison of hospital and livestock farm environments demonstrated a statistically significant association between environmental setting and E. coli recovery (χ²(1, N = 104) = 12.33, p < 0.001), with significantly higher isolation rates observed in livestock farm environments.

The overall rate of E. coli non-susceptibility to ≥ 1 antibiotic tested was 55.1% (n = 27); (hospitals = 11/15, 73.33%, livestock farms = 16/31, 51.61%, community = 0). There was no statistically significant association in the rate of non-susceptibility between the hospital and livestock E. coli isolates (χ²(1, N = 46) = 1.97, p = 0.161).

Highest non-susceptibility was for the antibiotic AMP (24/49, 48.97%), followed by SXT (16/49, 32.65%), CIP (13/49, 26.53%), AMC (9/49, 18.36%), CTX (7/49, 14.28%), CN (2/49, 4.08%), and MEM (1/49, 2.04%). Of the total isolates, 16.32% (8/49) were MDR.

Environmental samples from hospitals

Of the 54 environmental samples (wastewater [n = 42], soil [n = 12]) collected across the five hospital settings, 15 yielded E. coli, corresponding to an overall isolation rate of 27.77%. All isolates were recovered from wastewater samples (15/42, 35.71%), while none were recovered from soil samples (0/12), indicating a statistically significant association between E. coli recovery and sample type (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.024).

The E. coli isolation rates differed significantly among the five hospital settings (Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, p = 0.048). The highest isolation rate was observed in H2 (5/11; 45.45%), followed by H1 (4/9; 44.44%), H3 (3/10; 30.00%), and H4 (3/11; 27.27%), whereas no E. coli isolates were recovered from H5.

The rate of non-susceptibility (to ≥ 1 antibiotic tested), varied among E. coli isolates recovered from the different hospital settings. The highest non-susceptibility rate was observed in H4 (3/3, 100%), followed by H2 (4/5, 80.0%), H3 (2/3, 66.7%), and H1 (2/4, 50.0%). However, this variation was not statistically significant (Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, p = 0.692). Environmental samples from the livestock (pig, poultry and dairy) farms

Of the 50 environmental samples (wastewater [n = 32], soil [n = 18]) collected across the three categories of livestock farms, 31 yielded E. coli, corresponding to an overall isolation rate of 62%. Specifically, E. coli was recovered from 22 of 32 wastewater samples (68.8%) and 9 of 18 soil samples (50.0%). The E. coli isolation rates were highest in dairy farms (13/19, 68.4%), followed by poultry farms (10/16, 62.5%) and pig farms (8/15, 53.3%). No significant association was observed between farm type and E. coli isolation rate (Pearson’s χ² test, χ² = 0.82, df = 2, p = 0.665). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that environmental reservoirs of AMR E. coli are present in both hospital and livestock farm settings in the Gampaha District of Sri Lanka. Overall, E. coli was recovered from 36.6% (49/134) of environmental samples, with significantly higher isolation rates observed in livestock farms than in hospital environments (62.0% vs. 27.8%, χ² = 12.33, p < 0.001), indicating greater environmental fecal contamination within livestock production systems. However, E. coli isolates derived from hospital environmental samples exhibited a substantially higher burden of antimicrobial non-susceptibility (73.3% vs. 51.6%) and MDR (33.3% vs. 9.67%) than E. coli isolates from livestock farm environmental samples. These findings suggest that livestock farms may serve as important environmental reservoirs for the dissemination of E. coli, whereas hospital environments may represent stronger hotspots for the selection and persistence of AMR E. coli. In contrast, E. coli recovery from community groundwater was low (3/30, 10.0%), and no AMR E. coli were detected, suggesting limited contamination of groundwater sources in the study area, despite their proximity to the hospital environment. However, as groundwater sampling was limited to one hospital community, it may not fully represent district-wide contamination of groundwater sources, which can vary due to antibiotics used, waste management, and local conditions.

A clear variation was observed in the recovery of E. coli between environmental sample types in hospital and livestock farm settings. In hospitals, E. coli was recovered exclusively from wastewater samples (15/42, 35.7%), while no E. coli was recovered from soil samples (0/12), resulting in a significant association between sample type and E. coli recovery (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.024). This finding suggests that fecal contamination in hospital environments is primarily concentrated within wastewater systems in the healthcare settings investigated. In contrast, E. coli was recovered from both wastewater and soil samples in livestock farms, with isolation rates of 68.8% (22/32) and 50.0% (9/18), respectively. Although recovery was higher in wastewater than in soil samples, the difference was not statistically significant (Pearson χ² = 1.719, df = 1, p = 0.190). The detection of E. coli in both environmental matrices suggests that fecal contamination is widely distributed throughout livestock farm environments rather than being confined to a single environmental compartment. The absence of E. coli in hospital soil contrasts with its recovery from livestock farm soils in the present study, indicating that environmental conditions and contamination dynamics differ substantially between these settings. The continuous shedding of E. coli in animal feces, accumulation of manure, runoff from animal housing areas, and routine farm management practices likely contribute to the dissemination of enteric bacteria into both wastewater and surrounding soils. In contrast, hospital waste-dumping sites may receive relatively limited direct fecal inputs, and any contamination present may be sporadic or indirect. Consequently, livestock farms may provide multiple environmental niches that support the persistence and circulation of E. coli, increasing opportunities for environmental dissemination and environmental exposure at the human–animal interface. However, the absence of E. coli in hospital soil samples may also be attributed to the limited environmental survival of this organism outside nutrient-rich habitats such as the intestinal tract and wastewater systems. Environmental stresses including desiccation, ultraviolet radiation, temperature fluctuations, nutrient limitation, and competition with native soil microbiota can contribute to rapid die-off of E. coli in soil. In addition, intermittent contamination events, spatial heterogeneity of bacterial distribution in soil, and the relatively small sample size may have reduced the likelihood of E. coli isolation in this study [19, 20].

An additional finding of concern was the recovery of E. coli from all hospital WWTP influent and effluent samples, demonstrating the persistence of this organism throughout the wastewater treatment process. The presence of E. coli in influent samples was expected due to the continuous discharge of fecal waste into hospital sewer systems; however, the recovery of E. coli from all effluent samples suggests that treated effluent may remain a potential source of environmental dissemination of fecal indicator bacteria. Given that only seven WWTP effluent samples were analysed, these findings should be interpreted with caution and are suggestive rather than conclusive evidence regarding the effectiveness of wastewater treatment processes. Further studies involving a larger number of treatment facilities and longitudinal sampling are needed to better evaluate the effectiveness of wastewater treatment processes across different hospital settings.

Notably, 85.71% (6/7) of WWTP isolates exhibited non-susceptibility to at least one antibiotic tested, including isolates recovered from treated effluent. The detection of antibiotic non-susceptible E. coli in effluent is of particular concern because treated wastewater discharged into the environment may contribute to the dissemination of ARB and resistance determinants into surrounding environments. Hospitals are recognized sources of antimicrobial residues, resistant bacteria, and resistance genes, which may create selective pressures within wastewater systems that favour the persistence and spread of resistant organisms. Although the small number of WWTP samples analysed limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions, these findings are consistent with previous studies reporting the survival of AMR E. coli through wastewater treatment processes and support growing evidence that hospital wastewater treatment systems can act as reservoirs and potential dissemination pathways for AMR E. coli into receiving environments [21]. Furthermore, the recovery of non-susceptible E. coli isolates from both influent and effluent samples highlights the importance of strengthening wastewater treatment and surveillance strategies to reduce the environmental release of ARB from healthcare facilities.

Of the five hospitals investigated, the E. coli recovery rates differed significantly (0–45.5%; Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, p = 0.048), indicating variation in the frequency of E. coli detection among hospital environments. The highest E. coli isolation rates were observed in the teaching hospital (H1) and the respiratory specialized hospital (H2), followed by the rheumatology specialized hospital (H3) and the district general hospital (H4), while no E. coli isolates were recovered from the divisional hospital (H5). This pattern suggests a possible trend towards higher E. coli recovery in larger tertiary and specialized healthcare facilities compared with smaller healthcare settings in this study. However, because only one hospital from each category was included in the study and several potentially influential factors, such as patient load, infection prevention and control practices, and the design and condition of on-site sanitation and wastewater systems were not assessed, it is not possible to attribute the observed variation to specific operational or structural determinants. Overall, our findings only indicate that environmental contamination with E. coli differs across the hospital settings studied and may be influenced by site-specific characteristics.

The hospital environments demonstrated a considerable burden of AMR E. coli: 73.3% of isolates were non-susceptible to ≥ 1 antibiotic tested, and one-third were MDR. The AMR patterns observed among the hospital E. coli isolates highlight the impact of antibiotic selection pressure in clinical environments. The highest levels of non-susceptibility were observed for the antibiotics AMP and CIP, followed by CTX and AMC, indicating reduced effectiveness of commonly used first- and second-line antibiotics. This pattern is consistent with frequent use of these agents in hospital settings, which promotes selective pressure and persistence of resistant isolates. In contrast, low levels of resistance to CN and MEM suggest that these antibiotics remain relatively effective, likely due to more restricted use, particularly for severe or resistant infections. However, the detection of even low-level resistance to MEM is concerning, as it represents a last resort antibiotic and may indicate the early emergence of resistance to critical antimicrobial agents. Local antibiotic consumption data and prior studies further support this interpretation. A previous study aimed to identify antibiotic resistance in outpatient settings and its link to consumption of antibiotics in two Teaching Hospitals in Colombo revealed that amoxicillin was the most frequently consumed antibiotic (2.65 Defined Daily Doses (DDD) per 1000 inhabitants per day) and E. coli was most resistant to AMP (85%) [22]. Another study at Colombo North Teaching Hospital and National Hospital for Respiratory Diseases found that 37.8% of patients with community-acquired pneumonia were not managed in accordance with any of the three antibiotic guidelines that were published locally and internationally [23].

Overall, more than half of the E. coli isolates recovered from livestock farm environments (51.61%, 16/31) exhibited non-susceptibility to ≥ 1 antibiotic tested, with AMP showing the highest rate of resistance (14/31, 45.16%), followed by SXT (11/31, 35.48%), CIP (5/31, 16.12%), AMC (3/31, 9.67%). Notably, all isolates remained susceptible to CTX, CN and MEM, suggesting that resistance to critically important third-generation cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, and carbapenems was not evident among the isolates examined. Although the overall prevalence of MDR (3/31, 9.67%) was relatively low, the detection of non-susceptible E. coli in farm environments highlights the potential for exposure through food chains, environmental pathways, and direct human contact.

A statistically significant association was found between farm type and antimicrobial non-susceptibility (Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, p = 0.021), indicating that livestock production systems may influence the occurrence and distribution of resistant E. coli. The rates of non-susceptible E. coli to ≥ 1 antibiotic tested among the livestock farms were highest in poultry farms (8/10, 80%), followed by pig farms (5/8, 62.5%), and lowest in dairy farms (3/13, 23.07%). The resistance profiles also differed among farm types. In poultry farms, the highest non-susceptibility was observed for AMP (8/10, 80%), followed by SXT (5/10, 50%) and CIP (4/10, 40%), AMC (2/10, 20%) with no resistance observed to CTX, CN, or MEM. The relatively high frequency of CIP non-susceptibility among poultry isolates is noteworthy, as fluoroquinolones are considered critically important antimicrobials in human medicine. In pig farms, resistance was predominantly observed for SXT (5/8, 62.5%), followed by AMP (4/8, 50%) and with no resistance to the other antibiotics tested. In contrast, dairy farm isolates exhibited comparatively low levels of non-susceptibility, with AMP (2/13, 15.38%), followed by AMC, SXT and CIP (1/13, 7.69% each), with no resistance to the remaining antibiotics. The predominance of resistance to AMP and SXT across all farm types may reflect the long-standing and widespread use of these antimicrobial classes in livestock production. Their extensive use over many years has likely contributed to the selection and persistence of resistant E. coli populations within farm environments. Conversely, the absence of resistance to CTX, CN, and MEM may indicate limited exposure of livestock-associated E. coli to these antimicrobial agents, particularly MEM, which is generally not approved for veterinary use and is reserved for the treatment of severe infections in humans.

Similar trends have been reported in previous studies, where poultry production systems frequently harbour higher levels of AMR E. coli compared with other livestock sectors. This pattern has often been attributed to differences in antimicrobial usage practices, higher stocking densities, rapid flock turnover, and the intensive nature of poultry production, all of which may increase selective pressure for the emergence and maintenance of resistant bacteria. A study reported 63% of isolates from livestock and associated environmental samples in Sri Lanka were resistant to at least one antimicrobial agent. Resistance was detected against 11 out of 12 tested antibiotics with highest for AMP (52.1%), followed by tetracycline (39.1%), 36.9% to streptomycin, nalidixic acid, and SXT and 30% to CIP [24]. A study investigating the presence of multidrug-resistant enteric organisms in food animals in Colombo district found that 50% of isolates exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotics, with poultry accounting for the majority (48%). Additionally, the study reported a statistically significant increase in E. coli non-susceptibility among broiler chickens, particularly to CIP, AMP, and SXT, compared to isolates from cattle and swine [25]. A study reported that antibiotics in poultry were primarily used for disease prevention and metaphylaxis, while in cattle, they were routinely used to treat mastitis [26]. Livestock farmers also used antibiotics to reduce mortality and boost production. A cross-sectional study in Kegalle District, Sri Lanka, found that antibiotic activity in poultry drinking water due to additives was very high. Antibacterial effects were detected in 33.3% of farms, and 78% of farm owners admitted to using antibiotic-containing additives in drinking water [27]. Taken together, these findings suggest that poultry and pig production systems may represent more important reservoirs of AMR E. coli than dairy farms in the study area. The observed differences between farm types highlight the need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship, improved waste management practices, and continued surveillance of AMR within livestock production systems to limit the emergence and environmental dissemination of resistant bacteria.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of a purposive sampling strategy targeting high-risk sites may limit the generalizability of the results beyond the selected hospitals, livestock farms, and community setting. The study was also conducted within a single district in Sri Lanka, and therefore may not fully represent environmental AMR patterns in other geographical regions.

Second, the sample size within each subgroup, particularly for groundwater and individual hospital or farm categories, was relatively small, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences between groups. In addition, the use of composite sampling for wastewater and soil, while improving representativeness of each site, may have masked spatial variability and reduced the ability to assess point-specific contamination.

Third, the study relied on culture-based methods for the isolation and identification of E. coli, which have lower sensitivity than molecular methods, and the study may underestimate the true burden of AMR, as other resistant bacteria were not assessed. Furthermore, AST was limited to phenotypic methods, and molecular characterization of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) was not performed. As a result, the genetic mechanisms underlying the observed resistance patterns could not be determined.

Fourth, the study focused on the presence and antimicrobial susceptibility of E. coli as an indicator organism and did not quantify bacterial load or assess the presence of other clinically relevant pathogens. Additionally, environmental factors such as antibiotic residue concentrations, wastewater treatment efficiency, and farm management practices were not measured, limiting the ability to identify drivers of resistance.

Finally, groundwater sampling was restricted to wells located within a 500 m radius of a single hospital, which may not adequately capture variability in contamination of community water sources or reflect broader environmental contamination pathways.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important baseline data on environmental AMR across human, animal, and community interfaces, highlighting the need for more comprehensive, large-scale, and longitudinal investigations incorporating molecular and environmental analyses.

Conclusion

Overall, this study highlights the distinct roles of hospital and livestock environments in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Hospital settings demonstrated higher levels of AMR and multidrug resistance, whereas livestock farms contributed a greater burden of environmental E. coli contamination. These findings support the integration of environmental surveillance into national AMR control strategies and reinforce the importance of coordinated interventions across the human, animal, and environmental sectors within a One Health framework. Larger multicentre studies across diverse ecological settings are needed to better characterize the environmental epidemiology of AMR and inform evidence-based surveillance and control strategies in Sri Lanka.
