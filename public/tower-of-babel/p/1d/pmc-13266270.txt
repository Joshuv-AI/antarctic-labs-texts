Evaluating Components of Vocal Effort in Transgender Women
Authors: Kayen Tang, Tanya K Meyer, Joseph Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPrior literature has examined vocal characteristics that transgender individuals may modify to align voice and gender identity, including pitch, resonance, and intonation. These factors, however, do not fully capture the effect of voice on quality of life. Vocal effort, a multidimensional phenomenon which includes mental and physical demand components, correlates with quality of life in adductor laryngeal dystonia (ADLD) but has not been quantitatively assessed in the transgender population. This study aims to evaluate differences in vocal effort among trans women compared to cisgender individuals and those with voice disorders.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study recruited four groups: an experimental and negative control group, trans women and cisgender individuals without laryngeal pathology, and positive control groups, ADLD, and unilateral vocal fold paralysis (UVFP), conditions associated with elevated vocal effort. Participants completed a spontaneous speaking task followed by the NASA Task Load Index (NASA‐TLX) and VHI‐10. The NASA‐TLX has been explored as a novel tool to evaluate vocal effort components, including mental and physical demand.ResultsParticipants included 15 trans women, 16 cisgender individuals, 12 ADLD patients, and 9 UVFP patients. Trans women reported significantly increased mental demand compared to both cisgender (p < 0.01) and ADLD patients (p < 0.01). Trans women, ADLD, and UVFP participants exhibited increased physical demand compared to cisgender individuals (p < 0.0001, p < 0.001, p < 0.01, respectively). VHI‐10 scores were elevated in the trans women, ADLD, and UVFP participants compared to cisgender individuals (p < 0.0001).ConclusionThis study suggests that trans women experience greater vocal effort, including both physical and mental components, compared to cisgender individuals. These findings provide another perspective to understand the effects of gender affirming voice care.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: cognitive workload, gender‐affirming care, NASA‐TLX, transgender voice, vocal effort

Introduction

Transgender is a broad umbrella term used to describe incongruence between one's personal identification of their own gender and their sex assigned at birth. This often leads to a sense of incongruence or misalignment between one's physical attributes and identity [1]. The discomfort or distress associated with this incongruence or misalignment is gender dysphoria. Gender dysphoria can profoundly affect nearly every aspect of one's life and has been linked to decreased overall quality of life [2]. One established approach to alleviate gender dysphoria is through gender affirming care—a range of actions aimed at enhancing an individual's comfort with their gender presentation. Gender affirming care has been associated with increased happiness, higher employment rates, and reduced rates of depression and suicidality among transgender individuals [3, 4, 5].

One critical aspect of gender affirming care involves modifying vocal communication to align with an individual's gender identity. Perceptions of gendered communication are influenced by numerous vocal factors, including pitch, resonance, articulation, and intonation [6]. Gender affirming voice surgery literature has focused on changes in vocal pitch as existing techniques are thought to affect pitch primarily. Interestingly, even patients with no change in speaking fundamental frequency following Wendler glottoplasty, and no change in acoustic measures reflecting voice quality, have improvements in trans woman specific voice related quality of life [7], suggesting that there are other factors affecting speech in this population beyond pitch and the sound of the voice. In the adductor laryngeal dystonia (ADLD) population, vocal effort, or the perceived workload associated with a vocal task or speaking, has been identified as a contributor to voice related quality of life that is not detectable by listeners [8, 9]. Although prior literature has examined factors influencing gender perception in speech, differences in vocal effort among transgender individuals have not been quantitatively evaluated.

The aim of this study is to determine whether vocal effort differs in trans women compared with cisgender individuals. Consensus descriptions of vocal effort note that it is a multidimensional perceptual phenomenon with known physiological and psychological contributors [10]. Prior evaluation of vocal effort in the ADLD population assessed both physical and mental components of vocal effort using the National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index (NASA‐TLX) [11]. The NASA‐TLX is a validated measure of workload that is administered immediately following a task and assesses six dimensions of workload, including mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, effort, frustration, and performance [12, 13]. Building on this prior work, we utilized the NASA‐TLX to obtain a more comprehensive assessment of vocal effort in trans women. We hypothesize that trans women will demonstrate an increase in both the mental and physical NASA‐TLX subscales relative to cisgender individuals.

Materials and Methods

Approval was obtained from the University of Washington Institutional Review Board (STUDY00005453) to perform this study. Participants were recruited from University of Washington Medical Center Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery clinics. All subjects were pre‐screened to determine eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria detailed below. Study groups include the group of interest, trans women; negative control, cisgender individuals; and positive controls, patients with ADLD or unilateral vocal fold paralysis (UVFP). ADLD and UVFP patients were selected as positive controls since prior studies have suggested elevated physical and mental components of vocal effort in ADLD [11] and elevated physical effort in UVFP patients [14].

Inclusion criteria for this study included the following: adults 18 years or older, fluent in English, and meeting study group criteria; trans women without laryngeal pathology, such as vocal fold lesions, infection, vocal fold motion abnormalities, and laryngeal tremor or dystonia, who have not undergone gender affirming vocal surgery; cisgender individuals without laryngeal pathology; and cisgender adults diagnosed with ADLD or UVFP.

Measures

Study measures included the NASA‐TLX, Voice Handicap Index‐10 (VHI‐10), Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7), and Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9).

VHI‐10, GAD‐7, and PHQ‐9 are validated measures of voice‐related quality of life, anxiety, and depression, respectively [15, 16, 17]. VHI‐10 is graded on a scale of 0–40 with higher values indicating lower quality of life. GAD‐7 is graded from 0 to 21 with higher values indicating increased anxiety. PHQ‐9 is graded on a scale of 0–27 with higher values indicating increased depressive ideation. We used the NASA‐TLX, a validated measure of workload, to measure vocal effort components. The NASA‐TLX subscales (mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, effort, frustration, and performance) have traditionally been combined into a single composite score, and validation has been performed on this composite score. However, recent literature asserts that the traditional methods of combining subscales into a single composite score “constitute a serious violation of construct validity: The conceptual equivalent of averaging different temperatures, some in Fahrenheit and some in Celsius” and that individual assessment of the subscales has greater construct validity [18]. As such, subsequent analysis in this manuscript has been performed on individual subscales rather than the previously validated method using composite scores. Although vocal effort is defined as a multidimensional concept [10], only mental and physical demand have been assessed in the context of vocal effort using the NASA‐TLX [11]. Analysis in this study, therefore, was performed only on the mental and physical demand subscales. NASA‐TLX subscales are graded on a 100‐point scale with increased scores indicating increased subscale demand.

Procedures

Participants were first given standardized instructions explaining the NASA‐TLX subscales in written and verbal form. They were then shown a standardized image of Norman Rockwell's “The Waiting Room” and instructed to use “the best version of their ideal voice” to describe the image for 2 min. Immediately following the task, participants completed the NASA‐TLX, VHI‐10, GAD‐7, and PHQ‐9. Description of a standardized image has been previously suggested in the literature as a method of generating spontaneous speech [19]. This prompt was thought to generate more consistent speech content across participants while still preserving spontaneous speech compared to the prompt, “What did you do last weekend?”, which has been used in prior studies using NASA‐TLX to assess vocal load [11]. Spontaneous speech tasks were performed in person or over a video call.

Statistical Analysis and Data Management

A one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD was performed to determine differences across groups in the outcome variables. A Spearman's rank correlation coefficient analysis was performed to determine the direction and impact strength of outcome variables. Spearman's rho less than 0.3 was considered poor, between 0.3 and 0.5 was considered fair, between 0.6 and 0.8 was considered moderately strong, and greater than 0.8 was a very strong correlation [20]. A statistically significant value of p < 0.05 was used in all statistics unless otherwise reported. Statistical analysis was performed with R studio, version 2024.09.1+394. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the Institute of Translational Health Sciences [21, 22].

Results

A total of 52 participants met inclusion criteria and were enrolled between June 2025 and October 2025. The cohort included 15 trans women, 16 cisgender patients, 12 ADLD patients, and 9 UVFP patients. Positive control groups (ADLD and UVFP) were greater in age than the trans and cisgender groups (ANOVA p < 0.0001, Tukey p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in age between the trans woman and cisgender groups (Tukey p = 0.99) (Table 1).

Discussion

Trans women experienced increased vocal effort compared to the cisgender comparison group in this study. Both mental and physical demand components of vocal effort, as measured by NASA‐TLX subscales, were elevated in the trans women group compared to the cisgender group (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001, respectively). Our results complement prior literature suggesting increased voice‐related burden in trans women. For example, Chang et al. demonstrated impaired voice‐related work productivity, while Dacakis et al. noted several psychosocial voice‐related difficulties in trans women [23, 24, 25]. Moreover, while elevated vocal effort in trans women has been identified in qualitative interviews [26], to our knowledge the findings of this study represent the first quantitative report of elevated vocal effort in trans women.

Characterizing the vocal effort profile of trans women may offer an important additional perspective in gender affirming voice care. For instance, Chang et al. examined acoustic measures and the Trans Woman Voice Questionnaire (TWVQ), a measure of trans woman voice related quality of life, before and after Wendler glottoplasty. Patients who had no change in average speaking fundamental frequency still noted improvement in quality of life [7]. These findings suggest that factors beyond speaking pitch, perhaps loss of access to lower pitch registers [27] or vocal effort, may contribute meaningfully to voice‐related quality of life. While changes in pitch are readily measurable and perceived by listeners, effort is not easily audible and can only be measured by subjective self‐assessment by speakers. Prior work has shown that speakers' assessment of vocal effort does not correlate with listener‐perceived effort [28]. Thus, understanding vocal effort from a patients' reporting can provide valuable insight that may facilitate more tailored and comprehensive gender affirming voice interventions.

The elevated vocal effort among trans women in this study highlights the complex cognitive and physical burdens associated with speech production in this population. Producing a voice that aligns with one's gender identity may require ongoing modulation of pitch, resonance, and pragmatics [29]. This continuous self‐regulation may contribute to a heightened sense of mental demand and fatigue. From a physiologic perspective, efforts to feminize voice production may involve increased laryngeal tension and elevated maximum flow declination rates [30]. Additionally, aerodynamic studies have shown that trans women often exhibit patterns more consistent with normative male high‐pitch phonation, as well as sound pressure levels exceeding female norms [31]. These sustained, nonhabitual physiological adaptations may lead to earlier vocal fatigue, instability in voice production, and increased physical strain.

While both positive control groups showed expected elevation in voice‐related physical demand, our ADLD positive control did not demonstrate elevated mental demand compared to cisgender participants. This finding contrasts with prior work by Chang et al., which reported higher mental demand NASA‐TLX subscale scores in ADLD patients than seen in this study [11]. Notably, no comparison was made to a healthy control group to confirm elevated mental demand in the ADLD group in that study. One possible explanation for the possible discrepancy in mental demand measurements is the difference in experimental design. Chang et al. employed a brief 30 s recall task (“please discuss what you did over the weekend”) while using their ideal voice, requiring simultaneous memory retrieval and speech production, whereas our two‐minute image description task lacked a memory recall component, potentially reducing cognitive demand. This highlights the importance of methodological consistency, as comparison between studies will be more difficult with different durations of spontaneous speech or prompt changes. For example, if we prompt ADLD patients to read paragraphs with a high percentage of voiced sounds, patients may report greater voice‐related physical demand but lower mental demand than a spontaneous speech task.

Notably, trans women exhibited greater mental demand than even ADLD patients. This may reflect the relatively long duration, 2 min, provided for the spontaneous speech task. ADLD patients may have less time pressure as they utilize their behavioral coping strategies, such as deliberate word choice, to minimize speaking challenges. It is possible that reduced time pressure may not change the cognitive load in trans women as they need to monitor every word and phrase spoken throughout the entirety of the task regardless of the word choice; voice‐related dysphoria is unlikely to be tied to specific phonemes or words. Previous research shows that deliberate control and self‐monitoring of voice production engages auditory‐motor feedback and executive control networks [32, 33, 34]. Prolonged reliance on these systems may increase cognitive load, especially in transgender individuals who must continuously monitor and adjust vocal output to maintain gender‐congruent speech.

We also observed significantly higher GAD‐7 and PHQ‐9 scores, measures of anxiety and depression, in trans women compared to ADLD participants, and higher PHQ‐9 scores in trans women compared to cisgender participants. This finding contrasts with prior literature reporting elevated psychiatric comorbidity in individuals with ADLD relative to the general population [35]. Anecdotally, ADLD participants in our study reported that their symptoms were well controlled with regular botulinum toxin injections, which may have mitigated anxiety and stress related to voice use. We did, however, administer the NASA‐TLX just prior to botulinum toxin injection, a time at which speech demands are typically greater. The higher PHQ‐9 score in trans women compared to the cisgender group may suggest some level of confounding with the primary findings of this study, greater mental and physical components of vocal effort in trans women compared to cisgender individuals. Indeed, we observed significant correlations between psychological metrics and vocal effort, with both the PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7 scores demonstrating stronger associations with mental demand than with physical demand. These findings suggest that psychological state may modulate perceived vocal effort, consistent with existing literature [36] and consensus definitions of vocal effort [10]. On the other hand, it is conceivable that greater vocal effort, particularly in those with vocal gender dysphoria, may also lead to worsened psychological state. This relationship highlights the importance of considering emotional well‐being when evaluating vocal effort and designing voice interventions [37]. These considerations are particularly relevant given the elevated prevalence of depression, anxiety, and suicidality among transgender individuals [38]. The relationship of depression and vocal effort in the trans population may be further examined in larger studies with greater power to correct for these variables.

Limitations of this study include the use of subjective self‐report measures only. Per consensus definitions of vocal effort, vocal effort is “the vocalists' perception of exertion and work associated with voice production [and] is by definition measured via self‐report” [10]. Use of subjective measures is therefore essential for capturing pertinent individualized and perceptual experiences. However, self‐report measures may be influenced by psychological state, interpretation biases, and response biases. Incorporating objective physiologic and behavioral measures, such as heart rate variability, eye‐tracking, or aerodynamic measurements, could provide complementary insight into vocal effort.

Additionally, limitations include relatively small sample sizes that may limit statistical power and generalizability, as well as increase susceptibility to the influence of outliers. Although these sample sizes are comparable to prior exploratory work in voice research, a larger cohort could allow for more robust group comparisons and subgroup analyses. Furthermore, given our cross‐sectional study design and elevated mental health metrics in trans women, we are not able to determine causality in this study. Finally, as mentioned previously, age represents a potential confounding factor, as participants with ADLD and UVFP were older on average than transgender or cisgender participants. This risk of confounding is particularly relevant as age‐related vocal atrophy would be expected to increase vocal effort. Notably, age was not different between the trans and cisgender groups; therefore, previously mentioned comparisons would not be affected by age‐related confounding. In fact, our finding of increased mental demand in transgender women compared to ADLD patients despite potential greater age‐related vocal fold changes may further attest to the severity of their vocal effort. Future studies employing fully age‐matched cohorts would further clarify vocal effort profiles in these populations.

Conclusion

In summary, trans women exhibited elevated vocal effort, including physical and mental components, compared to cisgender individuals in this study. Increased vocal effort, in addition to voice‐related misgendering, may contribute to decreased voice‐related quality of life in transgender individuals. These findings may provide another perspective to understand the effects of gender affirming care.
