Risk of hospitalization and opioid use in older people with dementia
Authors: Christina Jensen-Dahm, Janet Janbek, Thomas Munk Laursen, Christiane Gasse, Gunhild Waldemar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder people with dementia are frequently prescribed opioids, but little is known about adverse events associated with opioid use in this population. The aim was to investigate whether opioid use was associated with an increased risk of hospitalization in older people with dementia.MethodsMatched cohort study using Danish nationwide registries. All Danish residents (≥ 65 years) diagnosed with dementia between 2008 and 2018 were included. Individuals with opioid use in the year prior to dementia diagnosis were excluded. Exposure was first redeemed opioid prescription after dementia diagnosis. Persons exposed to opioids were matched with up to two unexposed persons on age and sex. Outcome was all-cause 180-days hospitalization. Cox proportional hazards models compared rates of hospitalization within 180 days between the exposed and unexposed and adjusted for potential confounders.ResultsForty-two percent (31,619/75,471) of older people with dementia redeemed an opioid prescription after diagnosis. Among exposed, 7,356 (23.3%) were hospitalized within 180 days, compared with 7,618 (12.1%) of unexposed, and opioid use was associated with a 4-fold increased risk of hospitalization (fully adjusted hazard ratio: 3.86 (95% confidence interval, 3.72–4.01)), and 8-fold within the first 14 days ((7.77 (7.15–8.45)). Exposed were more often admitted due to fractures, infections, and cardiovascular events.ConclusionOpioid use was associated with increased risk of hospitalization, which may be due to the opioid, the indication, or both. This observation calls for further research into potential harmful effects associated with opioid use as it can have important implications for patient safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07347-4.

Introduction

Currently, more than 55 million people are living with dementia worldwide and every year, there are nearly 10 million new cases [1]. Older people with dementia often suffer from multimorbidity, and pain-causing conditions are frequent [2, 3]. Previous research has shown that opioid use was frequent among older people in Denmark and was more frequent among those with dementia [4, 5], which could not be explained by a higher degree of comorbidity alone [4]. The frequent opioid use in older people with dementia may be related to the fact that behavioral symptoms in some cases were interpreted as related to pain [6, 7]. Opioids have several well-known side effects such as constipation, nausea, sedation, and respiratory depression. In a population of surgical patients, 10% experienced an opioid-related adverse event that was associated with higher risk of death, longer hospital stays, and higher readmission rates [8]. In the general population, opioid use has also been associated with adverse events such as an increased risk of falls [9, 10] and fractures [11] and the risk may be dose dependent and highest with strong opioids [9]. Thus, opioids are among the fall-risk increasing drugs [12]. Furthermore, opioid use has been associated with an increased risk of pneumonia [13] and cardiovascular events [14, 15] .

Studies on adverse events associated with opioid use in older people with dementia are sparse and this population is typically excluded from randomized controlled trials on analgesics limiting the knowledge we have about effect and side effects [16]. Prior studies have shown that opioids are associated with an increased risk of hospitalization for pneumonia [17] and hip fracture in patients with Alzheimer’s disease [18]. Whether the risk only relates to specific disorders or relates to all-cause hospitalization is unknown, but dementia is a risk factor for opioid-related adverse events in older people [19, 20]. In a recent study we found that new opioid use was associated with an increased mortality risk in older people with dementia [21]. Thus, it is crucial to advance our knowledge about adverse events associated with opioid use in older people with dementia to ensure appropriate prescribing. The aim of this study was to investigate if new opioid use compared to no opioid use was associated with an increased risk of all-cause hospitalization in a nationwide population of older people with dementia. A secondary aim was to investigate the most frequent causes of hospitalization.

Method

Data sources

The study was based on data from nationwide Danish registers, which contain information on all inhabitants of Denmark. Denmark has a tax-financed health care system that provides equal access to all residents. Because individuals are given a permanent personal civil registration number at the time of birth or immigration, nationwide registries allow data retrieval at an individual level [22]. This study linked individual-level data from national registries using the civil registration number. Data on all hospital admissions (since 1977), outpatient contacts, and emergency room visits (since 1995) were retrieved from the National Patient Register [23]. The Psychiatric Central Research Register has information on all admissions to psychiatric hospitals since 1969 and outpatient contacts since 1995 [24]. Information on date of death was retrieved from Statistics Denmark and nursing home residence from the Danish Health Data Authority. The Danish National Prescription Register contains data on all prescription drugs dispensed since 1995 [25].

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective matched cohort study. The study population included individuals with incident dementia (first record of dementia) from January 1, 2008 (start of inclusion) to December 31, 2018 (end of study) who were 65 years or older at the time of diagnosis. Individuals were defined as having dementia if they had been registered with a dementia diagnosis (definition Table S1) during admission or at an outpatient visit and/or had redeemed at least one prescription for an anti-dementia drug (ATC: N06D). If individuals had been diagnosed with dementia and/or redeemed a prescription for an anti-dementia drug before January 1, 2008 (searching back from the beginning of the registries) they were excluded. A “new user” design was used to avoid survival bias due to inclusion of prevalent opioid users [26]. Individuals were included in the cohort as ‘‘exposed’’ at the time of the first filled opioid prescription after dementia diagnosis and the day of first opioid exposure was defined as the index date. On the day of the first redemption of an opioid drug prescription, the exposed individuals were matched on age (age ± 180 days) and sex with up to two individuals without an opioid prescription at the time. For unexposed, the matching date was defined as the index date. Unexposed individuals could become exposed later if they redeemed an opioid prescription. Matching was performed to balance the population according to age and sex.

We applied the following exclusion criteria. A washout period of 365 days of non-opioid-use before dementia diagnosis was applied to avoid bias due to inclusion of prevalent users [26]. Exposed and unexposed were excluded if they at any time before index date (1) had been diagnosed with “opioid addiction” (ICD-10: F112x, F113x, F114x; ICD-8: 304.09, 304.19), or (2) had redeemed a prescription of methadone (N07BC02). Exposed individuals were also excluded if their first opioid prescription was in the form of subcutaneous/iv administration as this could indicate terminal illness. We also excluded if the first prescription was sublingual buprenorphine as this is used in the treatment of opioid addiction.

Exposure /Opioid treatment

Exposure was defined as a first community purchased opioid prescription (ATC: N02A + R05DA04 (Codeine)), for exact ATC codes see Table S2) after dementia diagnosis. Opioids included in anti-tussive agents were not included except for codeine (R05DA04).

Covariates

Covariates were chosen based on their potential for confounding the relation between opioid use and hospital admission and were evaluated at index date. We included sociodemographic factors (i.e., age, sex). As measures of dementia severity, we used time since dementia diagnosis and living status (community dwelling or nursing home). As indicators of disease burden, we included all-cause hospital admissions (dichotomous) in the 30 days before index date, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI, excluding dementia)(see Table S3 for definition) [27] and chronic pain diagnosis (Table S4A). To assess comorbidity beyond registered diagnoses in hospital registries, we used data on the total number of drugs used other than opioids in the 6 months before index date (grouped as 0, 1–4, 5–9, ≥ 10 drugs) [28, 29].

Outcome

Outcome was all-cause hospitalization within 180 days after index date (opioid exposure or matching). Hospitalization was defined as first inpatient or emergency room visit after index date. We included both inpatient and emergency room visits to capture all acute hospital contacts. We also investigated the most frequent causes of hospitalization by exploring the five most frequently registered discharge diagnoses (primary and secondary) and frequency of diagnosis per ICD-10 chapter. Furthermore, we specifically wanted to look at the frequency of hospitalization with fractures, any infection, respiratory infections, and cardiovascular events (for diagnostic code see Table S4B).

Statistical analysis

Exposed and unexposed individuals were followed from index date until the date of hospitalization (outcome), death, drug discontinuation (exposed only), emigration, completion of 180 days of follow-up, or the end of study, whichever occurred first. Unexposed individuals were censored on the date they redeemed a prescription for an opioid and became exposed. The individual could receive multiple opioid prescriptions, but once a person had been exposed, they no longer contributed person-time as unexposed. If the drug was discontinued the exposed individual was censored. The opioid was considered discontinued if they did not redeem a new prescription within 45 days (30 days + 14 days grace period). A Cox regression analysis with time since index date as the time scale was applied (proc phreg). As the hazard rate (HR) was not constant, the proportional hazards assumption was not met for the exposure (p < 0.0001). Thus, the HR needs to be interpreted as a weighted average of the true HRs over the entire follow-up period [30, 31]. However, we addressed this by conducting a stratified Cox model to assess hazard rate in different time intervals (0–14 days, 15–30 days, 31–60 days, 61–90 days and 91–180 days). Statistical analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). We first calculated age and sex adjusted hazard rate estimates. The two populations differed in terms of baseline characteristics and to account for differences in baseline characteristics the analyses were adjusted for age, sex, time since dementia diagnosis, nursing home residency, admission to hospital within the past 30 days, CCI, chronic pain diagnosis, and number of drugs in the past 6 months (fully adjusted analysis). We estimated the short-term (0–14 days, 15–30 days, 31–60 days) and long-term hospitalization HR (61–90 and 91–180 days). We examined the hazard rate associated with all opioids, strong opioids, weak opioids (Table S2 for ATC codes), and individual opioids (morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl buprenorphine, tramadol and codeine). Opioid subtype was determined by the first prescription and for weak opioids censoring was performed if they switched to a strong opioid.

To evaluate the impact of the competing risk of death, we performed a Cox regression analysis and calculated the HR for the following: (1) Hospitalization (main analysis), (2) Death, and (3) a composite endpoint of hospitalization or death, whichever occurred first. We also calculated cumulative incidence function of (1) Hospitalization while accounting for death as a competing event and (2) Death while accounting for hospitalization as a competing event, using the Aalen–Johansen estimator for both outcomes [32].

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted. To investigate if the hazard rate of hospitalization was associated with terminal phases of dementia, we first restricted the analysis to new opioid use within the first year after diagnosis. To control for readmissions, we performed two separate analyses: (1) We excluded patients who had been admitted to the hospital within the past 30 days (all admissions) and (2) We started follow-up seven days after index date. To control for confounding by indication we excluded patients who had been given a cancer diagnosis within the past 2 years and performed an analysis only including individuals with a chronic pain disorder. Last, we stratified the analysis by sex, age-groups (< 80 years and ≥ 80 years) and calendar year (2008–2012 and 2013–2018). To explore if censoring at discontinuation affected our results, we performed a sensitivity analysis where we did not censor at discontinuation. A post-hoc analysis including information about the use of an antipsychotic (N05A) or sedative/hypnotic (N05B and N05C) in the 6 months prior to index was performed.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The selection of the population can be found in the supplemental material (Figure S1). From January 1, 2008 to December 31, 2018 there were 92,475 people (age ≥ 65 years) diagnosed with dementia for the first time. After applying exclusion criteria, 75,471 individuals with incident dementia were eligible for the study. Of them, 31,619 exposed individuals were matched to 63,235 unexposed (three exposed had only one control). Exposed individuals were more likely to live at a nursing home, had more comorbidity (higher CCI and chronic pain disorders), more likely to have been admitted to the hospital within the past 30 days and used more prescription drugs (Table 1).

Risk of hospitalization associated with opioid use

Among the exposed, 23.3% (7356/31,619) were hospitalized within 180 days after initiating their first opioid prescription compared to 12.1% (7618/63,235) of the unexposed. Table 2 presents the crude hospitalization rates and the fully adjusted hazard rates. Exposure to any opioid was associated with a 4-fold increased hazard rate of hospitalization within 180 days (fully adjusted HR: 3.86 (95% confidence interval (CI), 3.72–4.01)). For all opioids the hazard rate was greatest within the first 14 days where opioid use was associated with an 8-fold increased hazard rate of hospitalization (7.77 (7.15–8.45)), but there was still a 3-fold increased hazard rate of hospitalization after 90 days (3.04 (2.80–3.30)). Excluding those with an admission within the past 30 days lowered the hazard rate significantly, but opioid use was still associated with a 3-fold increased hazard rate (Table S7-S9). Other sensitivity analyses did not change the overall results (Tables S7-9). A post-hoc analysis where we additionally adjusted for use of an antipsychotic or sedative/hypnotic did not change the result (3.85 (3.71–3.99)).

To assess the competing risk of death we assessed the hazard rate of any event i.e., either hospitalization or death within 180 days. 43% of exposed were either hospitalized or died within 180 days after initiating an opioid, but only 16% of the unexposed (adjusted HR: 4.36 (4.24–4.49)) (Table 3). The cumulative incidence curves for hospitalization are shown in Fig. 1A and for death in Fig. 1B and numbers are given in Table S6. For all opioids, the cumulative incidence of hospitalization, when taking death as a competing risk into account, was 11.2% (95% CI; 10.8–11.5%) at day 15 and 33.7% (32.9–34.5%) at day 180. For the unexposed, the cumulative incidence of hospitalization was 1.3% (1.2–1.4%) at day 15 and 12.8% (12.5–13.1%) at day 180 (Table S6).

Subtypes of opioids

The hazard rate of hospitalization was slightly lower for strong opioids compared to weak opioids, but when looking at a combined measure of hospitalization and death the hazard rate was higher for strong opioids (Table 3). When looking at subtypes of strong opioids, oxycodone was associated with the highest hazard rate of hospitalization (5.18 (4.47–6.01)) and fentanyl (2.72 (2.32–3.19)), tramadol (3.11 (2.96–3.27)) and codeine (2.82 (2.58–3.07)) the lowest. When the hazard rate of any event (hospitalization and death combined) was considered, morphine, oxycodone and fentanyl were associated with a 5-fold hazard rate of any event. Discussion

In this nationwide registry-based study, we explored the risk of hospitalization associated with opioid use in patients with dementia, which has received little prior attention. 23% of those exposed to an opioid were hospitalized within 180-days and opioid use was associated with a 4-fold hazard rate of hospitalization within 180 days, and an 8-fold hazard rate within the first 14 days. A large proportion of both exposed and unexposed individuals with dementia were hospitalized for unspecified diagnoses. Exposed were more likely to be diagnosed with a fracture, an infection, or cardiovascular disease, which could be related to side effects of opioids, although we cannot exclude that confounding by indication may have affected our results.

To our knowledge, no prior study has investigated the hazard rate of all-cause hospitalization associated with opioid use in older people with dementia. We found a 4-fold increased hazard rate of all-cause hospitalization. An increased risk of hospitalization after opioid exposure has previously been described in other populations such as primary care patients [33], patients with arthritis [34], kidney disorders [33, 35] and chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder [20]. We found that the hazard rate of hospitalization was slightly lower for strong opioids compared to weak opioids. When looking at individual opioids the hazard rate of hospitalization was highest for morphine and oxycodone, intermediate for tramadol and codeine and lowest for fentanyl and buprenorphine. However, 51.2% of fentanyl users died before (potentially) being hospitalized or the end of follow-up, and in comparison, only 4.6% of tramadol users. Thus, the competing risk of death is significant among fentanyl users but also for the other strong opioids. Therefore, when taking the combined hazard rate of events in to account the hazard rate was higher for strong opioids than for weak opioids. In previous studies long-acting opioids was shown to be associated with opioid related adverse events [8, 19], which is in accordance with our findings.

In the general population, opioid use has been associated with an increased risk of falls/fracture [9, 11, 36–38], pneumonia [13] and cardiovascular events [15, 39, 40]. In a study by Hamina et al. they investigated the risk of pneumonia associated with new opioid use in older people with Alzheimer’s disease and reported a 34% increased risk of hospital-treated pneumonia among opioid users compared to non-users, and this risk was most profound in the first 60 days of opioid use. The same study group found that opioid use has been associated with an increased risk of hip fracture in patients with Alzheimer’s disease [18]. In our study, a large proportion of both exposed and unexposed were diagnosed with unspecified diagnoses at hospitalization, but the exposed were more likely to be diagnosed with a fracture, any infection, respiratory infections, or cardiovascular disease. In accordance with our study, previous studies have found an increased short-term risk [17, 18, 41, 42]. We did not aim to investigate the risk of pneumonia or hip fracture but found an almost 4-fold increased hazard rate of all-cause hospital admissions. The study by Hamina et al. is more selected compared to our population as they exclude nursing home residents and patients with previous pneumonia, admission within the past 6 month and cancer, which may, partly, explain the difference in magnitude of the associations.

The increased hazard rate of hospitalization could have several possible explanations. Opioids are associated with side-effects such as sedation/somnolence and dizziness, which may lead to hospitalizations with falls and fractures and opioids are a fall-risk increasing drug [12]. It has also been shown that opioids can lead to respiratory depression and have immunosuppressive properties [43], which may contribute to increased frequency of hospitalizations due to infections. Further, opioids have been associated with myocardial ischemia and the development of heart failure [14, 40, 44], which in turn can lead to increased hospitalizations with these conditions. We found that exposed individuals were more often admitted due to infections, pneumonia, fractures and cardiovascular events, which could explain part of the increased hazard rate of hospitalization. In our study, two of the most frequently registered diagnoses upon hospitalizations following opioid use reflect unspecified symptoms (Z diagnoses -ICD-10 classification), which could indicate that many of the patients were admitted due to a change in health status, but no specific disease could be identified during hospitalization. Patients with dementia have problems communicating their symptoms due to aphasia, reduced insight and/or memory problems and for these reasons, it may be difficult to establish the cause. Previous studies have shown an association between opioid use and the incidence of dementia [45, 46]. Opioid use after a dementia diagnosis may exacerbate dementia-related symptoms, thereby potentially increasing the risk of hospitalization in the presence of multimorbidity. In our study, the individuals exposed to opioids were more likely to be exposed to polypharmacy. Older people are more prone to adverse effects from opioids than younger individuals because of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes and polypharmacy secondary to their higher rates of comorbidities [2, 16, 47] and part of the increased hazard rate of hospitalizations may be explained by drug-drug interactions leading to hospital admission. In a study investigating opioid-related adverse events in older people, dementia was found to be the strongest risk factor [19]. Our study showed that opioid use in older people with dementia are associated with a 4-fold hazard rate of all cause hospitalization. Due to the observational study design, causal interpretation is not possible, and the detected increased hazard rate could be due to confounding by indication related to the severity of the underlying condition for which the opioids were prescribed. Thus, more studies are needed to investigate the risk associated with opioid use, but regardless of the causal pathway, it is important for clinicians to bear this in mind when older people with dementia are admitted to the hospital, as it may be due to side-effects from opioids.

One of the major strengths of this study was that it is based on data from Danish healthcare registries, which have complete coverage of the entire Danish population including all diagnosed dementia cases and without loss to follow-up. Furthermore, it contains information on all redeemed opioid prescriptions and hospital admissions. Weaknesses of this study include the observational study design and therefore it is not possible to draw conclusions regarding causality. Second, those exposed to opioids had a higher disease burden, which may influence the rate of hospitalization. Furthermore, hospitalization is common among nursing home residents due to various factors [48], which could influence our results. We accounted for disease burden in our analysis by adjusting for CCI, chronic pain disorders, hospital admission with the past 30 days and number of prescription drugs, which changed the results slightly. As measures of dementia severity, we included information about nursing home residency and time since dementia diagnosis, but our data does not have information about stage of disease or cognitive function. However, these measures may not have captured the entire disease burden or accounted for the severity of illness, and we cannot exclude residual confounding. Third, 35% of exposed individuals had been admitted to the hospital within the past 30 days before starting an opioid. To account for readmissions, we first excluded all individuals who had been admitted to the hospital within 30 days before index date and second, only assessed events occurring seven days after index date, which decreased the estimated hazard rate of hospitalization, but did not change the overall result. Fourth, we investigated the causes of admissions, and the majority appeared to be unplanned and acute, but we cannot rule out planned hospitalizations. Fifth, we are unable to account for the indication for opioid use and confounding by indication is a major concern in observational studies where the associated risk can be attributed to the indication for the drug rather than the drug itself. For example, confounding by indication may result from our inability to account for the severity of pain, which may lead to the prescription of opioids, but in a subgroup analysis of individuals with a chronic pain diagnosis the results remained unchanged. Both NSAIDs and paracetamol are available over the counter and including them as active-comparator drugs would lead to exposure misclassification. Opioids may be used in end-of-life palliative care, which could confound the association. We addressed this issue by excluding people with exposure to parenteral opioid formulations as these are often used in end-of-life palliative care outside hospitals. Furthermore, we restricted our analysis to only assessing events from the seventh day after the index date of a first prescription for an opioid, which decreased the association slightly but did not change the overall results. However, we cannot exclude that our results are contaminated by residual confounding by end-of-life treatment. Furthermore, opioids may be used to treat behavioral symptoms based on an assumption of underlying pain [6, 7], which may be due to undiagnosed medical conditions, leading to increased risk of hospitalization. Sixth, the Danish registries do not provide valid data on the actual consumed dose, but only dates of redeemed prescriptions to assess the duration of exposure, and our data were therefore analyzed as “intention-to-treat”. We considered the drug to be discontinued if the exposed did not redeem a new prescription within 45 days, but if it was discontinued before it could underestimate the risk since the individuals were still considered exposed. Seventh, a registry-based dementia diagnosis has been shown to have a high validity [49], but similar to other populations it is estimated that around 50% of dementia cases remained undiagnosed in Denmark. Thus, we cannot know whether our results apply to undiagnosed cases. Eighth, to capture cases diagnosed in the primary health care sector, we included redemption of an anti-dementia drugs as an indicator of dementia. We cannot exclude off-label use of anti-dementia drugs and therefor misclassification, but to our knowledge this is not common practice. Lastly, opioid prescribing varies considerably between countries with prevalence ranging from 3.6% to 44% [50–52]. Opioid use is frequent in Denmark [53] and our findings may not apply to countries with more restrictive opioid prescribing practices.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results from this nationwide study of older people with incident dementia showed a 4-fold increased 180-day hazard rate of hospitalization among patients initiating an opioid, with an 8-fold increased hazard rate in the first two weeks. Exposed individuals were more likely to be discharged with a diagnosis of fracture, infection, respiratory infection, or cardiovascular events. Due to the observational study design, it is important to emphasize that we cannot exclude that confounding by indication may affect our results. Nevertheless, the observed associations in our study are important to consider for patient safety, and we call for more research to investigate potential harmful effects associated with opioid use.
