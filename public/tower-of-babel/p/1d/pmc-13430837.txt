Predicting short birth intervals in Bangladesh using stacked machine learning and SHAP explainability: evidence from BDHS 2022
Authors: Mostakim Mia, Shanti Akter, Takyeatun Nesa Sraboni, Md. Merajul Islam, Mst Tawabunnahar, Md. Maniruzamman, Md. Golam Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShort birth interval (SBI) is a major public health concern, associated with adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal depletion. Identifying its influential predictors is essential for improving family planning and maternal health interventions. The study aimed to predict SBI and identify its influential predictors using stacked machine learning and SHAP explainability.MethodsThis study used data, obtained from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), 2022. The dataset comprised 11,872 respondents, of whom 2,137 experienced SBI (18.0%). Class imbalance problem was addressed by applying class weighting during model training. Boruta and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-based methods were applied to identify the most important predictors of SBI. Subsequently, multiple machine learning models (including logistic regression, random forest, extreme gradient boosting, categorical boosting, artificial neural network, and stacking ensemble) were used to predict SBI and evaluated their model performances using accuracy, precision, sensitivity, F1-score, and the area under the curve (AUC). Finally, SHAP explainability were employed to identify the most influential predictors of SBI.ResultsThe stacking ensemble model achieved the highest predictive performance compared to the individual models, with an accuracy of 65.8%, precision of 42.6%, sensitivity of 72.3%, F1-score of 53.6%, and modest AUC of approximately 0.667. The SHAP analysis showed that no educated respondents, higher parity, and do not intend to use contraceptive method were the most influential predictors of SBI.ConclusionInterventions could therefore focus on improving female education, expanding access to contraceptives, and promoting awareness of optimal birth spacing. Policymakers may incorporate SHAP explainability to support data-driven reproductive health strategies to reduce SBI in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02367-0.

Introduction

The interval between consecutive births is a crucial aspect of fertility management and family planning [1]. Short birth interval (SBI) is an important reproductive-health indicator, as it is linked to maternal health [2, 3], family-planning programs [4], and demographic transitions [5, 6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines SBI as less than 33 months between two successive live births [7]. Globally, approximately one-fourth of live births occurred within short intervals [8]. The prevalence of SBI was 57% in Sub-Saharan Africa [9] and 33% in Central Asia [8]. In Bangladesh, several studies have showed that the prevalence of SBI ranges from approximately 18% to 26% [10–14]. SBI prevalence varied across regions, highlighting regional difference in maternal and child health outcomes [8].

The variation in SBI prevalence across regions may be due to differences in maternal education, access to family planning services, socio-economic status, and cultural practices [8, 14, 15]. SBI are associated with adverse maternal and child outcomes, including preterm birth [6], low birth weight [6], small-for-gestational-age infants [16], neonatal and infant mortality [17] and maternal complications such as preeclampsia, hypertension and premature rupture of membranes [5, 18]. These complications contribute substantially to maternal and child mortality in low- and middle-income countries, including Bangladesh.

Despite improvements in family planning coverage and maternal health services, SBI remains prevalent and continues to contribute to maternal and child health burdens in Bangladesh [19]. Predicting which women are at higher risk of SBI and identifying its influential predictors is essential for designing effective interventions to promote optimal birth spacing and improve maternal and child health outcomes. Such interventions in align with the Millennium Development Goals (MDG)/ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) framing, noting that the MDGs concluded in 2015, and that the subsequent SDGs retain emphasis on SDG target 3.1 (Reducing Maternal Mortality) and SDG target 3.7 (Universal Access to Sexual and Reproductive Health Services) [20]. Previous studies in Bangladesh have primarily used traditional statistical methods such as logistic regression to examine predictors of SBI [10, 21, 22]. However, traditional models often assume linear relationships between predictors and outcomes, and may not capture complex nonlinear relationship or higher-order interactions among sociodemographic, reproductive, and household variables. As a result, such methods may overlook the influence predictors, limiting the effectiveness of SBI risk prediction. At present, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful method for predictive modeling, offering the ability to handle large, heterogeneous data, capture complex nonlinear relationships, and facilitate deeper insights into the interconnected predictors of reproductive-health outcomes [23, 24]. Stacking is an ensemble ML method that combines predictions from multiple base learners using a meta-learner, to exploit their complementary strengths, reduce individual model bias and variance, and improve overall predictive performance and generalizability on unseen data [25, 26].

Moreover, the interpretability of ML models has been enhanced through Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) explainability (global and local) [27, 28], which enables to identify the contribution of each predictor to individual predictions. SHAP facilitates actionable insights for policymakers and health program planners. When combined with SHAP explainability, ML further translates model outputs into interpretable insights that support targeted, equity-focused policy interventions, such as prioritizing postpartum family-planning services, improving breastfeeding support, and addressing access barriers among vulnerable groups. Thus, ML with SHAP explainability serves as a decision-support tool that strengthens evidence-based reproductive-health policymaking rather than being used only for prediction.

Previously, several studies have been conducted by ML approaches to predict SBI and identify its influential predictors among reproductive-aged women in Ethiopia [29] and East-Africa [30]. These studies highlight the potential of ML based methods to uncover complex patterns and inform evidence-based health interventions. Despite this progress globally, no study has yet applied ML techniques to predict SBI in Bangladesh. To address this gap, the present study applies a stacking model combined with SHAP explainability to predict SBI and identify its influential predictors among reproductive-aged women in Bangladesh using the latest nationally representative Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), 2022 data. The BDHS, 2022 provides comprehensive, up-to-date information on reproductive behavior, reproductive-health inequalities (postpartum family planning access, health system barriers, breastfeeding duration), maternal and child health, household characteristics, and contraceptive use, offering a valuable resource for predictive modeling. This approach not only enhances predictive performance by incorporating stacking ML-based model but also provides interpretable insights by SHAP explainability, allowing policymakers and health practitioners to support data-driven reproductive health strategies to reduce SBI in Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This study utilized secondary data, extracted from BDHS, 2022 survey. It was designed to collect comprehensive information on health, fertility, and demographic characteristics [31]. Data collection followed a two-stage stratified sampling design. In the 1st stage, 675 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling, comprising 237 urban and 438 rural EAs. In the 2nd stage, a complete listing of households within each selected EA was conducted, and 45 households per EA were systematically selected for interviews. In total, 30,330 households were included, of which 19,665 from rural and 10,665 from urban areas.

Study population and sample selection

The study targeted ever-married women of reproductive age (15–49 years), and 20,220 women were interviewed. Respondents were asked to provide a comprehensive birth history for all live births occurring within the survey period. The BDHS, 2022 dataset initially comprised 64,722 women. After excluding missing and uncertain responses (don’t know), 11,872 cases were retained for the final analysis as shown in Fig. 1.

Variable description

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was SBI, defined by the duration between two consecutive live births. According to WHO guideline, a birth interval of less than 33 months for the most recent birth interval is considered as a SBI [7]. For analysis, the variable was coded as binary outcome: “1” for intervals < 33 months and “0” for intervals ≥ 33 months.

Explanatory variables

We considered a set of explanatory variables for SBI based on the previous studies and the availability of data in the BDHS, 2022 database [9, 10, 13, 21, 32]. This study used explanatory variables such as (i) demographic factors (age, sex, division, residence, and religion); (ii) educational and economic factors (education, husband’s education, husband’s occupation, working status, and wealth status); (iii) health and reproductive factors (body mass index (BMI), contraceptive use, birth order, age at first birth, child sex, living children, and total children); and (iv) media and communication factors (watching television, mobile phone, radio, newspaper/ magazine, and internet use) as presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The data was prepared using survey weights before conducting statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study participant, while cross-tabulations were examined the frequency and percentages of SBI status across explanatory variables as well as evaluated their association using the chi-square test (p-value < 0.05). These significant variables were incorporated in the subsequent analysis. The dataset was then partitioned into training (80%, n = 9,498) and testing (20%, n = 2,374) sets using a stratified sampling [33]. Class imbalance was handled using class weighting, followed by feature selection, model training on the training set, and SBI prediction on the testing set.

Model development and stacking ensemble architecture

To improve the performance of ML-based predictive models, it is essential to retain only the most relevant features. This study applied widely used Boruta and LASSO based regression methods inside the training fold for candidate predictors selection. Boruta, a random forest-based wrapper method [34], compares real features with shadow features over multiple iterations and labels them as confirmed, rejected, or tentative. Only confirmed features were retained. Whereas, LASSO is an embedded method which minimizes the sum of squared errors with an L1 penalty and taken only the predictors with non-zero coefficients [35]. Finally, the common covariates were determined among the candidate predictors, identified by Boruta and LASSO-based methods. Where, \:n denotes the candidate predictors identification methods (here, n = 2), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the set of identified candidate predictors. To predict the occurrence of SBI, five individual ML algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), categorical boosting (CatBoost), and artificial neural network (ANN), along with a stacking ensemble model were implemented. These algorithms were selected because they represent diverse modeling paradigms: a linear model (LR), a tree-based bagging approach (RF), boosting methods (XGB) and (CatBoost), a non-linear model (ANN), and a meta-learning ensemble approach (stacking). Such diverse models have shown strong predictive performance across various datasets from different countries, particularly those based on demographic and health surveys [36–38]. The individual (RF, XGBoost, CatBoost, and ANN) models were first developed separately, and the stacking model was constructed using a two-level architecture. At the 1st level, RF, XGBoost, CatBoost, and ANN were employed as base learners and generated out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. The base learners were trained on the training data using 10-fold cross-validation (CV) to ensure robust training and minimize overfitting. The OOF predictions from these base learners and the original feature set, were then passed to meta-learner (LR) at the second level (Supplementary: Figure S1). The choice of LR as the meta-learner was supported by both theoretical and empirical evidence. Wolpert [39], who pioneered the stacking framework, recommended linear meta-learners such as LR due to their robustness in combining base model outputs while reducing the risk of overfitting [40]. Subsequent studies, including those by Ting and Witten [41], demonstrated LR superiority over more complex alternatives in maintaining interpretability and generalizability. In healthcare-related contexts, Zhou [42] further validated LR suitability for meta-learning, highlighting its stability when integrating heterogeneous base classifiers. The stacking is an ensemble ML method that combines predictions from multiple base learners using a meta-learner, to exploit their complementary strengths, reduce individual model bias and variance, and improve overall predictive performance and generalizability on unseen data [26]. The rationality of choosing stacking model is shown in Supplementary: Figure S2.

Hyperparameter tuning

To optimize the performance of the ML models, we applied particle swarm optimization (PSO) for hyper parameter tuning. PSO is a population-based metaheuristic that efficiently explores the search space and avoids local minima compared to traditional grid or random search [43]. For each model, PSO was implemented using a 10-fold CV and the objective being optimized by PR-AUC. SHAP explainability

To investigate the interpretability of the ML model, we employed the SHAP explainability framework, introduced by Lundberg and Lee [27]. It is based on cooperative game theory, which quantifies both the global and local contribution of each predictor to the model’s predictions [28]. SHAP analysis was employed to interpret the meta-learner of the stacking ML-based model for predicting SBI. To enhance interpretability, only the original features were included in the SHAP computation, whereas stacking-level prediction features from base learners were excluded, as they are derived from the original features and may introduce redundancy and double counting in feature attribution. This approach ensures a clear and policy-relevant insights into the determinants of the SBI. Categorical variables were transformed using label encoding prior to model fitting. SHAP values were estimated on the probability scale, enabling direct interpretation in terms of changes in the predicted risk of SBI.

Global explanation

In SHAP analysis, global explanation, explain how a ML model performs across all data points, rather than a single, local prediction [46]. In this study, we employed SHAP summary plot and SHAP mean absolute bar plot to offer the global interpretability. SHAP summary plot visualizes the impact and direction of each feature’s effect on model predictions across the all data points. The plot’s Y-axis represents the feature values in descending order of importance, and the X-axis represents the SHAP value, indicating the contribution of that feature’s value toward a higher or lower model output. The mean absolute SHAP bar plot illustrates as a direct measure of feature importance, where the X-axis represent the mean absolute SHAP value for each feature, and the Y-axis lists the features in order of this average magnitude of impact, regardless of the direction of influence.

Local explanation

In SHAP analysis, local explanation, explain how each feature contributes to a model’s prediction for an individual observation [47]. In this study, we employed SHAP waterfall plot and SHAP force plot to offer local interpretability. The waterfall plot is a vertical representation of the same local explanation, where the Y-axis lists features in order of their impact magnitude, and the X-axis shows the incremental change in the prediction value from the expected baseline to the final output. The force plot visualizes a single prediction, where the X-axis represents the model’s output value (probability). Features that increase the prediction are shown in red (right), and features that decrease the prediction are shown in blue (left). The overall pipeline of this study is presented in Fig. 2.

Experimental sittings

SBI dataset was extracted from BDHS database using SPSS (v27). Data preprocessing and bi-variate analysis were also performed by SPSS (v27). Boruta feature selection was carried out in R (Boruta package v9.0.0). LASSO feature selection was conducted in scikit-learn (v1.6.1). ML-based models were implemented using scikit-learn (v1.6.1), XGBoost (v3.0.3), and CatBoost (v1.2.8). Class imbalance was addressed by applying class weighting (class_weight = “balanced”). Stratified 10-fold CV (StratifiedKFold, random_state = 42) was applied throughout the analyses. Hyperparameters were tuned using Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) through the pyswarms package (v1.3.0). Stacking model was developed via Stacking Classifier, with meta-learner (LR). SHAP explainability was performed using SHAP (v0.48.0).

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

Table 3 summarizes the distribution of SBI across the selected explanatory variables. Among the 11,872 women, most of them were aged 35 years or above (63.2%). A larger proportion of women was resided in rural residents (70.1%) and only 6.5% had higher education. The prevalence of SBI was varied across regions, highest in Sylhet (27.6%), and lowest in Khulna (12.0%). Validation of the proposed model

We have incorporated external validation using an earlier BDHS 2017-18. The dataset comprised 12,672 samples and followed the same protocols as the current study. The results of the external validation are presented in Table 7. Among the models, the proposed stacking model achieved better performance with accuracy of 62.6 (95% CI: 57.4–65.6) precision of 42.8% (95% CI: 37.5–44.6), sensitivity (SBI) of 75.3% (95% CI: 62.1–85.3), F1-score of 54.5% (95% CI: 51.1–57.0), MCC of 24.4% (95% CI: 21.1–28.3), Brier score of 20.1% (95% CI: 19.3–20.9), PR-AUC of 0.429 (95% CI: 39.5–46.6), while the LR model showed the highest specificity of 59.7 (95% CI: 32.7–66.4). Model interpretation

To interpret the model, we incorporated global and local SHAP explainability on the stacking model to determine the influential predictors of SBI.

Global explanation

Figure 7 shows the SHAP summary plot, which explains the overall contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction of SBI across all data points. Each dot in the plot represents an individual point. Color (red = high, blue = low) indicates the feature value, and its position on the X-axis shows the influence on the model prediction (positive values = higher chance of SBI, negative values = lower chance of SBI). Total number of children was identified as the most contributing predictor. It showed the widest spread of SHAP values, indicating that they were associated with higher predicted probabilities of SBI within the model. Women with lower education levels were associated with positive SHAP values, while higher education levels were associated with negative SHAP values, indicating a lower predicted probability. Women with higher birth orders were associated with positive SHAP values, suggesting a higher predicted probability of SBI. Younger women were associated with higher SHAP values compared to older women. In contrast, contraceptive use, BMI, television, and internet showed relatively smaller spreads, indicating lower contribution to the model prediction.

Other features, including respondent age (0.02) and division (0.02), show moderate contributions, while features such as living children, do not intend to use contraceptive, BMI, and television exposure have relatively small mean SHAP values (≈ 0.01), indicating lower influence on the prediction.

Discussion

This study employed stacking model with SHAP explainability to predict SBI and identify its influential predictors. Initially, Boruta and LASSO methods were employed to determine the common covariates of SBI. We have found that birth order, contraception use, television, internet, division, education, age, BMI, living children, and total children are the common covariates. Subsequently, individual and stacking model were developved based on the common covariates. The stacking model exhibited the best performeing model for predicting SBI. To interpret the stacking model, SHAP analysis (global and local) was conducted. Global explanations (summary and bar plot) exhibited the overall contribution of predictors, while local explanations (waterfall and force plot) exhibited the individual prediction. The findings revealed that total number of children, educational attainment, birth order, maternal age, and administrative division are the influential predictors of SBI. Across administrative divisions, the prevalence of SBI varied by region, with Sylhet and Chattogram divisions showing higher prevalence, while the Khulna division showing lower prevalence. Women residing in the Eastern division of Bangladesh, particularly in Sylhet, were found to have a higher prevalence of experiencing SBI. Evidence from nationally representative BDHS data indicates that Sylhet consistently exhibits the highest prevalence of SBIs in Bangladesh [21, 48]. This higher prevalence can be largely attributed to persistently low modern contraceptive use and a high unmet need for family planning, which are more pronounced in Sylhet than in other divisions [49]. Notably, Sylhet has the lowest contraceptive prevalence rate nationally, reflecting limited access to family planning services and weaker program outreach, particularly in rural and hard-to-reach areas. These structural barriers substantially increase the likelihood of unintended and closely spaced pregnancies. In addition, regional demographic characteristics such as earlier age at marriage and first birth contribute to shorter birth intervals in Sylhet [49, 50].

Early initiation of childbearing extends the reproductive period and is strongly associated with subsequent short spacing, especially when combined with lower female educational attainment and reduced reproductive autonomy. Moreover, sociocultural patterns, including more conservative attitudes toward fertility regulation and comparatively lower female decision-making power, may further discourage optimal birth spacing in this region. High levels of male out-migration may also disrupt continuity of reproductive health counseling and postpartum family planning use. Collectively, these region-specific demographic and sociocultural factors provide a plausible explanation for the large division-level effects observed and underscore the need for geographically targeted interventions.

The findings of the SHAP interpretability of this study is also related to fuzzy and multi-criteria decision frameworks from Nwokoro’s prior research [51–55]. They found that women’s age, educational attainment, and region are significantly associated with maternal health care outcomes. Among the influential predictors, women with no education, higher parity, and do not intend to use contraceptive had higher likelihood of experiencing SBI.

Several studies have reported similar findings, showing that women with low or no formal education are more likely to experience SBI compared to those with secondary or higher education [56–58]. Education is a critical factor influencing women’s reproductive behavior, autonomy, and decision-making regarding family planning. Women with limited education are often less aware of the health and may have restricted access to reproductive health information and services. Whereas, educated women are more likely to use modern contraceptive methods effectively, exercise greater autonomy in fertility-related decisions, and delay subsequent pregnancies. Therefore, improving women’s education, particularly reproductive health education, remains essential for promoting optimal birth spacing and improving maternal and child health outcomes. Women with higher parity has consistently been identified as a significant factor associated with SBI in numerous demographic and health studies [59, 60]. Women who have given birth to several children tend to experience shorter intervals between successive births due to a combination of biological, behavioral, and sociocultural factors. Biologically, and older multiparous women may attempt to complete childbearing before their fertility declines, intentionally shortening the interval between pregnancies.

Higher parity women often demonstrate lower uptake or inconsistent use of postpartum family planning services, either due to misconceptions about reduced fertility after multiple births or limited access to healthcare facilities. The reduced utilization of modern contraceptive methods significantly increases the likelihood of SBIs. Hassen et al. [61] found that higher parity was a consistent predictor of SBI across the Asia-Pacific region, emphasizing that multiparous women were more likely to conceive within less than 24 months after the preceding birth. Similarly, evidence from Bangladesh indicates that SBIs are disproportionately common among high-parity women and are linked to adverse maternal and child health outcomes. Socioeconomic constraints and limited educational attainment further exacerbate this trend, as women from poorer households or rural areas may have less autonomy and fewer opportunities to make informed reproductive choices.

Do not intend to use contraceptive was observed another influential predictor of SBI. Previous studies have shown that women who do not use contraceptives are significantly more likely to experience SBI compared with those who adopt modern family planning methods soon after childbirth [62, 63]. The non-use or inconsistent use of contraception after delivery increases the risk of unintended pregnancies, which in turn leads to shorter interpregnancy intervals. Several factors contribute to low contraceptive use, including limited access to family planning services, fear of side effects, partner opposition, misconceptions about contraceptive methods, and inadequate postpartum counseling. Therefore, expanding postpartum family planning services and providing consistent contraceptive counseling immediately after delivery may play a key role in reducing the risk of SBI.

Improved prediction of SBI using the stacking model can significantly contribute to reducing SBI through several pathways. The model enables early identification of women at higher risk of SBI, allowing health programs to target interventions more effectively. Timely identification of at-risk women facilitates monitoring and follow-up, which can prevent unintended pregnancies and ensure adequate birth spacing. High-risk women can receive tailored postpartum family planning counseling, education on contraceptive use, and support for partner involvement in reproductive decisions. Collectively, these measures can lead to longer birth intervals, reduced maternal and child health risks, and overall improvement in reproductive health outcomes. Therefore, the improved predictive performance of the stacking model serves as a practical tool for guiding interventions and ultimately reducing the incidence of SBI in the population.

From the policy perspective, these findings highlight the need for comprehensive reproductive health interventions to reduce the risk of SBI. Integrating postpartum family planning counseling into maternal and child health programs is essential. In addition, increasing male involvement in family planning decisions and addressing sociocultural norms that support closely spaced births can further help reduce SBI. Policymakers should prioritize expanding access to affordable and high-quality family planning services, with particular attention to education, contraceptive use, and high-parity women. Furthermore, investing in women’s empowerment programs can strengthen reproductive autonomy and support informed decision-making regarding birth spacing.

These findings can guide targeted family planning interventions by identifying population at higher risk of SBI, particularly women with no education, higher parity, and not intend to use contraceptives, family planning interventions can be more effectively targeted and resource allocation can be optimized. Prioritizing postpartum family planning counseling for these high-risk groups within routine maternal and child health services, such as antenatal care, delivery, and postnatal follow-up visits, may help prevent unintended and closely spaced pregnancies. In addition, engaging male partners in family planning initiatives is critical, as spousal communication and male approval strongly influence contraceptive decision-making in many sociocultural contexts. Community-based interventions that address prevailing norms favoring SBI and large family sizes may further enhance contraceptive acceptance and sustained use. Expanding access to affordable, high-quality family planning services, particularly in underserved and low-resource settings remains essential. This includes ensuring the availability of a wide range of modern contraceptive methods, strengthening counseling quality, and reducing financial and geographic barriers to service utilization. Moreover, strengthening women’s empowerment initiatives through education, economic opportunities, and decision-making autonomy can substantially improve reproductive autonomy. Collectively, these strategies can contribute to improved birth spacing practices, better maternal and child health outcomes, and progress toward national and global reproductive health goals.

Policy implications

Increasing awareness of contraceptive use and reproductive health particularly among women with lower education and higher parity can play a crucial role in reducing the prevalence of SBI and improving maternal and child health outcomes. Policymakers should prioritize targeted health education campaigns, community-based interventions, and improved access to family planning services, especially in underserved and vulnerable populations. This study demonstrates that the integration of the stacking model with explainable AI into national health systems can significantly improve the the early identification of women at high risk of SBI. This approach can support healthcare providers and program planners in delivering targeted counseling, personalized interventions for improving birth spacing and maternal-child health outcomes in Bangladesh. Furthermore, adopting data-driven decision-making tools within national health systems can strengthen monitoring and evaluation processes, enabling more precise and evidence-based policy formulation. This study also aligns with global development frameworks, particularly, SDG target 3.1 (reducing maternal mortality) and SDG target 3.7 (ensuring universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare services). By addressing SBI through targeted, model-informed interventions, policymakers can contribute meaningfully to achieving these global health targets.

Limitations of the study

The study has several limitations such as: Cross-sectional data limits temporality. Self-reported reproductive histories subject to recall bias. Unmeasured potential confounding (postpartum amenorrhea, breastfeeding duration, supply-side constraints, pregnancy intention, partner involvement). Model training entirely dependent on DHS variables. This study is based on nationally representative data from Bangladesh; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Variations in socio-cultural norms, healthcare infrastructure, living conditions, fertility preferences, and access to family planning services between Bangladesh and other LMICs may result in different outcomes. Future studies should be conducted in diverse LMIC settings to assess the consistency of these findings and enhance their broader applicability.

Conclusion

This study applied several individual models as well as a stacking model to predict women at risk of SBI. Based on the performance metrics, the stacking model showed modest predictive performance, slightly outperforming the individual models. In addition, we used SHAP explainability to interpret the model and identify the most influential predictors of SBI. The results indicated that no education, higher parity, and no intention to use contraceptives were the key factors associated with a higher risk of SBI. These findings suggest that increasing awareness of contraceptive use and reproductive health, especially among women with lower education and higher parity, could help reduce the prevalence of SBI and improve maternal and child health outcomes. Furthermore, integrating the stacking model with SHAP explainability into maternal and child health programs offers an opportunity for early identification of high-risk women and the delivery of targeted counseling and interventions. Such data-driven approaches can improve health outcomes and support broader global development goals.
