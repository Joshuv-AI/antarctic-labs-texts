Normal speed versus time-compressed speech in long-term memory retention: a randomized, within subjects experimental design
Authors: Chadi Massoud, Myriam El Khoury-Malhame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 has accelerated the educational shift towards different modalities and media types including online resources and video recordings. Students have sometimes used this opportunity to watch lecture recordings and posted material at faster playback speed. This function was also integrated in the daily use of social media like WhatsApp. The few existing studies show conflicting results when assessing its potential learning improvements and the effect of audio processing speed on cognitive load. As such, this study investigates the impact of time-compressed speech on long-term memory retention, one of the proxies for stable learning. The study aims to provide insight on the limited evidence pertaining to long-term memory recall in time compressed speech in episodic memory in early higher education learners. Based on previous studies, students are expected to recall less information when listening to time-compressed speech which would be reflected by lower cued recall scores.MethodsA total of forty university students were recruited. They were assessed using the Memory Assessment Scale and were counterbalanced on exposure to 23 s in times-compressed and a 23 s normal speed speech and a within subject comparison was made. In addition to demographics, the study also tracked the frequency of the subjects’ use of time-compressed speech in a learning environment as well as their attitude towards the benefits/downsides of using time-compressed speech.ResultsOur results show students had a higher-than-average tendency to use time-compressed speech and mostly noted its negative impact. Participants remembered around 50% of information accurately with a statistically significant difference between modalities: using normal speech led to an improvement of 27% for cued recall questions as compared to time-compressed speed.ConclusionsThis data adds to the literature pointing to a gain in time offset by a loss in learning efficiency when students use time compressed material. Knowing these results can vary among individuals and learning materials, further studies could shed more lights on additional factors moderating the impact of time-compressed speech on cognitive performances or cognitive load.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04398-5.

Background

With the rise of online education and many platforms offering recorded lectures for students to learn from and the introduction of inverse classroom and asynchronous learning, studies from different institutions have shown that students have started increasing playback speeds to watch lectures faster [1, 2]. This form of educational delivery can affect the way students learn [3]. Therefore, it is crucial to understand long-term memory retention in the context of time-compressed speech and the possible effects on cognitive load. Time-compressed speech is an audio recording that has been compressed to playback at a faster rate than the original audio. It is used in many instances to save time when studying, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to the spread of hybrid learning, online education, and the “flipped classroom” methods [4]. Since the learning material is presented in the form of audio-visual recordings, many students opt to save time studying by watching the recordings at a faster rate. This method is being used and implemented with unknown impacts on the learning experience. Time-compressed speech has been studied in terms of language comprehension and for understanding its benefits for individuals with learning deficits. Time-compressed speech has yet to be extensively studied in the context of long-term memory retention for educational media, and the determination of the effects of specific playback speed [5]. The study hypothesis is that time-compressed speech through watching recordings at two times playback speed will reduce the amount of information retained.

Studies have investigated how the brain adapts proprioception to accommodate time-compressed speech. A particular study by Dias et al. suggested that although time-compressed speech may initially present challenges for comprehension, listeners can adapt over time, potentially modifying any opposing effects on language comprehension [6].

According to Rönnberg et al. [7], who proposed the Ease of Language Understanding (ELU) model, listeners modify their processing tactics in response to contextual signals. This interaction between cognitive mechanisms and auditory processing helps in speech understanding. Understanding time-compressed speech is challenging since listeners have to use more cognitive resources to meet the more complex demands of faster speech, particularly when the brain has not yet adapted and thus needs to fill in more gaps.

This idea was also supported by further studies experimenting on students, which also indicated significant differences in performance based on the playback rate of the time-compressed speech [8].

Previous research by Song et al. [9] argues that subjective performance when listening to time-compressed speech is improved as indicated by participants’ perception on surveys, yet the objective performance was overall worse.

One study claims that memory retention is unaffected by time-compressed speech as long as the playback speed does not exceed two times, though these studies allowed students to watch the recordings multiple times and directly prior to being tested [10].

Some studies also claim that watching at two times playback speed twice improves performance relative to listening to it at normal playback speed and then at two times playback speed [11].

Methods

Aim

The primary objective is to assess whether time-compression of speech affects the listener’s ability to retain and recall information. The study aims to provide insight on the limited evidence pertaining to long-term memory recall in time compressed speech in episodic memory in early higher education learners The study will add more insight into teaching methods and educational media and increase our understanding of human cognition and adjust methods of communication in educational settings when incorporated with other variables.

Sample

Ethical approval was granted for the study by the Lebanese American University Institutional Review Board (LAU.SAS.MM12.26/Apr/2024). Informed consent was provided to the applicants at the beginning of the survey and before the experiment in written form. The recruitment of participants was done through posts on social media and convenience sampling. University graduates and undergraduates aged 18–25 were targeted.

Procedure

A total of 40 Lebanese university students were included. Students were comfortable seated in front of a computer screen in a sound-proof room within the premises of the Lebanese American University. The inclusion criteria was being enrolled in an English-instructing Lebanese higher education institution. The exclusion criteria included hearing, learning, or neuropsychiatric disorders. None of the students had any hearing, learning, or neuropsychiatric disorder or any self-reported obstacle in learning or hearing. The students were assumed to be proficient in the English language as their primary educational medium is an English-instructing institution.

Participants were asked to listen to two stories narrated by one of the experimenters and uploaded to YouTube to use for playback. The use of YouTube for both stories ensured a standardized compression quality, audio quality, and playback rate in both stories.

Both stories were twenty-three seconds long in duration and were separated by a 5-minute survey to gather demographic information as well as information about their time-compressed speech use habits. The stories themselves consist of four sentences each. A random number generator was used to pick out of the two available stories to start with and to determine whether the first story would be listened to at normal playback speed or time compressed speed.

The student listens to the first story, either at a normal rate, or as time-compressed speech, as determined by the randomizer. They were then asked to play a basic Minesweeper game for 10 min to avoid rehearsal of the information and to transition the information out of the working memory. Participants were then probed the story using Cued Recall, which were delayed by the Minesweeper gaming time. The questions the participants were asked required them to reproduce details and events. The story and the questions were ten minutes apart as per the Memory Assessment Scale. They then filled out the demographics data before listening to the second story, in the other format, and the same process was repeated for long-term memory probing. The experiment was counterbalanced for order of speech speed presentation and participants were randomized to either setting.

Before the story at double playback speed, participants were habituated to the speed by listening to random words prior at 2 times playback speed to ensure proper adaptation and understanding of speech [12].

The demographic form included questions about age, gender, and the university and department they study in. Students were also asked to assess the frequency of the subjects’ use of time-compressed speech in a learning environment on a scale from 1 to 5 (1 being very rarely to 5 being very frequently) as well as their attitude towards the potential benefits/downsides of using time-compressed speech (with 1 being a positive attitude to 5 being a negative attitude).

Memory assessment scale instrument

The assessment of long-term memory was done using an exercise from the Memory Assessment Scale [13]. The scale includes a short story, adapted to the Lebanese population by simply replacing location names with typical Lebanese locations. Though the Memory Assessment Scale has yet to be formally validated in a Lebanese population, it was used using the standardized procedures from the manual, and two raters took part of the scoring to ensure consensus. Long-term memory was then assessed through cued recall questions related to the short story. The nine cued recall questions targeted key details within the stories concerning the sequence, as well as minor details with no significance to the story. The participants were asked two questions about specific events that occurred in the story and seven questions about descriptive details pertaining to objects or people in the story.

Based on the Memory Assessment Scale Manual, the formal scoring is done based on the cued recall open-ended questions. The score indicates whether the questions were answered correctly, and the details were correctly remembered. The answer is considered correct if the participant is able to recall the detail or event exactly as it is stated in the story, this can include colour of item, type of object, number of items, location, time of event, or sequence of events.

To ensure scoring consistency, responses were scored using the aforementioned predefined rubric. Two raters reviewed each response independently and any discrepancies were resolved to reach a consensus score. Inter-rater reliability was not calculated as the separate scores prior to the consensus were not retained. Similarly, item-level responses were not retained, and thus internal consistency could not be calculated.

Statistical analysis

Mean and standard deviations were calculated using SPSS (version 29). Data between time compressed and regular speed was analysed using paired t-test at a p-values < 0.05. A priori power analysis for a paired t-test (α = 0.05, 80% power), assuming a medium within-subject effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) indicated a sample size of 34 participants, making our sample of 40 participants adequate.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of time compressed speech versus normal speech on long term memory recall in a sample of Lebanese university student. The study provides important lessons for academic settings related to the fact that that time-compressed speech was found to reduce long-term memory retention. To the best of our knowledge this is the first study investigating such variables in the Arab culture post-COVID-19 pandemic, and as such it provides unique insight into the topic in this cultural region. As such, moving forward it would be interesting to account for those cognitive models, especially that the perception of time-compressed speech has been documented to be more significant if the language is the participant’s second language instead of their native ones [14].

Within the ELU model, time-compressed speech could lead to an increase in processing mismatch. This results in a greater need for explicit working memory use, which would help with short-term comprehension, yet at a cost towards encoding into long-term memory. This is due to comprehension becoming more effortful due to less clarity in temporal cues from the rapid speech [7]. On the other hand, Baddeley’s model would suggest that the accelerated input would overwhelm the phonological loop and central executive. As a result, rehearsal and organization both will be limited and thus decreasing the encoding of information into long-term memory [15].

In our study, participants made more errors in recall using the time-compressed story telling versus normal speech. This poorer performance of long-term memory retention of episodic recall indicates that listening to the speech faster, albeit saves time, comes at a cognitive cost as it made it more difficult for students to properly remember the information they are exposed to. According to the cognitive load theory, human information processing is known to be limited to 5–9 “chunks” at any given time, restraining the working memory to a finite number of words or information [16]. This might help explain the poor performance in the higher playback rates due to higher cognitive load within a short period of time, making it increasingly difficult to process the same information presented rapidly [8]. This would be true even after prior habituation and understanding as our experimental design did indeed control for understanding of the higher speeds by habituation prior to listening at twice the speed.

Additionally, distractibility could play a role in tentatively explaining the poorer performance on rapid tracks, as missing a second in a 1x playback rate could be corrected for as per the sentence completion capacity of our minds [17]; but in time-compressed speech 1 s would cause a greater loss of information, making it more difficult to compensate for especially when couple with the increased cognitive load of continuous processing at faster rates [18].

Individual differences are also documented to play a role in speech understanding, as previous studies indicate that differences in speech perception plays a role in understanding and learning in adverse conditions such as time-compressed speech [19, 20]. Authors show that prior familiarity with accelerated speech could improve recall performance, though our study did not show this link in the self-reported familiarity when assessing and accounting for the regular use of time-compressed speech in the past. This difference adds to previous studies investigating difficulty in language processing at higher speeds and an increased cognitive load [8]. The findings imply that pushing to speed-efficient learning on digital platforms may compromise retention, notably for episodic recall. If time-compressed speech is a time-saving need in academia, it could be interesting to sharpen students’ capacities by training individuals to master this cognitive skill as prior multi-day exercise has been linked to improvement in memory and perception of time-compressed speech relative to untrained individuals [21].

Normal speed is likely to have improved delayed recall due to more time to segment the story into meaningful chunks, which can help overcome the limitations of working memory and provide more time for encoding the information into long-term memory [22]. This suggests that the time saved by watching at a higher playback rate will have to trade off some encoding strength, which would lead to weaker retention. This is backed by previous studies on the subject [23]. In addition, the speed of speech might limit the ability to process the information in time, where it will be too late and the recording has moved on to other information, this concept of limitation in time for speech processing is also supported throughout the literature [24, 25]. Some previous studies claim that there are no significant differences yet some of these studies assess immediate recall, or assess perception rather than performance, and some studies have shown minimal effects up to a playback speed of 2x [26–29]. The study uses delayed recall and single exposure, both of which help discern long-term memory from short-term memory while simultaneously limiting the input to the singular playback time. In particular, cued recall was implemented to be able to assess the retention of details from the story specifically as an indication of how thorough the encoding was. It adds to previous studies by implementing a validated tool, the Memory Assessment Scale, for a more objective measurement of long-term memory rather than a perceived measurement or self-reported measure, thus reducing subjectivity bias.

Limitations

Although we found no influence of the order of presentation and presentation modalities were randomly counterbalances, other covariation factors may have influenced the results. In fact, some participants reported at debriefing that they tried to retain the information in their working memory and reinforced it through repetition while playing Minesweeper. Memory performance could also be influenced by mood and stress levels of participants which could be assessed in future studies to control for decreased ability to focus, burnout, and lower retention induced by mental distress [30]. Additional factors moderating cognitive abilities could also relate to time of day, amount of energy, and location not being one’s regular learning environment. Furthermore, convenience sampling and the fact that the students belong to Lebanese University students poses an obstacle to the generalizability of the study. Additionally, since exact age was not recorded but noted within an interval, more specific age-related comparisons cannot be done, thus not accounting for possible age differences in early and late higher education. This could decrease the impact of the implications on higher education. Lastly, the story format capitalizes on previously validated and standardized tasks to assess long term memory in a laboratory setting, it would be beneficial to additionally probe for learning-like material that better mimic academic learning content. Including a larger sample size and varying levels of education might provide more rigorous generalizability of results. A wider scope of time-compressed speech rates could be investigated, in an attempt to optimize learning speed.

Implications for practice

The results of our study are indicative that time-compressed speech should be carefully approached by users in educational learning platforms. Media players previewing lectures could restrict the playback rate to the normal speed or, upon further studies, to less time-compressed rates. Based on the study and previous studies, guidelines could be given to students to be able to identify when to use increased playback speed. For instance, one could use the increased speed for revision rather than first time exposures or if one is experienced in the topic and merely needs a refresher. Another option could be to encourage students who use higher playback rates to employ supplementary learning techniques to improve retention, such as pausing and summarizing, note-taking, or retrieval practice. The study helps add to the overall understanding of cognitive abilities, notably long-term memory, adding to the literature about time-compressed speech and episodic memory.

Conclusion

Our results focus on long term memory recall as it mostly relates to students needs when studying and being tested on studied material in academia. It would be interesting to further investigates the impact of time compression on working memory. These findings could also be applied to fields such as psycholinguistics and its complex interactions with cognitive psychology and neuropsychology as the field remains to date in its infancy. Future studies could attempt to include other languages in studies mirroring real-life applications. This can include the visual stimuli that accompany recorded educational sessions, different devices used for listening, different topics that are being listened to, and whether the type of information is made for memorization or understanding, and a distinction between episodic and semantic memory in this aspect.
