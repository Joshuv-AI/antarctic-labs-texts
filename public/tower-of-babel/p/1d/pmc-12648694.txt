Alternative approaches to glucose measurements during oral glucose tolerance testing in youth with cystic fibrosis: a mixed methods pilot study
Authors: Seema Meighan, Robert J Gallop, Melissa S Putman, Andrea Kelly, Brynn E Marks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis-related diabetes (CFRD) is one of the most common non-pulmonary manifestations of cystic fibrosis (CF), affecting 25 % of people over the age of 10 living with CF [1]. Early initiation of insulin therapy in people with CFRD is associated with improvements in body mass index, pulmonary function, and mortality [[2], [3], [4]]. However, the insidious nature of CFRD can result in delayed diagnosis. As a result, the CF Foundation recommends annual CFRD screening with a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) in all individuals with CF ≥ 10 years who do not have CFRD [5]. In a study of more than 3500 individuals with CF followed for over 6 years through the CF Foundation Patient Registry, pulmonary function tests and BMIs for individuals diagnosed with CFRD were higher at centers with higher rates of OGTT screening as compared to centers with lower rates of screening, likely due to higher rates of CFRD diagnosis and treatment [6]. However, in 2023, only 64 % of youth and just 35 % of adults captured in the CF Foundation Patient Registry completed OGTT screening [1]. The most commonly cited reasons for not completing OGTT screening were the fasting burden and additional time commitment cast upon people who already contend with multiple medical interventions [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

The ability to perform OGTT using alternative approaches to glucose measurement that do not require phlebotomy may increase rates of screening for CFRD. The GTT@home is an OGTT kit that uses a built-in timer and audio-visual cues to guide users to collect capillary blood samples (Supplementary Fig. 1). This kit uses a calibration algorithm, resulting in glucose values that agreed well with the plasma glucose measurements in adults at risk for gestational and type 2 diabetes [11,12]. Continuous glucose monitors (CGM) are wearable technologies that measure interstitial glucose every 5 min. Studies exploring the agreement between plasma glucose and CGM glucose in subjects without diabetes under free living conditions and during OGTT testing have demonstrated acceptable accuracy [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. However, other studies found that CGM performed poorly in distinguishing categories of glucose tolerance in youth with CF [17].

The aims of this study were 1) to assess for agreement between plasma glucose and two alternative approaches to glucose measurement obtained concomitantly during an OGTT, and 2) to understand the experiences and preferences of youth with CF and their caregivers.

Methods

We conducted a prospective, single-center mixed methods study to assess the accuracy, feasibility, and acceptability of two novel approaches to glucose measurement during an OGTT that could facilitate conducting this screening test in the home setting.

Study population

Individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of CF, ≥10 years of age, who were fluent in English were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria included hospitalization or treatment with IV antibiotics or supraphysiologic glucocorticoids within 4 weeks, major medical or psychiatric disorders other than CF, use of medications known to impact the accuracy of the Dexcom G7 CGM (hydroxyurea, >2g acetaminophen per day), history of severe adhesive reactions that may lead to an inability to tolerate CGM wear, and current pregnancy. The study was approved by the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Institutional Review Board. Written or electronic parental informed consent and verbal youth assent was obtained for all participants.

Study schedule

A virtual study visit was conducted before the OGTT. To account for CGM inaccuracy during the first and last 24 h of wear [Wadwa, 2018 #543;Garg, 2022 #295], a research team member provided virtual support to assist participants in placing the CGM 24–216 h before the OGTT. Efforts were made to schedule the OGTT around 96 h of CGM wear whenever possible. Participants received the manufacturer’s written instructions and a video explaining how to use the GTT@home. No one from the study team discussed the use of the GTT@home with participants.

The OGTT was completed in the outpatient clinical research center after a minimum of 8 h of fasting. Glucola was administered at a dose of 1.75 g/kg (maximum 75 g). Plasma glucose levels were collected at 0, 60, and 120 min and was analyzed using a colorimetric technique (Vitros 4600 Analyzer, QuidelOrtho). After plasma glucose was collected, participants collected their own capillary glucose samples using the GTT@home kit at 0 and 120 min (Supplementary Fig. 1). The GTT@home was designed to collect glucose values at these two time points, so a study team member collected the 60 min glucose using a second kit. Study team members intervened if the participant had not successfully completed the necessary steps within 3 min of the plasma glucose collection. A trained study team member used an 11-item checklist to assess whether participants successfully and independently completed all necessary steps of the GTT@home for the 0 and 120 min timepoints [11,12]. CGM data points were collected fasting, and every five minutes for twenty minutes at the 1-hour and 2-hour time points to account for lag time.

The CGM apps necessary for share/ follow were set up on the parent’s phone, and the CGM sensor was worn for up to 10 days. Normal glucose ranges were reviewed, and participants were cautioned about the potential for falsely low CGM readings. Participants and their parents were scheduled for semi-structured interviews within 2 weeks of removing the CGM, aiming for day 12 after CGM placement whenever possible.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA (Version 18.0, College Station, TX) and SAS (Version 9.4, Cary, NC). Absolute glucose and the relative differences between plasma and alternative glucose measurements are reported for each device at each time point. Agreement for glucose measurements was calculated by subtracting the plasma glucose value from the alternative measurement. Categorical agreement was determined by comparing the diagnoses made using data from each device against the categorical diagnoses made from plasma glucose levels. For CGM data, Youden’s index was used to determine the optimal timepoint where the sum of the area under the curve (AUC) for discrimination between each of the categorical diagnoses of glucose tolerance yielded maximum discrimination [18]. Once the optimal timepoint was identified, the receiver operating characteristic curve was used to identify the optimal CGM glucose threshold that maximized sensitivity and specificity in discriminating between these categorical diagnoses of glucose tolerance (optimal operating point) [19,20].

Qualitative analysis

Semi-structured dyadic interviews lasting ∼ 30 min were conducted via teleconferencing software. A 6-item, open-ended interview guide was used (Supplement 2); follow-up prompts and spontaneous probing questions were asked to solicit additional details. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were de-identified before being uploaded into Dedoose qualitative software (Version 10.0, Los Angeles, CA). Two investigators (S.M., B.E.M.) inductively developed codes and compiled codes into a codebook that was used to code all interviews. Inductive coding with two independent coders was used for thematic analysis. Codes containing patterns of shared meaning united by a central concept were grouped to create themes [21].

Results

The clinical characteristics of all study participants (n = 14) are shown in Table 1. Based on plasma glucose values from the OGTT, 8 (57 %) subjects were diagnosed with NGT, 5 (36 %) with IGT, and 1 (7 %) with CFRD. Diagnostic criteria for the categories of glucose tolerance are shown in Table 2 for reference. Immediate access to CGM data facilitated insulin initiation with the clinical team for the participant diagnosed with CFRD. No participants were diagnosed with INDET. Median plasma glucose levels were: fasting 93 mg/dL (IQR 86, 97), 1-hour 172 mg/dL (IQR 150, 198), and 2-hour 118 mg/dL (IQR 98, 156).

GTT@home

Four of the 14 (43 %) GTT@home kits had missing glucose values at the 60-minute (n = 3) and 120-minute (n = 1) timepoints due to device failures. The four kits with missing glucose values that prevented assessment of glucose tolerance were categorized as an incorrect diagnosis. For the GTT@home glucose measurements, the median fasting glucose level was −10 mg/dL (IQR −16, −4) lower than the plasma glucose (Table 3). The 1-hour and 2-hour glucose values were −14 mg/dL (IQR −17, 37) and −14 mg/dL (IQR −38, 18) lower than the plasma glucose. Just 6 of the 14 (43 %) categorical assessments of glucose tolerance agreed with the categories defined by the plasma glucose.

Twelve of the 14 (86 %) parent–child dyads successfully completed all necessary steps for using the GTT@home kit without the need for support from a study team member. The two participants who required assistance 1) could not identify how to open cover over the test strip in the kit and 2) forgot to set the timer for subsequent glucose measurements.

CGM analyses

CGM glucose values at 0, 60, and 120 min were all higher than the plasma glucose by 16 mg/dL (IQR 5, 23), 52 mg/dL (IQR 39, 82), and 46 mg/dL (IQR 15, 53) points, respectively. Despite relatively poor absolute agreement, CGM glucose values at times 0, 60, and 120 min correctly identified categorical glucose tolerance in 13 of 14 (93 %) participants. One subject with IGT by plasma glucose was mis-diagnosed as INDET.

Using Youden’s index, the CGM glucose value at 125 min was identified as the optimal timepoint that yielded maximum discrimination for all categories of glucose tolerance. The AUC values were 0.979, 0.867, and 1.00 for NGT, IGT, and CFRD respectively. Discrimination at the 60, 130, and 135 min times performed nearly comparably (AUC for NGT, IGT, and CFRD all ≥ 0.96, 0.84, and 1.0, respectively), therefore illustrating the durability of the CGM glucose values within a 15 min window (Fig. 1). For NGT, a glucose threshold of 154 mg/dL had a 100 % sensitivity (6/6) and 87.5 % specificity (7/8). A glucose threshold of 182 mg/dL had a 80 % sensitivity (4/5) and 88.9 % specificity (88.9 %) for IGT. Although limited to data from a single subject who was diagnosed for CFRD during the study, a glucose threshold of 282 mg/dL showed a 100 % (1/1) sensitivity and 100 % specificity (13/13) for CFRD.

Qualitative data

Median interview length was 32 min (IQR 23.5, 33.5) with a minimum length of 22 min and maximum length of 44 min. The following themes emerged:

Despite these benefits, parents expressed concerns about the accuracy of these alternative approaches to measuring glucose during an OGTT given the implications of a diagnosis of CFRD. 3)Assuming equal device accuracy, opinions were mixed as to which approach to in-home OGTT testing was preferred. In total, 9 (64 %) of dyads preferred CGM, 4 (29 %) preferred the GTT@home, and 1 (7 %) preferred the conventional OGTT. The simplicity of CGM and lack of a visible needle was preferred by some, including the mother of a 13 year old: “Yeah, I think {CGM} takes a lot of the guesswork out because you just- there's not a timing to remember. You just report this is the time that you drank the juice thing, then all the data is there and then everybody from clinic who's actually reading it can figure it out.” Experiencing false low glucoses during the 10 days of sensor wear led some dyads to question the accuracy of CGM and led them to prefer the GTT@home. Some dyads also preferred not to have a device attached and liked the clear, step by step instructions with the GTT@home, including the mother of a 15 year old: “With all of the steps it is sort of like dummy proof, you know somebody having to set the timer on their own- it was a lot easier not to mess up.” Parents discussed the anxiety surrounding the OGTT testing, with some expressing comfort in being able to see the CGM glucose values immediately rather than having to wait to hear results from their CF teams. Some expressed that not seeing glucose values immediately with the GTT@home led them to question whether the test was completed properly.

The families that opted to participate in this study were highly adherent to annual OGTT testing and understood the importance of diagnosing CFRD. Six of the youth had prior experience with CGM/ SMBG whereas 8 were naïve to home glucose monitoring. Whereas many of those who had previously monitored glucose levels, particularly those with a history IGT or INDET, expressed using the data to make dietary changes, those with NGT who were seeing glucose data for the first time appeared less distressed by hyperglycemia. The mother of a 13 year old with NGT stated: “We saw predictable spikes, like you could tell where he’d eaten. It was interesting for me to see it during the school day when I don’t know when he’s eating or how much he’s eating or to see this is what’s happening when you’re pretty active. And so, yeah, I think nothing really surprised us. Except for the thing that’s not surprising, that you ate a bunch of sugar and your blood sugar went up, so… oops.” Although attitudes about glucose and CFRD did not appear to change, being able to experience CGM made the possibility of being diagnosed with CFRD less frightening. The mother of a 13 year old boy with IGT noted: “Like if {CFRD} ends up being in your future, the Dexcom is a really great thing to be able to have. It's really amazing, you know, the devices they have now that makes managing diabetes like much easier than a long time ago.”.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional observational study, alternative methods of assessing glucose levels during the OGTT were found to be discrepant from plasma values, with correct classification of glycemic category in 43 % (n = 6) with GTT@home and 93 % (n = 13) with CGM. The accuracy of CGM measures used during OGTT was improved by using alternative glucose thresholds of 154 mg/dL for NGT and 182 mg/dL for IGT at 125 min. Despite embracing the convenience of these in-home testing options, parent–child dyads expressed concerns about performing medical procedures in the home alongside concerns about the accuracy of these alternative glucose measurements given the implications of a CFRD diagnosis. These results suggest that further research needs to be done to identify accurate and patient-acceptable approaches to OGTT screening.

OGTT has been shown to correlate with declines in BMI and pulmonary function and to date remains the only screening test recommended screen for CFRD [5,22]. The ability to perform an OGTT without the need for venipuncture could facilitate in home testing and may improve rates of screening for CFRD. While HbA1c is highly correlated with average CGM glucose levels, early CFRD is defined by isolated post-prandial glycemic excursions that are short-lived and not well-captured by HbA1c [16,23]. CGM’s ability to detect these transient post-prandial excursions has led to interest in its use as a screening tool for CFRD. CGM has been shown to detect hyperglycemia in children and adults with NGT as diagnosed by OGTT [[22], [23], [24]]. Several studies have assessed the relationship between CGM glucose values and pulmonary function and BMI in individuals with CF under free-living conditions. In youth, hyperglycemia and glycemic variability documented during CGM wear under free-living conditions have been shown to negatively correlate with pulmonary function, BMI, and β-cell function [17]. However, this pediatric CGM data failed to discriminate between the categorical diagnosis of glucose tolerance made during an OGTT. Among adults with CF who were found to have a greater degree of hyperglycemia and glycemic variability during free-living CGM wear, >3.4 % CGM time above range > 180 mg/dL had a 91 % sensitivity and 95 % specificity in detecting individuals with CFRD as diagnosed by OGTT [16].

While there is great interest in the use of CGM under free-living conditions, the lack of long-term, prospective data correlating CGM metrics with long-term outcomes of interest in CF, including BMI, pulmonary function, and mortality, has limited its applicability. Recognizing that interstitial glucose and plasma glucose are fundamentally different, the identification of CGM-specific thresholds to diagnose glucose tolerance during an OGTT may help to overcome the current limitations of free-living CGM data. The thresholds for NGT and IGT identified in this small sample provide the groundwork for future, larger studies to validate our findings.

Despite promising earlier data suggesting the utility of GTT@home kits [11,12] for in-home OGTT screening for gestational and type 2 diabetes, high rates of device failure and poor accuracy led to poor agreement in our study. The development of a dedicated kit for CFRD, which includes fasting, 60-minute, and 120-minute blood collection spots, may improve the performance of the device in this population.

Screening tests are only useful when they are completed, highlighting the importance of including the patient and caregiver voice in assessing new approaches to CFRD screening. While dyads valued the convenience of in-home screening, accuracy was an equally important consideration for our participants. Parents also expressed concerns about the challenges of performing a medical procedure in the home setting, especially with younger children. Gaining experience in the use of any home-based testing kit with direct oversight from the medical team would be helpful for parents to support future in-home testing. The use of CGM as a screening for CFRD has additional potential benefits for those diagnosed with CFRD by provide useful information for clinicians regarding glycemia in free-living conditions. In the one participant who was diagnosed with CFRD during the OGTT, the immediate availability of CGM data provide the information needed for his treating clinician to quickly determine an appropriate insulin regimen.

Despite promising preliminary mixed-methods data from this study, we recognize its limitations. The small sample size of 14 individuals, which included only a single individual with CFRD and no one with INDET, limits our ability to assess diagnostic accuracy and determine appropriate CGM-thresholds to define categories of glucose tolerance. In addition, there was limited racial and ethnic diversity in this sample, and adults over 18 years were not included. These populations have higher rates of CFRD and non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic individuals are diagnosed with CFRD at younger ages [1,24]. Further research will be needed to investigate alternative screening approaches for CFRD in a larger and more diverse sample across a broader age range.

In conclusion, alternative approaches to glucose measurements that do not require venipuncture and that can be conducted in the home setting may have the potential to improve rates of screening for CFRD but require further assessments of accuracy and usability prior to implementation. The preliminary findings suggest that CGM glucose values at minute 125 of an OGTT can distinguish between NGT and IGT. These results support the need for future larger studies investigating alternative approaches to CFRD screening in people with CF.
