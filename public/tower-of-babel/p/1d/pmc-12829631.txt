Central giant cell granuloma of the mandible: A case report with review of literature
Authors: Uday Kiran Uppada, PN Ramaraj, Milind A Bhatkule, Unnati D Gedam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Central giant cell granuloma (CGCG) is an unusual, benign osteolytic lesion of the maxillofacial region. By virtue of its manifestation as an aggressive locally destructive lesion in the mandible of young individuals, its diagnosis is often misleading since it mimics certain clinical entities with regards to their clinical and radiographic presentation. This necessitates the need for a thorough understanding of CGCG. Therefore, to enable a detailed understanding of this clinical entity, we put forth a case report of CGCG with a comprehensive review of literature.

INTRODUCTION

It is a well-known fact that the anterior mandible is a unique anatomical area in the maxillofacial skeleton.[1] Among the osseous components of the maxillofacial skeleton, the mandible is usually affected by a wide variety of pathologies of both odontogenic and non-odontogenic origin with a greater predisposition for the mandibular angle and posterior body region.[2345] The anterior mandible is seldom affected by pathologies of non-odontogenic origin.[2]

Central giant cell granuloma (CGCG) is one such clinical entity that is encountered in this anatomical region. It is a locally aggressive and destructive osteolytic lesion of osteoclastic origin, accounting for <7% of all benign tumors of the jaw.[6] It is usually encountered in younger individuals with a greater female predilection[6] and manifests primarily as an asymptomatic lesion, but as it progresses, it becomes expansile causing pain, cortical perforation, and root resorption.[78] Once a reparative lesion but was later suggested as an inflammatory lesion, a reactive lesion, or a lesion of endocrine origin.[9] Till date the pathogenesis and the nature of this clinical entity still remain uncertain.[810] A change in the hemostatic–vascular balance that results in alteration in the bone is currently being proposed.[11]

CASE REPORT

A 42-year-old female patient reported to our unit complaining of pain in the right lower jaw region for 8 months. She gave a history of undergoing root canal treatment for the teeth in the same region due to pain 6 months back in a dental clinical at her locality. However, there was no relief from the pain. Later, she underwent extraction of her teeth due to pain in the same region but the pain did not subside.

Clinical examination revealed no gross facial asymmetry [Figure 1a]. Intraoral examination revealed missing 43 and 44 with root canal treated 41 and 42 at the anatomical region where the patient complained of pain [Figure 1b]. There was no swelling in the buccal and lingual aspects of the mandible on the right side, but tenderness was elicited on palpation in the anterior region of the mandible on the right side. The overlying mucosa in the area of suspicion was normal. All the teeth in the mandible were firm and vital on the pulp vitality test except the root can treat 41 and 42.

Orthopantomogram revealed two independent well-defined radiolucent area present one above another in the right side of the anterior mandible. The upper radiolucency was oval in shape, measuring 2 × 2.5 cm extending antero-posteriorly from the midline toward the lateral aspect involving the 41 and 42 teeth and superior-inferiorly from the crest of the alveolar bone downwards. The lower radiolucency was round in shape, measuring 2 × 2 cm below the upper radiolucency sparing the lower border of the mandible. Both the radiolucencies were separated by a thin slice of good-quality bone [Figure 1c]. CT scan revealed a bicortical perforation with regard to both the radiolucent lesions [Figure 1d]. Fine needle aspiration was negative. Incisional biopsy was performed, and the microscopic evaluation revealed a CGCG. Based on the history, clinical manifestation, and radiographic findings coupled with histopathologic interpretation, a giant cell granuloma involving the right mandible was considered as a probable diagnosis.

Since radiographically it exhibited multiple areas of radiolucency in the mandible, clinical entities like ossifying fibroma, hyperparathyroidism, and an aneurysmal bone cyst were considered in the differential diagnosis. Patient consent was taken, and marginal resection of the mandible was planned under GA [Figure 2a-f]. Considering the age of the patient, reconstruction was planned with patient-specific implants. Following the resection, the specimen was sent for histopathological examination which showed bony trabeculae with osteoblastic rimming and intervening tissue composed of fibroblastic proliferation with patchy distributed multinucleated giant cells and extravasated erythrocytes and hemosiderin-laden macrophages suggestive of central giant cell granuloma [Figure 3a].

Following an appropriate healing period, the abutments were exposed using electrocautery [Figure 3b]. Temporization was achieved using self-cure acrylic resin to preserve the abutment height and facilitate soft tissue contouring. Intraorally, cylindrical self-cure acrylic patterns were fabricated to evaluate parallelism and optimize the abutment height. These patterns were subsequently sent to the laboratory for casting. After receiving the cast metal copings, their fit was meticulously assessed intraorally and cemented in place [Figure 3c]. A definitive impression was made using addition silicone impression material, and the jaw relation was recorded. Both the final impression and jaw relation record were forwarded to the laboratory for the fabrication of a fixed acrylic hybrid prosthesis. A trial fit of the prosthesis was conducted, and final cementation was performed using glass ionomer cement [Figure 3d]. The postoperative period was uneventful without any recurrence after 6 months [Figure 3e and f].

DISCUSSION

Precise diagnosis of osseous tumors of the maxillofacial skeleton is often challenging. Clinical entities like the CGCG, aneurysmal bone cyst, ameloblastoma, ossifying fibroma, odontogenic myxoma, and sarcomas usually show radiographically identical soap bubble or honeycomb appearance with scalloped margins.[1213] Histologically, several osseous tumors such as the ossifying fibromas, aneurysmal bone cysts, and high-grade malignant sarcomas exhibit multinucleated giant cells identical to CGCG.[14] Metabolic disorders like hyperparathyroidism also exhibit masses of reactive osteoclast-like giant cells histopathologically identical to CGCG.[14] Serum calcium and alkaline phosphatase levels help in differentiating CGCG and hyperparathyroidism, while bioimpedance assays aid in ruling out malignant lesions.[6]

CGCG is usually managed with simple enucleation and curettage. However, segmental resection may be required in clinical situations where there are multiple recurrences or invasions to surrounding soft tissues as seen in this case.[15] Due to the complex anatomy and defect uniqueness in the maxillofacial region coupled with the high aesthetic expectation of the patient, the surgical repair and reconstruction of maxillofacial defects are often challenging.[16]

The advent of additive manufacturing, three-dimensional (3D) printing, and the recent advances in those technologies has positively influenced the biomedical field, leading to the utilization of patient-specific implants (PSIs) in the surgical repair of maxillofacial defects.[17] The use of PSI offers higher accuracy, better site adaptation, and shortened operating time compared to pre-bent or pre-made implants.[18] The only downside to the use of PSI is its high cost.[16] A fixed hybrid acrylic prosthesis was chosen over a fixed metal-ceramic prosthesis due to the increased interocclusal space, which would have made the metal-ceramic option excessively bulky and heavy. The acrylic prosthesis offers several advantages, including its lighter weight and the ease of retrieval in case of complications like fracture or chipping, wear of acrylic teeth, soft tissue irritation, debonding of prosthesis, denture base fractures, and color instability ensuring more convenient management if adjustments or repairs are required in the future.

CONCLUSION

To accomplish an ideal functional and aesthetic outcome in such clinical scenarios, a multidisciplinary approach between the radiologist, surgeon, and pathologist is essential. The use of computer-designed PSI facilitates the precise reconstruction of maxillofacial defects, eliminating the usual complications seen in preformed implants, thereby enabling greater patient satisfaction.
