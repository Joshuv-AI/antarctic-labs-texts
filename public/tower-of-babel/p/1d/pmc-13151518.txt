Parasutterella excrementihominis exacerbates experimental colitis and colitis-associated colorectal cancer via pathogenic NETosis activation
Authors: Huishi Tan, Linwen Huang, Jun Wang, Hongli Huang, Zelong Lin, Siqi Yang, Yanqiang Shi, Jierui Li, Haiyan Zhang, Yongjian Zhou, Chongyang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParasutterella excrementihominis (P. excrementihominis), a Betaproteobacteria species enriched in ulcerative colitis (UC) patients, is implicated in chronic inflammation. However, its mechanistic role in UC progression and colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study investigates the pathogenic role of P. excrementihominis in UC and CAC, focusing on its induction of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and underlying mechanisms.DesignClinical stool samples from UC patients and healthy controls were analysed for P. excrementihominis abundance. Murine models of dextran sulphate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis and azoxymethane/DSS-induced CAC were used to evaluate bacterial pathogenicity. RNA sequencing and metabolomic analyses were conducted on germ-free mice with monocolonisation, and in vitro cell experiments were carried out to elucidate the role of bacterial metabolites in NETosis.ResultsP. excrementihominis was significantly enriched in UC patients and exacerbated colitis and CAC in mice by expanding colonic neutrophils and NETs formation. Metabolomic profiling revealed that P. excrementihominis enhances the host’s carbohydrate metabolic capacity, leading to increased production of succinic acid (Suc) and 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid (6-HHA). These metabolites activated gasdermin D (GSDMD)-dependent NETosis in lipopolysaccharide-primed neutrophils through the succinate receptor 1/G protein-coupled receptor 84 signalling pathway. Conversely, neutrophil-specific GSDMD deletion attenuated metabolite-driven tumourigenesis.ConclusionOur findings identify P. excrementihominis as a critical microbial driver of UC and CAC pathogenesis. This bacterium significantly accelerates disease progression by producing specific metabolites (Suc and 6-HHA) that induce pathogenic NETosis. Targeting this bacterium or its metabolic axis offers novel therapeutic strategies for inflammation-driven colorectal carcinogenesis.Keywords: BACTERIAL PATHOGENESIS, IBD BASIC RESEARCH, ULCERATIVE COLITIS

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC), a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), significantly elevates the risk of colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) due to sustained mucosal injury and dysregulated immune responses.1,3 Dysbiosis, particularly the enrichment of Proteobacteria, is a hallmark of UC and CAC. However, the mechanistic role of specific pathobionts within this phylum remains poorly defined.4

Parasutterella, a genus within the Betaproteobacteria family, is widely accepted as a resident of the human gastrointestinal (GI) tract linked to several host health outcomes, including IBD.5 6 In humans, Parasutterella excrementihominis (P. excrementihominis) stands as the primary species within the Parasutterella genus.7 P. excrementihominis is considered a potential pathogen due to its link with metabolic disorders and IBD severity. Recent studies showed Parasutterella plays a role in fatty acid production and weight gain on a high-carb diet, especially in those with type 2 diabetes and obesity.8 Despite its prevalence in UC patients, the precise role linking P. excrementihominis to disease progression remains undefined.

Chronic inflammation in UC and CAC is driven by dysregulated immune responses, including aberrant neutrophil activation.9 Recent studies highlight neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) as key mediators of mucosal damage in UC pathogenesis.10 NETs, a web-like structure released during NETosis, can worsen epithelial damage via reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inflammatory mediators, including S100A8/9 and IL-1β.11 Myeloperoxidase (MPO), neutrophil elastase (NE) and citrullinated histones are key components of NETs. Intracellular calcium accumulation and peptidyl arginine deaminase 4 (PADI4) activation drive NETs formation.12 PADI4 converts specific positively charged arginine residues to neutral citrulline residues on histones, thereby enhancing the release of citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3).13 Gasdermin D (GSDMD), a protein critical for pyroptotic cell death, plays a pivotal role in NETs formation and the rapid release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs).14 GSDMD-dependent NETosis is crucial in the development and progression of chronic inflammatory disorders.15 16 Dysregulated NETosis could potentially exacerbate mucosal damage in UC. However, the microbial factors triggering NETs formation remain poorly defined. Accordingly, therapeutic blockage of NETosis could mitigate barrier disruption and inflammatory responses, thereby slowing UC and CAC progression.17 18

The imbalance of the intestinal microbiome during the active stage of UC underscores the key role of specific microbes in dysregulated immune responses and gut mucosal inflammation.19 This study examines how P. excrementihominis and its metabolites promote NETosis, chronic inflammation and CAC progression. P. excrementihominis is frequently found in the faeces of UC patients and linked to disease severity. Colonisation of P. excrementihominis in germ-free (GF) mice promotes carbohydrate metabolism and organic acid production. Metabolites derived from P. excrementihominis promote NETs formation, sustaining chronic colitis progression and barrier dysfunction in experimental colitis and CAC. This study offers new insights into the pathogenic role of the P. excrementihominis-NETs axis in UC and CAC.

Methods

GMrepo database analysis

The microbiota relative abundances in the stool samples of the healthy individuals and UC patients were obtained from the GMrepo database. We first assessed the data quality by consulting the description of the samples and clinical information. Then, the relative abundance of the Parasutterella genus for the healthy and IBD patients was extracted and analysed in online supplemental table S1.

Human sample collection

The intestinal tissues and faecal samples were collected from patients with UC, and non-UC healthy donors visiting the Department of Gastroenterology. The procedure adhered to ethical guidelines as outlined by the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided informed consent. Diagnoses and assessments of clinical disease activity were determined using a standardised combination of clinical, endoscopic, histological and radiological criteria. This study received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Guangzhou First People’s Hospital (approval number: K-2021-117-03). Demographic and clinical characteristics are shown in online supplemental table S2. Multivariable linear regression analysis of factors associated with the abundance of P.excrementihominis in UC patients is revealed in online supplemental table S3.

Animal experiments

8-week-old and 18-month-old wild-type C57BL/6J mice, Padi4 knockout mice (online supplemental figure S4) and GSDMDfl/flS100A8-Cre mice (online supplemental figure S17) were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences and used for the experiments. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in accredited animal facilities at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. Briefly, all mice were housed in a room with controlled temperature (21–23℃) and lighting (12/12-hour light-dark cycle) and were given free access to water and food. Before samples collection, mice were anaesthetised and euthanised by cervical dislocation. All efforts were made to minimise the suffering and discomfort experienced by the animals.

To homogenise the microbiome, all wild-type C57BL/6J mice were co-housed for 1 week prior to the start of the experiment. All animal research protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (animal ethical statement no: 2023038). After the newborn mice reach 3 weeks of age, they will be numbered and their tails will be clipped for genetic identification, with the identified PCR primers (online supplemental table S4 and S5).

Disease activity index score evaluation

Disease activity index (DAI) tracks the severity of the disease by scoring the degree of weight loss, faecal consistency and blood in the faeces. The score is determined based on weight loss, faecal consistency and rectal bleeding, as shown in online supplemental table S6.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed as previously described.20 Briefly, total RNA was extracted from snap-frozen colon tissues and cells using the TRIzol reagent (AGBIO, Hunan, China; AG21102), and a reverse transcript reaction was performed with a reverse transcript kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China; 11 151ES60) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR amplification was conducted using Hieff UNICON qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Cat# 11 185ES08). The quantitative real-time PCR was performed on the Roche LightCycler 96 system. In summary, gene expression levels were calculated using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method. mRNA quantities of the designated genes were normalised in relation to 18S. Online supplemental table S7 and S8 displays the primers used for the specified genes.

DNA extraction and bacterial quantification

QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (51604, Qiagen) was used for bacterial DNA extraction from human and mice stool and intestinal samples according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Bacterial quantification was measured by quantitative real-time PCR in an ABI Quant Studio 7 Flex system. Each reaction was performed in triplicate with 2X SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Tracking Kit (ROX2 plus, AG11735, Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd), primers and template DNA. Universal eubacteria 16S was used as the internal reference gene for stool samples. The genomic DNA was extracted from the bacterial culture suspension, and a segment of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers detailed in online supplemental table S9 for the bacterial analysis. The amplified gene segments were then sequenced, and the taxonomy of each strain was identified via a Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST, https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov).

FISH

Paraffin-embedded colon sections were used for fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) assay using the Bacterial FISH Assay kit (0.25911fh, JenerBio). The bacterial FISH protocol for paraffin sections of the human colon involves baking slides at 75℃ for 15 min, deparaffinising in xylene substitute (5 min, three times), rehydrating through an ethanol series (100%, 85%, 70%; 3 min each) and rinsing in 1× phosphate buffer saline (PBS)-diethypyrocarbonate (DEPC). This is followed by sequential 15-min incubations at 37℃ with acidification, permeabilisation and digestion solutions, each followed by 1×PBS (DEPC) washes. Blocking is performed at 55℃ for 1 hour. The FISH probe (Junengbio) is diluted 1:50 in 25% hybridisation buffer, denatured at 85℃ for 3 min, equilibrated at 37℃ for 2 min, applied to the sample and hybridised at 37℃ for 36 hours in a light-proof humid chamber. The probe information is listed in online supplemental table S10. Post-hybridisation, slides are washed in 1× Wash Buffer I (5 min, two times, light-protected) and prewarmed 1× Wash Buffer II (10 min, three times, 45℃). Finally, slides are air-dried, stained with DAPI (ab104139, Abcam), coverslipped and imaged by fluorescence microscopy (CSU W1, Nikon).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software 8.0. Normality was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test was employed to assess intergroup differences. Unpaired or paired Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups when data met assumptions of normality and equal variance. All results were expressed as mean±SE of the Mean (SEM). Logistic regression analysis was performed on the selected microbial strain using the pROC package (V.1.18.5) in R to evaluate its diagnostic significance in UC. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (area under the curve (AUC)) was calculated to further assess the predictive accuracy of these features. Multivariable regression analysis of microbial value was performed in R to evaluate the factors associated with the abundance of P. excrementihominis in patients with UC. Significance was defined as P values <0.05.

Supplementary methods are provided in online supplemental information.

Results

Increased abundance of P. excrementihominis in patients with UC and CAC

We initially analysed the abundance of P. excrementihominis in stool samples from patients with IBD using the GMrepo database. Our results revealed a higher abundance of the Parasutterella genus in patients with IBD (PRJEB794921 and PRJNA38594922) and UC (PRJNA36896623 and PRJNA39808924) than in healthy individuals across the four datasets (online supplemental table S1). Notably, analysis of metagenomic next-generation sequencing data has revealed a higher abundance of P. excrementihominis in two IBD cohorts, using the GMrepo dataset. The first cohort is from the School of Biosciences, Cardiff University, with the accession number PRJEB7949, UK. The second cohort is from the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, with the accession number PRJNA385949, USA (figure 1A–C). Subsequently, we validated the P. excrementihominis levels in the stool samples of 115 UC patients (comprising 36 in remission and 79 with active disease) and 20 healthy controls from our hospital in Guangzhou, China (online supplemental table S2). Quantitative PCR revealed that P. excrementihominis abundance (2ˆ-ΔΔCt) was significantly elevated in patients with active UC, compared with patients in remission and healthy controls (figure 1D). Besides, the abundance of P. excrementihominis was correlated to disease activity of UC, as evidenced by a positive correlation between P. excrementihominis abundance and the Mayo scores (R2=0.5432), C-reactive protein (R2=0.54) and erythrocyte sedimentation rates (R2=0.484) (figure 1E). The ROC curve analysis further indicated the diagnostic value of P. excrementihominis for active UC, with an AUC of 0.875 (online supplemental figure S1A). Logistic regression analysis showed that P. excrementihominis abundance was an independent risk factor for active UC (OR=1.108, 95% CI 1.022 to 1.183) (online supplemental figure S1B).

Interestingly, the abundance of P. excrementihominis increased in UC patients as the disease duration extended or when colon cancer was present (figure 1F,G). However, the multivariate regression analysis unveiled several crucial findings regarding the factors associated with the abundance of P. excrementihominis in patients with UC. The disease duration serves as a robust predictor (β=4.01, p <0.001), and age also exhibits a significant positive correlation (β=0.78, p <0.001). In contrast, the presence of CRC demonstrates no association (p=0.810). Gender (p=0.889) and body mass index (p=0.340) are not significant predictors (online supplemental figure S1C, online supplemental table S3). Moreover, the abundance of P. excrementihominis was also elevated in inflamed colonic sections as compared with non-inflamed areas in active UC patients (figure 1H). To spatially localise P. excrementihominis within the gut mucosa, we performed FISH assay. This analysis confirmed the presence of P. excrementihominis clusters within the inflamed colonic mucosa of active UC patients, while they were scarce or absent in the mucosa of patients in remission (figure 1I,J). Overall, these findings suggest that P. excrementihominis is enriched in UC and correlates with disease severity.

P. excrementihominis aggravates colitis development in mice.

To validate the potential pathogenic role of P. excrementihominis suggested by clinical data, we established murine models of colitis. Before the bacterial colonisation experiment, we confirmed the bacterial identity of P. excrementihominis with sequencing of PCR amplicon and a BLAST search (online supplemental figure S2A and B). We also assessed its growth viability in an anaerobic environment (online supplemental figure S2C). Mice were gavaged with live or heat-killed bacteria, P. excrementihominis culture supernatant or control medium and then subjected to dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis (figure 2A). To investigate the temporal dynamics of P. excrementihominis colonisation, we analysed its abundance in GI compartments during DSS-induced colitis (figure 2B–D). Our data showed P. excrementihominis preferentially colonised antibiotic-treated mice, showing a 70-fold increase in stool samples and over a 60-fold increase in colonic tissues, while the small intestine exhibited only a 10-fold increase. Following DSS treatment, colonisation levels declined gradually but remained detectable in all tissues examined, indicating persistent bacterium presence during the development of colitis (figure 2B–D).

Our findings revealed that colonisation with live P. excrementihominis significantly exacerbated DSS-induced colitis, as evidenced by increased body weight loss, higher disease activity index and greater colon shortening (figure 2E–H). Histological analysis corroborated these findings, showing widespread epithelial damage and immune cell infiltration (figure 2I,J). Crucially, the administration of the bacterial culture supernatant recapitulated the pathogenic effects of live bacteria, whereas heat-killed bacteria did not (figure 2E–J). These results suggested that P. excrementihominis may induce intestinal inflammation via specific metabolites.

To understand its pathogenic role, colonic immune cell infiltration was assessed using immunostaining. As depicted in figure 2K,L, P. excrementihominis induced infiltration of Ly6G+ neutrophils into the colonic lamina propria, whereas other immune cells, including CD8+ T cells and F4/80+ monocytes, exhibited non-significant differences among groups. These findings indicate a potential interaction between P. excrementihominis and pro-inflammatory neutrophils during colitis progression. Consequently, mice were administered a Ly6G-neutralising antibody to deplete neutrophils in P. excrementihominis-colonised mice during DSS treatment (online supplemental figure S3A,B). Neutrophil depletion effectively reduced colonic inflammation caused by P. excrementihominis (online supplemental figure S3C-H), suggesting P. excrementihominis aggravates colonic inflammation by boosting inflammatory neutrophil activation.

P. excrementihominis triggers NETs to intensify intestinal inflammation

To investigate the mechanism by which P. excrementihominis activates neutrophils, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on colonic tissues from mice treated with live bacteria or control medium, either with or without DSS (figure 3A). The colonisation of P. excrementihominis following DSS treatment resulted in the upregulation of 179 genes in the colon (figure 3B,C). CSF3R, CXCL2 and CXCR2 mRNA expression levels showed the same trend as neutrophil infiltration in IHC analysis (figure 3D,E). Moreover, genes associated with NETs, including PADI4, S100A8/9, MMP8/9 and LCN2, were significantly increased in P. excrementihominis-treated mice compared with control mice following DSS treatment (figure 3D). Besides, we found that specific pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, IL-17A, CXCL2/3, CCL2/4/7 and IL-1α/β, were increased in P. excrementihominis-treated mice post-DSS treatment (figure 3E). Several inflammatory mediators, including NLRP3, NLRP12, G protein-coupled receptor 84 (GPR84) and ACOD1, were highly expressed in the colons of P. excrementihominis-treated mice after DSS exposure (figure 3F). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further indicated that genes upregulated by P. excrementihominis were significantly enriched in pathways related to NETs formation and IBD (figure 3G).

Results of qPCR analysis further validated the RNA-seq findings, as shown by a significant upregulation of genes related to neutrophil activation and NETs formation (S100A8/9, MMP8/9, CTSG, CXCR2, LCN2, CSF3/3R and PADI4) in the colons of P. excrementihominis-treated DSS mice (figure 3H). To directly study P. excrementihominis’s effect on intestinal NETs formation during colitis, we conducted double immunofluorescence staining for MPO and CitH3 in colon tissues. This analysis revealed that P. excrementihominis colonisation promoted MPO+/CitH3+ cells infiltration in DSS-treated colon (figure 3I,J). We then introduced mice with PADI4 gene deficiency to explore the potential role of NETs driven by P. excrementihominis (online supplemental figure S4A-C). Notably, PADI4 deletion reduced P. excrementihominis-driven intestinal inflammation, as shown by similar levels of faecal bleeding, body weight loss, colon shortening and histological scores (figure 3K–P). These results indicate P. excrementihominis partly triggers colitis by causing NETs formation.

To rule out potential confounding impacts of antibiotic pretreatment on immune responses, we also implemented a specific colonisation strategy without antibiotics5 (online supplemental figure S5A). Consistent with our previous findings, colonisation exclusively with P. excrementihominis was sufficient to exacerbate DSS-induced colitis, as demonstrated by increased weight loss, a higher DAI and more significant colon shortening (online supplemental figure S5B-H). Additionally, neutrophil-associated genes (S100A8/9, CXCR2 and CSF3R) were upregulated in the P. excrementihominis-colonised colon following DSS treatment (online supplemental figure S5I).

P. excrementihominis promotes colitis-associated tumourigenesis

Long-term chronic intestinal inflammation promotes oncogenesis in patients with UC.25 We hypothesised that P. excrementihominis could hijack neutrophil traps to fuel the development of chronic colitis and its associated tumourigenesis. Following azoxymethane (AOM)/DSS-induced CAC modelling, mice with P. excrementihominis showed exacerbated colonic inflammation, indicated by significant weight loss and colon shortening (figure 4A,B). Similar results were noted in the CAC model, as the administration of bacterial culture supernatants also promotes tumour progression (figure 4C–F).

P. excrementihominis and its bacterial supernatants significantly increased the number of Ki67-positive cells in CAC-induced tumours (figure 4G–I). Furthermore, a greater proportion of MPO/CitH3-positive cells and increased expression of genes associated with inflammatory neutrophil, such as MPO, S100A8/9, MMP3/9/10, CCL4, CAMP, LTF and CTSG, were observed in the colon of CAC mice treated with P. excrementihominis and its culture supernatants (figure 4H–J). Moreover, immunofluorescence analysis showed increased intestinal barrier permeability due to P. excrementihominis colonisation (online supplemental figure S6A-C). These findings suggest P. excrementihominis enhances NETs production, worsening colitis-related tumourigenesis.

To investigate the role of PADI4-mediated NETosis in the tumour-promoting effects of P. excrementihominis, we colonised both PADI4-KO mice and wild-type littermates with this bacterium, using the AOM/DSS model for CAC. Genetic deletion of PADI4 conferred protection against P. excrementihominis-driven tumourigenesis and colonic inflammation (online supplemental figure S7A-G). PADI4-KO mice exposed to P. excrementihominis exhibited reduced colonic tumour burden, accompanied by lower expression levels of MPO, S100A8/9 and MMP9/10 in colonic tissues (online supplemental figure S7C-G).

P. excrementihominis induces a metabolic shift in GF mice

The gnotobiotic model was established to investigate the metabolic regulatory effects of P. excrementihominis on the host (online supplemental figure S8A). Blood plate smears and Gram staining confirmed P. excrementihominis colonisation in GF mice’s GI tract (online supplemental figure S8B and C). Colonisation by P. excrementihominis did not influence the body weight of GF mice (online supplemental figure S8D and E). Notably, histological examination of colonic tissues displayed no signs of epithelial damage or immune cell infiltration in GF mice colonised with P. excrementihominis (online supplemental figure S8F). Besides, P. excrementihominis colonisation did not affect the tight junction or pro-inflammatory genes expression in GF colon tissues (online supplemental figure S8G-I).

Next, we conducted RNA sequencing on colon tissues from GF mice, both with and without P. excrementihominis colonisation. A total of 248 genes showed differential expression within the P. excrementihominis-colonised colon (figure 5A), with elevated expression of several metabolic genes such as Ins2, G6pc2 and Mafb (figure 5B,C). Ins2 gene encodes insulin, a key hormone that regulates carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, whereas Mafb activates the insulin and glucagon promoters. G6pc2 is involved in gluconeogenesis and glycolysis.26 27 Differentially expressed genes suggested P. excrementihominis reduces blood glucose and promotes gluconeogenesis. GO pathway enrichment analysis revealed that P. excrementihominis modulates the host immune response, affecting bacterial defence, B cell receptor signalling and phagocytosis (figure 5D). KEGG pathway analysis showed P. excrementihominis colonisation alters metabolic processes in GF mice, activating pathways related to lipolysis, starch and sucrose metabolism, bile secretion and fat absorption (figure 5E). Protein-protein interaction network analysis suggested P. excrementihominis may affect colonic gene expression by modulating transcription factor Fos, indicating its role in metabolic disruptions linked to colitis-associated tumourigenesis (figure 5F).

P. excrementihominis regulates 6-HHA and Suc synthesis through carbohydrate metabolism.

We then conducted metabolomic analysis of serum and faecal samples from GF mice, both with or without colonisation by P. excrementihominis, as well as on culture supernatants and control medium (figure 6A). Metabolomics profiling uncovered significant variations in metabolite profiles between control and P. excrementihominis-colonised samples (figure 6B–D and online supplemental figure S9A-C).

Here, P. excrementihominis colonisation principally influenced several metabolite classes, including carbohydrates, organic acids and indoles (figure 6E–G). Faecal levels of glucose residues, such as sucrose, ketose and diverse malto-oligosaccharides, were markedly decreased in P. excrementihominis-colonised mice (online supplemental figure S9D). Consistently, blood levels of galactinol, trehalose and raffinose were lower in these mice (online supplemental figure S9E), suggesting that P. excrementihominis may boost host carbohydrate absorption and metabolism. Tryptophan metabolic pathway is critical for immune regulation and antioxidant defence.28 The faecal levels of tryptophan metabolites in P. excrementihominis-colonised mice exhibited complexity, with increased levels of certain indole derivatives, including 4-hydroxyindole and indolelactic acid (ILA), and decreased levels of others, including indole-3-carboxylic acid and 7-hydroxystaurosporine (online supplemental figure S9D). Pathway analysis indicated that the bacterial colonisation impacted host metabolism, particularly fatty acid oxidation, ketone body metabolism and the citric acid cycle (online supplemental figure S10A-D).

We next sought to identify the specific metabolites produced by P. excrementihominis. Metabolomic profiling revealed that P. excrementihominis-colonised mice had elevated plasma levels of several organic acids, including 3-hydroxybutanoic acid, 2-hydroxybutyric acid, 3-phenyllactic acid (3-PLA) and 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid (6-HHA) (figure 6G,J). The faeces of P. excrementihominis-colonised mice displayed elevated levels of 3-PLA, nicotinic acid (NA), ILA, 3-hydroxydodecanoic acid (3-HHA), 6-HAA and succinic acid (Suc) levels (figure 6F,I). Strikingly, the bacterial culture supernatant itself was enriched in three key metabolites, including malic acid (MA), 6-HHA and Suc (figure 6E,H). This overlapping pattern between in vivo colonisation and in vitro culture confirms that P. excrementihominis is a direct source of these metabolites. In conclusion, P. excrementihominis can convert carbohydrates into tricarboxylic acid cycle-related organic acids.

6-HHA and Suc facilitate LPS-induced NETosis in neutrophils through GPR84/SUCNR1 signalling

Subsequently, we investigated metabolites from P. excrementihominis that trigger NETosis during colitis progression. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated bone marrow (BM)-derived neutrophils were, respectively, exposed to metabolites (Suc, NA, ILA, MA, 3-HHA, 6-HHA and 3-PLA) and then analysed for inflammatory cytokine levels. Our results demonstrated that only 6-HHA and Suc enhanced the expression of several inflammatory mediators, such as ELANE, S100A9, CCL4 and IL-1β (figure 7A). However, 6-HHA and Suc did not cause upregulation of inflammatory factor expression in Phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA)-treated neutrophils (online supplemental figure S11A). Further observation revealed that 6-HHA and Suc not only upregulated the expression of NETs-associated genes, including ELANE, S100A8/9, LCN2, CAMP and MMP9/10, but also augmented the expression of various chemokines, including CCL2/3/4 and CXCL2/9 (figure 7B). Consistently, 6-HHA and Suc promoted NETosis, as evidenced by the increased number of LPS-stimulated cells that tested for MPO+/CitH3+ immunostaining and SYTOX Green staining (figure 7C–F). Our results indicated that 6-HHA and Suc significantly increased NLRP3 inflammasome activation and GSDMD cleavage in LPS-primed BM neutrophils (figure 7G,H). Similar findings were noted in human polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) stimulated by LPS and treated with 6-HHA and Suc. In these PMNs, heightened expression levels of pro-inflammatory genes (S100A8/9, ELANE, CAMP, MMP9, etc) were detected, along with an augmented number of cells positive for MPO/CitH3 immunostaining and SYTOX Green staining (online supplemental figure S12A-D). Moreover, 6-HHA and Suc also triggered the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the cleavage of GSDMD in LPS-treated PMNs (online supplemental figure S12E).

Previous studies indicated that GPR84 recognises medium-chain fatty acids, whereas succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1) identifies Suc.29 30 The activation of both receptors is involved in modulating immune and inflammatory responses.31 32 The molecular docking results showed that Suc has a moderate affinity for SUCNR1 (Vina score: −5 kal/mol) (online supplemental figure S13A). Meanwhile, the molecular docking results indicated that 6-HHA exhibits a preferable affinity for GPR84 (Vina score: −5.3 kal/mol) (online supplemental figure S13B). Besides, our results demonstrated that 6-HHA activated GPR84, and Suc activated SUCNR1 in BM neutrophils (online supplemental figure S13C). Consistently, analysing the GEO datasets (GSE945233 and GSE3871334), both GPR84 and SUCNR1 are upregulated in the colon sections of active UC patients compared with healthy controls (online supplemental figure S13D). These findings underscore the intricate interplay between metabolite-sensing receptors and immune responses within the context of intestinal inflammation.

6-HHA and Suc elicite GSDMD-dependent NETs formation to foster colitis and CAC development

To confirm the in vivo significance of these findings, 6-HHA and Suc were administered to mice following DSS treatment. In wild-type mice, the administration of 6-HHA and Suc exacerbated colitis symptoms, including weight loss, faecal bleeding and colon shortening (figure 8A–D). Histological analysis showed more severe tissue damage and infiltration of inflammatory cells in the colons of wild-type mice treated with 6-HHA and Suc (figure 8E,F). In the CAC model, the presence of 6-HHA and Suc in wild-type mice led to an increased number and size of tumours in the colon (figure 8G–M). In the colon tissues of CAC mice treated with 6-HHA and Suc, the protein expression levels of NETs formation-related indicators, including MPO, S100A8/9 and cleaved GSDMD, were upregulated (figure 8N).

To determine whether colitis exacerbation is specific to P. excrementihominis, GF mice were colonised with five commensal bacteria and subsequently challenged with DSS (online supplemental figure S14A). We compared P. excrementihominis with other bacteria, including Streptococcus gordonii (S. gordonii), Bacteroides vulgatus (B. vulgatus), Ruminococcus bromii (R. bromii) and Escherichia coli (E. coli).35,37 Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis of the bacterial culture supernatant showed that P. excrementihominis produced both 6-HHA and Suc, while S. gordonii produced only 6-HHA, and B. vulgatus produced only Suc (online supplemental figure S14B-E). R. bromii produces neither of the metabolites, and E. coli served as a representative control for Proteobacteria. Faecal samples from mono-colonised GF mice were analysed for the presence of bacterial strains and the 6-HHA/Suc levels (online supplemental figure S15A-E). Faecal metabolomics confirmed that colonisation by P. excrementihominis or B. vulgatus significantly increased faecal Suc levels (online supplemental figure S15B,C). Conversely, mono-association with P. excrementihominis or S. gordonii led to a marked increase in faecal 6-HHA (online supplemental figure S15D and E). Metabolic output was directly correlated with disease severity. Only P. excrementihominis monocolonisation recapitulated the full severe colitis phenotype, including weight loss, elevated DAI, colon shortening and histological damage (online supplemental figure S16A-F). This included increased MPO/CitH3-positive cells and significant upregulation of NETosis-associated genes, including S100A8/9, CSF3/CSF3R, PADI4, MMP8/9, LCN2, IL-1β and CXCR2 (online supplemental figure S16G-I). While S. gordonii and B. vulgatus caused milder phenotypes compared with P. excrementihominis, R. bromii and E. coli did not worsen colitis versus DSS-only controls. The stronger pathogenicity of P. excrementihominis indicates that concurrent 6-HHA and Suc production drives its unique pro-inflammatory synergy.

Considering GSDMD’s key role in controlling NETs formation and inflammatory substances release,38 neutrophil-specific GSDMD-deficient mice (GSDMDflflS100A8-Cre) were used to validate metabolite-driven NETosis (online supplemental figure S17A-C). The depletion of GSDMD effectively inhibited the progression of CAC accelerated by 6-HHA and Suc, as evidenced by the comparable body weight reduction, colonic shortening and colonic tumour burden (figure 9A–G). GSDMDflflS100A8-Cre mice showed similar expression levels of MPO, S100A8/9 and MMP9/10 across the 6-HHA, Suc and control groups (figure 9H). These data directly link neutrophil GSDMD to the metabolite-induced NETs formation in vivo, reinforcing its crucial role in mediating the pro-inflammatory effects of 6-HHA and Suc.

Aging-associated increase in P. excrementihominis fosters colitis-associated colorectal tumourigenesis

Previous study indicated elevated faecal levels of P. excrementihominis in elderly individuals.39 To investigate this further, we examined the microbial composition in both aged (18 months) and young (2 months) mice using 16S rRNA analysis. Ageing is associated with a marked expansion of P. excrementihominis in mice (online supplemental figure S18A-C). Notably, faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from aged donors accelerated AOM/DSS-induced tumourigenic phenotypes in recipient mice, emphasising the key role of age-related microbial imbalance in promoting CAC (online supplemental figure 17D-H). Histological analysis further confirmed that age-associated increase in P. excrementihominis exacerbated colonic tumourigenesis (online supplemental figure S18I,J).

Given the link between P. excrementihominis and carbohydrate metabolism, we next investigated how specific dietary components influence its colonisation and expansion. Young mice colonised with P. excrementihominis received eight distinct dietary regimens for 2 weeks (online supplemental figure S19A). These diets included control diet (AIN93G), high-fat diet (HFD), high-carbohydrate/low-fat diet (HC), inulin-supplemented diet (Inulin), cellulose-supplemented diet (high-fibre, HFB), vitamin D3-supplemented diet (VD), a low branched-chain amino acids diet (LBCAA) and a ketogenic diet (KTD) (online supplemental table S1). HFD and HC consumption were associated with a tendency for increased body weight gain in mice, while HFB, LBCAA and KTD resulted in relatively lower body weights (online supplemental figure S19B). Notably, 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that diet greatly changed microbial composition (online supplemental figure S19C,D). KTD diets increased α-diversity compared with the control diet, as measured by the Simpson and Shannon indices (online supplemental figure S19E). Bacterial analysis revealed higher Parasutterella abundance in VD-fed and HFD-fed mice. Notably, Parasutterella levels were elevated in mice on the HC diet, suggesting that high carbohydrate intake may specifically promote the growth of this pathogen (online supplemental figure S19F). Conversely, mice fed HFB, LBCAA and KTD diets showed a slight reduction in Parasutterella abundance, with the KTD diet having the most pronounced effect (online supplemental figure S19F). Bacterial network analysis showed Parasutterella levels are influenced by Allobaculum in mice on KTD diet versus other diets, suggesting potential interplay between these genera in ketogenic dietary modulation (online supplemental figure S19G). PCR quantification confirmed that P. excrementihominis colonisation was enhanced in HC-fed and HFD-fed mice, while it was suppressed in KTD-fed mice (online supplemental figure S19H). Similar trends were observed in the aged mice subjected to dietary intervention (online supplemental figure S19I,J). The plasticity of P. excrementihominis abundance in response to dietary intervention offers prospective therapeutic avenues.

Discussion

Previous studies have identified several bacteria associated with UC, among which E. coli, Fusobacterium and Bacteroides fragilis are frequently implicated.40 41 P. excrementihominis has received growing attention due to its complex relationship with human health. UC is marked by lower microbial diversity and higher levels of Proteobacteria, which includes Parasutterella.40 This dysbiotic state is potentially linked to heightened intestinal barrier dysfunction and elevated inflammatory markers, suggesting a pathogenic role for Parasutterella in disease severity.19 This study outlines a pathogenic process where P. excrementihominis worsens experimental colitis and CAC by triggering NETosis via metabolites. P. excrementihominis uniquely produces 6-HHA and Suc to activate GPR84/SUCNR1, connecting bacterial metabolism to neutrophil overactivation.

Clinical studies have reported the correlation between Parasutterella and many metabolic and immune disorders, such as obesity, diabetes and IBD, particularly those with active diseases.8 This aligns with our finding that P. excrementihominis is abundantly present in the faeces of patients with active UC. P. excrementihominis is notable for its ability to modulate cholesterol metabolism and bile acid profiles, which might be critical for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and regulating inflammation.6 Colonisation of the murine model with Parasutterella correlates with shifts in ileal bile acid transporter gene expression (eg, Asbt and Ostα/β) and hepatic bile acid synthesis genes (eg, Cyp7a1), suggesting its role in cholesterol metabolism and bile acid homeostasis.6 42

Our analysis revealed that colonisation by P. excrementihominis led to decreased colonic expression of 3-hydroxymethyl-2-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (Hmgcs1) in GF mice. Hmgcs1 is an enzyme integral to the mevalonate pathway, a critical process for synthesising cholesterol and various isoprenoid compounds.43 Dysregulation of Hmgcs1 could potentially disrupt cholesterol metabolism and alter the bile acid pool, thereby exacerbating mucosal inflammation and epithelial damage in UC.44 On analysing the bacterial metabolites, we discovered that P. excrementihominis contributes to converting carbohydrates into organic acids. In particular, P.excrementihominis produces 6-HHA and Suc both in vitro (within culture media) and in vivo (within faecal samples).

Recent investigation highlight P. excrementihominis as a key driver in the development of chronic intestinal inflammation among patients with irritable bowel syndrome.45 However, its precise role in modulating immune response remains largely uncertain. Our study identified P. excrementihominis as a critical pathobiont that exacerbates UC and CAC by activating pathogenic NETosis activation through its metabolites (6-HHA and Suc). This process critically depends on PADI4, the enzyme responsible for histone citrullination during NETs formation. However, the role of PADI4 in gut inflammation is intricate and context-dependent.46 Recent evidence indicated that PADI4-mediated NETosis forms fibrin-based immunothrombosis, limiting rectal bleeding in DSS-induced colitis. In contrast, other evidence demonstrated that neutrophil-derived PADI4, delivered via extracellular vesicles, can citrullinate CKMT1 in intestinal epithelial cells, which disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism and barrier function.12 47 Thus, NETosis activation may have dual functions in colitis development.48 This discrepancy in NETosis outcomes may be influenced by the specific microbial triggers and the inflammatory milieu. For instance, metabolites (6-HHA/Suc) from pathobionts like P. excrementihominis may skew NETosis towards a more cytotoxic and sustained inflammatory response, mediated through the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome and release of DAMPs.

LPS-primed BM-neutrophils exposed to P. excrementihominis metabolites, 6-HHA and Suc, showed increased NLRP3 inflammasome activation and NETs formation. In murine UC and CAC models, P. excrementihominis or its culture medium promoted NETs and inflammatory cytokines in the colon. However, 6-HHA and Suc specifically enhanced LPS-primed NETosis without affecting PMA-induced NETs, suggesting different signalling pathways for LPS and PMA.49 50 In contrast, PMA directly activates protein kinase C, bypassing membrane receptors to drive robust ROS generation and NETosis through a TLR-independent mechanism.51 The selective enhancement of LPS-induced NETosis by 6-HHA and Suc suggests that these metabolites synergise with TLR4-mediated signalling to amplify downstream pro-NETotic events. Suc, a tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediate, can increase mitochondrial ROS production in LPS-primed neutrophils.52 6-HHA, a medium-chain fatty acid derivative, may enhance GPR84 signalling, amplifying TLR4 responses.53 This synergy may reduce the threshold for NLRP3 inflammasome activation and GSDMD cleavage, key for NETosis. This shows the context-dependent nature of microbial metabolite activity and the importance of the inflammatory microenvironment in shaping neutrophil responses.

Previous studies have indicated that P. excrementihominis was highly present in elderly individuals.39 Here, our FMT experiments demonstrated that age-associated increase in P. excrementihominis promotes chronic colitis and tumourigenesis in CAC mice. While ageing causes many immune and microbial alterations, the expansion of P. excrementihominis is a contributing factor in this dysbiotic landscape.54 55 The plasticity of P. excrementihominis abundance in response to dietary intervention offers prospective therapeutic avenues.8 The observation that a high-carbohydrate diet robustly promoted P. excrementihominis expansion aligns with its known capacity for enhanced carbohydrate metabolism and organic acid production.6 8 56 Conversely, the significant suppression of P. excrementihominis by KTD offers promising therapeutic insights. The KTD’s pronounced effect likely stems from reduced carbohydrates, starving the bacterium of its preferred energy. Our network analysis reveals a significant negative correlation between Parasutterella and Allobaculum within a ketogenic regime, pointing to an intricate ecological interplay potentially driven by niche competition. While our multivariate analysis linked high P. excrementihominis abundance to UC duration, further validation in larger cohorts is needed to generalise our findings. Besides, future research ought to incorporate longitudinal analyses of UC patient cohorts to investigate the temporal dynamics of P. excrementihominis abundance and its relationship with disease progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identifies that P. excrementihominis plays a pathogenic role in UC by sustaining chronic inflammation and barrier dysfunction. From a mechanistic standpoint, P. excrementihominis facilitates the generation of 6-HHA and Suc, which fosters the formation of NETs and the subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines by activating GPR84 and SUCNR1, respectively (online supplemental figure S20). Targeting P. excrementihominis via dietary intervention or inhibition of NETosis may offer a potential strategy to restore intestinal homeostasis in UC and CAC patients.
