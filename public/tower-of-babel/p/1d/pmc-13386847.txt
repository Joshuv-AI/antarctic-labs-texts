Current evidence does not demonstrate decreased ICU length of stay and delirium incidence associated with the use of volatile sedation in critically ill patients: re-analysis of data from a recent systematic review
Authors: Jiawen Deng
Published: 2026

The recent systematic review by Feng et al. [1] examining the use of inhaled volatile sedation in critically ill patients represents an important attempt to consolidate evidence in a rapidly evolving area of research. Based on 15 randomised controlled trials, the reviewers reported that volatile sedation significantly decreased awakening time, time to extubation, ICU length of stay, and incidence of delirium. Unfortunately, these conclusions rest on improper application of systematic review methodologies, inclusion of multiple ineligible studies, and major errors in the extracted data.

First, with respect to methodology, the reviewers claimed to have followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [2]. While the appended checklist was complete, multiple required items were absent from the manuscript itself. Of note, the analysis of the impact of missing data (i.e. publication bias) and assessment of the certainty of evidence (usually via the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation [GRADE] framework [3]) were not presented in the publication. Assessment of publication bias was particularly important in this case; as the reviewers themselves noted, the previous review on this topic by Jerath et al. demonstrated evidence of publication bias [4], a limitation that should have been explicitly re-examined in this updated analysis.

In addition to these reporting deficiencies, the reviewers’ choice of effect measures deviated from those specified in their PROSPERO registration and in the manuscript’s methods section. The PROSPERO record indicated that standardised mean differences would be used for continuous outcomes, yet simple mean differences were used in the actual analyses instead. For delirium, the manuscript reported the use of odds ratios, whereas the corresponding forest plot presented risk differences. These inconsistencies raise broader concerns regarding the reproducibility and validity of the reviewers’ statistical analyses.

There was also a lack of transparency regarding the handling of non-parametric data for continuous outcomes. While the reviewers claimed to use SPSS for imputing medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) into means and standard deviations (SDs) for analysis, the exact methodology was not described. It should be noted that SPSS has no native methods that would facilitate such an imputation. The imputed SDs presented by the authors do not conform with those generated by standard methods—such as division of the IQR by 1.35 [5]—and the imputed results appeared overly precise which could artificially narrow confidence intervals in the resulting meta-analyses. Given that a majority of the continuous data presented by the included studies were non-parametric, improper imputation of these data could create misleading significant findings where none exists.

Secondly, despite reporting that non-randomised studies and duplicate publications were excluded, three such studies were nonetheless incorporated. The study reported by Marcos-Vidal et al. in 2014 [6] used alternating allocation, where patients were assigned to each treatment arm in alternating patterns and not truly randomised. The trial reported by Sackey et al. in 2008 [7] was the same cohort described by Sackey et al. in 2004 [8]. Similarly, the cohort reported by Wąsowicz et al. in 2018 [9] was a subset of the same trial population described by Jerath et al. in 2015 [10], and both publications shared an identical ClinicalTrials.gov registration number. In the reviewers’ meta-analysis of time to extubation, both Wąsowicz et al. and Jerath et al. were included, which effectively double-counted patients and inflated the pooled precision.

Lastly, there was a large number of errors and inconsistencies in the extracted outcome data which renders the authors’ pooled treatment effects unreliable. These are summarised in Table 1. In light of these concerns, I re-analysed the reviewers’ data after correcting the extraction errors, excluding non-randomised and duplicate studies, and applying appropriate imputation methods for non-parametric data using methods described by Luo et al. [11] and Wan et al. [5]. In contrast to the results reported by the reviewers, the revised analyses demonstrated that the confidence intervals for both awakening time and time to extubation widened substantially, which appropriately reflects the imprecision inherent in the small underlying sample sizes. More importantly, with appropriate methods, the use of volatile sedation was no longer associated with significantly decreased ICU length of stay (in both cardiac and non-cardiac ICU patients) nor decreased incidence of delirium compared to IV sedation. These findings are important to interpret in context. Given that volatile agents are known to offset quickly with pulmonary elimination, decreased awakening and extubation times associated with their use are not surprising and are unlikely to meaningfully influence patient outcomes. By contrast, ICU length of stay and delirium carry direct implications for patient recovery and resource utilisation. Had these endpoints demonstrated consistent benefit, they would have provided a compelling rationale for wider adoption of volatile sedation in ICU settings. However, based on the results of the corrected meta-analyses, the currently available evidence does not demonstrate any substantive benefit in these outcomes with the use of volatile sedation.
