Burden and future trends of facial fractures in China: a global perspective with machine learning projections to 2036
Authors: Yuyang Wang, Xuanli Zhao, Lu Fan, Yueyuan Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFacial fractures are among the most common types of fractures, potentially resulting in severe facial deformities, functional impairments, and even life-threatening complications. This study aims to evaluate the burden of facial fractures in China within a global context and provide a scientific foundation for the development of relevant healthcare policies.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data, this study assessed China’s facial fracture burden, analyzing its distribution by age and sex. It examined 32-year trends and used time-series machine learning models to project the burden until 2036. The age-period-cohort (APC) model evaluated age, period, and birth cohort effects. Additionally, in a global context, the study explored socioeconomic influences through cross-country inequality and frontier analyses, identifying China’s relative global position.ResultsIn 2021, China reported 1,194,145 (95% CI: 909,426 to 1,571,247) new facial fracture cases and 291,005 (95% CI: 251,615 to 340,269) total cases. The burden was higher in males, with 20–24 and 85–94 age groups at highest risk. From 1990 to 2021, the burden continued to rise, and projections suggest this trend may persist until 2036. While disparities among socio-demographic index (SDI) groups are narrowing, a mismatch between economic growth and healthcare development in China remains.ConclusionsChina bears a higher burden of facial fractures compared to the global average, highlighting the need for targeted preventive and control measures for high-risk populations to effectively reduce the disease burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07076-x.

Introduction

Fractures represent a common type of injury, with severe cases potentially resulting in disability or death, thereby imposing a substantial burden on individual health, socioeconomic stability, and public health systems [1]. In 2019, an estimated 76.4 million individuals worldwide sustained fractures, with facial fractures accounting for nearly one-seventh of these cases [2]. Facial fractures involve multiple anatomical structures, including the mandible, nasal bone, and zygomatic bone [3]. Given the unique anatomical characteristics of the face, such fractures not only compromise facial aesthetics—often leading to significant psychological distress [4], but may also result in visual and auditory impairment or, in severe cases, craniocerebral injury, which can be life-threatening [5]. Furthermore, the high incidence of facial fractures, the complexity of surgical management, and the associated economic burden pose significant challenges to both healthcare systems and socioeconomic frameworks [6, 7].

Facial fractures are caused by a variety of factors, among which falls have been identified in multiple epidemiological studies as one of the most common and concerning causes, particularly among the elderly and children [8, 9]. Age-related physiological changes—such as osteoporosis, reduced muscle strength, and impaired balance—make older adults especially susceptible to falls that can result in severe facial trauma. With ongoing socioeconomic development and rapid population aging, the incidence of fall-related facial fractures has been rising [10]. By 2050, the global population is projected to reach 9.7 billion, with individuals aged 60 and above accounting for 22% of the total [11]. In China alone, the population aged 65 and over is expected to reach 400 million, including approximately 150 million people aged 80 or above [12]. Therefore, in-depth research on the disease burden of facial fractures and their association with population aging is crucial for informing effective prevention and control strategies.

Although previous studies have offered preliminary insights into the global burden of facial fractures, most have remained at a broad global level and lack in-depth, country-specific analysis—particularly for China, the world’s most populous nation. Moreover, much of the existing literature relies on data from GBD 2019 or earlier, limiting the ability to capture recent shifts in disease burden [8, 9, 13]. Current research also often falls short in conducting systematic evaluations of long-term temporal trends, decomposing demographic influences, and examining socioeconomic disparities and performance gaps between countries. Furthermore, predictive research on future disease burden remains scarce, especially in the context of China’s rapid population aging and transportation transformation. To address these gaps, the present study utilizes data from the GBD Study 2021 to conduct a comprehensive assessment of the facial fracture burden in China. Age- and sex-specific patterns are analyzed, and Joinpoint regression is applied to examine temporal trends from 1990 to 2021. An age-period-cohort (APC) model is used to disentangle the effects of age, period, and birth cohort. To contextualize China’s burden within a global framework, cross-country inequality and frontier analyses are performed to evaluate the influence of socioeconomic factors and benchmark national performance. Lastly, a suite of time-series machine learning models is employed to forecast trends in incidence and prevalence through 2036.

In this study, analyzing the disease burden of facial fractures in China within a global context carries important implications across several dimensions. First, international comparisons help position China within the global landscape, indicating whether the country belongs to a high-burden or low-burden category in terms of disease prevention and control. Second, a global framework enables the identification of potential shortcomings in China’s health sector amid its rapid economic growth, highlighting possible mismatches between socioeconomic development and public health advancement. Third, benchmarking against countries or regions with outstanding performance provides a valuable reference for China in formulating evidence-based policies and adopting effective intervention strategies. Finally, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database employed in this study offers high cross-national comparability, making it particularly well-suited for research conducted within a global comparative framework.

Methods

Data sources and relevant parameter

The GBD 2021, initiated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington, encompasses data from 204 countries and territories and includes assessments of 371 diseases and 88 risk factors and is openly accessible through the GBD Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/) [14]. As the most comprehensive dataset available for evaluating the global burden of disease [15], the GBD study employs years lived with disability (YLDs) to quantify the number of years lost to disease or disability, thereby capturing the nonfatal burden of disease [16]. Additionally, the sociodemographic index (SDI)—a composite metric incorporating per capita income, mean years of education among individuals aged 15 years and older, and the fertility rate among women under 25 years—serves as an indicator of a country’s or region’s socioeconomic development level [17].

In this study, data specific to facial fractures (as classified under the “Injury” category) in China were extracted from the GBD 2021 database. The selection was based on the following criteria: Location: China; Cause: facial fracture; Metrics: incidence, prevalence, and YLDs; Year range: 1990 to 2021; Sex and age: sex was categorized as male, female, and both, age was divided into 19 groups in 5-year increments (5–9 years, 10–14 years, …, 90–94 years, and 95+); Measure type: all-age numbers and age-standardized rates. GBD data are openly accessible and have undergone review by the University of Washington’s Institutional Review Board, which has granted a waiver for informed consent [18].

Descriptive analysis

To comprehensively assess the burden of facial fractures in China and globally from 1990 to 2021, descriptive analyses stratified by age and sex were performed to visually depict incidence, prevalence, YLDs, and their corresponding age-standardized rates.These values were directly obtained from the GBD 2021 database and reported with 95% Uncertainty Intervals (UI), which were generated for every metric using the 25th and 975th ordered 1000 draw values of the posterior distribution.

Trend analysis

In this study, a Joinpoint regression model was employed to analyze the temporal patterns and dynamic evolution of facial fractures in China and globally. The model applies the least-squares method to estimate disease trends by segmenting the time series into distinct intervals and fitting separate regression models within each segment [19]. This approach effectively addresses the limitations of traditional linear trend analyses, enhancing the precision of trend estimation. Analyses were conducted using Joinpoint software (version 4.9.1.0; National Cancer Institute, Rockville, MD, USA), and both the annual percentage change (APC) and the average annual percentage change (AAPC) were calculated to assess overall trends [20]. An APC or AAPC greater than 0 indicates an increasing trend, whereas values less than 0 signify a decreasing trend.

Age-period-cohort analysis

In this study, the age-period-cohort (APC) model was applied to evaluate the effects of age, period, and birth cohort on the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of facial fractures. The APC model, based on a Poisson distribution, integrates these three factors simultaneously [21], thereby mitigating the issue of non-identifiability that may arise when analyzed separately [22]. Specifically, the age effect reflects the risk of morbidity across different age groups, the period effect captures the influence of temporal shifts in the social environment on the overall population, and the cohort effect assesses variations in disease burden across birth cohorts [23]. For this analysis, data from 1992 to 2021 were partitioned into consecutive 5-year intervals, and age was categorized into 19 groups in 5-year increments (5–9 years, 10–14 years, …, 90–94 years, and 95+). Based on the 5-year groupings of age and period, the corresponding birth cohorts were automatically generated by the APC model as consecutive 5-year intervals, ranging from 1892 to 2012 (e.g., 1892–1897, 1897–1902,., 2007–2012). Analyses were conducted using a web-based tool (https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/) [24].

Cross-country inequality analysis

To further explore the impact of socioeconomic factors on the burden of facial fractures, a health inequality analysis was conducted. This analysis quantified both absolute and relative disparities in disease burden across countries and regions using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index (CI) [25]. The SII is calculated by performing a weighted linear regression of disease indicators against ranked Socio-demographic Index (SDI) values, with the resulting slope representing the extent of absolute inequality [26]. The CI is derived from the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of equality, reflecting the degree to which the disease burden is concentrated among different socioeconomic groups. Positive CI values indicate that the burden is more concentrated in countries with higher socioeconomic status, while negative values indicate a greater burden in lower-income countries. The larger the absolute value of the index, the greater the inequality in the distribution of disease burden across countries; conversely, smaller absolute values suggest a more equitable distribution and better overall health equity [27].

The Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CI) were calculated using R (version 4.3.2), with the assistance of several packages including car, MASS, mgcv, splines, broom, and ggplot2 for regression modeling, Lorenz curve fitting, and result visualization.

Frontier analysis

To evaluate global disparities in the burden of facial fractures and to determine China’s relative position worldwide, a frontier analysis was conducted.This method identifies the best-performing countries and constructs nonlinear frontiers to benchmark the performance of other nations [28]. In frontier analysis, the distance between a country’s health indicator and the frontier boundary—termed the efficiency gap—quantifies the discrepancy between that country and the benchmark nation [29]. This approach provides a visual representation of each country’s relative strengths and areas for improvement in controlling the disease burden, offering data-driven insights to inform policy development. Frontier analysis was conducted using R (version 4.3.2), with the assistance of the purrr, tidyr, ggplot2, and ggrepel packages for data manipulation and visualization.

Machine learning in predictive analysis

To further explore the future trends in the burden of facial fractures in China, a series of time-series machine learning models were employed to predict the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) from 2022 to 2036.

First, the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model, a classical time-series forecasting method, integrates autoregressive (AR), moving average (MA), and differencing (d) components to effectively smooth data and enhance forecasting accuracy [30]. Second, the multiple adaptive regression spline (MARS) method captures complex patterns by constructing a segmented linear regression model, allowing for the identification of nonlinear relationships and interaction effects among input variables [31]. Additionally, the Random Forest, an ensemble learning method that enhances accuracy and generalizability by building multiple decision trees and aggregating their outputs was applied [32]. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) further refines prediction accuracy through gradient optimization and robust regularization techniques, effectively mitigating overfitting [33]. Support Vector Machines (SVM) were also utilized due to their strength in handling high-dimensional data and nonlinear relationships by maximizing margins between data classes [34]. Finally, Prophet were employed, a time-series forecasting model developed by Facebook, which is particularly effective in capturing complex temporal patterns and providing accurate long-term projections [35].

To ensure the reliability of the predictive models, their performance was evaluated using key metrics, including root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and the coefficient of determination (R²), which collectively assess model fit and predictive accuracy, providing strong quantitative validation for the results. Among these metrics, an R²value closer to 1 indicates a better fit of the model to the observed data, while lower RMSE and MAPE values reflect smaller prediction errors. Therefore, this study identifies the model with the highest R²and the lowest RMSE and MAPE as the best-performing predictive model based on a comprehensive evaluation of these three indicators. All machine learning models—including ARIMA, MARS, Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, and Prophet—were implemented in R (version 4.3.2) using the following packages: forecast, ggplot2, ggpubr, Metrics, earth, prophet, randomForest, e1071, and xgboost.

All statistical analyses were conducted using standard thresholds, with a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive analysis

In 2021, the estimated number of new facial fracture cases in China was 1,194,145 (95% UI: 909,426 to 1,571,247), corresponding to an ASIR of 90.5 per 100,000 (95% UI: 69.81 to 118.79). The total prevalence was 291,005 cases (95% UI: 251,615 to 340,269), with an ASPR of 18.15 per 100,000 (95% UI: 15.30–21.82). The age-standardized YLDs rate was 1.18 per 100,000 (95% UI: 0.72 to 1.75) (Table 1). Notably, ASIR, ASPR, and age-standardized YLD rates in China were slightly lower than global estimates.

Age and sex patterns of facial fractures in China

In 2021, the highest incidence of facial fractures in China was observed in the 30–34-year age group, whereas the ASIR peaked in the 20–24 and 85–94-year age groups (Fig. 1A). Globally, both prevalence and ASIR were highest in the 15–24-year age group (Supplementary Fig. 1). Regarding prevalence, the highest number of cases in China occurred in the 55–59-year age group, while the ASPR exhibited a steady increase with advancing age (Fig. 1B). Notably, compared to 1990, the burden of facial fractures has declined in younger age groups but has risen substantially among middle-aged and older populations, suggesting a shifting disease burden disproportionately affecting these age groups. In the gender subgroup analysis, the burden of facial fractures was significantly higher in men than in women (Fig. 1C and D).

The effects of age, period and cohort on incidence of facial fracture

As shown in Fig. 3, the APC analysis demonstrated the influence of age, period, and birth cohort effects on the incidence of facial fractures. After adjusting for period and birth cohort effects, age had a significant impact on facial fracture incidence (P = 0.00), with the highest risk observed among children (0–5 years) and the elderly (80 + years) (Fig. 3A and Table S7). The period effect analysis revealed a declining relative risk of facial fractures in China from 1990 to 2010, reaching its lowest point in 2010, followed by a gradual increase, peaking in 2021 (Fig. 3B and Table S7). The birth cohort effect analysis indicated that individuals born in 1990 exhibited a significantly higher relative risk compared to all other cohorts, while no increasing trend was observed in subsequent birth cohorts (Fig. 3C and Table S7).

Globally, APC analysis identified children (0–5 years) and young adults (20–24 years) as high-risk groups (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Table S8). The period effect analysis showed a continuous decline in relative risk from 1990 to 2021, whereas the birth cohort effect analysis indicated a gradual decrease in relative risk across successive birth cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Table S8).

Cross-country inequality analysis

The health inequality analysis demonstrated a decline in absolute inequality in the burden of facial fractures over time (Figs. 4A, B, and C). Specifically, the SII for the ASIR decreased from 105.04 in 1990 to −91.40 in 2021, the SII for the ASPR declined from 19.90 in 1990 to 8.98 in 2021, and the SII for age-standardized YLDs rates decreased from 1.30 in 1990 to −1.34 in 2021. These trends suggest a narrowing disparity in the disease burden across SDI groups.

Although countries with low SDI continue to bear a disproportionately higher burden of facial fractures, relative inequality has declined since 1990. The CI value decreased from 0.21 to 0.04, indicating a trend toward greater equity in the distribution of disease burden across socioeconomic groups (Figs. 4D, E, and F).

Frontier analysis

The frontier analysis illustrates trends in the SDI-related burden of facial fractures from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries and regions (Fig. 5 and Table S10-12). In 2021, the five high-SDI countries (SDI > 0.85) that were closest to the frontier level—indicating optimal performance in reducing facial fracture burden—were Andorra, Austria, Belgium, Canada, and Denmark.

Over the past three decades, China’s SDI has shown a consistent upward trajectory, reflecting continuous socioeconomic advancement. However, despite this progress, China continues to experience a high burden of facial fractures, with a persistent gap compared to leading countries at the frontier. This disparity suggests that while China has made substantial economic strides, improvements in its public health system have not kept pace, highlighting a potential misalignment between economic development and healthcare system strengthening.

Discussion

In this study, data from the GBD 2021 were utilized to conduct a comprehensive assessment of epidemiologic trends in facial fractures in China over the past 32 years, highlighting variations in disease burden across age and sex. The APC model was applied to examine the influence of age, period, and birth cohort effects on facial fracture incidence. Additionally, cross-country inequality analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of socioeconomic factors within a global context. Frontier analysis was used to establish theoretical benchmarks for disease control by identifying countries that have achieved optimal outcomes and clarifying China’s relative standing in the global landscape. Finally, multiple time-series machine learning models were employed to project the trajectory of facial fracture burden in China through 2036. These findings provide a robust scientific foundation for the development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies and offer valuable insights for policymakers and public health professionals.

The study found that the ASIR of facial fractures in China is lower than the global average, and this discrepancy may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, barriers to medical consultation still exist in some regions of China, particularly in primary healthcare settings. Patients with mild symptoms may not seek timely medical attention or may remain undiagnosed and unreported, potentially leading to an underestimation of the actual disease burden in the statistical data [36]. Secondly, in recent years, China has made continuous progress in traffic safety, occupational protection, and urban infrastructure. The implementation of stricter traffic regulations, enhanced workplace safety measures, and improved social governance may have contributed to a decline in facial fractures and other injury-related conditions [37]. Finally, the global ASIR is influenced by countries or regions with a high incidence of facial fractures—such as those with high traffic density, frequent interpersonal violence, or ongoing armed conflict—which may elevate the global average and widen the observed gap between China and the rest of the world [38].

Significant age-related differences were observed in the distribution of facial fractures. In China, the peak ASIR for facial fractures was concentrated in the 20–24 and 85–94-year age groups, whereas globally, the peak ASIR primarily occurred in the 15–24-year age group. Several factors may contribute to this pattern. Globally, individuals aged 15–24 years are in adolescence and early adulthood, a period associated with higher participation in contact sports, increased engagement in risk-prone behaviors such as drunk driving and reckless driving, and a greater likelihood of interpersonal violence [39, 40]. In contrast, in China, the secondary ASIR peak observed in the 85–94-year age group is likely driven by population aging and an elevated risk of falls among the elderly. Falls have been identified as the leading cause of facial fractures in older adults [41], with age-related physiological changes—such as osteoporosis, muscle weakness, and impaired balance—further compounding fall risk and subsequent fractures [42]. China’s aging population has grown substantially in recent years. According to the 2021 Seventh National Census, China’s total population has surpassed 1.4 billion, with a rising proportion of elderly individuals [43]. This demographic shift partly explains the notable increase in facial fracture burden among older adults compared with 1990. Conversely, a decline in facial fracture burden among younger age groups relative to 1990 may reflect advancements in transportation safety regulations, enhanced workplace safety measures, and greater public awareness of injury prevention. For example, the enforcement of stricter road traffic laws and the widespread promotion of mandatory helmet and seatbelt use have contributed to reduced injury rates [44].

The burden of facial fractures exhibits a pronounced sex disparity, with significantly higher incidence rates in men compared to women. Several factors may contribute to this difference. First, occupational exposure plays a critical role. Men are more frequently employed in high-risk industries such as construction, manufacturing, and mining, where the likelihood of mechanical injuries and collisions is substantially higher—key mechanisms underlying facial fractures [45]. Additionally, alcohol consumption patterns may further contribute to this disparity. Men generally have higher rates of alcohol abuse than women, and excessive alcohol intake impairs self-control and judgment, increasing the risk of interpersonal conflicts, traffic accidents, and other hazardous behaviors that predispose individuals to facial trauma [46, 47].

Trend analysis revealed that, in contrast to the global decline, the ASIR, ASPR, and age-standardized YLDs rate for facial fractures have been increasing in China in recent years. Several factors may contribute to this trend. First, advancements in diagnostic techniques likely play a significant role. The widespread adoption of CT scanning and 3D reconstruction technologies has markedly improved the detection rate of facial fractures. Compared with conventional X-rays, these imaging modalities offer superior resolution and greater accuracy in identifying subtle and complex fractures, thereby reducing the likelihood of underdiagnosis [48]. Beyond diagnostic improvements, shifts in the epidemiology of facial fractures are also noteworthy. China’s ongoing population growth and aging have expanded the at-risk population. Additionally, rapid economic development and urbanization have led to a surge in traffic volume, particularly with the widespread use of electric bicycles and motorcycles. Users of these vehicles face a heightened risk of facial injuries due to inadequate protective measures in traffic accidents [49]. Furthermore, China’s rapidly expanding courier and delivery industries have introduced a new occupational risk factor. The growing number of delivery workers and couriers navigating urban roads for extended periods places them at an increased risk of traffic accidents, which frequently result in facial trauma [50].

After adjusting for age, period, and birth cohort effects using the APC model, the findings further reinforced the conclusions of the previous analysis. Notably, a key contribution of the APC analysis was the identification of birth cohort effects, revealing that individuals born in 1990 had the highest risk of facial fractures. This phenomenon may be closely related to the socioeconomic environment and the lack of safety measures at that time.

Cross-country inequality analyses indicate that the gap in facial fracture burden among different SDI groups has been gradually narrowing, which may be attributed to several factors. In low-SDI countries, limited healthcare resources and poor access to emergency care contribute to higher rates of missed diagnoses, leading to an underestimation of the true disease burden [9]. In contrast, high-SDI countries benefit from well-developed healthcare systems and advanced diagnostic technologies, which enhance the detection rate of facial fractures and may contribute to a statistically higher reported burden of disease. Additionally, high-SDI countries typically have more complex transportation networks and greater motor vehicle densities, increasing the incidence of traffic accidents—a major risk factor for facial fractures [51]. Furthermore, population aging has become an increasingly significant issue in many developed nations. As the proportion of elderly individuals rises, the number of fall-related facial fracture cases correspondingly increases, further exacerbating the overall burden of disease in these countries.

Meanwhile, findings from the frontier analysis indicate that while China’s SDI has steadily increased over the past 32 years, the burden of facial fractures remains higher than that of benchmark countries. This disparity suggests a potential misalignment between economic development and healthcare system improvements, highlighting the need for more targeted public health interventions and healthcare infrastructure enhancements to effectively address the growing burden of facial fractures.

Projections indicate that from 2022 to 2036, the ASIR and ASPR of facial fractures in China will continue to rise. This trend underscores the urgent need to strengthen prevention and control strategies to mitigate the growing burden of facial fractures. First, fall prevention measures for the elderly should be optimized. Age-appropriate environmental modifications, routine osteoporosis screening, and rehabilitation programs can help reduce the risk of fall-related facial injuries. Second, transportation safety management must be reinforced, particularly for electric vehicle and motorcycle users. Increasing the adoption of safety helmets and implementing stricter enforcement of traffic regulations can significantly lower the incidence of facial fractures caused by road accidents. Additionally, occupational safety protections should be enhanced, particularly in high-risk industries such as construction, manufacturing, and mining, to reduce work-related injuries. Finally, optimizing the allocation of medical resources is essential. Strengthening trauma care capacity, improving access to high-quality treatment, and ensuring equitable development of regional healthcare systems will enable timely intervention and rehabilitation services, ultimately minimizing the long-term health consequences of facial fractures.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the GBD data inherently come with limitations, including potential inconsistencies due to differences in data collection methods and quality across multiple countries. Additionally, since the data are based on estimates and model projections, there is an element of uncertainty. Secondly, the assumptions underlying the models used in this study may not fully capture the complex interactions of socioeconomic and policy changes, which could affect the accuracy of the results to some extent. Finally, due to limitations in data availability, this study could not further explore regional heterogeneity within China. Future studies should integrate regional-level data and population characteristics to provide a more precise assessment of disease burden and to develop targeted prevention and control strategies tailored to specific subpopulations.

Conclusion

This study indicates that the burden of facial fractures in China has been rising over the past 32 years, and predictive analysis suggests that this trend may persist until 2036. Compared with global patterns, the high-prevalence age group in China includes not only individuals aged 20–24 years but also a significant concentration among those aged 85–94 years, underscoring the growing impact of an aging population. Cross-country inequality analysis reveals a gradual narrowing of disparities in the burden of facial fractures among different SDI groups. However, frontier analysis suggests a potential incongruity between China’s economic development and the advancement of its healthcare system. Moving forward, efforts should focus on strengthening fall prevention measures, enhancing transportation safety management, and ensuring the equitable allocation of medical resources, particularly for the elderly.
