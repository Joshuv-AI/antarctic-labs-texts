MdRLKT1–MdRAX2–MdMKS1 Module Positively Regulating Resistance to Cytospora mali in Apple
Authors: Yanan Tang, Guangyao Li, Yang Li, Yuzhu Wang, Hao Feng, Lili Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTValsa canker (caused by Cytospora mali = Valsa mali. C. mali ) is one of the most destructive diseases affecting apple cultivation. The scarcity of natural germplasm resources with high resistance and immunity underscores the importance of exploring plant immune regulation factors of disease‐resistant breeding. Protein post‐translational modifications, particularly phosphorylation, are critical regulatory mechanisms in plant immunity. This study investigates how the apple receptor‐like kinase MdRLKT1 modulates resistance to Valsa canker through the phosphorylation of the transcription factor MdRAX2. We found that MdRLKT1‐interference (RNAi) transgenic lines exhibit increased susceptibility to C. mali infection compared to wild‐type controls, indicating that MdRLKT1 positively regulates apple immune responses. Notably, MdRLKT1 interacts with the MYB transcription factor MdRAX2, facilitating its translocation into the nucleus. In vitro phosphorylation assays identified serine 147 (Ser147) as the phosphorylation site of MdRAX2 by MdRLKT1. Mutant MdRAX2S147A, with this phosphorylation site inactivated, demonstrated reduced resistance to C. mali . Further analysis revealed that MdRAX2 binds to the promoter region of MdMKS1, transcriptionally repressingits expression, whereas MdRAX2S147A failed to regulate MdMKS1 transcriptionally. Overexpression of MdMKS1 in apple resulted in reduced resistance to C. mali , suggesting that MdMKS1 negatively regulates apple immunity. These findings establish that the MdRLKT1‐MdRAX2‐MdMKS1 module plays a positive regulatory role in enhancing apple resistance to C. mali . In conclusion, MdRLKT1 activates the transcriptional repressor function of MdRAX2 through phosphorylation, thereby alleviating the negative regulatory effect of MdMKS1 on disease resistance and ultimately boosting the defensive capabilities of apple against pathogens.Keywords: apple Valsa canker, phosphorylation, receptor‐like kinase, transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Plants are exposed to invading pathogens with different lifestyles, including bacteria, fungi and oomycetes (Trivedi et al. 2020). To defend against pathogen infection, plants have developed a two‐layered immune system. The first layer is initiated by the recognition of pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or plant‐derived endogenous danger‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and phytocytokines by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), resulting in pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) (Irieda et al. 2019; Saijo et al. 2018). Plants, in turn, have evolved a second layer of defence, called effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), which is mediated by nucleotide‐binding domain leucine‐rich repeat proteins (NBS‐LRRs or NLRs) that recognise pathogen effectors or effector‐triggered perturbations (Boller and Felix 2009; Lolle et al. 2020). PTI is generally considered to be a relatively weak immune response that confers resistance to broad‐spectrum pathogens (Lolle et al. 2020), while ETI is R gene‐mediated and typically confers race‐specific disease resistance (Yuan et al. 2021). Although PTI and ETI employ different mechanisms for receptor activation and early signalling, they share many components and induce a series of overlapping immune responses, including cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+]cyt) influx, reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst, activation of mitogen‐activated protein kinases, transcriptional reprogramming and defence hormone production (Kadota et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2024).

Cell surface receptors can be broadly classified into two categories: transmembrane receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) and transmembrane receptor‐like proteins (RLPs). RLKs and RLPs contain a variable extracellular domain (ECD) at the N‐terminus, which is responsible for ligand recognition, and either a single‐pass transmembrane domain that anchors the protein in the plasma membrane or, in some RLPs, a conjugated lipid anchor (Huang and Joosten 2025). The defining difference between RLKs and RLPs is the presence of an intracellular kinase domain in the C‐terminal region of RLKs. The ECDs of RLKs/RLPs comprise several different classes, and the activation and downstream signalling pathways of RLKs/RLPs have been well characterised (DeFalco and Zipfel 2021). A well‐known RLK is the Arabidopsis receptor kinase FLS2, which recognises a conserved 22‐amino acid N‐terminal sequence of the bacterial flagellin protein (flg22) (Zhao et al. 2024). The extracellular leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) domain perceives flg22 and rapidly recruits another LRR receptor‐like kinase called BAK1, which acts as a coreceptor of flg22, resulting in the formation of a signalling complex that triggers plant defence responses and lead to the activation of downstream intracellular signalling (Li et al. 2014; Huang and Joosten 2025). Another important RLK is the lysine motif containing receptor‐like kinase (LysM‐RLK) that recognises fungal chitin (Gong et al. 2020). Chitin induces oligomerization of Arabidopsis chitin receptor AtLYK5 and coreceptor AtCERK1 and promotes the phosphorylation and activation of AtCERK1, which in turn phosphorylates and activates receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinase (RLCK) VII members, such as BIK1 and PBL19/27. BIK1 subsequently phosphorylates and activates CNGC2/4 and RbohD to regulate chitin‐induced Ca2+ influx and reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst (Cao et al. 2014; Miya et al. 2007; Wan et al. 2008). Thereby triggering a plant immune response.

Post‐translational modifications are crucial for the physiological roles of transcription factors in plants, and phosphorylation is one of the most important regulatory mechanisms. Previous studies have shown that regulating transcriptions factor through phosphorylation often enhances plant immunity. For instance, phosphorylation of a transcriptional repressor, AL7, enhances its ubiquitination by UBR7, thereby reducing AL7 stability and alleviating AL7‐induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, which contributes to the dynamic regulation of ROS homeostasis during plant immune responses (Zhang et al. 2025). OsMAPK3 phosphorylates OsNAC29 at Thr304 to prevent its proteasome‐mediated degradation and promote its function against rice blast fungus (Lu et al. 2024). Phosphorylation of Arabidopsis ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR 6 (ERF6) by MPK3/MPK6 results in the accumulation of ERF6 protein in vivo, which positively regulates defence gene expression and plant resistance to the necrotrophic fungal pathogen Botrytis cinerea (Meng et al. 2013). The susceptibility gene TaPsIPK1 phosphorylates the transcription factor TaCBF1d to mediate gene regulation, thereby modulating the resistance of wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) to Puccinia striiformis f. sp. Tritici (Pst) (Wang et al. 2022). The phosphorylation of transcription factors (TFs) represents a pivotal post‐translational modification that bridges upstream immune signal perception and downstream defence gene activation, and the major kinases involved include mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs), RLCKs, and CPKs (Li et al. 2016). For instance, OsWRKY31 is phosphorylated by OsMPK3, OsMPK4 and OsMPK6. Phosphomimetic OsWRKY31 exhibits elevated DNA‐binding activity and confers enhanced rice resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae (Wang et al. 2023). WRKY33 functions as a convergent substrate of CPK5/CPK6 and MPK3/MPK6, which cooperatively regulate camalexin biosynthesis via the differential phospho‐regulation of WRKY33 activity (Zhou et al. 2020). MAPKs can phosphorylate some WRKYs, which control the expression of RBOHs encoding NADPH oxidases for ROS production. The receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinase (RLCK) BIK1 directly phosphorylates the plasma membrane‐resident NADPH oxidase RBOHD, which together with calcium signalling‐mediated RBOHD regulation results in the transient production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Macho and Zipfel 2014).

Apple tree Valsa canker, primarily caused by the pathogenic fungus Cytospora mali ( C. mali ), is one of the most destructive branch diseases affecting apple production worldwide (Abe et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2014; Yin et al. 2015). The pathogen typically invades through wounds, leading to bark cankers, tissue necrosis, and in severe cases, branch dieback or even the death of entire trees, causing devastating impacts on orchards (Ke et al. 2013). Consequently, apple Valsa canker poses a serious threat to the sustainable development of the apple industry; however, the molecular mechanisms of plant immunity, particularly the role of protein phosphorylation in signal transduction, require further elucidation. Notably, a previous study demonstrated that the C. mali milRNA Vm‐milR1 suppresses host immunity and promotes fungal infection by silencing the apple receptor‐like kinase gene MdRLKT1(Xu et al. 2022), suggesting that MdRLKT1 may function as a key regulator in apple defence responses. Nevertheless, how MdRLKT1 transduces immune signals and whether it regulates downstream targets through phosphorylation remain unknown. In this study, we investigated the biological function of the apple receptor‐like kinase MdRLKT1 and its associated phosphorylation cascade in resistance against C. mali . The results demonstrate that MdRLKT1 enhances the transcriptional repression activity of the transcription factor MdRAX2 by specifically phosphorylating its serine 147 residue, thereby suppressing the expression of the negative regulator MdMKS1 and ultimately positively regulating apple resistance to C. mali . Our findings reveal a previously uncharacterized MdRLKT1–MdRAX2–MdMKS1 signalling module in the apple immune response but also provide new theoretical insights into the molecular mechanisms by which plants precisely regulate immune responses through phosphorylation cascades.

Results

The Receptor‐Like Kinase MdRLKT1 Plays a Positive Role in Apples Responding to Resistance Cytospora mali

The molecular mechanism by which MdRLKT1 regulates apple immunity is still unknown. To explore the function of MdRLKT1 in disease resistance, two stable transgenic apple plants with suppressed expression of MdRLKT1 were created, designated Ri1 and Ri2 (Figure S1). qPCR analyses showed that the relative expression levels of MdRLKT1 were significantly decreased in Ri plants compared with wild type (WT; Figure 1a), Importantly, silencing of MdRLKT1 significantly decreased the resistance of apple leaves to C. mali (Figure 1b,c). Subsequently, the accumulation of ROS and callose was examined in apple leaves, and the results showed that their levels were lower in Ri plants than in the control (Figure 1d,e). Moreover, silencing of MdRLKT1 significantly decreased the transcription levels of the apple defence‐related genes, MdPR1, MdPR2, MdPR4, MdPR5 and the ROS production‐related gene, MdRBOHD (Figure 1f). Taken together, these results indicate that MdRLKT1 plays a positive role in the host resistance to C. mali by activating the host defence responses.

MdRLKT1 Interacts With MdRAX2

To clarify the role of MdRLKT1 in plant immunity, the coding sequence of MdRLKT1 was used as bait to screen an apple cDNA library using yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assays; interacting proteins were identified. As a result, 67 interacting molecules were identified (Table S2). Nucleotide sequence analysis further revealed one cDNA fragment encoding the transcription factors MdRAX2. MdRAX2, a member of the MYB transcription factors (TF) family, had transcriptional activation activity (Figure S3a). As shown in Figure 2a, the yeast cells transformed with BD‐MdRLKT1 and AD‐MdRAX2 constructs turned blue on SD/‐Trp‐Leu‐His‐Ade medium containing X‐α‐gal, indicating that MdRLKT1 interacted with MdRAX2 in yeast cells. Moreover, co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays showed that MdRLKT1CD, the intracellular domain of MdRLKT1, was co‐immunoprecipitated with MdRAX2 only when GFP‐MdRAX2 was coexpressed with mCherry‐MdRLKT1 CD (Figure 2b). In luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays, the fluorescence signals were observed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves coinfiltrated with nLUC‐MdRLKT1 and cLUC‐MdRAX2 (Figure 2c). Furthermore, when YNE‐MdRLKT1 and YCE‐MdRAX2 were cotransfected into N. benthamiana leaves, strong fluorescence signals were observed in the cell membrane, indicating that MdRLKT1 co‐localised with MdRAX2 (Figure 2d). The interaction between the MdRLKT1 intracellular domain and MdRAX2 was tested by microscale thermophoresis (MST). A clear increase in normalised fluorescence was detected for MdRLKT1 in the presence of MdRAX2 (Figure 2e). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the intracellular domain of MdRLKT1 interacts with MdRAX2 in planta.

Bioinformatic analysis revealed that MdRAX2 contains two SANT domains (Figure S2a), and belongs to the MYB class of transcription factors. Transcription factors typically exert their regulatory functions in the nucleus; however, MdRLKT1 and MdRAX2 interact at the plasma membrane. To determine the subcellular localization of MdRAX2, MdRAX2‐GFP was transiently expressed in N. benthamiana via Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation. As shown in Figure 2f–h, confocal microscopy analysis revealed that MdRAX2‐GFP exhibits a distinct dual localization pattern. Co‐localization analysis demonstrated that MdRAX2‐GFP signals overlapped with those of the nuclear marker DAPI, confirming its presence in the nucleus. Furthermore, MdRAX2‐GFP also co‐localised with the plasma membrane marker TaW16‐mCherry. Notably, in plasmolyzed cells, both the MdRAX2‐GFP and TaW16‐mCherry signals retracted synchronously from the cell wall and converged towards the cytoplasm, providing strong evidence for its plasma membrane association. Collectively, these results indicate that MdRAX2 is a dual‐localised protein, residing in both the nucleus and the plasma membrane. To determine the effect of MdRLKT1 on MdRAX2 subcellular localization, a co‐expression assay was performed. When co‐expressed with an mCherry control vector, MdRAX2‐GFP was distributed in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. Strikingly, upon co‐expression with MdRLKT1‐mCherry, the MdRAX2‐GFP signal exhibited a pronounced accumulation in the nucleus. This redistribution suggests that the interaction with MdRLKT1 promotes the nuclear import of MdRAX2 (Figure 2i,j).

MdRAX2 Positively Regulates Modulates Resistance to Cytospora mali

Transient expression assays were subsequently performed. Transgenic leaves of GL‐3 tissue‐cultured seedlings expressing either MdRAX2 or an empty vector (EV) were generated and inoculated with C. mali for 32 h (Figure 3a). Results confirmed that transient overexpression of MdRAX2 in apple leaves exhibited enhanced resistance to C. mali , with lesion areas decreasing by 10.6% compared with the EV control (Figure 3b,c). Furthermore, apple leaves overexpressing MdRAX2 showed enhanced ROS accumulation and callose deposition following inoculation with the wild‐type C. mali (Figure 3d,e). Conversely, lesion areas in MdRAX2‐RNAi apple leaves were 41.8% larger than those in EV‐treated leaves (Figure 3f–h). Transient silencing of MdRAX2 reduced ROS accumulation and callose deposition (Figure 3i,j). Taken together, these results indicate that MdRAX2 positively modulates apple resistance to C. mali .

To characterise the genetic interaction between MdRLKT1 with MdRAX2, Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation was performed in GL‐3 apple leaves with treatments including MdRLKT1 overexpression alone or MdRLKT1 overexpression combined with MdRAX2 silencing. After inoculation with C. mali , compared with the empty vector overexpression control, MdRLKT1 overexpression significantly enhanced apple leaves resistance to C. mali . However, the concurrent silencing of MdRAX2 in the MdRLKT1‐overexpressing background completely abolished the resistance phenotype induced by MdRLKT1 overexpression, indicating that MdRAX2 is required for MdRLKT1‐mediated resistance to C. mali (Figure S4a,b).

MdRLKT1 Phosphorylates Ser‐147 of MdRAX2 In Vitro

To investigate whether MdRAX2 is a phosphorylation substrate of MdRLKT1, in vitro kinase assays were performed following the confirmation of their direct interaction. When GST‐MdRLKT1CD proteins were incubated with GST‐MdRAX2‐His, a clear phosphorylation band corresponding to the molecular weight of GST‐MdRAX2‐His proteins was observed. In contrast, no phosphorylation signal was detected when GST–MdRAX2–His was incubated alone, when ATP was omitted from the reaction system, or when the kinase‐dead mutant MdRLKT1KM (MdRLKT1D432A mutant) was used instead of wild‐type MdRLKT1CD, indicating that MdRLKT1 kinase activity is required for the phosphorylation of MdRAX2 (Figure 4a). Treatment with λ‐phosphatase abolished the phosphorylation band of GST‐MdRAX2‐His (Figure 4b), demonstrating that MdRLKT1 phosphorylates MdRAX2 in vitro. To determine which amino acid residues of MdRAX2 were phosphorylated by MdRLKT1, LC–MS/MS analysis was performed, which revealed several potential phosphorylation sites (Figure S5). Further examination of these sites by in vitro kinase assays revealed that only mutation of serine (Ser, S) 147 to alanine (A) greatly abolished in vitro phosphorylation of MdRAX2 by MdRLKT1, indicating that Ser 147 targeted by MdRLKT1 is essential for phosphorylation of MdRAX2 (Figure 4c). To investigate the biological effects of MdRAX2 phosphorylation, overexpression of MdRAX2 or the dephosphorylation‐mimicking mutant MdRAX2 S147A was performed in apple leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. Overexpression of MdRAX2 significantly enhanced the resistance of apple leaves to C. mali; however, overexpression of MdRAX2 S147A resulted in lesion areas similar to those observed in leaves expressing the GFP empty vector (Figure 4d). These results suggest that phosphorylation of MdRAX2 by MdRLKT1 at Ser‐147 enhances resistance of apple leaves to C. mali . To investigate the evolutionary conservation of MdRAX2, multiple sequence alignment of its homologues across diverse species was performed. The analysis revealed that the serine residue at position 147 (Ser147) is highly conserved. Although the flanking sequences surrounding Ser147 are variable, this specific serine residue remains invariant in nearly all homologues examined, suggesting its critical functional or structural importance (Figure S9).

MdRAX2 Binds to the MdMKS1 Promoter and Inhibits Its Expression

Since MdRAX2 functions as a TF (Figure S3a), it may play a role in the immune response by modulating the expression of target genes. DAP‐seq was performed to identify MdRAX2 binding sites at a genome‐wide scale. A detailed analysis of the binding profiles revealed that MdRAX2 bound sites (14.35%) were located within promoter regions, 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) (6.62%), 3′ UTRs (2.86%), exons (7.22%), introns (26.47%) and intergenic regions (36.76%) (Figure 5a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that most of the target genes were localised in the nucleus (Figure 5b). Among the candidates identified, MKS1 (MAP kinase substrate 1) was selected for further analysis due to its function as a substrate of mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs), which play critical roles in plant immune responses. Notably, MdRAX2 exhibited the highest binding affinity to AG‐rich motifs (Figure S10a). The promoter of MdMKS1 contains such AG‐rich sequences, suggesting that MdRAX2 may directly regulate the translation of MdMKS1 through its binding to this motif. To further verify, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed to verify the binding of MdRAX2 to the MdMKS1 promoter. Competitive binding assays showed that the binding signal decreased with the addition of unlabeled competitors, whereas the MdRAX2 protein failed to bind to the mutant probe (Figure 5c). Further, site‐directed mutagenesis was performed to verify the true interaction site between MdRAX2 and the MdMKS1 promoter. The results showed that the interaction was abolished only when the sequence ‘GGGGAG’ was mutated to ‘TTTTCT’ (Figure 5c). In addition, yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays were performed to further determine the in vitro binding of MdRAX2 to the MdMKS1 promoter (Figure 5d). The results confirmed that MdRAX2 binds directly to the MdMKS1 promoter. To investigate whether its phosphorylation at Ser‐147 by MdRLKT1 affects this binding, EMSA and Y1H assays were performed. These results showed that mutation of Ser147 did not affect the binding ability of MdRAX2 to the MdMKS1 promoter (Figure 5d, Figure S11), indicating that MdMKS1 is a direct target gene of MdRAX2.

To further characterise the transcriptional regulation of MdMKS1 by MdRAX2, the constructs 35Spro::MdRAX2 or 35Spro::MdRAX2S147A were co‐infiltrated with proMdMKS1‐LUC into apple callus. The luminescence signal and luciferase (LUC) activity for 35Spro::MdRAX2 and proMdmks1‐LUC were more significantl than those for the 35Spro and proMdmks1‐LUC, whereas 35Spro::MdRAX2S147A showed similar levels of luminescence and LUC activity to the 35Spro control results (Figure 5f,g). In addition, a β‐glucuronidase (GUS) activity assay was performed to confirm these results. Consistently, the GUS activity in apple callus co‐infiltrated with 35Spro and proMdMKS1‐GUS constructs was higher than that in leaves co‐infiltrated with the 35Spro:: MdRAX2 and proMdmks1‐GUS constructs, whereas GUS activity in apple callus co infiltrated with 35Spro::MdRAX2S147A and proMdMKS1‐GUS was comparable to that of the 35Spro control (Figure 5h).

To determine whether MdRLKT1 affects the binding of MdRAX2 to the MdMKS1 promoter, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed. After incubation with MdRLKT1 in kinase reaction buffer in the presence of ATP, an increase in the DNA‐binding affinity of GST‐MdRAX2 towards the MdMKS1 promoter was observed. In contrast, after incubation with MdRLKT1, the DNA‐binding ability of GST‐MdRAX2S147A showed little change regardless of whether ATP was added (Figure 5i). These results indicate that MdRAX2 transcriptionally represses the expression of MdMKS1 and that phosphorylation of MdRAX2 at Ser147 is essential for its transcriptional regulation activity.

MdMKS1 Negatively Regulates Modulates Resistance to Cytospora mali

MKS1, officially named MAP kinase substrate 1, encodes a protein that functions primarily as a substrate of mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs). MdMKS1 has been implicated in regulating various stress responses and developmental processes in plants by acting as a mediator in the MAPK signalling pathway. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the N‐terminus of MdMKS1 contains a conserved valine‐glutamine (VQ)‐motif (Figure S12a). To investigate the role of MdMKS1 in apple resistance, MdMKS1 was transiently overexpressed or silenced in apple leaves. Compared with the control, the transcript levels of MdMKS1 were significantly increased in overexpression lines, whereas it was significantly reduced in MdMKS1‐silenced leaves (Figure 6c,f). Functionally, overexpression of MdMKS1 significantly reduced the resistance of apple leaves to C. mali (Figure 6a,b), whereas silencing of MdMKS1 markedly enhanced the resistance of apple leaves to C. mali (Figure 6d,e). Moreover, overexpression of MdMKS1 significantly increased the transcription levels of defence‐related genes, including MdPR1, MdPR, MdPR4 and MdPR5, whereas silencing of MdMKS1 resulted in significantly elevated expression of MdPR2, MdPR4 and MdPR5 (Figure 6g). Collectively, these results indicate that MdMKS1 negatively regulates host resistance to C. mali, likely through repression of pathogenesis‐related (PR) gene expression.

Discussion

Apple tree Valsa cancer, caused by C. mali (C. mali), is one of the major constraints in apple production. Understanding the intrinsic disease resistance mechanisms and immune responses in apples is essential for developing effective disease control strategies. In this study, we define a novel MdRLKT1–MdRAX2–MdMKS1 regulatory module, which plays a pivotal role in apple resistance to C. mali . Our results, supported by multiple lines of evidence, demonstrate that the receptor‐like kinase MdRLKT1 positively regulates apple immune responses by phosphorylating the transcription factor MdRAX2 at a conserved serine residue (Ser147). This phosphorylation activates MdRAX2's transcriptional repression of MdMKS1, a negative regulator of disease resistance. This study not only elucidates a novel signalling mechanism mediated by an apple receptor‐like kinase but also provides a crucial theoretical foundation for the long‐term improvement of disease resistance in fruit trees.

MdRLKT1 Is a Key Positive Regulatory Factor in the Immune Response of Apple Trees

Receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) are essential components of plant innate immunity, responsible for perceiving extracellular signals and transducing them into intracellular defence responses through phosphorylation cascades. RLKs are widely involved in plant immunity in different plants. For example, in Arabidopsis, FLAGELLIN‐SENSING 2 (FLS2) and ELONGATION FACTOR‐TU RECEPTOR (EFR) recognise bacterial flagellar proteins by detecting their flg22 epitope. These receptors interact with the BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1‐associated kinase 1 (BAK1), and their reciprocal activation leads to plant immunity (Sun et al. 2013; Zipfel et al. 2006). Furthermore, BRI1‐ASSOCIATED KINASE 1 (BAK1) (Li et al. 2002), CHITIN ELICITOR RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (CERK1) (Miya et al. 2007), and SUPPRESSOR OF BIR1‐1 (SOBIR1) (Liebrand et al. 2013) are common co‐receptors that play a crucial role in plant resistance. The enhanced susceptibility of MdRLKT1‐RNAi lines to C. mali infection suggests that MdRLKT1 functions as a positive regulator of apple defence (Figure 1). This finding is consistent with the well‐established role of RLKs in immune activation and further confirms the central role of RLKs in the plant defence system.

Plant cell‐surface immune receptors are tightly regulation. In this context, phosphorylation plays a crucial role in the activation of RLKs and the subsequent transduction of downstream immune signalling. In plant innate immunity, plasma membrane‐localised RLKs typically trigger downstream signalling through the phosphorylation of receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs) (Liang and Zhou 2018; de Escocard Azevedo Manhães et al. 2021; Bender and Zipfel 2023). RLK‐RLCK modules regulate hormone synthesis and responses, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, Ca2+ signalling, activation of mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), and immune gene expression, all of which contribute to immunity (Hailemariam et al. 2024). For instance, upon recognising flg22, the plant cell‐surface receptor FLS2 forms a heterodimer with its co‐receptor BAK1. This complex then rapidly phosphorylates the receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinase BIK1, triggering its dissociation from the receptor complex (Lu et al. 2010). The liberated BIK1 subsequently initiates a phosphorylation cascade that modulates multiple downstream immune responses, including calcium influx (Thor et al. 2020), reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst (Kong et al. 2024). However, this study reveals the MdRLKT1–MdRAX2 signalling module, in which MdRLKT1 directly phosphorylates the MYB transcription factor MdRAX2, thereby regulating its subcellular localization and transcriptional repression activity (Figures 2 and 5). This mechanism, whereby a receptor kinase bypasses cytoplasmic kinases to directly modulate a transcription factor, suggests that plants may employ this more straightforward pathway to achieve rapid and precise control of nuclear gene expression for immunity. It is also conceivable that this direct interaction helps to insulate the MdRAX2‐regulated gene network from interference by other RLCK‐dependent pathways.

Phosphorylation Is Essential for the Immune‐Regulatory Function of MdRAX2

Plants trigger a series of immune responses upon pathogen attack, including transcriptional reprogramming, highlighting the crucial roles of transcriptional regulators in plant immunity (Jones and Dangl 2006; Khan et al. 2022). The MYB transcription factors constitute the largest family of plant‐specific TFs and play critical roles in plant immunity (Lin et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). Recently, the R2R3‐MYB transcription factor MYB22 regulates rice resistance to brown planthopper (BPH) via its EAR motif, which represses F3′H gene expression (Sun et al. 2023). The SG2‐type R2R3‐MYB transcription factor MYB15 is required for the defence‐induced synthesis of G‐rich lignin and the constitutive synthesis of the coumarin metabolite scopoletin, which contribute to disease resistance against a hemibiotrophic bacterial pathogen (Chezem et al. 2017). MYB30 is a key transcription factor that reactive ROS‐dependent developmental responses resulting in cellular elongation in the root, as well as some of its target genes, which function not only in ROS‐dependent developmental processes but also in defence responses to bacterial elicitors (Mabuchi et al. 2018). In this study, we found that transient silencing of MdRAX2 increased susceptibility, while MdRAX2‐overexpressing plants showed enhanced resistance to C. mali (Figure 3). Our results identified an immunity‐related MYB TF, MdRAX2 in apple, and MdRAX2 functions as a positive regulator in the apple immune response to C. mali .

Phosphorylation, a key post‐translational modification in immune signalling, regulates protein activity by modulating its properties. This study demonstrates that the receptor‐like kinase MdRLKT1 directly interacts with and phosphorylates the MYB transcription factor MdRAX2. An in vitro kinase assay identified Serine 147 (Ser147) as the specific phosphorylation site. Crucially, transgenic plants overexpressing the dephosphorylation‐mimicking mutant MdRAX2S147Aexhibited significantly reduced resistance to C. mali , unequivocally confirming that phosphorylation at Ser147 is essential for MdRAX2 to fully activate its disease‐resistant function. This study confirms that MdRLKT1 not only interacts with the transcription factor MdRAX2, but also significantly promotes its translocation into the nucleus (Figure 2). This result suggests that MdRLKT1 may actively regulate the nucleocytoplasmic distribution of MdRAX2 through phosphorylation modification, thereby enhancing its transcriptional repressive activity. Similar mechanisms have also been reported in other plants. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana , CIPK14 promotes the accumulation of WHY1 in the nucleus through phosphorylation, thereby enhancing its binding ability to the WRKY53 promoter and regulating leaf senescence (Ren et al. 2017). In addition, in wheat, the cytoplasmic TaNAC1‐7A is phosphorylated by TaCDPK20 at the S184/S258 sites, which promotes its nuclear translocation and thus enhances its transcriptional regulatory activity (Liu et al. 2024). These findings are highly consistent with the regulatory mode of the MdRLKT1–MdRAX2 pathway in this study, indicating that the phosphorylation‐dependent nuclear translocation mechanism may be a widely existing conserved strategy in plants, used to precisely regulate the spatiotemporal activity and functional output of transcription factors.

Furthermore, the functional importance of Ser147 phosphorylation is highlighted by its exceptional evolutionary conservation. Our sequence alignment shows that this residue is invariant across diverse plant species, while the flanking sequences exhibit more variability (Figure S9). This finding suggests that the MdRLKT1‐MdRAX2 signalling axis described here is not an isolated phenomenon in apple, but rather that Ser147 serves as a conserved cornerstone for the regulation of plant immunity.

3: MdRAX2 Inhibits the Transcription of MdMKS1 Through a Phosphorylation Dependent Pathway

To precisely identify the downstream regulatory targets of MdRAX2, this study employed DNA Affinity Purification Sequencing (DAP‐Seq) for a genome‐wide screening of its binding sites. The promoter region of MdMKS1 was unambiguously identified as a direct binding target of MdRAX2. Further experimental validation confirmed that MdRAX2 functions as a transcriptional repressor by binding to the MdMKS1 promoter, significantly suppressing MdMKS1 gene expression. More importantly, the transcriptional repression activity of MdRAX2 on MdMKS1 was completely abolished when its Serine 147 (Ser147) was mutated to Alanine (S147A), directly demonstrating that the phosphorylation status of this residue is a core prerequisite for MdRAX2's transcriptional regulatory function. Subsequent binding affinity assays revealed that MdRLKT1‐mediated phosphorylation of MdRAX2 substantially enhances its binding affinity for the MdMKS1 promoter. Collectively, these results indicate that the transcriptional repression function of MdRAX2 is dependent on MdRLKT1‐mediated phosphorylation.

Valine‐glutamine (VQ) genes (VQs) encode VQ motif‐containing proteins (VQs), which are generally regarded as a group of plant‐specific proteins characterised by the conserved VQ motif structure FxxhVQxhTG (F, Phenylalanine; x, any amino acid; h, hydrophobic residue; V, Valine; Q, Glutamine; T, Tryptophan, G, Glycine) (Jiang et al. 2018). Many VQs have been functionally characterised and shown to play roles in response to various pathogens, including jasmonate‐associated VQ motif gene 1 (JAV1), Arabidopsis MKS1, and MdVQ29 among other VQ proteins (Andreasson et al. 2005; Hu et al. 2013; Pecher et al. 2014; Han, Wang, et al. 2024). In Arabidopsis thaliana , MKS1 (MAP KINASE SUBSTRATE 1) is a well‐studied protein containing a VQ domain. It serves as a key component of the MPK4 signalling pathway. The MKS1 mutant exhibits enhanced susceptibility to Pseudomonas syringae and Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis, with significantly increased pathogen growth and reduced expression of the pathogenesis‐related gene PR1. This demonstrates that MKS1 plays a negative regulatory role in plant immunity (Petersen et al. 2010; Qiu et al. 2008). In this study, it was found that transient overexpression of MdMKS1 in apple leaves reduced resistance to C. mali (Figure 6a–c), whereas transient silencing of MdMKS1 enhanced resistance to C. mali (Figure 6d–f). Furthermore, the expression levels of the pathogenesis‐related genes PR1, PR2, PR4 and PR5 were significantly decreased in MdMKS1‐overexpressing plants. In contrast, the expression levels of PR2, PR4 and PR5 were significantly increased in MdMKS1‐silenced plants (Figure 6g). Collectively, these data establish MdMKS1 as a functional homologue of Arabidopsis MKS1, acting as a negative regulator of immune responses.

Previously, research on MdMKS1 has predominantly focused on its role as a substrate for the MPK4 kinase and its function as a transcriptional co‐regulator within immune signalling complexes. However, the upstream transcriptional machinery governing MdMKS1 expression itself has remained a significant gap in knowledge. Our study directly addresses this fundamental question by identifying the MYB transcription factor MdRAX2 as a key upstream transcriptional regulator of MdMKS1. We provide compelling evidence that MdRAX2 directly binds to the promoter of MdMKS1 and functions as a potent transcriptional repressor (Figure 5). This discovery reveals that the expression of this critical negative immune regulator is itself under precise transcriptional control. This establishes a novel regulatory layer, suggesting that the positive immune signal MdRAX2 may act to fine‐tune the plant's defence response by suppressing the expression of the negative immune component MdMKS1.

Taken together, we demonstrate that MdRLKT1 confers resistance to C. mali in apple by phosphorylating MdRAX2 at serine 147, which enhances MdRAX2's ability to repress the transcription of the immunity suppressor gene MdMKS1. Our findings reveal a mechanism in which RLK directly phosphorylates transcription factors, thereby transcriptionally regulates gene expression to modulate apple resistance to C. mali (Figure 7).

A working model for MdRLKT1 phosphorylating MdRAX2 to transcriptionally repress MdMKS1, thereby conferring resistance to C. mali in apple.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

The GL‐3 tissue culture seedlings were cultivated on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 0.2 mg/L 6‐benzyl aminopurine (6‐BA) and 0.2 mg/L indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) (Dai et al. 2013). N. benthamiana seedlings were grown in a phytotron. They were grown under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod at 25°C. The apple calli were grown on MS medium containing 0.4 mg/L 6‐BA and 1.5 mg/L 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4‐D) at 25°C in the dark. The C. mali WT strain 03‐8 was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium in a 25°C incubator. Escherichia coli strains DH5α were cultured in Luria‐Bertani (LB) medium at 37°C. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains GV3101 and EHA105 were cultivated at 28°C on LB medium.

RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Total RNA extraction was performed using a Quick RNA Isolation Kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology, Beijing, China). cDNA synthesis was performed using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RealStar Green Mixture (GenStar, Beijing, China) was used for RT‐qPCR analysis. RT‐qPCR assays were performed on the LightCycler 96 System (Roche, Germany) under the following conditions: 10 min at 95°C, 40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 30 s at 60°C, and 30 s at 72°C. MdEF1α was selected as the internal control for normalisation of apple gene expression (Yin et al. 2016). The 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001) was used for quantitative analysis of the results. The experiments were repeated three times. All the primers used for the RT‐qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

Bioinformatics Analysis

The transmembrane helices in proteins were identified based on TMHMM websites (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/). The protein conserved domains were analysed through the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). The protein sequences were obtained from the NCBI database by Blast search programmes (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Multiple sequence alignment was performed by ClustalW (https://www.genome.jp/tools‐bin/clustalw), and alignment visualisation was performed using ESPript (http://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA 11 and displayed by iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Genetic Transformation

The pK7GWIWG2D (II)‐GFP vector was used for both stable and transient silencing assays. The pCAMBIA1302‐GFP vector was used for transient overexpression/ectopic expression assays. The ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used for vector recombination. The primers are listed in Table S1 used in this experiment.

The Agrobacterium strains EHA105 and GV3101 (pSoup‐P19) were used for stable and transient expression. These vectors were genetically introduced into GL‐3 tissue culture seedlings, N. benthamiana seedlings, and apple calli using Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation as described previously (Lei et al. 2025). For pathogen infection assays, the mycelium plugs of C. mali were incubated onto leaves of GL‐3 tissue culture seedlings. The lesion areas were measured using ImageJ software. Inoculation was performed according to Han's method with the minor modifications (2024). The experiments were performed with at least three replications.

Subcellular Localization of MdRAX2 and MdMKS1

The coding sequences of MdRAX2 and MdMKS1 were fused to pCAMBIA1302‐GFP vector and transformed into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 by heat shock. Agrobacterium with MdRAX2‐GFP and MdMKS1‐GFP expression constructs were infiltrated into 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves. After 2 days, fluorescence signals were observed using a LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope (ZEISS). The pCAMBIA1302‐GFP empty vector was used as a control. The TaWPI6 protein was used as a plasma membrane location marker (Koike et al. 2005), and DAPI was labelled with nuclear localization (Han, Wang, et al. 2024). Fluorescence intensity was analysed using ImageJ software. The subcellular localization experiments were repeated three times.

LUC Assays and GUS Activity Detection

MdRLKT1‐nLUC and MdRAX2‐cLUC were generated for the LCI assay. The assay was performed according to Zhang et al. (2020). The primers are listed in Table S1 used in this experiment. For the luminescence signal and LUC/GUS activity analysis, MdRAX2 was inserted into pGreenII 62‐SK to generate 35Spro:: MdRAX2 constructs. Promoters of MdMKS1 were inserted into pGreenII 0800 and pCB308 to generate proMdMKS1‐LUC and pro MdMKS1‐GUS constructs. Leaves of N. benthamiana seedlings were analysed 48 h after infiltration. The primers are listed in Table S1 used in this experiment. d‐Luciferin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used for the LUC imaging. The luminescence signal was observed using the PlantView100 multispectral dynamic fluorescence microscopic imaging system (Biolight Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China). Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kits (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and a GloMax Navigator microplate luminescence detector (Promega Corporation, USA) were used for LUC activity analysis. Assays were conducted following the instructions of manufacturers. Each experimental treatment was repeated at least three times.

Y1H and Y2H

For the Y1H assays, MdRAX2 was inserted into pGADT7 to generate the MdRAX2‐AD construct. Promoters of MdMKS1 were inserted into pHIS2 to generate the proMdMKS1‐pHIS2 construct. Self‐activation was inhibited by 3‐AT (3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole) (Coolaber, Beijing, China). The yeast strain Y187 was used for Y1H. This assay was performed as described by Yu et al. (2020). The primers are listed in Table S1 used in this experiment.

For library construction, RNA was isolated from apple twigs of GALA inoculated with C. mali and sampled at 3 dpi post‐inoculation (dpi). The yeast two‐hybrid library was constructed with vector pGADT7 by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). For library screening, MdRLKT1 gene was inserted into pGBKT7 to generate MdRLKT1‐BD constructs. MdRAX2 gene was inserted into pGADT7 to generate the MdRAX2‐AD construct. Various combinations of these vectors were co‐transformed into Y2hGold yeast strain. These yeast cells were cultured on SD/‐Trp/‐Leu (DDO) and SD/‐Trp/‐Leu/–His/‐Ade (QDO) (with X‐α‐gal) plates to verify the interactions. This assay was performed following the description by Han, Wang, et al. (2024) and Han, Zhang, et al. (2024). Primers used in this experiment are listed in Table S1.

Co‐IP, BiFC and Microscale Thermophoresis Assay

For the Co‐IP assays, MdRLKT1‐CD and MdRAX2 were respectively ligated into pCAMBIA1302‐GFP and PICH86988‐mCherry. The GFP‐Trap beads (Chromotek) were used to separate fusion proteins. This assay was performed as described previously (Nie et al. 2021). For the BiFC assays, MdRLKT1 and MdRAX2 were respectively cloned into pSPYCE and pSPYNE to generate MdRLKT1‐nYFP and MdRAX2‐cYFP constructs. The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium strain EHA105 and coexpressed in N. benthamiana leaves. The signals were observed under a LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss) at 48 h after agroinfiltration. Assays were repeated at least three times. MdRLKT1‐CD was purified and labelled with the Monolith NT Protein Labeling Kit RED (Nanotemper Technologies, Germany; https://nanotempertech.com/) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Labelled proteins were incubated with a gradient dilution of MdRAX2 or MdRAX2S147A recombinant protein for 30 min at RT. Standard treated capillaries (Nanotemper Technologies) were loaded and the measurements were performed via Monolith NT.115 (Nanotemper Technologies). Similar results were obtained from three biological replicates, and data were analysed by MO. Affinity ANALYSIS v2.3.0 software.

Western Blotting

For Western blotting analysis, total proteins were extracted using the Native Lysis Buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) from agro‐infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Tiangen) following the manufacturer's instructions. The same amount of total proteins per line was subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE), and the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Roche). After blocking with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer (Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 8 h at 4°C, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibody: mouse anti‐His (Abways), 1:5000, mouse anti‐GST (Abmart), 1:5000; mouse anti‐GFP (Abmart), 1:5000, rabbit anti‐mCherry (Proteintech), 1:5000. The membranes were then washed five times with TBST buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween, pH 7.5) and incubated with 1:10 000 dilution of goat anti‐rabbit horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Abmart) or goat anti‐mouse horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Abmart) at 4°C for 4 h. After another seven washes with TBST buffer, chemiluminescent signals were detected by using the ECL Plus Kit.

In Vitro Phosphorylation and Phos‐Tag

Recombinant GST‐MdRLKT1CD, GST‐MdRAX2‐His and its mutant protein were expressed in E. coli BL21. For in vitro phosphorylation of MdRAX2 by MdRLKT1, 4 μg purified GST‐MdRLKT1CD and 0, 2, 4, 8 or 10 μg GST‐MdRAX2‐His protein were incubated in kinase buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.5 mM ATP) at 30°C for 1 h. For the phosphorylation of MdRAX2 and its mutant by MdRLKT1, 4 μg GST‐MdRLKT1CD and 4 μg GST‐MdRAX2‐His or mutated proteins were incubated in kinase buffer. For λ‐phosphatase treatment, 1 U λ‐phosphatase (Lambda Protein Phosphatase, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used. The reaction was stopped by heating at 95°C for 5 min with SDS loading buffer. The phosphorylated proteins were separated by Phos‐tag SDS‐PAGE and analysed by western blotting with the corresponding antibodies (Wang et al. 2022). Phosphorylated MdRAX2 was then subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis to identify the phosphorylation sites. Mass spectrometry data were deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the iProX repository, with dataset identifier PXD072787 (Chen et al. 2022). The analysis was conducted by Beijing BiorechPack.

DAP‐Seq and EMSA Assays

The DAP‐seq assay was conducted by the Genedenovo Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). High‐throughput data results can be obtained by efficient sequencing means in combination with high‐throughput sequencing technology. Candidate target genes can be screened from the obtained gene annotation tables. All DAP‐Seq data have been submitted to the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) BioProject: PRJCA055114.

The pGEX6p‐1 vector was used for producing the GST‐MdRAX2 fusion protein. Chemiluminescent EMSA Kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were used for EMSA. Biotin labeling of the probes was performed by Sangon Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The assay was performed as described by Li et al. (2022). The experiment was repeated three times. The primers were listed in Table S1 used in this experiment.

Statistical Analysis

Each experiment was replicated at least three times. Experimental results were analysed using GraphPad Prism 8 or SPSS software. Significant differences were evaluated through one‐way ANOVA or Student's t‐test (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01). The data were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD).
