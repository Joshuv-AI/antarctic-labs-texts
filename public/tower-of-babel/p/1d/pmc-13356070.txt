A metabolomic signatures in hyperuricemia: a systematic review
Authors: Yingnan Wu, Xu Han, Jiazhu Jin, Yunhua Chen, Danyang Cui, Xiaoxia Ma, Hongtao Guo, Miao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHyperuricemia (HUA) is traditionally viewed as a disorder of purine metabolism. However, its broader metabolic alterations remain incompletely understood. Metabolomics provides a useful approach for exploring metabolite changes associated with HUA, but a comprehensive synthesis of existing findings is still lacking.Aim of reviewThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to characterize the systemic metabolic signature of HUA beyond purine pathways. By synthesizing data from 27 metabolomics studies involving 12,335 participants, the study sought to identify consistent metabolite biomarkers and key dysregulated pathways to provide new insights for diagnosis and therapeutic targeting.Key scientific concepts of reviewThis review included 27 metabolomics studies involving 12,335 participants and identified 1,187 metabolites reported in association with HUA. Qualitative synthesis showed 54 consistently elevated and 20 consistently decreased blood metabolites, mainly involving amino acids, lipid-related metabolites, energy-related compounds, vitamins and their derivatives, and purine nucleoside metabolites. The meta-analysis was limited to two eligible studies, with one study contributing most of the statistical weight; it suggested higher levels of Alanine, Leucine, Phenylalanine, and Tyrosine and lower Histidine levels in HUA. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted “One carbon pool by folate,” “Arginine biosynthesis,” “Glutathione metabolism,” and related amino acid and energy metabolism pathways. Overall, these findings suggest that HUA may be associated with metabolic perturbations beyond purine metabolism alone, but the candidate metabolites and pathways require further validation in longitudinal, standardized, and mechanistic studies.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a metabolic disorder characterized by persistently elevated serum uric acid (SUA) levels (Li et al., 2019). As uric acid (UA) accumulation progresses, patients are at heightened risk of developing gout, kidney stones, chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiovascular complications, metabolic syndrome, obesity, hypertension, and diabetes (Bonino et al., 2020; Geng et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2014; Lee & Song, 2019; Zong et al., 2021). Epidemiological data indicate that HUA prevalence varies widely, from approximately 2.6 to 36% across different populations (Du et al., 2024). According to World Health Organization estimates, 5–15% of adults globally are affected by HUA (Huang et al., 2020). Notably, around 5–12% of individuals with HUA eventually progress to gout, contributing substantially to the global burden of HUA and gout, which reached an estimated 930 million people in 2020 (Chi et al., 2024). The economic impact is equally pronounced: in Italy, for instance, the annual healthcare cost per HUA patient ranges from €2,752 to €4,607 (Fang Jiawen et al., 2024). With an aging population and changing lifestyles, the prevalence of HUA is expected to continue rising, intensifying its long-term burden on public health systems (Fang Ningyuan et al., 2023); therefore, improving early recognition and management of HUA remains an important public health issue with clinical and socioeconomic relevance.

The effective management of HUA is currently hindered by several unresolved challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. A primary challenge is its often asymptomatic nature, which delays clinical recognition (Zhang 2021b). Standard diagnostic methods, reliant on serum uric acid measurements, are susceptible to fluctuations from diet and comorbidities (Lee & Song, 2024), limiting their utility for stable and comprehensive metabolic assessment (Zhang et al., 2019). Furthermore, current treatment strategies primarily rely on uric acid-lowering therapies, including xanthine oxidase inhibitors and uricosuric agents (Du et al., 2024). Although effective in lowering uric acid, these agents have notable limitations such as hypersensitivity reactions, hepatic adverse effects, and the potential to induce gout flare during the early treatment phase (Lai et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). Other complementary therapeutic approaches, such as traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), have been explored as potential alternatives, but the lack of standardized formulations and well-elucidated mechanisms hampers widespread application (Peng Hongcheng, 2023). More importantly, the optimal timing and target levels for initiating urate-lowering therapies in asymptomatic patients remain controversial (Tien et al., 2022). Therefore, a more proactive strategy focusing on early prevention and risk stratification has gained attention, but this requires more sensitive approaches to characterize metabolic alterations associated with HUA.

Against this backdrop, metabolomics has emerged as a useful approach for exploring candidate disease biomarkers and metabolic pathways. By applying advanced spectrometry and separation techniques, metabolomics enables comprehensive profiling of small-molecule metabolites, thus providing insights into metabolic alterations that may occur before or alongside clinical manifestations (Guijas et al., 2018; Yanai et al., 2021). This approach has been applied to biomarker exploration in various chronic diseases, such as diabetes, liver cancer, and osteoarthritis (Guijas et al., 2018; Jin & Ma, 2021; Sampson et al., 2017). Recent investigations into HUA have yielded encouraging preliminary data; for instance, elevated levels of amino acids (e.g., cysteine, phenylalanine) and long-chain acylcarnitines have been linked to incident HUA in middle-aged and older adults (Wang et al. 2020). In other studies, targeted metabolomics has identified distinctive patterns of purine intermediates and lipid metabolites in patients with HUA or gout (Liang et al., 2024; Waheed et al., 2021). These findings suggest the potential value of metabolomic markers for future studies on early detection and risk stratification of HUA.

Despite growing interest in metabolomic markers of HUA, a uniform synthesis or consensus of these findings is still lacking. Such a gap arises partly from variations in study design, analytical techniques, and population characteristics. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to: (1) identify and consolidate metabolites consistently altered in HUA; (2) quantitatively assess their effect sizes where sufficient data are available; and (3) explore potential metabolic pathways that may provide clues for future biomarker validation and mechanistic studies.

Materials and methods

The systematic review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Page et al., 2021). Additionally, this study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD 42022367382).

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) observational clinical studies; (2) studies including confirmed adult patients with HUA, including asymptomatic cases, and healthy control (HC) subjects with serum uric acid levels within the normal reference range, no history of metabolic diseases, and normal liver and kidney function indicators; and (3) studies using metabolomics techniques and reporting metabolite-related data in sufficient detail.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies including patients diagnosed with gout, participants receiving uric-acid-lowering medications, or patients with complex comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and chronic kidney diseases, that could substantially interfere with the assessment of HUA-related metabolic characteristics; (2) intervention-based studies and non-original research, such as review articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, case reports, and expert opinions; and (3) studies with substantial missing data or duplicate datasets.

Search strategy

All records published up to September 10, 2025, were retrieved from a comprehensive range of databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, and VIP Data. The search strategy incorporated key terms such as “hyperuricemia” and “metabolomics”, along with their relevant synonyms. Supplementary Table S1 provides detailed information about the search terms and their combinations. No restrictions were imposed on the publication date or language.

Study selection

All identified records were imported into Endnote X9. Duplicate records were removed by both automated and manual methods. The studies were screened by two independent reviewers (XH and YNW) based on titles and abstracts, and potentially eligible studies were further assessed through full-text review. Any discrepancies were discussed with a third reviewer (DYC) until consensus was reached. The process was overseen by a senior researcher (MJ).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was independently assessed by two researchers (YNW and XH) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The NOS is a tool for evaluating the quality of observational studies (Stang, 2010). Studies were assessed using a “star-rating system” based on selection, comparability, and exposure.

Data collection

The extracted data covered multiple aspects, including the first author’s name, publication year, country of origin, study design, study language, participant age, sample size, patient diagnostic criteria, statistical methods, detection techniques, metabolomics platforms, and the trends and concentrations of metabolites in both the HUA and HC groups when available. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. These data were obtained from the main articles and their supplementary materials. When data were presented graphically and detailed numerical data were unavailable after attempts to contact the original authors, Web Plot Digitizer (version 4.2; Ankit Rohatgi, San Francisco, CA, USA, 2019) was used to extract data from figures. Additionally, for articles with a validation cohort, data from the validation cohort were also included in the analysis.

Data synthesis

The qualitative synthesis was conducted to describe and summarize the metabolite profile of HUA by counting the statistically significant direction of differential metabolites between the HUA and HC groups across the included studies.

For quantitative synthesis, meta-analysis was performed only when studies provided sufficient and comparable quantitative data. Eligible studies were required to report complete mean, standard deviation, and sample size data, or statistics that could be converted into these values, for the same metabolite measured in comparable biological samples and participant populations. Prior to pooling, metabolite concentration data were standardized to uniform units where possible. Data presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) were converted using the formula recommended by the Cochrane Handbook (Supplementary Table S2). For data presented as quartiles, conversion was performed using an established statistical method that assessed data skewness and applied a piecewise function based on sample size (Luo et al. 2018a).

The primary effect measure was the weighted mean difference (WMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). A fixed-effect model was applied for low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I² ≤ 50%), while a random-effects model was used for substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%). When heterogeneity was extreme (I² > 85%) or when fixed- and random-effects models produced substantially divergent results, pooled estimates were not presented to avoid misleading conclusions. Sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, and publication bias assessments were planned when sufficient studies were available. However, these analyses were not performed when fewer than two studies were included in a meta-analysis, and funnel plots or Egger’s tests were not conducted unless at least ten studies were available. All statistical analyses were performed using the “meta” package in R software (Version 3.6.2).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway analysis was performed on the metabolites extracted from the included studies using MetaboAnalyst (v6.0) (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Integrated enrichment and topology analysis was conducted against the human KEGG pathway library, and pathways with an FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature search

A total of 4037 records were initially identified, of which 1293 duplicate entries were removed. Following screening based on titles and abstracts, 2625 irrelevant studies were excluded. Full-text articles were retrieved for 110 of the 119 remaining records; 9 were unavailable. Upon full-text review of these 110 articles, 83 were excluded. The specific reasons for exclusion, ordered by frequency, were: incorrect methodology (n = 22), incorrect patient population (n = 21), interventional study design (n = 15), ineligible publication format (n = 14), repeated publication of data (n = 6), and use of an ineligible control group (n = 5). Finally, 27 studies (Cheng et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2017; Cui 2020; Han et al. 2018; Lin & Yang 2025; Liu et al. 2022, 2023a, 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Lv 2022; Ma 2013; Ma et al. 2023a; Motta et al. 2016; Qin 2021; Rodgers et al. 2005; Shen et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2020, 2023; Wu et al. 2025; Yang 2013; Yu et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018, 2023, 2024; Zhang 2013, 2021a; Zhong et al., 2011) were included. Characteristics of included studies

A total of 27 eligible studies involving 12,335 participants (2,995 HUA, 9,340 HC) were included, showing a significant sample size imbalance. This discrepancy was primarily driven by three large-scale investigations, which featured pronounced disparities between HUA and HC group sizes [174 vs. 672 (Cheng et al., 2023), 1216 vs. 5662 (Lin & Yang, 2025), and 375 vs. 1246 (Wang et al. 2020), respectively].

The included studies, published between 2005 and 2025. Of the 27 studies, 24 studies were case-control investigations, 3 studies were prospective cohort designs. Among 16 articles were published in English and 11 articles in Chinese. Participants were recruited from China and the United States. Four studies failed to specify the ages of both HUA and HC groups, while 1 study reported only the age of the HUA group. Based on sample type, the studies comprised 23 on blood (including one using Dried Blood Spot (DBS), 2 on urine, and 1 on saliva study and 1 on seminal plasma study.

Regarding metabolomics platforms, 20 studies employed mass spectrometry (MS) coupled with liquid-phase chromatography (LC); 2 studies employed mass spectrometry (MS) coupled with gas-phase and liquid-phase chromatography (GC and LC); 1 study applied direct injection mass spectrometry (MS); 4 studies used proton (1H) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy for metabolite quantification; 1 study implemented capillary ion chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (CIC–MS/MS). Risk of bias of included studies

The risk of bias for all included studies was evaluated with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Overall, the quality of the studies was generally acceptable to high: 23 studies of 27 studies (85.2%) received over 7 stars. The remaining 4 studies received lower scores: 2 studies due to an inadequate case definition, and the other 2 because of insufficient reporting of baseline data (e.g., age, BMI, biochemical parameters), which precluded the assessment of group comparability.

Qualitative synthesis

Our systematic review of 27 studies identified a comprehensive landscape of 1,187 distinct metabolites reported in association with HUA, including amino acids and their derivatives, lipid-related species, nucleotides, vitamins, and gut microbiota products.

To identify metabolites repeatedly reported across studies, we performed a qualitative synthesis on metabolites reported in at least two studies. This subset comprised 274 distinct metabolites. These metabolites were primarily identified in blood samples (n = 220), with smaller numbers from seminal plasma (n = 5) and saliva (n = 3). Among the 220 metabolites in blood, 54 were consistently elevated, 20 were consistently decreased, and 26 showed inconsistent changes across studies. In seminal plasma, lactic acid was decreased while the other 4 showed inconsistent changes. For the saliva metabolites, 5-hydroxyisouric acid was elevated, whereas homocysteine exhibited inconsistent changes. The specific metabolites corresponding to these trends are detailed in Table 2.

Amino acids

Alterations in amino acid (AA) profiles have frequently reported in hyperuricemia (HUA), with 20 studies comparing levels between patients and healthy controls (Cui et al. 2017; Cui 2020; Han et al. 2018; Lin & Yang 2025; Liu et al. 2023a, 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Lv 2022; Motta et al. 2016; Qin 2021; Shen et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2020, 2023; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2018, 2023, 2024; Zhang 2013, 2021a; Zhong et al., 2011).

Alanine was one of the most frequently reported AAs, with 5 independent studies reporting significantly higher levels in HUA patients (Han et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2018). Glutamate (Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Zhang et al. 2018) and Threonine (Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Zhang et al. 2018) were also found to be consistently elevated. Across three separate studies for the two amino acids, their levels were significantly higher in HUA subjects compared to controls. Phosphocreatine (Cui et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2021) and Tryptophan (Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b) were each assessed in two studies, and both metabolites were consistently reported to be positively associated with HUA.

Furthermore, beyond Tryptophan itself, its downstream metabolite Kynurenic acid was reported to be decreased in HUA patients, suggesting potential dysregulation of the kynurenine pathway (Wang et al. 2023; Zhang 2021a).

In stark contrast, Ornithine levels were repeatedly reported to be lower. All 4 studies that quantified this amino acid reported lower levels in HUA subjects (Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2025). Serine also showed a similar decreasing trend. Of the 4 studies that measured it, three reported a significant inverse association with HUA (Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2025).

The findings for Tyrosine were conflicting. Although it was frequently reported as elevated in 4 studies (Han et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2018), 1 study identified an inverse association, a discrepancy potentially attributable to the use of dried-blood-spot sampling (Liu et al. 2023c). Similarly, the association for Phenylalanine was inconsistent. While 3 of 5 studies found a significant positive association with HUA (Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2018, 2024), the other 2 reported no significant association. Results for Proline (Liu et al. 2023c; Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020) and Valine (Luo et al. 2018b; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang 2013) were also contradictory, with different studies reporting positive, negative, or no significant correlations with HUA.

Lipid metabolites

A total of 24 studies reported lipid-related metabolites. Among them, 7 studies evaluated the levels of phosphatidylcholine (PC) and its metabolite lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) in different participants. Two case-control studies (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2011) reported a negative association between plasma PC and HUA. Another study (Qin, 2021) reported lower LPC levels in the HUA group than in the control group. Five studies (Liu et al. 2022; Lv 2022; Qin 2021; Wang et al. 2023; Zhang 2021a) reported higher LPC levels in the HUA group than in the control group.

Beyond the investigations on PC and LPC, research has also been conducted on the relationship between sphingomyelin (SM) and HUA. Using high-throughput techniques, researchers identified dozens of differentially expressed phospholipids in the HUA group, including sphingomyelin (SM), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidic acid (PA), and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Among the four studies that determined the SM level, two (Lv, 2022; Qin, 2021) reported a positive association between SM and HUA.

In addition, 3 studies (Qin 2021; Wang et al. 2023; Zhang 2021a) focused on the relationship between low-carbon-number-saturated lipids and HUA. Specifically, palmitic acid and stearic acid were found to have a close association with HUA. Moreover, it was reported that triglyceride, very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), and saturated fatty acid (SFA) were positively correlated with HUA. In contrast, arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and oleic acid were negatively correlated with HUA.

In summary, the research on lipid metabolites and HUA has yielded a complex picture. Different lipid metabolites show diverse correlations with HUA, and these findings may provide clues for future studies on lipid metabolic alterations in HUA.

Energy-related metabolites

Lactate exhibited a sample-dependent association with HUA. While its levels were consistently elevated in serum and plasma across multiple studies (Zhang 2021a; Zhang et al. 2018; Zhong et al., 2011), an opposing, negative correlation was reported in seminal plasma (Cui, 2020). Another investigation of L- Lactic acid in seminal plasma revealed a discrepancy between analytical platforms (Qin, 2021). An initial untargeted analysis indicated a decrease, whereas a follow-up targeted assay reported a significant increase, a difference that may reflect differences in analytical platform and quantitative precision.

Two studies (Zhang, 2013; Zhong et al., 2011) reported lower plasma glucose levels in HUA group, while another 2 studies (Shen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018) found higher serum levels of β-glucose, α-glucose, GDP-mannose, and GDP-glucose among carbohydrate metabolites.

Vitamins and their derivatives

A total of seven studies investigated the association between various vitamins and HUA (Cui 2020; Liu et al. 2023a; Ma 2013; Shen et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023; Zhang 2021a).

Inverse associations were reported for several vitamins. Serum levels of vitamin C (ascorbic acid) and vitamin B5 were found to be inversely associated with HUA (Ma, 2013; Shen et al., 2021) This finding for vitamin C was further supported by its decreased concentration in the seminal plasma of HUA patients (Ma, 2013). Similarly, vitamin E was reported to be negatively associated with HUA (Ma, 2013; Yu et al., 2016).

Conversely, other vitamins were associated with an increased risk of HUA. Both vitamin A (retinol) and the vitamin B6 derivative, pyridoxamine, exhibited a positive correlation with serum uric acid levels (Ma, 2013; Shen et al., 2021). Vitamin B1 was also reported to be positively associated with HUA in one study (Ma, 2013).

Furthermore, a key folate derivative, 5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (5-methyl-THF), was found to be significantly and negatively correlated with HUA (Ma, 2013; Shen et al., 2021). This finding may be consistent with perturbations in folate-dependent metabolic processes, although further validation is needed.

Purine nucleoside metabolites

The relationship between purine nucleoside metabolites and HUA has been reported in 10 studies. Five studies (Cui et al. 2017; Cui 2020; Luo et al. 2018b; Rodgers et al. 2005; Zhang 2021a) have explored the association between uric acid and HUA, reporting positive associations between serum, plasma, or salivary uric acid and HUA, whereas seminal plasma showed an opposite pattern. Inosine was mentioned three times, all in relation to serum samples. Two of them were positively correlated with HUA (Rodgers et al., 2005; Shen et al., 2021), while one was negatively correlated (Motta et al., 2016). Xanthine was detected in 2 studies (Liu et al. 2023a; Rodgers et al. 2005), which 1 study (Rodgers et al., 2005) indicated a positive correlation with HUA.

Other metabolites

Glutathione: Glutathione, a metabolite related to oxidative stress and redox homeostasis, was reported in two independent studies. In seminal plasma (Cui, 2020), HUA men exhibited higher glutathione concentrations than the HC group, whereas serum levels (Wang et al., 2023) were significantly lower in HUA patients. Among its direct metabolites, oxidized glutathione (Zhang et al., 2023) and S-formylmethyl glutathione (Shen et al., 2021) both displayed significant inverse correlations with HUA in serum, whereas S-lactoylglutathione (Cui et al., 2017) was reported to be elevated in saliva of HUA patients.

Steroid Hormone Metabolites: Two distinct research have been conducted, and higher serum concentrations of androsterone sulfate (a metabolite derived from testosterone metabolism) and DHEA sulfate (an important adrenal-derived steroid hormone metabolite) have been identified as the results of these two research endeavors (Wang et al. 2023; Zhang 2021a).

Other Related Metabolites: In specific individual reports, associations (positive or negative) with HUA, though based on limited evidence, have been detected for bile pigment, polyamines, nitrogen-containing metabolites, and their characteristic metabolites (Shen et al., 2021).Although these associations are based on limited evidence, they suggest potential novel avenues for further research into the complex etiology of HUA.

Furthermore, a 13-metabolite panel has shown high utility for HUA diagnosis. This signature, comprising metabolites like Tyrosine, Threonine, and various lipid species, achieved excellent discriminatory performance with AUC values of 0.91 (discovery) and 0.90 (validation) for separating HUA patients from controls (Zhang et al., 2024).

Meta-analysis

Only two studies met the eligibility criteria for quantitative synthesis, and 16 amino acids were included in the meta-analysis. The limited quantitative synthesis suggested higher concentrations of Alanine (MD = 32.19, 95% CI 4.43 to 59.95), Leucine (MD = 6.13, 95% CI 3.10 to 9.15), Phenylalanine (MD = 2.17, 95% CI 1.00 to 3.33), and Tyrosine (MD = 2.07, 95% CI 0.61 to 3.52) in the HUA group, while Histidine was lower in the HUA group (MD = −1.48, 95% CI −2.95 to −0.01). No significant differences were observed for Tryptophan (MD = 0.50, 95% CI −2.03 to 3.03), Methionine (MD = 0.16, 95% CI −0.36 to 0.68), Threonine (MD = 0.16, 95% CI −5.98 to 6.30), Proline (MD = −0.55, 95% CI −7.64 to 6.55), Isoleucine (MD = 5.66, 95% CI −3.10 to 14.41), Valine (MD = 16.70, 95% CI −0.05 to 33.45), Ornithine (MD = 3.99, 95% CI −3.57 to 11.54), and Serine (MD = −22.08, 95% CI −52.75 to 8.59). Summary estimates for Glutamate, Lysine, and Glycine were not presented because of extreme heterogeneity. Notably, Wang et al. (2020) contributed approximately 90% of the statistical weight in the pooled analyses. Therefore, these pooled findings should be interpreted with considerable caution and regarded as exploratory quantitative summaries rather than definitive meta-analytic evidence. Owing to the small number of included studies, sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, and funnel plot analyses were not conducted. Pathway analysis highlights potential metabolic perturbations

Pathway analysis of the 1,187 identified metabolites identified 17 significantly enriched metabolic pathways (FDR < 0.05), as summarized in the bubble plot (Fig. 3). The most statistically significant pathway was “One carbon pool by folate” (FDR = 1.38 × 10⁻⁵, Impact = 0.45). Other significantly enriched pathways included “Arginine biosynthesis” (FDR = 5.41 × 10⁻⁴, Impact = 0.72) and “Glutathione metabolism” (FDR = 2.40 × 10⁻³, Impact = 0.46). Several pathways, such as “Caffeine metabolism” and “Phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis,” showed high pathway impact scores (Impact = 1.0), suggesting that the mapped metabolites occupied relatively influential positions within these pathway topologies. To further explore the metabolic network in hyperuricemia, pathway enrichment was conducted on 62 metabolites that were reported in at least two studies and showed a consistent directional trend. As illustrated in the figure, the top 25 enriched metabolic pathways are presented. The analysis highlighted enrichment of pathways primarily involved in amino acid metabolism, including valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis/degradation, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism, as well as energy metabolism, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism. Furthermore, pathways related to oxidative stress regulation (glutathione metabolism), lipid metabolism (biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and linoleic acid metabolism), and one-carbon unit metabolism (one carbon pool by folate) were also significantly enriched. The enrichment of these pathways suggests their potential relevance to the metabolic signature of hyperuricemia and may link urate dysregulation to broader alterations in nitrogen handling, cellular energetics, redox homeostasis, and lipid balance. Discussion

This systematic review summarized the reported metabolomic alterations associated with HUA. The qualitative synthesis identified 1,187 metabolites, including 54 metabolites consistently elevated and 20 consistently decreased in blood samples across the included studies. The limited quantitative synthesis suggested higher levels of Alanine, Leucine, Phenylalanine, and Tyrosine, and lower levels of Histidine in the HUA group. Pathway analysis identified significant enrichment in 17 pathways, with One carbon pool by folate, Arginine biosynthesis, and Glutathione metabolism being the most prominent. Overall, these findings support the view that HUA may be associated with broader metabolic perturbations beyond purine metabolism alone, including amino acid metabolism, one-carbon metabolism, oxidative stress-related pathways, and energy metabolism.

The elevation of specific amino acids, such as alanine and phenylalanine, is supported by mechanistic evidence from other studies. Increased alanine levels may reflect hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction, as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is often elevated in HUA models (Liu et al. 2023b; Zhao et al. 2025). The potential relevance of alanine-related metabolism is also suggested by studies showing that its isomer, beta-alanine, may inhibit xanthine oxidase (XOD) activity (Chen et al., 2025). Phenylalanine accumulation has been linked to the induction of endothelial cell pyroptosis, a process that contributes to vascular damage associated with HUA (Copur et al., 2022). The enrichment of “Arginine biosynthesis” in our pathway analysis is also consistent with reports that the arginine-ornithine metabolism axis may be altered in HUA (Liu et al. 2023b; Zhao et al. 2025).

Lipid metabolites showed heterogeneous associations with HUA, with triglycerides and saturated fatty acids showing positive associations, while arachidonic and linoleic acids were negatively linked to HUA, reflecting the interplay between dyslipidemia, oxidative stress, and uric acid metabolism (Chen et al., 2020). Certain phosphatidylcholine species have been identified as metabolic mediators that link exposure to environmental pollutants with an increased risk of HUA (You et al., 2022). Similarly, specific sphingomyelin subtypes are associated with both impaired renal function and a higher risk of HUA, suggesting they may serve as markers for HUA-related nephropathy (Yun et al., 2022). These findings indicate that while the overall lipid profile is complex, individual lipid molecules may play distinct roles in the pathogenesis of HUA.

Energy metabolism may represent a relevant dimension of HUA-associated metabolic alterations, particularly through changes in lactate and glucose-related pathways. The consistent elevation of lactate is mechanistically significant. As an organic anion, lactate competitively inhibits renal urate excretion by interfering with its reabsorption via tubular transporters such as URAT1 and OAT1/OAT3 (Kanai, 2008; Mount et al., 2006; Roch-Ramel & Guisan, 1999). This direct competition in the proximal tubules diminishes urate clearance, consequently leading to elevated serum uric acid levels. Furthermore, perturbations in glucose homeostasis may contribute to HUA by modulating purine metabolism. A state of relative energy deficiency, potentially indicated by altered glucose levels, can trigger a catabolic stress response (Akbari et al., 2022). This response enhances the breakdown of endogenous purine nucleotides to meet energy demands, thereby increasing the substrate pool for xanthine oxidase and accelerating uric acid production. In summary, dysregulated energy metabolism impacts HUA through a dual mechanism: impaired renal excretion driven by lactate, and accelerated purine catabolism linked to altered glucose homeostasis.

Vitamin metabolism may be associated with HUA through pathways related to antioxidant capacity and renal urate handling. Vitamin C, for instance, has been reported to lower serum uric acid by suppressing uric acid synthesis through antioxidant effects and promoting urate excretion through competitive interactions with renal transporters such as URAT1 (Jia et al., 2016). While vitamin E may offer protection through similar antioxidant pathways, vitamin A and pyridoxamine have been associated with increased HUA risk, highlighting the importance of dietary antioxidant balance (Chi et al., 2024). These findings are broadly consistent with our pathway enrichment results, in which “One carbon pool by folate” emerged as a significantly enriched pathway. The observed decrease in the folate derivative 5-methyl-THF may provide a potential link to folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism and methylation-related processes, although this pathway should be interpreted as a candidate metabolic signal requiring further validation.

The observed alterations in purine metabolites are consistent with their established roles in uric acid (UA) homeostasis. The role of inosine, a direct UA precursor, is particularly complex and may explain the inconsistent correlations reported. On one hand, its accumulation can promote hyperuricemia by serving as a direct substrate for xanthine oxidase (XOD)-mediated UA synthesis, an effect confirmed in both clinical and animal models (Chen et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022, 2025). Furthermore, elevated inosine may exacerbate HUA by impairing renal function, potentially through the inhibition of urate transporters like ABCG2 or by inducing tubular epithelial injury (Ohashi et al., 2023). On the other hand, the bimodal nature of inosine is highlighted by reports of its protective functions. For instance, inosine derived from gut microbiota has been shown to ameliorate HUA-associated intestinal dysfunction (Pfeifer et al., 2024), and it is also known to possess immunomodulatory properties (Han et al., 2025). These context-dependent, opposing effects likely contribute to the varied associations observed. The concurrent elevation of xanthine is mechanistically consistent with an increased substrate flux through the purine degradation pathway, as it is the immediate intermediate catalyzed by XOD (Gao et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings suggest that purine metabolism remains closely related to HUA and may involve both increased substrate availability for UA production and altered renal excretion pathways.

Fructose intake may also influence uric acid production. Excess fructose, particularly from sugar-sweetened beverages and other processed foods containing added sugars, can increase uric acid through the relatively unregulated hepatic fructokinase pathway (Ayoub-Charette et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2010). In this pathway, fructose is rapidly phosphorylated to fructose-1-phosphate with ATP consumption, leading to AMP accumulation and subsequent degradation to uric acid. Fructose may also promote de novo purine synthesis, further increasing the substrate flux toward uric acid production (Ayoub-Charette et al., 2021). However, this effect appears to depend on exposure context, including dose, food source, and food matrix; controlled feeding trials suggest that sugar-sweetened beverages show a more consistent uric-acid-raising effect, whereas fruit or 100% fruit juice may have different effects (Ayoub-Charette et al., 2021). Population-based data also suggest that fructose intake under ordinary dietary conditions may not be directly comparable with acute or high-dose experimental exposure (Sun et al., 2010). In the present review, however, the included metabolomics studies generally did not report dietary fructose intake, sugar-sweetened beverage consumption, or processed-food intake; therefore, the contribution of fructose exposure to the identified metabolomic signatures could not be directly evaluated. Moreover, most included studies were conducted in Chinese populations, whose dietary sources and levels of fructose exposure may differ from those in Western populations. Future metabolomics studies of HUA should collect detailed dietary information, particularly fructose-containing food sources, total energy intake, and broader dietary patterns, to clarify the contribution of fructose-related uric acid production to HUA-associated metabolic signatures.

The perturbation of other key metabolites and pathways also has mechanistic relevance. The prominence of “Glutathione metabolism” as a top pathway is consistent with the established role of oxidative stress in HUA. In the HUA group, glutathione levels were reported to be depleted, and restoration of glutathione-related antioxidant capacity has been suggested to mitigate oxidative damage (Chen et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2023b). From this perspective, oxidative stress may also be linked to upstream mitochondrial energy processes. Deuterium-related effects on mitochondrial proton transfer and ATP synthase have been proposed to slow proton-coupled electron transport and increase electron leakage and free radical generation (Olgun, 2007). Although ATP synthase function, mitochondrial proton transport, metabolic water production, and molecular crowding were not directly assessed in the metabolomics studies included in this review, these cellular biophysical processes may provide a theoretical framework for understanding the possible connection between disturbed energy metabolism and redox imbalance in HUA. Future studies integrating isotope-resolved metabolomics and mitochondrial functional assays may help clarify these mechanisms.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the included studies varied in population characteristics, sample types, metabolomics platforms, analytical methods, and data preprocessing, which may affect comparability. Second, because most studies were observational case-control studies, the findings mainly indicate associations between metabolite alterations and HUA, while causality requires further validation. Third, quantitative synthesis was limited by inconsistent metabolite reporting, as many studies reported only fold changes, VIP scores, P values, relative abundances, normalized peak intensities, or direction of change; consequently, only two studies were eligible for meta-analysis, and Wang et al. (2020) contributed approximately 90% of the statistical weight, limiting the robustness and interpretability of the pooled estimates. Fourth, most studies were conducted in Chinese populations, which may limit generalizability to populations with different genetic, dietary, and environmental backgrounds. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs, standardized metabolomics workflows, and complete quantitative reporting. Further mechanistic studies are also needed to evaluate the biological relevance of the candidate pathways identified in this review.

Conclusion

Our systematic review summarizes the reported metabolomic alterations associated with HUA. The findings suggest that HUA may involve metabolic perturbations beyond purine metabolism alone, including alterations in amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, energy metabolism, oxidative stress-related pathways, and one-carbon metabolism. These results provide a data-derived overview of candidate metabolites and potentially relevant pathways, but they should be interpreted in light of the heterogeneity of the included studies and the limited quantitative evidence. Future longitudinal, standardized, and mechanistic studies are needed to validate these candidate metabolic signals and clarify their relevance to HUA progression, risk stratification, and potential intervention strategies.
