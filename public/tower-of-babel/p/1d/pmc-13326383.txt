The impact of abiraterone and enzalutamide on hypokalemia incidence in patients with prostate cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yibo Lou, Dejun Fan, Tiejiang Chen, Kai Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the incidence of hypokalemia associated with novel androgen receptor axis-targeted therapies (ARATs)—specifically abiraterone, enzalutamide, and their combination—in patients with advanced prostate cancer.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was conducted to identify published randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. A random-effects model was employed to pool risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, and subgroup analyses were stratified by the specific ARAT regimen. Publication bias was evaluated utilizing funnel plots.ResultsEight unique RCTs (comprising 9 published reports) involving 9,298 patients were included. The overall pooled analysis demonstrated a significantly increased risk of all-grade hypokalemia in patients receiving ARATs compared to controls (RR = 3.11, 95% CI: 1.88–5.15, P < 0.001; I2 = 91.9%). Subgroup analyses revealed that this risk was significantly elevated with abiraterone (RR = 3.25, 95% CI: 1.75–6.02, I2 = 88.3%) and the combination of abiraterone plus enzalutamide (RR = 4.62, 95% CI: 2.65–8.03, I2 = 48.6%), but not with enzalutamide (RR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.96–1.39). Similarly, the risk of severe (grade ≥ 3) hypokalemia was significantly higher with ARAT treatment (RR = 4.54, 95% CI: 2.42–8.51, I2 = 48.4%); the risk signal was strongest in combination regimens (RR = 9.34, 95% CI: 1.71–50.90), although this estimate was based on limited data.ConclusionBased on our preliminary findings, ARAT therapies, particularly abiraterone and combination regimens, are significantly related to the increased risk of hypokalemia in patients with advanced prostate cancer. Vigilant monitoring and the proactive management of serum potassium levels are strongly warranted in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16108-w.

Introduction

Prostate cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in men globally [1–3]. The therapeutic landscape for advanced disease has been transformed by the introduction of novel androgen receptor axis-targeted therapies (ARATs), such as abiraterone acetate (Abi) and enzalutamide (Enza) [4, 5]. These agents, used in both hormone-sensitive (HSPC) and castration-resistant (CRPC) settings, have demonstrated significant survival benefits in pivotal phase III trials [6]. However, their expanded use has been accompanied by a growing recognition of associated adverse effects, among which electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypokalemia, are notable due to their potential to cause muscle weakness, cardiac arrhythmias, and increased mortality [7].

Hypokalemia has been reported across various ARAT trials [8–10], but its incidence and severity appear variable, potentially influenced by the specific agent, disease stage, and concomitant therapies. While the mineralocorticoid-related effects of abiraterone are a well-known contributor [11], a comprehensive and comparative assessment of hypokalemia risk across different ARAT regimens is lacking. Existing meta-analyses often pool all ARATs together or focus on a broader spectrum of adverse events, potentially obscuring regimen-specific risks [12, 13]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to quantitatively synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to precisely evaluate and compare the risk of hypokalemia associated with abiraterone, enzalutamide, and their combination in patients with prostate cancer.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic literature search was performed in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [14]. Electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, were searched from their inception up to Jan, 2026. The literature search was restricted to English-language publications. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text words, primarily restricted to the Title/Abstract fields. Key search concepts included "prostate cancer," "abiraterone," "enzalutamide," "androgen receptor antagonist," "hypokalemia," and "randomized controlled trial." The complete, reproducible search strings and logic for PubMed database are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. Reference lists of relevant reviews and included articles were manually screened to identify additional studies."

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) phase II or III RCTs; (2) enrolled adult patients with prostate cancer (any stage); (3) compared an ARAT (abiraterone, enzalutamide, or their combination) plus standard care against placebo/control plus standard care; (4) reported incidence data for hypokalemia (all-grade and/or grade ≥ 3) in both arms. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-randomized studies, single-arm trials, or observational studies; (2) trials without a placebo or active control group; (3) studies not reporting hypokalemia as an outcome; (4) duplicate publications or secondary analyses of already included trials.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators independently extracted data using a standardized, pre-piloted data extraction form. The extracted variables included: first author, publication year, trial identifier (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov number), study phase, patient population setting (e.g., mHSPC, mCRPC), intervention and control regimens, sample sizes, number of hypokalemia events (all-grade and grade ≥ 3), and median follow-up duration. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or adjudication by a third senior reviewer.

The methodological quality of the included RCTs was evaluated using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0) [15]. This tool assesses five distinct domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results. The overall risk of bias for each trial was ultimately categorized as "low risk," "some concerns," or "high risk."

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was the risk of all-grade hypokalemia, while the secondary endpoint was the risk of severe (grade ≥ 3) hypokalemia. For both endpoints, pooled risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. To pool the dichotomous event data, the Mantel–Haenszel (M-H) method was employed, as it provides more robust estimates for sparse data and unbalanced sample sizes compared to the inverse variance method. For trials reporting zero events in one arm, a standard continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to the cell frequencies to enable variance and RR calculations.

Given the anticipated clinical heterogeneity stemming from diverse disease settings and specific ARAT regimens, a random-effects model using the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) method was selected a priori as the primary analytical framework. To further validate the methodological robustness of our findings—particularly for the sparse grade ≥ 3 events—a sensitivity analysis utilizing the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator was also conducted. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test (with a significance threshold of P < 0.10) and quantified via the I2 statistic (with 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively). Pre-specified subgroup analyses were stratified by the specific ARAT regimen (abiraterone, enzalutamide, or their combination).

Notably, for multi-arm platform trials (i.e., STAMPEDE) contributing multiple distinct intervention groups, we meticulously evaluated the control group allocations to preclude unit-of-analysis errors and double-counting. Because the STAMPEDE reports utilized distinct, contemporaneous control cohorts corresponding to the specific randomization periods of each active treatment arm, these comparisons were treated and entered as independent pairs in all pooled analyses. Due to the limited number of eligible reports (n = 9), meta-regression was not performed to explore covariates, as it would lack adequate statistical power. Publication bias was assessed visually via contour-enhanced funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s test (performed when ≥ 10 comparisons were available). All statistical analyses were two-sided and performed using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), primarily utilizing the meta and metafor packages.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The initial database search yielded 5,422 records. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 298 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 9 published reports [8–10, 16–21] detailing 8 unique RCTs involving 9298 patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Notably, the STAMPEDE platform trial contributed multiple independent treatment comparisons across different reports. The key characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The trials encompassed major studies such as STAMPEDE (multiple arms), LATITUDE, COU-AA-301, COU-AA-302, and AFFIRM, covering disease states from non-metastatic HSPC to metastatic CRPC. Sample sizes ranged from 197 to 1,199 patients. The control arms predominantly received placebo plus androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) or standard care.

Risk of bias assessment

The assessment of the risk of bias for the included studies is detailed in Supplemental Table 2. Overall, the methodological quality of the included trials was generally robust. The majority of the trials demonstrated a low risk of bias across most assessed domains, particularly concerning the randomization process and the selection of the reported result. While a few trials were rated as having "some concerns" in specific domains, no single study was judged to have a high overall risk of bias. This indicates that the synthesized evidence underlying our meta-analysis is of satisfactory methodological quality.

Risk of all-grade hypokalemia

A total of 10 treatment comparisons from the 8 included RCTs reported data on all-grade hypokalemia. The pooled meta-analysis demonstrated that ARAT treatment was associated with a significantly increased risk of all-grade hypokalemia compared to control (RR = 3.11, 95% CI: 1.88–5.15, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). In the prespecified subgroup analysis by ARAT regimen, abiraterone (seven comparisons) was associated with a significantly increased risk (RR = 3.25, 95% CI: 1.75–6.02, P < 0.001; I2 = 88.3%). The combination of abiraterone plus enzalutamide (two comparisons) demonstrated a higher risk (RR = 4.62, 95% CI: 2.65–8.03, P < 0.001; I2 = 48.6%). Conversely, data for enzalutamide, which now exclusively relies on the AFFIRM trial, showed no statistically significant increase in risk (RR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.96–1.39, P = 0.13). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (chi2 = 29.19, P < 0.001).

Risk of grade ≥ 3 hypokalemia

Seven treatment comparisons provided data on severe (grade ≥ 3) hypokalemia. The overall pooled analysis indicated that ARAT treatment significantly increased the risk of grade ≥ 3 hypokalemia (RR = 4.54, 95% CI: 2.42–8.51, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 48.4%). Subgroup analysis revealed that the combination regimen showed the most pronounced risk increase (RR = 9.34, 95% CI: 1.71–50.90, P = 0.01), although the estimate was based on limited data. The risk was also significantly elevated in the abiraterone subgroup (RR = 3.95, 95% CI: 1.53–10.19, P = 0.005) and the enzalutamide subgroup (based solely on the AFFIRM trial: RR = 4.99, 95% CI: 2.00–12.41, P < 0.001).Fig. 3Forest plot of grade ≥ 3 Hypokalemia for different ARATs. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding individual studies (leave-one-out analysis) were performed. The resulting influence plots demonstrated that the omission of any single study did not significantly alter the primary pooled risk ratios for either all-grade (Supplemental Fig. 1) or grade ≥ 3 (Supplemental Fig. 2) hypokalemia, indicating high robustness of the findings. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses utilizing the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) model yielded results consistent with the primary DL model, confirming that the conclusions were not sensitive to the choice of the between-study variance estimator. For publication bias, visual inspection of funnel plots for all-grade hypokalemia (Supplemental Fig. 3) and grade ≥ 3 (Supplemental Fig. 4) hypokalemia were performed. For all-grade hypokalemia, Egger's test indicated the potential presence of small-study effects or publication bias (P = 0.014). As predefined in our methodology, formal statistical testing using Egger's test was not performed for grade ≥ 3 hypokalemia due to the limited number of comparisons (n < 10).

Discussion

This meta-analysis provides a detailed and comparative quantification of the hypokalemia risk associated with contemporary androgen receptor axis-targeted therapies (ARATs) in patients with prostate cancer. The key finding is that while ARATs, as a class, significantly increase this risk, the magnitude is highly regimen-dependent. Abiraterone-based therapies, particularly when combined with enzalutamide, carry the highest risk, whereas enzalutamide alone appears to have a minimal and non-significant effect on serum potassium levels.

The biological plausibility for this discrepancy lies in their distinct mechanisms of action. Abiraterone is a CYP17A1 inhibitor that suppresses androgen synthesis but simultaneously leads to a compensatory upstream accumulation of mineralocorticoid precursors, such as deoxycorticosterone [22]. This mineralocorticoid excess promotes renal potassium wasting and sodium retention, clinically manifesting as hypokalemia, hypertension, and fluid overload [23, 24]. Although the prophylactic administration of low-dose corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone or prednisolone) is standard practice with abiraterone to suppress adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and mitigate these effects [25], the dosage variations across trials directly impact the degree of ACTH suppression. For instance, earlier trials in the mCRPC setting (e.g., COU-AA-301, COU-AA-302) utilized prednisone 5 mg twice daily (bid), whereas later trials in the mHSPC setting (e.g., LATITUDE, STAMPEDE) adopted a 5 mg once daily (qd) regimen to minimize long-term glucocorticoid toxicity. A 5 mg qd regimen may result in less complete suppression of mineralocorticoid precursors, serving as a crucial source of clinical heterogeneity and explaining why the risk of hypokalemia cannot be entirely eliminated. In contrast, enzalutamide is a pure androgen receptor signaling inhibitor with no known direct effect on steroidogenic enzymes or mineralocorticoid pathways [26], which accounts for its highly favorable electrolyte profile.

The substantial statistical heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis (I2 > 80%), particularly within the all-grade abiraterone subgroup, warrants a cautious interpretation of the absolute effect sizes. As the limited number of eligible trials precluded the use of meta-regression, several critical clinical and methodological confounders driving this heterogeneity must be acknowledged. First, the included trials spanned a wide spectrum of disease states—from non-metastatic HSPC to heavily pretreated mCRPC—which inherently involve varying baseline patient frailties, cumulative toxicities, and background ADT regimens. Second, variations in trial design, particularly the contrast between open-label studies and strict double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, might have introduced detection bias for adverse events. Third, the median follow-up durations varied extensively across trials (ranging from 3.9 to 96 months), inevitably impacting the cumulative incidence of all-grade events. Consequently, while the pooled relative risk robustly demonstrates a generalized signal of harm, the absolute magnitude of this risk should be interpreted by cautiously factoring in the patient's specific disease stage, background therapy, and anticipated treatment duration.

Our findings have direct and actionable clinical implications. For patients initiating abiraterone, especially those with predisposing factors (e.g., concurrent diuretic use, poor nutritional intake, or chronic diarrhea), rigorous baseline and periodic monitoring of serum potassium is essential. Proactive management, including dietary counseling and timely potassium supplementation, should be prioritized. The choice of ARAT must also be individualized based on a patient's comorbid conditions; for instance, in patients with heart failure or a history of arrhythmias where electrolyte stability is paramount, enzalutamide may be the preferred agent. Furthermore, the pronounced risk signal observed with the abiraterone and enzalutamide combination therapy highlights the absolute necessity for vigilant monitoring within clinical trials exploring such intensive regimens.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the protocol for this systematic review was not prospectively registered on PROSPERO, which may slightly limit methodological transparency, although we strictly adhered to the PRISMA guidelines throughout the process. Second, the analysis relies on study-level data rather than individual patient data, restricting our ability to adjust for patient-level risk predictors. Third, the number of trials in the combination and enzalutamide subgroups was relatively small, indicating that more data are needed to definitively confirm their effects. Fourth, because the included trials spanned over a decade, different versions of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, e.g., v3.0 to v5.0) were utilized. However, since the specific serum potassium thresholds defining all-grade and severe (grade ≥ 3) hypokalemia remained uniform across these versions, the risk of misclassification bias is considered minimal. Finally, Egger's test suggested the potential presence of publication bias or small-study effects for all-grade hypokalemia (P = 0.014). This asymmetry is likely driven not only by the true non-publication of negative trials but also by the profound clinical heterogeneity discussed above. Nevertheless, our leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed that the primary pooled results remained statistically robust.

Moving forward, to bridge the remaining evidence gaps, future research must evolve beyond generic trial designs. Prospective studies are urgently warranted to identify vulnerable patient subpopulations—particularly those with baseline cardiovascular comorbidities—who are at the highest risk for severe hypokalemia. Furthermore, there is a critical need to establish standardized, evidence-based protocols for prophylactic potassium supplementation and optimal monitoring frequencies. Incorporating real-world data (RWD) and pharmacovigilance studies will be essential to evaluate the long-term cardiovascular consequences of ARAT-induced electrolyte disturbances outside the highly controlled environment of clinical trials.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis confirms that novel androgen receptor axis-targeted therapies (ARATs) significantly increase the risk of hypokalemia in patients with advanced prostate cancer. Importantly, this risk profile is highly regimen-specific. While enzalutamide poses a substantially lower threat, the risk is significantly elevated with abiraterone use. Furthermore, the signal was strongest in combination regimens, although the estimate was based on limited data. These findings underscore the critical importance of regimen-specific awareness, proactive monitoring, and the prompt management of electrolyte imbalances to ensure the safe delivery of these life-prolonging therapies. Future research should focus on identifying patient subgroups at the highest risk and developing standardized monitoring protocols.
