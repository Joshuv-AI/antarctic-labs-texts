Ingestion of a lipid-rich meat matrix blunts the postexercise increase of myofibrillar protein synthesis rates in healthy adults: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Žan Zupančič, Andrew T Askow, Takeshi M Barnes, Max T Deutz, Alexander V Ulanov, Ryan N Dilger, Anna C Dilger, Jared W Willard, Richard WA Mackenzie, Jocelyn E Harseim, Diego Hernández-Saavedra, Nicholas A Burd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is considerable variation in the anabolic action of ingesting protein-dense foods on the stimulation of postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates (MPS) despite ingesting similar amounts of protein and essential amino acids (EAA).ObjectivesThis study aims to determine the effects of consuming high-fat pork (HFP), low-fat pork (LFP), or a carbohydrate control (CHO) on the MPS response.MethodsIn a semicrossover design, 16 physically active adults (25 ± 5 y; 25.0 ± 2.3 kg/m2; 12M, 4F) received primed-constant infusions of L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine and performed an acute bout of resistance exercise. Following exercise, participants ingested either HFP (266 kcal, 20 g protein, 20.6 g fat), LFP (120 kcal, 20 g protein, 4.4 g fat), or CHO (266 kcal, 0 g protein, 0 g fat, 73.3 g carbohydrate). Repeated blood and muscle samples were collected at rest and throughout 0–5 h postexercise recovery to measure plasma variables and MPS. Linear mixed-effects models with time and condition as fixed factors were used to detect differences.ResultsBoth HFP and LFP conditions increased MPS above postabsorptive values (P = 0.028 and P < 0.001, respectively), with LFP eliciting a greater postexercise MPS {0.106 ± 0.026 %/h [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.088, 0.118]} than HFP [0.072 ± 0.027 %/h (95% CI: 0.057, 0.087); P = 0.030] and CHO [0.056 ± 0.035 %/h (95% CI: 0.41, 0.71); P < 0.001]. The absolute change in MPS from basal was significantly correlated with peak EAA and leucine concentrations (r = 0.4638, P = 0.017; r = 0.4211, P = 0.032).ConclusionsLFP stimulated MPS more than HFP or CHO conditions. Our work demonstrated that other nutrients in the food matrix, beyond total ingested protein or EAAs, can impact the regulation of MPS in healthy adults.This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05876299.Keywords: skeletal muscle, resistance exercise, pork, protein quality, leucine, protein recommendations, whole foods

Introduction

Dietary protein-derived amino acids are the main substrates to stimulate postprandial muscle protein synthesis rates and remodeling to support an adequate quality and quantity of muscle mass in the longer term [1]. There has been an increased interest in defining the potential anabolic role of nonprotein nutritional components in the regulation of muscle protein synthesis rates to better characterize the remodeling process in response to whole-food or whole-pattern interventions [2]. Past efforts have shown that mixing isolated nutrients, such as protein and fat [3] or protein and carbohydrate [4,5], does not further modulate the postprandial stimulation of muscle protein synthesis rates compared with protein ingestion alone in healthy adults. These studies have been fundamental to underpin the role of dietary-derived essential amino acids (EAA) as the primary stimulus for postprandial muscle protein synthesis rates [6], and not the energy content of a protein meal [7] or postprandial insulinemia [8]. However, the potential regulatory role of other macronutrients within a whole-food matrix on potentiating the anabolic action of a meal remains unclear [9,10].

We [10,11] and several others [[12], [13], [14], [15]] have attempted to further characterize the role of the food matrix in stimulating postexercise muscle protein synthesis rates. The food matrix describes the interaction between nutritive (e.g., proteins, fats, vitamins, etc.) and nonnutritive (e.g., physical structure) components of foods, which can impact how food is digested, absorbed, and modulate the subsequent metabolic effects [16]. For example, we have previously demonstrated that the ingestion of a whole-egg matrix was more potent for the stimulation of postexercise myofibrillar protein synthesis rates when compared with an isonitrogenous amount of separated egg whites in young adults [10]. Additionally, we have demonstrated a moderate treatment effect (d = 0.44) of whole-food salmon matrix on the postexercise stimulation of myofibrillar protein synthesis rates when compared with ingesting the same nutrients as an isolated mixture beverage in young adults [11]. These studies support the thesis that not all protein foods result in the same postexercise rise in myofibrillar protein synthesis, even when consumed in iso-nitrogenous amounts [10,11].

In this study, we were particularly interested in testing the anabolic properties of other lipid-rich foods, such as meat, during recovery from resistance exercise. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to assess the impact of ingesting high-fat ground pork (HFP), low-fat ground pork (LFP), or a carbohydrate beverage (CHO) on the stimulation of myofibrillar protein synthesis rates during recovery from resistance exercise in young adults. As a secondary outcome, we examined the phosphorylation of signaling targets within the Akt-mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) cascade to assess the influence of the food matrix/lipid composition on exercise-induced anabolism. We hypothesized that the anabolic response to exercise would be potentiated in the following manner: HFP > LFP > CHO conditions.

Methods

Participants and ethical approval

Sixteen healthy adults (males = 12 and females = 4; mean ± SD: age: 25 ± 4.5 y; BMI: 25 ± 2.3 kg/m2) volunteered to participate in the study (Figure 1 for CONSORT diagram). Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. Baseline physical activity was assessed using the Godin-Shephard Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire and International Physical Activity Questionnaires (IPAQ) [17]. Only participants scoring >14 (Godin-Shepherd) and >1500 (IPAQ) were considered for participation in the study. All participants were deemed healthy based on their responses to a health and medical screening questionnaire and were weight stable for at least the preceding 6 mo prior to enrollment in the study. Fourteen participants performed both infusion trials, and 2 completed 1 infusion trial in our clinical laboratory at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign from July 2023 to April 2024. Female participants performed the trials during the assumed follicular phase (i.e., the 3rd to the 12th day of their menstrual cycle). Prior to participation, all participants were informed of the experimental procedures, the purpose of the study, and the potential risks associated with involvement prior to signing an informed consent form approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (IRB #23257). All procedures involving human subjects conformed to standards for the use of human participants in research as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial was preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT05876299. The reported anabolic signaling data was not a preregistered outcome. It was completed post hoc to provide greater mechanistic insight into our primary outcome.

Experimental design

A randomized, semicrossover design was used, whereby participants performed 2 of the 3 potential comparisons, to test our primary aim. We acknowledge that this model does not allow for a “complete” within-subject design, as not all participants completed all 3 treatment conditions. We chose this design as it still provides reduced variability compared with a complete between-subject design, which may introduce greater variability due to genetics, motivation, etc., as well as reduce participant burden due to the greater number of biopsies required otherwise.

Pretesting

One week prior to the first experimental infusion, participants reported to the laboratory in the morning for preliminary testing in the postabsorptive state. Participants’ height and body mass were measured prior to the determination of blood pressure using an automated blood pressure cuff (HEM-907XL; Omron Healthcare Inc.). Body composition was determined by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (QDR 4500A; Hologic). Resting metabolic rate was measured using standard procedures [18]. Finally, participants underwent measurement of maximal strength by assessment of 10-repetition maximums (10RM) for the leg press and leg extension as described previously [19]. Following a preliminary testing session, participants were randomly assigned to 1 of the 3 conditions (i.e., HFP, LFP, or CHO condition) in a counterbalanced fashion for the first experimental infusion trial. Block randomization stratified by sex (male and female) was used to ensure balanced allocation of participants across conditions and trials.

Dietary and physical activity guidance

To assess baseline habitual dietary intakes, participants were instructed to record their dietary intake over 3 predefined consecutive days (1 weekend, 2 weekdays) as 24 h recalls prior to the first infusion trial using the Automated Self-Administered 24-h Dietary Assessment Tool (version 2022; National Cancer Institute) [20]. Participants were also provided with a standardized meal by the research team to consume in the evening prior to each infusion trial. Meals contained ∼30% of estimated caloric needs, with 25% kcal from protein, 50% from carbohydrate, and 25% from fat. Participants were also instructed to refrain from exercise and alcohol consumption for 72 and 48 h, respectively, prior to each infusion trial.

Infusion protocol

Participants arrived at the laboratory at ∼07:00 after an overnight (>10 h) fast, and an intravenous catheter was inserted into an antecubital vein before collecting a baseline arterialized blood sample. Subsequently, a primed-constant infusion of L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine (prime: 2 μmol/kg; 0.05 μmol/kg/min) was initiated and maintained throughout the trial. A second intravenous catheter was placed in a contralateral arm for repeated blood sampling and remained patent by a 0.9% saline drip. Repeated blood and muscle samples were collected throughout the infusions for measurement of baseline (trial 1) and postprandial (trials 1 and 2) myofibrillar protein synthesis rates (Figure 2). After collecting the resting muscle biopsy sample at t = −30 min for both trials, the participants performed an acute bout of resistance exercise to be consistent with physical activity guidelines [21], and food matrix effects may be most potent during recovery from exercise [16]. The acute resistance exercise bout consisted of 4 sets of 10 repetitions at 90% of 10RM for both the leg press and leg extension exercises. After completion of the exercise, participants consumed 1 of the 3 treatment conditions (Table 2). For trial 2, the infusion was initiated 1 h prior to the first biopsy to ensure isotopic steady state. Muscle biopsies were performed with a Bergström needle and obtained from the vastus lateralis under local anesthetic using standard procedures [11]. Volume load (i.e., repetitions × load) was matched between crossover trials. The time between crossover trials was 9.8 ± 5.2 d.

Treatment conditions

The energy content and macronutrient composition of treatments are shown in Table 2. The ground pork was processed at the Meat Science Laboratory, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. A mix of lean and fatty pork trimmings was used to specifically formulate the 2 treatment conditions (i.e., HFP and LFP), which were subsequently shaped into patties and frozen for individual use at experimental infusions. Compositional analysis was performed on uncooked pork for protein, amino acid, fat, and carbohydrate concentrations based on methods established by the Association of Official Analytical Collaboration [22]. The portion size of the ground pork was selected to provide an amount of protein (20 g; or ∼3 oz-equivalents of protein foods within the USDA’s MyPlate) that has been shown to robustly stimulate postexercise myofibrillar protein synthesis rates while also limiting excess whole-body amino acid oxidation [23] and is also consistent with our past efforts [10,11]. The differential quantities of fat between pork conditions were selected to approximate typical 80/20 ground pork or lean ground pork products. Ground pork patties were prepared in the oven for ∼30 min at 200°C or until the internal temperature reached 75°C. For the CHO treatment, energy content was matched to the HFP condition using a commercially available CHO (Gatorade; PepsiCo), which was dissolved in 600 mL of water on the day of the infusion trial. The CHO condition served as an exercise control by allowing for the measurement of postexercise stimulation of myofibrillar protein synthesis independent from the provision of dietary amino acids.

Plasma analyses

Plasma insulin concentrations were determined using commercially available ELISA [Alpco Diagnostics, limits of quantification (LOQ): 3.0–200 μIU/mL]. Plasma glucose concentrations were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (2900 Stat Plus; YSI Life Sciences, LOQ: 5–900 mg/dL). Plasma samples (5 μL) were deproteinized using 190 μL of a mixture of ddH2O:acetonitrile:isopropyl alcohol (2:3:3 v/v/v) and spiked with 5 μL DL-p-chlorophenylalanine (0.01 mg/mL), followed by centrifugation at 20,817× g for 10 min at 4°C. Following transfer of 100 μL supernatant to a new tube, samples were dried under vacuum before resuspension in 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid in preparation for analysis. Plasma amino acid concentrations (LOQ: 2–2000 ng/mL) and enrichments (LOQ: 0.75–2000 ng/mL) were determined by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) using a 5500 QTRAP (SCIEX) and 1290 Infinity II LC system (Agilent Technologies, Inc.). Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using the Analyst 1.7 software. The LC separation was performed on a Hypersil GOLD C18 (ThermoFisher Scientific ) column (3 × 150 mm, 3 μm) with mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), and the flow rate was 0.45 mL/min. The autosampler and LC column temperatures were set at 4°C and 50°C, respectively, with an injection volume of 0.5 μL. Mass spectra were acquired under positive electrospray ionization. Calibration curves were built for the 0.05–5 μg/mL concentration range.

Plasma free fatty acids (FFAs) were extracted as follows: each sample (5 μL plasma) was spiked with 5 μL of internal standard mixture (C10:1-d19, C16:0-d5, C18:1-d17, C20:0-d2, C20:4-d5, C20:5-d5, C22:6-d5; 50 μg/mL) and extracted with 190 μL of methanol:chloroform (1:2 v/v) with BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene; 2 g/L), centrifuged at 20,817 ×g for 10 min at 4°C and 100 μL of supernatant was transferred to a glass autosampler vial for injection. 1 μL was injected into the LC-MS system consisting of an Agilent 6495C triple quadrupole (LC/TQ) mass spectrometer (LOQ: 2.5–5000 ng/mL), an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system equipped with Waters ACQUITY Premier BEH C18 column (1.7 μm × 2.1 × 150 mm) (Waters). Fatty acids were separated using mobile phase A [5 mM ammonia acetate, water/methanol (7:3 v/v), 0.05% acetic acid] and B [5 mM ammonium acetate, isopropanol/methanol (4:6 v/v), 0.05% acetic acid]. Acquired chromatograms were evaluated using MassHunter Workstation 12.1 (Agilent Technologies, Inc.) software. To ensure assay quality control, we included human plasma samples with certified amino acid concentration (NIST1950), Bio IVT serum samples as external QC, and standard solutions in every batch for amino acid and fatty acid quantification. All samples were prepared identically. Additionally, we used blank samples with multiple instruments and methods analyses to monitor system contamination and carryover.

Muscle analyses

Myofibrillar protein-enriched fractions were extracted from ∼50 mg wet muscle using an ice-cold lysis buffer (Pierce RIPA (Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay) Buffer; ThermoFisher Scientific; 10 μL/mg) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor tablets (cOmplete and PhosSTOP; Roche Applied Science). Myofibrillar proteins were isolated by differential centrifugation and analyzed for 13C phenylalanine enrichments as described previously [24]. The remaining muscle homogenate was stored at −80°C for subsequent Western blot analysis for target proteins, which was completed as described previously [24]. The following primary antibodies were used to identify proteins of interest: rabbit anti-α-tubulin (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 2144), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein S6 kinase β1 (p70S6K; Thr389) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9205), rabbit anti-p70S6K (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9202), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein 4E-BP1 (Thr37/46)(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9459), rabbit anti-4E-BP1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9452), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein Akt (Ser473) (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 9271), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein Akt (Thr308) (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 4056), rabbit anti-Akt (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 9272), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein AMPKα (Thr172) (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 2531), phosphoribosomal protein anti-AMPKα (Thr172) (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 2532).

Calculations

Fractional synthesis rates (%/h) were calculated by standard precursor-product equations by dividing the change in myofibrillar protein-bound L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichment by a weighted average of the plasma precursor pool enrichment over time as described previously [24]. The net incremental AUC (iAUC) for plasma insulin, glucose, and amino acid concentrations was assessed over the 5-h postprandial period after meal consumption. The iAUC was calculated using the trapezoidal rule, with plasma concentrations t = 0 min as the baseline. Peak concentration values (Cmax) and time to peak (Tmax) were calculated for all plasma × time curves.

Statistical analyses

All data are presented as mean ± SD and/or mean difference [95% confidence interval (CI)]. A priori power analysis was conducted using GPOWER version 3.1.9.2 based on previous research [10,11,25], showing large, standardized effects. Our power analysis determined that a sample size of 10 participants per condition was adequate to detect differences in postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates between conditions when using a repeated-measures statistical test (P < 0.05, 80% power, f = 0.45). Because our sample size calculation was based on a simpler repeated-measures analysis of variance, it should be considered as an estimate of power for the more complex linear mixed-effects models used in our final analysis, particularly for the time × condition interaction.

Differences in blood variables, myofibrillar protein synthesis rates, and anabolic signaling were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with time and condition as fixed factors and the participant-specific random intercept. Plasma EAA, leucine, total amino acids (TAA), phenylalanine, insulin, and glucose iAUC were calculated for the postprandial period, and differences were determined using linear mixed-effects models with the condition as a fixed factor and the participant-specific random intercept. Bonferroni’s post hoc test was conducted when a significant main effect or interaction was identified. Bonferroni adjustments were applied separately for pairwise comparisons across conditions within each time point and for comparisons across time points within each condition to control the family-wise error rate at each level of comparison. Pearson's product-moment correlation was used to examine the relationship between changes in myofibrillar protein synthesis rates and amino acid/s Cmax. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. Normality was assessed via visual inspection of normal Q–Q plots and skewness/kurtosis values. Missing data were handled within the mixed-effects model framework. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 29.0.2.0; IBM Corporation).

Results

Exercise variables

There were no significant differences in resistance exercise volume load for leg press in the HFP (4894 ± 1813 kg), LFP (4576 ± 1823 kg), or CHO trials (5377 ± 1610 kg; P = 0.595). Similarly, leg extension volume load did not differ between the HFP (3000 ± 1097 kg), LFP (3051 ± 1156 kg), and CHO (3462 ± 846 kg; P = 0.561) trials.

Plasma glucose and insulin

Plasma glucose concentrations increased only in the CHO condition (all P < 0.001; Figure 3A). Significant differences in postprandial iAUC were observed between HFP and CHO conditions (P = 0.038); however, not between LFP and CHO (P = 0.338). Plasma insulin concentrations increased to a greater extent in the CHO, resulting in a greater iAUC for postprandial plasma insulin concentrations compared with HFP and LFP conditions (all P < 0.001; Figure 3B). Plasma amino acid concentrations

Plasma TAA, EAA, and leucine concentrations increased only in LFP and HFP conditions (Figure 4). TAA Cmax was greater in LFP (3182.3 ± 602.5 μmol/L) compared with CHO condition (2339.1 ± 363.5 μmol/L; P = 0.005). However, TAA Cmax was not different between the LFP and HFP conditions (2685.2 ± 440.4 μmol/L; P = 0.122). Plasma TAA iAUC was significantly greater in LFP and HFP compared with the CHO condition (P < 0.001; P = 0.004). Plasma EAA Cmax was greater in the LFP condition (1272.2 ± 171.7 μmol/L) when compared with HFP (1085.0 ± 123.5 μmol/L; P = 0.018) and CHO conditions (785.0 ± 75.4 μmol/L; P < 0.001). EAA Tmax did not differ between LFP (90.0 ± 24.5 min) and HFP conditions (108.0 ± 35.2 min; P = 0.262). Total postprandial plasma EAA iAUC was significantly greater in LFP and HFP conditions than in CHO (both P < 0.001). Leucine Cmax was significantly greater in LFP (134.0 ± 25.8 μmol/L) as compared with HFP (107.7 ± 18.2 μmol/L; P = 0.005) and CHO conditions (68.6 ± 9.2 μmol/L; P < 0.001). Moreover, leucine Tmax occurred earlier in LFP (90.0 ± 24.5 min) compared with the HFP condition (129.0 ± 40.1 min; P = 0.038). Plasma leucine iAUC was significantly greater in LFP and HFP compared with the CHO condition (both, P < 0.001). Phenylalanine Cmax was significantly greater in LFP (92.1 ± 33.2 μmol/L) compared with CHO (55.9 ± 9.0; P = 0.033), but not from HFP condition (70.8 ± 17.4 μmol/L; P = 0.188; Figure 5B). Postprandial phenylalanine iAUC was significantly greater in LFP than in the CHO condition (P = 0.003) and tended to be greater between HFP and the CHO condition (P = 0.059). Plasma enrichments

Postabsorptive plasma L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichment did not differ between treatments (P > 0.05; Figure 5A). Postprandial plasma L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichments were higher in the CHO compared with the HFP and LFP conditions (P < 0.05). For further details, see Supplemental Table 7.

Plasma FFAs

Plasma total FFA concentrations changed over time irrespective of condition (P < 0.001; Figure 6A). Plasma saturated FFA concentrations differed at 60 min between LFP and CHO (P = 0.017) (Figure 6B). Plasma unsaturated FFA concentrations increased above baseline in all conditions at 300 min (all, P < 0.05) (Figure 6C). Myofibrillar protein synthesis

Postabsorptive myofibrillar protein synthesis rates did not differ between conditions (P = 1.000). Postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates were elevated above baseline values for LFP (from 0.047 ± 0.013 to 0.103 ± 0.026 %/h; P < 0.001) and HFP conditions (from 0.044 ± 0.013 to 0.072 ± 0.027 %/h; P = 0.010). However, postprandial/postexercise myofibrillar protein synthesis rates were not elevated above baseline in the CHO condition (from 0.040 ± 0.007 to 0.056 ± 0.035%/h; P = 0.067; Figure 7A). Postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates were greater in LFP when compared with the HFP [MD (95% CI): 0.031 (0.005, 0.056) %/h; P = 0.016] and the CHO condition [MD (95% CI): 0.047 (0.021, 0.073) %/h; P < 0.001]. Postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates did not differ between HFP and CHO [MD (95% CI): 0.016 (−0.010, 0.042) %/h; P = 0.396]. Discussion

This is the first study to characterize the anabolic properties of ingesting ground pork containing higher or lower amounts of lipids in healthy adults. Particularly, the production of a lipid-rich meat matrix is distinct from other protein-dense foods such as chicken eggs [10] and salmon [11]. Although the latter are naturally formed, the meat matrix is created by blending lean and fat trimmings to achieve a specific fat-to-lean ratio. Here, we demonstrate that ingestion of LFP leads to a more rapid and pronounced peak in plasma EAA and leucine concentrations, resulting in a greater stimulation of postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates when compared with ingesting HFP and CHO. Moreover, the postexercise stimulation of myofibrillar protein synthesis rates did not differ between the HFP and CHO conditions, suggesting ingestion of a lipid-rich meal immediately postexercise may blunt the stimulation of muscle protein synthesis, potentially due to the blunted aminoacidemia (e.g., EAA/leucine Cmax) when compared with the ingestion of LFP.

The question then arises as to what components within the HFP matrix may have differentially regulated the anabolic response compared with the LFP matrix? Indeed, the nonnutrient components of the pork matrices were similarly manipulated in terms of their physical structure as both were ground and cooked using the same standardized procedures. This is relevant as the extent of meat processing [26] and heat treatment [27] has been shown to modulate the anabolic properties of the ingested food protein source. Therefore, the most straightforward answer is that differences in the lipid content of the pork conditions are known to result in slowed gastric emptying [28]. The latter likely impacted the differential postprandial aminoacidemia observed and the subsequent enhancement of the anabolic properties of ingesting LFP compared with HFP. For example, past efforts have demonstrated that fast-digested proteins that contain a higher proportion of leucine are particularly effective at stimulating a postprandial rise in muscle protein synthesis rates [29,30]. This concept has been coined the leucine trigger hypothesis and has largely only been pertinent when comparing isolated protein sources such as whey or casein [29]. Here, we demonstrated a more rapid and greater postprandial rise in plasma leucine concentrations in the LFP compared with the HFP condition (Figure 4B). We also observed a significant relationship between peak plasma EAA and leucine concentrations (Cmax) and the extent of stimulation of the myofibrillar protein synthetic response (Figure 7). Hence, the enhanced anabolic effectiveness of the LFP condition may have simply been related to the leucine trigger, particularly because both LFP and HFP demonstrated a similar total net exposure to postprandial amino acids (i.e., 0–5 h iAUC; Figure 4). It is worth noting, however, that past efforts examining other animal protein foods (e.g., eggs and dairy) have shown a diminished reliance on the leucine trigger to elicit a robust postprandial myofibrillar response [10,25], thereby demonstrating the unique nature of whole-food matrices in terms of how animal protein foods regulate postprandial myofibrillar protein synthesis rates. The pronounced postprandial rise in leucinemia in the HFP condition compared with the CHO condition (Figure 4) calls into question the relevance of the leucine trigger for the stimulation of myofibrillar protein synthesis rates in this context. The leucine trigger refers to a state initiated by the ingestion of dietary amino acids (i.e., LFP compared with HFP conditions). This is important because the mechanistic basis for the limits of the postexercise anabolic response varies after protein ingestion [29] compared with glucose supplementation [31]. The latter generally induces a decrease in plasma amino acid availability due to insulin [31]. If protein is not ingested, the intramuscular free amino acid pool is not replenished, which limits a sustained increase in myofibrillar protein synthesis rates during recovery from exercise [32]. Overall, our results further contribute to the idea that the food matrix effects modulate the anabolic properties of a particular protein food despite ingesting similar amounts of protein and EAA.

Few studies are available that examine the impact of circulating lipids on regulating the anabolic properties of elevated plasma amino acid concentrations. Stephens et al. [33] demonstrated that increasing baseline and fed-state plasma nonesterified fatty acid (NEFA) availability via lipid infusions induced an anabolic resistance of the postprandial muscle protein synthetic response in healthy young males. This lipid-induced anabolic resistance was likely underpinned by the accumulation of muscle intracellular lipids due to the prolonged elevation of plasma NEFA concentrations [33]; an effect that would not be mirrored under normal meal-like conditions [34]. Moreover, this lipid-induced anabolic resistance by infusion can be mitigated by a single bout of strenuous exercise in otherwise sedentary adults [35]. Therefore, based on our design, it seems that the blunting of the postexercise myofibrillar protein synthetic response in the HFP condition was likely due to an altered pattern of aminoacidemia (e.g., a ↓leucine trigger) as opposed to a “true” acute lipid-induced anabolic resistance as noted by lipid infusion studies [33].

The examination of the Akt-mTOR signaling, a key pathway in the regulation of muscle protein synthesis [36], provided very little mechanistic insight into the differential anabolic responses between the HFP and LFP conditions. This is not completely surprising because mTOR signaling is commonly disconnected from changes in myofibrillar protein synthesis, especially when examining protein phosphorylation at later recovery time points (i.e., 300 min) [[37], [38], [39]]. Here, we observed that the phosphorylation of p70S6K at Thr389 increased above baseline regardless of condition, which is consistent with the potent effect of resistance exercise on prolonged mRNA translation events during recovery, regardless of protein intake [40,41]. Indeed, it is generally considered that p70S6K activation provides more mechanistic insight into the regulation of muscle protein synthesis rates compared with other upstream proteins of mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin), such as Akt [37]. However, we demonstrated a differential activation of Akt at Ser473 and Thr308, as well as AMPK at Thr172 between conditions (Figure 8). Akt has been shown to be particularly responsive to nutrients [42] and higher plasma insulin concentrations [8]. As such, the higher plasma insulin concentrations in the CHO condition likely contributed to the increased phosphorylation of Akt at Ser473, which was also significantly more phosphorylated compared with the LFP condition. Notably, this increased phosphorylated state on Ser473 was not observed in the phosphorylation of Akt on Thr308, which may be attributed to the distinct activation loops of Akt on Thr308 and Ser473 [43]. Similarly, the increased phosphorylation of AMPK at Thr172 in the HFP conditions may relate to the energy/lipid content differences in the ingested pork conditions. Past efforts have shown that palmitic acid and linoleic acid fatty acids increased AMPK activation and AMPK phosphorylation at Thr172 in L6 skeletal muscle myotubes [44]. Both fatty acids are contained in higher amounts in pork fat [45]. Nevertheless, the difference in higher intake of fatty acids in the HFP condition did not correspond to significant differences in plasma fatty acid concentrations between conditions, and elevation in postprandial triglycerides does not appear to be a significant modulator of muscle protein synthesis rates [3,34]. This again leads us to the thesis that differential plasma amino acid profiles were likely the most significant player in modulating the anabolic properties of ingested HFP or LFP.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the ingestion of LFP enhanced the stimulation of postexercise rates of myofibrillar protein synthesis compared with HFP or a CHO beverage. These results contradict our past efforts demonstrating the enhanced anabolic action of a lipid- and protein-dense food matrix on the regulation of postexercise myofibrillar protein synthesis rates [10], which underlines the unique nature of each individual animal food matrix, particularly when ingesting protein in relatively modest amounts (∼20 g protein). Although this study provides important insights into the impact of ground pork lipid composition on the regulation of myofibrillar protein synthesis rates, its generalizability may be limited to healthy young adults. Hence, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to other groups, such as clinical populations and older adults.
