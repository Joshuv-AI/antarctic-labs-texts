Nine Years of Experience With Bonebridge: Long‐Term Clinical and Audiological Outcomes
Authors: Tallita dos Santos Souza Boaventura, Eduardo Boaventura Oliveira, Valdeia Vieira de Oliveira, Rubens Vuono de Brito Neto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe active bone conduction hearing implant Bonebridge is a therapeutic option for hearing rehabilitation of patients with conductive and mixed hearing loss, as well as for those with unilateral sensorineural hearing loss. It has demonstrated satisfactory audiological gain, comparable to conventional hearing aids and percutaneous bone‐anchored hearing implants, with low rates of surgical and postoperative complications. However, long‐term follow‐up data are scarce.MethodsThis retrospective, observational, and longitudinal study included 26 patients who underwent Bonebridge implantation between 2014 and 2021 at the Hospital for Rehabilitation of Craniofacial Anomalies, University of São Paulo. We reviewed medical records, audiological evaluations, and satisfaction questionnaires collected at activation, 12 months post‐surgery, and at the latest assessment and also analyzed the incidence of clinical and technical complications during follow‐up.ResultsThe mean follow‐up period was 68.8 months (±21.5), ranging from 24 to 108 months. Functional gain decreased, while Sentence Recognition Threshold improved over time; effective gain and Speech Recognition Score remained stable. User satisfaction also remained stable, except in the service and costs domains of the SADL questionnaire.ConclusionBonebridge provides safe and effective long‐term audiological rehabilitation for adults and children with conductive or mixed hearing loss, with stable outcomes over time.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: bone‐anchored prosthesis, conductive hearing loss, correction of hearing impairment, long‐term care, mixed hearing loss, patient‐reported outcome measures

Introduction

Patients with conductive hearing loss (CHL) or mixed hearing loss (MHL) who cannot use conventional HA are usually fitted with bone conduction devices (BCDs). Percutaneous bone‐anchored hearing devices (pBAHDs) may present skin complications and extrusion due to failure in osseointegration. Despite a low prevalence of approximately 5%, postoperative wound infection around the pin has a significant negative impact on the clinical use and appeal of the percutaneous system [1].

Transcutaneous bone‐anchored hearing devices (tBAHDs) include different types of passive prostheses, where a passive magnetic implant is stimulated transcutaneously by an external stimulator, and active systems like the Bonebridge (BB) (MED‐EL Corporation, Innsbruck, Austria). The system consists of a bone conduction implant (BCI) fixed by two self‐tapping titanium screws, with a floating mass transducer (BC‐FMT) positioned under intact skin containing a magnet that holds the audio processor over the implant, which does not require osseointegration.

Implant location decisions (sinodural angle, the retrosigmoid area, or middle fossa) depend on anatomy and surgical history. The procedure can be conducted either under general or local anesthesia; it is safe and straightforward, especially compared to middle ear prostheses.

The literature shows favorable results for the use of BB for the auditory rehabilitation of patients with CHL and/or MHL, as well as for patients with SSD, with satisfactory audiological gain comparable to HA and pBAHD and low rates of surgical and postoperative complications. However, long‐term follow‐up data are scarce.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This is a retrospective, nonrandomized, longitudinal study designed as a subject‐controlled study. Patients who underwent surgical implantation of BB (Model BCI 601) at the Hospital for Rehabilitation of Craniofacial Anomalies of the University of São Paulo (HRAC‐USP) between 2014 and 2021 were included. Medical records were evaluated, and data collection was conducted between January and March 2025. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of São Paulo (FMUSP). Informed Consent Terms have been obtained for all participants.

Audiological Assessment

Pure‐tone audiometry thresholds (frequencies from 250 to 8000 Hz bilaterally for air conduction and 500 to 4000 Hz for bone conduction) and audiometric thresholds in a sound field, at frequencies from 500 to 4000 Hz, were obtained, with the calculation of functional gain (FG) (the difference in thresholds without and with the device) and effective gain (EG) (the difference between thresholds with the device and bone conduction thresholds), as well as data from a sound field Speech Recognition Score (SRS) obtained in silence at 65 dBHL [2] and the Sentence Recognition Threshold (SRT) in silence [3]. The implant was fitted and programmed by an experienced audiologist, and individual adjustments were made at each visit based on the patient's responses to obtain comfortable hearing levels.

Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures

The Hearing Handicap Inventory for Adults (HHIAs) [4] consists of 25 items divided into two subscales: social and emotional. Scores can range from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate worse perceived restriction related to their hearing impairment.

The Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit (APHAB) assesses the benefits provided by hearing devices [5]. It consists of 24 questions divided into 4 assessment subscales: ease of communication in relatively favorable environments (EC), communication in reverberant environments (RV), communication in locations with high background noise (BN), and discomfort to intense environmental sounds (AV).

The Satisfaction with Amplification in Daily Life (SADL) questionnaire for Brazilian Portuguese [6] contains 15 questions that assess the multidimensional nature of satisfaction with the devices across four main domains: Positive effects, service and costs, negative factors, and personal image.

Data Analysis

Objective and subjective audiological gain with BB was evaluated at three moments: activation of the prosthesis, 1 year of use, and the most recent evaluation. The statistical significance level adopted was 5% (p ≤ 0.05), and data analysis was performed using Jamovi software, version 2.6. The theoretical basis for statistical analysis followed the methodological guidelines detailed by Field [7]. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify the assumption of normality. Repeated measures ANOVA and the Tukey post hoc test were applied for normally distributed data, and the Friedman test and the Durbin‐Conover post hoc test were used for non‐normal data.

FG and EG

The mean FG at frequencies of 500, 1, 2, and 4 kHz (PTA4) at the time of activation was 26.9 dB (SD ±4.6), 28.5 dB (SD ±3.4) after 1 year and 25.6 dB (SD ±4.4) at the most recent evaluation. A statistically significant reduction was observed between the 12 months and the latest evaluation (p = 0.034).

The analysis of FG by frequency (Table 2) revealed a statistically significant difference only at the frequency of 3 kHz, between the moments of activation and the latest evaluation (p = 0.023), and between 12 months and the latest evaluation (p = 0.015).

The mean EG (PTA4) at the time of activation was −16.7 dB (SD ±8.5), varying to −14.6 dB (SD ±9.2) after 1 year, and −17 dB (SD ±8) at the most recent evaluation, with no significant difference. In the comparative analysis by frequency (Table 2), a significant reduction was observed between the moment of activation and the latest evaluation at the frequency of 500 Hz (p = 0.052) and at the frequency of 3 kHz (p = 0.059); and between the 12‐month evaluation and the latest evaluation in the frequencies of 2 kHz (p = 0.043) and 3 kHz (p < 0.001).

Speech Recognition in Silence

The mean values of the SRS were 90.9% (SD ±7.6) at the time of activation, 91.4% (SD ±7.3) at 12 months, and 91.2% (SD ±8.5) at the most recent evaluation, with no significant statistical differences over time. Alternately, the SRT (dBHL) at the time of activation was 37.4 (SD ±9.5), 34.5 (SD ±11.2) after 1 year, and 30.2 (SD ±7.5) at the most recent evaluation, with a statistically significant reduction between the activation moment and the latest evaluation (p = 0.001).

Clinical and Technical Complications

Most incidents were related to technical issues, with overall device failure being the most frequent (12 events). Other technical problems included breakage or physical damage to the processor (six events), incorrect or intermittent operation/heating (four events), loss of the processor (three events) and audio problems, such as feedback or noise (three events). Among the clinical incidents, which occurred in 38.5% of patients (n = 10), hyperemia, redness, or local infection at the implant site (nine events), and mild cranioencephalic trauma (one event) were highlighted. Incidents related to adaptation and use of the device were also reported, such as difficulty adapting or temporarily ineffective use (two events) and problems with the magnet's fixation, including low fixation or positional change (two events).

Discussion

The mean FG of 26.9 dB at activation, 28.5 dB at 12 months of BB use, and 25.6 dB at the latest evaluation are similar to the data described by Cywka [8], who found a mean FG of 27.0 dB NA (SD = 10.7) in a study evaluating 42 patients with CHL or MHL implanted with BB, with a 12‐month follow‐up, and slightly below the average of 32.7 ± 16 dB found by Magele et al. [9] in the systematic review published in 2018. Logistical constraints prevented the periodic replacement of microphone covers, although device integrity was monitored through visual inspections and patient education on basic cleaning protocols to mitigate the effects of device wear; however, this limitation can impact sound quality over time. In addition, to prioritize patient‐centered comfort and real‐world satisfaction, programming was adjusted at each follow‐up based on individual feedback rather than strictly adhering to prescriptive targets, acknowledging the importance of clinical acclimatization over time.

Snik et al. [10] highlighted that the traditional measurement of FG (the difference in thresholds without and with the device) is not applicable in patients with CHL or MHL implanted with BCD, recommending, therefore, the calculation of EG in these cases (the difference between thresholds with the device and bone conduction thresholds). If the air‐bone gap is completely closed, these two measures will be equal, and EG will be zero, meaning that CHL will be fully compensated and restored to normal with the device; therefore, values closer to zero indicate better outcomes [11]. In three studies that reported EG with BB [1, 12, 13], totaling 64 patients, an average of −11.11 dB was found. Dias et al. [14] compared the results of FG and EG in patients using BAHD and emphasized that in mixed or CHL, EG would be a more appropriate alternative for evaluation, as it has a better correlation with speech perception test results, which are associated with user satisfaction, indicating that better speech recognition rates result in greater satisfaction.

The SRS values with the device remained stable, while the SRT values decreased over the follow‐up, which indicates improvement with acclimatization. In addition to this, patients demonstrated satisfaction with the device and maintained regular use of it at the end of the study. It is essential to use tests with sentences that can measure speech recognition in conditions that reflect daily life, including evaluation in noisy situations. In complex acoustic environments, speech intelligibility can be challenging, as the amount of auditory cues necessary for understanding decreases significantly [15]. However, in this study, there was not enough data available in the records for evaluation.

In the APHAB questionnaire, the environmental noise domain decreased from the time of activation to 12 months of implant use. Amplification of environmental noise can be bothersome to the patient, especially immediately after BB activation, causing discomfort with noise [16]. Particularly in unilateral implantation, as the implant will not fully compensate for bilateral hearing, the patient does not benefit from bilateral noise reduction postoperatively and might experience discomfort with cross‐talk between ears. Nevertheless, this difference was not observed at the most recent evaluation, which is related to acclimatization to prosthesis use over time. Skarżyński et al. [17] also used the APHAB questionnaire to evaluate the benefits of patients implanted with BB during a 6‐month follow‐up, demonstrating an increase in quality of life, as did Garcier et al. [18] with 24 BB users and Cywka [8] after a 12‐month follow‐up of 42 patients.

The HHIA questionnaire is used to assess “participation restriction” due to hearing loss, covering dimensions not directly related to hearing but that impose limitations on the individual in the appropriate performance of routine tasks within their social context, which is closely related to the individual's quality of life [19]. There was a statistically significant difference in the total HHIA score between the moment of activation and 12 months after the implant, indicating a significant decrease in the perception of participation restriction related to their hearing impairment during this period; however, this was not observed in the latest follow‐up.

The SADL questionnaire showed a significant decrease in the service and costs domain over time. Some analyses do not take this domain into account when the prosthesis is provided by the public health system, which has no cost for the patient; however, we chose to maintain it in our evaluation precisely because incidents related to device failures were the most frequently observed, and the cost of repair or replacement of the device often became a barrier to regular use of the prosthesis.

Clinical complications were mostly minor incidents. Only one patient experienced a major incident, requiring reoperation for implant replacement due to internal device failure after traumatic injury (mild cranioencephalic trauma). In 2022, Souza et al. [20] identified a total complication rate of 21.3% in a review of complications of transcutaneous BAHD, including BB, Sophono, and BAHA Attract. We believe the higher rate found in our study can be explained by the longer follow‐up. While a direct comparison with percutaneous systems was beyond the scope of this work and remains a focus for future studies, pBAHDs have been addressed by our group in previous research, where Succar et al. [21] reported a complication incidence of 61.36% among 44 patients using percutaneous BAHDs at this same hospital, confirming the superiority of the transcutaneous prosthesis in this aspect.

Complications occurred more frequently, at a rate of 9.4%, when the BB was placed in a radical cavity, often resulting in cavity infection and subsequent skin dehiscence [12]. The retrosigmoid and middle fossa accesses were reported as safe and effective options for these cases [22, 23]. Implant location, screw type, and lift height did not interfere with the sound transmission of BB [24].

In this study, patients with chronic otitis media had bilateral open cavities with meatoplasty; therefore, retrosigmoid access was preferred. Oliveira [25] evaluated 13 patients with chronic otitis media who were implanted with retrosigmoid BB. The implant provided a higher FG (32.3 dB) than HA (24.01 dB) and superior SRT and SRS results. In terms of satisfaction, BB was preferred over HA regarding negative aspects (such as noise amplification, feedback, and telephone use) and personal image (related to aesthetics and stigma).

Due to technical problems with the device, some patients experienced periods without the BB, either waiting for the processor to be repaired or for a replacement. The difficulty in obtaining a new device, due to high costs and dependence on the public health system for replacements, is a key factor affecting both audiological performance and user satisfaction. Although records did not provide exact data on how long patients were without the device, and exact data on the daily usage of the implants over time were not registered in charts, we believe this interruption may have influenced the results of the subjective evaluation of the prosthesis's benefits. This is evidenced by the decrease in scores in the service and costs domain of the SADL questionnaire over time. This study has limitations regarding sample size and diversity, as the inclusion of other tBAHDs was restricted by high prosthetic costs and specific public health system coverage criteria.

All patients maintained their auditory thresholds after the surgical procedure, and there was no progression of hearing loss in the patients followed in this study. Despite a long follow‐up period with an average of 68.76 months (±21.54), the evaluated population consisted of young individuals, with a mean age of 26.11 ± 13.61 years. Longer‐term studies are needed to assess the stability of audiological results as the population of implanted patients presents age‐related hearing loss progression.

Conclusion

BB offers safe and effective long‐term auditory rehabilitation for adults and children with CHL or MHL over an extended period. After a follow‐up of up to 9 years, with an average of 68.76 months (±21.54), ranging from 24 to 108 months, BB demonstrated stable audiological objective and subjective improvements, along with a low complication rate. There was a decline in FG over the follow‐up and improvement of SRT, while EG and SRS remained stable. Patients showed stable satisfaction and benefit with the prosthesis over the long term, except regarding services and maintenance costs.
