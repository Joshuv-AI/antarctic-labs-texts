Effects of different modes of computerised cognitive training in older adults with reversible cognitive frailty: a randomised controlled trial in nursing homes
Authors: Minrui Li, Yu Zhang, Yuanhong Lin, Haiyuan Li, Lanxin Deng, Jingting Liu, Yulin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to evaluate the effects of computerised cognitive training (CCT) on cognitive functions in older adults with reversible cognitive frailty (RCF) and compare memory-focused training (MT) with multi-domain cognitive training (MDCT).MethodsIn this double-blind, CONSORT-compliant randomised controlled trial (RCT), 52 older adults with RCF living in a nursing home were stratified by frailty status (pre-frail/frail) and randomly allocated to MT (active control) or MDCT for 12 weeks (two sessions per week). Outcomes were assessed at baseline and 3 months (post-intervention), and 4 months (1-month follow-up), using generalised estimating equations (GEE) with intention-to-treat analysis.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated substantial and statistically significant improvements across all cognitive measures over time (Time effect: P < 0.001). Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores increased significantly (Wald χ² = 20.211, P < 0.001), with a mean gain of 1.02 points from baseline to 4 months (95% CI: 0.46–1.58, P < 0.001, effect size r = 0.369), with no significant between-group differences (P = 0.568). Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD) scores also improved significantly (Wald χ² = 73.374, P < 0.001), with a mean reduction of 2.71 points (95% CI: 2.03–3.39, P < 0.001). STT-A and B completion times decreased markedly by averages of 27.57 and 92.99 s, respectively. Notably, for STT-A, performance was significantly faster in the MT group compared to the MDCT group (64.79 vs. 80.46 s, P = 0.038). regarding STT-B, although the interaction was not significant, the MDCT group showed a substantial magnitude of recovery. Clock Drawing Test (CDT) scores improved (Wald χ² = 11.229, P = 0.004), with a mean increase of 0.21 points (95% CI: 0.06–0.36, P = 0.002). The MDCT group showed marginally higher CDT scores, though not statistically significant (P = 0.068).ConclusionsBoth MT and MDCT were feasible and yielded comparable short-term global cognitive gains. However, they exhibited distinct therapeutic profiles where MT facilitated transfer effects in processing speed while MDCT effectively restored executive function. Concurrent improvements in sleep and mood were also observed. These findings support a flexible implementation strategy based on available resources, allowing clinicians to select the modality that best targets an individual’s specific cognitive profile.Trial registrationThis study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 8 February 2023 (ChiCTR2300068132).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04791-y.

Background

Cognitive frailty (CF) is an important frailty subtype and has recently become a major research focus [1]. The International Academy of Nutrition and Aging and the International Association of Gerontology and Geriatrics [2] first defined CF in 2013 as the coexistence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment, explicitly excluding Alzheimer’s disease or other dementias. In 2015, Ruan et al. further classified CF into potentially reversible cognitive frailty and reversible cognitive frailty (RCF) [3]. RCF is a critical risk factor for health deterioration in older adults, substantially increasing the likelihood of falls, disability, loss of independence, and mortality [4, 5]. It undermines autonomy and quality of life and imposes a growing older adult care burden on families and society. Evidence highlights RCF as a robust predictor of adverse outcomes and a potential therapeutic target for disability prevention in older adults [5, 6]. Its prevalence is high and rises with age, with older adults in nursing homes being particularly vulnerable. In China, RCF detection rates in this population range from 8.0% to 66.2% [7]. Older adults with RCF typically present with slowed gait, weight loss, reduced muscle strength, and cognitive decline. Several non-pharmacological interventions have shown benefit in alleviating CF in older adults [8–10].

Computerised cognitive training (CCT) is a pivotal non-pharmacological intervention for ameliorating or reversing RCF [11]. With adaptable difficulty and task flexibility, CCT enhances intervention precision and efficacy through personalised protocols, offering distinct advantages for RCF prevention and early management compared to traditional, non-computerised cognitive interventions (e.g., paper-and-pencil tasks or group-based cognitive activities) [8]. These advantages include automated difficulty progression, immediate performance feedback, enhanced engagement through interactive formats, and the ability to deliver standardized yet individualized training in resource-limited settings such as nursing homes. However, existing CCT studies show substantial heterogeneity in modes, task content, and implementation, complicating synthesis and generalisability of findings. Moreover, robust domestic research on institutionalised older adults with RCF is lacking. It remains unclear whether single-domain (memory-focused) or multi-domain cognitive training (MDCT) is more effective in improving cognitive and physical outcomes in this population. Single-domain training targets a specific cognitive function with a focused protocol, whereas multi-domain training engages multiple cognitive processes simultaneously, requiring more varied tasks and greater cognitive flexibility. However, few studies have directly compared their relative effectiveness in real-world settings. Therefore, we aimed to compare the effects of memory-focused and multi-domain CCT on cognitive function in older adults with RCF residing in nursing homes, with the goal of developing practical, evidence-based protocols for RCF management. We hypothesized that while both MT and MDCT would alleviate cognitive decline, MDCT would yield greater benefits in executive and visuospatial functions due to its potential to engage broader neural networks in older adults with RCF.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT 2010 Statement). It was a double-blind, randomised controlled trial performed between May and October 2023 in a comprehensive nursing home. Ethical approval was obtained from an institutional review board (approval No. 26, 2023), and the trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300068132) in compliance with the requirements for randomised controlled trials. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Older adults with RCF were eligible if they: (i) were ≥ 60 years; (ii) had completed at least primary education; (iii) resided in the selected nursing facility for at least 6 months; (iv) met the diagnostic criteria for RCF; and (v) regularly performed moderate-intensity exercise (≥ 30 min/session, 3–5 times weekly) for ≥ 3 months before enrolment, with intent to continue throughout the study. Exclusion criteria were current use of medications known to affect cognitive function or refusal to participate after being informed of study procedures.

Withdrawal criteria

Participants were withdrawn if: (1) they voluntarily withdrew consent at any stage; (2) could not continue due to newly diagnosed or worsening medical conditions, as determined by a healthcare professional; (3) were lost to follow-up despite reasonable contact efforts; or (4) had major protocol violations or non-compliance, as judged by the principal investigator.

Criteria for RCF

All enrolled participants met the diagnostic criteria for RCF, which required the coexistence of physical frailty (assessed via FFP) and subjective cognitive decline (SCD-I criteria), in the absence of objective cognitive impairment (MoCA ≥ 26, education-adjusted). This diagnostic verification was performed during the screening phase and constituted a prerequisite for inclusion.

Physical frailty was assessed using the Fried Frailty Phenotype (FFP), following the Chinese Medical Association’s Expert Consensus on Frailty Assessment [12], which includes five criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slowness, and weakness. Low grip strength was defined using gender- and BMI-stratified cut-offs. Specifically, the thresholds for weakness ranged from ≤ 29–32 kg for men and ≤ 17–21 kg for women, adjusted according to the participant’s BMI quartiles (e.g., Men with BMI ≤ 24.0 kg/m² were cut off at ≤ 29 kg; Women with BMI ≤ 23.0 kg/m² were cut off at ≤ 17 kg) [12]. The total FFP score ranges from 0 to 5, with 0 indicating robustness, 1–2 pre-frailty, and 3–5 frailty.

SCD was diagnosed according to the criteria recommended by the International SCD Initiative (SCD-I) Working Group [13, 14]. It was defined as a persistent, self-perceived decline in cognitive capacity (particularly memory) compared with an individual’s previous normal function, not attributable to acute stressors or major life events (e.g., family conflict or newly diagnosed illness). Participants were also required to demonstrate normal performance on standardised neurocognitive assessments (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA ≥ 26, adjusted for education level where appropriate), while excluding cognitive decline secondary to psychiatric or neurological disorders or medication effects. Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using PASS 2021, based on anticipated MoCA score changes. The calculation referred to a previous study reporting an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.947 for cognitive training interventions [15]. Assuming a two-sided significance level (α = 0.05), power (1 – β = 0.80), and estimated dropout rate of 20%, the minimum required sample size was 48 participants (24 per group). Ultimately, 52 participants were included (26 per group).

Allocation concealment and blinding

Computer-generated stratified block randomisation (1:1) was applied, with stratification by frailty status (pre-frail [n = 19] and frail [n = 7]) to ensure balanced distribution across intervention arms. Twenty-six participants were allocated to each treatment group, reflecting the actual baseline distribution of pre-frailty and frailty among older adults in the nursing home. Allocation sequences were generated with IBM SPSS 27.0 by an independent statistician uninvolved in recruitment or data collection. Sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing group assignments were prepared for the screening researcher and opened only after eligibility confirmation, informed consent, and baseline assessment completion.

A double-blind design was employed to minimise bias. Data collectors were blinded to group assignments, ensuring objective assessments. Participants, engaged in CCT in a game-like format, were also unaware of allocation. The memory training (MT) group focused on memory enhancement, whereas the MDCT group targeted multiple cognitive functions. Both groups followed standardised protocols, which maintained participant blinding, ensuring unbiased data collection, compliance, and methodological rigour.

Interventions

Health education

Within 1 week prior to the intervention, both groups attended a standardised 40-minute health education session tailored for older adults. The curriculum addressed key topics, including the concept of CF, recommendations and precautions for daily physical activity and healthy eating, interactive question-and-answer discussions, and the viewing of relevant educational videos.

Cognitive training

Cognitive training was delivered using the Cogniplus Cognitive Ability Training System (SCHUHFRIED; https://www.schuhfried.com/en/cogniplus/), a computer-based platform designed for cognitive rehabilitation. The system offers adaptive task difficulty and supports individualized training protocols. The intervention team included the project instructor, three researchers, a geriatrician, and two nursing assistants, all trained in protocol adherence. Training was provided in a one-to-one format in a designated conference room within the nursing facility, offering a controlled, distraction-free environment. The adaptive training system adjusted task difficulty according to participants’ performance: complexity increased with repeated success and decreased with higher error rates or difficulty. This ensured individually appropriate training levels, optimising intervention effectiveness.

MT group

The structured intervention programme was delivered twice weekly, with each session lasting 30 min, over a 12-week period. Outcome measures

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was the primary outcome and was assessed using a standardised neuropsychological battery. We selected MoCA for global cognition, STT for executive function, CDT for visuospatial ability, and SCD-Q9 for subjective complaints because these domains are most frequently impaired in RCF and are sensitive to cognitive training effects. While MDCT targets multiple domains, these measures capture key areas relevant to functional independence and cognitive reserve.

The Chinese version of the MoCA [17] was used as the principal measure for global cognition. The MoCA evaluates eight domains: visuospatial and executive function, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation. Scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores reflecting better cognitive performance. An additional point was awarded to participants with fewer than 12 years of education. The MoCA has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with a test–retest reliability of 0.857 and a Cronbach’s α of 0.818 [17].

Domain-specific cognitive functions were assessed using validated instruments. Executive function was measured with the Shape Trails Test A & B (STT-A and STT-B) [18]. In Part A, participants sequentially connected numbers (1–25), assessing visuospatial scanning and processing speed. In Part B, participants alternated between numbers and geometric shapes (squares and circles), evaluating working memory and cognitive flexibility. Performance was measured by completion time in seconds, with longer times indicating greater executive dysfunction. Visuospatial ability was assessed using the Clock Drawing Test (CDT) [19]. Participants were instructed to draw a clock displaying the time “11:10”. Scores range from 0 to 4, based on the accuracy of the clock face, digit placement, and hand positioning, with lower scores indicating poorer visuospatial function.

Subjective cognitive complaints were measured using the 9-item Subjective Cognitive Decline Questionnaire (SCD-Q9) [20]. The Chinese version, adapted by Han et al. in 2016, has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.870–0.881) and split-half reliability of 0.800 [21]. Scores range from 0 to 9, with higher values reflecting greater risk of early cognitive impairment.

Psychosocial indicators

Anxiety and depression severity were assessed using the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) [22] and the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale, Brief Version (GDS-15) [23], respectively, with higher scores reflecting greater symptom severity. Sleep quality was measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which yields a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality [24].

Data collection

Data were collected one-to-one at three time points: within 2 weeks before the intervention (T0), immediately after completion (T1), and 1-month post-intervention (T2). Demographic data (age, sex, education, marital status, and living arrangements) were obtained through standardised questionnaires during baseline interviews. Functional status was assessed using two validated instruments. The Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (IADL) evaluates shopping, meal preparation, housekeeping, laundry, transportation, telephone use, medication management, and money management. Scores range from 0 to 24, with higher scores reflecting greater independence [25]. The IADL has demonstrated strong psychometric properties (test–retest reliability, 0.950; Cronbach’s α, 0.838) [26]. The Basic Activities of Daily Living Scale (BADL) [27] was also used to assess self-care tasks such as eating and bathing. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater functional independence. Questionnaires were collected on-site, with incomplete or erroneous responses corrected immediately in consultation with participants. Qualitative feedback on participants’ overall experiences and suggestions for improvement were obtained through semi-structured face-to-face interviews (see Supplementary Material 1 for the complete interview guide).

Quality control

To ensure intervention reliability and validity, all researchers received comprehensive training covering familiarisation with the study protocol, CCT system operation, health education content, and standardised administration of assessment tools and scales. To promote participant compliance, contact information was collected for timely communication and follow-up. Before each session, researchers explained training benefits and precautions; after each session, they provided individualised support, answered questions, and offered small incentives to encourage engagement. Participants were informed of the intervention schedule and the importance of adherence to cognitive training, with researchers accompanying them throughout the intervention, actively reinforcing the benefits of training to enhance motivation. Each participant signed an informed consent form with a confidentiality clause, ensuring that no training-related information was shared between groups. A weekly task list was established, with emphasis on time management and punctual participation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Continuous variables (e.g., age and scale scores) were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with between-group comparisons performed using independent samples t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. For comparisons of repeated measurements at different time points between groups, repeated-measures ANOVA was applied. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), with between-group comparisons conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Longitudinal changes in non-normally distributed data were analysed using generalised estimating equations (GEE). Categorical variables are summarised as frequencies and percentages n (%). Between-group differences in unordered categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square (χ2) test, while ordered categorical variables were analysed with the Mann–Whitney U test. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (α = 0.05). All analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Participants who met the inclusion criteria, completed at least one study-related assessment, and had available post-baseline data were included in the ITT analyses. Missing data due to dropout were handled using the last observation carried forward method.

Results

Participant characteristics

In the MT group, all participants completed the intervention, with one participant lost to the 1-month follow-up assessment due to withdrawal for personal health reasons. Similarly, all participants in the MDCT group completed the intervention, but two were lost to the 1-month follow-up: one relocated from the nursing home and could not be contacted, and another was temporarily absent and did not return during the follow-up period. Attendance for both the health education session and the 24 cognitive training sessions was 100% in both groups. No adverse events or unintended effects were reported or observed in either group throughout the study period. Participant flow is shown in Fig. 2.

Cognitive outcomes

MoCA scores increased significantly over time (Wald χ² = 20.211, P < 0.001), with a mean improvement of 1.02 points from baseline to 4 months (95% CI: 0.46–1.58, P < 0.001). Between-group differences were not significant (P = 0.568). SCD scores decreased significantly over time (Wald χ² = 73.374, P < 0.001), with a mean reduction of 2.71 points from baseline to 4 months (95% CI: 2.03–3.39, P < 0.001). Between-group differences were not significant (P = 0.214), and no group × time interaction was detected (P = 0.193), indicating similar declines in both groups. STT-A and STT-B completion times improved significantly. STT-A times decreased by 27.57 s overall, with the MT group showing better performance than that of the MDCT group (64.79 vs. 80.46 s, P = 0.038). STT-B times decreased by 92.99 s, with a trend toward better performance in the MDCT group (166.56 vs. 197.27 s, P = 0.057). Significant time effects were observed for both STT-A (Wald χ²(2) = 62.440, P < 0.001) and STT-B (Wald χ²(2) = 98.395, P < 0.001). STT-A seconds decreased from baseline (Estimated Marginal Mean [EMM] = 88.48) to 3 months (EMM = 60.91, P < 0.001), with partial rebound at 4 months (EMM = 69.83, P < 0.001 vs. baseline). No group × time interaction was found (P = 0.340), indicating similar temporal patterns in both groups. For STT-B, seconds decreased significantly from baseline (EMM = 243.76) to 3 months (EMM = 150.76, P < 0.001) and remained improved at 4 months (EMM = 162.09, P < 0.001). No group × time interaction was detected (P = 0.971), suggesting parallel improvements across both groups. CDT scores also improved significantly over time (Wald χ² = 11.229, P = 0.004), with a mean increase of 0.21 points from baseline to 4 months (95% CI: 0.06–0.36, P = 0.002). Although the MDCT group showed slightly higher scores, the between-group difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.068).

The GEE analysis showed no significant group difference in anxiety scores (Wald χ²(1) = 1.761, P = 0.185). A marginal time effect was observed (Wald χ²(2) = 5.343, P = 0.069), with scores decreasing from baseline (EMM = 4.78) to 3 months (EMM = 3.19, P = 0.048). Depression scores decreased significantly over time (Wald χ² = 12.935, P = 0.002), with a mean reduction of 1.81 points from baseline to 3 months (95% CI: 0.60–3.03, P = 0.001), with no significant between-group differences (P = 0.798). PSQI scores showed a significant time effect (Wald χ² = 104.121, P < 0.001), with no significant between-group differences (P = 0.185). Sleep quality improved from baseline (EMM = 12.75) to 3 months (EMM = 10.59, P < 0.001) and further to 4 months (EMM = 8.68, P < 0.001). Details are presented in Table 4and Fig. 3.

Discussion

Comparative effectiveness on global cognition

After 12 weeks of intervention, both memory-focused and MDCT significantly improved overall cognitive function in older adults with RCF. These gains were largely maintained at the 1-month follow-up, consistent with previous research [28–30]. The effectiveness of these interventions may be attributed to their simulation of real-life scenarios, engagement of multiple sensory modalities, and adaptive task difficulty, which together enhance attention, memory, and other cognitive domains [31, 32]. Neuroimaging studies have further shown that sustained cognitive training can induce structural and functional changes in the aging brain, including increased grey matter volume in the cerebral cortex [33] and improved integrity of white matter connectivity [34, 35]. Notably, brain function may persist at a certain level even after training ends, helping prevent immediate memory decline.

MDCT yielded greater improvements in visuospatial performance than did MT alone, consistent with neuroimaging evidence that diversified cognitive engagement enhances parietal–occipital activation [36]. The distinct cognitive benefits observed between training paradigms highlight the domain-specific nature of neuroplastic adaptations in older adults. These results support the hypothesis that cognitive training modifies brain activation patterns during task-related states, with brain regions directly engaged by training being most responsive. Collectively, our findings, together with the 2023 Chinese Expert Consensus, support CCT as an effective, sustainable strategy for enhancing cognitive function and reserve in older adults with RCF.

Domain-specific trajectories of transfer effects and executive restoration

Twelve weeks of MT not only improved memory but also led to improvements in attention and executive function in older adults with RCF, suggesting a notable transfer effect across cognitive domains [37]. This finding aligns with meta-analytic evidence showing that cognitive training can produce both near and far transfer, improving abilities beyond the specific domain targeted in community-dwelling and institutionalized older adults [38]. The adaptive difficulty and multimodal design of the training likely contributed to these effects by simultaneously engaging attention and information processing alongside memory. Notably, participants also reported subjective improvements in attention, observation, and reaction, consistent with research demonstrating that working MT can produce lasting enhancements in executive function [39]. These results suggest that targeted cognitive training may promote neuroplasticity in related brain regions, supporting broader cognitive benefits. Future studies could further leverage artificial intelligence (AI)-driven personalised interventions to optimise gains across multiple domains.

Both groups showed significant reductions in STT-A and STT-B completion times after the intervention and at the 1-month follow-up, indicating that CCT yields meaningful and potentially durable improvements in executive function among older adults with RCF. These findings are consistent with the ACTIVE study, which reported that processing speed training produced cognitive gains persisting for up to 10 years and helped maintain daily functioning in older adults [40]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of 64 CCT trials confirmed that interventions targeting core executive functions can enhance both trained and untrained cognitive domains, with benefits sustained over time [41]. Although our study assessed outcomes only at 1-month post-intervention due to time constraints, future research should extend follow-up periods, incorporate booster sessions, and further investigate the long-term effects of CCT on executive function in institutionalised older adults with RCF.

Observed changes in depressive symptoms and sleep quality

Beyond these cognitive domains, we observed time-dependent improvements in sleep quality and depressive symptoms across both groups. The mechanism underlying these gains likely involves a synergy of specific and non-specific factors. Theoretically, targeting executive function engages the cognitive control network, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), which shares neural substrates with emotional regulation circuits [42–44]. Although we did not measure neural markers directly, the significant improvement in executive function (STT-B) in our study aligns with this hypothesis. The intervention also likely worked through a psychological mechanism. The “mastery experience” of completing tasks may have helped restore participants’ sense of self-efficacy, which could counteract the helplessness that often accompanies frailty. However, it is crucial to interpret these findings with caution. As these improvements occurred in both active groups without a passive control, they may also reflect non-specific effects common to structured care, such as regular social interaction, established routines, and attention from research staff. Thus, these improvements may reflect the integrated benefits of participating in a structured, cognitively-oriented activity rather than a direct effect of CCT.

Feasibility and acceptability in nursing home settings

CCT proved highly feasible for older adults with RCF, with all participants completing the training and demonstrating high compliance. Both subjective feedback and objective data indicated strong engagement and adaptability. The programme’s individualised mode provided personalised guidance, supporting adherence and optimising cognitive benefit. While one-on-one training facilitates individualised support—particularly for task clarification—evidence suggests that group-based and individual formats achieve comparable efficacy [45]. Therefore, flexible application of training modes tailored to participants’ cognitive profiles is recommended to maintain engagement and motivation. The high adherence and positive feedback in this selected sample suggest that CCT is acceptable among motivated, higher-functioning nursing home residents. As older adults increasingly adopt new technologies and digital tools [46], CCT holds significant potential for broader implementation in community and clinical settings. Future implementation research should test its feasibility and adaptability in more diverse and frail institutional populations, and explore strategies to support technology adoption among older adults with varying levels of digital literacy.

Clinical implications and real-world impact

Due to the non-normal distribution of our cognitive outcome data, we assessed the clinical significance using non-parametric effect size estimators and clinical improvement percentages. For MoCA, the improvement of 1.02 points corresponds to a Z-based effect size (r) of 0.367, indicating a medium effect. Crucially, this magnitude must be interpreted against the “ceiling effect” observed at baseline: with a median score of 28/30, participants had very limited room for improvement. Consequently, a gain of ~ 1 point represents a recovery of over 50% of the residual cognitive deficit, maximizing the potential for reversibility in this specific population. This stabilization of global cognition is further corroborated by remarkable relative improvements in domain-specific and subjective functions. Notably, the MDCT group achieved a 32.6% reduction in STT-B completion time (median: 263s to 177s), reflecting a clinically distinct enhancement in executive processing efficiency. Furthermore, Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD) scores improved by 57.9% (median: 5.94 to 2.50), suggesting that the intervention successfully alleviated more than half of the participants’ perceived cognitive burden. The convergence of these objective and subjective metrics confirms that the statistical significance observed translates into tangible improvements in cognitive speed, accuracy, and self-confidence.

Limitations and future directions

First, as a small pilot study with 52 participants, the findings have limited generalisability. The single-centre design and modest attrition (1–2 participants lost to follow-up per group), despite ITT analysis, may further restrict external validity. However, rigorous randomisation, double blinding, and ITT analysis enhance internal validity and provide pilot evidence to guide larger multicentre trials. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed.

Second, to minimize confounding factors, our study strictly included only older adults with regular exercise habits. While this deliberate design choice strengthened the internal validity of the trial by treating physical activity as a controlled constant, it inevitably involves a trade-off regarding external validity. Consequently, our findings may not be directly generalizable to sedentary older adults or those with severe physical frailty. Future studies should investigate whether these cognitive training modes are equally effective in less active populations.

Third, this study was designed as a head-to-head comparison between two active CCT modalities (MT vs. MDCT), and therefore did not include a non-intervention or passive control group (e.g., wait-list or usual care). This design choice aligns with a comparative effectiveness research framework, aiming to determine the relative efficacy of two plausible clinical options while controlling for non-specific factors (e.g., social interaction, digital exposure) through the use of an active comparator. Consequently, while our results inform the choice between MT and MDCT, they do not quantify the absolute treatment effect compared to no intervention.

Finally, regarding the potential for practice effects due to repeated testing, two factors mitigate this concern. The randomized parallel-group design ensures that any practice effects are distributed equally across groups, ensuring the validity of between-group comparisons. Furthermore, the magnitude of improvement observed—particularly the > 120-second reduction in STT-B completion time in the MDCT group (which occurred despite a higher numerical baseline burden) — substantially exceeds what would typically be expected from mere test familiarity. This suggests a genuine therapeutic effect driven by neural plasticity rather than a simple retest artifact.

Conclusions

In summary, this randomised comparative trial demonstrates that both MT and MDCT were feasible and were associated with short-term improvements in cognitive test performance among a selected subgroup of nursing home residents with RCF. Both MT and MDCT yielded comparable gains in global cognition, yet they offered differential benefits where MT facilitated transfer effects to non-targeted processing speed while MDCT effectively restored executive function in individuals with numerically heavier baseline deficits. Exploratory analyses noted concurrent improvements in depressive symptoms and sleep quality, although the specific role of cognitive training versus non-specific factors in these changes requires further investigation. These findings support the need for larger, pragmatic trials with passive control groups, longer follow-up, and functional outcomes to establish definitive efficacy and optimal implementation strategies for broader RCF populations.
