Exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming: effects on thyroid cancer progression and tumor microenvironment remodeling
Authors: Shouhua Li, Hengtong Han, Kaili Yang, Xiaoxiao Li, Libin Ma, Ze Yang, Yong-xun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic reprogramming is one of the fundamental characteristics of thyroid cancer (TC), which meets its energy and biosynthetic demands through mitochondrial dysfunction, glycolysis activation, lipid metabolism imbalance, and glutamine dependency, thereby promoting metastasis and reshaping the immune microenvironment. Exosomes, as extracellular vesicles, play a crucial role in TC by delivering bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. In the tumor microenvironment (TME) of TC, exosomes secreted by both tumor and non-tumor cells interact with each other, driving metabolic reprogramming and forming a bidirectional regulatory network. This significantly alters the biological characteristics of TC cells, including proliferation, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and the acquisition of drug resistance and immune tolerance, ultimately influencing the process of immune escape in TC. This review systematically summarizes how exosomes in the TME of TC promote tumor progression through metabolic reprogramming, providing new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for patients with locally advanced, radioiodine-refractory TC.Keywords: Thyroid cancer, Exosomes, Metabolic reprogramming, Tumor microenvironment, Immune escape

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is composed of stromal cells, immune components, vascular networks, and the extracellular matrix. It drives metabolic reprogramming by regulating nutrient supply, oxygen concentration, and signaling pathways. This is primarily manifested through tumor cells altering the expression of transcription factors, signaling molecules, and metabolic enzymes related to energy metabolism, leading to adaptive changes in glucose, amino acid, and lipid metabolism. By reshaping metabolic pathways, tumor cells gain a competitive advantage in nutrient competition. Additionally, the metabolic processes and products further remodel the functional states of immune cells within the TME, creating a vicious cycle that supports tumor progression [1, 2]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), membrane-coated vesicles secreted by various cells in the TME—including tumor cells, immune cells (such as macrophages, Tregs, monocytes, neutrophils, DCs, and mast cells), stromal cells (cancer-associated fibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and adipocytes), and others—are widely present in body fluids (such as blood, urine, ascites, and saliva) and contain specific biomarkers that could be detected; based on their size and biogenesis pathways, EVs can be classified into exosomes, ectosomes (microvesicles), and apoptotic bodies [3, 4]. Studies have shown that EVs play a key role in the TME by inducing various biological activities through a variety of functional components such as nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and complex sugars [4]. The composition and characteristics of their contents are significantly different due to the different cell sources, states and microenvironments, while its bilayer lipid structure and biocompatibility not only protect the integrity of metabolism-related molecules (such as metabolic enzymes, metabolites and signaling molecules), but also reduce the immunogenicity, giving them a unique advantage in intercellular communication [5]. As a key signal mediator, EVs can mediate the metabolic reprogramming of related cells in the TME, promote the metabolic adaptive changes of tumor cells, immune escape, angiogenesis, invasion and drug resistance, thereby creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment and promoting tumor progression. Therefore, EVs have gradually become important biomarkers for early diagnosis, progression evaluation, efficacy monitoring and prognosis prediction of tumors [6].

This feature is present in various cancers, including thyroid cancer (TC), which is the most common endocrine system malignancy and the ninth most common malignant tumor worldwide [7]. TC is mainly divided into papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC), medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) according to histological features. Among them, PTC accounts for the largest proportion, accounting for about 80%−85% of all cases. Its prognosis is good, and the 10-year disease-specific survival rate is more than 90% [8]. However, ATC, one of the subtypes of TC, is associated with a poor prognosis, with approximately 20–50% of patients developing distant metastases, a median survival time of 3–6 months, and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% [9]. Most patients with early diagnosis are treated with surgical resection and endocrine suppression therapy, while patients with advanced or radioiodine-refractory disease rely on targeted therapy (such as BRAF inhibitors, RET inhibitors) and immunotherapy. Although these treatments show promise, they still face problems of low response rates and short-term drug resistance, and new regimens are urgently needed to overcome the limitations of current treatment [10, 11].

Among these EVs, exosomes have garnered significant attention due to their unique role in mediating intercellular communication and metabolic reprogramming, particularly in the context of tumor biology. This review focuses on the central role of exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming in the dynamic microenvironment in the occurrence and progression of TC, and systematically reviews the key role of exosomes in intercellular communication and their impact on TC tumor biology. This article focuses on how proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and metabolic active molecules carried by exosomes affect the proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance, immune escape and remodeling of TME by regulating metabolic pathways of TC. These studies provide new insights and ideas for the diagnosis and treatment of TC.

Overview of metabolic reprogramming in TC

Metabolic reprogramming is a series of adaptive metabolic changes caused by the metabolic stress of TC cells in the microenvironment of intense nutritional competition and hypoxia, which is one of the most consistent signs of cancer proliferation, metastasis and drug resistance [12]. The rapid proliferation of cancer cells rapidly depletes nutrients in the TME. The low oxygen concentration and high acidity in the TME create a survival crisis for these cells. Therefore, cancer cells must adapt to these constraints and find ways to overcome environmental challenges. The disordered metabolic pathways in the TME not only promote the proliferation of tumor cells but also weaken the immune system’s ability to clear tumors, thereby limiting the clinical remission effects following immunotherapy. This is considered a key driving factor in cancer progression [12]. TC increases the activity of enzymes related to glucose uptake and glycolysis (Fig. 1), and then relies on aerobic glycolysis instead of the traditional oxidative phosphorylation pathway to synthesize adenosine triphosphate. In this way, tumor cells not only gain energy for continuous growth, but also increase lactate excretion. In turn, it promotes tumor progression and metastasis [13]. In addition, in order to support the rapid proliferation and continuous growth of cells, glutamine metabolism and fatty acid synthesis pathways are also significantly enhanced (Fig. 1). While providing energy for cells, they can also be used as signal transduction mediators to mediate cell signal transduction, promote the synthesis of macromolecules, and produce more cell membrane lipids to meet the high demand of cells for energy and biosynthetic precursors [13].

Metabolic reprogramming in TC. In the TME, TC cells reprogram their energy metabolism to gain an advantage. Compared with normal cells, TC cells take up more glucose, which is affected by the warburg effect and produces more lactate. In order to adapt to this process, the expression of transport receptors and metabolic enzymes involved in the glucose metabolism pathway is increased. In addition, the glycolytic pathway not only produces ATP, but also provides important metabolic intermediates for other biosynthetic pathways. Both fatty acid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation are upregulated to promote the progression of TC. The metabolic pathways of glutamine and serine are significantly upregulated, providing nitrogen and one-carbon units for the proliferation and invasion of TC cells. Reprogramming of glucose metabolism

Glucose metabolism plays a key role in tumor cells, not only providing a rapid energy source for tumor cells, but also supporting the synthesis of important biological macromolecules such as lipids and nucleic acids, thereby promoting rapid cell division and growth. In addition, glucose metabolism also enhances the adaptability of TC cells to adverse microenvironments such as hypoxia by regulating metabolic intermediates [14]. The “Warburg effect” points out that tumor cells have a significant increase in glucose consumption. Even under the condition of sufficient oxygen, tumor cells are more dependent on the glycolytic pathway to produce more lactate, instead of the dominant aerobic respiratory pathway in normal cells [14]. This metabolic reprogramming provides multiple advantages for tumor growth, which are manifested in the following aspects: 1). By providing energy through high rate of glucose metabolism, cancer cells can gain an advantage in nutritional competition with immune cells. 2). The accumulation of lactic acid leads to acidification of the microenvironment, which promotes cancer cell migration and immune escape. 3). Providing Biosynthetic precursors for cancer cells supports their rapid proliferation and growth. 4). It helps to maintain the balance of ROS in TME to prevent cancer cell damage [15]. In TC, changes in key links such as glucose transport, pyruvate kinase activity and lactate metabolism drive glucose metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells [14].

Studies have shown that hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) significantly up-regulates the activity of 90% of glycolytic reactive enzymes under hypoxic conditions, and promotes the expression of the glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) and glucose transporter type 3 (GLUT3) and enhances glucose uptake by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway, which in turn is closely related to tumor invasion and differentiation loss [16, 17]. It has been confirmed that Hexokinase 2 (HK2), phosphofructokinase 1 (PFK1) and M2-type pyruvate kinase (PKM2), as the key rate-limiting enzymes of glycolysis, play a central regulatory role in the glycolysis enhancement process of TC, which is related to the rapid and uncontrolled proliferation of TC [18]. In PTC, the high expression of HK2 promotes the growth and migration of cancer cells by activating the AKT/mTOR/HK2 pathway and promoting glycolysis [19]. In addition, PKM2 overexpression is associated with lymph node metastasis and advanced cancer staging, and BRAF mutation up-regulates PKM2 expression by activating ERK and STAT3 signaling pathways, increasing lactate and ATP production while promoting glucose consumption, which promotes metabolic reprogramming and malignant progression of tumors [20, 21]. It has been reported that lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB) reversibly catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate. In PTC, Hypoxia up-regulated 1 (HYOU1) is involved in the up-regulation of LDHB expression by indirectly down-regulating the expression of miR-375 and promoting its proliferation, migration and invasion [22]. Lactate is not only a metabolic waste, but also can be regarded as an energy carrier. Monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4) is responsible for lactate efflux, and its expression is increased in TC (Fig. 1) [23]. Energy waste is avoided through the lactate shuttle mechanism, and inhibition of this process significantly reduces the proliferation and glycolytic ability of ATC cells [24]. Due to the disorder of signal transduction, the remodeling of glycolytic metabolism can further enhance the metabolic heterogeneity and plasticity of cancer cells by changing the composition or energy state of metabolites, thereby promoting tumor growth [25]. These studies suggest that metabolic reprogramming is not only a core driving force in TC progression, but also provides potential targets for therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic pathways.

Reprogramming of lipid metabolism

Lipids are essential for cellular function and survival. They maintain cell membrane structure, facilitate intercellular signaling, and serve as energy reserves to support metabolism and growth [26]. Transcriptional regulators of lipid biosynthesis act as downstream targets of oncogene and tumor suppressor pathways, prompting cancer cells to adjust lipid uptake and synthesis according to changes in TME [26].

The enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis, such as fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), exhibit significant changes in expression in TC (Fig. 1). FASN plays a crucial role in de novo fatty acid synthesis and is overexpressed in PTC and ATC, with crosstalk observed with the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [27]. ACC is the rate-limiting enzyme in de novo lipid synthesis and the inhibition of fatty acid oxidation (FAO). In TC, highly expressed circPCNXL2 enhances ACC1 activity by reducing its phosphorylation, promoting fatty acid synthesis to meet energy demands and drive cell growth [28]. In addition, it has been reported that the expression of ACLY, an indicator of cancer invasiveness, is up-regulated in ATC, which acts as a key enzyme at the intersection of glucose and lipid metabolism to promote fatty acid synthesis and stimulate cell proliferation [29]. It has been confirmed that in PTC and FTC lesions, ROS levels are increased, which significantly promotes Lipid peroxidation, resulting in the generation of some unstable peroxides. Including 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal, malondialdehyde and hexanal, these substances have the effect of promoting angiogenesis and tumor growth [26]. Enhanced fatty acid synthesis pathways promote increased acetyl-coA production, thus providing precursors for cholesterol synthesis to meet membrane structural requirements. 27-hydroxycholesterol promotes the proliferation and migration of thyroid anaplastic cell lines and promotes the malignant progression of tumors. CYP7B1, a member of the cytochrome-P450 (CYP450) family, is involved in cholesterol hydroxylation, but its expression level is decreased in PTC and ATC. This alteration results in an increased concentration of its oxidative metabolite, 27-hydroxycholesterol [30, 31]. It has been reported that Low-Density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) and 3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl-CoA Reductase (HMGCR) enzyme, which controls de novo cholesterol synthesis, are upregulated in more malignant poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (PDTC)/ATC tumors (Fig. 1), which further leads to elevated levels of 27-hydroxycholesterol [30]. Hypoxia promotes the expression of HIF-1α in PTC cells and induces the up-regulation of Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), the first rate-limiting enzyme in FAO, which helps cells adapt to hypoxia through lipid metabolic reprogramming. Reduce oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, and support tumor cell growth [32]. Sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs) are major transcription factors that regulate cellular adipogenesis and lipid balance. The expression of SREBPs is high in DTC and regulated by PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 signaling pathway. It regulates FASN, LDLR, acyl-CoA cholesterol acyltransferase (ACAT), HMGCR, and is related to the progression of tumors [33, 34]. TC cells change the nutritional pattern by increasing the expression levels of lipid-related receptors and key enzymes, and enhance lipid uptake and synthesis to support their high energy demand and immunosuppression, and promote survival and development. These findings shed light on the mechanism of PTC progression and may provide new targets for cancer prevention and improved prognosis [35].

Reprogramming of amino acid metabolism

TC cells alter amino acid metabolism to support rapid proliferation, enhance amino acid uptake to meet protein and nucleotide synthesis needs, and provide an energy source, while helping to maintain redox balance and promote cell survival [36]. In this process, L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1) acts in concert with alanine-serine-cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2) to regulate the glutamine uptake process. Studies have shown that the expression of LAT1 in ATC is significantly increased and negatively correlated with the long-term survival rate of PTC patients [37]. Glutamine plays an important role as the most abundant free amino acid in cancer cells. In glutamine metabolism, key proteins, including ASCT2, which facilitates the entry of glutamine into the cell, glutaminase 1 (GLS1), which converts glutamine to glutamate, and glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), which converts glutamate to α-ketoglutarate for use in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle), are all expressed at an increased level in the PTC of the BRAF V600E mutation(Fig. 1), which is associated with thyroid outgrowth and lymph node metastasis [38]. In addition, the expression of stromal antigen 2 (STAG2) is decreased in BRAF-mutant TC cells, which reduces the stability of c-Myc protein through ERK/AKT/GSK3β feedback pathway, thereby inhibiting the expression of its downstream targets solute carrier family 1 member 5 (SLC1A5), glutathione (GSH) and GLS2, and thereby impairing glutamine metabolism of cells [39]. In TC tissues, SIRT4 levels are significantly reduced compared to non-tumor tissues. In vitro, SIRT4 downregulation in BCPAP cells enhances GDH activity, promotes glutamate and glutamine metabolism, increases ATP production, and ultimately drives cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [40]. In PTC with BRAF V600E mutation, serine/glycine metabolism-related proteins such as phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), phosphoserine aminotransferase (PSAT), phosphoserine phosphatase (PSPH) and serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT) are expressed at a higher rate [41]. PHGDH regulates the invasion and prognosis of TC by regulating the expression of stem cell markers (Oct4, KLF4, Sox2 and Nanog) [42], Relevant data show that after inhibiting the rate-limiting enzyme PHGDH, ROS accumulation in ATC tissue increases, which in turn causes protein oxidative damage. Finally, it inhibited cell proliferation and significantly enhanced the therapeutic effect of lenvatinib and sorafenib [43]. In addition, the overexpression of SHMT2 significantly promoted the metastasis of PTC by inhibiting PTEN and activating AKT signaling pathway [44]. The expression of indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), which catalyzes the degradation of tryptophan to kyrenine in PTC, is up-regulated, and it can shape the tumor suppressive immune microenvironment by regulating the number of FoxP3 + Treg lymphocytes [45]. Lysine crotonylation (Kcr) is highly expressed in PTC, which may affect the occurrence and metastasis of TCs by regulating substance metabolism, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and N-cadherin expression [46]. GSH is an important antioxidant that acts as a cofactor of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) overexpressed in PTC to inhibit the activation of ferroptosis, thereby promoting tumor cell migration [47]. In conclusion, as nitrogen and carbon donors, TC cells can precisely regulate the expression of related metabolic enzymes and the production of metabolites by changing amino acid metabolism to meet their needs for energy, nucleic acid synthesis, signaling pathway regulation, redox balance and epigenetic modification [41].

Overview of exosomes in TC

Exosomes biogenesis and mode of action

As an important carrier of intercellular communication, EVs are mainly divided into three categories: apoptotic bodies, microvesicles and exosomes. Among them, exosomes are small extracellular vesicles with a diameter of 30–150 nm, and ALIX, TSG101, CD9, CD63 and CD81 are their markers. EVs (including exosomes) regulate physiological or pathological processes of cells by delivering amino acids, lipids, nucleic acids, membrane receptors, and metabolites in endocrine, autocrine, and paracrine ways (Fig. 2) [48–50].

Mechanisms of exosomes biogenesis and internalization. Early endosomes (EE) are formed by the fusion of endocytosed vesicles, late endosomes are also known as MVBs, and exosomes are formed by the inward budding of multivesicular body membranes in the form of ILVs, which can be degraded by the lysosome or can also be released from the lumen into the extracellular compartment. Subsequently, exosomes can exert their biological activity through endocytosis, fusion with target cell membranes to release their contents, and direct interaction with cell surface receptors. Exosomes are composed of DNA, RNA, lipids, proteins, MHC receptors, transmembrane tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81), integrins, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), flotillin, ALG-2 interacting protein X (ALIX), heat shock proteins (HSP70s), tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101), Ras-related protein Rab27a (Rab27a), Heat shock cognate 10 (HSC10), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)

The process of exosomes biogenesis involves several key steps. First of all, Cytoplasmic membrane budding inward to receive early endosomes produced by Clathrin-independent endocytosis (CIE) and clathrin-dependent endocytosis (CDE) endocytosis, then intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) are formed (Fig. 2) [51]. The early endosomes then act as sorting stations for cargo, depending on the cargo type and cell specificity, endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent or ESCRT-independent sorting was used. ESCRT consists of five different proteins (ESCRT-0, -I, -II, -III, and AAA atpase Vps4 complex) (Fig. 2) [52]. With the help of Golgi complex, these early endosomes mature into late endosomes. multivesicular bodies containing multiple ILVs are called multivesicular bodies (MVBs). MVB can fuse with lysosomes and autophagosomes for degradation, or with the plasma membrane, resulting in the release of exosomes (Fig. 2) [53].

After being released, exosomes exert their effects in three main ways. First, exosome membrane proteins recognize the receptor of the recipient cell and stimulate downstream signaling. Second, exosomes can be directly delivered to recipient cells through endocytosis, which can be mediated by lipid rafts, clathrin, or caveolin. Third, the plasma membranes of target cells and exosomes fuse with each other to absorb substances in them (Fig. 2) [51]. Additionally, actin drives the pseudopodia of target cells, inducing plasma membrane invagination to form intracellular compartments called macropinosomes, thereby facilitating the uptake of exosomes; meanwhile, immune cells, such as macrophages and dendritic cells, can also envelop extracellular exosomes through membrane deformation, forming phagosomes, which ultimately internalize the exosomes and enable their functional activity [54].

Exosome cargo and role in TC

Exosomal proteins

In view of the differential expression of exosome proteins in different stages of development and types of tumors, they can be applied to early cancer detection and identification of cancer cell types [55]. Proteins are sorted into exosomes through various post-translational modification mechanisms, such as ubiquitination, phosphorylation, and glycosylation [56]. Due to the rich variety and long half-life of exosomal proteins, they can provide rich and stable biological information in TC. With the in-depth application of mass spectrometry detection technology and proteomics tools in exosome proteins, researchers have a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanism of tumor occurrence and development [55].

For example, exosome MHC class I and MHC class II can be used as new structures recognized by immune receptors to induce immune responses by presenting antigens [57]. In addition, exosomal four transmembrane proteins (such as CD9, CD63, CD81, CD37, CD82, CD53) play an important regulatory role in cancer progression, among which the first three proteins are often used as biomarkers of exosomes due to their high content in exosomes [58]. Exosome surface glycoproteins, such as β-galactosidase, O-linker glycan, N-linker glycan, can mediate the chemotaxis and migration of immune cells [59]. Meanwhile, exosome surface proteins, such as TNF, FasL and TNF superfamily ligands, can induce tumor cell apoptosis [60]. The protein SRC is upregulated in serum exosomes of patients with lymph node metastasis of PTC. SRC can modulate pro-tumor functions by activating downstream signaling pathways, such as the MAPK/ERK or FAK pathways (Table 1) [61, 62]. Exosomal annexin A1 (ANXA1) was up-regulated in TC tissues. In the xenograft mouse model, ANXA1 activated TGF-β/Smad2 and Akt signaling pathways by down-regulating E-cadherin expression and up-regulating N-cadherin expression. Promote the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process of TC (Table 1) [63] It is worth noting that changes in urinary exosomal Tg concentration after thyroidectomy or ablation treatment can be used as an indicator of TC recurrence for clinical monitoring [80].

Exosomal MicroRNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a highly conserved family of small tissue-specific non-coding RNAs that are the most abundant of all RNA types in exosomes. As important genetic regulators, miRNAs are involved in the regulation of a variety of developmental and cellular processes by modulating the expression of target genes. Because they are not easily degraded by RNA enzymes, they are stable and thus regarded as key molecules in exosome research [81]. Accumulating data suggest that the expression of various miRNAs is dysregulated in TCs, which can serve as potential cancer biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis by affecting processes such as cell cycle checkpoints and apoptosis, providing new perspectives for cancer screening [82].

For example, Myriem Boufraqech et al. found that miR-145 expression was significantly down-regulated in ATC tissues but elevated in PTC, FTC. It induces G2/M cell cycle arrest by altering the expression of cell cycle protein D1, and inhibits cell proliferation and metastasis by blocking the PI3K/Akt pathway (Table 1) [64]. In addition, miR-145 directly silences Dual specific phosphatase 6 (DUSP6) and inhibits ERK1/2 pathway activation in TC cells, thereby inhibiting TC development and progression (Table 1) [65]. In BRAF-mutated PTC, miR-146b is overexpressed due to the activation of the RET-RAS-BRAF-MAPK signaling pathway, which hinders TGF-β signaling through inhibition of SMAD4 in TC and thus enhances the replication of TPC cells, and is a potential biomarker of lymph node metastasis in PTC (Table 1) [66]. Meanwhile, miR-146b-5p promotes cancer cell proliferation in PTC by inhibiting zinc RING finger 3, a negative regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and induces EMT by downregulating E-cadherin and upregulating N-cadherin (Table 1) [67]. It has been demonstrated that miR-222 is up-regulated in PTC tissues and is a strong predictor of PTC recurrence. By modulating the activation of the PPP2R2A/AKT pathway, miR-222 enhances the migration and invasion of PTC cells in vitro and increases the incidence of distant lung metastasis in nude mice in vivo (Table 1) [68]. MiR-144 is widely down-regulated in PTC and can directly target and inhibit the expression of the pro-proliferative factor E2F8, thereby modulating the role of the G1/S transition regulator Cyclin D1, which ultimately leads to a reduction in the proliferative capacity of PTC cells [83]. Notably, miR-144 also showed low expression in ATC cells and tissues, which enhanced cell sensitivity to cisplatin by inhibiting the function of TGF-α and activating the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway, as well as inhibiting the expression of Ki67, a marker of tumor proliferation, and increasing apoptosis through the inhibition of autophagy (Table 1) [69]. In addition, miR-144 can directly inhibit the translation of its targets, e-calmodulin blockers zeb1 and zeb2, and ultimately inhibit the invasion and migration of TC cells (Table 1) [70].

MiRNAs are considered key mediators in the TME of TC, regulating bioactive molecules that play critical roles in immune response modulation, TME remodeling and tumor metastasis by altering cell cycle protein status, regulating oncogenic signaling pathways, as well as mediating inflammatory cell infiltration and immune escape. Fully investigating the physiological functions and sorting mechanisms of miRNAs not only helps to elucidate their specific roles in TC progression, but also provides new ideas and potential targets for cancer treatment and disease prevention [84].

Exosomal long non-coding RNAs

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) with lengths exceeding 200 nucleotides, which are frequently dysregulated in TC and have emerged as critical regulators in the development and progression of TC, closely associated with cancer progression. They can function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) by competitively occupying the shared binding sequences of miRNAs, thereby influencing the transduction of downstream signaling pathways [85]. Despite the vast diversity of lncRNAs, the roles of most in TC remain unclear, with only a small fraction having been studied.

For example, the lncRNA HOXA-AS2 is upregulated in PTC tissues. It enhances the viability of PTC cells by suppressing the expression of miR-520c-3p and targeting S100 calcium-binding protein A4 (S100A4) (Table 1) [71]. In addition, the expression of DOCK9 antisense RNA2 (DOCK9-AS2), an exosomal lncRNA derived from PTC-CSCs, was up-regulated, which interacted with SP1 and miR-1972 to promote the transcription of catenin beta 1 (CTNNB1) and activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which in turn drove the proliferation and invasion of PTC cells (Table 1) [72]. Wang et al. found that lncRNA GAS5 expression was reduced in PTC and inhibited BRAFV600E gene mutation, angiogenesis, and metastasis in TC through the IFN/STAT1 antitumor signaling pathway (Table 1) [73]. LncRNA AK023507, whose expression is low in PTC tissues, acts through the β-catenin/Wnt signaling pathway to regulate the expression of β-catenin, an epithelial marker, as well as mesenchymal markers, such as vimentin, to prevent the development of PTC (Table 1) [74]. In contrast, lncRNA FALEC expression was increased in PTC tissues and promoted proliferation, migration and invasion of PTC cells by regulating the expression of molecular markers of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (Table 1) [75].

LncRNAs act as regulators of cancer pathways and are involved in the proliferation, migration, apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and invasion processes of TC cells, and correlate with a variety of clinical features (tumor size, histological grade, TNM staging) and prognosis, which can predict the incidence of cancer as well as serve as potent prognostic biomarkers, making them useful for early detection and customized therapeutic control. In-depth investigation of lncRNA function in TC not only expands our understanding of TC pathogenesis, but also provides an important basis for the development of novel biomarkers and personalized cancer treatment strategies [75].

Exosomal circular RNAs

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNAs characterized by their ring-like structure and covalent bonding, and are mainly classified into three types: exonic circRNAs, exon-intronic circRNAs, and cyclic intronic RNAs. These circRNAs are highly stable, abundant, and conserved in vivo, and are formed by reverse splicing followed by 5’-terminal and 3’-terminal junction formation. In recent years, studies have shown that circRNAs play an important role in the diagnosis and treatment of TC [86].

For example, Wen et al. found that circRNA-102,171, whose expression is elevated in PTC tissues, promotes PTC metastasis by interacting with the antitumor-acting gene β-catenin interacting protein 1(CTNNBIP1), which activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, leading to poorer biological behavior (Table 1) [76]. Circ-0079558, whose expression is elevated in PTC tissues, promotes receptor tyrosine kinase expression through the inhibition of miR-26b-5p, and ultimately inhibits PTC apoptosis through the AKT signaling pathway (Table 1) [77]. Elevated blood iodine levels facilitate the mutation of the BRAF V600E gene in PTC. In a high-iodine environment, circ-0004851 acts as a sponge for miR-296-3p, alleviating its inhibitory effect on FGF11. This mechanism promotes the proliferation and invasion of PTC cells (Table 1) [78]. In addition, circ-0067934 upregulated the expression of solute carrier family 7 membrane 11 (SLC7A11), a key gene for the negative regulation of iron death, through sponge miR-545-3p, which reduced the levels of Fe2 + and ROS, thereby inhibiting iron death in TC cells while promoting their proliferation in a mouse model (Table 1) [79].

In summary, circRNAs play a key role in the treatment and prevention of TC, which not only regulate the expression of coding and non-coding RNAs, but also significantly affect the biological characteristics of tumors, including the processes of invasion, metastasis, proliferation and drug resistance. An in-depth study of circRNA function and its mechanism of action will not only help to reveal the pathogenesis of TC, but will also help to develop new strategies for the efficient and economical diagnosis and treatment of TC, and provide new targets and directions for clinical treatment.

Exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming in TC progression

During the development of TC, exosomes play a key role in the relationship between TC cells, stromal cells and immune cells, acting as “couriers” to deliver various bioactive substances to the recipient cells, or directly interacting with them through surface molecules, thus altering the metabolic status of the target cells [87]. By enhancing glycolysis, glutamine catabolism and purine metabolism, exosomes provide more “fuel” for tumors, and at the same time, by delivering lipid mediators, such as cholesterol and phospholipids, they support tumors to survive under harsh conditions. In addition, exosomes can activate AMPK and other signaling pathways, regulate the TME, inhibit the function of immune cells, and help tumors evade the attack of the immune system [88]. This mechanism of exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming not only advances the progression of TC, but also provides new ideas and potential targets for future therapies.

Tumor proliferation

When exosomes carry pro-tumor factors, they not only promote tumor cell proliferation and advance the cell cycle process, but also enhance the metabolic ability of tumor cells, so that the tumor cells can obtain more energy support, in addition, the active ingredients in the exosomes can inhibit apoptosis, thus prolonging the survival time of tumor cells, and contributing to tumor growth and metastasis [89]. Efficient cell proliferation cycle, inhibition of apoptosis and enhanced glycolysis are major drivers of cancer growth. TC cells grow rapidly and metabolically, gaining an overwhelming advantage in the competition for nutrients with normal tissue [14]. Otto Warburg found that even in the case of sufficient oxygen and perfect mitochondrial structure and function, tumor cells will preferentially convert glucose to lactate, and its glycolysis rate is much higher than that of TCA-cycle-mediated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [90]. Exosomal factors accelerate the formation of pre-metastatic ecological niches in multiple cancers by inducing metabolic reprogramming [91]. Accumulation of lactic acid acid acidifies the TME and further promotes oncogenic processes, including tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis and immune escape [2].

Exosomes have been shown to enhance TC growth by inducing glycolysis. For example, circCCDC66 from TC cells was able to interact with miR-211-5p to deregulate the latter’s inhibitory effect on pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4), thereby inhibiting the expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and key enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid cycle and promoting glycolysis in PTC cells Metabolism (Fig. 3) [92]. In addition, up-regulated circPUM1 could sponge miR-21-5 in PTC tissues and cells to promote the expression of MAPK1, which in turn activates the expression of glucose transport proteins (e.g., GLUT1) and HK2, thereby accelerating the glycolytic process (Fig. 3) [93]. circRAD18, which is highly expressed in PTC, promotes the expression of PDK1 protein through the sponge miR-516b, activates the AKT signaling pathway, and promotes cellular glucose uptake in PTC cells to meet the energy demand of tumor cells (Fig. 3) [94]. The expression of circPHGDH is increased in PTC tissues, which enhances the activity of its downstream target PKM2 by inhibiting miR-122-5p, enhancing energy acquisition and biosynthesis, promoting the acceleration of glycolysis, and supporting the proliferation and growth of PTC cells (Fig. 3) [95]. Ji et al. showed that circNRIP1 promotes glucose consumption in tumor cells by up-regulating the expression of PKM2, a key enzyme in glycolysis, and promotes the proliferation of PTC cells (Fig. 3) [96].

Tumor angiogenesis

The thyroid gland is a highly vascularized organ, and its vascular system plays a key role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, being responsible for oxygen delivery, nutrient supply, and metabolic waste removal. However, abnormal vascular proliferation provides cancer cells with growth advantages and becomes an important driver of tumor progression [97]. To combat the TME of ischemia, hypoxia, and acidosis, tumor cells activate angiogenic signaling pathways by receiving signals from exosomes, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) or matrix metalloproteinase (MMP), and increase the viability of vascular endothelial cells (ECs). New blood vessels can be derived through angiogenesis, vascular remodeling or vascular mimicry to meet the needs of tumor cell metabolism [98].

Glycolysis in ECs plays a critical role in angiogenesis, as VEGF promotes tumor angiogenesis by inducing a metabolic shift in ECs from OXPHOS to glycolysis (Fig. 3). PFKFB3, a key enzyme in glycolysis, further drives EC proliferation and angiogenic sprouting [99]. Additionally, exosomes can transfer to target ECs, inducing glycolysis and enhancing angiogenic activity in ECs [6]. CircPVT1 acts as a molecular sponge for miR-195, promoting VEGFA expression by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. VEGF-A induces tumor angiogenesis by acting on VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2 (Fig. 3), and promotes lymphangiogenesis via the VEGFR-2 signaling pathway, thereby influencing TNM staging and lymph node metastasis in PTC [100, 101]. In TC, enhanced glycolysis in tumor cells and the accumulation of metabolic waste collectively shape a hypoxic and acidotic TME. Under these conditions, HIF-1α, a critical mediator of cellular adaptation to hypoxia, significantly upregulates the expression of miR-126. miR-126 targets and suppresses the immunoinflammatory protein high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) through the YAP/Hippo signaling pathway, thereby modulating the secretion of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and inflammatory cytokines. This ultimately inhibits the activity of VEGF-A, disrupts endothelial cell barrier function, and reduces vascular permeability, consequently suppressing tumor cell escape through the bloodstream and distant metastasis (Fig. 3) [102, 103]. Additionally, the increased levels of exosomal miR-21-5p derived from hypoxic PTC cells are transported to endothelial cells, where they significantly enhance angiogenesis activity and density in PTC by targeting and suppressing the anti-angiogenic factors TGFBI and COL4A1 (Fig. 3) [104]. Research by Du et al. demonstrated that miR-548c-3p can inhibit angiogenesis and colony-forming ability in PTC by suppressing HIF1α-mediated VEGF signaling (Fig. 3) [105].

Tumor drug resistance

In the field of TC treatment, a variety of targeted anti-tumor drugs have been developed and applied in clinical practice. For example, vandetanib and cabozantinib are used to treat metastatic MTC; lenvatinib, sorafenib, and cabozantinib are used to treat radioactive iodine (RAI)-refractory progressive DTC; while BRAF and MEK inhibitors such as dabrafenib and trametinib are used to treat ATC. However, long-term use of these drugs often leads to the development of drug resistance, which has become a major challenge in clinical therapy [106]. To counteract the cytotoxic effects of these drugs, tumor cells reprogram their metabolic pathways through exosomes, inducing drug resistance to adapt to the challenges within the TME, thereby promoting their growth and proliferation [107]. Exosome-mediated drug resistance in cancer cells primarily involves accelerating drug efflux, altering the PH of the TME, upregulating anti-apoptotic factors, inducing epigenetic changes, and promoting DNA repair, among other mechanisms [107].

Drug-resistant cancer cells can release exosomes that transfer drug efflux pumps to drug-sensitive cells, reducing intracellular drug concentrations to sub-lethal levels, thereby conferring a drug-resistant phenotype to these cells [108]. For example, in breast cancer, exosomes can transfer the anti-cancer pump P-glycoprotein (P-gp) to sensitive cells, making them resistant to docetaxel [109]. In liver cancer cells, chemotherapy stress upregulates the expression of linc-VLDLR, which in turn promotes the expression of the ATP-binding cassette transporter G2 (ABCG2) efflux pump, reducing chemotherapy-induced cell death [110]. However, the impact of exosome-mediated transfer of efflux pumps on drug resistance in TC requires further investigation. Increased glycolysis and the resulting high levels of lactate are linked to the development of cancer drug resistance. In the acidic TME, the release and cellular uptake of exosomes are significantly enhanced. Low pH is not only a hallmark of malignant tumors but also promotes the fusion of exosomes with tumor cell membranes [111]. In PTC, elevated expression of circ-PRKCI interacts with miR-335, promoting the expression of its target gene E2F3, which not only enhances the expression of cell migration markers MMP2, MMP3, and Snail but also regulates the transcriptional activity of glycolysis-related genes, thereby increasing glucose uptake and lactate production in PTC cells (Fig. 3) [112]. In addition, the overexpression of circ0011290 is associated with poorer outcomes in PTC. Circ0011290 acts on miR-1252 to promote the expression of Follistatin-like protein 1 (FSTL1). FSTL1 directly binds to PKM2 and facilitates its phosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and reduces its ubiquitination, collectively enhancing PKM2-dependent glycolysis and ultimately leading to increased glucose uptake (Fig. 3) [113, 114]. The extracellular acidic environment can upregulate the expression of certain ATP-binding cassette (ABC) family proteins, including P-gp (MDR1 or ABCB1), multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1 or ABCC1), and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP or ABCG2). Furthermore, the acidic environment promotes the ionization of alkaline drugs, hindering their penetration through the cell membrane, and reduces the expression of apoptosis-related proteins, thereby inhibiting cell death (Fig. 3) [115].

Tumor immunosuppression

Immune suppression is a major hallmark of TC progression and dominates the TME in advanced patients. The development of TC is influenced by the state of immune cells, but tumor cells themselves can also inhibit and evade anti-tumor immune cells, thereby creating a microenvironment conducive to their own growth [116, 117]. Komatsuda et al. found that TC tumor cells evade immunity by inhibiting antigen presentation, reducing immunogenicity, upregulating negative immune checkpoint molecules, and secreting soluble mediators [116]. Exosomes released by tumor cells can promote immune suppression and the induction of tumor-associated inflammation by regulating metabolic reprogramming, leading to the formation of an immune suppression network that allows tumor cells to evade detection by the immune system [117, 118].

Specifically, HMGB1 mRNA from tumor cells can competitively bind with the miR-200 family, upregulating the expression of the RICTOR, thereby activating the mTORC2-AKT-C-MYC pathway and promoting glutamine metabolism and PD-L1 expression. The HMGB1-RICTOR interaction leads to increased production of PD-L1 + exosomes, which can inhibit immune cell cytotoxicity and hinder the response to anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy (Fig. 3) [119]. Additionally, tumor-derived exosomal miR-155 induces metabolic changes in adipocytes, promoting the secretion of chemokines CCL2 and CCL5, which in turn recruit and polarize M2-like macrophages through the activation of the STAT3 pathway to support tumor growth (Fig. 3) [120]. Research by Ati Burassakarn et al. shows that exosomes release indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which reduces tryptophan (Trp) levels in the TME by triggering the IL-6/TNF-α axis, promoting the differentiation of CD4 T cells into Tregs, thereby inducing immune suppression (Fig. 3) [121]. Furthermore, exosomal ANXA1 promotes glucose uptake in macrophages by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway, subsequently stimulating an increase in M2 marker levels (Fig. 3) [122, 123].

Exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming in the remodeling of the TME

TME refers to a complex ecosystem in which tumor cells survive and develop, composed of tumor cells and their surrounding immune cells, inflammatory cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, various signaling molecules, extracellular matrix, and surrounding blood vessels [124]. Exosomes, which shuttle within the TME, are important mediators of immune regulation and the control of tumor biological behavior in the TME. Exosomes adjust the metabolic landscape of immune cells in specific ways, and the metabolic reprogramming they mediate not only directly affects the fate of tumor cells but also regulates stromal cells in the TME, acting as a feedback loop. This drives metabolic changes in immune cells or provides the metabolic resources required for cancer progression in poorly oxygenated and nutrient-deficient microenvironments. Such dynamic metabolic interactions influence the immune escape of TC and support the continuous development and metastasis of tumors [84, 125].

CAFs

Under the influence of exosomal TGF-β, cytokines, and chemokines, fibroblasts can transform into CAFs [126]. CAFs can be recruited to tumors from distant sources, such as bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells and adipocytes [127]. They can also arise through EMT and endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT) from epithelial and endothelial cells [127]. The heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs determine their dual functional roles. By activating multiple signaling pathways (including EGFR, JAK/STAT, TGF-β, and Wnt pathways), CAFs influence the formation of the TME and alter the fate of tumor cells and immune cells [128], On one hand, CAFs can secrete growth factors and cytokines such as CXCL5, CCL2, and FGF to promote tumor progression and metastasis. On the other hand, they can also inhibit tumor cell dissemination through IL-6, IL-10, TGFβ, and IFNγ [127, 128]. Exosomes derived from tumor cells and CAFs in the TME can interact with each other, altering metabolic pathways and playing a critical role in influencing the structure of the extracellular matrix (ECM) as well as the metabolic and immune reprogramming of the TME [129].

Studies by Liu et al. have shown that in carcinoma-associated fibroblast-derived exosomes(CDEs), the lncRNA LINC01614 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway by targeting ANXA2 and p65, thereby upregulating the expression of glutamine transporters SLC38A2 and SLC7A5 (Fig. 4). This significantly increases the uptake of glutamine by cancer cells, promoting tumor activity [130]. CDEs contain a complete intracellular metabolite library and, under conditions of nutrient deprivation or stress, provide nutrients to nutrient-deprived cancer cells through a process similar to macropinocytosis. They also reprogram cancer cell metabolism based on nutrient enrichment mechanisms, thereby promoting tumor growth [131]. During co-culture with ATC cells, factors secreted by ATC (such as chemokines and exosomes) upregulate the mRNA expression of GLUT-1 and LDH-A in fibroblasts (Fig. 4). These activated fibroblasts further promote TC progression by secreting soluble factors [132]. Further studies have revealed that exosomes derived from cancer cells reprogram CAFs through the regulatory factor Rab27A, upregulating the expression of MCT4 via the NF-κB p65 pathway (Fig. 4). This facilitates the export of lactate to cancer cells, which, in turn, enhance the expression of MCT1 to utilize lactate for increasing OXPHOS levels, thereby promoting tumor migration [133]. When the TME is nutrient-deprived and rich in metabolic waste, exosomal miR-105 secreted by cancer cells induces MYC to reprogram CAFs. This converts metabolic waste (including lactate and NH4+) into energy-rich metabolites to nourish cancer cells and detoxify, supporting sustained tumor growth (Fig. 4) [134]. Additionally, periostin carried by CDEs is upregulated in the TME by hypoxia-activated HIF-1α, inducing the Warburg effect in TC (Fig. 4). This promotes PTC proliferation and IL-4 expression through the integrin-FAK-STAT3 signaling pathway. IL-4, in turn, induces CAF activation and periostin expression, forming a positive feedback loop that continuously drives tumor progression [135, 136].

Lymphocytes

Lymphocytes are the core components of the immune system, mainly including T cells, B cells, and NK cells, which play a crucial role in the host’s immune response [117]. Studies have shown that the presence of lymphocytes in TCs, particularly CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, and B cells, is positively correlated with a reduction in tumor size [137]. T cells are classified into helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells, and Tregs based on their functions. FoxP3 is a specific marker and important transcriptional regulator of Tregs, which can inhibit the functions of CD8 + T cells, natural killer cells, B cells, and antigen-presenting cells by secreting cytokines, thereby promoting immune escape in TC [138, 139]. Inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 act as immune checkpoints, inducing immune tolerance or promoting T cell apoptosis, thus limiting the intensity and duration of immune responses [140]. Compared to T cells, the infiltration of B cells in the TME is relatively low. B cells are responsible for antibody production, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion. Regulatory B cells (Bregs) play a key role in maintaining peripheral immune tolerance. In patients with DTC, IL-10 produced by CD27 + B cells enhances the survival of activated CD4 + T cells and CD8 + T cells [141]. The abundance of CD56(bright) NK cells is significantly increased in PTC. These cells not only perform immune surveillance functions but can also directly recognize and kill tumor cells, showing a negative correlation with advanced disease stages, while cytotoxic NK cells are positively correlated with the disease stage in PTC patients [142].

PTC-secreted miR-519e-5p acts on CD8 + T cells to inhibit the Notch signaling pathway, thereby altering their glucose and lipid metabolism patterns, reducing energy consumption, leading to decreased granzyme B expression and increased PD-1 expression, ultimately impairing the anti-tumor capacity of CD8 + T cells (Fig. 4) [143, 144]. Additionally, the inhibition of Notch signaling can downregulate the expression of the glucose transporter GLUT1 in Th17 cells, impairing their glucose uptake function and further reducing cellular immune capacity (Fig. 4) [145]. Notably, IL-8 in tumor exosomes excessively activates PPARα, suppressing the expression of GLUT1 and HK2 in CD8 + T cells while upregulating CPT1A and ACOX1, reducing glucose metabolism and promoting fatty acid catabolism, further inhibiting their immune function (Fig. 4) [146]. Under IFN-γ stimulation, the content of HSP60 in thyroid cell-derived exosomes (IFN-γ-Exo) significantly increases (Fig. 4) [147]. HSP60 promotes NADPH synthesis in tumor cells by influencing the pentose phosphate pathway and activates AMPK and 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) to enhance fatty acid metabolism. In PTC, HSP60 can serve as an indicator of lymph node metastasis [148, 149]. Furthermore, IFN-γ-Exo promotes the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors (such as IFN-γ, IL-17 A, IL-22) by CD4 + T cells while inhibiting anti-inflammatory factors (such as IL-4, IL-10, TGF-β1), thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses [147]. Mir-150 targets the Myb gene, altering B cell activation pathways and inhibiting the cell surface presentation function of MHC class II molecules, weakening B cell immune responses (Fig. 4) [150]. MYB can also directly or indirectly inhibit LINC00092, affecting tumor cell glycolysis and promoting PTC EMT [151]. Additionally, miR-150 inhibits glucose metabolism in CD4 + T cells by suppressing solute carrier family 2 member 1 (SLC2A1)/GLUT1, thereby impairing their function (Fig. 4) [152]. NK cell-derived exosomal miR-1249-3p enhances glucose uptake under insulin stimulation by promoting GLUT4 expression in adipocytes and activating the p-Akt and PPARγ signaling pathways (Fig. 4). Simultaneously, miR-1249-3p reduces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) by inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway [153]. The hypoxic TME significantly elevates TGF-β1 levels in tumor cell-derived exosomes, which can inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ in NK cells [154, 155]. Exosomes also induce the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 in NK cells, downregulating the expression of the activating receptor NKG2D, further impairing NK cell activity (Fig. 4) [156] Moreover, EVs significantly reduce the expression of CD71 and CD98 in NK cells by inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway, impairing their glucose uptake capacity and thereby weakening their immune function (Fig. 4) [155, 157].

DCs

DCs are primarily divided into two categories: plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) and conventional DCs (cDCs) [158]. They originate from bone marrow progenitors of common myeloid progenitors (CMP) and are the most potent antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in the immune system. Upon receiving maturation signals, DCs are activated and driven by C-C chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7). This activation leads to the downregulation of antigen-capturing activity and an increase in the expression of MHC and co-stimulatory molecules, initiating and maintaining T cell immune responses to regulate tumor immunity [159, 160]. MDSCs can inhibit the immune activity of NK cells through various mechanisms and are considered markers of defective DC differentiation [161]. In PTC, increased DC infiltration is negatively correlated with recurrence [162]. After mature dendritic cells interact with and uptake tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs), they undergo metabolic reprogramming, which affects DC differentiation and induces phenotypic changes, thereby influencing the host’s anti-tumor immune response [160, 162].

TDEs play a crucial role in the metabolic reprogramming of DCs, particularly through the action of FAs contained within them. The FAs in TDEs promote lipid accumulation in DCs and enhance FAO by activating peroxisome peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma. The products of FAO further promote mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, shifting DC metabolism from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation. This metabolic shift ultimately inhibits DC maturation and the efficient transport of MHC-I peptide complexes to the cell surface, thereby impairing the antigen-presenting function of DCs(Fig. 4) [163, 164] In the regulation of fatty acid metabolism, stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD-1), a key enzyme in de novo fatty acid synthesis, plays a crucial role in ATC. When ATC cells uptake exosomes containing SCD-1 siRNA, the protein expression of SCD-1 is significantly inhibited, blocking fatty acid conversion and leading to lipid metabolism disorder. This disorder increases ROS levels, which participate in the maturation process of DCs through the p38-MAPK and ERK1/2 pathways, upregulating the expression of cell surface molecules MHC-II, CD40, and CD86, ultimately promoting the death of ATC cells (Fig. 4) [165, 166]. Additionally, circ-0002111 is highly expressed in PTC and promotes glucose consumption and lactate production by regulating the level of the glycolysis key enzyme HK2 (Fig. 4) [167]. The accumulation of lactate not only inhibits the normal differentiation process of DCs but also induces the proliferation of MDSCs, thereby weakening the body’s innate immune response to tumors [168]. Thyroid cell-derived exosomes (IFN-γ-Exo) significantly enhance the expression of co-stimulatory factors CD40 and CD80, as well as the maturation marker CD83 on DCs, and also significantly increase the expression level of IL-6 in DCs (Fig. 4) [162]. The IL-6 classical signaling pathway activates STAT3, which reprograms tumor cell glucose metabolism through the SIRT2/LDHA axis, directing glucose flux to anaerobic glycolysis and regulating the generation of pro-inflammatory factors, further influencing the immune regulation of the TME (Fig. 4) [169].

TAMs

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) originate from tumor-infiltrating monocytes in peripheral blood and constitute 30–50% of the stromal cells in the TME. Upon regulation by different signals, their functions are reprogrammed, and they can be classified into two phenotypes: M1-type classical macrophages and M2-type alternative macrophages. These phenotypes regulate the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses in the TME [170]. M1-type macrophages can directly kill tumor cells and recruit and activate immune cells in the early stages of cancer. In contrast, M2-type macrophages promote multiple tumor progression processes, such as tumor growth, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and metastasis, by secreting cytokines, chemokines, or through direct interactions with tumor cells [171]. Tumor-derived exosomes can modulate the polarization degree and range of macrophages by reprogramming metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, promoting their polarization toward the M2-type, thereby facilitating tumor progression [172].

In the TME of TC, M2 macrophages secrete exosomal miR-21-5p, which downregulates the expression of methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) in PTC and ATC cells (Fig. 4). This, in turn, upregulates the CD70 protein, leading to an increase in the number of immunosuppressive regulatory Tregs and the abundance of terminally exhausted T cells. Notably, the downregulation of METTL3 also induces resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy in tumor cells, providing a novel explanation for the mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance [173]. Further studies have shown that low levels of METTL3 can stimulate the expression of serine/threonine stress kinase 4 by inhibiting miR-222-3p. The STK kinase suppresses aerobic glycolysis through the mTOR pathway, thereby inhibiting the malignant phenotype of TC cells (Fig. 4) [174, 175]. Additionally, TC-derived exosomal lncRNA FGD5-AS1 can target miR-6838-5p, stimulating the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes PDK1, PFK1, and PKM2 in tumor cells. This promotes glucose metabolism and lactate production in tumor cells, inducing M2 polarization of macrophages and suppressing T cell activity, thereby enhancing tumor cell migration and invasion (Fig. 4) [176–178]. Research has also demonstrated that lncRNA AK083884 is highly expressed in M2-type exosomes and can inhibit the level of PKM2 protein by targeting the STAT6 signaling pathway. Upon silencing of PKM2, the mRNA levels of M2-type marker genes (such as Arg1, FIZZ1, and YM1) are significantly upregulated, while glycolysis and OXPHOS activities are markedly reduced, and lactate production is notably increased. This further enhances the immunosuppressive function of M2-type macrophages (Fig. 4) [179].

CSCs

The tumorigenic capacity of TC is derived from a subset of CSCs that constitute approximately 1–3% of the total TC cell population. These CSCs exhibit high aldehyde dehydrogenase activity and possess unlimited proliferative potential [180]. CSCs are a group of cells capable of self-renewal and tumor initiation. Key signaling pathways such as Notch, STAT3, Wnt/β-catenin, and Hedgehog (Hh) play significant roles in thyroid CSCs, regulating their proliferation, self-renewal, and angiogenesis. These pathways are also associated with resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and the cytotoxicity of 5-fluorouracil [181]. Compared to CSCs isolated from PTC and FTC, those from ATC demonstrate stronger invasiveness and tumorigenicity [180]. The hypoxic microenvironment in TC not only promotes an increase in the number of CSCs but also enhances their dedifferentiated state, leading to the upregulation of stem cell markers (such as Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog) at the mRNA level [182]. The enhancement of antioxidant defense mechanisms and/or the increase in the glycolysis-to-OXPHOS ratio in CSCs result in reduced levels of ROS, thereby regulating the spheroid-forming ability of TC cell lines [183]. CSC-derived exosomes can mediate intercellular communication by releasing cancer-associated lncRNAs and transport proteins, potentially activating the EMT process and contributing to cancer metastasis [184].

Exosomal lncRNA DOCK9-AS2 derived from PTC-CSCs activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through a dual mechanism: on one hand, it directly upregulates CTNNB1 expression by activating the transcription factor SP1 (Fig. 4), and on the other hand, it indirectly enhances CTNNB1 expression by sponging miR-1972, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation and invasion [72]. In ATC, this leads to a significant increase in the expression of the cytokine CCL4 and its receptor CCR5, which are involved in the recruitment of CD103 + dendritic cells, thereby participating in immune regulation and tumor progression [185]. The interaction between CCR5 and CCL5 plays a crucial role in tumor metabolic regulation, simultaneously activating AMPK and mTOR, which upregulates the expression of glucose transporters in T cells, promotes glucose uptake, drives glycolysis, and ultimately increases ATP synthesis, influencing immune responses in the TME (Fig. 4) [186]. CSC-derived exosomes also play a significant role in the polarization of TAMs and immune suppression. Studies have shown that exosomes secreted by CSCs can regulate the polarization of TAMs toward the M2 phenotype, thereby creating an immunosuppressive environment in the CSC niche. This mechanism further enhances the tumor’s ability to evade immune surveillance [172]. In TC tissues, CSC-derived exosomal lncRNA-MALAT1 is significantly upregulated [187]. This drives the polarization of TAMs toward the M2 type, stimulating the secretion of fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF2). FGF2 not only promotes the release of the inflammatory cytokine IL-10 but also facilitates vasculature formation, thereby supporting the proliferation and metastasis of TC cells (Fig. 4) [188]. However, IL-10 can also induce metabolic reprogramming in NK cells by activating mTORC1, increasing their glucose uptake, enhancing glycolysis and OXPHOS (Fig. 4), and leading to increased expression of IFN-γ, degranulation, and FasL, thereby significantly enhancing their cytotoxic functions [189].

Exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming as a therapeutic target

Exosomes exhibit unique advantages in cancer therapy due to their small size and high internal pressure, which enable them to easily penetrate cell membranes and achieve efficient drug delivery. As drug carriers, exosomes can achieve precise treatment through multiple strategies: First, therapeutic agents (such as small molecules or nucleic acid drugs) can be encapsulated into exosomes and delivered specifically to targeted cells or tissues. Second, the surface of exosomes can be modified through genetic engineering or chemical modifications, endowing them with cell-type-specific targeting capabilities [190]. Additionally, the phospholipid bilayer structure of exosomes provides effective protection for drugs, ensuring high bioavailability and functionality in vivo. Furthermore, exosome-mediated drug delivery systems are characterized by low toxicity, weak immunogenicity, and high engineering potential, making them a highly promising platform for targeted therapy [191].

Targeting metabolic pathways in TC treatment, which exhibits significant metabolic reprogramming, has become a new trend. Currently, targeted drugs addressing TC metabolic reprogramming cover multiple key pathways, including glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism [1]. In glucose metabolism, drugs such as metformin (targeting LDHA and PKM2), 3-BP (an HK2 inhibitor), and Oxalate (an LDH inhibitor) inhibit the glycolytic process, thereby blocking the energy supply of tumor cells [1, 12, 13]. In lipid metabolism, agents like ADP355 and ALY688 (APN receptor blockers), 5-Aza (a leptin receptor blocker), and cholesterol-lowering drugs such as rosuvastatin and simvastatin regulate lipid metabolism-related pathways, suppressing lipid synthesis and utilization in tumor cells [13, 192]. In amino acid metabolism, drugs such as CB-839 (a GLS1 inhibitor), AOA (a GDH inhibitor), NCT503 (a PHGDH inhibitor), SHIN1 (an SHMT inhibitor), and DON (a GLS inhibitor) interfere with the synthesis and utilization of amino acids in tumor cells, inhibiting their growth [1, 192].

In summary, exosomes play a crucial role in the metabolic reprogramming of TC. Therefore, targeting metabolic reprogramming mechanisms through exosomes holds promise for effective TC treatment. Studies have shown that the low expression of circ-100,395 in PTC tissues can inhibit the proliferation and invasion of PTC cells by suppressing the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. This is achieved by reducing the expression of GLUT1, HK2, and LDHA, thereby decreasing glucose uptake and lactate production [193]. In PDTC, miR-143 targets HK2, significantly downregulating its expression and inhibiting tumor cell glycolysis, providing a new direction for anti-cancer strategies in PDTC [194]. Research has also demonstrated that transfecting tumor cell-derived exosomes with miRNA inhibitors, such as miR-155 and/or miR-210, can reverse exosome-mediated metabolic reprogramming in fibroblasts, thereby suppressing tumorigenesis and progression [195]. Additionally, exosomes have been shown to effectively deliver siRNA and metformin to cancer cells, disrupting energy metabolism by modulating mitochondrial oxidative respiration and inhibiting tumor cell growth [196]. Tumor-derived exosomes can specifically deliver proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) to tumor sites, downregulating the expression of the SLC1A5 transporter and GLS, thereby inhibiting glutamine metabolism in tumor cells and reducing intracellular GSH and ATP production [197]. CD39 inhibitor POM1 and AMPK activator metformin have been encapsulated in cancer cell-derived exosomes as nanocarriers targeting the ATP-adenosine pathway, increasing pro-inflammatory extracellular ATP (eATP) levels while reducing immunosuppressive adenosine, thereby decreasing tumor cell oxygen consumption, reprogramming the TME, and enhancing NK cells cytotoxicity and DCs maturation, ultimately improving tumor clearance [198]. Furthermore, the expression of ENTPD2 in tumor-derived exosomes is closely associated with ATP consumption and adenosine accumulation. By promoting the adenosine-A2AR pathway, ENTPD2 mediates ATP-AMP-adenosine metabolism in activated CD8 + T cells, suppressing their function. Inhibiting ENTPD2 can reverse this immunosuppressive state [199].

Conclusion and future perspective

Since their identification, exosomes have rapidly become a focal point in biomedical research. They play a significant role in mechanisms of cancer development, immune regulation, and targeted drug delivery, offering new directions for innovation in clinical diagnosis and treatment methods. Cancer cells and neighboring stromal cells not only engage in bidirectional cargo transfer but also communicate through the release of exosomes, which are taken up by recipient cells, facilitating intercellular communication. This process ultimately drives metabolic dynamics in the TME, promoting tumor adaptation and accelerating tumor progression through various immune evasion mechanisms [200]. Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, representing an adaptive strategy employed by cancer cells to meet the rapidly increasing energy demands. Through this mechanism, cancer cells adjust their metabolic pathways to ensure sufficient energy and biosynthetic materials for rapid proliferation. Exosomes, as information carriers, are widely recognized for their role in regulating gene expression and cellular functions. They play a crucial role in cell communication, metabolic regulation, and disease treatment. Within the TME ecosystem, exosomes influence the adaptive capacity of tumor cells and the functional state of immune cells by altering their metabolic processes. The hypoxic, acidic, and nutrient-competitive TME also affects the production and cargo composition of exosomes [201]. Exosomes mediate the metabolic reprogramming of various cells in the TC TME, ultimately regulating its progression. In TC research, exosomes demonstrate significant therapeutic value. They not only serve as precise drug carriers for targeted delivery but also exert therapeutic effects by modulating immune responses. Furthermore, exosomes can be developed as vaccines to activate the body’s anti-tumor immune response, providing new strategies for TC treatment [202].

In the field of exosomes research, despite significant achievements, there are still many critical questions that require further exploration and resolution. First, the mechanisms by which exosomes achieve precise transport of biomolecules to target specific cells, as well as their potential roles in metabolic regulation, need to be further elucidated. Second, compared to the field of carbohydrate metabolism, research on exosomes in lipid and amino acid metabolism is relatively limited. Delving into these two key metabolic pathways will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the connection between exosomes and metabolic reprogramming. Additionally, much of the research on exosomes remains at the in vitro stage, and their dynamic behavior and functions within the in vivo environment require further validation. Strengthening the exploration of their applications in disease diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation will facilitate the development of exosome-based targeted therapies. Furthermore, the TME is closely related to the progression of tumors at different stages. The levels and contents of exosomes vary depending on the state of the cells, external environmental conditions, and the progression of the disease. It is not yet fully understood how exosomes dynamically adjust their contents under different conditions [203]. Addressing these challenging issues will not only improve the use of exosomes as diagnostic and prognostic markers for TC but also enable the engineering of exosomes to deliver therapeutic substances to specific cells and tissues, advancing the field of precision medicine and opening new pathways for treatment. In summary, the role of exosomes in TC and their impact on metabolic reprogramming are not only fascinating but also provide vast opportunities for in-depth exploration of the TME. These advancements have the potential to lead to groundbreaking breakthroughs in this field.
