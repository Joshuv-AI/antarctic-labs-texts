Genetic reduction or deletion of osteopontin in osteomalacic Hyp mice fails to ameliorate dentoalveolar defects
Authors: Aonjittra Phanrungsuwan, Bella Donnelly, Jinju Kim, Jack Sweet, Flavia Amadeu de Oliveira, Do-Gyoon Kim, Eva S Liu, José Luis Millán, Marc D McKee, Brian L Foster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractX-linked hypophosphatemia (XLH) is caused by inactivating mutations in PHEX, leading to disturbed mineral metabolism, rickets, and osteomalacia. Dentoalveolar defects affecting dentin, cementum, and alveolar bone, contribute to dental abscesses and periodontal breakdown. Limited efficacy of current therapies for ameliorating XLH-associated dentoalveolar effects suggests mechanisms of disease that remain unchecked. Increased production and abnormal distribution of osteopontin (OPN) is thought to contribute to XLH-associated mineralization defects. Osteopontin is a secreted phosphoprotein and mineralization inhibitor, and a substrate of PHEX. To investigate the potential role of OPN in the pathology of XLH-associated dentoalveolar defects, a genetic approach was used to ablate or diminish Spp1/OPN in the Hyp mouse model of XLH. One or both Spp1 alleles in Hyp mice were deleted and tissues were analyzed at 60 d postnatally (dpn). Micro-CT showed no differences in volumes or densities of dentin and alveolar bone in Hyp mice lacking one or both Spp1 alleles, as compared to control Hyp mice. Histological assessment showed no improvements in cementum or periodontal ligament attachment, and dynamic mechanical analysis revealed no normalization of periodontal mechanical properties. While OPN may play a role in regulating dentoalveolar mineralization, diminishing OPN alone was not sufficient to substantially ameliorate XLH-associated defects.

Introduction

X-linked hypophosphatemia (XLH) is the most common inherited form of rickets, affecting approximately 1:20 000 births.1 X-linked hypophosphatemia is caused by loss-of-function variants in the PHEX gene, leading to increased serum levels of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), inorganic phosphate (Pi) wasting, and decreased serum levels of 1,25 dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1,25D). Disturbed systemic mineral metabolism in the form of hypophosphatemia and diminished 1,25D contribute to skeletal mineralization defects, including rickets and osteomalacia, in turn contributing to a lifelong burden of disease that affects quality of life of affected individuals.2–4 Dentoalveolar tissues, that is, the teeth and their supporting structures, are also affected by XLH.5–9 The mineralized tissues, enamel, dentin, cementum, and alveolar bone, all can be compromised by XLH. Developmental mineralization defects in dentin contribute to increased incidence of spontaneous dental infections.8,10 Dental pulp infections can progress to dental abscesses, necrosis, localized swelling, and generalized infection, possibly requiring hospitalization.11 Increased prevalence of periodontal disease is associated with XLH, likely related to cementum and alveolar bone mineralization defects and compromised periodontal attachment and function.8,12

Until recently, therapy for XLH consists of providing Pi and 1,25D oral supplements. When administered consistently from a young age, this treatment provides some limited improvement to rickets and osteomalacia and partially reduces the prevalence of dental abscesses and periodontal disease.13–16 However, the effects of this approach are limited, it is often poorly tolerated, and can contribute to adverse effects, including secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypercalcemia, nephrocalcinosis, even more elevated FGF23 levels, and compliance issues.17,18 A more recently developed therapy using an FGF23-targeting antibody (FGF23ab; burosumab or Crysvita), which is becoming standard-of-care demonstrates positive outcomes by raising serum Pi levels, and reducing rickets severity.19,20 However, long-term outcomes remain uncertain. Moreover, diverging outcomes have been reported for oral health measures, with some studies suggesting an increased prevalence of dental abscesses in patients treated with burosumab compared to the previous treatment12,21,22 These studies have included limited numbers of patients over relatively short periods of time, so additional research is necessary to understand dentoalveolar manifestations of XLH and their resolution. Apparent limitations of both treatments to resolve dentoalveolar defects suggest additional underlying mechanisms of XLH that are unrecognized and persist unchecked with current therapies.

Therapeutic options for XLH have principally focused on correcting the systemic dysregulation of FGF23, Pi, and 1,25D in an effort to normalize mineral ion homeostasis. Consequences of the loss-of-function of PHEX have not been directly addressed by these approaches, particularly the enzyme’s local roles in the extracellular matrices (ECMs) of bones and teeth. PHEX substrates include ECM proteins that regulate mineralization. One of these is osteopontin (Gene: Spp1; protein: OPN), a phosphoprotein that acts as an inhibitor of mineralization and is expressed by skeletal, dental, and many other cell types.23–26 Osteopontin has been shown to be increased in skeletal and dental tissues in XLH as a result of PHEX loss-of-function.27,28 Osteopontin presumably functions as an inhibitor of mineralization via its many negatively-charged aspartic acid and phosphorylated serine residues, as well as through an acidic-serine and aspartate-rich motif (ASARM), allowing it to bind to hydroxyapatite in bones and teeth and inhibit crystal initiation and growth through stabilizing transient amorphous calcium phosphate precursor phases.29–31 Ablation of Spp1/OPN in mice affects dentoalveolar tissue development; a previous study from our lab demonstrated increased dentin and alveolar bone volume and/or density in the absence of Spp1 in mice,32 supporting a developmental role of OPN in dentoalveolar mineralized tissues. Ablation of Spp1/OPN in the Hyp mutant mouse model of XLH allowed some improvements in long bone mineralization.33 In our recent study, we observed that the deletion of Spp1/OPN in Hyp mice resulted in limited improvement in dentoalveolar tissues.34 Several factors may have contributed to reduced rescue from Spp1/OPN deletion, including the genetic background of the mice, which has a profound phenotypic effect,32,35,36 and the interconnectivity and redundancy of OPN with several other mineralization regulators, which may induce compensatory expression changes in other regulators and networks.33,37–39 In this study, we aimed to address these factors, increase samples sizes, include both males and females, and expand our analyses to consider additional functional outputs of the genetic experiment. We analyzed effects of both Spp1−/+ heterozygosity and Spp1−/− homozygosity on Hyp mice. We expanded our scope of analysis to include mechanical properties of periodontal tissues and gene expression in teeth and bone tissues. We hypothesized that reducing OPN either partially or completely would improve XLH-associated dentoalveolar mineralization defects in Hyp mice.

Materials and methods

Mice

Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at The Ohio State University (OSU) and followed ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Hyp mice on a BALB/C genetic background (BALB/cAnBomUrd-PhexHyp-Duk/J; strain 003905) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and have been previously described.40,41 Spp1−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background have been previously described.32,37,38 We backcrossed Spp1−/− mice from C57BL/6 toward BALB/C for 3 generations (achieving ≥87.5% BALB/c background) before setting up heterozygous Hyp;Spp1−/+ females to breed with Spp1+/− males to generate WT, Spp1−/−, Hyp, Hyp;Spp1−/+, and double-null Hyp;Spp1−/− as experimental groups. Phex is located on the X chromosome; therefore, WT (WT;Phex+) males were crossed with heterozygous (Phex+/mut) females to generate WT males and females, Hyp (hemizygous Phexmut) males, and heterozygous (Phex+/mut) females for the study. We included both females and males in experiments. Animals were housed at 25 °C under a 12 h light/12 dark cycle. Mice were fed with standard pelleted chow and allowed access to water ad libitum. Randomization and confounder controls were not used, no mice were excluded, and analyses described below were done blinded in terms of Spp1 genotype.

All mice were euthanized at 60 d postnatal (dpn), equivalent to young adulthood in mice and a time point when the dentition has been in function for approximately 1 mo (n = 5-8 mice/genotype). Maxillae, mandibles, and femurs were collected, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for 24 h, and stored in 70% ethanol until further analysis.

Blood biochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine at 60 dpn. Cardiac puncture was performed using a 21-gauge needle into the chest to collect cardiac blood (100-500 μL). Blood was placed into BD Microtainer blood-collection tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company). After blood collection, tubes were left at room temperature for 25 min, allowing the blood to clot before centrifugation; then, tubes were spun at 15 000 xg for 2 min in the centrifuge. The supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Serum from mice (n = 5/genotype) was analyzed at the clinical pathology laboratory at the College of Veterinary Medicine (The Ohio State University) to analyze the levels of calcium, Pi, and ALP in mouse serum. ELISA was performed to quantify levels of FGF23 using MicroVue mouse/rat FGF23 (C-term) ELISA kit (QuidelOrtho), and PTH levels were quantified by MicroVue mouse/Rat PTH 1-84 ELISA kit (QuidelOrtho).

Pyrophosphate assay

Following blood collection as previously outlined, blood samples were transferred into BD microtainer tubes containing lithium heparin/PST gel (Becton, Dickinson and Company) (50 μL). These tubes were promptly placed on ice and centrifuged at 4716 xg for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and placed into Spin-X columns (Costar 8160, Corning) for a centrifugation at the same speed for 4 min at 4 °C, after which it was stored at −80 °C. Plasma PPi concentrations were quantified using an ATP-sulfurylase-based luminescence assay, adapted from established protocols.42,43 In brief, 5 μL of deproteinized plasma or PPi standard (0.125-20 μM; sodium pyrophosphate tetrabasic or decahydrate; Sigma-Aldrich) was combined with 45 μL of assay buffer containing 90 μM adenosine 5′-phosphosulfate (APS; Sigma-Aldrich), 22.5 mM MgCl₂, and 11.25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), including 0.9 U/mL recombinant yeast ATP sulfurylase (R&D Systems, Inc.). This mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, then heat-inactivated at 90 °C for 10 min in a thermocycler. Two 10 μL aliquots of each sample were transferred to a white half 96-well plate in duplicate. The detection of ATP produced from the PPi conversion was achieved by adding 50 μL of BacTiter-Glo reagent (Promega) to each well. Luminescence was recorded after a 10-min incubation at room temperature in the dark, utilizing the SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices) at a wavelength of 578 nm.

Micro-CT

Fixed mandibles in 70% ethanol were scanned in a μCT 50 scanner (Scanco Medical) with parameters of 70 Kvp, 6 μm voxel dimension, 0.5 mm Al filter, and 1200 ms integration time. Standard hydroxyapatite (HA) phantoms of known density (0, 200, 400, 600, and 800 mg HA/cm3) were scanned using the same parameters and used to construct a standard curve to calculate absolute density units for samples.44 DICOM files were constructed from raw data, exported, and analyzed using AnalyzePro software 15.0 (AnalyzeDirect). The first mandibular molar and associated alveolar bone were segmented as regions of interest (ROI). The ROI was defined as the area between 240 μm mesial from the most mesial point of the mesial root of mandibular first molars and 240 μm distal from the most distal point of the distal root. Enamel was segmented above 1600 mg HA/cm3, dentin was segmented at 650-1600 mg HA/cm3, and alveolar bone was segmented at 450-1600 mg HA/cm3. While alveolar bone is sometimes segmented above 650 mg HA/cm3, defective mineralization in Hyp requires accommodation of density thresholds to more accurately capture bone volumes.40,41 Alveolar bone proper (ABP) was defined as a bone within 240 μm around the tooth root measured radially from root surfaces, to include buccal, lingual, radicular, and interproximal alveolar bone.

Histology

Both sides of maxillae and mandibles from each mouse were harvested, fixed in 10% NBF for 24 h, and decalcified in acetic acid/formalin/sodium chloride (AFS) solution and paraffin-embedded for 6 μm sections as previously described.45 Tissues were stained by H&E, toluidine blue, and picrosirius red (PR) stain.45 Picrosirius red-stained slides were imaged under polarized light microscopy to visualize collagen fiber arrangement in periodontal tissues. Femur sections were stained with safranin O to visualize growth plates.46

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (Sigma-Aldrich) was performed to identify the number of osteoclasts. We quantified osteoclasts in contact with the inner alveolar bone of the mandibular first molars and calculated normalized osteoclasts/mm (n = 5/group), and tissue sections were further analyzed using AnalyzePro software 15.0 (AnalyzeDirect).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using an avidin/biotin-based peroxidase immunostaining kit (Vectastain Elite ABC-HRP kit, Vector Laboratories) to produce a red-brown product (n = 5/group for each target). Primary antibodies included polyclonal rabbit anti-mouse bone sialoprotein (BSP; courtesy of Dr. Renny Franceschi, University of Michigan)47; LF-175 polyclonal rabbit anti-mouse osteopontin (OPN; Kerafast)34; Ab2557 monoclonal rat anti-mouse neutrophil NIMP (Abcam).48

Abscess score analysis

In order to quantitatively assess events that can contribute to dental abscess formation, including dental infection, dental pulp inflammation, and periapical tissue destruction, we used selective histology stains to evaluate and score: odontoblastic organization (reflecting bacterial insult), the presence of inflammatory cells (demonstrating neutrophil influx), and osteoclast cell numbers on alveolar bone surfaces surrounding the apical 1/3 of the molar root (indicating alveolar bone resorption and PDL widening). The organization of the odontoblastic layer in the coronal pulp was analyzed using H&E staining. The details are elaborated in Table S1. The presence of inflammatory cells (neutrophils) was evaluated using IHC with monoclonal rat anti-mouse neutrophil NIMP. We calculated the number of positive cells to evaluate the severity of pulpal infection. Osteoclast quantification was achieved by using TRAP+ to stain active osteoclasts. The length of the alveolar bone surrounding the maxillary and mandibular first and second molars was quantified by carefully tracing the bone surface in proximity to the teeth. In addition, osteoclast quantification was performed through manual counting, enabling the calculation of the number of osteoclasts per unit length of the dental socket.

The abscess scores were determined by classifying each characteristic into 4 categories: healthy pulp, mild infection, moderate infection, and severe infection. Each category was assigned a point score as follows: healthy pulp = 1 point, mild infection = 2 points, moderate infection = 3 points, and necrotic pulp = 4 points, applicable to both maxillary and mandibular first and second molars. Consequently, we assessed 4 teeth per mouse (n = 4-5/group), resulting in a minimum possible score of 4 and a maximum possible score of 16.

Dynamic mechanical analysis

We previously described the use of dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) to investigate the in situ mechanical stability of teeth at the periodontal socket in mice.36,40 Unfixed mandibles were wrapped in PBS-soaked gauze and stored at −20 °C until use. Hemi-mandibles (n = 5/genotype) were mounted in low expansion fast-setting laboratory plaster (Whip Mix) to stabilize them within the jig for a cyclic loading. Embedded samples were mounted on the stage of an electromagnetic loading machine (Bose Corporation). The machine is equipped with a high-resolution (15 nm) displacement transducer. The load cell capacity used in this study was ±45 N (10 Ibs). Loading was applied approximately 10 min after the hemi-mandible was placed in the plaster following 9 min setting time of the plaster. The irregular occlusal surface on teeth was flattened by adding the plaster to obtain uniform loading. Hemi-mandibles were maintained hydrated during the whole process of experiment with PBS-soaked gauze. A preload of 2 N was applied to confirm contact between loading jig and specimen. Dynamic mechanical analysis was performed by applying non-destructive compressive oscillatory displacement (−0.01 ± 0.005 mm) in the frequency range of 1-3 Hz. Dynamic complex stiffness (K*) was calculated by an equation of K* = K′ + iK″ using elastic (storage) (K′) and viscous (loss) (K″) stiffness. Tangent delta (tan δ) accounts for the energy dissipation ability. The values of the DMA parameters were averaged across frequencies.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Gingiva and soft tissues were removed from the alveolar bone. First, second, and third molars were physically extracted from the mandibles. Total RNA was collected from alveolar bone cut from the mesial aspect of the first molars to the distal aspect of the third molars, and RNA was also collected from molars separately. RNA was isolated from tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) under liquid nitrogen with tissue grinding by mortar and pestle. Reverse transcription was achieved using Superscript IV VILO with ezDNase as recommended by the manufacturer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After reverse transcription, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using TaqMan probes targeting 11 genes and 1 housekeeping control (GAPDH) loaded into 96-well plates (details in Table S2). TaqMan probe sequences are listed in Table S2. Target genes included: tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (Alpl), progressive ankylosis protein (Ank), dentin matrix protein 1 (Dmp1), dentin sialophosphoprotein (Dspp), ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase phosphodiesterase 1 (Enpp1), fibroblast growth factor 23 (Fgf23), BSP (Ibsp), matrix extracellular phosphoglycoprotein (Mepe), Phex, osteopontin (Spp1), and sclerostin (Sost). RT-qPCR was achieved using Quantstudio 3 real-time thermocycler using TaqMan Fast Advance Master mix following the standard protocol directed by the manufacturer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Analysis of samples was completed by ExpressionSuite Software.

Statistical analysis

Mean ± SD values are shown in all graphs. Continuous data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA test and post-hoc Tukey test. Normality was tested using Shapiro–Wilk test. Discrete data were analyzed with by Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s test to compare between 5 groups. p-values <.05 were considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 10.0 was used to perform statistical analysis and graph design.

Results

Total or partial ablation of the Spp1 gene does not alter blood biochemistry or appendicular skeletal defects in Hyp mice

We measured serum levels of mineral metabolism markers in all experimental groups at 60 dpn, including calcium (Ca2+), inorganic phosphate (Pi), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi), fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), and PTH (Figure 1). Ca2+ levels were unchanged across genotypes. Pi levels were consistently reduced ~40% across all Hyp groups regardless of Spp1 genotype, and ~5-fold elevated FGF23 levels were similarly unaffected by Spp1 status in Hyp mice. ALP levels were similarly elevated across all Hyp mouse groups vs WT and Spp1−/− controls. PTH levels showed a tendency to increase with either Spp1 deletion or in Hyp mice, and Hyp;Spp1−/+ mice showed a significant increase over WT. Circulating PPi levels showed no differences among groups.

Before analyzing dentoalveolar tissues, we assessed appendicular bone parameters in femurs. Hyp mice exhibited striking defects in femurs, including cortical bone (CB) with reduced thickness and increased porosity, and reduced trabecular bone (TB) and disorganized growth plates, compared to WT and Spp1−/− controls (Figure 2A). Femur phenotypes appeared unaffected by total or partial Spp1 ablation in Hyp mice at 60 dpn. Analysis of TB confirmed ~40% reduced TB volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV) and ~60% reduced trabecular number (Tb.N) measurements in Hyp vs control mice; these changes were not ameliorated by Spp1 ablation (Figure 2B). Analysis of CB confirmed ~25% reduced cortical thickness (Ct.Th), ~30% reduced cortical area fraction (Ct.Ar/Tt.Ar), dramatically increased cortical porosity (Ct.Po), and ~10% decreased cortical BMD, in Hyp vs control mice. None of these parameters were ameliorated by complete or partial Spp1 ablation.

Total or partial ablation of the Spp1 gene does not improve dentoalveolar defects or periodontal mechanical properties in Hyp mice

Micro-CT revealed thin dentin, widened pulp chambers, and reduced alveolar bone in Hyp mice with or without the Spp1 gene, compared to controls (Figure 4A). Compared to controls, Hyp mice exhibited ~30% reduced cementum/dentin volume, ~25% reduced alveolar bone density, ~60% ABP volume, and ~25% reduced ABP density; all affected parameters failed to improve with complete or partial Spp1 ablation (Figure 4B).

Functional outcomes are not improved by total or partial ablation of the Spp1 gene in Hyp mice

Dynamic mechanical analysis previously revealed significantly disturbed mechanical stability of teeth in the periodontal socket of Hyp vs control mice, reflecting compromised periodontal mineralization and attachment.40 Reduced dynamic stiffness (K*) of Hyp vs control mice were not improved by the deletion of Spp1 gene (Figure 5F). Increased energy dissipation (tan σ) of Hyp vs control mice tended to decrease with Spp1 deletion but was not different from values in either Hyp or control mice.

An abscess scoring system was developed to evaluate and quantify odontoblast disorganization (indicating negative effects of invading bacteria), presence of neutrophils in the dental pulp (indicating inflammatory response), and presence of osteoclasts on AB surfaces in the apical portion of the tooth (indicating progression toward periapical bone loss) (as described in more detail in Table S1). Compared to controls, Hyp mice exhibited greater degrees of odontoblast disorganization and neutrophils, though abscess score sums did not clearly show changes resulting from Spp1 ablation (Figure S2). Ablation of Spp1 did not provide a clear outcome; rather, mice lacking one or both Spp1 alleles showed scores that were not different from either controls or Hyp mice.

Altered gene expression related to Phex mutations or Spp1 KO

To identify genes responsive to Phex mutations and/or Spp1 ablation, we performed qPCR for mineralization regulators using total RNA collected separately from molars (representing mostly odontoblast-pulp expression) and alveolar bone (representing mostly osteoblast and osteocyte gene expression). In molars, Dspp increased in Hyp vs other mice and Dmp1 expression increased in response to Spp1 deletion, though we failed to observe other consistent changes related to genotype (Figure S3). In alveolar bone, Sost mRNA was decreased in Hyp mice and unaltered in other groups, though few other gene changes were recognized (Figure S4).

Discussion

We assessed the contribution of OPN to mineralization defects associated with XLH, using the Hyp mutant mouse model. Genetic ablation of one or both copies of the Spp1 allele did not improve biochemical alterations, skeletal or dentoalveolar mineralization defects, PDL attachment, or periodontal mechanical properties of Hyp vs control mice. RT-qPCR experiments did not aid in identification of factors compensating or cross-regulating dentoalveolar tissues in Hyp mice in the absence of Spp1/OPN. These results suggest OPN accumulation in XLH, in isolation, is not the major driver of the mineralization defects in the dentoalveolar tissues, at least in isolation. However, OPN may be contributing to disrupted mineralization in teeth through other direct or indirect mechanisms, though underlying mechanisms and functional consequences require further experiments.

Osteopontin is implicated in XLH-associated skeletal and dentoalveolar defects

Individuals affected by XLH experience an increased prevalence of dental abscesses and periodontal disease thought to arise from mineralization defects in dentin, cementum, and alveolar bone, which persist after Pi and 1,25D therapy.7,10,13 The more recently developed FGF23-neutralizing antibody (burosumab, Crysvita) does not appear to fully mitigate these dental defects and downstream manifestations, based on a small number of clinical trials and case reports to date, which have limitations of small sample sizes and short duration.21,22,49 These findings to date, as well as the fact that burosumab targets FGF23 and would only ameliorate downstream effects of this factor, suggests an incomplete understanding of the underlying pathologic mechanisms associated with XLH and lack of approaches to adequately counteract them.

The secreted phosphoprotein, OPN, accumulates in the ECM of bones and teeth in XLH and in the Hyp mouse model.27,28,33,34,50–52 Osteopontin is a member of the small integrin-binding ligand N-linked glycoprotein (SIBLING) family, a subgroup of secretory calcium-binding phosphoproteins, which regulate mineralization processes in bones and teeth.53–56 Osteopontin functions as a mineralization inhibitor via multiple aspartic acid residues and phosphorylated serines, as well as a highly negatively charged polyaspartic acid sequence and an ASARM region.23,57 OPN inhibits hydroxyapatite crystal growth in cell-free systems26,58 and regulates the axial and the appendicular skeleton, dentin, and alveolar bone in mice.24,32,37 OPN is a substrate for PHEX, which can extensively degrade and inactivate its mineralization inhibitory motifs, but in XLH this enzymatic activity is lost.23,28 Therefore, accumulating evidence to date suggests that increased and abnormally distributed OPN in XLH patients and the Hyp mouse model may act as a local mineralization inhibitor contributing to skeletal and/or dentoalveolar defects. Examining this notion was the basis of the current study.

Limited effects from genetic deletion of osteopontin in Hyp mice

We aimed to reduce OPN in the Hyp mouse model of XLH and determine the effects of this manipulation on the dentoalveolar defects. Effects of OPN reduction on the appendicular skeleton were compared to dentoalveolar tissue outcomes here and results from prior studies. In this study, we genetically ablated Spp1 in Hyp mice, examining both homozygous Spp1 KO (Hyp;Spp1−/− with no remaining Spp1 alleles) and heterozygous Spp1 deletion (1 remaining Spp1 allele). The rationale for including heterozygous Hyp;Spp1−/+ mice is derived from prior work documenting that expression of Spp1/OPN is closely linked to and co-regulated alongside several other mineralization regulators that control PPi levels, including tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (Alpl/TNAP), progressive ankylosis protein (Ank/ANK), and ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase phosphodiesterase 1 (Enpp1/ENPP1).29,38,39 The mineralization regulatory network associated with OPN is not yet fully elaborated or understood. By including the heterozygous Hyp;Spp1−/+ mice in the present study, we aimed to determine if partial genetic reduction of Spp1/OPN could ameliorate the XLH phenotype in Hyp mice without triggering regulation of Alpl, Ank, Enpp1, or as-yet unidentified factors, which could counteract or obfuscate phenotypic effects following the deletion of OPN.

No significant improvements were noted in femur trabecular or CB of 60 dpn Hyp mice when Spp1 was deleted. These results partially agree with a prior study that found persistent defects in tibia length, growth plate disorganization, and cortical porosity in Hyp;Spp1−/− vs Hyp mice at 1 mo.33 In the prior study, amelioration of osteoid was observed in lumbar vertebrae but not calvariae of Hyp;Spp1−/− vs Hyp mice. Differing results between the studies should be considered in the light of the different mouse genetic backgrounds (129 here vs C57BL/6 in the previous study), ages (60 vs 30 dpn), and target locations (femur vs tibia, vertebrae, and calvariae). Prior studies document more considerable improvements in skeletal tissues from Spp1 ablation. Spp1 homozygous deletion improved long bone and vertebral mineralization in Alpl−/− mice at 10 dpn.38 Spp1 homozygous deletion improved mineralization in femurs, tibias, and vertebrae in Phospho1−/− mice at 1 and 3 mo.37 Both of those factors are enzymes associated with pericellular and matrix vesicle Pi and PPi levels, prompting the observation that OPN deletion may be more successful at combatting some mineralization defects than others, based on the genes and underlying mechanisms involved.

Partial or complete deletion of Spp1 did not promote any significant improvements in dentin, cementum, alveolar bone, or periodontal attachment in Hyp mice at 60 dpn in this study. This observation is consistent with our previous findings, which indicated no substantial differences in dentoalveolar tissue volumes or densities in Hyp;Spp1−/− vs Hyp mice at 1 mo.34 We expanded the scope of our analyses here to provide functional outputs that would provide additional criteria for evaluation. First, we created a novel scoring system to evaluate the progression toward dental abscess formation in Hyp mice. This approach is based on prior publications,59,60 but adapted in light of our observations of pulp necrosis in Hyp mice.61 First, we analyzed odontoblast layers for disruption caused by bacterial insults and inflammation.60,62 Second, we evaluated inflammatory cell influx by counting neutrophils in the pulp. Pulpal infection can extend to the periapical tissues, leading to apical alveolar bone destruction and PDL widening.63 Therefore, third, we counted the number of TRAP+ osteoclasts around the tooth apex. While Hyp mice demonstrated disorganized odontoblasts and increased pulp neutrophil numbers vs control, Spp1 deletion did not seem to improve these conditions. We also added an additional functional outcome, dynamic mechanical testing (DMA), which showed Spp1 deletion was not successful in normalizing altered mechanical properties previously reported in Hyp mice.40

Given the hypothesis that complete deletion of Spp1 may disrupt the expression levels of other mineralization regulators, we used RT-qPCR to assay 11 genes associated with OPN, XLH, or dentoalveolar mineralization, in both molars and alveolar bone. Phex mRNA was detected in both molars and bone, consistent with the mice carrying mutations but not deletion of this gene; no expression patterns were noted across genotypes. Molars did not reveal significant differences in the expression levels of most genes between groups, except for Dmp1. Dmp1 expression was increased in Spp1−/− and Hyp;Spp1−/+ mice but was not different in Hyp vs control. Up-regulation of Dmp1 in response to Spp1 deletion is consistent with prior reports of co-regulation and counter-regulation between SIBLING proteins,64,65 particularly a report that Dmp1 mRNA and DMP1 protein increased in odontoblasts of Spp1−/− mice following dentin injury.66 Fgf23 expression in molars showed no pattern across genotypes. While Fgf23 is expressed by odontoblasts and cementocytes in the molar,67 Fgf23 expression was much less responsive in these cells compared to alveolar or appendicular bone.40

Alveolar bone showed no major alterations in the expression of mineralization-related genes across mouse groups, with the exception of Fgf23, which exhibited a marked increase in expression in Hyp;Spp1−/− mice compared to controls (with trends in circulating FGF23 levels noted as well). Fgf23 mRNA appeared to trend higher in alveolar bone of all Hyp mice, though did not show as significantly increased with multiple comparisons. Our previous study reported increased Fgf23 mRNA in alveolar bone similar to increases in femurs.40 In the preceding study on Spp1 deletion from Hyp mice,33 the analysis of gene expression in the tibiae of Hyp;Spp1−/− mice demonstrated a significant upregulation in the expression of Ank and Enpp. In this context, we observed that the alveolar bone in Hyp mice exhibits a distinct response to Spp1 KO compared to tibiae. Increased FGF23 could be a contributing factor to persistent alveolar bone defects because FGF23 is a hormone that is released by osteocytes and osteoblasts to regulate 1,25D production and Pi absorption.68–70 Local effects of FGF23 are not well delineated. We cannot rule out gene or protein expression changes in other organs and tissues that may be affecting systemic or local changes in skeletal and dental tissues.

Mineralization defects in XLH are likely multifactorial and other, yet untested factors, possibly contribute to the dentoalveolar defects. Reduced 1,25D and Pi and increased FGF23 are contributors to dental defects, though do not explain the full extent.61,71 Acidic-serine and aspartate-rich motif peptides from multiple SIBLING proteins may be less cleaved in XLH.23,28,52,57 Reduced TNAP activity and increased PPi have been reported in XLH.72 Other signaling pathways or functional protein groups not yet identified may be dysregulated in some manner by XLH.

We pursued one other approach to reduce OPN levels through pharmacologic means, though data are not included. Several prior studies successfully neutralized OPN using a targeting antibody.73–78 We subcutaneously injected Hyp mice with the 100D3 monoclonal anti-mouse OPN antibody (OPNab), effective at reducing colorectal tumor growth at a dose of approximately 8 mg/kg.78 Another study employing a different OPN-neutralizing antibody reported efficacy at reducing sepsis-induced lung injury at a dose of 50 μg/mouse, approximately 2 mg/kg.76 We administered OPNab at a dose of 10 mg/kg twice weekly. OPNab largely did not affect biochemical measurements and failed to improve trabecular and cortical femur parameters, alveolar bone volume or density, or first molar cementum thickness, PDL attachment, or periodontal mechanical properties. The results confirmed findings of the genetic approach. Though prior work validated the approach, we could not confirm OPNab levels, half-life in circulation in our model, or inactivating ability, so data were not included.

Conclusions and limitations

Within the constraints of our study on Hyp mice, we conclude that OPN, in isolation, does not appear to be a predominant factor in the dentoalveolar mineralization defects observed in XLH. There are notable limitations in our study approach that are important for interpretation. We include both male and female Hyp and WT mice to enhance scientific rigor and reproducibility and noted that XLH-associated dentoalveolar defects outweighed sex-related differences. However, potential for sex-related differences due to the location of PHEX on the X chromosome may complicate interpretation of outcomes. Our RT-qPCR analysis was limited to 11 genes. Alterations in other locally expressed genes, factors from other tissues, endocrine alterations, and/or regulation of factors through means other than transcription, cannot be ruled out. In particular, PHEX has other substrates, including matrix extracellular phosphoglycoprotein (MEPE), a protein closely related to OPN, and its potential involvement in mineralization defects in XLH remains uninvestigated.50,52 Gaps remain in understanding the potential roles of OPN and other factors in XLH-associated pathology and should be explored further.
