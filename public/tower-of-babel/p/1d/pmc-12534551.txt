An investigation of the effect of GMAW and SMAW processes on mechanical and microstructural properties of welded E350 grade steel
Authors: Vikash Kumar, Subodh Kumar Yadav, K. Vijetha, Vaishali Mendhe, Anita Kumari, Vipin Shrivastava, Geetesh Goga, Anurag Joshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the construction industry, power plants, shipbuilding, aerospace, automotive, oil and gas, and a wide variety of other industrial sectors, IS2062 steel is in high demand as a structural material. Gas metal arc welding (GMAW) process and shielded metal arc welding (SMAW) process, which are both traditional fusion arc welding methods, are utilised extensively for joining structural materials. This study presents the results of a comparative investigation into the mechanical properties and metallurgical characteristics of IS2062 E350 grade steel that has been fusion welded by GMAW and SMAW processes separately. Correlations were found between microstructures and the evaluation of tensile strength, impact strength and microhardness. Both GMAW and SMAW welded joints reached maximum transverse ultimate tensile strengths of 634 and 676 MPa, respectively. As an additional point of interest, the overall percentage of elongation for the GMAW and SMAW processes was 25.49% and 26.19%, respectively. Impact toughness of 235 J and 213 J was measured for joints that were created using GMAW and SMAW, respectively. When compared to other regions, the microhardness that was produced in the subcritical heat-affected zone was lower. The maximum microhardness obtained for GMAW and SMAW are 175 VHN and 185 VHN respectively. The investigation found that GMAW joints exhibited qualities that were somewhat better than those of SMAW joints. It has been discovered that the majority of weld failures occur in the subcritical heat affected zone. The reason behind these failures is that the weldment softens as a result of grain deformation.

Introduction

The most popular and adaptable welding technique in the fabrication and manufacturing industry is arc welding which is a fusion welding technique. In an arc welding, the heat generated between the electrode and base metal is utilized to melt the base materials, usually accompanied by the addition of a filler material to form a joint. It is an important step for producing a strong and long joint which includes metals, alloys, and thermoplastic. As welding plays an important role in many applications ranging from automotive to aerospace and construction, it is, therefore, important that one should understand the parameters that affect weld quality for the optimization of the welding process. There are a number of key parameters in fusion welding, all of which play an important role in the quality and integrity of the welded joint, such as the heat supply into materials being welded, mixture of shielding gases, welding speed, and types of filler wire. The heat intensity is essential for the melting of the base metal and filler electrodes. It also affects the mechanical as well as metallurgical properties of the weld joint1–3.

There should be enough control in the rate of heat input so as to avoid rapid thermal cycles which can cause distortion or permanent softening of the joint. Also, selecting and using the correct shielding gas is critical for protecting the weld area from contamination from atmospheric constituents, especially oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen. These are inert or semi-inert gases that help in avoiding oxidation or porosity or any other defect in the root of the welds as it affects the structural integrity of welds. Along with the gas type, the flow rate determines the capability of welding4.

Fusion arc welding method are divided into a few subtypes, each having its own technique to fuse the metals. The key principle involved in all these fusion welding processes is the heating of the base material to achieve a molten stage where they fuse together and form a solid joint on cooling. The most widely used arc welding processes are GMAW, GTAW, and SMAW for joining structural materials, particularly steels both in similar and dissimilar combinations5–10. It can be used in both AC/DC power source types which means it provides flexibility in its application based on the requirement of weld and material being welded11,12. GMAW and SMAW processes employ a unique set of welding techniques and applications for the purpose of efficiently and effectively weld joints13. By establishing an arc between a continuous, copper-coated consumable solid filler wire and the metal that is being welded, the GMAW technique is an example of a fusion arc welding procedure. When it comes to joining steel alloy plates of varying thicknesses, the GMAW process is the method of choice because of its many benefits, including its attractive appearance, low failure rate, low cost, ease of mechanisation, and high efficiency14–16.

SMAW is another notable fusion arc welding techniques. It is typically utilised for the welding of carbon steels and possesses the highest degree of flexibility among all of the welding processes. This technique’s versatility is truly remarkable: not only can it weld ferrous and nonferrous metals, but it can do so in any position (flat, vertical, horizontal, and overhead), with practically any thickness of base metal, even in windy or otherwise inaccessible places. Welding factors have a significant impact on the qualities that are pertinent to certain applications. Filler materials used in SMAW and different types of electrodes and composition of the shielding gases used in GMAW determine the quality of the welded joints. Different welding inputs have a considerable effect on the metallurgical and mechanical strength of the weld bead. The Total Accumulated Weld Volume and different groove angle also affect the ultimate tensile strength of the welded joints17–19. As a result of the research conducted by Kumar et al.20, it was discovered that the tensile strength of the welded joints improves when the percentage of carbon dioxide in the shielding gas mixtures decreased. The toughness of the joint increase as the percentage of carbon dioxide increases in the shielding gas mixture. When the shielding gas comprised a composition of Argon 92% and Carbon dioxide 8%, a higher hardness value was attained. The investigators Ibrahim et al.21 during the course of their investigation, they discovered that the penetration grew as the value of welding current increased. When the electrical current 90 A, the hardness value was higher, but it gradually decreased until it reached 150 A. At 210 A, the value increased slightly more than 150 A. By using SMAW and GMAW, Hariprasath et al.22 were able to successfully weld high strength low alloy steel grade DMR249A. Authors found that SMAW and GMAW joints had an impact hardness of 174 J and 178 J respectively.

Arc voltage was shown to be particularly important in increasing heat affected zone (HAZ) by Tagimalek et al.23 due to the fact that it has the ability to generate a compressive weld pool and a greater heat input. The arc voltage influences the width and size of the HAZ. The thickness of the HAZ increased in proportion to the increase in arc voltage24,25. Coarser grains appear as a result of a wider weld pool caused by an increase in the electrode wire feed rate23. For high strength steel material (LNE 700) with a thickness of 4.5 mm and filler metals with a diameter of 1 mm, the smallest possible HAZ was attained by using a welding speed of 10 mm per second, 23.5 V, and a wire supply rate of 12.6 m per minute24. So, the width of the HAZ was claimed to be a function of the electrode wire supply speed25.

HAZ was well-defined by Lermen et al.24, who established that it also depends on the welding speed. They found that the width of HAZ decreased due to the welding speed increased. The welding speed significantly influences the cooling rate and HAZ during and after welding. The welding speed decreases; penetration increases. The cooling rate indicates the ultimate microstructure of the weld bead26. Khamari et al.27 explored the SMAW joints for mild steel plates with factors such as current and base metal thicknesses. They mentioned that penetration improved with a rise in current, and maximum penetration was observed in thicker welded joints than in thinner ones. Chiong et al.28 inspected the impact of current and electrode composition on metallographic and mechanical features of carbon steel joints employing SMAW. Hardness, toughness, and impact strength of weldment are largely improved with the variation of welding current. These improvements in mechanical characteristics are due to the development of acicular or dendrite ferrite, refined grains, and additional alloying elements. Yadav et al.29 evaluated the influence of variables and edge preparations in SMAW of steel weldments. Increasing welding current and speed, strength, impact energy, and hardness drop for straight edge joint while increasing except hardness values for single bevelled and single V groove joints. The best mechanical characteristics were testified for a single V groove joint. It was discovered by Widodo et al.30 that the changes in SMAW welding current have an effect on the metal of grade AISI 304. The researchers used electric currents of 70Amp, 80Amp, and 90Amp to determine the influence of the variation of mechanical strength. Additionally, researchers discovered that the specimens of current 70A had a tensile strength value of 498.66 MPa, which was the lowest value ever measured.

While earlier studies have explored the influence of welding parameters on different steels, very few have focused on a direct comparison between GMAW and SMAW processes for IS2062 E350 grade steel. This work is distinctive as it combines mechanical testing (tensile, impact, and hardness) with advanced microstructural analysis techniques such as SEM, EDS, and XRD. By linking the observed microstructural changes with variations in mechanical properties, this study provides a deeper understanding of how the two processes perform under identical conditions, offering practical guidance for selecting the appropriate welding method in structural applications.

The welded sample as shown in Fig. 4 have been undergone mechanical test. These tests consist of bending and unbending tests, tensile test which are carried out on the samples by means of a Servo-hydraulic tensile testing machine that is capable of putting one thousand kilonewtons31,32. The ASTM E8-M4 standards were followed in order to generate two different numbers of tensile specimens for each of the welded samples. The transverse section of the welds was uses as the specimens for analysis. The weld zone must lie in the middle of the of the tensile test specimen. The yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and percentage elongation (%) were taken during the testing process. The tensile tests were conducted under ambient laboratory conditions (27 ± 2 °C, 55–60% relative humidity) with a constant crosshead speed of 2 mm/min, following ASTM E8-M4 standards. The bend tests were performed using a mandrill with a diameter of 38 mm and an angle of bend of 180 degrees in accordance with the criteria of ASME IX. These examinations were carried out with the intention of determining soundness of the welds from the face and root sides. All bending operations were performed at room temperature (27 ± 2 °C), ensuring that no external thermal effects influenced the results.Fig. 4Welded sample of (a) GMAW and (b) SMAW.

Under room temperature conditions, the Charpy impact test was performed on a specimen of welded samples of dimension 10 mm by 6 mm. The test of the samples was performed using the impact testing machine at the centre of the weld. The results of the test were analysed by taking the average of three impact test specimens that had been made from each weld in accordance with ASME IX. Charpy V-notch impact tests were carried out at ambient laboratory temperature (27 °C). The average of three readings was reported for each welded joint.

For the micro-hardness and metallurgical analysis, the welded specimens were cut transversely middle way using wire cut electric discharge machining in the dimension of 25 × 15x15 mm from each welded joint at the weld centre, then grinded with sand paper and polished to diamond paste. Hardness testing was conducted on the Zwick/ ZHV30 Vickers hardness tester. The measurements of the hardness were taken on the weld bead in the transverse direction at the interval of 0.5 mm apart using a half-kilogram weight and fifteen seconds of loading time. A load of 500 g was applied for a dwell time of 10 s, and indentations were taken at room temperature (27 ± 2 °C, 55–60% relative humidity). The indentations mark were taken at the different locations on the polished surface, beginning with the base metal, the HAZ, and the fusion zone. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used in the microscopic examinations undertaken for the purpose of examining the morphology and microstructure of the specimen. The samples were subjected to etching by using etchant of 5% Nital which is a solution of nitric acid in ethanol for 10 s. This was done for the purpose of conducting a microstructure features examination. An investigation of the microstructure image of the weld metal zone, interface zone, the HAZ, and the parent metal was carried out with the assistance of an Axio Zeiss optical microscope and SEM. All mechanical and metallurgical tests were performed under controlled laboratory conditions at 27 ± 2 °C and 55–60% relative humidity, to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

The Weld joints made by using a supply of the higher electrical current generate more heat input to the weld, thereby reducing the cooling rate. Consequently, the formation of grain boundary of soft and ductile phases occurs. This phase includes both acicular ferrite and polygonal ferrite.

Micro hardness evaluation

Vickers hardness tester has been used to assess the microhardness of GMAW and SMAW joints at the different zones. Indentations were performed to measure the Vickers hardness number (VHN) in the weld metal zone, fine grain HAZ, coarse grain HAZ and base metal. Figure 13 shows the results of Vickers hardness number (VHN) under the application of 0.5 kg loads for 10 s in transverse directions. The degree to which the hardness of the weld metal zone varies with the parent metal is mostly determined by the rate at which heat is dissipated from the weld centre35. All of the different regions, including the base metal, the fusion zone, the HAZ, and the weld zone, are evaluated in terms of their respective hardness for the parent metal. For GMAW joints, the recrystallised weld metal zone had a micro-hardness value of 170 VHN, while for SMAW joints, it had a hardness value of 185 VHN. There was a maximum microhardness of 175 VHN (for GMAW) and 185 VHN (for SMAW) in the region of the coarser grain that was influenced by heated conditions. The succeeding HAZ was the location where the lower hardness was seen. It was found that the HAZ to base metal interface had the lowest hardness36. The reduced microhardness that leads to weld failure in both GMAW and SMAW welded joints is due to these softened zones. The variations of the hardness may be due to different output performances resulting from distinct constructions of filler wires and different microstructure in various zones due to differing heating and cooling rates. The development of phases like martensite and bainite ensures the conditions necessary for the rising the value of hardness levels. A number of morphologies, such as ferrite (acicular, polygonal, grain boundary, and so on), have lower hardness values, and the existence of these morphologies does not result in an increase in the hardness of the relevant region. The circumstances that are necessary for raising the levels of hardness are ensured by the growth of martensite or bainite.Fig. 13Measurement of microhardness at the mid-thickness.

Macrostructure

In order to make an investigation on the metallurgical features of the both arc welded joints (GMAW and SMAW), the microstructural analysis is an extremely useful instrument. Both optical microscopy and SEM were utilized in order to carry out the examination of the microstructure. Figure 14 shows the optical macrograph of the of V-grooved weld joints of the GMAW and SMAW welding processes. As can be seen, each of the weld samples demonstrated complete penetration and did not exhibit any defects in the welding process. In order to identify visual flaws and distinguish between the various parts of the weldment, the macrostructure was utilised. Figure 15 clearly shows the macrograph of weld joint of GMAW and SMAW welded samples, also shows the various zones in the cross-section like weld metal zone, HAZ including both coarse and fine grain HAZ and base metal. In comparison to SMAW, it was noted that GMAW provides a greater depth of penetration. This is due to the increased electrical resistance of solid wire in comparison to stick electrodes. Because of the increased heat input, a wider HAZ is produced by solid wires that have a higher electrical resistance. In this particular instance, the penetration was deeper and narrower than that of a SMAW while the power of the two arcs produced is same. This was due to the axial metal transfer phenomena that were occurred in spray transfer with solid wire. Consequently, the melted regions in the base metal were equivalent in size but varied in configuration. The penetration depth exhibits linear correlations with the characteristics of the heat source. The rate of heat supplied to the base metal for the unit length is influenced by the welding variables and welding parameters. An increased in the rate of heat input leads to greater melting of the base metal, resulting in enhanced penetration depth37–39. An increased quantity of heat supplied to the weld pool zone indicates deeper penetration40. If the heat input is inadequate, the possibility of fusion failure increases41. Moreover, a reduction in travel speed increases the concentration of heat input, leading to enhanced penetration. Additionally, it showed that the weld zone’s microstructure consisted of elongated columnar grain orientation at the top layers and equally spaced grain orientation at the bottom layers. Heat input during the filler welding passes in multi-pass welding has a significant impact on the bottom layer of the welding during the process. Furthermore, as a consequence of this, the weld quality and joint integrity of the welding joints are immediately impacted42. The heat-affected zone (HAZ) is a critical region in any welded joint because, although it does not melt during welding, it undergoes significant thermal alteration that changes its original metallurgical features. The degree of heat supplied to the weld plays a decisive role in defining the nature of the HAZ. As the heat input rises, the grains within this region tend to coarsen, leading to a progressive widening of the HAZ. Such microstructural changes can reduce the toughness of the material, thereby creating weak spots that are more prone to failure under loading43. In general, both the breadth of the HAZ and the grain size within the weld metal increase with higher heat input, which highlights the importance of controlling welding parameters to ensure structural integrity25,44.

Figure 15 presents the microstructural features of the HAZ, where two distinct regions can be observed. The coarse-grained HAZ (CGHAZ) is dominated by bainite and acicular ferrite. These structures develop primarily due to the slower rate of heat dissipation, which allows more time for the grains to grow. The presence of bainitic phases can also be linked to the segregation of alloying elements near the weld interface, which enhances hardness but reduces toughness. By contrast, the fine-grained HAZ (FGHAZ) consists of ferrite and pearlite. This region generally exhibits a higher hardness value compared to the base metal; however, its impact strength is relatively lower. Hence, although FGHAZ resists indentation better, it is more brittle and prone to fracture during sudden loading.

The interface between the HAZ and the parent metal, commonly referred to as the subcritical region, also deserves particular attention. This narrow band is composed mainly of polygonal ferrite and is characterized by reduced hardness. The presence of such softened zones is strongly associated with weld failures, especially during tensile testing, as observed in the present study (Fig. 15). These findings confirm earlier reports where the subcritical HAZ was consistently identified as the weakest link in welded structures45.

The morphology of the base metal is depicted in Fig. 16, showing its relatively uniform ferritic structure. When comparing this with the weld regions (Fig. 17), it becomes clear that welding introduces a wide range of microstructural variations. The weld metal zone itself contains polygonal ferrite (PF), acicular ferrite (AF), and Widmanstätten ferrite (WF). The balance between these ferrite morphologies has a strong influence on both weldability and the mechanical response of the joints. For instance, acicular ferrite is well known to improve toughness due to its interlocking structure, whereas Widmanstätten ferrite, though harder, can promote brittleness. The current investigation revealed that both GMAW and SMAW welds developed these phases, but their proportions differed, which explains the variation in mechanical behavior between the two processes.Fig. 17SEM Microstructural image of the different Zone.

The chemical composition of the weld zones was further examined through energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). As shown in Fig. 19, the main detected elements were Fe, Ni, Cu, and Si, with Fe and Ni being predominant in both SMAW and GMAW welds. The elemental pattern not only confirms compatibility with the base and filler materials but also explains the formation of specific phases within the fusion zone48. The enrichment of Ni in particular contributes to increased toughness, while the presence of Cu and Si enhances strength and resistance to corrosion.Fig. 19Elemental analysis on weld region for (a) GMAW and (b) SMAW.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to verify the phases detected in microstructural examinations (Figs. 20, 21). The diffraction patterns revealed the presence of α-Fe, Fe–C, Fe–Ni, and Fe–Mn phases in both types of joints. In the SMAW welds, distinct peaks were recorded at 45.22° (1000) and 65.46° (346), with corresponding d-spacings of 2.0052 Å for (111) and 1.4258 Å for (200). In GMAW welds, peaks appeared at 42° (750), 48° (300), 73° (70), 88° (110), and 93° (60). Their associated d-spacings were 2.0871 Å (111), 1.8075 Å (2000), 1.2781 Å (202), 1.0901 Å (311), and 1.0436 Å (222). The Miller indices confirmed that SMAW welds mainly exhibited (101), (200), and (111) planes, whereas GMAW welds showed (111) and (201) orientations. These results highlight the crystallographic differences arising from the two welding methods and further explain the variations in their mechanical performance.Fig. 20Patterns of X-ray diffraction for joints achieved using GMAW.Fig. 21Patterns of X-ray diffraction for joints achieved using SMAW.

In summary, the combined macrostructural and microstructural analyses reveal that the distribution of ferrite morphologies, the segregation of alloying elements, and the crystallographic orientations together dictate the strength, toughness, and overall weldability of the joints. While both GMAW and SMAW joints displayed common features such as bainite and ferrite phases, subtle differences in grain structure, hardness distribution, and crystallinity ultimately accounted for the superior mechanical performance observed in GMAW joints compared to SMAW joints.

Conclusions

The conclusions are as follows:The welded joints exhibited maximum yield and ultimate tensile strengths of 616 MPa and 641 MPa for the GMAW process, while the corresponding values for SMAW were 634 MPa and 676 MPa. The joints also showed good ductility, with maximum elongations of 25.49% for GMAW and 26.19% for SMAW.The Charpy impact tests revealed that the joints produced by the GMAW process absorbed 235 J of energy, whereas those fabricated using SMAW absorbed 213 J, indicating a relatively higher toughness in the GMAW welds.For GMAW and SMAW joints, the hardness values of the weld zone were 170 VHN and 185 VHN, respectively. The coarse-grained heat-affected region had a maximum microhardness of 175 VHN for GMAW and 185 VHN for SMAW.The weld bead revealed distinct microstructural variations, with equiaxed grains dominating the lower layers and elongated columnar grains forming in the upper regions.
