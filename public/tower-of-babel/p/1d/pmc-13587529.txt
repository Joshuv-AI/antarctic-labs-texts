Factors associated with short-term response after systemic corticosteroid treatment in invasively ventilated preterm infants
Authors: Gyeong Eun Yeom, Seung Han Shin, Han-Suk Kim, Ee-Kyung Kim, Ju Sun Heo, Seh Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study evaluated the factors affecting the short-term efficacy of systemic corticosteroid (CS) therapy, focusing on pulmonary hypertension (PH) and other clinical conditions in preterm infants with evolving or established bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on preterm infants (< 32 weeks of gestation and/or < 1,500 g birth weight) who received systemic CS therapy for BPD. Responders were defined as infants who were extubated within 14 days and/or achieved a ≥ 60% reduction in the respiratory severity score (RSS).ResultsOf the 62 infants, 32 were classified as responders and 30 as non-responders. Non-responders had a significantly higher prevalence of PH requiring treatment (70.0% vs. 21.9%, p < 0.001) and hemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus (hsPDA) at treatment initiation (30.0% vs. 3.1%, p = 0.005). Responders showed a greater reduction in RSS from day 1 to day 4 (33.8% vs. 11.7%, p = 0.002). In multivariate analysis, PH requiring treatment (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 6.66, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.39–31.92, p = 0.018) and hsPDA (aOR: 12.54, 95% CI: 1.16–135.05, p = 0.037) at treatment initiation were significant predictors of non-response.ConclusionIdentifying the vascular phenotypes of BPD, including PH and hsPDA, before initiating systemic CS therapy may improve patient selection and optimize treatment outcomes. Incorporating early changes in RSS from treatment day 1 to day 4 enhanced the predictive accuracy for treatment failure and may facilitate the timely identification of non-responders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07119-4.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) remains a major cause of morbidity in preterm infants, primarily due to disrupted alveolar development during the critical period of lung growth [1]. This contributes to prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and long-term pulmonary complications [2]. BPD affects approximately 36–75% of extremely preterm infants born before 28 weeks of gestation, with survival rates at discharge now exceeding 80% in developed countries [3–5]. Survivors face a substantial burden of long-term sequelae, ranging from recurrent pulmonary infections and rehospitalization to neurodevelopmental impairments including cognitive delays and cerebral palsy (CP) [6–9]. Traditionally, BPD has been considered the result of ongoing lung inflammation, which impairs tissue repair and disrupts lung development.

However, recent studies have highlighted its heterogeneity, encompassing vascular, parenchymal, and airway abnormalities [10]. Pulmonary hypertension (PH), previously considered a complication of BPD, is now recognized as a distinct phenotype that reflects early pulmonary vascular disease in preterm infants [11, 12]. Identifying the predominant BPD phenotypes in individual patients may allow for more targeted therapeutic interventions.

Postnatal systemic corticosteroids (CS), particularly dexamethasone, are commonly used to prevent or treat BPD due to their potent anti-inflammatory effects. However, responses to CS therapy vary widely. Extubation failure rates following the first course of CS range from 24% to 53%, with lower gestational age, higher oxygen requirements, and elevated ventilator settings as risk factors [13]. While the vascular phenotype of BPD has been established as a distinct entity [14] and CS are known to act primarily through anti-inflammatory mechanisms targeting parenchymal disease [15], no clinical study has empirically examined whether vascular-dominant BPD phenotypes show a reduced response to corticosteroid therapy. Moreover, predictors of CS non-response remain poorly characterized, with prior work limited to ventilator parameters such as gestational age, FiO2, and MAP [16, 17].

This study aimed to investigate whether PH and related vascular conditions affect the short-term CS responses in preterm infants with evolving or established BPD, and whether early treatment response within the first few days of therapy could enhance the prediction of treatment failure.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included preterm infants born at < 32 weeks of gestation and/or with a birth weight of < 1,500 g who were admitted to Seoul National University Hospital between January 2013 and December 2023. All clinical data were obtained from the electronic medical records of Seoul National University Hospital, a single tertiary referral center, and were retrospectively reviewed and extracted between August 2024 and February 2025. Infants were eligible if they received a single course of systemic CS using a modified DART (Dexamethasone: A Randomized Trial) protocol (1.1 mg/kg over 10 days) for evolving BPD (persistent ventilator dependence with elevated RSS beyond PND 14, supported by radiological findings) or established BPD (oxygen or respiratory support dependence at 28 days of postnatal age) while on invasive mechanical ventilation [18]. In cases of multiple courses, only the first was analyzed. Infants with major congenital anomalies such as congenital heart disease, chromosomal abnormalities, or structural airway anomalies confirmed by laryngoscopy were excluded. Over the study period, annual survival rates (range: 81.2–94.0%), prevalence of extremely low birth weight infants (ELBWI, < 1,000 g; range: 6.37–11.14%), and BPD incidence (range: 13.1–26.3%) remained stable with no significant temporal trend, suggesting that secular changes in clinical practice did not substantially affect the study results.

Definition

Hemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus (hsPDA) was defined as the presence of clinical or radiographic findings suggestive of cardiomegaly or pulmonary edema, combined with at least one echocardiographic criterion: (1) ductal diameter > 1.4 mm/kg; (2) end-diastolic flow reversal in the descending aorta; or (3) a left atrium-to-aorta ratio ≥ 1.4 [19]. Clinical manifestations included hypotension requiring inotropic support without other identifiable causes, persistent oliguria, or renal failure. Medical treatment included ibuprofen (up to two courses) or acetaminophen. Surgical ligation or device closure was considered in refractory cases, when medical therapy was contraindicated, or in cases of massive pulmonary hemorrhage due to PDA-induced left-to-right shunting.

Echocardiography was performed when recurrent hypoxemia raised clinical concern for PH. The diagnosis was established by at least one of the following findings on echocardiography: (1) a peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity exceeding 2.8 m/s, (2) any degree of interventricular septal flattening, or (3) bidirectional/right-to-left flow across a cardiac shunt [11]. In infants with suspected PH, the peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) target was set at 95%, with fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) adjusted accordingly. PH-targeted therapy was initiated when FiO2 requirements reached ≥ 0.6–0.7 or the SpO2 target could not be maintained. Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) was used as first-line therapy given its selective pulmonary vasodilatory effect, with sildenafil (a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor) added for persistent PH or to facilitate iNO weaning. In refractory cases, treprostinil (Remodulin, a prostacyclin analogue), and bosentan (Tracleer, a dual endothelin receptor antagonist) were added to achieve further vasodilation [20]. For statistical analysis, PH was defined as requiring active treatment rather than echocardiographic findings alone.

RSS-guided systemic CS administration

The respiratory severity score (RSS) is computed by multiplying mean airway pressure (MAP) by FiO2, yielding a continuous, non-invasive measure of respiratory burden that correlates closely with the oxygenation index in mechanically ventilated neonates [21]. Unlike the oxygenation index, RSS requires no arterial blood sampling, making it readily applicable in routine clinical monitoring. Our unit previously demonstrated that RSS values on postnatal days (PND) 14, 21, and 28 were strongly associated with the subsequent development of severe BPD or death in extremely preterm infants. Specifically, an RSS ≥ 3.0 on day 14 and RSS ≥ 3.6 on day 21 were reliable cutoffs for predicting these outcomes [22]. These results are consistent with those of previous studies [23, 24], and informed the development of our institutional RSS-guided systemic CS protocol.

Per our institutional protocol, preterm infants born at < 32 weeks of gestation who remained on mechanical ventilation beyond PND 14 entered a weekly RSS-based evaluation cycle. Evolving BPD was identified based on persistent ventilator dependence with elevated RSS, supported by radiological findings including diffuse bilateral haziness, interstitial thickening, or areas of atelectasis or hyperinflation on chest radiograph. When RSS remained below 4 and ventilator requirements were stable or declining, steroid initiation was postponed. Conversely, an RSS of ≥ 4 or worsening ventilator needs prompted echocardiographic assessment for hsPDA and PH. If either condition was identified and judged to be impeding extubation progress, targeted treatment was prioritized before steroid consideration. In the absence of hsPDA, PH, or active infection, intravenous dexamethasone was initiated using the modified DART regimen (total 1.1 mg/kg over 10 days [18]). Dosing proceeded as follows: 0.1 mg/kg every 12 h on days 1–2; 0.075 mg/kg every 12 h on days 3–4; 0.05 mg/kg every 12 h on days 5–7; no dose on day 8; 0.05 mg/kg every 12 h on day 9; and treatment concluded on day 10 (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Medical records from the initiation of the first systemic dexamethasone course for evolving or established BPD were retrospectively reviewed. Prenatal variables included antenatal steroid administration, oligohydramnios, maternal diabetes mellitus (DM), preeclampsia, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), and histological chorioamnionitis. Neonatal variables included gestational age, birth weight, small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status, sex, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), air leak, pulmonary hemorrhage, and culture-proven sepsis prior to the initiation of systemic CS therapy. BPD severity at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age (PMA) was classified using the Jensen 2019 criteria [25]. Clinical data at the time of systemic CS initiation included PMA, PND, extubation outcomes, and concurrent medications. The RSS was calculated hourly using continuous vital sign data from the hospital’s electronic medical records, and daily RSS was defined as the average of hourly values. RSS monitoring continued through day 14 to assess treatment response and extubation success. Echocardiography was performed by a pediatric cardiologist within 1 week of CS initiation to evaluate the presence of hsPDA or PH.

Study group allocation

Infants were classified as responders if they met at least one of the following criteria: (1) successful extubation within 14 days of initiating systemic CS therapy, without re-intubation for at least 72 h; or (2) a ≥ 60% reduction in RSS from baseline at the time of treatment initiation. The 60% threshold was selected based on previous studies evaluating changes in RSS following systemic CS therapy in preterm infants [26]. The 14-day window was selected to allow sufficient time for clinical response following completion of the 10-day treatment course, as used in prior studies evaluating short-term CS response in preterm infants [26]. Non-responders were defined as infants who did not achieve extubation within 14 days, required re-intubation within 72 h, or failed to show a ≥ 60% reduction in RSS from baseline.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with a p-value of < 0.05 in the univariate analysis, along with clinically relevant factors, were included in multivariate logistic regression models to identify independent predictors of non-response. Two models were constructed: (1) Model 1 included gestational age, oligohydramnios, PMA at corticosteroid initiation, presence of hsPDA, use of any PH medication at the time of CS initiation, and baseline RSS at CS initiation. (2) Model 2 included all variables in Model 1, with the addition of the percentage change in RSS from treatment day 1 to day 4. This variable was included to reflect real-world practice, as RSS trajectory by day 4 may support timely decisions regarding early discontinuation of therapy. Model performance was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), McFadden’s R², area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and likelihood ratio test. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (2024; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

During the study period, 1,149 neonates born at < 32 weeks of gestation and/or with a birth weight < 1,500 g were admitted. Among them, 76 received systemic dexamethasone therapy for evolving or established BPD (Fig. 2). Fourteen infants were excluded: six who received dexamethasone while on noninvasive ventilation, four with significant airway abnormalities that precluded successful extubation (e.g., tracheomalacia, subglottic stenosis, or type C tracheoesophageal fistula), one with congenital heart disease, two with genetic disorders, and one with incomplete data, yielding a final cohort of 62 infants. Of these, 32 were classified as responders and 30 as non-responders.

Multivariate analysis for predicting non-response to systemic dexamethasone

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified hsPDA (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 12.54, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.16–135.05, p = 0.037) and treatment for PH (aOR: 6.66, 95% CI: 1.39–31.92, p = 0.018) as independent predictors of non-response to systemic dexamethasone therapy in Model 1 (Table 3). Model 2, which additionally included the percentage change in RSS from Day 1 to Day 4, identified three significant predictors of non-response: hsPDA (aOR: 41.53, 95% CI: 2.13–811.53, p = 0.014), PH treatment (aOR: 10.06, 95% CI: 1.10–92.39, p = 0.041), and RSS change (%) from Day 1 to Day 4 (aOR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.85–0.96, p = 0.001). Model 2 demonstrated improved predictive performance compared with Model 1, with a lower AIC (60.438 vs. 77.492), higher McFadden’s R2 (0.476 vs. 0.237), and greater AUC (0.923 vs. 0.808) (E-Table 1). Both models exhibited good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow, p > 0.05), and a likelihood ratio test confirmed the superior fit of Model 2 (χ2 = 19.054, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study identified key clinical factors associated with an insufficient short-term response to systemic CS therapy in invasively ventilated preterm infants with evolving or established BPD. Our findings demonstrate that both PH and hsPDA at the time of systemic CS initiation were independent predictors of treatment non-responsiveness. These conditions may reflect a distinct “vascular phenotype” of BPD, which could attenuate the expected anti-inflammatory and lung maturation effects of corticosteroids. Furthermore, incorporating early changes in RSS within the first four days of therapy significantly improved the prediction of short-term treatment outcomes.

Systemic CS reduces the incidence of BPD and improves respiratory outcomes in preterm infants [13, 14, 27]. O’Connor et al. reported a 39% extubation failure rate following the first course of systemic dexamethasone in ventilator-dependent preterm infants, with lower gestational age, higher FiO₂, and elevated MAP at treatment initiation being significant predictors of treatment failure [17]. Similarly, a retrospective study reported a 21.6% extubation failure rate among preterm infants receiving systemic CS during invasive ventilation, with the use of high-frequency oscillatory ventilation and higher FiO₂ levels associated with treatment failure [16]. Data from the National Neonatal Research Database, which included 84,440 preterm infants born before 32 weeks of gestation, showed extubation failure rates ranging from 21.4% to 38%, depending on the timing of CS administration [28].

Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on ventilator parameters and the timing of CS administration, the present study highlights the significance of pulmonary vascular conditions—specifically hsPDA and PH—as key predictors of insufficient response to systemic dexamethasone. Severe BPD encompasses a spectrum of phenotypes, including homogeneous or heterogeneous lung disease, airway narrowing, and abnormal pulmonary vascular development [14]. When a specific phenotype predominantly contributes to disease severity, treatment strategies should be tailored to address the underlying pathology; conversely, targeting non-dominant features may offer limited therapeutic benefit. For example, airway abnormalities such as tracheobronchomalacia, which often impede successful extubation, may reduce the effectiveness of systemic CS. Corticosteroids exert their primary therapeutic effects through anti-inflammatory mechanisms targeting parenchymal lung disease. In infants whose BPD is predominantly driven by vascular pathology—whether increased pulmonary blood flow from hsPDA or pulmonary vascular remodeling from PH—the dominant pathological process lies outside the therapeutic target of corticosteroids. Furthermore, hsPDA may continue to influence the pulmonary vasculature even in the presence of PH, as high-pressure, pulsatile flow can persist despite partial attenuation of left-to-right shunting, contributing to ongoing vascular stress and remodeling [29]. This suggests that hsPDA and PH may exert concurrent rather than mutually offsetting effects on the pulmonary vascular bed, further shifting the disease phenotype toward a predominantly vascular process—and providing a plausible mechanistic hypothesis for the reduced CS response observed in these infants.

PH associated with BPD (BPD-PH) is a major complication of BPD, affecting approximately 25% of infants with moderate to severe disease. It adversely impacts both the cardiac and pulmonary systems and is associated with increased mortality in this population [30]. Its pathophysiology includes impaired alveolar-capillary gas exchange, abnormal vascular remodeling, and pulmonary vascular rarefaction—all of which lead to increased pulmonary vascular resistance and eventual right heart failure [15]. Although PH was diagnosed using a single-criterion echocardiographic approach based on Mourani et al. [11], the variable used in multivariate analysis was PH requiring active treatment, which represents a more stringent clinical threshold and minimizes the potential for overdiagnosis based on transient echocardiographic findings.

The association between PDA and BPD is well established [31, 32]. A persistent left-to-right shunt through the PDA increases pulmonary blood flow, disrupts pulmonary mechanics, and prolongs the need for mechanical ventilation—all of which adversely affect alveolar development and contribute to BPD-PH. Recent studies have shown that prolonged exposure to hsPDA is associated with the development of BPD-PH [33]. One case-control study demonstrated a significant association between moderate-to-large PDA and BPD-PH, with longer exposure duration independently correlated with BPD-PH development [34]. These findings suggest that hsPDA may exacerbate pulmonary vascular disease in preterm infants with BPD, particularly in those with an underlying or evolving pulmonary vascular pathology.

Early changes in RSS during the first 4 days of CS therapy emerged as a strong predictor of short-term treatment responses. Although early postnatal administration of systemic CS has been linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, including an increased risk of CP, leading to the discontinuation of routine CS use during the first week of life [35], later administration still carries potential risks such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, gastrointestinal perforation, and gastrointestinal bleeding [8].

Therefore, early identification of infants unlikely to benefit from CS therapy may help minimize these risks by avoiding unnecessary exposure. The RSS, a simple, rapid, and noninvasive tool for assessing respiratory distress in newborns [36], demonstrated substantial predictive utility in this study. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the responder group demonstrated a more rapid early decline in RSS compared to non-responders, with the two groups beginning to diverge from treatment day 1 and the separation most evident by day 4. By Day 4, the responder group showed a 33.8% reduction in RSS compared to only 11.7% in the non-responder group, and incorporating this early change into the prediction model significantly improved its accuracy (E-Table 1). In clinical practice, RSS is monitored continuously during corticosteroid therapy, and the trajectory observed by treatment day 4 may serve as a practical bedside decision-support tool. Infants showing minimal RSS improvement by day 4 may represent likely non-responders, for whom early discontinuation of therapy could be considered to minimize unnecessary steroid exposure and its associated risks.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and relatively small sample size. The wide confidence intervals for hsPDA in both models reflect the limited sample size and the small number of events,and these estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution. A notable limitation relates to the design of our institutional protocol, which recommends addressing hsPDA and PH prior to CS initiation. This approach may introduce some degree of circularity, as vascular phenotypes are considered less likely to respond to CS. However, in actual clinical practice, CS was frequently initiated before full resolution of these vascular conditions. At treatment onset, hsPDA was present in 30.0% and PH requiring treatment in 70.0% of non-responders, of whom 60.0% were receiving iNO. These observations indicate that CS exposure was not strictly limited to a predefined subset of patients. Accordingly, our findings reflect real-world clinical practice and provide quantitative estimates of the association between vascular conditions and reduced treatment response.

The use of PH as a surrogate marker in our analysis may not have fully captured the severity of PH prior to therapy. Furthermore, a potential limitation of Model 2 is the conceptual overlap between the percentage change in RSS from treatment day 1 to day 4 and the final RSS reduction that forms part of the composite outcome. Infants showing substantial RSS reduction by day 4 are already progressing toward the 60% threshold, meaning the predictor and outcome reflect the same biological process measured at different timepoints. This may inflate the apparent predictive value of Model 2, and results should be interpreted with caution. In addition, as this was a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Lastly, this study focused on short-term responses rather than the long-term efficacy in reducing the incidence of BPD. Nevertheless, the lower prevalence of severe BPD in the responder group supports the clinical relevance of our findings and suggests that BPD-PH may serve as a marker of greater disease severity.

In invasively ventilated preterm infants, hsPDA and PH were significant predictors of insufficient response to systemic dexamethasone therapy, reflecting distinct BPD phenotypes. These findings reflect reduced rather than absent treatment efficacy, and tempered expectations―rather than steroid avoidance―are recommended in infants with hsPDA or PH. Collectively, these results emphasize the importance of careful patient selection and early management of pulmonary circulatory conditions to optimize treatment effectiveness. Incorporating early changes in the RSS improved the prediction of treatment failure, underscoring the need to refine the target population and consider early discontinuation of therapy in infants showing insufficient response to minimize unnecessary steroid exposure and reduce potential neurodevelopmental risks.
