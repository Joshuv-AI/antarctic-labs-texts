Arthroscopically-Assisted Medial Collateral Ligament Repair of Proximal Tears with Inside-out Suture Technique
Authors: Chun-Lin Tao, Wei-Lin Chang, Kun-Hui Chen, En-Rung Chiang, Hsuan-Hsiao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries are among the most common ligamentous injuries of the knee. Although most MCL injuries have favorable outcomes with conservative treatment, surgical intervention may be considered in cases of chronic instability or in conjunction with injuries to other knee structures, such as the anterior cruciate ligament or meniscus. In this Technical Note, we describe a minimally invasive arthroscopic-assisted technique for femoral-sided MCL repair using an inside-out suture method with suture anchor fixation and internal bracing using FiberTape. This technique offers biomechanical support, proprioceptive preservation, and reduced soft-tissue trauma. Although early results are promising, further long-term studies are warranted to assess durability, clinical outcomes, and potential complications.

Medial collateral ligament (MCL) injury is one of the most common injuries of ligaments of the knee.1 The MCL consists of 3 layers: the first layer is mainly composed of the deep crural fascia, the second layer is the superficial medial collateral ligament (sMCL), and the third layer is the deep medial collateral ligament (dMCL).2 The sMCL is considered the largest structure of the medial knee ,with 1 femoral attachment and 2 tibial attachments, spanning approximately 10 to 12 cm in length. The dMCL is a part of the medial joint capsule, consisting of meniscofemoral and meniscotibial ligaments.3 Together, the sMCL and dMCL act as the main stabilizer of the medial compartment of the knee; hence, injury to the structure leads to tibiofemoral laxity and instability of the knee.4

There is plenty of healing potential in MCL injuries, so conservative treatment is popular among grade 1 to 3 injuries, whereas some injuries with chronic laxity and risk of degenerative arthritis may call for the consideration of surgical intervention.5 However, in combined injuries to other structures of the knee needing surgical intervention, most commonly the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and medial meniscus, surgical intervention for the MCL can be performed with the corresponding surgeries. Although there’s still no consensus on the superiority between MCL repair or MCL reconstruction,6 previous studies have suggested no significant difference in postoperative outcomes.7,8 In this Technical Note and short video, we aim to show an arthroscopic surgical technique for femoral-side MCL injury, involving an inside-out technique and suture anchor fixation with additional internal bracing. The technique provides a simple method with minimal surgical wounds and without the need of graft preparation and can be performed in conjunction with arthroscopic interventions in combined injuries involving other structures of the knee.

Preoperative Evaluations

A documentation of the mechanism and presentation of the injury should be performed to evaluate the risk of combined injury of multiple structures. Physical examination regarding the instability of the injured knee should be performed to detect presence of multiligament injuries, including anterior drawer test, posterior drawer test, Lachman test, McMurray test, pivot shift test, valgus, and varus stress test. Preoperative radiograph of the injured knee also should be taken to detect avulsion fractures or fracture needing operative treatments. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging is preferred for evaluation of integrity of the ligamentous structures.9 In this case, the patient showed positive valgus stress test, anterior drawer test, Lachman test and pivot shift test. The patient’s radiograph showed no obvious fracture, and magnetic resonance imaging showed complete tear of femoral side medial collateral ligament (Fig 1), ruptured ACL, and lateral meniscus tear.

Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred imaging modality owing to its power in soft-tissue differentiation, especially in a coronal view for evaluation of integrity of collateral ligaments of the knee. This figure shows complete disruption of the right femoral-sided medial collateral ligament (MCL) and accompanying soft tissue edema under coronal MRI, indicating a grade III MCL injury.

Surgical Technique

Patient Preparation

The patient is placed in supine position after administration of spinal anesthesia. A mechanical tourniquet is placed proximal to the injured knee for better intraoperative bleeding control. The injured leg is thoroughly disinfected with Hibiscrub and betadine alcohol, and the patient is then draped with only the injured knee exposed. A portal for passage of the arthroscope is created just lateral to the patellar tendon, and a portal for insertion of surgical instruments is created just medial to the patellar tendon.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

Diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to validate preoperative findings and lesions or to detect other previously occult injury. A valgus stress under arthroscope to elicit the “drive-through” sign also can be performed to evaluate laxity of the medial compartment. “Lift-off” or “floating” medial meniscus sign also can be elicited by pushing a probe against the medial meniscus to evaluate the need for meniscal repair. In this case, diagnostic arthroscopic findings were compatible with preoperative evaluations; therefore, ACL reconstruction and lateral meniscus repair were then successfully performed.

Suture Entry for MCL Repair

A No. 2 ETHIBOND suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) is inserted outside-in right above the medial meniscus through all 3 layers of MCL with a passing sheath of a ConMed Linvatec meniscal repair device. It is then passed inside-out again through the skin, with about 2 cm of distance covered by the ETHIBOND suture (Fig 2). Then, the ETHIBOND suture guides the entry of a FiberTape (Arthrex, Naples, FL) suture (Fig 3).

Placing the FiberTape right above the medial meniscus provides maximal length of suture coverage on the femoral side of the MCL. The FiberTape can thus provide structural support along the complete length of the meniscofemoral ligament. The suture should not penetrate the medial meniscus directly. This is to avoid causing excessive strain on the medial meniscus, which is intact in a tear of the femoral side. Compared with suturing at the tibial insertion of the MCL, arthroscopy can be used to confirm the suture penetrates all 3 layers of the MCL to lower the risk of detachment of the suture, and to minimize the risk of involving the medial meniscus in the path of the suture (Fig 4).

An incision of approximately 1.5 cm in size is made superficial to the medial epicondyle of femur (incision 1), where the desired femoral attachment of sMCL is located.3 A pair of grasping forceps is then inserted between the first and second layer of the MCL, grasping the previously inserted FiberTape suture (Fig 5), allowing the passage of the FiberTape suture through the incision on the medial epicondyle of femur. The free ends of the FiberTape suture are now positioned superficial to the sMCL, secured by the suture previously made above the medial meniscus.

Suture Anchor at the Femoral Side

The free ends pulled out through the incision made on medial epicondyle of femur allow the suture anchor onto the desired femoral insertion of sMCL. In addition, the previously made incision, although small in size, provides a field for surgeons to place the suture anchor without the need of the arthroscope and with ease of manipulating instruments. The FiberTape suture is anchored onto the femoral insertion of the sMCL near the medial epicondyle of femur with a 4.75-mm knotless suture anchor (SwiveLock; Arthrex) with position of flexion of 30° and varus stress (Fig 6). The remaining suture is kept for further internal bracing.

Internal Bracing Technique

An additional incision is made medial to the attachment of patellar tendon on tibial tuberosity, 6 cm distal to joint line. A pair of grasping forceps is inserted into the incision and passed through first and second layer of the MCL, reaching incision 1. The remaining FiberTape suture of the previous suture anchor is then collected by the grasp and pulled out from incision 2. The FiberTape suture is then anchored onto the exposed tibial insertion of the sMCL through incision 2 with SwiveLock knotless suture anchor with position of flexion of 30° and varus stress (Fig 7). This maneuver allowed additional structural bracing for the MCL.

Internal bracing on tibial insertion. (A) An additional incision is made medial to the tibial tuberosity and about 6 cm from the joint line. A pair of grasping forceps is inserted to collect the remaining FiberTape suture from the suture anchor on the femoral insertion through first and second layer of the MCL (along dashed white arrow). (B) Internal bracing is achieved with suture anchor on the tibial insertion with the SwiveLock suture anchor (yellow arrow). The internal bracing provides additional structural support for the repaired MCL. (MCL, medial collateral ligament.)

Rehabilitation Protocol

Postoperative rehabilitation focuses on early restoration of range of motion (ROM) and neuromuscular control. A locked brace is applied at full extension for the first 2 weeks, with partial weight-bearing advised. The brace can be temporarily unlocked in various exercises for incremental increases in ROM. The brace may be removed after 4 weeks with evaluations on ROM and muscle strength at outpatient follow-ups. After the removal of the brace, strengthening exercises and aggressive muscle strength training begin, followed by a standard rehabilitation protocol for MCL repair.

Discussion

Injuries to the MCL often are treated nonoperatively across grade I-III injuries because isolated MCL injuries can heal well. Surgical interventions to the MCL are usually reserved for grade III injuries with chronic medial compartment laxity or lesions with degenerative changes to the knee, and it’s important to evaluate grade III injuries because not all MCL with grade III injuries heal properly.10 According to previous studies published by LaPrade et al.8 and Braaten et al.,7 sMCL reconstruction and sMCL augmentation or repair had similar postoperative clinical outcomes. In this Technical Note, we chose to perform an MCL repair, preserving the native ligament with limited dissection, and thus maintaining the proprioceptive function. In concomitant ACL and MCL injuries, ACL reconstruction with conservative treatment for the MCL remains the current general consensus.11 Conservative treatment for the MCL can be performed first before ACL reconstruction, because a combined operation on the MCL and ACL at the same time increases the risk of postoperative joint stiffness.12 However, surgical intervention for the MCL may be considered because the ACL acts as the secondary stabilizer for valgus stress, especially in cases with marked medial compartment instability. In previous literature, van Eck et al.13 suggested that MCL reconstruction with suture tape augmentation had smaller postoperative displacement under valgus stress compared with MCL repair alone; suture tape augmentation was thus selected for our technique.

A similar technique involving suture anchor and internal bracing has been proposed.14,15 However, we provided additional structural support with the suture superior to the medial meniscus while keeping the 2 necessary incisions as small as possible. These advantages provided the patient an opportunity to faster recovery and postoperative-healing. Previous patients who underwent the same operation have shown good functional recovery well after 6 months of follow-up.

The internal bracing technique involves implementation of ultrahigh–molecular-weight polyethylene suture tapes, in this case the FiberTape suture tapes. The technique provides an additional augmentation for structural stability under stress. Similar techniques involving internal bracing had been performed on other tendinous or ligamentous structures, which showed positive results with lower risks of complications.16 Although the technique provides advantages in preserving native MCL and minimally invasive approach, long-term studies may be conducted to evaluate the clinical outcomes and complication rates of similar techniques performed on the MCL.
