Recombinant LGR4 extracellular domain attenuates osteoclastogenesis and prevents bone metastasis via RANKL inhibition
Authors: Meng Fu, Guowei Chang, Shuilin Shao, Liying Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Recombinant LGR4-ECD was produced in CHO cells with 95% purity and 2.38 mg/L yield.•rLGR4-ECD directly binds RANKL and inhibits RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis dose-dependently.•rLGR4-ECD suppresses osteoclast-specific genes (Acp5, Ctsk, Nfatc1) at mRNA and protein levels.•In vivo, rLGR4-ECD reduces bone metastatic burden by 67% and osteoclast surface by 56%.•rLGR4-ECD extends survival without affecting lung metastasis, demonstrating bone-specific efficacy.

Introduction

Bone metastasis represents a devastating complication of advanced cancers, particularly breast and prostate malignancies, affecting over 70% of terminal-stage patients and causing severe skeletal-related events including pathologic fractures, spinal cord compression, and hypercalcemia [1], [2]. These pathological changes result from the disruption of physiological bone remodeling, wherein tumor cells hijack the RANK/RANKL/OPG signaling axis to fuel a self-perpetuating vicious cycle [3]. In this cycle, metastatic cells secrete RANK ligand (RANKL), which hyperactivates osteoclasts through binding to receptor activator of nuclear factor κB (RANK) on precursor cells, leading to excessive bone resorption [4]. Concurrently, bone-derived growth factors released during osteolysis further promote tumor proliferation and survival [5]. Current osteoclast-targeting therapies, such as nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates and the monoclonal anti-RANKL antibody denosumab, delay skeletal-related events but exhibit significant limitations. Bisphosphonates associate with renal toxicity and osteonecrosis of the jaw, while denosumab increases infection susceptibility and necessitates indefinite administration to avoid rebound vertebral fractures [6], [7]. Critically, these agents fail to fully abrogate metastatic progression, as evidenced by median survival improvements of merely 1–2 months in pivotal trials, underscoring an urgent need for mechanistically distinct inhibitors that more effectively disrupt the osteolytic cascade without compromising systemic safety [8], [9].

The leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 4 (LGR4) has emerged as a pivotal regulator of skeletal homeostasis, functioning as a physiological brake on osteoclastogenesis through competitive antagonism of RANKL signaling [10]. This receptor possesses a large extracellular domain characterized by 17 leucine-rich repeats that form a horseshoe-shaped ligand-binding interface, enabling high-affinity interactions with RANKL [11]. Seminal work has demonstrated that LGR4 serves as a decoy receptor for RANKL, with its extracellular domain competing with RANK for binding to RANKL, thereby suppressing canonical NF-κB and NFATc1 activation in osteoclast precursors while simultaneously initiating non-canonical signaling pathways that further inhibit osteoclast differentiation [12]. This dual regulatory mechanism is evidenced by severe osteopenia in global Lgr4-knockout mice, which exhibit significantly increased osteoclast numbers and reduced trabecular bone volume compared to wild-type littermates [13]. Importantly, recombinant soluble LGR4-ECD retains these inhibitory properties in vitro, suppressing RANKL-induced osteoclast formation at nanomolar concentrations with potency comparable to denosumab, yet operating through structurally distinct epitopes that avoid interference with osteoprotegerin binding [14]. These attributes position LGR4-ECD as a mechanistically innovative candidate for disrupting the osteolytic vicious cycle in bone metastasis, potentially overcoming the efficacy ceilings and safety liabilities of current antiresorptive agents.

Building upon this mechanistic rationale, we hypothesized that CHO-derived recombinant LGR4-ECD could serve as a next-generation biologic to attenuate cancer-induced osteolysis by effectively disrupting RANKL-dependent osteoclast activation and impeding metastatic progression. To test this premise, we employed Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) expression systems, a regulatory-compliant platform enabling scalable production of fully glycosylated, bioactive rLGR4-ECD with extended plasma half-life [15]. This study pioneers two transformative innovations: first, the repurposing of rLGR4-ECD from osteoporosis to oncology contexts, leveraging its capacity to competitively inhibit RANKL-RANK interactions while suppressing NFATc1-driven osteoclastogenesis; and second, the demonstration of direct antimetastatic effects through comprehensive in vivo models. Through integrated biochemical, cellular, and in vivo analyses, we provide conclusive evidence that rLGR4-ECD significantly reduces osteoclast density, suppresses bone lesion progression, and extends survival, offering a mechanistically distinct therapeutic strategy against skeletal metastasis that transcends the limitations of current antiresorptive agents.

Materials and methods

Recombinant plasmid construction and validation

The cDNA encoding the human LGR4 extracellular domain (LGR4-ECD, amino acids 25–540) was amplified by PCR using KOD Plus DNA Polymerase (Toyobo, KOD-201) with specific primers containing Hind III and BamH I restriction sites: forward 5′-CCCAAGCTTATGGCCTTACCAGTGACCGCCT TGCTCCTGCCGCTGGCCTTGCTGCTCCACGCCGCCGATTACAAGGATGACGATGACAAGCTCTGCGCGGCGGCCTGCAG-3′ and reverse 5′-TCACATCACCATCACCATCACCCTCGCCCTGGACACCAACCAGCTGAAGTCTGTTCCTGATGGGATTTTTGATCGCCTGACCAGCTTGCAGAAAATTTGGCTTCATACAAACCCTTGGGACTGCAGTTGTCCCAGGATTGACTATCTCAGTCGCTGGTTAAATAAAAACTCACAGAAGGAACAGGGCAGTGCTAAATGTTCCGGGTCCGGCAAGCCCGTCCGAAGTATCATCTGCCCTACTCATCATCACCATCACCATTAAGGGATCCCTACACAGGGTGTCCAG-3′. The PCR product was purified using a gel extraction kit (Tiangen, DP209), digested with Hind III (New England Biolabs, R3104S) and BamH I (New England Biolabs, R3136S) for 2 h at 37°C, and ligated into the similarly digested pcDNA4/TO vector (Thermo Fisher, V102020) using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, M0202S) overnight at 16°C. The ligation product was transformed into DH5α competent cells (TransGen Biotech, CD201) and plated on LB agar containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin (Sigma-Aldrich, A9518). Positive clones were validated by Sanger sequencing (GENEWIZ). Restriction digestion with Hind III and BamH I was performed to confirm insert presence.

Cell culture and stable cell line establishment

CHO-K1 cells (ATCC CCL-61) were maintained in CD CHO medium (Gibco, 10743029) under suspension culture conditions using polycarbonate Erlenmeyer flasks (Corning, 431146) at 120 rpm, 37°C, and 5% CO2. For transient transfection, cells were transfected with 4 μg of plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection efficiency was monitored via GFP fluorescence at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection using fluorescence microscopy at 200 × magnification. To generate stable cell pools, transfected cells were selected with 5 μg/mL puromycin (InvivoGen, ant-pr-1) for 4 weeks. Monoclonal colonies were expanded and assessed for viability and morphology using phase-contrast microscopy at 50 × and 200 × magnification.

Protein expression and purification

Stable CHO-K1 clones expressing 6x His and Flag-tagged rLGR4-ECD were cultured in serum-free CD CHO medium in fed-batch mode. Cell-free supernatant was harvested by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The recombinant protein was purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Qiagen, 30210): supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA resin (1:100 v/v) for 1 h at 4°C, washed with 20 mM imidazole (Sigma-Aldrich, I2399) in PBS, and eluted with 250 mM imidazole. Eluted fractions were dialyzed against PBS for 12 h with three buffer changes and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (10 kDa MWCO, Millipore, UFC901008). Protein expression and purification were evaluated by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie blue staining and Western blot using anti-His antibody (1:2000, Abcam, ab18184). Protein concentration was determined via BCA assay (Thermo Fisher, 23225), and purity exceeded 95% as quantified by densitometric analysis.

Protein-protein interaction assays

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed to evaluate the binding affinity of rLGR4-ECD to RANKL and RSPO4 ligands. HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with RANKL or RSPO4 overexpression plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001). Cell lysates containing 500 μg of total protein were incubated with purified rLGR4-ECD (1000 ng/mL) for 12 h at 4°C with gentle rotation. Anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823) were added to the mixture and incubated for an additional 3 h at 4°C. Beads were washed four times with TBST buffer, and bound proteins were eluted with 2 × Laemmli buffer by boiling at 95°C for 10 min. Input samples and eluates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Western blotting was performed using anti-FLAG antibody (1:2000, Abcam ab205606) to detect rLGR4-ECD and anti-RANKL(1:1000, Abcam, ab239607) or anti-RSPO4 antibody (1:1000, Abcam, ab189515) to detect RANKL or RSPO4. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software version 1.53.

Osteoclast differentiation and in vitro inhibition assay

Bone marrow monocytes (BMMs) were isolated from femurs and tibiae of 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice by flushing the marrow cavities with α-MEM medium (Gibco, 12571063). Erythrocytes were lysed using ACK buffer, and remaining cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 30 ng/mL M-CSF (R&D Systems, 416-ML-050) for 24 h. Non-adherent cells were collected and seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 24-well plates. Cells were differentiated with 30 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL RANKL (R&D Systems, 462-TEC-010) in the presence or absence of rLGR4-ECD at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 40, and 80 ng/mL for 6 days, with medium replacement every 48 h. For TRAP staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained using the Acid Phosphatase, Leukocyte (TRAP) Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, 387A) according to the manufacturer's instructions. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells containing three or more nuclei were counted in five random fields per well under brightfield microscopy at 200 × magnification. Representative images were captured for each treatment group.

Molecular analysis of osteoclast differentiation

Total RNA was extracted from differentiated osteoclasts using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596026) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Qiagen, 79254) to remove genomic DNA contamination, and cDNA was synthesized using the AffinityScript QPCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (Agilent,600559). Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, 04887352001) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using the following primer pairs: Acp5 forward 5′-CTGGAGTGCACGATGCCAGCG-3′ and reverse 5′-TCCGTGCTCGGCGATGGACCAG-3′; Ctsk forward 5′-CTTCCAATACGTGCAGCAGA-3′ and reverse 5′-TCTTCAGGGCTTTCTCGTTC-3′; Nfatc1 forward 5′-GTCACCATCGTGTTTGTGCT-3′ and reverse 5′-CAGGTAGGCATTGGTGAAGG-3′; Gapdh forward 5′-AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG-3′ and reverse 5′-TGTAGACCATGTAGTTGAGGTCA-3′. The reaction conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Gene expression levels were normalized to Gapdh and calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method.

Western blot analysis of osteoclast markers

Protein extracts from osteoclasts were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and supernatants were collected for protein quantification using BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, ISEQ00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-TRAP (1:1000, Abcam, ab52750), anti-Cathepsin K (1:1000, Abcam, ab187647), anti-NFATc1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab2796), and anti-β-actin (1:5000, Abcam, ab7817). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva, RPN2232) and quantified using Image Lab Software version 6.1.

Animal model of bone metastasis

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee (DQPH-2024–098) and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g, Sibefo Bio) were anesthetized with isoflurane (Sigma-Aldrich, 792632) and injected intracardially with 1 × 10^5 4 T1-Luc breast cancer cells suspended in 100 μL PBS. After 24 h for tumor cell dissemination, mice were randomly divided into three experimental groups: control group (no tumor cells), PBS treatment group (tumor cells + PBS), and rLGR4-ECD treatment group (tumor cells + 1 mg/kg rLGR4-ECD). Treatment was administered daily via tail vein injection until mice were sacrificed. Body weight and clinical signs were monitored twice weekly. Mice were sacrificed upon reaching humane endpoints (weight loss > 20%, paralysis, or lethargy), and survival data were recorded for Kaplan-Meier analysis. Bone tissues including femurs, tibiae, and calvariae were collected for subsequent analysis.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

Metastatic progression was monitored weekly using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer). Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin (Gold Biotechnology, LUCK-100) and imaged 10 min post-injection under isoflurane anesthesia. Bioluminescence images were acquired with an exposure time of 1–60 s, binning factor of 8, and f/stop of 1. Total photon flux (photons/second/cm2/steradian) was quantified within consistent regions of interest using Living Image software version 4.7.1. Imaging was performed on all three experimental groups (control, PBS-treated, and rLGR4-ECD-treated) with six mice per group at day 12.

TRAP staining of mouse calvaria

After euthanasia, mouse calvariae were carefully dissected and cleaned thoroughly to remove all attached soft tissues, ensuring minimal interference with subsequent TRAP staining. The cleaned calvariae were fixed in 1% formaldehyde solution overnight at 4°C. Following fixation, the samples were washed twice with PBS to remove residual formaldehyde and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. After permeabilization, the calvariae were washed twice with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20) to thoroughly remove Triton X-100. TRAP staining was performed by incubating each calvarium in 1.5–2 mL of TRAP staining solution for approximately 2 h at 37°C in the dark. After staining, the calvariae were rinsed gently with distilled water and imaged immediately under a camera (Nikon). The area of TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts on the bone surface was quantified using ImageJ software version 1.53 from five randomly selected fields per calvarium.

H&E staining of mouse lung tissue

Lung tissues were collected at the time of sacrifice and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4°C. The fixed tissues were processed through a series of ethanol dehydrations, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to distilled water. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed as follows: sections were stained in hematoxylin solution for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for 30 s, and blued in 0.2% ammonia water. After washing, the sections were counterstained in eosin solution for 3 min, followed by dehydration through graded ethanols and clearing in xylene. The stained sections were mounted with neutral balsam and examined under a bright-field microscope at 200 × magnification (Nikon). Metastatic nodules were identified based on morphological characteristics and counted in five randomly selected fields per section. Images were captured using a digital camera attached to the microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data represent Mean ± SEM. Multiple groups were compared by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test using GraphPad Prism v9.0. Pairwise comparisons used unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Survival analysis employed Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests. Significance thresholds were defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. Sample sizes (n) for each experiment are specified in figure legends, with a minimum of n = 6 for in vivo studies to ensure statistical power. All experiments included three biological replicates unless otherwise stated.

Results

The expression plasmid pcDNA4/TO-LGR4-ECD was constructed, incorporating the gene fragment into the vector backbone, as illustrated in the protein structure schematic and vector map (Fig. 1a). Restriction enzyme digestion using Hind III and BamH I confirmed correct insertion, with a clear band of approximately 1.5 kb corresponding to rLGR4-ECD observed across six parallel replicates alongside a molecular weight marker (Fig. 1b). Transient transfection efficiency in CHO-K1 cells, assessed via GFP fluorescence, exceeded 85% at 48 and 72 h post-transfection under 200 × magnification (Fig. 1c). Stable monoclonal cell lines were subsequently established, exhibiting normal morphology and viability as shown in bright-field images at 50 × and 200 × magnification (Fig. 1d). Overexpression of LGR4-ECD in cell lysates was confirmed by Coomassie blue staining, with a prominent band at approximately 62 kDa visible in three independent overexpression groups compared to the control, as indicated within the red box (Fig. 1e). Purification of recombinant protein via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography yielded high-purity rLGR4-ECD, as demonstrated by Coomassie staining of elution fractions alongside control and wash samples; the black arrow highlights the purified target protein in elution lanes (Fig. 1f). LGR4-ECD blocks RANKL signal pathway and suppresses osteoclastogenesis in vitro

Recombinant LGR4-ECD was first characterized for molecular integrity and binding functionality. Western blot analysis of the purified protein under reducing conditions showed a single band at the expected molecular weight across three independent replicates, as indicated by a black arrow (Fig. 2a). Co-immunoprecipitation assays confirmed specific and high-affinity binding of rLGR4-ECD to RANKL, with a clear band observed in the complex pulled down by anti-FLAG beads (Fig. 2B, left). Additionally, rLGR4-ECD also interacted with RSPO4 proteins (a classic ligand of LGR4), as evidenced in parallel co-IP experiments (Fig. 2b, right). Functionally, rLGR4-ECD significantly inhibited RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation in a dose-dependent manner. TRAP staining of bone marrow-derived monocytes revealed that treatment with 10, 20, 40, and 80 ng/mL rLGR4-ECD reduced the formation of multinucleated osteoclasts compared to the 50 ng/mL RANKL-only group, with nearly complete inhibition at the highest dose under 200 × microscopy (IC50 = 42.38 ng/ml, n = 3, **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001, Fig. 2c). Consistent with cellular morphology, qPCR analysis demonstrated marked downregulation of osteoclast-specific genes: Acp5 expression decreased by 91.4% (n = 3, ***p < 0.001), Ctsk by 87.1% (n = 3, ***p < 0.001), and Nfatc1 by 81.2% (n = 3, ***p < 0.001) at 80 ng/mL rLGR4-ECD relative to RANKL-stimulated controls (Fig. 2d). Western blot results further corroborated these findings, showing reduced protein levels of TRAP (83.3% reduction, n = 3, ***p < 0.001), Cathepsin K (91.7%, n = 3, ***p < 0.001), and NFATc1 (84.5%, n = 3, ***p < 0.001) across the same dose gradient (Fig. 2e-f). Given the near-complete ablation of TRAP + multinucleated cell formation and Cathepsin K expression, these data indicate that rLGR4-ECD treatment consequently suppresses the bone-resorbing capacity of osteoclast lineage cells. rLGR4-ECD inhibits osteoclast activity and bone metastasis in vivo

The efficacy of rLGR4-ECD in suppressing osteoclastogenesis and metastatic progression was further evaluated in a murine model of breast cancer bone metastasis. In vivo bioluminescence imaging performed after intracardially injection of highly bone-metastatic breast cancer cells revealed a substantial reduction in metastatic burden following treatment with rLGR4-ECD. Quantitative analysis showed a 67.3% (n = 6, ***p < 0.001) decrease in photon flux in the rLGR4-ECD treated group compared to the PBS-treated group, while the control group without tumor cells showed no signal (Fig. 3a). Histological assessment of mouse calvaria through TRAP staining demonstrated a marked decrease in osteoclast number and activity in the rLGR4-ECD group compared to the PBS-treated groups. Higher magnification views of boxed areas clearly showed diminished multinucleated osteoclast formation along bone surfaces, and quantitative analysis confirmed a 56.2% reduction (Oc.S/BS, n = 6, ***p < 0.001) in osteoclast surface per bone surface (Fig. 3b). Molecular analysis of bone tissue extracts indicated significant downregulation of osteoclast-related gene expression in the rLGR4-ECD treatment group relative to the PBS control, with Acp5 mRNA levels reduced by 71.3% (n = 6, ***p < 0.001), Ctsk by 40.1% (n = 6, ***p < 0.001), and Nfatc1 by 86.5% (n = 6, ***p < 0.001) (Fig. 3c). Treatment with rLGR4-ECD significantly improved survival outcomes, extending median survival by 41.7% (12 days versus 17 days, n = 10) compared to the PBS-treated group, as shown in the Kaplan-Meier curve (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, by conducting H&E staining on lung tissue to assess the situation of lung metastasis of cancer cells, it was found that under 200 × magnification, the number of metastatic nodules in the rLGR4-ECD treatment group did not show significant changes compared to the PBS group (n = 6). This indicates that rLGR4-ECD indeed inhibits cancer bone metastasis by suppressing osteoclasts without affecting the metastasis of cancer cells to other organs(Fig. 3e). Discussion

This study demonstrates that the rLGR4-ECD functions as a potent therapeutic agent capable of simultaneously suppressing osteoclastogenesis and inhibiting bone metastasis in preclinical models. Through optimized expression in CHO-K1 cells, we obtained a highly purified recombinant protein exhibiting favorable binding activity, particularly high affinity for RANKL. Co-IP confirmed that rLGR4-ECD directly interacts with RANKL via its leucine-rich repeat domain, effectively competing with RANK for ligand binding and disrupting the critical signaling axis that drives osteoclast differentiation. Further in vitro assays demonstrated that rLGR4-ECD inhibits osteoclast formation in a dose-dependent manner, significantly reducing the number of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells and downregulating key osteoclast markers—including Acp5, Ctsk, and Nfatc1—at both transcriptional and translational levels. These findings elucidate the fundamental mechanism by which LGR4-ECD exerts its anti-osteoclastogenic effects, providing a solid foundation for its therapeutic application in bone metastatic diseases.

The compelling in vivo efficacy of rLGR4-ECD was unequivocally demonstrated in a murine model of breast cancer bone metastasis. Daily administration of rLGR4-ECD significantly attenuated tumor-induced osteolysis and metastatic progression. Bioluminescence imaging revealed a remarkable reduction in metastatic burden within bone following rLGR4-ECD treatment, accompanied by a pronounced decrease in osteoclast surface per bone surface as quantified by TRAP staining of calvarial sections. These morphological findings were corroborated by molecular analyses showing substantial downregulation of osteoclast-specific genes within bone tissue, confirming that the therapeutic effects extend beyond symptomatic improvement to fundamental modulation of the bone microenvironment [16]. The survival benefit conferred by rLGR4-ECD treatment was particularly striking, with a significant extension in median survival compared to PBS-treated animals. This enhanced efficacy likely stems from the specific mechanism of action employed by LGR4-ECD, which targets RANKL-mediated osteoclast activation, thereby disrupting the vicious cycle that perpetuates metastatic growth in bone.

Notably, the antimetastatic effects of LGR4-ECD were specific to bone tissues. Histological assessment of lung sections revealed no significant difference in pulmonary metastatic nodules between the rLGR4-ECD treatment group and PBS-treated controls. This observation indicates that LGR4-ECD selectively inhibits bone metastasis through suppression of osteoclast-mediated bone destruction without affecting metastatic spread to other organs such as the lungs. The specificity of this action is consistent with the mechanism whereby LGR4-ECD exclusively interferes with RANKL signaling and osteoclast function, without directly influencing tumor cell dissemination or extraosseous metastasis.

It is important to note that while our in vitro assays utilized soluble RANKL, physiological osteoclastogenesis in the metastatic niche is largely driven by membrane-bound RANKL (mRANKL) presented by osteoblastic lineage cells. Prior work has demonstrated that decoy receptors, including OPG and LGR4-ECD, can also inhibit cell–cell contact-mediated signaling by disrupting the juxtacrine interaction between osteoblast mRANKL and osteoclast precursor RANK [17]. Future co-culture studies utilizing osteoblasts and osteoclast precursors will be necessary to fully delineate the relative efficacy of rLGR4-ECD against mRANKL versus soluble RANKL in the tumor-bone microenvironment.

The translational potential of LGR4-ECD is strengthened by its efficient production using established CHO expression systems, which yielded substantial quantities of correctly folded and glycosylated protein suitable for preclinical development [18]. The production process achieved high purity exceeding 95% with considerable yield, meeting critical benchmarks for biotherapeutic development. These practical advantages, combined with the mechanistic benefits demonstrated in our studies, create a compelling case for continued development of rLGR4-ECD as a next-generation therapeutic for bone metastatic diseases.

Notwithstanding these promising results, several limitations of this study warrant consideration for future research and clinical translation. The 20-day treatment window, while sufficient to demonstrate significant efficacy in our murine model, may not fully recapitulate the chronic nature of metastatic progression in human patients [19]. Extended duration studies in immunocompetent models with spontaneous metastasis would provide valuable insights into the sustainability of the therapeutic effects and possible adaptive responses by tumor cells. Additionally, while no immunogenicity was observed in our murine studies, the inherent species differences in immune recognition necessitate further safety assessment in humanized models or non-human primates to fully evaluate the risk of anti-drug antibody formation in clinical settings. Furthermore, while TRAP staining of whole-mount calvariae reliably quantifies osteoclast surface coverage, future studies employing micro-computed tomography (microCT) and decalcified histomorphometry of long bones will be essential to fully characterize changes in trabecular microarchitecture and cortical bone integrity beyond osteoclast surface measurements. Additionally, future biophysical characterization using Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) will be performed to precisely quantify the binding kinetics and affinity (KD) of rLGR4-ECD for RANKL, thereby further validating the competitive inhibition mechanism proposed herein.

The mechanistic novelty of targeting RANKL signaling creates opportunities for rational combination therapies. For instance, pairing rLGR4-ECD with bone anabolic agents could address the osteoblast suppression commonly observed in osteolytic lesions [20], while combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors might further enhance antitumor responses [21]. Despite these considerations, the comprehensive dataset presented here provides strong justification for continued development of rLGR4-ECD toward clinical application. With its mechanistically distinct action, favorable safety profile, and manufacturability in compliant CHO platforms, rLGR4-ECD represents not merely another antiresorptive agent but a potential disease-modifying therapy capable of fundamentally altering the vicious cycle of bone metastasis. Future studies will focus on optimizing dosing regimens, exploring combination strategies, and validating biomarkers for patient selection, ultimately aiming to translate these compelling preclinical results into meaningful clinical benefits for patients with bone metastatic disease.
