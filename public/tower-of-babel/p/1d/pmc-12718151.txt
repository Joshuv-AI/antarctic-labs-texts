Impact of genomic sequencing information on physicians’ treatment recommendations for children with congenital heart disease
Authors: Danton S Char, Natalie T Deuitch, Matthew K Berent, Wendy K Chung, Jon A Krosnick, David Magnus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGenomic sequencing (GS) is increasingly part of diagnostic evaluations of children with congenital heart disease (CHD). Past research suggests that clinicians might envision the most immediate benefits of GS to be guidance in decisions regarding withdrawal of care and rationing of scarce resources. This study sought to document whether providing GS information to physicians caring for critically ill children with CHD might lead them to alter recommendations regarding care strategies.MethodsA probability sample of 202 cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, neonatologists, and critical-care physicians who provide care for critically ill children with CHD in the United States, using the well-established vignette methodology, were randomly assigned different patient scenarios and different genomic information. Physicians then reported their likelihood of recommending particular treatment options for each patient.ResultsOf the participating physicians, 98.3% had seen genetic test results, 84.5% had used results when making recommendations regarding clinical care, 67.5% had seen GS results, and 37.9% had used GS results when making treatment recommendations. In the survey experiments, receipt of GS results predicting intellectual disability, autism, or a childhood onset cancer syndrome each independently significantly reduced the likelihood of physicians recommending surgical palliation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or heart transplant.ConclusionThis survey demonstrates that physicians caring for critically ill children with CHD might be influenced by genomic results when making recommendations about whether to forego or withdraw certain treatment options. This raises important ethical issues that should be considered carefully in the future.Keywords: Congenital heart disease, Critical care, Futility, Genomic testing, Pediatrics

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common type of congenital anomaly leading to critical illness and death in the United States.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Parents of children with CHD often face difficult decisions about which treatment options to pursue for their children. They rely on physicians to provide accurate, relevant, and complete information about available courses of action. Ideally, physicians explain and make treatment recommendations that recognize prognostic uncertainty. Ideally, the decisions that parents of children with CHD make after difficult conversations with physicians align with their personal values and are in the best interest of a child.

Genomic sequencing (GS) of children with CHD is one of many factors that physicians might consider when discussing or recommending treatment options with parents. Genetics plays a major role in the epidemiology of structural CHD and CHD comorbidities,8 including heart failure, arrhythmia, neurocognitive outcomes, and higher cancer rates in individuals with CHD.8,9 Recent studies have demonstrated a striking shared genetic etiology between CHD and neurodevelopmental disorders,8,10 particularly autism and intellectual disability, in addition to the known association between 22q11 deletion and schizophrenia.11,12 Because genetics contributes to an ever-increasing amount of the overall burden and heterogeneity in outcomes of CHD,13 a thorough understanding of the underlying genetics is essential for improving care. As such, GS is increasingly becoming part of CHD research and diagnostic evaluation.14, 15, 16

Incorporating GS into discussion with parents may pose an ethical dilemma for physicians. Clinicians and health care systems are regularly forced to make choices because of resource limitations or problematic benefit-to-burden ratios of invasive therapies. It might seem reasonable for physicians to use whatever information is available to inform their recommendations to parents to pursue, avoid, or withdraw a treatment option.15 GS may suggest a range and probability of possible futures for a patient, but cannot definitively prognosticate outcomes and associated clinical features, especially for many of the newly identified genetic conditions with sparse data. A potential dilemma faced by physicians, then, is whether, when, and how to use GS to inform their recommendations to pursue, avoid, or withdraw a treatment option.16

Although research is ongoing to strengthen outcome associations with genomic findings,17 the preliminary introduction of GS into the care of critically ill children with CHD appears to be affecting care decisions in sometimes unexpected and significant ways. Clinicians envision the most immediate potential benefits of genomic findings to be allowing earlier declaration of futility and better guidance in recommendations involving withdrawal and rationing of scarce resources.16 In a qualitative interview study, CHD clinicians discussed the predictive power of genomics and the impacts of emerging genomic associations, such as CHD and autism,11,18 intellectual disability,8,10 and schizophrenia12 on care recommendations, as well as the impact of secondary findings, such as cancer syndromes, on organ allocation.16

Preliminary implementation studies suggest that rapid genomic testing services for critically ill children may be more useful regarding decisions about transitioning to palliative care than regarding decisions about changes in medication, procedures, or counseling.19, 20, 21 A recent survey of pediatric solid organ transplant programs revealed a growing use of genomic results, especially secondary findings, such as variants consistent with cancer syndromes and neurocognitive disability, to guide organ allocation.22 Collectively, these findings suggest that clinicians are using, and anticipate expanding the use of, genomic results to justify declarations of futility, withdrawal of care, and rationing of scarce resources, such as organ transplantation.16,19,22,23

Given the complexity of disease and the invasive and costly procedures required for children with CHD, the care of this patient population often involves difficult clinical decisions, such as whether to list the patient for organ transplantation and end-of-life care questions, as well as rationing discussions among clinicians and families.24 Clinicians must decide which therapies to recommend. Clinicians and families must assess whether the therapies offered prove to be useful or prolong needless suffering.25,26

This article reports the findings of experiments embedded in a best-practices national survey of clinicians caring for critically ill children with CHD to assess whether, and to what extent, clinicians might use genomic information to guide care recommendations. There is limited systematic and reliable knowledge of the views of clinicians who might utilize genomic information as they discuss treatment options with parents of children with CHD. This survey was meant to fill that gap.

Materials and Methods

Questionnaire

The experiments embedded in the questionnaire used vignette methodology. Vignette methodology is a well-established technique, both within and outside of medical research, for identifying factors that influence decision making (see Supplemental Information A for a short review of this methodology). By providing different bits of GS information to different decision makers and then measuring their decisions, we were able to identify what information influenced those decisions.

The questionnaire topics were developed based on prior interview work, and the specific questions were constructed to follow current questionnaire design best practices.27

Before fielding the questionnaire, members of the target population completed cognitive pretest interviews. Physicians read initial drafts of the questions, restated those questions using different words and phrases, and then described the thoughts they had while constructing answers. This technique allowed us to revise the questionnaire to yield a final set of questions that physicians interpreted as intended.

The questionnaire asked physicians to read 3 vignettes describing 3 hypothetical patients and made recommendations about treatment of each patient.

Each respondent read 2 vignettes and was randomly assigned to 1 of various versions of each vignette. The first vignette described an infant girl with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS). Physicians were randomly assigned to read (1) no GS information about the patient, (2) that GS indicated that the patient had a high probability of developing adult onset breast cancer, (3) that GS indicated that the patient had a high probability of developing childhood onset cancer, (4) that GS indicated that the patient had a high probability of developing severe intellectual disabilities, or (5) that GS information indicated that the patient had a high probability of developing profound intellectual disabilities and autism.

The second vignette involved a 12-year-old boy with ToF/PA/MAPCAS. Physicians were randomly assigned to read (1) no GS information about the patient, or (2) that GS indicated that the patient had a high probability of developing schizophrenia.

Dependent variables

After reading each vignette, physicians reported their treatment recommendations for the patient. For the vignette about the infant girl with HLHS, physicians answered questions about their likelihood of recommending each of 3 different treatment options: (1) surgical palliation through the single ventricle/Fontan pathway, (2) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) if the infant becomes acutely unstable, and (3) listing the infant as a heart transplant candidate if the infant goes into heart failure. Answers to these questions were coded using the following scheme: “Not likely at all” =.00, “Slightly likely” =.25, “Moderately likely” =.50, “Very likely” =.75, and “Extremely likely” = 1.00.

For the vignette involving the 12-year-old boy with ToF/PA/MAPCAS, physicians reported if they would (1) recommend listing the boy as a transplant candidate, and (2) place the boy on ECMO if the boy becomes acutely unstable. Answers to these questions were coded 0 of a physician would not recommend an option and 1 if the physician would.

In addition, respondents described their familiarity with genetic testing in general, their use of GS information in particular, and their demographics and professional histories.

Respondents

The questionnaire was completed by physicians providing care for children with CHD in 2 phases: a pilot study and the main study. Data from the pilot study and the main study were combined to yield the results reported below.

Pilot study sample

The people invited to participate in the pilot sample were a simple random sample of 100 individuals drawn from a list of 1991 pediatric CHD physicians (pediatric cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, neonatologists, geneticists, and critical-care physicians) who were on the staff (according to the hospital websites) at the top 25 children’s hospitals treating cardiac conditions according to US News and World Report in 2017.28 US News and World Report ranked all hospitals with at least 1 full time attending pediatric cardiothoracic surgeon and 1 full time attending staff in any of the following: pediatric cardiac intensivist (from training in cardiology, pediatric critical care or anesthesiology), pediatric cardiac interventionalist, pediatric cardiac electrophysiologist, pediatric anesthesiologist with specialty cardiac training, or pediatric radiologist. The rankings were based on a combination of the following: (1) clinical outcomes (eg, patient survival, infection rates, and complications), (2) level and quality of hospital resources directly related to patient care (eg, staffing, technology, and special services), (3) delivery of health care (eg, programs that prevent infections and adherence to best practices), and (4) expert opinion among pediatric specialists. The list of staff members and their contact information was obtained from publicly available information and by getting clinician rosters from hospital departments.

Main sample

The main sample was created in 2 stages. First, a simple random sample of 50 hospitals was selected from a list of 157 hospitals in the United States that treat children with critical cardiac conditions (including the 25 hospitals used to select the pilot study sample). That list was created using information published by US News and World Report, the Children’s Hospital Association, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons, and Wikipedia. We then built a list of all physicians working at those 50 hospitals who treated cardiac conditions among children, which included 2148 people. From that list, 30 were selected via simple random sampling to be invited to participate in the main study.

Four of the 630 selected physicians were selected into both the pilot study sample and the main study sample. Three of those individuals received 2 invitations to complete the questionnaire, once as part of the pilot study and again as part of the main study, and none of those 3 ever completed the questionnaire. The fourth person selected into both the pilot study sample and the main study sample completed the questionnaire after being invited to complete it during the pilot study and was not invited again to complete the questionnaire again during the main study data collection. That individual was replaced in the main study sample by another physician who was randomly selected from among the 2148.

Recruitment

Respondent recruitment for the pilot study and the main survey followed procedures recommended by Dillman for maximizing response rates in mail and online surveys.29 First, sampled physicians were sent an email telling them briefly about the survey and that they will be invited in the future to participate. About 1 week later, these physicians were sent an email with a link to the questionnaire. People who did not complete the questionnaire during the ensuing 3 days were sent another email encouraging them to participate. Additional email reminders were sent once every 5 days to physicians who had not yet opened the online questionnaire until the survey data collection period closed approximately 1 month after the first email was sent to each physician. These procedures have been shown in numerous publications to maximize response rates for self-administered questionnaires.30, 31, 32, 33

Approximately 4 weeks after the initial emails were sent to prospective respondents for the main survey, a simple random sample of 100 physicians was drawn from among the people who had not yet completed the online questionnaire. These people were mailed a paper version of the questionnaire. This is a well-established procedure called “subsampling” nonrespondents at the end of the field period for a survey.34, 35, 36 Completed paper questionnaires were received by mail from some of these people, none of whom completed the questionnaire online.

Dispositions

Of the 727 physicians who were invited to complete the questionnaire, 190 opened it online, 174 completed it fully or partially online, 29 completed it fully or partially on paper, 30 sent an email saying that they would not complete the questionnaire, and 6 said that they were ineligible. An estimate of the number of ineligible physicians in the survey’s intended sample is the product of the number of physicians in the sample multiplied by the ineligibility rate among people who replied somehow to our invitations (2.5%). This yields a total estimated eligible sample of 708 physicians (see Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 1 in the supplemental information).

Sampling statistics.

Response rate

Using those numbers, it is possible to compute a response rate for the 2 samples combined (the American Association for Public Opinion Research RR4): 28.7%.37 No criterion can be used to determine whether this response rate or the response rate of any survey is sufficiently high. The reason is that a lower response rate indicates increased risk of nonresponse bias (meaning that the participating sample differs meaningfully from the population of interest), not increased presence of nonresponse bias. A large and growing set of studies have shown that when survey data collection from a random sample is done after rigorous procedures such as those outlined above, decreasing response rates are not associated with more nonresponse bias (see Supplemental Information B). Therefore, no conclusions about the accuracy of this survey should be drawn from its response rate.

Approximately 86% were married when they completed the questionnaire. A total of 48% lived in households with no children under 18 years of age, whereas 18% lived in a household with 1 child less than 18 years old, and 34% lived with 2 or more such children.

Sample representativeness

According to the White House Office of Management and Budget (which reviews and approves all federal surveys), researchers should assess nonresponse bias by comparing attributes of participating respondents with those of the population of interest.38,39 Demographic characteristics of the population of physicians were obtained from Redi-Data, a company authorized by the American Medical Association to maintain and license American Medical Association physician professional data. The data provided by Redi-Data included the numbers of physicians in different age, sex, and region categories who specialized in pediatric critical care medicine, congenital cardiac surgery (thoracic surgery), neonatal-perinatal medicine, pediatric anesthesiology, pediatric cardiothoracic surgery, pediatric cardiology, or pediatrics/medical genetics.

A nonresponse bias assessment was conducted for the sample after accounting for unequal selection probabilities using what are called “base weights” (see Supplemental Information C for a description of the logic for computing base weights).40 For physicians from top 25 hospitals, initial base weights were computed by dividing the total number of physicians in top 25 hospitals by the total number of physicians from top 25 hospitals selected into either intended sample with physicians selected into both intended samples counting twice. For physicians from non-top-25 hospitals, initial base weights were computed by dividing the total number of physicians in non-top-25 hospitals by the total number of physicians from non-top-25 hospitals selected into the main sample. Thus, each physician’s initial base weight indicates the number of physicians in the population his or her responses are intended to represent. Final base weights were then computed by dividing each initial base weight by the average base weight across all physicians who completed the questionnaire. Distributions of sex, age, and hospital region of physicians in the final sample after accounting for unequal selection probabilities are presented in Table 2 (see column 1).

The response rate among physicians from top 25 hospitals in the intended sample (23.1%) was lower than the response rate among physicians from other hospitals in the intended sample (33.0%). To account for this nonresponse bias, each physician’s base weight was multiplied by a factor proportional to the inverse of the response rate for the physician’s group. This procedure ensured that physicians from the top 25 hospitals were not underrepresented in the final sample.

After applying the nonresponse adjusted base weights to the sample, demographics of the respondents deviated from those of the population (compare columns 2 and 4 in Table 2). Standard practice in survey methodology is to eliminate those discrepancies via poststratification weighting. Weights were computed using _anesrake_ for R.41 The poststratification weights entirely eliminated the differences between the participating respondents and the population (compare columns 3 and 4 in Table 2).

Analysis strategy

For each vignette, the primary analyses estimated the effect of GS information on treatment recommendations using ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions in which dummy variables indicating which GS information the respondent received predicted treatment recommendations. Treatment recommendations were coded 0 for physicians who did not recommend the treatment and 1 for physicians who did recommend the treatment. Dummy variables identified which vignette each physician read. For both vignettes 1 and 2, physicians who were given no GS results were the omitted comparison group.

The primary OLS regression parameter estimated were used to calculate the likelihood of recommending each treatment option in each scenario condition. The estimated likelihoods were generated by setting all the control variables at their weighted sample means. These regression results can therefore be viewed as documenting how the proportion of physicians recommending each treatment option changed as the result of providing additional information.

We also calculated how the proportion of patients receiving a particular treatment option changed as the result of providing information to the physicians. To do so, we weighted each physician by the number of critically ill children with cardiac disease that he or she treated, which varied considerably across respondents (see Table 1). To do so, the physician weights used thus far were multiplied by a 4-level variable representing the number of children cared for by each physician (see Table 1); that product was divided by the mean of the products; therefore, the average of the weights was 1.0. The number of children seen was recoded into 4 categories to preserve the positive skew of the distribution while minimizing the distorting impact of outliers.

Physicians from the same hospital worked in the same environment, which creates nonindependence of the respondents from one another. To account for this, the OLS regressions treated hospital as the primary sampling unit.42

The results reported above suggest that the effects of GS among physicians who cared for many children with CHD were similar to the effects among physicians who cared for few such children. Additional OLS regressions formally tested the possibility that the effects of GS varied systematically by the number of children cared for by physicians. These OLS regressions included number of children cared for by physicians and the interactions between number of children cared for and GS conditions as predictors. The results suggested the effects did not vary (see rows 6 through 9 in Supplemental Table 6, and row 3 in Supplemental Table 7 in the supplemental information). Of the 14 interactions tested, only 1 was statistically significant and 2 were marginally statistically different. This pattern is not much different than what would be expected by chance, suggesting the effects at the patient level are comparable to the effects at the physician level.

Discussion

The survey reported here quantitatively tests the likely impact of GS results on physicians’ recommendations to parents about care for children with CHD. Experiments embedded in an online questionnaire found that providing physicians with GS results suggesting future neurobehavioral and health concerns for a child with CHD reduced the likelihood that the physicians would recommend extreme treatment options for that child. The first experiment showed that physicians were less likely to recommend surgical palliation, ECMO, or a heart transplant for an infant girl whose GS results suggested she was likely to develop severe intellectual disabilities, profound intellectual disabilities and autism, or childhood onset cancer syndrome. The second experiment showed that fewer physicians would recommend listing a 12 year old boy as a transplant candidate when GS results indicated the boy was likely to develop schizophrenia. This suggests that GS results factor into physician’s decisions to recommend potential life-sustaining treatment options for children with CHD.

One reason to have confidence in the external validity of these findings is the methodology used to select and recruit the sample of participating physicians. Specifically, there are good reasons to have confidence that the physicians who participated in the current study approximate the population of all physicians in the United States who treat children with CHD. Our sampling process began by defining the populations of interest (both hospitals and physicians), randomly sampled hospitals, and then randomly sampled from the populations of physicians working in the sampled hospitals. Systematic recruitment methods produced a 28.5% response rate, which is on the high end of published norms for this hard-to-access specialist physicians,43,44 and well above surveys in other arenas that have yielded highly accurate population estimates.45,46 Finally, the sample was statistically weighted to account for unequal selection probabilities and to maximize representation of demographic characteristics of physicians in the target population. This sort of methodology has been shown to yield accurate estimates in many studies.

The internal validity of this investigation is also high. Random assignment of each respondent to various forms of vignettes ensured that the experimental effects observed are unrelated to chance and instead are attributable to the impact of GS information.

Additional confidence in the present findings is justified by their consistency with from past research showing that clinicians caring for children with CHD view GS as allowing earlier declaration of futility of some treatment options, earlier recommendations to transition to comfort care, and recommendations against pursuing organ transplantation.16,19, 20, 21, 22,47

Furthermore, the present finding that genomic results indicative of profound autism factor into recommendations to forego treatment options or transplantation complement similar findings regarding intellectual disability.22,23 Extending from clinical or phenotypic manifestations of severe intellectual disability to use of genomic findings strongly suggestive of profound autism broadens the population of children affected by such considerations.

The vignettes presented to physicians involved conditions with high procedural morbidity and tested the extremes of difficult to treat cardiac conditions with the most severe associated clinical features. The ratio of benefits to burdens in this context is close to 1. The value-based influences clinicians use in this narrow benefit/burden context have been well described for the HLHS because of the prognostic uncertainty and high morbidity.48 Subjective considerations around quality of life are influenced by additional clinical findings beyond the primary cardiac disease.48

Considering GS results when formulating treatment recommendations seems reasonable. The treatment recommendations by clinicians caring for children with CHD must balance multiple, possibly contradictory interests. With the scarcity of hearts available for pediatric transplantation and the known acceleration of neoplastic disease with immunosuppressive therapies,49 withholding transplantation when GS results reveal likely childhood onset cancer syndrome is a reasonable medical consideration. Our results show that GS is one source of information that clinicians are likely to use to consider children with CHD holistically.

However, because many families may want to prolong life as much as possible, it is less clear whether recommendations against pursuing ECMO or surgical palliation for patients with aggressive childhood onset cancer syndrome are reasonable. Even more problematic may be decisions not to pursue transplants or use ECMO when GS indicates that a patient is at risk of schizophrenia, profound autism, or intellectual disability.50, 51, 52 Counseling against palliative surgery or ECMO for such children may be too life-limiting.

The present findings raise a question about the proper role of GS information in situations of prognostic uncertainty. Whether genomic findings suggesting intellectual disability or profound autism provide precise guidance is unclear. The prognostic certainty for this cohort of children is dynamic with improving information about rare genetic conditions simultaneous with improving outcomes for children undergoing both HLHS and ToF/PA/MAPCAS repairs.53,54 The present findings also highlight that with sparse data for some conditions, confidence intervals and accuracy of estimates is not yet precise. With time and more data, precision will improve. However, in the interim, clinicians need ways to quantitatively communicate risk and the precision upon which those risks are estimated, to allow clinicians and families to make fully informed decisions. Many hospitals use multidisciplinary consultations, to help teams, patients, and families navigate interpretation of genome sequencing analysis (and for any type of genetic testing) in intensive care. Within multidisciplinary discussion, medical geneticists and genetic counselors offer critically important expertise and support for both families and the health care team in navigating discussion of GS results with patients and families and contextualizing information.

Extensive ethical guidance exists for genomic identification of late onset conditions or conditions for which there is no therapy.55,56 In care of children with CHD and critical illness, genomic findings with prognostic potential can have significant influence over decisions to transition, ration or allocate therapeutic options. Clinicians must consider whether the genotype-phenotype associations strong enough to justify using GS for specific treatment recommendations, especially if we have not yet tested the ability to intervene based upon an early genetic diagnosis.16,57

Trust in several public institutions is currently low, partly because of public perceptions of how these institutions have handled ethical dilemmas.58 Yet, realizing the prognostic potential of genomic testing requires trust from and cooperation with families. Our survey findings suggest that clinicians consider genomic findings in ways that raise unresolved ethical concerns and may compromise families’ trust in them. Transparency and open discussion around these challenging considerations is needed, both to maintain public trust and to identify approaches to addressing these challenges.

Limitations

The full range of factors that influence high-stakes medical treatment recommendations cannot be captured in a single survey. All real-world cases of children with CHD involve unique constellations of patients, guardians, and caregivers, each of whom has their own personal preferences and knowledge. Situational factors may also vary considerably across cases. Physicians must formulate treatment recommendations that balance multiple and diverse interests and considerations. The results of this study do not suggest that GS results are the only factors on which physicians base treatment recommendations for children with CHD or even the most consequential factor. Rather, the study found that GS results were one of the potentially many factors that physicians did use when formulating treatment recommendations.

The results of this study also do not shed light on factors that might moderate the influence of GS results of physicians’ treatment recommendation. For example, the effect of GS results among physicians within one specialty might be substantially different than the effect of that same information among physicians within other specialties. The number of physician specialties represented in our sample, in combination with the number of scenario conditions used, prevented an exploration of the potential moderating influences of specialties. We look forward to future research examining differences in the effects of GS results across these, and other, potential moderators.

Conclusion

The current survey shows that, for some clinicians, GS results influenced predictions of treatment recommendations for children with CHD. As such, this study highlights several important areas that need further exploration, most notably, future qualitative studies to better understand providers’ thinking, differences among providers, and better capturing bedside nurses in these studies. Most significantly, clinicians need to determine how accurately GS results inform prognosis to guide such difficult situations. The benefits of genomic testing could be undermined if public concerns regarding rationing and justifications for transitioning to comfort care are not recognized and discussed.16,58
