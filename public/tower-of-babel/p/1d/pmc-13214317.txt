A randomised controlled study of the efficacy of Internet of Things-based telerespiratory rehabilitation for chronic respiratory diseases
Authors: Mengmeng Wu, Wenjuan Xu, Xin Zhao, Ningning Fang, Kaishu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation for chronic respiratory disease patients at home.DesignThis was a randomised controlled trial.SettingThe study took place in patients’ homes (post-discharge) and the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine of Binzhou People’s Hospital, Shandong First Medical University (inpatient period).Participants123 patients (mean age 68.85 ± 10.72 years, 55% male) with chronic respiratory diseases.InterventionsPatients were randomly assigned to either a 6-month Internet of Things (IoT)-based remote respiratory rehabilitation programme after hospital discharge (study group, n = 60) or usual care with conventional respiratory rehabilitation (control group, n = 63).Main outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were the 6-min walking test (6MWT) and lung function (forced vital capacity [FVC], forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1] and peak expiratory flow [PEF]). The secondary outcomes included the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea index, diaphragm thickness and rehospitalisation rate. Outcomes were assessed at baseline (during hospitalisation, approximately 4 weeks before the intervention started) and after 6 months.ResultsThe study group showed significantly greater improvements in 6MWT (mean difference 63.74 m, 95% CI 45.21–82.27), FVC (0.54 L, 95% CI 0.43–0.65), FEV1 (0.68 L, 95% CI 0.54–0.82), PEF (73.16 L/min, 95% CI 46.22–100) and diaphragm thickness (6.79 mm, 95% CI 4.68–8.90) than the control group (all p < 0.001). The between-group difference in 6MWT (63.74 m) exceeded the minimal clinically important difference (30 m) for chronic respiratory diseases.ConclusionA 6-month IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation programme significantly improved exercise capacity, lung function, dyspnoea index and respiratory muscle strength and reduced rehospitalisations when compared with conventional rehabilitation in patients with chronic respiratory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-026-01562-1.

Introduction

Respiratory rehabilitation is an evidence-based, comprehensive intervention based on thorough patient assessment followed by patient-tailored therapies designed to improve the physical and psychological condition of people with respiratory disease and promote long-term adherence to health-enhancing behaviours [1, 2]. The development of respiratory rehabilitation can be traced back to the 1960s, when it was mainly focused on the rehabilitation needs of patients with tuberculosis. In recent years, the scope of respiratory rehabilitation has expanded from its initial focus primarily on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) to include interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, lung cancer and other chronic respiratory conditions [3]. International guidelines recommend that respiratory rehabilitation should continue for 8–12 weeks with two to three supervised training sessions per week to achieve optimal effects [4, 5]. With the advancement of Internet of Things (IoT) technology, ‘IoT healthcare’ has emerged as a novel concept that provides a visual management platform for clinical medicine and patient health management. Furthermore, it facilitates the development of remote respiratory rehabilitation models that have enhanced the popularity and acceptance of respiratory rehabilitation [6–8]. To address challenges in access and uptake, alternative respiratory rehabilitation models have emerged, including telerehabilitation and home- and community-based programmes. These alternative models have been shown to overcome barriers, such as geographical distance and limited healthcare resources, making rehabilitation more accessible [9]. The recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that these models can effectively improve exercise capacity, reduce dyspnoea, enhance quality of life and potentially reduce healthcare utilisation in patients with chronic respiratory conditions [9]. Telerehabilitation refers to the remote delivery of rehabilitation services, which may utilise IoT technology (e.g. wearable devices, real-time data transmission) as a tool. The IoT is a subset of telehealth; it focuses on interconnected devices for data collection, whereas telehealth encompasses broader remote healthcare services [6, 9]. Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University implemented IoT-based home disease management for patients using home non-invasive ventilation. By establishing an IoT chronic disease management platform, they achieved remote monitoring of patient-related parameters, set early warning indicators to identify potential risks and intervened promptly, ensuring continuous out-of-hospital management [10].

Although IoT-based technology shows promise for addressing the limitations of conventional respiratory rehabilitation, its practical application still faces multiple challenges [11–13]. First, IoT-based rehabilitation requires patients to have access to and proficiency with technology, which may be a barrier for elderly or low-literacy populations [3]. Second, the cost of wearable devices and platform maintenance can be prohibitive, especially in resource-limited settings [5]. The exploration of tele-rehabilitation technologies (e.g. home respiratory training platforms) offers the possibility of improving accessibility; however, there is still a lack of high-quality evidence of its impact on adherence, respiratory muscle function and long-term prognosis [9, 14]. More importantly, existing studies mostly focus on short-term intervention effects [11, 14]; there is insufficient analysis of the long-term benefits and economic costs of IoT technology-driven whole-process closed-loop management models (e.g. hospital–community–family linkage), which hinders policy development and technology scale-up.

In this context, this study systematically evaluates the efficacy of an IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation model in improving exercise capacity (measured by the 6-min walking test [6MWT]) and lung function in patients with chronic respiratory diseases through a prospective randomised controlled trial, aiming to provide empirical evidence to promote the wider application of respiratory rehabilitation.

Results

A randomised controlled trial was conducted comparing IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation with conventional rehabilitation in 123 patients with chronic respiratory diseases (Fig. 1). A total of 156 patients were screened for eligibility between September 2022 and October 2024. Of these, 33 were excluded (18 due to acute exacerbation, 8 with severe cardiovascular disease and 7 unable to complete assessments). The remaining 123 patients were randomised to the study group (n = 60) or control group (n = 63) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1CONSORT flow diagram of participant progress through the phases of the trial. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups are presented in Table 1. The age of the patients was 40–85 years, with an overall mean age of 68.85 ± 10.72 years. There were no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups in terms of gender, age or duration of disease, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) per cent predicted or the FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). All participants were admitted electively during a clinically stable period (no exacerbation in the preceding 4 weeks) for a comprehensive respiratory assessment. The average length of hospital stay (5.3 ± 2.1 days) reflects the duration of inpatient rehabilitation training before discharge, which was identical for both groups. These results indicate that the two groups were well-matched at baseline, ensuring the comparability of the study populations. Comparison of the 6-min walking test, modified Medical Research Council dyspnoea index and rehospitalisation rate between the two groups

The 6MWT, modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea index and rehospitalisation rate are important indicators for evaluating the rehabilitation effect and prognosis of patients with chronic respiratory diseases. Comparing these indicators between the study group and the control group can help to establish the impact of different rehabilitation models on patients’ physical function and disease recurrence.

The differences between the two groups in 6MWT and mMRC dyspnoea index before treatment were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). In the study group, the 6MWT after treatment (383.68 ± 24.49 m) was significantly higher than that before treatment (299.16 ± 31.19 m), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05); the mMRC dyspnoea index after treatment (1 [0, 2] grade) was significantly lower than that before treatment (3 [2, 4] grade), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). In the control group, the 6MWT after treatment (319.94 ± 32.93 m) was significantly higher than that before treatment (297.75 ± 18.26 m), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05); the mMRC dyspnoea index after treatment (2 [1, 3] grade) was significantly lower than that before treatment (3 [2, 4] grade), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). After treatment, the 6MWT of patients in the study group was significantly higher than that of the control group (383.68 ± 24.49 m vs 319.94 ± 32.93 m), and the mMRC dyspnoea index (1 [0, 2] vs 2 [1, 3]) and the number of rehospitalisations (18.33% vs 36.51%) of patients in the study group were significantly lower than that of the control group, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.001). Comparison of pulmonary function parameters between the two groups

Pulmonary function parameters are crucial for assessing the effectiveness of respiratory rehabilitation. Analysing the differences in FVC, FEV1 and peak expiratory flow (PEF) between the two groups can provide evidence for the impact of the IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation on patients’ lung function.

The differences between the two groups in FVC, FEV1 and PEF before treatment were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). In the study group, FVC after treatment (2.79 ± 0.27 L) was significantly higher than that before treatment (1.96 ± 0.19 L), FEV1 after treatment (2.18 ± 0.37 L) was significantly higher than that before treatment (1.22 ± 0.25 L) and PEF after treatment (530.53 ± 51.15 L/min) was significantly higher than that before treatment (406.32 ± 44.12 L/min), with statistically significant differences (all p < 0.05). In the control group, FVC after treatment (2.25 ± 0.18 L) was significantly higher than that before treatment (1.96 ± 0.20 L), FEV1 after treatment (1.50 ± 0.25 L) was significantly higher than that before treatment (1.21 ± 0.23 L) and PEF after treatment (457.37 ± 63.58 L/min) was significantly higher than that before treatment (403.16 ± 40.69 L/min), with statistically significant differences (all p < 0.05). After treatment, the FVC (2.79 ± 0.27 L vs 2.25 ± 0.18 L), FEV1 (2.18 ± 0.37 L vs 1.50 ± 0.25 L) and PEF (530.53 ± 51.15 L/min vs 457.37 ± 63.58 L/min) of patients in the study group were significantly higher than those of the control group, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Intervention implementation and adherence

In the study group, patients completed a mean of 22.4 ± 3.1 out of 24 planned weekly IoT-based sessions (adherence 92.98%). Interventions included guided breathing exercises (three times/week), virtual walking training (twice/week) and monthly video consultations. The control group attended 4.2 ± 1.5 of 6 planned post-discharge telephone follow-ups (adherence 69.8%). See Table 5. Intervention implementation details

Protocol deviations and adverse events

Three patients in the study group withdrew due to technical difficulties with IoT devices. No serious adverse events were reported in either group. Minor adverse events included muscle soreness (n = 5 in the study group, n = 3 in the control group), which was resolved with rest.

Analytical approaches

All reported results are based on intention-to-treat analysis. Per-protocol analysis (n = 112) showed similar findings: 6MWT mean difference 61.23 m (95% CI 48.11–74.35, p < 0.001). Worst-case scenario analysis yielded a smaller but still significant 6MWT difference: 52.89 m (95% CI 39.76–66.02, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The key findings of this study demonstrate that a 6-month IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation programme significantly improved exercise capacity (6MWT), lung function (FVC, FEV1, PEF) and diaphragm thickness and reduced rehospitalisation rates when compared with conventional rehabilitation. These results align with previous studies showing that telerehabilitation can enhance respiratory outcomes in chronic respiratory diseases [9, 10]. The development of the remote respiratory rehabilitation model provides a visual and convenient support platform for patients’ at-home rehabilitation, and the application of IoT technology in respiratory rehabilitation has become increasingly widespread, mainly in the areas of data management, patient education and telemedicine. Through information technology, medical institutions can better collect and analyse patients’ health data, providing a basis for the development of personalised rehabilitation programmes. At the same time, information technology also provides patients with rich educational resources to help them better understand their diseases and the rehabilitation process, thus improving their participation and self-management ability [15–20].

The higher adherence in the IoT group (93.3% vs 70.0%) may be attributed to real-time feedback and personalised adjustments via the platform, consistent with findings that IoT-driven feedback mechanisms improve compliance [21]. Rehabilitators (standardised terminology) can instantly monitor these data, and once abnormalities or deviations from the preset rehabilitation goals are detected, they can react quickly and send adjustment suggestions or reminders to the patients through the platform. Based on the physiological data uploaded by patients and their rehabilitation progress, the system is able to automatically analyse and generate rehabilitation programmes that meet individual needs [22, 23]. Weekly online assessments and dynamic programme adjustments based on uploaded data likely contributed to the observed physiological improvements, such as increased diaphragm thickness and lung function parameters.

The significant improvements in pulmonary function parameters (FVC, FEV1 and PEF) and diaphragm thickness observed in the two groups reflect physiological changes associated with the rehabilitation programme. These findings suggest that systematic respiratory training can lead to measurable improvements in respiratory function. Breathing training can enhance the strength and endurance of the respiratory muscles and improve the ventilation and gas exchange function of the lungs [24–27]. Additionally, IoT-based technology was able to significantly enhance patients’ lung function and respiratory muscle strength and improve the 6MWT and dyspnoea index, which is more consistent with the findings of Zhu Kai et al. [23]. Internet of Things technology allows respiratory training to be performed continuously in the home environment, regardless of time and place. The home-based IoT rehabilitation model used in our study enabled participants to engage in more consistent training sessions, as reflected in our monitoring data. The significant improvements in both functional measures (6MWT) and physiological parameters (lung function tests) suggest clinical benefits from this approach.

The strengths of this study include its prospective randomised design, adherence to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trails guidelines and comprehensive outcome assessment. This prospective study looked at pulmonary function, 6MWT, respiratory muscle strength and mMRC scores. These evaluation metrics come from different dimensions and aim to measure the impact of using respiratory rehabilitation on multiple aspects of patients with chronic respiratory diseases [24, 25]. Lung function measurements capture the improvement in lung function before and after respiratory rehabilitation in patients with chronic respiratory disease [26]; the 6MWT measures the improvement in exercise capacity before and after respiratory rehabilitation in patients with chronic respiratory disease; the mMRC dyspnoea index can measure the improvement of quality of life before and after respiratory rehabilitation in patients with chronic respiratory diseases [16]; maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP), maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) and PEF were used to measure the improvement of respiratory muscle strength before and after respiratory rehabilitation in patients with chronic respiratory diseases [15]. Telemedicine technology enables healthcare services to transcend geographic limitations, realising real-time monitoring and feedback to patients, helping doctors to adjust rehabilitation programmes in a timely manner and enhancing patients’ sense of participation and adherence [27]. The application of these IoT technologies marks the entry of respiratory rehabilitation into a more intelligent and personalised era. However, relevant studies have shown that many patients with chronic respiratory diseases refuse to accept this type of treatment due to various reasons, such as lack of understanding of IoT medicine, family support and economic factors [16]. The construction of a remote respiratory rehabilitation system needs multi-dimensional policy support, such as family doctor contracting, happy breathing programmes, health insurance support and rehabilitation day hospitalisation programmes. Crucially, it also needs policy support at the national level, recognising the importance and value of rehabilitation medicine [21]. Relevant studies have shown that the biofeedback respiratory rehabilitation training system based on virtual reality technology can monitor the patient’s respiratory situation in real time and give immediate feedback to help patients improve their breathing patterns. In addition, the application of IoT technology has effectively enhanced the efficiency of healthcare services, reduced the time of patients’ visits and improved the overall experience of patients’ medical treatment [22]. Through the advancement of these technological tools, the future of respiratory rehabilitation will become more efficient, convenient and personalised [23].

This study has certain research limitations. First, we used convenience sampling to recruit participants, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to the broader population of patients with chronic respiratory diseases. Second, the sample size of this study is relatively small, and there may be some bias in extrapolating the results. We will consider using a multicentre large-sample study for further validation in future studies. Subsequently, influences such as patients’ nutrition and psychological status were also not considered during the study, and we will consider the role of patients’ personality characteristics in future studies. Finally, we did not focus on in-hospital management for homogeneous management, which may have some impact. It should be noted that in this study, the control group received conventional respiratory rehabilitation treatment, which is different from a conventional control group receiving only routine medical treatment. This may have influenced the results to some extent. However, the significant superiority of the study group in multiple indicators still indicates the advantages of the IoT-based telerespiratory rehabilitation model. Future research will add a control group receiving only routine medical treatment to further verify the effectiveness of this rehabilitation method, aiming to provide more accurate evidence for the application of telerespiratory rehabilitation. Potential drawbacks include the need for wearable devices (e.g. lung function meters, oximeters) and digital literacy for both patients (to operate devices and platforms) and healthcare professionals (to monitor data and adjust programmes). Elderly patients or those with limited access to technology may face barriers, which could affect adherence [18, 22].

Conclusion

A 6-month IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation programme significantly improved exercise capacity (6MWT), lung function and respiratory muscle strength and reduced rehospitalisations when compared with conventional rehabilitation in patients with chronic respiratory diseases. These findings support the use of IoT technology to enhance access to effective respiratory rehabilitation, particularly for patients with limited mobility or healthcare access.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study used a randomised controlled trial design. A total of 123 patients with chronic respiratory diseases admitted to the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine of Binzhou People’s Hospital, Shandong First Medical University between September 2022 and October 2024 were selected as the study participants using convenience sampling. For randomisation, a computer-generated random number sequence generated with SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used; each patient was assigned a unique random number, and the numbers were sorted in ascending order. The first 63 patients were allocated to the control group and the remaining 60 to the study group. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Conventional respiratory rehabilitation was used in the control group, including the following: (1) in-hospital training—pursed-lip respiration breathing instruction (twice/day, 10 min/session); (2) exercise training—walking training (30 min/day, 50–60 m/min); (3) health education—smoking cessation and medication counselling (twice/week); (4) post-discharge telephone follow-up (once/month) for 6 months, adhering to the Chinese Guidelines for Respiratory Rehabilitation Management [2]. Internet of Things tele-respiratory rehabilitation was executed in the study group (IoT-based tele-interactive model and low-cost, home-based rehabilitation model for 6 months after discharge from the hospital in the study group). Healthcare professionals administering interventions were not blinded due to the distinct nature of in-person versus remote delivery. However, data collectors, analysts and third-party assessors remained blinded to group allocation to ensure objectivity. Baseline assessments were completed during hospitalisation, including lung function, exercise capacity, quality of life and respiratory muscle function tests. The rehabilitation intervention began 4 weeks after discharge, ensuring patient stability in the home environment.

The target sample size was calculated a priori to detect a between-group difference of 30 m in the change from baseline in the 6MWT, which represents the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for patients with chronic respiratory diseases. Based on data from previous telerehabilitation trials in comparable populations, we assumed an SD of 45 m for 6MWT change scores. This corresponds to a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.67). Using a two-sample independent t-test with a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and 80% statistical power, the required sample size was 56 participants per group. Anticipating a 10% dropout rate over the 6-month intervention period, we planned to enrol 62 participants per group, resulting in a total target sample size of 124 participants.

All participants underwent baseline assessments during their index hospitalisation. Eligibility required clinical stability with no acute exacerbation in the preceding 4 weeks. The IoT-based or conventional rehabilitation intervention commenced exactly 4 weeks after hospital discharge. Follow-up outcome assessments were conducted 6 months after the intervention start date. Thus, the interval between baseline assessment and follow-up was approximately 6 months and 4 weeks, although the primary analysis window refers to the 6-month intervention period.

Randomisation was performed using a computer-generated block randomisation sequence with a 1:1 allocation ratio and a fixed block size of 4, produced by an independent statistician using SPSS version 26.0. The statistician had no involvement in participant recruitment, intervention delivery or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was maintained through the use of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Each envelope contained a card indicating group assignment (IoT-based rehabilitation or conventional rehabilitation). Participant enrolment was performed by trained research coordinators at Binzhou People’s Hospital. After eligibility confirmation and completion of baseline assessments during hospitalisation, a designated clinical administrator—who was not involved in recruitment or care delivery—opened the next available envelope in sequence to reveal the assigned intervention arm. This workflow ensured that individuals enrolling participants remained blinded to upcoming allocations throughout the study period.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 40–85 years; (2) diagnosed chronic respiratory disease (including COPD, bronchiectasis, interstitial lung disease and lung cancer); (3) ability to understand study requirements, verified via a brief questionnaire assessing comprehension of the intervention protocol, administered by research nurses; (4) electively admitted for respiratory assessment during a stable disease state; (5) clinically stable condition, defined as no exacerbation in the 4 weeks prior to the study initiation.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) disease in acute stage (respiratory infection or exacerbation); (2) severe cardiovascular disease (unstable angina, myocardial infarction within the past 3 months, decompensated heart failure). Severe cardiovascular diseases were excluded to minimise confounding effects on exercise capacity (e.g. 6MWT performance) and to reduce safety risks during rehabilitation [11]; (3) neuromuscular, skeletal or psychiatric disorders affecting exercise capacity; (4) inability to complete assessment tests or intervention measures (e.g. unable to perform the 6MWT); (5) planned participation in other interventions that may affect outcomes during the study period.

The study was conducted with the informed consent of the patients and their families and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Binzhou People’s Hospital, Shandong First Medical University (Ethics No. 2023475).

Intervention

Both groups of patients were treated as follows during their stay in the hospital.

Inpatient respiratory rehabilitation programme

Inpatient rehabilitation included endurance training (walking), strength training (isotonic and resistance exercises) and health education (smoking cessation, medication counselling) in line with international guidelines [4]. The specific interventions were as follows: (1) breathing techniques (pursed-lip respiration, abdominal respiration, active cycle of breathing techniques); (2) airway clearing (postural drainage, effective cough training); (3) structured exercises (08:00–08:30 on alternate days). Hospital stay duration varied by patient (mean 5.3 ± 2.1 days) based on clinical stability, with no fixed 12-week programme. Inpatient rehabilitation was provided daily during the stay. Health education (smoking cessation and medication counselling) was conducted twice weekly during the in-hospital training sessions, at the same time as other inpatient rehabilitation activities.

Conventional respiratory rehabilitation in the control group included both inpatient and post-discharge phases, as follows:

During hospitalisation: (1) in-hospital training—pursed-lip respiration breathing instruction (twice/day, 10 min/session); (2) exercise training—walking training (30 min/day, 50–60 m/min); (3) health education—smoking cessation and medication counselling (twice/week). These interventions were provided daily during the patient’s hospital stay (mean 5.3 ± 2.1 days) based on clinical stability.

After discharge: the conventional group received post-discharge telephone follow-up (once/month) for 6 months, focusing on reinforcing rehabilitation guidance, monitoring adherence to home exercise and addressing medication-related questions.

Standard medical treatment

Basic treatment was given, including anti-inflammatory medication, oxygen therapy, cough and phlegm medication, physiotherapy (mechanically assisted phlegm expulsion) and other conventional treatments, as well as education on smoking and alcohol cessation and the distribution of health education brochures.

The study group received remote respiratory rehabilitation based on the IoT in addition to conventional treatment after hospital discharge and post-discharge conventional care (consistent with the control group). The IoT-based remote respiratory rehabilitation system comprised three integrated components: (1) wearable sensing devices—patients used a Bluetooth 5.0-enabled handheld spirometer (model: RH-SP200, accuracy ± 3% for FVC/FEV1), a fingertip pulse oximeter (sampling rate 1 Hz, SpO₂ accuracy ± 2%) and a respiratory inductance plethysmography belt to monitor diaphragm excursion. All devices transmitted encrypted data via 4G/Wi-Fi to a secure cloud server every 5 min during training sessions; (2) patient mobile application—a custom Android/iOS app provided guided breathing exercises (pursed-lip, diaphragmatic), real-time biofeedback on inhalation/exhalation ratios and scheduled virtual walking sessions. The app logged adherence and allowed patients to report symptoms; (3) clinician dashboard—hosted on the hospital’s ‘Respiratory Rehabilitation Full-Course Management Platform’, this web-based interface enabled rehabilitation specialists to visualise longitudinal trends in lung function, exercise compliance and vital signs. Automated alerts were triggered if PEF dropped > 20% from baseline or SpO₂ was < 90% for > 2 min. The system architecture followed a three-tier design (device layer → cloud middleware → application layer) with end-to-end AES-256 encryption compliant with China’s Cybersecurity Law and hospital data governance policies. Data were stored on a private server managed by Binzhou People’s Hospital, with access restricted to authorised personnel via two-factor authentication.

All interventions were administered by respiratory therapists and trained nurses from the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, Binzhou People’s Hospital.

In-hospital training included printed breathing technique diagrams and exercise logs; post-discharge follow-up used standardised telephone interview scripts. In-hospital training involved one-on-one demonstration and correction of breathing techniques; exercise training was supervised by nurses with real-time heart rate and oxygen saturation monitoring. Post-discharge telephone follow-ups followed a structured checklist to assess exercise adherence and symptom changes.

Outcome measures

The two groups of patients were studied for 6 months, and the relevant indices of the patients were recorded 1 month before the intervention and 6 months after the intervention, respectively. The primary outcome measures were the 6MWT and lung function indices (FVC, FEV1, PEF); secondary outcome measures included the mMRC dyspnoea index, diaphragm thickness, rehospitalisation rate and respiratory muscle strength indices (MIP, MEP).

Respiratory muscle strength indices

Respiratory muscle strength was measured by the same pulmonary function instrument (PF810) or respiratory training instrument (PE100), and the differences in MIP, MEP and PEF were compared between the two groups.

Clinical symptoms and functional capacity

Lung function tests (FVC, FEV1) were performed by a pulmonary function technician using a pulmonary function instrument. Functional capacity was assessed using the 6MWT with the Multidimensional Measurement System (MMS), a device that simultaneously records walking distance, heart rate and oxygen saturation during the 6MWT, validated for respiratory disease populations [15]. The mMRC dyspnoea index was used to evaluate clinical symptoms of dyspnoea, conducted according to American Thoracic Society guidelines in a 30-m straight corridor. Patients were instructed as follows: ‘Walk as fast as possible for 6 min [15]; you may rest or slow down as needed, but note the number of rests’. Distance was recorded using an MMS; heart rate and SpO₂ were monitored throughout the test.

Respiratory muscle strength

Respiratory muscle strength is an important aspect of respiratory function. Diaphragm thickness was measured via ultrasound (Siemens Acuson S2000) at functional residual capacity, as described in [16]. Comparing the diaphragm thickness and other respiratory muscle-related indicators between the study group and the control group can help evaluate the effect of different rehabilitation methods on respiratory muscle strength.

Adherence assessment

Adherence was monitored via platform logs for the IoT group and self-reported exercise diaries for the control group.

Statistical analysis

Excel 2016 was used to organise the collected data, and IBM SPSS 26.0 was used to analyse the data statistically. Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± SD if normally distributed (assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test) or median (interquartile range) if non-normally distributed. Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-parametric) as appropriate. Qualitative variables were presented as n (%), with between-group comparisons using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact test (when expected cell counts < 5). A p-value of < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Sample size was based on the MCID of 30 m in the 6MWT, which is widely accepted for chronic respiratory diseases, including COPD, validated for respiratory disease [17]. Calculations used G*Power 3.1 with 90% power, α = 0.05 and 15% dropout rate, requiring 60 patients per group (total 120).

For continuous outcomes, mean differences (95% CI) were reported; for categorical outcomes, relative risks (95% CI) were calculated. These metrics were included in the Results section and corresponding tables.

The per-protocol analysis included only participants who completed ≥ 80% of the intervention sessions and had no major protocol violations (e.g. concurrent participation in other rehabilitation programmes). For the worst-case scenario analysis, missing post-intervention data were imputed with baseline values to assess the robustness of the findings.
