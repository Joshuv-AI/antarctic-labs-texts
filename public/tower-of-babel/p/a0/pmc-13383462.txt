Robotic gynecologic surgery in Saudi Arabia: a descriptive retrospective cohort study of contemporary second-decade perioperative outcomes
Authors: Khalid Akkour, Eman Alshehri, Hani Alhalal, Ali Bassi, Mujahid Bukhari, Mansour Alshamari, Assem Kalantan, Lolowah Alghuson, Ahmed Sherif Abdel-Hamid Abdel-Wahab, Amal El Shabrawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimFollowing early feasibility reports in 2010, this retrospective cohort study evaluates contemporary robotic gynecologic outcomes after ten years of institutional integration. We aim to assess perioperative safety and explore clinical trends associated with adverse events during this phase at a Saudi tertiary center.Patients and methodsA retrospective cohort study of robotic-assisted surgeries performed between January 2018 and December 2020 at King Saud University Medical City, Riyadh. Adult women (N=112) undergoing elective robotic surgery for benign (e.g., symptomatic fibroids, AUB) or malignant conditions were included. We excluded emergency cases, patients scheduled for primary open/laparoscopic approaches, and those with incomplete perioperative records. Data on demographics, surgical metrics (OR time, blood loss), and complications were analyzed using binary logistic regression (p < 0.05) to identify risk factors.ResultsAmong 112 patients (mean age 56.9 years, mean BMI 36.67 kg/m2), 72.3% had prior abdominal surgeries and 54.5% had multiple comorbidities. The conversion rate to laparotomy was 0%. Indications included abnormal uterine bleeding (28.6%), endometrial cancer (27.7%), and hyperplasia (17%). Median blood loss was 100 mL, and the mean operation duration was 142 minutes. Most patients (75.9%) were discharged within one day. Five complications occurred (4.5%): two gastrointestinal, two bladder injuries, and one hernia/obstruction. Univariate analysis identified potential exploratory associations between perioperative events and prior cesarean sections (p=0.048), blood loss (p=0.016), and operating room (OR) duration (p=0.005). Due to the low event rate (4.5%), a multivariate model could not reliably confirm independent risk factors.ConclusionThis second-decade experience demonstrates a high level of safety and technical feasibility in robotic gynecology. The zero conversion rate and low overall morbidity, even in high-BMI and multi-scarred patients, affirm robotic surgery as a dependable primary modality. The identified risk factors should be interpreted strictly as exploratory trends for perioperative planning rather than definitive predictors.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04684-9.Keywords: Robot-assisted surgery, RAS, Minimally invasive surgery

Introduction

Robot-assisted surgery (RAS) is a minimally invasive modality that utilizes specialized instrumentation controlled via a surgeon console. Unlike conventional laparotomy, RAS typically employs small incisions to host multi-articulated robotic arms and a high-definition 3D camera. This platform provides the surgeon with an enhanced range of motion and stereoscopic visualization, allowing for precise dissection and suturing within a confined operative field [1, 2]. By translating the surgeon’s hand movements into filtered, digitized instrument maneuvers, these systems aim to overcome the ergonomic and technical limitations associated with traditional laparoscopic instruments [3].

In Saudi Arabia, it was first introduced in April 2003. In 2019, there were 19 robot-assisted platform systems installed. In the Middle East, there were six in Qatar, three in the UAE, two in each of Kuwait and Lebanon, and one in Egypt [4]. Given the limited knowledge and the increasing trend toward switching from Conventional Laparoscopic Surgery (CLS) and Open Abdominal Surgery (OAS) to RAS, it is necessary to conduct a study that helps establish objective criteria for deciding whether to perform RAS or CLS. Also, to assess the efficacy, safety, and feasibility of RAS among specific populations, such as elderly and obese patients.

The latest Cochrane systematic review comparing RAS with CLS and OAS concluded that the effectiveness and safety of RAS are comparable to those of CLS and OAS for both malignant and non-malignant diseases, but the evidence is of low certainty. The measured outcomes were Intraoperative and postoperative complications, operative time, Length of hospital stay, conversion rate (RAS vs. CLS), estimated blood loss, and blood transfusion [5]. Focusing on endometrial cancer, the latest meta-analysis that compared the three different approaches concluded that Robotic surgery is a safer surgical approach than laparoscopy and laparotomy in the surgical staging of endometrial cancer. The intra-operative complication rate was significantly lower in RAS than in CLS, but not significantly different from OAS. However, estimated blood loss and blood transfusion were significantly lower in RAS than in OAS, but not in CLS. While operative time was significantly longer in RAS, hospital stay was shorter compared to CSL and OAS. Regarding the harvested lymph nodes, there was no statistically significant difference among the three approaches [6].

As obesity and overweight are now considered a global epidemic, it is well known to affect the surgical outcomes. However, despite the prolonged use of maximum (at 30 degrees) Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum, which can result in adverse physiologic events. RAS is generally safe and feasible across a wide range of ages and BMIs. In addition, anesthetic and perioperative complications rarely occur [7].

While early reports from 2010 to 2012 [8, 9] established the initial feasibility of Robot-assisted surgery (RAS) in Saudi Arabia, there remains a significant literature gap regarding the long-term maturation of these programs. Most existing regional data focus on the ‘learning curve’ phase. This study is unique as it evaluates the ‘second-decade experience’ of a high-volume tertiary center, offering a novel perspective on operative maturity, safety, and standardized outcomes.

Patients and methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Saud University Medical City (Ref. No. 20/0023/IRB) and conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Standard written informed consent for the primary surgical intervention and anesthesia was routinely obtained from all patients prior to their procedures. Because this investigation utilized a retrospective cohort design analyzing de-identified electronic medical records with minimal risk to participants, the requirement for specific patient informed consent for research data utilization was formally reviewed and waived by the Institutional Review Board. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout data extraction and analysis.

This study was conducted and reported in strict accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies.

Inclusion criteria

This study includes adult women aged 18 years and older who underwent robot-assisted gynecological surgeries using the Da Vinci Si system at King Saud University Medical City between January 2018 and December 2020. These surgical procedures were performed for a range of benign conditions, such as symptomatic fibroids, abnormal uterine bleeding, and endometrial hyperplasia, as well as malignant conditions like endometrial and cervical cancers.

Exclusion criteria included patients who were initially scheduled for traditional open surgery or laparoscopy, as well as those with incomplete records lacking key perioperative details such as operative time, blood loss estimates, or primary histopathological results. Additionally, emergency gynecological surgeries performed on an urgent basis that did not permit standard robotic docking procedures were excluded.

The demographics and risk factors include: age, body mass index, past medical history, past surgical history, parity, number of cesarean sections, and abortions. Other risk factors during and after the operation include: estimated blood loss, hemoglobin level, blood transfusion, duration of the operation, length of stay in the hospital, type of procedure, indication of the procedure, final histopathology, staging, rate of laparotomy conversion, patient setting, inpatient and discharge medications.

Surgical complications and adverse events were classified into intraoperative and postoperative categories for clear reporting. Intraoperative adverse events (IAEs) refer to mechanical or visceral injuries that occur from the initial skin incision to wound closure. Postoperative complications were systematically recorded and graded according to the Clavien–Dindo Classification, focusing on deviations from the normal recovery process. Within this system, Grade II complications involve the need for pharmacological treatment, such as blood transfusions or antibiotics. Grade IIIb complications specifically indicate adverse events that require secondary surgical, endoscopic, or radiological interventions under general anesthesia.

Complications were identified through a comprehensive retrospective review of electronic medical records (EMR), cross-referencing surgical reports, inpatient progress notes, emergency department logs, and outpatient follow-up records to ensure all morbidities were captured.

Postoperative Follow-Up and Outcome Documentation: To thoroughly evaluate postoperative morbidity, a minimum follow-up of 6 months within the hospital’s integrated electronic medical record (EMR) was mandatory for inclusion. The median follow-up duration for the final group was 14.5 months (range: 6–34 months), with all 112 patients meeting the 6-month minimum surveillance. To reduce missing data, a systematic audit was conducted by two investigators using a standardized electronic data collection form. Instead of relying solely on outpatient notes, the institutional database was queried across four sources: (1) Emergency Department logs, (2) secondary or unplanned inpatient readmission codes, (3) outpatient surgical clinic documentation, and (4) radiology or interventional registries.

Statistical analysis: Descriptive statistics were used to summarize cohort characteristics, with continuous variables presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and range, as appropriate. Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify potential risk factors for complications; however, results were interpreted with caution, given the small number of events. A multivariate model was initially explored, but it is presented with the explicit caveat of limited statistical power due to the low overall incidence of morbidity.

Results

The study involved all women who underwent Robot-assisted gynecologic surgery at King Saud University Medical City in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Out of 112 women, the average age of patients undergoing robot-assisted gynecologic surgery was 56.94 ± 8.40 years (ranging 42–82 years), with a mean BMI of 36.6706 ± 7.03927 kg/m² (ranging 21.34–53.56 kg/m²) [data not tabulated]. Most of the patients had multiple comorbidities reported, 61 (54.5%), while only 19 (17.0%) females were medically free. In addition, 81 (72.3%) women had previous abdominopelvic surgeries, of whom 45 (40.2%) were for gynecological indications and 36 (32.1%) for non-gynecological indications. Additionally, the average parity among all women in the study was 5.99 ± 3.50418. Of these, 18 women (16.1%) had a history of previous cesarean section. The mean number of abortions was 0.81 ± 1.174.

The cohort demonstrated favorable intraoperative and perioperative metrics (Table 1). The mean estimated blood loss (EBL) during robotic procedures was 135.27 ± 144.05 mL, ranging from 50 to 1000 mL. The baseline preoperative hemoglobin level for the patient population averaged 12.15 ± 1.47 g/dL (range: 7.50–16.00 g/dL). Regarding procedural efficiency, the mean total operating room (OR) duration—encompassing both pre- and post-anesthesia preparation—was 142.05 ± 52.75 min, with an absolute range of 60 to 376 min [Data not tabulated].

All patients underwent the procedures as inpatients, with an average length of stay of 1.42 ± 1.054 days; 85 (75.9%) were admitted for 1 day only. Out of 112 procedures, 109 were hysterectomies with salpingectomies, with an ovarian preservation rate of less than 3% (2.7%). The conversion rate to laparotomy was 0%.

Perioperative Complications and Adverse Events: The overall perioperative morbidity rate in this cohort was 4.5% (n = 5). Discussion

Our results and their interpretation

The study analyzed 112 patients undergoing robotic gynecologic surgery, with a mean age of 56.9 years, a BMI of 36.7 kg/m2, and 54.5% with multiple comorbidities. Most procedures were hysterectomies with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (62.5%), primarily for endometrial cancer (27.7%) and abnormal uterine bleeding (28.6%). Perioperative adverse events were rare, occurring in 4.5% of the cohort. True intraoperative adverse events, limited to four cases (3.6%), included bladder and bowel injuries (1.8% each), all successfully managed robotically. Only one postoperative complication occurred: a hernia with intestinal obstruction (0.9%) requiring reoperation. Overall, 95.5% had uneventful perioperative courses, with a 0% conversion rate to laparotomy.

Postoperative stays were short (75.9% discharged in 1 day), with pain managed predominantly by paracetamol and NSAIDs. These findings suggest robotic surgery is safe in complex cases, with low morbidity and efficient recovery in a high-risk population.

All robotic procedures were performed by a core team of ten senior consultants specializing in gynecologic oncology and minimally invasive surgery. These surgeons possess extensive backgrounds in robotic platforms, having completed formal fellowship training and collectively performed over 250 robotic cases prior to the study period. This high level of institutional expertise and the transition beyond the initial learning curve are fundamental to the technical maturity observed in this cohort, specifically the zero conversion rate and minimal intraoperative morbidity.

Univariate analysis identified four factors associated with complications: histopathology (neoplastic uterine conditions reduced risk, OR = 0.378, p = 0.037), number of cesarean sections (OR = 1.798, p = 0.048), blood loss (OR = 1.005, p = 0.016), and operative duration (OR = 1.019, p = 0.005). While our univariate data suggest that surgical history and operative complexity (time and blood loss) trend toward higher risk, our study was not powered to establish them as independent predictors in multivariate modeling. This low event rate, while a limitation for statistical modeling, is a clinically positive finding that reflects the maturation of robotic surgery outcomes at our institution.

The results highlight the safety of robotic surgery in gynecology, with complication rates aligning with global benchmarks. The lack of independent predictors in multivariate analysis underscores the multifactorial nature of surgical risk, emphasizing the need for larger studies to clarify associations. Short hospital stays and minimal opioid use suggest effective enhanced recovery protocols.

Comparison of our results to similar studies

Two early Saudi experiences, reported about a decade ago, laid the groundwork for robotic gynecologic surgery in our region. Al-Badawi et al. [8] analyzed 60 consecutive patients (Dec 2005–Dec 2010) with benign and malignant disease undergoing robotic procedures at a single center, noting a mean age of around 43 years and a BMI of 30.3 kg/m2. The case mix included benign complex pathology and endometrial, ovarian, and cervical cancers, with mean operative times around 95 min overall and about 2 days of mean hospital stay; conversions were rare (about 3%), and complications occurred in roughly 6–7% of cases. Sait [9] reviewed 35 patients across 62 procedures (2008–2010), reporting a similar learning curve, a 6.5% conversion rate, 14% overall complications (not robot-related), a mean postoperative stay of 3 days, and rapid docking times. Together, these studies established feasibility and safety and documented a gradual learning curve that informed subsequent Saudi robotic programs. Our study can be viewed as the second decade of experience in Saudi Arabia’s robotic gynecology, building on early experiences and extending to a more diverse, contemporary practice. It reflects mature techniques, broader indications, improved outcomes, and increased institutional adoption, signaling continued growth beyond the initial decade for women’s health services.

Our study aligns with Perutelli et al. [10], which assessed robotic gynecologic surgery and reported similar themes: operative time, blood loss, prior surgeries, and patient factors influence outcomes. Peuritelli et al. observed a postoperative complication rate of 10.7% (11/138) with longer operative and morcellation times in heavier uteri, and identified BMI and prior surgeries as influential in multivariable analysis. Our results show very few complications (5 in 112 cases), with univariate signals for histopathology, blood loss, prior cesarean, and longer operative time, though multivariate findings are unstable due to sparse events. Overall, our cohort achieved lower complication rates, comparable or favorable operative metrics, and similar predictor patterns, suggesting excellent perioperative performance.

In 2019, Rajanbabu et al. [11] reported a substantial single-center experience with 181 robotic gynecologic procedures, mainly for endometrial cancer, achieving very low blood loss (about 25 mL) and short hospital stays, with steadily improving operative metrics. Our series includes 112 cases from 2018 to 2020, showing similarly low morbidity and minimal transfusion needs, with favorable intraoperative outcomes, including low blood loss and efficient docking and console times. Both datasets demonstrate streamlined robotic workflows and mature perioperative care, reflecting robust technique and consistent performance across centers. When juxtaposed, our results align with the high-quality benchmarks described in 2019 studies: comparable blood loss, rapid recovery, and efficient surgical workflows. This parity supports the view that our center delivers outcomes on par with established standards for robotic gynecology, reinforcing confidence in continuing and expanding these capabilities within our practice.

Our findings are strongly supported by the recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Lenfant et al. [12], which analyzed 24 studies (110,306 robotic hysterectomies) and demonstrated robotic surgery’s advantages over open approaches, including shorter hospital stays (p < 0.0001), reduced blood loss (p = 0.009), and fewer complications (OR: 0.42, p = 0.0001). Like our cohort (n = 112), which showed low complication rates (5 events) and minimal blood loss, Lenfant’s data confirm the safety and efficiency of robotic surgery. The review also highlights robotic superiority in managing complex cases, such as large uteri or prior surgeries, a trend our univariate signals (e.g., prior CS, histopathology) indirectly reflect. While Lenfant notes robotic outcomes align closely with laparoscopy, our results mirror this, underscoring robotic surgery’s role in minimizing morbidity. This alignment with large-scale evidence reinforces the validity of our findings and positions our center’s outcomes within global benchmarks for safe, effective robotic gynecologic care.

Clinical Implications of our study: Our study demonstrates that robotic gynecologic surgery achieves low complication rates (5 events in 112 cases) and minimal intraoperative blood loss, even in complex cases, reinforcing its safety and feasibility in diverse settings. These findings suggest robotic approaches can be prioritized for patients with obesity, prior surgeries, or comorbidities, where open techniques pose higher risks.

Strengths and limitations of our study

This study adds to the growing evidence supporting the safety of robotic gynecologic surgery, with low complication rates (5 events in 112 cases) and minimal blood loss, aligning with global benchmarks. The cohort reflects a contemporary, diverse case mix, including malignancy and complex benign conditions, enhancing real-world relevance. Standardized robotic workflows and institutional expertise likely contributed to favorable outcomes, reinforcing feasibility in resource-equipped settings.

A key strength of this study is its practical value for preoperative counseling and surgical decision-making. By establishing local benchmarks, including a 0% conversion rate, surgeons can offer evidence-based reassurance to patients with high BMI or previous scars. Additionally, recognizing blood loss and operative duration as indicators of potential complications enables more personalized consent discussions and better perioperative resource planning. This information supports the move toward using robotic platforms as the preferred approach for complex gynecologic surgeries in the region.

Limitations

Despite the clinical significance of our findings, this study has limitations. As a retrospective chart review, it is subject to the inherent constraints of electronic record documentation, though these were mitigated by multi-source cross-referencing of surgical, inpatient, and emergency logs. Additionally, while the 0% conversion rate and low morbidity (4.5%) reflect a high level of surgical proficiency, the low number of adverse events (n = 5) limited the statistical power of our multivariate logistic regression model. Consequently, the predictors identified in the univariate analysis should be interpreted as exploratory trends rather than definitive independent risk factors. Finally, while our data extraction protocol involved a highly systematic search of all emergency, readmission, and outpatient electronic registries within King Saud University Medical City, the retrospective design means we cannot completely rule out out-of-network attrition. Minor, self-limiting complications or those managed at local peripheral clinics outside our immediate tertiary care network may have escaped capture. However, given that our facility serves as a primary regional tertiary hub, it is highly likely that any clinically significant morbidities or reoperations would have been referred to our center for definitive care.

Recommendations for future studies

Larger, multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up are needed to validate predictors of complications and refine risk stratification. Comparative trials directly contrasting robotic, laparoscopic, and open approaches in matched cohorts could clarify cost-benefit ratios. Structured training protocols should be evaluated to address learning curves.

Conclusion

Our study reflects a contemporary cohort of robotic gynecologic experiences, characterized by an excellent safety profile and zero conversions to open surgery. While the low number of adverse events limits our ability to mathematically define independent predictors of risk, the exploratory trends highlight the clinical necessity of careful preoperative planning in patients with prolonged operative times or prior uterine scarring.
