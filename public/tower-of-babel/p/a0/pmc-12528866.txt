Peripheral Nerve Blocks in Patients With Osteogenesis Imperfecta Undergoing Extremity Procedures are Safe and Effective
Authors: Isaiah J. Rhodes, Timothy E. Hereford, Kesavan Sadacharam, Kenneth J. Rogers, Manish Purohit, Robert S. Lang, Jeanne M. Franzone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA paucity of data is available regarding regional nerve blocks in patients with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) undergoing extremity procedures. We report a series of regional nerve blocks for patients with OI undergoing reconstructive extremity procedures.MethodsThis was a retrospective review of patients with OI undergoing extremity orthopaedic procedures from July 2018 to September 2023. Chart review was completed for demographics, OI type, procedures, type of block, opioid use, pain scores and any complication.ResultsNinety surgical encounters involving 58 patients (34 male) were identified. The average age at surgery was 11.8 (2–33) years. Osteogenesis imperfecta type III was the most common type encountered (35.6% of patients). Femoral realignment and rodding (26.6%) were the most common procedures performed (13 primary, 22 revisions). The most common block was the lumbar plexus (36). Intraoperative average morphine milligram equivalent (MME)/kg was 1.0 (0–3.1). Pediatric acute care unit (PACU) average MME/kg was 0.1 (0–0.4). Total MME/kg for the first 24 h and from 24 to 48 h postoperatively were 0.3 (0–1.4) and 0.4 (0–1.7) respectively. Average pain scores in the PACU, the first 24 h, and 24–48 h postoperatively was 1.2 (0–9, standard deviation [SD] = 1.9), 2.1 (0–8, SD = 1.8), and 2.2 (0–8, SD = 2.0), respectively. No significant adverse events related to the regional blocks were noted.ConclusionIn this series of patients with OI undergoing extremity procedures, the use of regional blocks and indwelling catheters was associated with no adverse events. Patients had low pain scores and minimal postoperative opioid use.Level of EvidenceIVKey Concepts(1)Postoperative pain control is critical for OI patients with frequent surgeries.(2)Peripheral nerve blocks with catheters are safe and effective for OI patients.(3)Peripheral nerve blocks with catheters are best part of a multimodal pain regimen.(4)Use special care with brittle bones when positioning for catheter placement.(5)Plan with the OR team to avoid catheter dressings interfering with the sterile field.

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in collagen-related genes, most commonly COL1A1 and COL1A2, which code for the alpha 1 and alpha 2 chains of type I collagen [1,2]. This defect results in what is often described as “brittle bone” disease, with variable clinical manifestations. Sillence et al. first published a classification system that included four subtypes of OI, including mild (type I), lethal (type II), severe (type III), and moderate (type 4) [3]. This classification has been modified and expanded to include other subtypes and descriptive groupings [4]. Type VIII OI is a rare recessive form of OI typically presenting with a severe phenotype, and type XI OI is also known as Bruck Syndrome, a severe form of OI with clinical manifestations including joint contractures similar to those seen with arthrogryposis [4]. The incidence of OI varies from region to region but is estimated to be one in 10,000 live births in the United States, making it one of the most frequent skeletal dysplasias [1]. Advancements in medical treatment, such as bisphosphonate therapy and other therapeutic agents, have helped improve bone strength in patients with OI [5,6]. However, surgical treatment, such as intramedullary rodding is often necessary and has been pivotal in improving the quality of life for patients with OI [[7], [8], [9]].

Perioperative pain control in patients with OI is an important aspect of surgical care. A multimodal approach, including opioid and non-opioid agents is usually taken. The detrimental effects of these medications, particularly opioids, need to be considered. It is common for many patients with OI to undergo multiple surgical procedures in their lifetime. Repeated exposure to narcotics, benzodiazepines, and various muscle relaxants after fractures or surgical repair puts many patients with OI at increased risk of tolerance and, subsequently, long-term chronic pain and anxiety [10].

It is also reported that patients with OI are naturally tolerant to opioids and sometimes require higher doses to achieve the same decrease in pain intensity [11]. As a result, neuraxial and regional anesthesia are ideal for these patients. However, there is some debate about the safety of these procedures due to the potential risks of iatrogenic fracture during positioning, bleeding, and spinal cord injury [10,[12], [13], [14]]. Regional nerve blocks are being used more frequently for patients without OI due to their effective pain control, excellent safety profile, and enhanced recovery compared with epidural anesthesia [15,16]. Until now, there have been few reports of the use of regional neuromuscular blockade for patients with OI who are receiving orthopaedic surgery. Given the current paucity of data available, the objective of this current study was to report a series of regional nerve blocks for patients with OI who underwent reconstructive and realignment extremity procedures.

Methods

After organizational ethics approval (institutional review board) was obtained, a retrospective chart review was conducted on all patients with OI undergoing extremity orthopaedic procedures from July 2018 to September 2023. At our institution, OI patients undergoing extremity procedures routinely receive either a peripheral nerve block or a neuraxial block. The only exception to this is on the rare occasion when a parent may refuse a block for their child. In this particular series, none of the patients or families refused a block, likely due to a coordinated recommendation by the surgeon and anesthesia team as well as shared positive experiences among the OI community. Because of this, there was no comparative group of patients in this study who did not receive a block. Patients who received neuraxial blocks (epidural or caudal blocks) were excluded. The decision on whether to give a peripheral block vs neuraxial block was made by the anesthesiologist after discussion with the surgeon. Typically, surgeries involving the bilateral lower extremities received a neuraxial block and were excluded from this cohort.

Chart review was completed for demographics, OI type, procedure type and location, type of block, and any related complications. Opioid use was recorded intraoperatively, in the recovery area (post-anesthesia care unit [Pediatric acute care unit (PACU)]), in the first 24 h postoperatively, and from 24 to 48 h postoperatively. Average pain scores (0–10) were assessed in the PACU, in the first 24 h postoperatively, and 24–48 h postoperatively. A pain score of 0 indicates no pain at all, and a pain score of 10 indicates the worst pain of their life. In our experience, the initial 48 h postoperatively represented the most painful period for OI patients. Accordingly, the pain service followed the patients in this series on a daily basis for the first 48 h postoperatively prior to transitioning pain care to the orthopaedic team. During the first 48 h, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, and diazepam were administered on a scheduled basis, while opioids were ordered as needed. After 48 h, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, and diazepam were transitioned to as-needed use, and the opioid regimen continued on an as-needed basis. Most patients were discharged around 48 h postoperatively, at which point the orthopaedic team provided a short (approximately 5-day) opioid prescription for home use. Descriptive and frequency statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v.27 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Ninety surgical encounters involving 58 patients (34 male) were identified. Demographics can be found in Table 1. The average age at surgery was 11.8 years (range: 2–33 years). Average weight was 27.5 kg (range: 9.1–81.9 kg). Osteogenesis imperfecta type III was the most common type encountered and was seen in 35.6% of patients. Procedures performed are summarized in Table 2. Femoral realignment and rodding (26.6%) were the most common procedures performed (13 primary, 22 revisions), followed by humeral (11 primary, six revisions) and tibial procedures (six primary, 13 revisions). The average length of hospital stay was 4.9 days (range: 1–63 days). Regional nerve blocks performed in this cohort are listed in Table 3. The most common block was the lumbar plexus (36), followed by subgluteal sciatic (14), and supraclavicular (14). Other blocks successfully performed included adductor canal, anterior sciatic, fascia iliaca, femoral, ilioinguinal, lumbar plexus, popliteal sciatic, quadratus lumborum, saphenous, sciatic, axillary, infraclavicular, and interscalene. Twenty-three surgeries (25.6%) had two types of blocks. Indwelling catheter use was 87.7%. Table 4 lists narcotic administration in morphine milligram equivalent per kilogram (MME/kg). Intraoperative average MME/kg was 1.0 (range: 0–3.1). Average MME/kg in the PACU was 0.1 (range: 0–0.4). Average total MME/kg for the first 24 h and from 24 to 48 h were 0.3 (0–1.4) and 0.4 (0–1.7), respectively. In the first 48 h postoperatively, patients received an average of three doses of non-steroidal anti-inflammatories a day and three doses of acetaminophen a day. Of the 90 total surgical encounters, 58 (64.4%) required no narcotics in the PACU and, of these 58, 24 (26.7%) required no narcotics 24 h after surgery. Pain scores in the PACU, the first 24 h, and from 24 to 48 h were 1.2 (0–9, standard deviation [SD] = 1.9), 2.1 (0–8, SD = 1.8), and 2.2 (0–8, SD = 2.0), respectively. No adverse events related to the regional blocks were noted. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest reported cohort of patients with OI undergoing extremity procedures who received regional nerve blocks. There were no major complications. The most common overall and lower extremity block was the lumbar plexus block. The most common upper extremity block was supraclavicular. Nearly 90% of the blocks included an indwelling catheter. The average age of patients in this cohort was 11.8 years, and the average weight was 27.5 kg. Average pain scores in the recovery area, in the 24 h after surgery, and from 24 to 48 h after surgery were 1.2, 2.1, and 2.2, respectively. At our institution, the pain management team categorizes scores of 0–4 as “low pain,” 4 to 7 as “moderate pain,” and 7 to 10 as “severe pain.” All the average pain scores in this cohort were well within the low pain category (Fig. 1). Few other studies have evaluated regional nerve blocks in patients with OI; however, the available evidence suggests that they are safe and effective. Rothschild et al. performed peripheral nerve blocks on 33 of their series of 83 patients with OI. They had no difficult cases or negative outcomes. They reported two postoperative fractures that were unrelated to nerve block placement [17]. Liang et al. reported on 29 peripheral nerve blocks in children with OI and reported no difficulty or failure with their placement. They also reported no iatrogenic fractures or bleeding [18]. A narrative review of the literature by Beethe et al., which included the patients from the Rothschild study, described regional and/or neuraxial techniques in the postoperative period for 161 patients with OI, of whom 44 received regional anesthesia [10]. They found no complications that could be definitively linked to a peripheral block and no negative outcomes.

At our institution, many steps are taken to mitigate the risk of complications related to peripheral nerve blocks in patients with OI. First, OI blocks are performed by a small subset of our anesthesiology staff who have extensive experience performing blocks in this population. Operating room staff, who also have experience working with patients with OI, help move, position, and pad the patient (Fig. 2). A wide area is exposed and prepped for the block (Fig. 3). While performing the block, a team member will also physically hold the patient to ensure that they do not change position. Placement of the regional nerve block catheter is critical, and its location must be discussed with the orthopaedic team to ensure that it is outside the surgical field (Fig. 4). During draping of the surgical field, the catheter site is protected and covered to prevent the adhesive portion of the drapes from attaching to and pulling out the catheter (Fig. 5). An additional benefit of the OI blocks being performed by an experienced subset of our anesthesiology staff is the minimization of the time taken to safely perform the block and catheter placement. This minimal delay in the case start time is a small subset of overall OI time and is well worth the benefits of improved postoperative pain control. The main limitation to the present study is the absence of a comparative control group of patients with OI who did not receive a regional block. Our study design limited the ability to form a meaningful comparison group of OI patients undergoing extremity procedures prior to the routine initiation of regional anesthesia due to the heterogeneity of pain control prior to the routine use of regional anesthesia. Along with the use of regional anesthesia, multimodal pain control including scheduled acetaminophen and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, has also helped to improve postoperative pain control. This case series indicates the safety and efficacy of peripheral nerve blocks with catheters for patients with OI when combined with a multimodal pain regimen, but we cannot definitively make conclusions about the superiority of treatment.

In summary, this case series of patients with OI who received peripheral nerve blocks had no adverse events related to the regional blocks. The patients required minimal amounts of opioids in the postoperative period and were able to maintain low pain levels.
