Qingre Runzao oral liquid and its key compound ophiopogonin D inhibit CXCL9 and block CXCL9/CXCR3 axis to ameliorate asthma by suppressing RAS/ERK pathway
Authors: Lingxue Jiang, Huan Li, Wenping Bao, Aihua Zhang, Shiyu Guo, Jianguo Sun, Dongyun Li, Danxia Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQingre Runzao oral liquid (QRRZ) is a Chinese medicine prescription. Previous studies have confirmed its good therapeutic effect on bronchial asthma (BA), but the mechanisms remain unclear. This study aims to clarify the anti-BA effect and mechanisms of QRRZ, and further reveal the key compounds of QRRZ that ameliorate BA.MethodsFirstly, the BA model mice were used to evaluate the therapeutic effect of QRRZ on BA. Then, RNA-seq analysis was performed to reveal the key targets and mechanisms of QRRZ in ameliorating BA, and the results were verified by RT-PCR and Western Blotting. Molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and CETSA were used to screen the key compounds of QRRZ in ameliorating BA. The potential of key compounds on BA was evaluated in vitro. Finally, the mechanisms were verified, and a rescue experiment was conducted.ResultsQRRZ can ameliorate bronchial pathological changes and airway hyperresponsiveness, alleviate the progression of BA by reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, especially neutrophils. QRRZ significantly inhibits the expression of MUC5AC and Th2-related cytokines IL-6 and IL-13 in BALF solution and upregulates the Th1-related cytokine IL-2. RNA-seq results showed QRRZ mainly blocks CXCL9/CXCR3 axis and inhibits RAS/ERK pathway activation to ameliorate BA, and CXCL9 is the key protein of QRRZ. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation results showed that Ophiopogonin D (Oph D) can bind to CXCR3 (CXCL9 receptor) tightly. CETSA and CO-IP assays confirm that Oph D can bind stably to CXCR3. Then, Oph D exhibited potent anti-BA efficacy both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, results indicated Oph D can down-regulate CXCL9 and block the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis to inhibit RAS/ERK pathway activation in 16HBE cells.ConclusionQRRZ could block the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis to inhibit RAS/ERK pathway activation, reduce neutrophil infiltration in bronchioles, and improve bronchial asthma. Among these, Oph D is the key component of QRRZ in ameliorating BA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03723-1.

Introduction

Bronchial asthma (BA) is a common chronic inflammatory airway disease, accompanied by airway hyperresponsiveness and variable airflow limitation, and its clinical manifestations include recurrent symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, coughing, and chest tightness [1]. The global prevalence rate of asthma is 3.57%, and the estimated overall prevalence rate in China is 4.2%, with the rate increasing every year [2, 3]. Currently, GINA guidelines recommend a personalized, stepwise treatment approach based on asthma severity. For initial therapy (Steps 1–2), as-needed low-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)-formoterol is the preferred reliever and controller (Track 1), or daily low-dose ICS with as-needed SABA as an alternative (Track 2). For moderate to severe persistent asthma, maintenance ICS-long-acting beta2-agonists or ICS-formoterol MART is indicated [4, 5]; however, these medications only address symptoms and do not change the fundamental characteristics of the disease. Obesity is associated with poor asthma control and reduced responsiveness to ICS, particularly in patients with T2-low inflammatory phenotypes. For obese patients, high-dose ICS therapy should be given, but this approach may result in issues such as drug resistance and long-term airway inflammation caused by neutrophil infiltration [6]. Moreover, long-term high-dose ICS may lead to adverse effects such as hypokalemia, hypertension, and glucose metabolism disorders [7–9]. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop safer and more effective anti-BA drugs.

It is well known that inflammatory cell infiltration and airway wall damage are the main causes of BA pathogenesis, and its main pathological features are airway inflammation and airway remodeling. Asthma is the result of the interaction between epithelial cells and immune cells, and is characterized by an imbalance between Th2 and Th1 cells during disease progression. It is generally believed that in the development of asthma, Th1 cells decrease while Th2 cells increase, and Th2 cells are considered a key pathogenic cell subset in asthma. Some studies have confirmed that Th2-related cytokines significantly increase in BALF or blood of bronchial asthma, including IL-4, IL-6, IL-13, etc., and this phenomenon has also been confirmed in the mouse model of asthma induced by Ovalbumin (OVA). For a long time, Th1 cells were believed to play a protective role in asthma, and treatment for asthma was also summarized as a transition from Th2 to Th1. But, more and more evidence indicates the pathogenic role of Th1 cells in asthma [10, 11]. Except for IL-2 and IL-12, CXCR3 is a recognized Th1-related cytokine that plays an important role in asthma. It is reported that CXCR3 and its receptors (CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11) are significantly upregulated in the serum of severe asthma patients, which explains why anti-Th2 therapy is not always effective for asthma patients [12]. It is worth noting that the CXCLx/CXCR3 axis may also be related to the recruitment of neutrophils for infiltration into the pulmonary bronchioles. As a classic inflammatory cell, studies have shown that CXCL9 is a potential inflammatory marker for asthma [13], while another study demonstrated that CXCR3 knockout significantly ameliorated asthma-related inflammation in mice [14]; these results well explain this inference. Furthermore, studies have shown that ICS therapy can regulate the balance of CXCR3 + and CCR4 + cells in BA patients [15, 16]. There are also studies confirming that the CXCR3 antagonist TAK-779 shows satisfactory therapeutic effects on asthma [17]. Overall, these studies suggest that blocking the CXCLx/CXCR3 axis has significant promise in asthma treatment.

Many studies have confirmed that traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has shown satisfactory therapeutic effects in ameliorating asthma, indicating its strong therapeutic potential for asthma. The Qingre Runzao oral liquid (QRRZ) has been used clinically for over 30 years to treat asthma. It is a traditional Chinese herbal formula composed of 13 medicinal ingredients, including Mori folium (Morus alba L. namely Sangye in China, and the dosage is 10 g), Armeniacae semen amarum (Prunus armeniaca L. var. ansu Maxim., namely Kuxingren in China, and the dosage is 6 g), Adenophorae Radix (Adenophora tetraphylla (Thunb.) Fisch., namely Nanshashen in China, and the dosage is 12 g), Ophiopogonis Radix (Ophiopogon japonicus (L.f.) ker-Gawl., namely Maidong in China, and the dosage is 10 g), Phragmitis Rhizoma (Phragmites communis Trin., namely Lugen in China, and the dosage is 15 g), fritillariae thunbergii bulbus (Fritillaria thunbergii Miq., namely Zhebeimu in China, and the dosage is 10 g), coicis semen (Coix lacryma-jobi L.var.mayuen (Roman.) Stapf., namely Yiyiren in China, and the dosage is 15 g), Benincasae semen (Benincasa hispida (Thunb.) Cogn., namely Dongguaren in China, and the dosage is 15 g), Citri reticulatae pericarpium (Citrus reticulata Blanco., namely Chenpi in China, and the dosage is 3 g), Poria (Poria cocos (Schw.) Wolf., namely Fuling in China, and the dosage is 10 g), Pinelliae rhizoma praeparatum (Pinellia ternata (Thunb.) Breit., namely Jingbanxia in China, and the dosage is 10 g), Setariae fructus germinatus (Setaria italica (L.) Beauv., namely Guya in China, and the dosage is 15 g), and hordēi fructus germinatus (Hordeum vulgare L., namely Maiya in China, and the dosage is 15 g). Our previous research confirmed that QRRZ can reduce the levels of IgE and OVA-IgE in serum and inhibit MUC5AC secretion in asthma mice to ameliorate asthma [18, 19], and clinical studies have shown that QRRZ effectively alleviates asthma symptoms in patients [20]. However, the mechanisms of QRRZ and its key compounds for exerting efficacy remain unclear.

In this study, the efficacy of QRRZ in the treatment of BA was evaluated in vivo. Then, the mechanisms of QRRZ in ameliorating asthma were revealed through RNA-seq analysis and a comprehensive validation of the mechanisms. Moreover, key compounds of QRRZ were identified by molecular docking and CETSA assay, and the anti-BA effects of the key compounds were verified in vitro and in vivo. Overall, our findings provide an effective potential therapeutic agent for BA, and also provide the data to support further research and clinical application of QRRZ.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

QRRZ was provided by the Department of Pharmacy, Third Affiliated Hospital of Yunnan University of Chinese Medicine (Lot: KMSZ-241014), and quality control of QRRZ was conducted according to our previous studies (1 mL QRRZ contains 3.94 g crude drugs). House dust mites (D. pteronyssinus, HDM) (Lot: XPB82D3A2.5)was ontained from Greer Labs (North Carolina, America). The standards of dexamethasone (DXM) (Lot: HY-14648), methacholine (Lot: HY-A0083R), ophiopogonin D (Lot: HY-N0515), and hesperidin (Lot: HY-15337) were provided by MCE Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). RIPA Lysis buffer and BCA assay Kit were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). PVDF membranes (0.25 μm) were sourced from Millipore (Bedford, Massachusetts). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), 12% SDS-PAGE gel, and Non-fat goat milk were purchased from Meilun Biotech (Dalian, China). Keratinocyte (Lot: #2101) medium and Penicillin/Steptomycin Solution (Lot: #0513) were purchased from Sciencell Co., Ltd. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Gibco (Brazil). The ELISA assay Kits of IgE (Lot: E-EL-M3107), IL-2 (Lot: E-EL-M0042), IL-6 (Lot: E-EL-M0044), IL-13 (Lot: E-EL-M0727), TNF-α (Lot: E-EL-M3036), and CXCL9 (Lot: E-EL-M3077) were procured from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). All antibodies are listed in Table S1.

Animal experiment procedures and assessment of airway hyper-responsiveness

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Yunnan University of Chinese Medicine [License number: R-062022G105]. A total of 60 five-week-old BALB/c mice, weighing 20 ± 2 g were purchased from Shanghai Yishang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [License number: SCXK(hu)-2022-0011]. All animals were housed in a constant temperature environment at 24 °C with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the China’s national standard GB/T 35,892 − 2018 (Guidelines for Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare) to ensure experimental compliance and animal welfare.

Experiment 1

A total of 30 mice were divided into five groups, i.e., control (CTL), model, low-dose QRRZ (QRRZ-L), high-dose QRRZ (QRRZ-H), and DXM group. Except for the CTL group, all groups received a tracheotomy injection of 3 µg of house dust mite (HDM) on day 1. Starting on day 7, the mice underwent 10 µg of HDM intranasally once daily for 5 consecutive days. The QRRZ groups and the DXM group received treatment throughout the experiment. According to the clinical dosage and body surface area normalization method [19], the dosage of QRRZ-L and QRRZ-H was set at4.32 g/kg/d and 8.64 g/kg/d, respectively. The DXM group received dexamethasone at a dose of 1.56 mg/kg/d. All drugs were administered via oral gavage.

Experiment 2

A total of 30 BALB/c mice were divided into five groups, i.e., control (CTL), model group, low- and high-dose Oph D groups, and the QRRZ-H group (positive control). Except for the CTL mice, all animals were used to establish BA models using the same method as in Experiment 1. After successful modeling, the low- and high-dose Oph D groups were treated with 10 mg/kg/d and 20 mg/kg/d of Oph D, respectively. high-dose QRRZ was selected as the positive control, its dosage were identical to those in Experiment 1. All drugs were administered via oral gavage. After the airway hyperresponsiveness assessment, the mice were euthanized with deep isoflurane (5%) anesthesia, and the lung bronchial tissue was harvested.

Airway hyper-responsiveness assessment

Airway hyper-responsiveness was assessed non-invasively by whole-body plethysmography (WBP), and enhanced pause (Penh) served as the indicator. Mice were placed in a whole-body plethysmograph chamber (WBP-4 M, TOW-INT, China) and challenged with aerosolized methacholine at different concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 mg/mL) for 1 min. Changes in Penh values were measured at 10-second intervals for 5 min after each challenge. For each methacholine concentration, the average of the last 30 Penh values was taken as the Penh value of the mouse under the current concentration.

Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) analysis

After the final drug administration, the thoracic cavities of mice were opened via the abdomen, and a V-shaped incision was made on the main bronchus after clamping the right bronchus. Then, a syringe was inserted into the left bronchus to inject 0.3 mL of PBS buffer. After three repeated aspirations, the BALF solution was collected, with a recovery rate of over 70% considered acceptable. The collected BALF solution was centrifuged at 4 °C and 2000 rpm for 5 min, then divided and stored in a -80 °C refrigerator. Ten microliters of the lower cell suspension from the BALF solution were taken for cell counting under an inverted microscope to determine the total cell count in the suspension. Additionally, 20 µL of the cell suspension was taken and stained according to the Wright-Giemsa staining instructions to count the number of white blood cells, monocytes, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils in the BALF solution.

ELISA assay

The levels of related proteins in serum or BALF solution were detected by ELISA assay, including IL-2, IL-6, IL-13, TNF-α, IgE, and CXCL9. The ELISA assay was carried out in accordance with the instructions provided by the reagent manufacturer. Upon completion, the OD of each well was measured at 450 nm. A standard curve was generated using a four-parameter logistic fit to calculate the target protein concentrations in each group.

Histopathological examination and immunohistochemical staining

Mouse bronchial and pulmonary bronchiolar tissues were collected for histopathological examination and immunohistochemical staining. First, the tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices. Referring to previous studies [21], H&E and MASSON staining were performed on bronchiolar sections, and H&E and PAS staining were performed on bronchial sections. In addition, immunohistochemical staining was used to detect the expression of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) in bronchi and myeloperoxidase (MPO) in bronchioles. All pathological examinations and staining were commissioned to Shanghai Ruiyu Co Ltd.

RNA-seq analysis

The bronchioles of mice from each group were collected, flushed with PBS, and then TRIzol lysis solution was added to collect a total RNA suspension for RNA-seq analysis, which was entrusted to Shanghai OE Biotechnology Company. The data were processed as follows: Firstly, PCA analysis was performed to observe the transcriptional profile changes of samples in each group. Then, differential analysis was carried out using the Limma package, and differentially expressed genes were filtered with the criteria of Log|FC|>1, P < 0.05, and FDR < 0.05. Venn analysis was used to identify potential targets of QRRZ in ameliorating BA, with a focus on genes reversed in the treated group compared to the model group. Subsequently, these potential targets were imported into the STRING database for PPI analysis, and the PPI network was constructed using CytoScape3.9.2 software. The Cutohubba plugin was employed to perform topological analysis on the PPI network and screen out core targets. Finally, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using the Metascape database, and all results were visualized using the ggplot2 package in R.

Western blotting

The total protein content was extracted from cell and tissue samples using RIPA lysis buffer mixed with PMSF buffer at 4℃, protein concentration was detected through the BCA method, and RIPA lysis buffer was used to normalize the total protein concentration in each group. Then, the proteins were added with loading buffer and denatured at high temperature for 10 min. The proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE before being transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ on the membranes. Following primary incubation, the membrane was washed with TBST buffer, and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied to the membranes and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the ECL solution was applied evenly to the membranes for signal detection through chemiluminescence imaging. Signal quantification was performed using ImageJ software. Results were measured using ImageJ software, and GAPDH was used as an internal reference.

RT-qPCR assay

The total RNA content was extracted from tissue samples using CellAmp™ Direct RNA Prep Kit, and RNA purity was detected by measuring the OD260/OD280 ratio using a Nano Drop spectrophotometer. Then, cDNA was synthesized, and reverse transcription was performed as stated by PrimeScript RT Master Mix, and RT-qPCR assay was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ and commercial primers according to the manual of the reagent kit. All specific primers were obtained from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and primer sequences are listed in Supplement Table S2. The results of mRNA expression level were normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCT method to calculate the relative gene expression.

UPLC-Q/TOF-MS analysis of QRRZ

A precisely measured 1 mL QRRZ was put into an EP tube, mixed with 1 mL of 50% ethanol, and ultrasonicated for 30 min to obtain a test solution at a concentration of 1.97 g/mL. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane before injection. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system coupled with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the injection volume was 1 µL. The detection wavelength was set at 280 nm. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0 min, 5% A; 10 min, 12% A; 23 min, 30% A; 30 min, 72% A; 35 min, 75% A; and 38–40 min, 95% A. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out using an AB SCIEX X500B QTOF high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in both positive and negative ion modes. The parameters for the positive ion mode were as follows: curtain gas, 35 psi; Ion Source Gas 1 and 2, 40 psi; source temperature, 500 °C; ionspray voltage, 5000 V; declustering potential, 70 V; collision energy, 5 V; and scan range, m/z 80–1500. For the negative ion mode, the settings were: curtain gas, 35 psi; Gas 1 and 2, 40 psi; source temperature, 450 °C; ionspray voltage, -4500 V; declustering potential, -70 V; collision energy, -5 V; and scan range, m/z 80–1500. Data acquisition and processing were performed using SCIEX OS. Structural identification of the chromatographic peaks was achieved by matching their accurate mass and MS/MS fragmentation patterns with an in-house database of reported chemical components.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamic simulation

The human CXCR3 protein structure (PDB ID: 8K2W) was obtained from PDB database, and CXCR3 was preprocessed, such as removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens and so on. Our previous research identified 21 major compounds of QRRZ by UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Then, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the affinity between these compounds and CXCR3 using AutoDock 1.5.7 software. Results were ranked by binding energy to identify key compounds of QRRZ. Finally, PyMOL software was employed to display the docking conformations of key compounds with CXCR3. In addition, molecular dynamics simulation was employed to assess their binding stability using the Desmond program of Schrödinger software. NVT and NPT couplings were used to equilibrate system, and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was run at 300 K and 1.013 bar, with a 30-femtoseconds time step. Finally, the binding of key compounds to CXCR3 was analyzed by calculating RMSD, RMSF, and hydrogen bond number.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Bronchiolar tissue lysates were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer and divided into two equal portions. One portion was incubated with 50 µM Ophiopogonin D or Hesperidin for 1 h, and the other with an equal volume of DMSO. Samples were then divided into six equal parts and heated for 10 min at different temperatures (35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65 °C). Then, samples were centrifuged at 17,400 g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Finally, CXCR3 expression level in supernatant was detected by Western blotting, and the Boltzmann sigmoidal function was used to fit melting curve of CXCR3.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

16HBE cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes and allowed to adhere overnight. Following adhesion, cells were treated with or without 50 µM Oph D for 2 h, and subsequently stimulated with recombinant CXCL9 (100 ng/mL) for 30 min. After stimulation, the medium was discarded, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Cells were then lysed on ice for 30 min using IP lysis buffer supplemented with a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. A portion of each lysate was reserved as input control, while the remaining samples were pre-cleared with Protein A/G agarose beads for 1 h at 4 °C to minimize non-specific binding. The pre-cleared lysates were incubated overnight with either anti-CXCR3 antibody or rabbit IgG isotype control at 4 °C with gentle rotation, followed by incubation with fresh Protein A/G agarose beads for an additional 4 h. The immunocomplexes were collected by centrifugation and washed four times with ice-cold IP wash buffer. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2x SDS sample loading buffer for 5 min and subsequently analyzed by Western blotting to assess the CXCL9 and CXCR3 interaction. Input samples were also analyzed to confirm equal protein loading and expression levels across groups.

Cell culture and CCK-8 assay

Human bronchial epithelial cell line 16HBE (Lot: ARIS241205) was procured from the Shanghai Aoruishai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., which has been authenticated using STR profiling. 16HBE cells were cultured in KM medium with supplement with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in an incubator with an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

For CCK-8 assay, 16HBE cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h, and 16HBE cells were stimulated with 800U/mL HDM extract to establish an asthma cell model. Then, cells were treated with different concentrations of Ophiopogonin D (Oph D) or Hesperidin (Hesp) for 24 h. The positive control group was treated with 50µM DXM. Finally, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h in a cell culture incubator. Absorbance at a wavelength of 490 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software. The results were expressed as means ± SEM. The Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare multiple groups, and Dunnett’s method was used for post hoc test. Statistical significance was denoted by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

QRRZ significantly alleviates BA symptoms

The therapeutic effect of QRRZ was evaluated in HDM-induced allergic airway inflammation model (Fig. 1A). Airway hyper-responsiveness (AHR) is a hallmark of asthma, which was evaluated by measuring Penh responses in conscious mice following methacholine challenge. Results show that compared with the control group, HDM-sensitized mice exhibited significantly elevated Penh values, indicating increased AHR; this elevation was significantly attenuated by QRRZ and DXM treatment (Fig. 1B). H&E staining of bronchioles showed that, compared with the control group, the model group exhibited extensive inflammatory cell infiltration around the alveolar septa and bronchioles, while the QRRZ treatment group showed significant alleviation of pulmonary lesions, and the mean linear intercept (MLI) of lung tissue increased (Fig. 1C and Fig S1A). MASSON staining of bronchioles showed that the model group exhibited thickened alveolar septa and extensive fibrotic lesions around the alveolar septa and bronchioles. In contrast, the QRRZ treatment group showed reduced alveolar septal thickness and markedly attenuated fibrosis (Fig. 1D).

Additionally, H&E staining of bronchi showed a disorganized arrangement of columnar ciliated epithelium and substantial epithelial shedding in the BA model group. In the QRRZ treatment group, bronchial epithelium lesions were significantly mitigated, with only a small number of inflammatory cells infiltrating the submucosal layer (Fig. 1E). PAS staining of the bronchi revealed scattered PAS-positive signals in the bronchial epithelial cells of the model group, indicating an increase in mucus granules in the bronchial epithelial cells. No PAS-positive signals were observed in the QRRZ treatment group (Fig. 1F and Fig S1B). Finally, ZO-1 expression levels were detected through IHC staining, and we confirmed the protective effect of QRRZ on bronchial epithelial barrier damage (Fig. 1G). The results showed that in the model group and the low-dose QRRZ group, the bronchial epithelial cells were loosely arranged, and the ZO-1 signal at the apical end of the epithelial cells was reduced (red arrows). In the high-dose QRRZ group, the bronchial epithelial cells were tightly arranged, and continuous positive signals in dotted lines were observed at the junctions (green arrows). In summary, these results demonstrate that QRRZ attenuates key pathological features of HDM-induced allergic airway inflammation in vivo.

QRRZ alleviates airway inflammation in BA mice

The effects of QRRZ on BA-related inflammation were assessed by measuring serum IgE levels. The results showed that, compared with the control group, serum IgE levels were significantly elevated in the model group. Both QRRZ and DXM treatment significantly reduced IgE levels (Fig. 2A). MUC5AC is the predominant mucin in asthma pathogenesis and a major contributor to airway obstruction; in this study, our results further showed that QRRZ markedly decreased MUC5AC concentration in BALF (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the results showed that the levels of the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α in BALF were significantly increased in the model group and significantly reduced after QRRZ treatment (Fig. 2C). Additionally, we assessed the levels of the Th1-related cytokine IL-2, and the results showed that QRRZ treatment significantly upregulated IL-2 levels compared to the model group (Fig. 2D). We then further assessed the levels of Th2 cytokines IL-13 and IL-6 in BALF. The results showed that IL-13 and IL-6 levels were significantly elevated in BALF from the model group, and both IL-13 and IL-6 levels decreased after QRRZ treatment (Fig. 2E-F). These results indicate that QRRZ treatment could significantly suppress BA inflammation.

QRRZ alleviates airway inflammation by reducing neutrophil infiltration

An analysis of the cell count in the BALF was conducted, including the total cell count, lymphocytes, neutrophils, white blood cells, monocytes, and eosinophils. The results showed that QRRZ treatment significantly reduced the total cell count (Fig. 3A), lymphocytes (Fig. 3B), neutrophils (Fig. 3C), leukocytes (Fig. 3D), monocytes (Fig. 3E), and eosinophils (Fig. 3F) in BALF. Among these, the inhibitory effect of QRRZ on neutrophils was the most pronounced, consistent with the results of H&E staining. Additionally, we further assessed the impact of QRRZ on neutrophil infiltration by detecting MPO levels in bronchioles through IHC staining. The results showed that the model group exhibited extensive MPO-positive neutrophil infiltration around the alveolar septa and bronchioles, while only a few scattered neutrophil infiltrations were observed in the lungs of QRRZ-L group mice, and no differences were noted in the lungs of QRRZ-H group (Fig. 3G). In summary, the results show that QRRZ significantly reduces inflammatory cell infiltration in the bronchi to alleviate airway inflammation.

Identification of the compounds of QRRZ using UPLC-MS/MS

We performed qualitative analysis of the chemical constituents of QRRZ by UPLC-MS/MS. By consulting relevant literature and comparing with databases, the main compounds in QRRZ were identified. Ultimately, 21 major chemical compounds of QRRZ were successfully identified from the TIC chromatogram (Fig. 4), and Table 1 provides detailed information on these 21 compounds.

RNA-seq analysis reveals CXCL9 as a core target for QRRZ in alleviating BA

RNA-seq analysis revealed the potential mechanism by which QRRZ mitigates BA. PCA results showed that, compared with the control group, the transcription profile of the bronchioles in the BA model group and the QRRZ treatment group were significantly changed (Fig. 5A). Differential analysis results showed that 205 targets were downregulated in the BA group and upregulated after QRRZ treatment (Fig. 5B). Similarly, 293 targets were upregulated in the BA group and downregulated after QRRZ treatment (Fig. 5C). These 498 targets represent the potential targets for QRRZ to alleviate BA (Fig. 5D). PPI network analysis showed that Tnf, Pecam1, Gapdh, Cldn5, and Cxcl9 were key nodes in the network (Fig. 5E), and topological analysis further confirmed this (Fig. 5F). Subsequently, the GeneMANIA database was used to assess the targets and molecular functions associated with these key nodes. The results showed that their primary functions include cellular response to chemokine, response to chemokine, and neutrophil migration, with Ccl22, Ccl20, and Cxcl9 occupying central positions in the network (Fig. 5G).

Then, RT-qPCR results were used to detect the expression levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CCL20, and CCL22 in bronchioles. The results showed that QRRZ treatment inhibited the expression of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, particularly CXCL9 (Fig. 6A-C). Additionally, the expression levels of CCL20 and CCL22 were significantly downregulated in the QRRZ treatment group (Fig. 6D-E). Furthermore, the expression level of CXCL9 was evaluated at the protein level through ELISA assay, and the results again confirmed the inhibitory effect of QRRZ on CXCL9 (Fig. 6F). Finally, Western blotting confirmed that QRRZ did not alter CXCR3 levels but significantly suppressed CXCL9 induction in BA (Fig. 6G-H). In summary, CXCL9 is the core target of QRRZ in alleviating BA, and QRRZ may exerts its effects by inhibiting the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis.

QRRZ inhibits CXCL9 expression to alleviate BA by inhibiting RAS/ERK axis

Through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, the potential pathways associated with the targets of QRRZ in alleviating BA were elucidated. The results of GO enrichment analysis indicated that the potential targets were primarily associated with biological processes such as positive regulation of cell migration, blood vessel morphogenesis, and RAS protein signal transduction. These targets were closely related to cellular components such as the external side of plasma membrane, membrane raft, and G protein-coupled receptor complex. Additionally, they were involved in molecular functions such as adrenomedullin receptor activity, growth factor binding, and cytokine binding (Fig. 7A). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the potential targets of QRRZ in combating BA were primarily enriched in the RAS and MAPK pathways, and were also closely related to pathways such as asthma, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation (Fig. 7B). Key targets such as CXCL9 were enriched in these KEGG pathways (Fig. 7C). Finally, Western blotting of RAS and MAPK pathway-related proteins showed that QRRZ significantly inhibited the expression of RAS (Fig. 7D-E). For the MAPK pathway, QRRZ mainly inhibited the phosphorylation of ERK, with no significant effect on the phosphorylation levels of P38 (Fig. 7F-H). These findings indicate that QRRZ potentially exerts its anti-BA effect by inhibiting the activation of the RAS/ERK pathway.

Oph D is the key compound of QRRZ in alleviating BA

PPI analysis revealed that CXCL9 is the core target of QRRZ in combating BA, and experimental results confirmed that QRRZ can inhibit the expression of CXCL9 without affecting CXCR3. Based on this, we speculated that QRRZ can alleviate BA symptoms by inhibiting CXCL9 expression and blocking the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis. To explore this underlying mechanism, molecular docking was performed with CXCR3 as the receptor. The results showed that Oph D, deacetyl ophiopojaponin A, and hesperidin had the highest binding energies (Table S3). Oph D interacts with CXCR3 primarily through hydrogen bonding, and molecular dynamics simulation indicated that Ophiopogonin D binds to CXCR3 stably over a 100 ns simulation period (Fig. 8A-B and Fig. S2A-B). The simulation results further revealed that Ophiopogonin D interacts with CXCR3 through amino acid residues such as TYR-205, ARG-212, and TYR-316 (Fig. S2C-D). Notably, the results showed that Deacetyl ophiopojaponin A binds to CXCR3 but in an unstable manner (Fig. 8C-D and Fig. S3A-B), with the only binding site being TYR-60 (Fig. S3C-D). Finally, Hesperidin also exhibited strong binding affinity and stability with CXCR3 (Fig. 8E-F and Fig. S4A-B). Hesperidin primarily binds to CXCR3 through amino acid residues such as TYR-60, CYS-203, TYR-205, and SER-312 (Fig. S4C-D). These results suggest that Oph D and Hesperidin are potential antagonists of CXCR3. Therefore, further binding studies were conducted through CETSA, which showed that after treatment with Oph D, the thermal stability of CXCR3 protein significantly increased, and the protein degradation curve shifted to the right (Fig. 8G-H); and the hesperidin also demonstrated good affinity with CXCR3 (Fig. 8I-J). Furthermore, Co-IP results confirmed that Oph D binds to CXCR3 and blocks the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis (Fig. S5). Overall, these results indicate that Oph D exhibits the highest affinity for CXCR3, suggesting that it may represent the key compound responsible for the anti-BA effects of QRRZ.

Oph D ameliorates BA through inhibiting CXCL9 expression and RAS/ERK pathway activation

The 16BHE cells were used to establish a BA cell model induced by HDM, and DXM was used as a positive control to preliminarily evaluate the anti-BA potential of Oph D and Hesperidin in vitro. The results showed that compared to the control group, the cell proliferation in the HDM-induced BA model group was significantly inhibited. Treatment with Oph D significantly promoted the proliferation of 16BHE cells, and its effect was superior to that of hesperidin (Fig. 9A-B). Subsequently, ELISA assay results showed that Oph D therapy significantly down-regulate expression level of MUC5AC, IL-6, and IL-13, and significantly promotes IL-2 expression (Fig. 9C-F). Next, Western blotting results indicated that Oph D significantly inhibited the expression of CXCL9, with no noticeable effect on CXCR3 (Fig. 9G-I). The Oph D also inhibited the activation of the RAS/ERK pathway, consistent with the QRRZ results (Fig. 9J-K). Subsequently, the anti-BA effect of Oph D was further evaluated in vivo, Compared with the BA model group, Oph D treatment significantly reduced Penh values, indicating inhibition of AHR, with efficacy comparable to that of QRRZ (Fig. 10A). ELISA further revealed that Oph D significantly lowered serum IgE levels (Fig. 10B), and Oph D significantly reduced the levels of MUC5AC, TNF-α, IL-13, and IL-6 in BALF while upregulating IL-2 expression (Fig. 10C–G). Histologically, H&E staining showed that Oph D treatment markedly attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration around the bronchioles, and increased the MLI of lung tissue (Fig. 10H and Fig S6A). PAS staining showed compared with the control group, PAS positive signals were scattered in the bronchial epithelial cells of the model group, indicative of increased mucus secretion, and this phenomenon was significantly reversed following Oph D treatment (Fig. 10I and Fig. S6B). Finally, BALF differential cell counting demonstrated that Oph D treatment significantly reduced the total cell count (Fig. S6C), lymphocytes (Fig. S6D), neutrophils (Fig. S6E), leukocytes (Fig. S6F), monocytes (Fig. S6G) and eosinophils (Fig. S6H) in BALF. Collectively, these results in vivo demonstrate that Oph D effectively suppresses airway inflammation and ameliorates the key pathological features of BA. Notably, the anti-BA effects of Oph D were on a par with those of high-dose QRRZ.

Discussion

BA is a common chronic inflammatory respiratory disease that affects more than 300 million people worldwide, with the incidence rate increasing year by year [22, 23]. Currently, ICS and/or LABA are the first-line treatment options for BA [4], but patients receiving low-dose ICS treatment may still experience symptoms such as persistent coughing, wheezing, and chest tightness. Additionally, low-dose ICS treatment may exacerbate obesity, which in turn worsens asthma. When BA is poorly controlled, it is often necessary to increase the ICS dosage, but this inevitably leads to adverse effects such as hypokalemia, hypertension, and glucose disorders, as well as poor quality of life and treatment compliance in patients [6, 9, 24]. QRRZ is composed of 13 traditional Chinese herbs, and over 30 years of clinical application have demonstrated its good efficacy in treating asthma [20]. This study comprehensively evaluated the efficacy of QRRZ in treating BA in vivo, including reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, alleviating pulmonary inflammatory responses, and protecting the bronchial barrier. In addition, RNA-seq analysis revealed that CXCL9 is the core target of QRRZ in treating BA, and QRRZ exerts its anti-BA effects by inhibiting the activation of the RAS/ERK pathway through blocking the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis. Excitingly, this study also identified Oph D as one of the key component of QRRZ for alleviating BA.

Chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 9 (CXCL9), also known as monokine induced by interferon-gamma (MIG), is mainly produced by airway epithelial cells stimulated by IFN-γ during asthma progression. It’s been shown that CXCL9 is remarkably higher in asthma patients, making it a good T1 inflammatory biomarker for BA progression [12, 25, 26]. CXCR3 is the specific receptor for CXCL9, and it has been confirmed to be involved in BA progression. The results indicate that blocking CXCR3 signal transduction exhibits a potent therapeutic effect in BA treatment [27]. Another study showed that CXCR3 KO mice exhibited lower airway hyperresponsiveness and less mucus secretion in response to OVA-induced asthma, suggesting that CXCR3 is a potential therapeutic target for BA [28]. In addition, some researches have indicated that the CXCR3 antagonist AMG487 can block the CXCLx/CXCR3 axis, inhibiting airway remodeling and airway inflammation, and exhibiting good anti-BA effects. Other studies have also demonstrated that another CXCR3 antagonist, TAK779, can effectively relieve BA symptoms [17]. Unfortunately, there are currently no CXCR3 antagonists available for the treatment of BA in clinical practice, possibly due to concerns about the pharmacological properties and safety of existing antagonists. Therefore, developing potential CXCR3 antagonists from traditional Chinese medicine for the treatment of BA is may an effective and innovative treatment strategy.

This study demonstrates that QRRZ ameliorates asthma by blocking CXCL9/CXCR3 signaling, and identifies Oph D as the key component for this effect. This study is the first to report the therapeutic potential of Oph D on BA and further reveals that it can inhibit CXCL9 expression and block the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis to attenuate HDM-induced cellular damage in 16HBE cells by inhibiting the activation of the RAS/ERK pathway. Besides Oph D, our study also reported that hesperidin is the key compound of QRRZ; it has been confirmed to have good anti-BA effects. Study confirmed that hesperidin can significantly inhibit the number of inflammatory cells in BALF of asthmatic mice, reduce Th2 cytokine levels and serum OVA-IgE levels, and alleviate airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammatory cell infiltration, and excessive mucus secretion [29]. Another study pointed out that Hesperidin is a potential Th2 cytokine antagonist, exerting anti-BA effects by inhibiting Th2 cytokines and granulocyte chemokines [30]. Unfortunately, only one study has indirectly reported the effects of Oph D on BA. Its results indicate that Oph D is the primary component of LPP (a Korean herb), and inhalation of LPP can reduce airway hyperresponsiveness and pulmonary inflammatory responses [31]; result indirectly suggest the anti-BA potential of Oph D; however, there are no direct reports on the therapeutic effects of Oph D on BA. It is well known that the RAS/ERK pathway, as a common upstream pathway, has been extensively studied in BA, and thus will not be further elaborated on in this paper. Interestingly, in addition to binding to CXCR3 to activate the RAS/ERK pathway, CXCL9 may also exacerbate the progression of BA by recruiting neutrophilic infiltration. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental verification.

This study demonstrates that QRRZ ameliorates asthma, and for the first time, reports the critical role of the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. (1) Further studies using additional animal models of asthma are warranted to fully evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of QRRZ; moreover, the inclusion of invasive lung function measurement would be necessary. (2) Studies with an expanded sample size are warranted to validate the robustness of these findings. (3) In addition to CETSA and Co-IP assays, more experimental evidence, such as from SPR, BLI, or MST, is required to confirm the antagonistic interaction between CXCR3 and Oph D. (4) Given that Oph D is a macromolecular polar compound, its bioavailability in vivo may be suboptimal; therefore, further optimization of its administration route, such as via aerosol formulations, is needed. Overall, we have demonstrated that QRRZ exerts a therapeutic effect on BA by blocking the CXCL9/CXCR3 axis to suppress the RAS/ERK pathway for the first time. We have also revealed that Oph D is the key compound of QRRZ and shows good therapeutic potential against BA. Our findings provide support for the further clinical application of QRRZ and pave the way for its future application.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates for the first time that QRRZ alleviates asthma by blocking CXCL9/CXCR3 signal transduction, thereby inhibiting the activation of the RAS/ERK pathway. Furthermore, Oph D was identified as the key compound responsible for this anti-asthmatic effect (Fig. 12).
