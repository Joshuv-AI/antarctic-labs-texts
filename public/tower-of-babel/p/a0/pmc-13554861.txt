Recurrent Calcified Coronary Nodules
Authors: Rita-Maria Abdo, Katherine Chiswell, Sarah Fairley, Bharat Khialani, George Touma, Bernard Wong, Scott Harding, Cuneyt Ada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcified coronary nodules (CCNs) are a distinct and under-recognized subset of coronary artery calcification, characterized by irregular calcium deposits. Their unique morphology creates considerable technical challenges during percutaneous coronary intervention, contributing to an elevated risk of in-stent restenosis and stent failure, often due to eruptive nodular reprotrusion through stent struts. Although various calcium modification techniques have been proposed, the optimal management strategy for recurrent CCNs remains unclear. Here we present 3 cases of recurrent CCNs in the right coronary artery, each demonstrating different procedural strategies and outcomes.Key words: cardiovascular disease, percutaneous coronary intervention, acute coronary syndrome, coronary angiography

Three Cases of CCN Recurrence Despite Distinct Hybrid Calcium Modification Strategies

Calcified coronary nodules (CCNs) are an under-recognized but important cause of acute coronary syndrome, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) failure, and in-stent restenosis (ISR).1 CCNs are broadly classified as either eruptive or noneruptive subtypes. Eruptive CCNs are biologically active and characterized by an irregular surface with disruption of the fibrous cap with or without overlying thrombus and may cause acute coronary syndromes. Noneruptive CCNs are more stable and have a smooth surface with an intact fibrous cap.

Accurate diagnosis requires intracoronary imaging, as angiography alone often underestimates their prevalence. On intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), CCNs appear as protruding eccentric, hyperechoic calcified lesions with acoustic shadowing.2 Although noneruptive CCNs have a smooth and convex luminal surface, eruptive CCNs have an irregular surface with or without overlying thrombus. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) also enables differentiation by detecting fibrous cap disruption and superficial thrombus.3 In addition, intracoronary imaging plays an important role in assessing stent expansion during PCI, particularly given the high risk of ISR.

Eruptive CCNs are associated with better immediate outcomes, with larger minimal lumen areas, but worse long-term outcomes given higher rates of target lesion failure when compared with noneruptive nodules. These poorer long-term outcomes are thought to be due to reprotrusion of the eruptive nodular calcium through the stent leading to ISR. Reprotrusion through stent struts carries a worse prognosis than other mechanisms of ISR.4 In this case series, we describe 3 patients with recurrent CCNs, each highlighting the therapeutic challenges posed by this unique lesion subset.

Case 1

A 73-year-old man presented with angina pectoris. Computed tomography coronary angiography revealed triple-vessel disease with a calcium score of 4,712. Subsequent coronary angiography demonstrated multivessel calcific disease, with severe stenoses in the proximal, mid, and distal right coronary artery (RCA), as well as the right posterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, and left anterior descending artery.

Multiple CCNs were identified along the RCA on angiography appearing as step-like lesions (Figure 1A). IVUS confirmed sequential eruptive calcific nodules with eccentric calcific protrusions into the lumen (Figure 1B). Given the long segment of sequential calcific lesions, orbital atherectomy was used at both low and high speed, with antegrade and retrograde runs until satisfactory calcium modification was achieved. Two long drug-eluting stents (DESs) were successfully deployed with good apposition and lumen expansion on angiography and IVUS (Figure 1C).

At 6-month follow-up, repeat coronary angiography demonstrated recurrence of the mid-RCA CCN (Figure 1D). IVUS confirmed eruptive nodular reprotrusion with an irregular leading edge of calcium protruding through stent struts (Figure 1E). The lesion was treated with high-pressure noncompliant (NC) balloon expansion followed by repeat stenting (Figure 1F).

Case 2

A 69-year-old man with dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension presented with angina. Coronary angiography revealed a calcific chronic total occlusion (CTO) of the mid-RCA. Antegrade wire escalation was undertaken to cross the CTO. The lesion was predilated with sequential semicompliant and NC balloon inflation up to 3.0 mm, however extensive calcification precluded effective lesion preparation. Rotational atherectomy was therefore performed using a 1.5-mm burr, targeting proximal nodular calcium. Further modification was performed with 2.75 and 3.0 NC balloons, followed by deployment of overlapping DESs (2.25 × 12, 2.75 × 23, 3.0 × 33, 3.0 × 33, and 3.5 × 23 mm) from the right posterior descending artery to the RCA ostium. Postdilatation was performed with 3.0-, 3.5-, and 4.0-mm NC balloons at high pressure in a distal-to-proximal sequence. Final IVUS demonstrated adequate luminal expansion and stent apposition (Figures 2A to 2D).

Repeat coronary angiogram was performed 2 years later in the setting of transcatheter aortic valve replacement planning. This demonstrated recurrent calcific nodule with hinge-point narrowing and nodular reprotrusion in the mid-RCA, as well as neoatherosclerosis at the distal RCA, despite previously adequate stent sizing (Figure 2E). The distal RCA lesion was predilated with a 3.5 NC balloon at high pressure and treated with a 3.0 × 20 mm drug-coated balloon. The mid-RCA lesion was predilated with a 3.75 NC balloon with good expansion, followed by implantation of a 3.5 × 20 mm DES.

Case 3

A 69-year-old woman presented with an inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by profound hypotension requiring inotropic and vasopressor support. Coronary angiography demonstrated severe calcification of the RCA with an acute 100% thrombotic occlusion of the mid-distal segment. Lesion crossing was challenging given marked vessel tortuosity and unfavorable anatomy. With guide extension support, the lesion was successfully crossed, and sequential predilatation was performed with 1.0 × 10 mm to 3.0 × 15 mm NC balloons (up to 20 atm).

Given the heavy calcium burden and suboptimal lesion preparation, intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) was performed using a 3.5 × 12 mm balloon (80 pulses), achieving satisfactory calcium fracture on IVUS assessment. Overlapping DESs (3.5 × 20, 3.5 × 48, and 3.5 × 20 mm) were implanted and postdilated with a 4.0 × 12 mm NC balloon (14-16 atm), achieving a satisfactory angiographic result.

One year later, the patient re-presented with a further inferior STEMI while undergoing work-up for moderate to severe aortic stenosis and consideration of surgical revascularization. Coronary angiography revealed severe, sequential focal lesions in the mid and mid-distal RCA. The lesions were predilated with a NC balloon to facilitate passage of the OCT catheter. OCT demonstrated neoatherosclerosis at the distal and mid-distal vessel, and focal mid-RCA ISR characterized by eruptive calcific nodular reprotrusion with associated thrombus and stent fracture (Figure 3).

The mid-RCA eruptive calcific nodule was predilated with a 3.5 × 15 mm NC balloon and was treated with IVL (3.5 × 12 mm, 40 pulses), followed by further modification with a 3.5 × 10 mm cutting balloon. A DES was implanted to cover the fractured stent segment. The mid-distal and distal lesions were treated with a 3.5 × 40 mm drug-coated balloon. Final OCT and angiography confirmed good stent expansion and satisfactory result.

Discussion

CCNs are a distinct form of coronary calcification that typically occur in segments with hinge motion, left main involvement, or severe calcification and tortuosity.5,6 Once a nodule protrudes, recurrent disruption and reprotrusion through stent struts can occur, with or without stent fracture, and may precipitate restenosis and thrombotic events.5 Recognition of this distinct pathophysiology is crucial, as it differentiates CCN-related ISR from other mechanisms and requires tailored treatment strategies. In all 3 of the cases presented, eruptive nodular calcific reprotrusion was an important contributor to ISR, although additional mechanisms, including neoatherosclerosis, thrombus, and stent fracture, coexisted in some lesions.

Despite a shared mechanism of eruptive nodular reprotrusion, the 3 cases highlight important differences in patterns of recurrence. Case 1 involved long-segment nodular disease treated with orbital atherectomy and stenting, with early recurrence at 6 months, suggesting that extensive nodular burden may predispose to rapid reprotrusion despite adequate initial expansion. In contrast, case 2 represented a CTO with focal nodular calcium treated with rotational atherectomy, with a longer time to recurrence (2 years) and a mixed mechanism including both nodular reprotrusion and neoatherosclerosis. Case 3 differed further, presenting initially as an acute thrombotic event in which IVL was used for calcium modification. Recurrence occurred at 1 year and was associated not only with nodular reprotrusion but also with stent fracture and thrombus formation, representing a more complex failure mode.

Our cases highlight several important procedural principles. Reprotrusion is typically driven by eruptive nodules and may occur with or without associated thrombus, stent deformation, or fracture. Notably, recurrence was observed despite adequate lesion preparation, intravascular imaging guidance, and the use of different calcium modification strategies. This suggests that while these techniques are effective for acute lesion preparation, they do not eliminate the risk of subsequent nodular reprotrusion. In all 3 cases, high-pressure NC balloon angioplasty was effective in treating nodular reprotrusion. However, more complex recurrence patterns required adjunctive modification strategies, as demonstrated in case 3, where IVL was necessary in the setting of stent fracture and heavy calcific burden. Finally, intravascular imaging with IVUS or OCT is essential for both the diagnosis and management of CCNs. Imaging enables accurate differentiation between eruptive and noneruptive nodules, confirms the mechanism of ISR, and ensures adequate lesion preparation and stent expansion. In the absence of intracoronary imaging, CCNs may be underdiagnosed or misclassified as conventional calcific lesions, leading to suboptimal treatment.

In summary, these cases highlight several important observations: 1) ISR due to eruptive CCNs represents a distinct and recurrent clinical problem; 2) reprotrusion through stent struts, rather than neointimal proliferation, is the principal mechanism of failure in this series; 3) NC balloon angioplasty was effective for eruptive nodule recurrence, where hybrid calcium modification strategies were used on index PCI; and 4) intracoronary imaging should be considered routine in suspected CCN lesions. While derived from a small case series, these principles may help inform procedural planning and the design of larger clinical trials aimed at guiding optimal treatment approaches in this challenging lesion subset.
