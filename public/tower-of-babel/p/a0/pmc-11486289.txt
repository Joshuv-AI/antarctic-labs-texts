Nanoscale Radiotheranostics for Cancer Treatment: From Bench to Bedside
Authors: Xiaodan Jiao, Hao Hong, Weibo Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn recent years, the application of radionuclides‐containing nanomaterials in cancer treatment has garnered widespread attention. The diversity of nanomaterials allows researchers to selectively combine them with appropriate radionuclides for biomedical purposes, addressing challenges faced by peptides, small molecules, or antibodies used for radionuclide labeling. However, with advantages come challenges, and nanoradionuclides still encounter significant issues during clinical translation. This review summarized the recent progress of nanosized radionuclides for cancer treatment or diagnosis. The discussion began with representative radionuclides and the methods of incorporating them into nanomaterial structures. Subsequently, new combinations of nanomaterials and radionuclides, along with their applications, were introduced to demonstrate their future trends. The benefits of nanoradionuclides included optimized pharmacokinetic properties, enhanced disease‐targeting efficacy, and synergistic application with other treatment techniques. Besides, the basic rule of this section was to summarize how these nanoradionuclides can truly impact the diagnosis and therapy of various cancer types. In the last part, the focus was devoted to the nanoradionuclides currently applicable in clinics and how to address the existing issues and problems based on our knowledge.

Introduction

Utilizing radiopharmaceuticals for disease imaging and treatment represents an invaluable practice with significant utility in both preclinical and clinical environments. One key to successful radionuclide utilization lied in the thorough absorption and prolonged retention of radioactive pharmaceuticals within the target tissue(s). Conventional radiation therapy utilizes ionizing radiation, such as x‐rays and gamma rays, to penetrate human tissues and disrupt the DNA of cancer cells, ultimately hindering their growth and division. Subsequently, radioactive particles such as protons or heavy ions were employed to directly target and attack tumor cells, thereby minimizing harm to normal tissues to the greatest extent possible (Luderer, de la Puente, and Azab 2015; Roy et al. 2022). Selective radiation delivery was achieved through the use of suitable radiopharmaceuticals, capitalizing on the limited range of β particles (typically within millimeters) or the extremely short path length of α particles, as well as radionuclides emitting Auger electrons (Chatal and Hoefnagel 1999). In addition, the emissions of γ and β+ (positron) from radionuclides served as the purpose of accurately pinpointing the distribution of these radionuclides within the whole organism. Furthermore, relative biological effectiveness (RBE) was a concept used to compare the biological effects of different types of radiation on living organisms. It was calculated on the type of radiation, its energy, and the specific characteristics of the biological system. For instance, proton and heavy ion radiation typically had a higher RBE because they can induce stronger biological effects when traversing through biological tissues, whereas the RBE was lower for electrons, x‐rays, and gamma rays in comparison. In nuclear radiation, α particles have the highest RBE, followed by β particles and gamma rays (Kassis 2008). The higher the RBE, the greater the destructive effect of radiation could be witnessed for the same absorbed energy. Radiation with a higher linear energy transfer (LET), and thus a high RBE, was necessary for inducing sufficient therapeutic outcomes (Banerjee, Pillai, and Knapp 2015). Thus, nuclides with α and β−emissions, which were used as therapeutic radionuclides, can potently damage tumor cells, causing internal DNA damage and thereby achieving therapeutic goals (Figure 1).

The main emission types from radionuclides and their penetration ranges. Therapeutic radionuclides emit α or β− particles, or Auger electrons, while β+ and γ emitters were applicable for in vivo imaging (Banerjee, Pillai, and Knapp 2015; Chatal and Hoefnagel 1999; Kassis 2008).

Furthermore, since almost 82 years ago, when the first targeted radiotherapy for thyroid disorders, including thyroid cancer, was conducted using 131I (Jadvar 2017), many treatment combinations have emerged for various types of cancers, including glioma (Foster et al. 2021), prostate cancer (Lawal et al. 2022), pancreatic cancer (Watabe et al. 2020), and breast cancer (Bhusari et al. 2017). However, certain radiopharmaceuticals encountered challenges related to metabolic instability in the body, triggering rapid clearance in vivo, which reduced their effectiveness for treatment or imaging and restricted their disease applicability (De Ruysscher et al. 2019).

To further enhance the utilization of radionuclides at tumor sites, there has been recent utilization of molecular delivery vehicles such as peptides, antibodies, small molecules, and engineered antibody derivatives to accommodate radionuclides to tumors (Filippi et al. 2020; Peltek et al. 2019). For instance, monoclonal antibodies can recognize and bind to cell surface antigens with good specificity. However, challenges arise due to the large molecular weight of antibodies, the long circulation retention, and the limited penetration within tumors. Consequently, antibody fragments with smaller sizes were favored, but their labeling efficacy with radiometals could sometimes be quite limited (Roy et al. 2022). Peptides, with their smaller molecular size, exhibited swift diffusion into target tissues upon radionuclide labeling, providing rapid kinetic properties. Nevertheless, concerns persist regarding notable hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity in therapies utilizing radiolabeled peptides, small molecules, and antibodies, which limits the permissible injection dosages (Desai et al. 2022b).

Over the past few decades, there had been substantial growth in the development of nanomedicines for cancer treatment, driven by their potential to enhance existing cancer diagnosis and therapy methods. Notably, nanoradionuclides showed great promises for cancer diagnosis and treatment. Nanomaterials exhibited key physical/chemical characteristics, including the ability to adjust their size and morphology, a substantial surface area‐to‐volume ratio, adaptable porosity, a tunable circulation half‐life, and surface modifiability (Lamb and Holland 2018). Moreover, solid tumors usually exhibited a notable and well‐developed vascular network, frequently coupled with insufficient lymphatic drainage. These characteristics give rise to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, a crucial factor contributing to the specific accumulation of nanoparticles (NPs) within tumors (Maeda et al. 2000). These attributes confer distinct advantages for in vivo circulation of NPs, resulting in extended tumor residence and enhanced efficacy in cancer therapy. Ultimately, owing to their inherent characteristics, specific nanoparticles can inherently offer supplementary therapeutic functions [e.g., photothermal, chemodynamic therapy (CDT), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), etc.] or imaging capabilities (such as MRI, fluorescence, photoacoustic, etc.), which can be concurrently harnessed alongside nuclear medicine modalities (Chen et al. 2016; Ehlerding et al. 2018; Wang, Liu, Zheng, Jin, and Li, 2020). Additionally, the immune response induced by radiotherapy has also attracted attention. Increasing evidence suggests that radiotherapy not only exhibits direct cytotoxicity to cells but also modulates the immunogenicity of tumor cells, enhances immune responses against tumors, reduces immune suppression, and increases the tumor's responsiveness to radiation, leading to a systemic immune response (Filatenkov et al. 2015; Ozpiskin, Zhang, and Li 2019). Radiation mainly causes DNA breaks in cells, leading to abnormal DNA damage and repair, which can promote the antigenicity and adjuvanticity of targeted tumors, but there were few reports or clinical data on this. Additionally, only a small number of patients achieve a complete response with radiation therapy, and in most cases, radiation therapy only amplifies the pro‐immunogenic phenotype, making it difficult to transform the immunosuppressive environment into an immune‐stimulating environment. As a future trend, combination therapy, such as the combination of radiation therapy and immunotherapy, has become a new and promising direction.

Moreover, one of the benefits of employing nanomaterial techniques was that it allowed leveraging the size and modifiable characteristics of materials to enhance their accumulation at tumor sites, while reducing their presence in nontargeted areas, thereby enabling nuclear imaging or radiotherapy at reduced nanoparticle dosages. In this review, we tried to cover the latest developments in nanoscale radionuclides for oncology utilization, discussing techniques for incorporating radionuclide into nanomaterials, addressing current implementation challenges, and exploring future potential and opportunities.

The Strategies for Nanoparticle Radiolabeling

Successful radiolabeled probes required high chemical, radiochemical, and metabolic stability, along with favorable pharmacokinetic characteristics. An important practical consideration was to ensure that the radiolabeling steps were simple and reproducible. These requirements led researchers to develop various innovative solutions for producing radiolabeled nanoparticles. Here, we explored radiolabeling methods for different types of nanoparticles and they could be categorized into two main classes: classical labeling and nonclassical labeling (Tables 1 and 2).

Classical Labeling Method

Radiolabeling With Chelating Agents

Since most radionuclides are metals, coordination is a good strategy to accommodate them inside the nanostructures, among them, chelating molecules, as a commonly used class of radioligands, were widely employed in the radiolabeling of nanomaterials. The critical factor in chelation‐based labeling was to select an appropriate chelating agent for a given radionuclide. Surface modification of NPs with carboxyl, thiol, or amine groups was preferred before binding with the chelating agent, simplifying the subsequent radioactive labeling process. Functionalized chelating agents, including 1,4,7‐triazacyclononane‐1,4,7‐triacetic acid (NOTA), 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid (DOTA), desferrioxamine B (DFO), and so forth, can react with abovementioned chemical groups, allowing them to be incubated with the modified NPs until coupling was successfully achieved. Taking graphene oxide (GO) nanocomposites as an example, small chelating molecules were often used for the radiolabeling of radionuclides. As early as 2012, NOTA was used as a chelating molecule, conjugated to the NH2 groups on the surface of GO, and then reacted with 64CuCl2 to create 64Cu‐labeled GO, achieving a radiochemical yield of approximately 76 ± 8% and radiochemical purity exceeding 95% (Hong et al. 2012). In comparison, DFO could bind to ions such as Fe3+, Zr4+, Al3+, and exhibited a high affinity for gallium‐68 (68Ga; Sarpaki et al. 2020). For instance, Lamb et al. used DFO as a chelating agent, connected it to carboxylated graphene nanoflakes (GNF) via amide bonds, and subsequently radiolabeled it with 68Ga3+ ions using established methods (Lamb et al. 2019). In 2017, Li et al. developed an efficient method for labeling folic acid (FA)‐DFO‐liposomes with 89Zr (Li et al. 2017). This was accomplished through a ligand exchange reaction at room temperature between the 89Zr(8‐hydroxyquinoline)4 complex and the encapsulated DFO within the liposomal aqueous cavity, with the additional feature of FA decoration. Subsequently, a super small mesoporous silica nanoparticle (UPSN) was developed, and the amino‐functionalized UPSN was coupled with DOTA chelators under alkaline conditions to form 86Y‐DOTA‐UPSN, which was used for positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (Ferreira et al. 2021). Besides, Torres et al. reported a bifunctional chelator that combines a dithiocarbamate group for binding the positron emitter 64Cu for PET imaging and a bisphosphonate group for strong binding to several inorganic materials, such as superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles and rare‐earth metal oxides used as MRI contrast agents (Torres Martin de Rosales et al. 2011).

Particle Coating Modification

Particle coating's radioactive labeling involved chemical reactions similar to utilizing chelating agents. Unlike the need for surface‐anchoring groups, radioactive labeling on coatings introduced the radioactive group through the formation of covalent bonds. For example, Devaraj et al. introduced an innovative method involving the modification of glucose coatings with reactive azide groups, followed by 18F‐radiolabeling and cross‐linking of glucose‐coated iron oxide nanoparticles for PET/CT imaging (Devaraj et al. 2009). Using a presynthesized copper‐catalyzed click reaction with 18F‐PEG3 alkyne reagent, successful radiolabeling of azide‐functionalized cross‐linked glucose‐coated iron oxide nanoparticles was achieved within just 40 min at 40°C. This work highlighted the potential of click chemistry for efficiently producing complex radiolabeled nanostructures through a simple and bioorthogonal process. Additionally, Rong and colleagues constructed a photosensitizer molecule labeled with 64Cu by directly reacting 64Cu with the photosensitizer molecule HPPH, in which a strong binding was formed (Rong et al. 2014). Subsequently, they connected it to graphene oxide (GO) modified with PEG, achieving the labeling of the nanomaterial. Additionally, Tian et al. developed calcium bisphosphonate‐polyethylene glycol (CaBP‐PEG) nanoparticles through a PEG‐coated mineralization process, and due to the strong coordination affinity between bisphosphonates and metal ions, it was hypothesized that these nanoparticles could be labeled with 99mTc4+, resulting in a radioactive labeling yield of 70% (Tian et al. 2018).

Although the use of chelators was a widely used labeling method, the main limitation of classical functionalization lied in the absence of a universally applicable chelating agent for all nuclides, making it essential to choose the appropriate chelating agent before labeling to achieve effective modification. Nevertheless, this surface modification altered the pharmacokinetics of nanoparticles, subsequently diminishing the overall efficiency of radionuclides delivery. Additionally, the detachment of radionuclides also diminished specificity and the diagnostic and therapeutic effects of delivery (Goel et al. 2014; Liu 2008).

Nonclassical Labeling Methods

In addition to the classic methods mentioned above, established radioactive labeling approaches also included radiochemical doping, cation exchange, cavity encapsulation, direct chemisorption and neutron activated.

Radiochemical Doping

Radiochemical doping was primarily accomplished by introducing a small quantity of radionuclides into the reactants before the reaction, thereby producing nano‐sized particles with radioactivity. As an illustration, in 2017, 89Zr was successfully incorporated as a radioactive precursor into the UiO‐66 structure seamlessly during the synthesis process, with a decay‐corrected radiochemical yield of 85.7 ± 6.2% (D. Chen et al. 2017). For triple‐negative breast tumor targeting, 89Zr‐UiO‐66 was additionally functionalized with pyrene‐derived polyethylene glycol (Py–PGA‐PEG) and conjugated with the nucleolin‐targeting peptide ligand (F3). Additionally, Zheng et al. successfully radiolabeled gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) by incorporating 68Ga, resulting in the synthesis of 68Ga‐doped gold nanoclusters coated with glutathione and arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) peptide (68Ga‐RGD‐GSH@AuNCs; Zheng et al. 2021). These nanoclusters were employed for early tumor detection, showing effective accumulation in tumor tissues and subsequent renal excretion. This labeling technique mitigated the in vivo desorption and transchelation of radionuclides, thereby bolstering the radiochemical stability of the nanocarrier.

Cation Exchange

Cation exchange, a well‐established process in nanocrystal synthesis, involves substitution reactions on the surface or within nanocrystals, where incoming radionuclides chemically differ from replaced cations, allowing for charge‐independent substitutions with different atomic compositions (Son et al. 2004). Sun and colleagues utilized cation exchange to introduce 64Cu into CdSe/ZnS quantum dots, which revealed that as the Cu–Se ratio gradually increased, there was an initial decrease in the Zn–Se (anion) ratio, followed by reductions in both the Zn–Se and Cd–Se ratios. This observation implied that Cu could substitute for Zn and even infiltrate the core to replace Cd (Sun et al. 2014). However, when commercially acquired quantum dots undergo surface functionalization, the achievable efficiency of radiolabeling was usually about 10%. In response, Tang et al. introduced functionalized zinc sulfide (ZnS) quantum dots, allowing for straightforward radioactive labeling with nuclides like 64Cu or 68Ga through cation exchange at temperatures close to room temperature (Tang et al. 2019). By co‐incubating radioactive metal ions with ZnS, the radioactive labeling process was completed within 15 min at 37°C with an efficiency exceeding 95% for 68Ga and approximately 90% for 64Cu.

Cavity Encapsulation

Petersen et al. developed a remote loading method by encapsulating 64Cu through pre‐encapsulation of DOTA or 2‐hydroxyquinoline in liposomes, resulting in a high remote loading efficiency (> 95%), high purity (> 99% encapsulation), and good tumor accumulation (Petersen et al. 2011). The study revealed that 2‐hydroxyquinoline possesses advantageous properties for transporting 64Cu into liposomes and may also be applicable for remote loading of other metal‐based radionuclides. Subsequently, in 2014, Cisneros et al. introduced a method to simultaneously encapsulate Gd3+ ions and 64Cu2+ ions within ultra‐short single‐walled carbon nanotubes, generating stable radioactively labeled nanotubes for assessing pharmacokinetic characteristics using PET/MRI, despite partial 64Cu removal during serum clearance (Cisneros et al. 2014). Radioactive pharmaceuticals incorporated into the core of nanomaterials share common characteristics, including low isotope release rates, high stability, and a reduced likelihood of detaching from the nanocarrier.

Direct Chemisorption

During radiolabeling, chemical adsorption was primarily achieved through the formation of direct chemical bonds between the radioactive nuclide and the nanoparticles. For example, taking advantage of the deprotonated Si–O groups in mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN), which can form stable coordination complexes with oxygen–affine radioactive metals, Jung et al. employed 68Ga for MSN labeling (Jung et al. 2020). The labeling process, completed in about 90 min, resulted in a uniform distribution of 68Ga within the silica matrix, as confirmed by elemental composition maps generated using energy‐dispersive spectroscopy. In another study, researchers utilized a similar strategy to label near‐infrared fluorescent (NIRF) MSN with 89Zr, creating a dual‐modal PET/NIRF nanoplatform with a radiolabeling efficiency exceeding 98% (Harmsen et al. 2021). This agent facilitated whole‐body PET and high‐resolution NIRF imaging to investigate the fate of adoptively transferred CAR T cells, allowing comprehensive cell tracking for up to 1‐week post‐adoptive cell transplantation. Moreover, other examples of direct chemical adsorption include the interaction of 89Zr ions with preformed phosphonate groups in liposomes (Abou et al. 2013) and the radiolabeling of Al2O3‐coated MnFe2O4 and Fe3O4 nanoparticles with 18F (Cui et al. 2014). Overall, the use of direct chemical adsorption for radioactive labeling typically does not compromise the physical and biochemical characteristics of the nanoparticles.

Neutron Activation

An intriguing concept in the radiolabeling of nanoparticles involves the presynthesis of a nonradioactive variant. Subsequently, through in situ conversion of atoms using particle beams or reactor‐based activation, both neutron and proton activation methods can be employed to produce radioactively labeled nanoparticles. Wang et al. reported the preparation of nanocapsules initially containing the “cold” isotope 152Sm, which could be activated to the “hot” radioactive form 153Sm as needed through neutron irradiation (Wang, Klippstein, et al. 2020). The resulting nanocapsules exhibited significantly higher specific activity after neutron activation compared to previous methods of direct encapsulation of radionuclides. This not only allowed for in vivo nuclear imaging but also held promise for radiotherapy in lung tumors. Additionally, Ancira et al. synthesized Lu2O3 nanoparticles modified with prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA) targeted ligands (Lu2O3‐iPSMA NPs) (Ancira‐Cortez et al. 2020). Through neutron activation, 177Lu2O3‐iPSMA was obtained, which was utilized for dual‐modality imaging (optical and nuclear) and targeted therapy against cancer cells overexpressing PSMA protein. This innovative approach reduced personnel radiation exposure, prevented nuclear waste generation, and circumvented the time constraints associated with radioactive decay.

The Applications of Radiolabeled Nanoparticles

Radionuclides were employed to deliver radiation with distinct emission properties, such as α‐particles or β‐particles (Sgouros et al. 2020). The tissue range of α‐emitters spans 50–100 μm and was characterized by an exceptionally high LET. They were regarded as the most efficient method for inducing cell apoptosis. In contrast, β‐emitters exhibited a relatively lower LET and an extended tissue range, reaching up to 12 mm, thus obviating the need for cellular internalization to inflict damage on tumors. Thanks to their extended reach, β‐emitters compensate for variations in receptor expression on cancer cells, resulting in the demise of adjacent cells, a phenomenon known as the crossfire effect (Desai et al. 2022b). Nanoparticles Labeled With α‐Emitting Radionuclides

Alpha particles were positively charged helium ions emitted from the nuclei of unstable atoms. They had a higher capacity to induce DNA breaks compared to X‐rays and γ‐rays (Bannik et al. 2019). Furthermore, when compared to β particles or γ‐rays, the radiobiological effects associated with α‐emitting radionuclides were less sensitive to factors such as dose rate, hypoxia, and cell cycle distribution (Runge et al. 2016). For effective α therapy, rapid targeting of short‐lived radionuclides is highly preferred. In comparison, long‐lived nuclides, often members of an extended decay chain, can release recoil nuclides, causing detachment from the holding carrier and causing unexpected damage to healthy tissues. Thus, during the process of α radionuclides labeling, it is crucial to accommodate both the radionuclides and its daughter nuclides.

However, the chelation by most chelating agents was insufficient to reliably retain their daughter nuclides in most situations. Therefore, most nanocarriers employ encapsulation or cation exchange to protect the nuclides in the core, preventing leakage, ensuring a fast labeling process, and maintaining nanocarrier structural integrity. For representative α‐emitting radionuclides include actinium‐225 (225Ac), bismuth‐213 (213Bi), radium‐223 (223Ra), and astatine‐211 (211At), their interaction with nanomaterials constituted a central theme in our subsequent discussion.

Actinium‐225

225Ac, the parent nucleus of 213Bi, possesses highly appealing nuclear characteristics for radionuclides therapy, featuring a relatively extended half‐life of 9.9 days and releasing four net α‐particles per decay, enabling the sustained deposition of high radiation doses within tumors (Figure 2a; Cakici and Kilbas 2022; Fitzsimmons and Atcher 2007; Thakral et al. 2021). 225Ac exhibits multiple daughter nuclides in its decay chain, some of which can escape from the target site, circulate in the bloodstream, and exert toxicity in areas such as the kidneys and renal tracks (Muslimov et al. 2021). A significant challenge in α‐generative radiotherapy lied in the fact that conventional chelating agents failed to effectively manage the radioactive progeny within the conjugates. Consequently, there was a consensus within the scientific community to explore alternative approaches, to mitigate the recoil effects associated with the 225Ac progeny (Kleynhans et al. 2018). Various nanostructures that can encapsulate 225Ac and retain its subcomponents at the tumor site were subsequently investigated to mitigate the challenge of adverse cytotoxicity to healthy tissues.

Therefore, Stavroula et al. developed multivesicular liposomes (MUVELs) composed of different phospholipids and labeled with 225Ac (Sofou et al. 2007). The research revealed that PEGylated MUVELs effectively retained 98% of encapsulated 225Ac, with a retention rate of 31% for the final progeny, 213Bi, which reached the theoretical maximum retention rate. Subsequently, these MUVELs were conjugated with HER2/neu antibodies and assessed in SKOV3‐NMP2 ovarian cancer cells, demonstrating significant intracellular uptake (83%). This study suggested that antibody‐conjugated MUVELs may have the potential to deliver a higher proportion of alpha particles generated by 225Ac in comparison to regular 225Ac‐chelating molecules. Subsequently, in 2019, Kruijff et al. employed polymer encapsulation of 225Ac, injecting it into tumor‐bearing mice, and observed enhanced anti‐tumor activity with limited kidney toxicity incurred (Kruijff et al. 2019).

In addition to these organic nanocarriers, numerous inorganic nanomaterials had also been utilized for the labeling of 223Ac. For instance, Salvanou et al. recently conducted a study that assessed the use of 225Ac‐labeled gold nanoparticles (225Ac‐Au@TADOTAGA) as an injectable radiopharmaceutical for localized cancer radiotherapy, particularly for close‐range radiation therapy (Figure 2b; Salvanou et al. 2020). Furthermore, Cdrowska et al. proposed the use of titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles to hold both 225Ac and its decay products (Cędrowska et al. 2018). Functionalized TiO2 nanoparticles with peptide fragments (P‐substance) targeting the NK1 receptor on glioblastoma cells were prepared, and the labeled nanoconjugates demonstrated nearly complete retention of 225Ac in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), physiological saline, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for a duration of 10 days. As‐synthesized 225Ac‐TiO2‐PEG‐SP exhibited significant cytotoxicity against T98G glioblastoma cells in vitro. Therefore, it held promise as a novel radiopharmaceutical for targeted radionuclides therapy into brain tumors.

Additionally, McLaughlin et al. prepared lanthanide phosphate (LnPO4) nanoparticles encapsulating GdPO4 for targeted 225Ac therapy against lung cancer (McLaughlin et al. 2014). Lanthanide (La3+), gadolinium (Gd3+), and 225Ac3+ ion‐based NPs were coated with an additional layer of GdPO4, followed by gold coating via the citrate reduction method using NaAuCl4. Subsequently, modification of the nanoparticles involved the addition of the monoclonal antibody (MAb) 201b, targeting thrombomodulin in pulmonary endothelial cells. The results revealed that approximately 70% of 225Ac remained in lung tissues at 1 h post‐injection. Furthermore, compared to treatment with cold monoclonal antibodies, targeted 225Ac nanoparticles demonstrated significantly enhanced anticancer activity. The study concluded that formulated nanoparticles can effectively deliver 225Ac with more controllable pharmacokinetics (McLaughlin et al. 2014). Based on the findings from above‐mentioned studies, targeted nanoparticle delivery of 225Ac offered a sophisticated approach that achieved maximum uptake in tumors while limiting unnecessary retention in healthy tissues. This allowed for the effective and selective delivery of 225Ac to tumors without compromising the therapeutic properties of the radionuclides.

Bismuth‐213

The majority of the total particle energy released per decay of 213Bi primarily arises from α‐decay, accounting for 92.7%, while the remaining 7.3% results from β‐emissions. Within its complete decay cycle, 213Bi emits one net α‐particle. Due to its relatively short half‐life of 45.6 min, it is necessary that radioactivity should be delivered in a more accurate manner (Ahenkorah et al. 2021). Given the high costs of α‐emitting radionuclides, the limited physical half‐life of 213Bi, and radiation protection considerations, it is imperative to employ swift and gentle radiolabeling conditions to have a favorable applicability. Although circulation half‐lives of peptides or antibody fragments are more compatible to 213Bi, they are susceptible to affinity loss especially when bulky bifunctional chelating ligands are used (Feuerecker et al. 2021; Wharton et al. 2022). Thus, the conjunction with nanomaterials emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the specific targeting of 213Bi, mitigate harm to normal tissues, and amplify its therapeutic effectiveness.

There were relatively limited reports on the labeling of nanomaterials with 213Bi, and among these studies, Roobol and colleagues utilized a reverse nanoprecipitation method to fabricate nanoparticles with average diameters of 60 and 80 nm, which were labeled with 213Bi (Roobol et al. 2020). These polymersomes (PM) were formed through self‐assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers, combining the potential to mitigate recoil issues with highly adjustable and versatile properties (Figure 2c). Due to the short range and high LET of α‐emitting radionuclides, prolonged localization near target cells was required, which could be achieved by cellular uptake of physically anchored PMs. 213Bi was labeled onto PMs with a high radiochemical yield, and the researchers included an assessment of PM's cellular uptake kinetics, post‐uptake processing, and DNA damage induced by 213Bi. Their results demonstrated that PMs uptake was mediated through endocytosis, and in the presence of PMs within the cells, 213Bi‐PMs induced DNA damage in U2OS cell nuclei equivalent to that observed with external α‐irradiation, while no significant DNA damage induction was observed in the absence of PM inside the cells. This study demonstrated that the particle size and concentration of PMs could all significantly affect DNA damage. It also implied that we can modulate the in vivo localization of radionuclides by altering the physical properties of PMs or nanomaterials.

Radium‐223

223Ra is an α‐emitting radioactive nuclide with a half‐life of 11.4 days, producing 4α particles. It was the first radionuclide approved for the treatment of metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer with bone metastases. Its treatment functions depend on targeting tumor cells, pathological bone metabolism effectors, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts. Additionally, it may promote local anti‐tumor immune responses, sparking interest in the role of radionuclides in bone‐lesion therapies (Marques et al. 2018; Morris et al. 2019). As a cationic calcium analog, 223Ra had a natural tendency to accumulate in the bone matrix, especially hydroxyapatite, and current research primarily focused on its combination with chemotherapy drugs or immunotherapy, with emerging interest in the integration of nanomaterials.

In 2006, Jonasdottir and colleagues evaluated for the first time lipid‐encapsulated 223Ra as a potential α‐therapeutic agent using an ion carrier‐mediated loading method, encapsulating 223Ra within polyethylene glycol‐coated amphotericin liposomes (Jonasdottir et al. 2006). The findings demonstrated that the initial half‐life of 223Ra liposomes in the bloodstream exceeded 24 h, in stark contrast to the markedly shorter blood half‐life of cationic 223Ra, which was less than 1 h. As lipid‐encapsulated 223Ra underwent metabolic breakdown, the released 223Ra was either eliminated from the body or taken up by the skeletal system. Although bone uptake increased after 14 days of treatment, it did not reach the comparable levels observed in free 223Ra. This also demonstrated that 223Ra liposomes were relatively stable within the body, showcasing their potential for radioactive nuclide therapy and combination therapy with chemotherapy drugs. Subsequently, Piotrowska et al. utilized nano‐zeolite‐P as carriers for targeted alpha therapy with 223Ra radioactive nuclide (Piotrowska et al. 2017). The synthesis involved the template method to create the sodium form of type A nano‐zeolite (NaA), followed by binding the nano‐zeolite particles with P substance (5–11) peptide segments. The radioactive conjugate was prepared by exchanging Na+ with 223Ra2+ within the zeolite structure. The results showed that the 223Ra‐labeled nano‐zeolite bioconjugate retained nearly intact after 6 days in vitro, with retention rates for decay products ranging from 90% to 95%. Furthermore, it remained stable in various biological fluids, including human serum, making it suitable for localized treatment of glioma cancer cells.

Additionally, Gawęda and colleagues utilized barium ferrite nanoparticles (BaFeNPs) as carriers for targeted alpha therapy with 223Ra (Gawęda et al. 2020). In the study, a hydrothermal method was employed to replace Ba2+ cations with 223Ra, resulting in the labeling of barium ferrite nanoparticles (BaFeNPs) with a good radiochemical yield (~60%). Surface functionalization was achieved using 3‐phosphonopropionic acid (CEPA), followed by covalent conjugation with trastuzumab (Herceptin). The synthesized [223Ra]BaFe–CEPA–trastuzumab retained nearly quantitatively (> 98%) over 30 days and proved to be an effective drug for treating cancer cells overexpressing HER2 receptors, including breast and ovarian tumors (Figure 2d). Furthermore, the confirmed radiolabeled bioconjugate's magnetic properties allow for magnetic‐guided delivery of [223Ra]BaFe‐CEPA‐trastuzumab under an external magnetic field. The aforementioned three methods primarily involved the encapsulation of 223Ra through nanoparticle encapsulation and cation exchange, all of which exhibited excellent stability, reducing the release of radioactive metals. Furthermore, subsequent targeting with peptide ligands against cancer tissues allowed 223Ra to be applicable in various cancer therapies. Additionally, in 2022, researchers prepared nano‐micelles of radium dichloride ([223Ra]RaCl2) for the treatment of bone metastasis (Yang et al. 2022). They discovered that, compared to [223Ra]RaCl2, nano‐micelles at the same dose exhibited better efficacy (a 20% increase). These results further affirmed the effectiveness of nano‐systems and validated their success in radiotherapy.

Astatine‐211

211At exhibits a 100% α emission rate, releasing just one α particle per decay, preventing unpredictable dose localization caused by the separation of radioactive progeny from the carrier. Additionally, its 7.2‐h half‐life may reduce exposure to normal tissues while still being long enough for up to 3 h of chemical/radiopharmaceutical processing and providing sufficient remaining activity for clinical treatment (Albertsson et al. 2023). The first recorded use of 211At in humans was published in 1954 when Hamilton and colleagues explored its potential application for the treatment of thyroid diseases (Hamilton, Durbin, and Parrott 1954). Subsequently, its combination with nanomaterials has also become a recent research trend in recent years.

Kato et al. synthesized different sizes of Au nanoparticles (5, 13, 30, or 120 nm), labeled with 211At after mPEG modification (211At‐AuNP‐s‐mPEG), and administered them intratumorally in rodent models with either C6 glioma or subcutaneously transplanted PANC‐1 pancreatic cancer (Kato et al. 2021). The findings indicated that, upon intratumoral administration, 211At‐AuNP‐s‐mPEG remained localized within the tumor for approximately six half‐lives of 211At, without spreading to other organs throughout the body. This led to a significant inhibition of tumor growth in both C6 and PANC‐1 models. Notably, the treatment group with 5 nm particles exhibited the most potent anti‐tumor effect in the C6 animal model. This study, by labeling different‐sized Au nanoparticles with 211At, confirmed that the size of nanomaterials significantly influenced treatment efficacy, with smaller sizes yielding superior therapeutic results. Localized administration of nanoscale agents was also regarded as an effective and safe anticancer treatment. Furthermore, Dziawer and colleagues introduced a novel α‐nano close‐range treatment approach for the selective treatment of HER2‐positive breast cancer, which was a targeted anticancer therapy (Dziawer et al. 2019). This approach entailed the local intratumoral injection of 5 nm‐diameter AuNPs, followed by surface modification with PEG and immobilization of HER2‐specific monoclonal antibodies (trastuzumab). Subsequently, the high affinity between gold and heavy halogens was utilized to label the bioconjugate with 211At, obtained through α irradiation of a bismuth target. Besides, the labeling efficiency for 211At exceeded 99%, and it remained stable in human serum. Furthermore, the research demonstrates that compared to unmodified nanoparticles, 211At‐AuNP‐PEG‐trastuzumab exhibited higher affinity and cytotoxicity towards HER2‐overexpressing human ovarian SKOV‐3 cell lines, thereby validating the effectiveness of the combination of nanomaterials with targeted antibodies.

In addition to the two previously mentioned close‐range tumor treatments involving local injections of Au nanoparticles, Zhang and colleagues introduced a combination approach that integrates immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) with chemodynamic therapy (CDT) to study tumor metastasis and recurrence, overcoming the shortcomings of targeted alpha therapy (TAT; Figure 3a; Zhang, Li, et al. 2022). The design of this study was primarily motivated by the limitations of internal radiation therapy (IRT) and CDT in inducing a systemic immune response, despite their ability to kill tumor cells to some extent. While ICB has shown significant progress in clinical settings, it benefits less than 20% of cancer patients. However, the exciting prospect of combining IRT based on alpha radioisotopes with ICB is expected to trigger a robust immune response and inhibit tumor metastasis. The study successfully designed a manganese‐based radiomodulatory agent named 211At‐ATE‐MnO2‐BSA, composed of 211At, MnO2, and bovine serum albumin (BSA). The efficacy of 211At‐ATE‐MnO2‐BSA as a single agent or in combination with anti‐PD‐L1 was investigated in metastasis and recurrence models, and the results demonstrated that these radiomodulatory agents not only enhanced the therapeutic effects of TAT and CDT but also induced potent anti‐cancer immune activity. Compared to free 211At, the accumulation of 211At‐ATE‐MnO2‐BSA at the tumor site was significantly increased (Figure 3b) and markedly promoted the maturation of DC cells at the CT26 tumor site (Figure 3c). When used in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, 211At‐ATE‐MnO2‐BSA could effectively suppress the growth of both primary tumors and distant tumors (Figure 3d,e). This study combined the therapeutic function of nano MnO2 (CDT) with the radiotherapeutic efficacy of 211At, along with the addition of ICB, creating a more comprehensive and effective treatment approach. This addressed the limitations of α nuclides in close‐range therapy, extending their application to metastatic sites and suggesting a promising future for the combined use of nanomaterials and radionuclides in cancer treatment.

Nanoparticles Labeled With β‐Emitting Radionuclides

The emission of β particles primarily includes β− and β+ particles. Among them, there are ultra‐short‐lived high‐energy β+ emitters with half‐lives ranging from seconds to hours, emitting annihilation energy at 511 keV. These can be used for precise localization of radioactive nuclides in the living subjects, enabling PET imaging. Examples of such nuclides include 64Cu, 68Ga, and 18F, among others. And, β− emitters currently hold the largest share of the global therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals market (Mattoli et al. 2021). Compared to α‐emitters, longer tissue penetration range of β‐particles makes them suitable for relatively large tumors. This primarily includes formulations of iodine‐131 (131I), rhenium‐188 (188Re), lutetium‐177 (177Lu), and yttrium‐90 (90Y). In clinical practice, 131I and 90Y nuclides were used in 90% of targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy cases (Garg et al. 2021).

Nanoparticles Labeled With β−‐Emitting Radionuclides

Iodine‐131

The use of radioactive iodine therapy, utilizing 131I, has been a cornerstone of nuclear medicine for many years and its half‐life is 8 days. The introduction of 131I as a radioactive pharmaceutical for treating thyroid cancer in 1946 marked a significant milestone in the successful application of radioactive nuclides in medicine. Furthermore, the clinical application of 131I therapy was regarded as a pivotal example in molecular nuclear medicine and was characterized by its wide availability. This tracer, aside from emitting β particles, also emits penetrating γ radiation. While it was commonly used for diagnosing and staging thyroid cancer through gamma imaging, its high‐energy γ photons could lead to unnecessary radiation exposure for nontargeted organs and individuals in contact with the patient. In recent years, low‐dose 131I has been used for disease diagnosis via gamma imaging, while high‐dose 131I was later employed for thyroid ablation treatment. Most patients underwent multiple rounds of this therapy, which increased the risk of radiation‐related side effects (Nagarajah et al. 2017). For risk reduction and improved efficacy of 131I therapy, individual assessment of radiation doses to tumors and vulnerable organs is crucial.

Wu and colleagues developed tumor‐targeted polyethylene glycol‐poly lactic acid (PEG–PLA) nanoparticles for transporting chemotherapy drug 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) and 131I (Wu et al. 2020). The nanoparticles obtained, namely Cet‐PEG–PLA‐5Fu‐131I, displayed a spherical morphology with a diameter of around 110 nm. These nanoparticles exhibited pH sensitivity, allowing for the gradual release of 5‐FU under low pH conditions. When combined with 131I, they effectively inhibited the growth of colorectal cancer. Additionally, PEG–PLA‐based nanoparticles exhibited excellent biocompatibility and reliable safety, prolonging circulation time in the bloodstream and facilitating effective tumor‐targeted accumulation. Combining chemotherapy and radiotherapy using these nanoparticles offered superior therapeutic advantages compared to single treatments. In addition, researchers also combined functional nanomaterials with radioactive nuclides to achieve the goal of multi‐modal diagnosis and treatment. Yu et al. reported the use of radioactive semiconductor polymer nanoparticles (rSPNs) for multi‐modal therapy of breast cancer (Yu et al. 2022). Without using chelating agents, 131I was directly anchored onto the semiconductor polymer main chain (SPs) and the remaining side chains were modified with PEG to form amphiphilic SP‐PEG, which was self‐assembled into rSPNs, achieving a labeling efficiency of up to 99.6% (Figure 4a). Following systemic administration, effective accumulation at the 4T1 tumor site in mice was achieved, which could be clearly delineated by SPECT and NIRF imaging. Additionally, strong photothermal and photodynamic effects were exhibited by SPs under near‐infrared light. When combined with radiotherapy, tumor growth and metastasis in the liver and lungs were inhibited, thus providing a versatile and straightforward organic nanoplatform for multi‐modal diagnosis and treatment.

In contrast, composite materials also found their way in combination with radiotherapy. One example was that Tao and colleagues designed a PEG‐modified graphene‐manganese dioxide nanocomposite, which was labeled with 131I to create 131I‐RGO‐MnO2‐PEG (Tao et al. 2018). Under acidic conditions, O2 generation alleviated the tumor's hypoxic microenvironment, while released Mn2+ ions enabled MRI and 131I emitted γ radiation for SPECT imaging in mice. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, the combined effect of MRI/SPECT‐guided radiotherapy and photothermal therapy was achieved. This also marked the first report of graphene nanocomposites simultaneously addressing hypoxia while guiding imaging for radiotherapy. To further enhance treatment efficiency, the study combined 131I‐RGO‐MnO2‐PEG with the vascular‐disrupting agent DMXAA (5,6‐dimethylxanthenone‐4‐acetic acid), resulting in increased tumor uptake. This research report offered a suitable strategy for overcoming tumor hypoxia during radiotherapy.

Rhenium‐188

188Re emits β− particles with energies of 1.96 MeV (25.6%) and 2.12 MeV (71.1%) and has a half‐life of 16.9 h. It is considered one of the most promising generator‐type therapeutic β‐emitters (Chen et al. 2021). In clinical practice, radioactive drugs based on 188Re were utilized for the treatment of prostate and breast cancer. Furthermore, with the development of 188Re generator‐type radioactive drugs, their applications expanded rapidly.

The combination of 188Re with nanomaterials primarily occurred within organic materials such as liposomes, which serving as carriers to facilitate the delivery of 188Re to tumors or enhance the radiopharmaceutical's pharmacokinetics by utilizing their ease of labeling and passive targeting properties (Cikankowitz et al. 2017; Lepareur et al. 2019). Vanpouille‐Box et al. implanted 188Re‐labeled lipid nanocapsules (LNC188Re‐SSS) into the brain and subjected them to fractionated irradiation in a rat orthotopic glioma model, resulting in a notable survival response with an 83% cure rate (Vanpouille‐Box et al. 2011). Using lipid nanocapsules to load 188Re offered the benefit of avoiding rapid elimination post‐injection. Moreover, nanoscale entities used for internal radiotherapy can interact with the immune response, potentially triggering T‐cell immunity. Additionally, there was a growing trend in combining 188Re with functional nanomaterials for multimodal diagnosis and therapy. Aslan and colleagues pioneered a 188Re‐labeled magnetite protein nanocage, using its magnetic component for MRI contrast, with coupling to carbonyl rhenium complexes via histidine groups cross‐linked with glutaraldehyde (Aslan, Aşık, and Volkan 2016). Subsequently, the toxicity of this nanocage was assessed on the MDA‐MB‐231 human breast metastatic adenocarcinoma cell line, revealing promising prospects for the Re carbonyl complexes attached to magnetic ferritin, characterized by high labeling efficiency and low toxicity. This study primarily focused on exploring novel synthesis methods and conducting toxicity analysis of the synthesized materials, rather than their use in breast cancer treatment. It laid the groundwork for potential future applications in radiotherapy. Furthermore, Chao et al. harnessed tungsten's radiation‐absorbing property to directly labeled 188Re onto polyethylene glycol‐coated tungsten disulfide (WS2) nanosheets, creating a self‐enhancing near‐infrared radionuclides treatment nanomaterial (188Re‐WS2‐PEG) without the use of chelating agents (Figure 4b; Chao et al. 2016). These nanosheets enhanced 188Re‐induced cell damage while utilizing WS2's near‐infrared photothermal response to improve intratumoral blood flow, overcoming tumor hypoxia‐related radiation resistance and significantly enhancing treatment efficacy. Furthermore, SPECT imaging revealed extensive distribution of 188Re‐WS2‐PEG in mice, with high levels of radioactive accumulation observed in the liver, spleen, and tumor 24 h post‐injection (Figure 4c).

Lutetium‐177

177Lu, a low‐energy β−‐emitting radionuclides with a 6.65‐day half‐life, generates gamma photons [γ = 113 (6.4%) and 208 keV (11%)] usable for imaging. Furthermore, the radioactive properties of 177Lu, such as the energy and abundance of emitted β particles and γ photons, along with its half‐life, make it suitable for use as a therapeutic radionuclide in targeting primary tumors and metastatic sites. Its potential use in therapy, involving localization and pre‐treatment imaging, has been explored alongside carrier molecules like inorganic chelators, peptides, antibodies, and small molecules for targeted radiopharmaceutical development in radiation therapy.

The combinational use of Au NPs with 177Lu was a common choice, as these radiolabeled Au NPs represented unique, multifunctional, and targeted radiopharmaceuticals that can serve as a combined radiotherapy system, thermal ablation system, and multimodal imaging agent (Banerjee, Pillai, and Knapp 2015). To investigate the impact of surface modification on 177Lu‐labeled Au NPs, Yook and colleagues designed a PEG metal chelate polymer (MCP) modification, using DOTA as a chelating agent to label 177Lu onto Au NPs for breast cancer therapy (Yook et al. 2016). They also explored the influence of thiol functionalization on material stability and biodistribution. The results revealed that the Au NPs functionalized with multiple thiol linkers exhibited greater serum stability. 177Lu‐MCP was less prone to dissociate from Au NPs in vivo, showed low liver uptake, and more than 90% of the radioactive material was excreted through urine during circulation. It also confirmed that the attachment of multiple thiol functional groups to AuNPs was the most promising approach for constructing stable 177Lu‐MCP‐AuNPs for breast cancer radiotherapy. Subsequently, they proceeded to construct a dual receptor‐targeted radiation nanomedicine labeled with 177Lu (DRT‐Au NPs‐177Lu) for treating human breast cancer cells co‐expressing HER2 and EGFR, and compared it with a single receptor‐targeted radiation nanomedicine (SRT‐Au NPs‐177Lu; Yook et al. 2020). This radiotherapeutic nanomedicine comprised 15 nm‐diameter AuNPs, which have been modified to include both TmAb and PmAb for dual HER2 and EGFR targeting, along with a polymer conjugated with DOTA for binding 177Lu (Figure 4d). The results revealed that the DRT drug was more effective in killing BC cells co‐expressing HER2 and EGFR, especially those resistant to HER2‐targeted therapy, compared to the SRT drug. Furthermore, this study demonstrated the attractiveness of targeting a greater receptor population on tumor cells, providing impetus for the development of other multi‐targeted radiation nanomedicines for cancer.

Porphyrin derivatives are molecular carriers with tumor activity, and when appropriately radiolabeled, they have the potential to serve as agents for targeted cancer therapy. For instance, Yu et al. developed an in vivo biologically controlled tumor‐mitochondria‐targeted theranostic nanoplatform (Porphyrin‐PEG Nanocomplexes, PPNs) and labeled it with 177Lu, integrating fluorescence imaging, photodynamic therapy, PET, and internal radiotherapy (RT; Yu et al. 2018). Within the study, PPNs of various sizes were synthesized by complexing inner chiral‐tetra (4‐carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (mTCPP) with 8‐arm amine‐polyethylene glycol (aPEG). Using PET measurements to gauge maximum tumor accumulation, it was observed that the optimized 16 nm PPNs attained an ideal tumor‐to‐muscle ratio (16.6 at 72 h post‐injection) and achieved a maximum tumor accumulation of 15.6% ID/g. This finding highlighted the enhanced coherence between nanoparticle size and tumor‐specific accumulation. Furthermore, this work introduced a straightforward approach for producing multi‐modal nano‐diagnostic agents tailored for tumor targeting, enabling precise and radionuclides‐based combined cancer therapy.

Yttrium‐90

90Y is a pure β‐emitter, releasing high‐energy β− (E max = 2.27 MeV, E mean = 939 keV) with each decay event (Cremonesi et al. 2006). It is commonly used as a therapeutic agent due to its high‐energy emissions, limited in vivo propagation range (about 11 mm in soft tissue), and a half‐life of 2.67 days suitable for tracking biological processes (Giammarile et al. 2011; Tickner et al. 2020). 90Y also emits low‐yield Bremsstrahlung and annihilation photons, detectable with nuclear imaging instruments, albeit with reduced sensitivity. Moreover, 90Y was known for its efficient Cherenkov luminescence production, making it a valuable tool in optical imaging (Liu et al. 2010).

As yttrium itself is a rare‐earth element, it was often labeled by doping it within materials built from other rare‐earth elements. For instance, using inorganic materials based on nanocrystals with the unique ability to customize size, shape, and composition, Paik et al. developed a synthetic method to design radiolabeled nanocrystals with precise control over size and shape (Paik et al. 2015). In this study, a range of 90Y‐doped rare‐earth fluoride nanocrystals with highly uniform morphologies, including nanosheets, nanowires, and bicones, were synthesized by adjusting reaction parameters such as time, temperature, and the rare‐earth host type (gadolinium or yttrium). The incorporation of 90Y within the host during the nanocrystal growth process prevented the leakage of radioactive nuclides from the nanocrystals and achieved a radiochemical purity exceeding 99%. Besides, the excellent radiolabeling stability of 90Y‐labeled nanocrystals when surface‐modified with various ligands offered the potential for further surface modifications in biomedical imaging research, enabling targeted or therapeutic applications. Additionally, 90Y‐labeled GdF3 nanosheets offered optical imaging capabilities along with multifunctional imaging (MFI) capabilities, demonstrating that shape‐controlled 90Y‐labeled nanocrystals provided a promising platform for multimodal imaging applications. Furthermore, the use of nanomaterials as carriers for combined chemotherapy drugs and 90Y had also shown promising results.

Besides, Werner et al. devised a biologically targeted nanoparticle therapy for treating ovarian cancer peritoneal metastasis, utilizing folate‐targeted nanoparticles that encapsulate paclitaxel (Ptxl) as a chemotherapy agent and incorporate 90Y for therapeutic radionuclides treatment (Werner et al. 2011). Compared to folate‐targeted NPs with single therapy or any nontargeted NP therapy, folate‐targeted NPs incorporating both radiotherapy and chemotherapy represented the most effective treatment. Furthermore, their combination with immune antibodies significantly induced apoptosis in secondary tumors. For example, Cai et al. developed 90Y‐labeled gold nanoparticles (90Y‐Au NPs) for treating triple‐negative breast cancer tumors and induced apoptosis in nonirradiated tumors either alone or in combination with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy (Cai et al. 2022). The results revealed that, compared to the unmarked 90Y‐Au NPs group, 90Y‐Au NPs could significantly inhibit the growth of primary tumors, but the suppression of secondary tumors was only achieved when combined with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy. The biodistribution data indicated higher accumulation of 90Y‐Au NPs in primary tumors, with very low uptake in metastatic tumors and normal tissues, except for the kidneys. This study demonstrated that combining radiopharmaceutical nanoparticles with systemically administered anti‐PD‐L1 checkpoint antibodies enhanced the growth inhibition of these nonirradiated secondary tumors, suggesting that distant effects might be immune‐mediated.

Nanoparticles Labeled With β+‐Emitting Radionuclides

Copper‐64

Copper‐based radioactive nuclides, such as 64Cu and 61Cu, are currently under assessment as ideal radioactive nuclides for PET imaging due to copper's mature coordination chemistry, which enables the utilization of various chelating agent systems capable of binding with biologically relevant molecules (Shokeen and Anderson 2009). 64Cu is one of the few useful metal positron‐emitting radioactive nuclides, with a relatively long half‐life of 12.7 h, enabling research for up to 48 h after administration. Moreover, due to its relatively low maximum positron energy (0.66 MeV) and a positron range similar to that of 18F, PET images obtained using 64Cu are of exceptional quality. In research, 64Cu was consistently used to achieve multimodal diagnostics, often combined with therapeutic nanomaterials to achieve integrated diagnosis and treatment effects, allowing for the exploration of the material's biodistribution and pharmacokinetics.

Petersen and his team used a novel ion carrier, 2‐hydroxyquinoline, to remotely load 64Cu, enabling the transport of Cu(II) across preformed liposomal membranes and its delivery to encapsulated copper chelator DOTA (Petersen et al. 2011). Using this ion carrier, highly efficient loading (95.5 ± 1.6%) and stability retention (> 99%) were achieved, making Cu liposomes exceptionally well‐suited for PET imaging agents. This method primarily utilized the radioactivity of 64Cu and the passive targeting capability of liposomes to enable nuclear PET imaging. However, there was a challenge of rapid capture by the liver and spleen system. In addition to the aforementioned standalone PET imaging, 64Cu was frequently combined with other functional materials to serve as adjunct diagnostic agents, achieving multimodal diagnostics. For instance, Ling et al. synthesized a series of nanoalloys TeSex with varying selenium doping ratios, investigating their biocompatibility and developing their diagnostic and therapeutic functionalities (Ling et al. 2021). The incorporation of different Se ratios was found to variably enhance the toxicity mitigation of Te materials. These synthesized nanoalloys exhibited photothermal effects in the near‐infrared II range, allowing for photothermal therapy guided by PT/PA/PET/CT imaging through surface coordination labeling with 64Cu (Figure 5a). In addition, the labeling efficacy of 64Cu was 90.6%, thereby demonstrating a high ratio between tumors and surrounding tissues in vivo. Using PET imaging, it was easy to assess the biological distribution and pharmacokinetics of TeSex, which primarily accumulated in organs like the liver, spleen, and kidneys (Figure 5b). Despite its continuous excretion, notable levels persisted in these organs even after 24 h, potentially posing sustained toxicity risks.

Besides, Zhao et al. prepared ultra‐small 64Cu‐doped gold nanoclusters (64CuAuNC) functionalized with AMD3100 (a targeting ligand) for CXCR4‐targeted PET imaging, targeting the upregulated receptor CXCR4 in both the primary tumor and lung metastasis in the mouse 4T1 model (Zhao et al. 2016). In engineered tumors expressing receptors at varying levels, PET imaging using 64CuAuNCs‐AMD3100 (4.5 ± 0.4 nm) showcased sensitive and precise CXCR4 detection, with competitive receptor blocking studies confirming the nanoclusters' target specificity. Furthermore, in vivo pharmacokinetic evaluations revealed favorable organ distribution within 48 h post‐injection, characterized by significant clearance through the kidneys and feces. Compared to nontargeted groups, targeted 64CuAuNCs‐AMD3100 detected upregulated CXCR4 in early tumor and premetastatic niches earlier and with greater sensitivity. In summary, these CXCR4‐targeted nanoclusters showed potential in translational research for early cancer and metastasis diagnosis and offered valuable insights for guiding breast cancer treatment through CXCR4 inhibition or chemotherapy.

Gallium‐68

Its high positron emission fraction (89%, E max: 1899 keV, E mean: 890 keV) and half‐life of 68 min provide sufficient radioactivity levels for high‐quality images while minimizing the radiation dose to the patient and staff, requiring a short scan time and allowing for repeated examinations. The coordination chemistry of 68Ga, 90Y, and 177Lu is similar, making it possible to use the same carrier molecules for subsequent radiotherapy. Besides, Ga with considerably higher positron energies can be expected to perform at lower resolution, and it is worth mentioning that 68Ga has a relatively short half‐life, allowing repeated examinations on the same day (Velikyan 2013; Velikyan et al. 2010).

Some researchers chose nanoemulsions as carriers for the labeling of 68Ga, which were characterized by nanoscale droplets formed through the mixing of two immiscible liquids, resulting in a unified single‐phase system. The advantages of nanoemulsions, such as their biocompatible compositions, ease of production through soft and scalable methods, and enhanced drug loading capacity in comparison to liposomes, have been significant factors driving their utilization in the field of biomedicine (Sánchez‐López et al. 2019; Singh et al. 2017). Diez‐Villares et al. described a simple and efficient method for the preparation of sphingomyelin nanoemulsions (SN) using a lipid derivative chelating agent and labeling them with 67Ga and 68Ga (Figure 5c; Díez‐Villares et al. 2021). It was found that the radiolabeled preparations exhibited excellent radiochemical properties in vivo and were effectively tracked by nuclear imaging instruments. Notably, NOTA‐SNs had a relatively long circulation time, showing 4% of the injected dose in the blood at 10 h after intravenous injection. This in vivo pattern differed from other modalities recently reported 68Ga‐labeled emulsions with a shorter circulating half‐life, mainly due to differences in size and composition. It has also been demonstrated that the biodistribution of SNs can be modulated by changing surface properties, with the potential to develop advanced probes for nuclear imaging and nanodiagnostics. Li et al. reported the design of a novel nanoplatform based on glucose‐modified dendrimer embedded gold nanoparticles (Au DENPs; Li et al. 2022). The nanoparticles were radiolabeled with 68Ga by DOTA‐chelating radionuclide and conjugated to cytosine–guanine (CpG) oligonucleotide for PET/CT imaging and tumor immunotherapy. Among them, 68Ga showed excellent labeling efficiency (≥ 85%) and stability (≥ 95%). PET/CT results revealed that, compared to NT‐68Ga‐Au DENPs without glucose, DG‐68Ga‐Au DENPs exhibited a higher accumulation at the tumor site, with a lower accumulation in the bladder. This was attributed to their greater tendency to accumulate at the tumor site, leading to renal clearance and reduced retention in the bladder. The study showed that a specially designed 68Ga‐labeled DG‐Au DENPs, a promising nanoplatform for therapeutic diagnostics, effectively treated tumor‐bearing mice.

Fluorine‐18

Fluorine‐18 was considered the gold standard for PET radionuclides in routine clinical studies, which has a half‐life of 110 min and was also considered a near‐ideal positron emitting radionuclide because of its nuclear and chemical properties. Its half‐life allows synthesis and imaging within hours, thus facilitating kinetic studies. In metabolic substrates without fluorine, the introduction of fluorine atoms does not cause significant spatial displacement. Moreover, the 18F positron energy was the lowest among the first row of positron emitters, so it can be imaged at the highest resolution (Czernin and Phelps 2002; Varagnolo et al. 2000). Among them, 18F‐2‐fluoro‐2‐deoxy‐d‐glucose (18F‐FDG) was one of the most widely used PET tracers for studying cellular metabolism. Excessive 18F‐FDG uptake had been demonstrated in most cancers in vivo due to the high metabolic turnover of tumor cells, making 18F‐FDG‐PET an excellent staging method in various malignancies (Hoffmann et al. 2003).

The challenge associated with 18F labeling lied in achieving effective PET imaging, which accumulated significantly in tumors and facilitates the swift clearance of nontarget tissue, enhancing the contrast between the tumor and background. A promising strategy was the use of 18F‐labeled polymeric agents for targeting and specific imaging of cancer (Zhang, Yan, et al. 2022). Goos et al. designed PH‐stimulation‐responsive polymers for delivery of the hypoxic PET tracer fluoro‐18‐labeled fluormisonidazole ([18F]FMISO), a linear poly (N‐(2‐(hydroxypropyl) methylacrylamide) polymer that formed PH‐sensitive acyl hydrazone bonds when coupled to a [18F]FMISO ketone analog (Goos et al. 2020). Most HPMA polymers with a molecular weight of 35–40 kDa were cleared from the blood within 6–8 h after injection, matching the imagable time at the range of 3–4 half‐lives of 18F. After exposing the polymers to acidic conditions in the tumor microenvironment, compared with normoxic cells used for hypoxic PET imaging, F‐FMISO was rapidly released and mainly retained in hypoxic cells. Besides, Berke et al. developed 18F‐radiolabeled organosilicon fluoride receptor‐derived polymeric core–shell nanoparticles (SiFA) with different hydrodynamic diameters (Figure 5d; Berke et al. 2018). They found that nanoparticles with a 33‐nm particle size had the highest tumor‐muscle ratio at 4 h post‐injection. 18F PET was also used to monitor the anti‐tumor effect of paclitaxel‐loaded polymeric nanoparticles on A549 human alveolar basal epithelial cells.

Clinical Trials and Prospects of Nanoradionuclides

Due to their unique physicochemical and biological properties, NPs have been rapidly developed in the field of radiodiagnosis and treatment, both as a radionuclide delivery platform and as an adjuvant therapy. To date, many efforts have also been focused on translating NPs based radiopharyngology into clinical practice. However, in reality, their full application in diagnosis and treatment has been delayed due to technical and biological limitations, and none of them has been approved by the FDA.

Nanoradionuclides in Clinical Trials

In recent years, the FDA has approved several radiopharmaceuticals. Here, we summarize some of the radiopharmaceuticals and their target applications. The recent approval by the FDA of the luteal‐labeled somatostatin analog (177Lu‐dotatate, Lutathera) for gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEPNETs) positive for somatostatin receptors, 18F‐Piflufolastat used for identifying suspected metastases or recurrence in prostate cancer, 177Lu‐PSMA‐617 for metastatic prostate‐specific membrane antigen‐positive prostate cancer, 90Y Resin Sphere used for inoperable colorectal liver metastases, and 68Ga Gozetotide for PET identification of PSMA‐positive lesions in adult patients with mCRPC have revitalized the field. Coincidentally, this renewed interest in radiomedicine has aligned with a growing enthusiasm in nanomedicine, potentially facilitating an intersection between these two paradigms. Among the current nanomedicines in clinical practice, there exist instances of organic NPs undergoing clinical trials. Nanoliposomes, as a drug delivery system, possess numerous advantages such as uncomplicated formulation, self‐assembly capability, biocompatibility, high bioavailability, and a range of controllable physical and chemical properties that can be tailored to modulate their biological characteristics.

Additionally, lipid nanoparticles were the most prevalent class of nanomedicines approved by the FDA due to their numerous advantages. For instance, Doxil, the first FDA‐approved anticancer nanomedicine in 1995, was a PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin hydrochloride nanoparticle that effectively treated adult cancers such as ovarian cancer, multiple myeloma, and Karasee's sarcoma. It not only prolonged the drug's half‐life but also mitigated cardiac toxicity. In 2018, Onpattro, the world's first small interfering nucleic acid (siRNA) drug encapsulated in a nanoliposome developed by an American pharmaceutical company, received FDA approval. This groundbreaking medication targeted abnormal transthyretin (TTR) RNA to address peripheral nerve disease caused by hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis in adult patients. It represented the pioneering RNA interference (RNAi) therapy sanctioned by the FDA and remaind the sole approved treatment for peripheral nerve diseases. Despite numerous nanomedicines gaining FDA approval, no nanoradionuclide drugs have been authorized thus far. However, there were a few nanoradionuclides entering clinical trials.

In these few clinical cases, the researchers from Taipei Veterans General Hospital, had developed a nano‐liposome radiopharmaceutical (Wang et al. 2019). The 188Re‐BMEDA liposomes, marked with 188Re, can effectively target tumors. Encouraging data on tumor targeting, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, preclinical efficacy, and toxicity in animals led to a low‐level human clinical trial (NCT02271516). However, it was terminated by radioactive accumulation in the spleen and liver. Subsequently, Phillips and colleagues described the translation of ultra‐small (< 10 nm) inorganic nanoparticles (C dots) from animals to patients (Phillips et al. 2014). These C dots consisted of a silicon shell encapsulating a fluorescent Cy5 dye, coated with PEG, and decorated with a radiolabeled peptide targeting integrins (124I‐cRGDY). The primary objective of this first‐in‐human study was to explore the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of 124I‐cRGDY‐PEG‐C dots in the human body after injection, with molecular targeting and cancer imaging as secondary goals. This study initiated a microdose investigation in five human subjects with metastatic melanoma. All participants underwent three PET‐CT imaging assessments within 72 h post‐injection. By measuring average organ absorbed doses for all patients, it was found that the particles were primarily excreted through the kidneys. Compared to other tissues, the kidneys and bladder wall (post‐thyroid) exhibited the two highest average absorbed dose values at 72 h post‐injection. In patient 2, intravenous injection of 124I‐cRGDY‐PEG‐C dots revealed a ~5 mm distinctly bordered cystic lesion in the right anterior lobe of the pituitary. This was visible on axial and sagittal MR images and confirmed by multiplanar MRI and CT images. Initially considered a focus of intense activity, the intensity gradually increased at 72‐h intervals, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in surrounding background signal. This resulted in a high tumor‐to‐background ratio (500 with the brain; 50 with the liver). The increase in signal reflected the net accumulation of particle activity within the lesion over time. Data from the five melanoma patients suggested that this targeted inorganic imaging agent exhibited favorable pharmacokinetics, biodistribution characteristics, and safety. This study opened doors for the use of similar nanoparticles in humans.

To address the issue of highly heterogeneous tumor drug accumulation and identify methods for stratifying patients before treatment, Miedema et al. radiolabeled docetaxel‐encapsulated polymeric nanoparticles (89Zr‐CPC634) and conducted experiments on seven patients using PET/CT with two different doses, therapeutic and diagnostic doses, to investigate their accumulation at tumor sites (NCT03712423) (Miedema et al. 2022). To visualize the nanomaterials in the tumor, patients were imaged according to the treatment protocol (Figure 6a). After the baseline 18F‐FDG PET/CT, patients received the first cycle of CPC634, followed by administration of 89Zr‐CPC634, and underwent PET/CT scans at different time points. Furthermore, the results showed that the average pharmacokinetic half‐life of the high therapeutic dose group was 97.0 ± 14.4 h, and for the diagnostic dose, it was 62.4 ± 12.9 h. Subsequently, biodistribution analysis revealed the accumulation of 89Zr‐CPC634 in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow, which were known sites for nanoparticle uptake and elimination (Figure 6b). Over time, there was a decrease in the dose in the kidneys, suggesting that the kidneys may be the major excretory organ. Subsequently, the accumulation of 89Zr‐CPC634 at the tumor site was analyzed and observed (Figure 6c), and tumor accumulation was evident as early as 24 h post‐injection, significantly increased between 24 and 96 h, and remained stable between 96 and 144 h. Importantly, tumor accumulation of 89Zr‐CPC634 with lower doses may vary significantly within and between patients, partially explaining why nanodrug treatments often result in relatively disappointing clinical outcomes.

Apart from this, Lee et al. used 64Cu to label nanoparticles, resulting in 64Cu‐MM‐302(64Cu‐labeled HER2‐targeted PEG‐liposomal doxorubicin), and performed PET/CT imaging in 2017 (Lee et al. 2017). The aim of this study was to understand the biodistribution in metastatic breast cancer patients and assess the potential impact of the EPR effect on nanotherapy, in order to determine whether the ultimate tumor lesion delivery could be used to predict treatment outcomes. Two to three PET/CT scans were performed in 19 patients with HER2‐positive metastatic breast cancer after injection of 64Cu‐MM‐302 as part of a clinical trial of MM‐302 plus trastuzumab with or without cyclophosphamide (NCT01304797). Right after injection, the activity of 64Cu‐MM‐302 was predominantly localized in the blood pool due to the prolonged circulation characteristic of liposomes. By the second and third days, noticeable uptake of 64Cu‐MM‐302 was observed in the normal spleen and liver, as well as in various tumor lesions. A HER2‐positive breast cancer patient underwent three PET/CT scans at 0.7, 24 and 47 h post‐injection (patient 06) to examine the lesion deposition kinetics of 64Cu‐MM‐302. Compared to the signal from tumor tissue, the ratio of the tumor 64Cu‐MM‐302 signal generated by vasculature to the deposited 64Cu‐MM‐302 signal was minimal on days 2 to 3. This suggested that most of the 64Cu tumor signal after day 1 can be attributed to the deposition of 64Cu‐MM‐302 in the tumor tissue, which clearly indicates the presence of the EPR effect in human metastatic tumors. In conclusion, these data suggest the potential use of pretherapeutic imaging of nanoparticle deposition in tumors as a means to identify patients most likely to benefit from therapeutic nanoparticle treatment.

Additionally, Zheng et al. conducted the first study on 89Zr‐HDL in human oncology, radiolabeling the high‐density lipoprotein analog CER001 with 89Zr and utilizing PET/CT to assess the accumulation of administered high‐density lipoprotein nanoparticles in primary esophageal tumors (Figure 7a) (K. H. Zheng et al. 2022). Subsequent analysis focused on the biodistribution of 89Zr‐HDL (Figure 7b). Similar to Figure 6b, consecutive PET/CT imaging examinations revealed clear radioactive tracer signals in the blood pool, liver, spleen, and kidneys, confirming the kidneys as the main site of HDL breakdown and metabolism, showing similar characteristics in both cases. Besides, the study revealed that after administering 89Zr‐HDL, continuous PET/CT demonstrated the accumulation of HDL nanoparticles in esophageal tumors over time. Throughout the 72‐h study period, 89Zr‐HDL showed good tolerability with no reported adverse events. Traditionally, the extravasation of nanoparticles into tumors was believed to rely on the passive accumulation through the EPR effect. Subsequently, using clinical 18F‐FDG PET/CT scans, the study observed a distinct focal uptake pattern of esophageal tumors in 89Zr‐HDL PET/CT scans starting from 24 h (Figure 7c). Quantitative results indicated no correlation between 18F‐FDG uptake and 89Zr‐HDL uptake in the tumors (Figure 7d). These findings suggest that the accumulation of high‐density lipoprotein nanoparticles in esophageal cancer involves specific mechanisms rather than relying solely on passive vascular leakage.

Besides, Doughton et al. conducted the first human study of 68Ga‐nanocolloid PET/CT for imaging sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) in prostate cancer (Doughton et al. 2018). The PET tracer (iron oxide nanoparticle labeled with 68Ga) was injected transperineally into the prostate under ultrasound guidance after placing a reference point. The results showed successful identification of SLNs in all five participants without observed adverse reactions. In one subject, after injecting the tracer into the prostate, 45‐min imaging revealed focal uptake in the internal iliac SLN, inguinal SLN, and terminal thoracic duct near the left subclavian vein, with no intervention SLN stations identified in the abdomen or chest (Figure 7e). Additionally, a potential unexpected pathway for malignant cell transport and the expected regional drainage pathways were discovered when considering all participants. The tracer rapidly drained to pelvic bones, perivesical area, mesorectum, inguinal, and Virchow lymph nodes, demonstrating the successful use of 68Ga‐Nanocolloid PET/CT for SLN identification.

In 2023, Peking Union Medical College Hospital initiated a clinical trial of 64Cu/68Ga labeled (Evans blue) EB‐ss‐CPT (camptothecin) PET/CT scanning in colorectal cancer (NCT05891028). The amphiphilic EB‐ss‐CPT can form nanoparticles through self‐assembly in tumor tissues, bind to albumin in vivo, and release CPT by breaking the disulfide bond, which effectively enhances the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of camptothecin, thereby improving the therapeutic effect and increasing the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to drugs. In this study, 64Cu‐NOTA‐EB‐ss‐CPT (64Cu‐EB‐CPT) by 64Cu‐labeled EB‐ss‐CPT alone for PET/CT imaging, showed better tumor enrichment compared to the 64Cu‐labeled CPT group. EB‐CPT was expected to be more enriched in tumor tissue through the EPR effect of its nanoparticles.

In addition to nanoradionuclides, some nanomaterials containing certain special metals have also been used in clinical radiotherapy. For example, the M.D. Anderson Cancer Center initiated a clinical trial (NCT04505267) involving hafnium oxide‐containing nanoparticles NBTXR3 for radiation therapy of inoperable recurrent nonsmall cell lung cancer in 2020. NBTXR3 was a radioenhancer designed to increase the radiation dose deposition within tumor cells. Radiation therapy employs high‐energy x‐rays to kill cancer cells and reduce tumor size. Administering NBTXR3 alongside radiation therapy may enhance radiation‐dependent killing of tumor cells without increasing radiation exposure to healthy surrounding tissues. In 2021, Centre Jean Perrin had launched a clinical trial to assess the synergy of polysiloxane Gd chelates based nanoparticles (AGuIX) with radiotherapy and concomitant temozolomide in treating newly diagnosed glioblastoma. The primary objectives of this study were to determine the recommended dose of AGuIX in combination with radiotherapy and temozolomide (TMZ) during the concomitant radiochemotherapy period (phase I) and to assess the efficacy of the radiochemotherapy+AGuIX combination (at the recommended dose) by measuring the 6‐month progression‐free survival rate (PFS) (phase II).

Advantages of Nanoradionuclide

Multiple modes can be introduced in the same nanoparticle with minimal interference between each mode, making them effective as multimodal imaging contrast agents to provide complementary information for precise disease diagnosis. Inorganic NPs, for example, offer additional possibilities for multimodal imaging. Among them, magnetic NPs can be used as MRI contrast agents, Au NPs as x‐ray imaging contrast agents, and C‐based NPs as fluorescence imaging contrast agents. When they are combined with radionuclides, the effect of multimodal imaging can be achieved, which makes the diagnosis more concrete. For example, Lee et al. developed a probe to develop a dual‐functional iron oxide (IO) nanoparticle for PET and MRI scanning of tumor integrin αvβ3 expression, allowing PET and MRI imaging to be combined to monitor the biodistribution and tumor accumulation of NPs in U87MG tumor‐bearing mice (Lee et al. 2008). The results showed that the untargeted 64Cu‐IO NPs mainly accumulated in the liver, while 64Cu‐RGD‐IO NPs accumulated in large quantities in the tumor (9.8% ID g−1). In addition, the uptake of 64Cu‐RGD‐IO NPs was significantly reduced in the presence of the blocking dose of the RGD peptide, which showed the specificity of the targeting. Sun et al. prepared a self‐emitting QDS system by doping the positron‐emitting radionuclide 64Cu into CdSe/ZnS core/shell QDS via a cation exchange reaction (Sun et al. 2014). The nanoprobe showed high tumor targeting ability (12.7% ID/g at 17 h p.i.) in the U87MG glioblastoma xenograft model, and could perform in vivo tumor luminescence imaging without excitation light. This protocol combined the optical imaging of radionuclides with nanomaterials, which provided a favorable reference for multimodal imaging.

In addition, nanomaterials can be designed to enhance tumor retention time. Small molecule or peptide‐labeled radionuclides tend to have short blood half‐lives, short retention time at the tumor site, and rapid metabolism, whereas nanoradionuclide can be designed to enhance their retention at the tumor site. However, when it comes to antibodies, they exhibited a longer blood half‐life, necessitating the use of radionuclides with extended half‐lives, such as 89Zr or 124I, for radiolabeling. Moreover, antibodies have a tendency to accumulate in normal organs and tissues, such as the liver, spleen, and bone marrow, leading to suboptimal tumor‐to‐background ratios in imaging and the potential for false‐positive diagnoses. In comparison, nanomaterials offer a more extensive range of options for selecting radionuclides, allowing the choice of nanocarriers that align more closely with the specific half‐life of the selected isotope. Additionally, a diverse range of nanomedicines can achieve controlled drug release through various responsive mechanisms, which was not achievable with other radiolabeled drugs. Compared with small molecules, the use of nanoparticles as imaging agents or contrast carriers can improve biodistribution and enhance retention by increasing the particle size of the carrier, which was retained in tumors by in situ assembly, as opposed to degradation. The team of Hai et al. successfully developed gadolinium‐based particles that exhibit enhanced detectability on MRI scans (Hai et al. 2019). These particles were assembled in the presence of gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) and glutathione (GSH), resulting in a significantly higher uptake level compared to conventional gadolinium‐based contrast agents. Bulte and colleagues used a similar assembly approach to pair the cell‐penetrating peptide RVRR with the anticancer drug Olsalazine (Olsa‐RVRR: Ac‐Arg‐Val‐Arg‐Arg‐Cys (StBu)‐Lys (Olsalazine)‐CBT; Yuan et al. 2019). Upon cellular entry, Olsa‐RVRR undergoes reduction by GSH and subsequent cleavage by furin. Consequently, the particles self‐assemble through dimerization and π–π stacking interactions of the cleaved RVRR‐Olsa. However, Lin et al. developed a low molecular weight 18F‐labeled activable probe capable of dimerizing to form hydrophobic substances under reducing conditions (Lin et al. 2017). Under physiological conditions, hydrophobic material condensates into 120–140 nm particles due to π–π interactions, thus demonstrating that dimer formation and subsequent in situ condensation into nanoparticles enhances the concentration of the radioactive signal. In addition, lin et al. reported a similar peptide probe for PET imaging of tumor cell apoptosis where the probe was activated by caspase‐3, undergoes intermolecular cyclization, and subsequently forms approximately138 nm particles that enhance their accumulation and retention time at the tumor site (Wang, Gao, et al. 2020).

In addition to the points mentioned above, radionuclides can be combined with functional nanomaterials, thereby achieving multi‐modal diagnosis and treatment. The emerging treatment approach of Cerenkov radiation (CR)‐PDT utilizes functional nanomaterials to both image and treat tumors by activating photosensitizers with CR from radionuclides, resulting in the production of ROS and subsequent tissue damage or ischemia. Due to the variability of CR in radionuclides, it was safe to say that ROS production by high‐energy emitters was more efficient due to the presence of CR. Among them, Duan et al. first reported the use of 68Ga as a CR source to activate oxide (TiO2) NPs for PDT while labeling with 18F as a contrast (Duan et al. 2018). It was found that 68Ga‐BSA promoted more effective growth inhibition and had a better therapeutic effect than 18F‐FDG in vivo. In addition, (Zn0.4Mn0.6)Fe2O4 nanoparticles (MNPs) were guided to the desired tissue by an internal magnetic field, and the MNPs were labeled unchelated with 89Zr as the source of CR and bound to the porphyrin molecules of mesotetrayl (4 carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (TCPP) to stimulate ROS‐mediated tumor cell damage (Ni et al. 2018).

Besides, apart from external stimulus therapies (such as PDT/PTT/SDT, etc.), radionuclides were often combined with immunotherapy. Recent research has demonstrated that radiotherapy alone can elicit an immune‐stimulating effect. The primary objective of radiation was to induce DNA damage, and abnormal DNA damage repair led to a deficiency in DNA damage response (DDR), which was a crucial determinant of tumor immunogenicity in recent findings. Whether through directly inducing immunogenic cell death in tumor cells or by altering the tumor microenvironment, radiation can enhance the initiation and effector phases of the immune response (Ozpiskin, Zhang, and Li 2019). Expect for inducing cell death, the effective anti‐tumor immune response induced by irradiation was also a key factor in its therapeutic success. In addition to the inherent immunomodulatory effects of therapeutic nuclides mentioned earlier, for diagnostic nuclides, nanomaterials can serve as carriers to co‐administer nuclides with immunoadjuvants, achieving a combined function with the immune response. For example, Li et al. utilized DOTA to label 68Ga in gold nanoparticles embedded with CpG, enabling PET/CT dual‐modal imaging and immunotherapy for tumors (Li et al. 2022).

Challenges and Future Directions of Nanoradionuclides

It was clear that the fusion of nanotechnology and nuclear medicine has driven a large number of cancer imaging and treatment paradigms, and nano‐radiation offers profound implications for the development of nuclear medicine, but the inadequacy of nanomaterials itself limits their development in clinical aspects. There was an overview of what we perceive as the challenges in nanoradionuclides, along with some ideas and possibilities for overcoming them.

The nanomaterials may be altered upon incorporation of radionuclides. Although the structure of nanomaterials was stable, and radionuclides were commonly incorporated into nanomaterials by replacing cold elements with hot elements to obtain nanoradionuclides. The original crystal form, size, or morphology of the materials may undergo alterations during the synthesis process, either due to the change of synthesis conditions or the incorporation of radionuclide raw materials (Yang et al. 2014). If the chelating agent was used as a medium, it is easy to cause off‐target phenomenon, or change the function of the original material. This challenge remains for nanoradionuclides formulations unless the radionuclides were encapsulated in core‐shell nanostructures, or no chelating agents are used. In order to prevent the release of radionuclides, it may be possible to consider wrapping radionuclides in nanostructures, and using a chelator‐free way is certainly a good option. In addition, the half‐lives of the radionuclides used must be considered, as some reactions and surface modification procedures may require longer than one half‐life of the radionuclides, thus radionuclides with shorter half‐lives could cause difficulties during the nanomaterial synthesis. This reminded us that the matching half‐lives between nanocarriers and radionuclides could be important.

Besides, the long‐term toxicity of nanocarriers needed to be addressed urgently. In comparative studies in which the size was kept constant while other parameters changed, it was found that the cytotoxicity of NP was size‐dependent, and in general, the smaller the NP, the higher its cytotoxicity (Xia et al. 2011). This may be due to the fact that a smaller nanomaterial size increases its specific surface area, allowing more atoms to be exposed, thereby increasing the number of structural defects. In addition, size also affects its uptake by target cells (Verma and Stellacci 2010). Cell uptake was slower for larger materials, and large NPs (more than ~200 nm) were easily detected by the immune system and captured by the liver and spleen (Chomoucka et al. 2010) and very small NPs (less than 5–15 nm) can be excreted through renal filtration (Choi et al. 2007). Therefore, the optimal NP size range for intravenous (i.v.) delivery was between 10 and 100 nm, as these NPs had the longest blood circulation time (Rivera‐Gil et al. 2013). However, the threshold of NP sizes must be carefully interpreted, and they were only effective for non‐aggregated NP. It is important to balance the relationship between the two and the materials with different sizes will accumulate in various organs to different degrees. In addition, the toxicity of nanomaterials was closely related to their inherent properties (Wick et al. 2007).

Colloidal stability was a crucial physicochemical parameter for nanomaterials. It served as a significant criterion for assessing their performance, influencing various other parameters. Therefore, it played a pivotal role in cell toxicity. As a nano‐carrier, the stability of the material directly influences the extent of drug leakage, thereby affecting therapeutic or imaging outcomes. Nonetheless, it is crucial to highlight that excessive material stability could potentially result in metabolic issues, creating a scenario where entry was facilitated, but elimination became challenging. Additionally, the long‐term toxic effects of nondegradable materials should not be overlooked. Therefore, in designing nano‐carriers, a balance must be achieved between stability and toxicity to ensure an enhancement in efficacy while minimizing adverse impacts. Therefore, research has focused on synthesizing materials with various responsiveness, allowing them to fulfill drug delivery tasks while undergoing structural deconstruction for degradation, facilitating elimination, and reducing material toxicity. This can be achieved by incorporating internal responsive elements (such as pH‐sensitive components, small molecules, enzymes, etc.) or external stimuli‐responsive materials (such as light, sound, electricity, etc.). Utilizing the pH difference between tumor sites and normal tissues, polymers with functional groups were employed as nanoparticle carriers, wherein these functional groups can alter charge density based on pH fluctuations. Examples include poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), poly(acrylamide) (PAAm), poly(methyl acrylate) (PMA), poly(methacrylic acid) (PMAA), and so forth. The structure and hydrophobicity of the nano‐particle carrier can be modified through protonation or deprotonation (Bawa et al. 2009). Su et al. developed camptothecin (CPT) nanoparticles (NPs) coated with dopamine (PDA) and loaded with manganese dioxide (MnO2), releasing the drug at low pH and facilitating self‐deconstruction for elimination through the liver and kidneys (Su et al. 2022). Alternatively, silicon nanoparticles can be employed, as they can biodegrade into orthosilicic acid (Si(OH)4) and naturally excrete through urine.

Achieving targeted delivery of nanomaterials to specific sites also faced challenges. Research indicated that nano‐carriers can exploit the EPR effect to reach tumor sites in laboratory animals. However, the clinical efficacy of the EPR effect was often suboptimal due to heterogeneity (Chen et al. 2016). It was also precisely due to heterogeneity issues that the favorable traits observed in experiments did not translate into promising outcomes in clinical trials. Consequently, most nano‐radiotherapeutics rely on attaching targeting peptides or antibodies to achieve the desired targeting, which poses challenges, particularly for nuclides with a short half‐life. Leveraging the recent advancements in nanochemistry, the surface of nanoparticles can be functionalized through the attachment of conjugation groups. These groups should rapidly couple with complementary conjugation groups on targeting peptides or antibodies. Furthermore, during the circulatory process within the body, the connected targeting small molecules may experience off‐target effects, hindering the complete delivery of certain materials to the tumor site. This off‐target issue posed a limitation to the clinical application of targeting small molecules. To address this, thoughtful consideration in the design of connection methods was crucial, favoring covalent bonding or utilizing structural enveloping, such as cell membranes or exosomes. These approaches aim to enhance homing reactions, allowing the targeting molecules to autonomously navigate towards the desired locations. Alternatively, opting for magnetic nano‐carriers and controlling their targeting to tissues by applying an external magnetic field also represented a promising choice.

The capture of nanomaterials by the reticuloendothelial system was also a critical consideration in their clinical application. Despite the advantageous size that allows nanomaterials to easily enter and accumulate in tumor sites, this represented only a small percentage. The substantial capture of nanomaterials by the spleen and liver poses a significant obstacle, primarily due to interactions between nano‐carriers and immune cells, as well as the surrounding microenvironment. Following injection, nano‐carriers swiftly accumulate in the liver and spleen during blood circulation, resulting in their clearance and low absorption in the target organs intended for therapeutic purposes. The challenge of evading capture by the reticuloendothelial system remains a crucial issue that requires prompt resolution as experimental studies move towards clinical applications. Brenner et al., for example, utilized red blood cells to achieve evasion of immune cell clearance, thereby extending the half‐life of the material (Brenner et al. 2018). This study involved adsorbing nanomaterials onto the surface of red blood cells and investigating their distribution among various organs after different methods of injection into the body. Following intravenous injection, the materials first reached the lungs, and compared to the group with unadsorbed red blood cells, the lung‐to‐liver uptake ratio significantly increased. In addition to this approach, an alternative method involves improving the delivery of NPs by altering the injection threshold of the material, disrupting cellular uptake, and reducing uptake in the liver (Ouyang et al. 2020). Alternatively, adjusting the material's hardness can facilitate evasion of immune cells, altering its biodistribution. Studies have indicated that soft radiolabeled nanomaterials exhibit longer blood circulation times compared to their hard counterparts, thereby reducing immune cell absorption (Desai et al. 2022a). Besides, quality control and large‐scale production of nanomaterials pose crucial challenges for clinical translation. Minimizing differences between batches of nanomaterials was also a critical consideration in research. In the experimental stage, nanoradionuclides were often produced in small quantities. To scale up production, it is essential to ensure that nanomaterials are cost‐effective, synthesized under mild conditions, and have short labeling times.

Conclusion

This concise review tried to give an introduction on the emerging concept of transitioning radioactive nanomaterials from the laboratory to clinical applications. It summarized the main labeling methods for radioactive nanomaterials with both nonchelating and chelating agents, providing a focused classification and discussion on the properties of radioactive nuclides themselves, along with representative examples of their combination with nanoparticles. This article also emphasized the current clinical status of radioactive nanomaterials and highlighted some notable clinical trials. Summarizing the advantages and limitations of radioactive nanomaterials, it was noted that nanoparticles can carry high radionuclide payloads, offer flexibility in radiolabel selection, and allow control over physicochemical parameters. These features could be targeted and modified to enhance radionuclide delivery at the target site, improve tissue penetration, and enhance the efficiency of radiotherapy. While these advancements were still in the early stages, they marked each accumulating step toward the clinical translation of nanoradionuclides.
