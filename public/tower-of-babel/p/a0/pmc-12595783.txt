Association between nicotine-dependent patients and delirium in intensive care units: a retrospective cohort study using a large clinical database
Authors: Mengqing Yang, Zhengxin Hu, Yun Huang, Guohao Zhang, Fengguang Kang, Dezhu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough nicotine use is a significant and potentially modifiable risk factor for delirium in ICU patients, evidence on the association between nicotine dependence (ND) and ICU delirium remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the MIMIC-IV v3.1 database and included adult patients admitted to the ICU for ≥ 24 h with documented delirium assessments. Patients with schizophrenia, dementia, depression, alcohol abuse, or cerebrovascular disease were excluded. Patients were classified into ND and non-ND groups. The primary outcome was ICU delirium incidence; secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, ICU mortality, hospital length of stay, and ICU length of stay. Confounders were balanced using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM). Cox regression and competing risk models were used to assess the association between ND and delirium. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, and the effects of ICD coding versions and NRT treatment were also evaluated.ResultsA total of 24,043 patients were included, with 2,662 (11.07%) in the ND group. After PSM, baseline characteristics were balanced. The ND group had a significantly higher incidence of delirium than the non-ND group (30.8% vs. 27.2%, P = 0.004), with no significant differences in mortality or length of stay. Multivariate Cox and competing risk models confirmed that ND was independently associated with increased ICU delirium risk. Findings remained consistent in PSM and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionNicotine dependence significantly increases the risk of ICU delirium. These results highlight the need for targeted risk assessment and intervention strategies in ND patients admitted to the ICU.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07507-7.

Background

Nicotine dependence (ND), also known as tobacco dependence, is a neuroadaptive condition resulting from prolonged nicotine use, characterized by strong cravings for smoking, withdrawal symptoms (such as anxiety, depression, and cravings), and a tendency to relapse [1]. It affects more than 500 million individuals worldwide, and nearly half of daily smokers suffer from ND, significantly lowering smoking cessation success rates and posing persistent health threats [2]. In the United States, the prevalence of ND ranges from 22.8% to 63.6%, depending on the assessment criteria. Individuals with severe dependence often experience a decline in quality of life, reduced work efficiency, and increased consumption of medical resources [3]. ND is widely recognized as a chronic disease and frequently coexists with psychiatric disorders, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, and cancer [4–6]. Evidence indicates that long-term smoking significantly increases the risk of ICU admission after surgery, and heavy smokers are more susceptible to concurrent pulmonary conditions such as pneumonia and respiratory failure [7]. Delirium, an acute brain dysfunction triggered by multiple factors, is typically characterized by fluctuations in consciousness and cognitive function, including inattention, disorientation, and short-term memory impairment [8]. A multicenter cross-sectional study reported a 32.3% prevalence of delirium in ICU settings, with sedative use and invasive procedures identified as key precipitants [9]. In a prospective study involving ICU patients without mechanical ventilation, nearly 50% developed delirium [10], whereas the incidence among mechanically ventilated patients reached as high as 83% [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that nicotine and delirium may share a common neurobiological mechanism centered around acetylcholine imbalance, indicating a potential pathophysiological association between the two [12–14].

Previous studies have identified nicotine use as a significant and modifiable risk factor for ICU delirium [15]. Some research has found a significantly elevated risk of delirium among smokers during ICU stays [16], while others have failed to observe a statistically significant relationship [17]. Therefore, the current body of evidence remains insufficient to conclusively determine whether smoking is an independent risk factor for delirium in ICU or hospitalized patients [18]. In particular, studies explicitly examining the association between ND and delirium remain limited, and the lack of quantitative data poses challenges to clinical risk stratification and the development of targeted interventions. To address this gap, the present study employed a retrospective cohort design using a large-scale ICU database to investigate the association between ND and ICU delirium. By systematically quantifying this relationship, we aim to provide robust epidemiological evidence to support clinical identification of high-risk populations and inform the development of effective intervention strategies.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were obtained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV version 3.1 (MIMIC-IV v3.1) database, which contains detailed clinical records of patients admitted to the emergency department or intensive care unit (ICU) at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, USA, from 2008 to 2022. This publicly available, large-scale database anonymizes all personal identifiers to ensure patient privacy, thus waiving the requirement for informed consent and Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval [19]. All researchers involved in this study completed the mandatory training provided by the database custodians (Certificate Number: 13622593) and were granted access authorization.

Study population

We included all adult patients who were admitted to the ICU for the first time and had an ICU stay of at least 24 h. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) ICU stay < 24 h; (3) absence of documented delirium assessment; (4) RASS score < − 3; (5) hospital stay shorter than ICU stay; and (6) diagnosis of schizophrenia, dementia, depression, alcoholism, or cerebrovascular disease. ND was identified based on the criteria defined in the Fifth Edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [20], using ICD-9 code 305.1 and ICD-10 codes in the F17.2 series. A complete list of the ICD codes used can be found in Supplementary Table S6.

Data extraction

All variables were retrieved using Structured Query Language (SQL) on the Navicat (17.0.11) platform. The extracted data included: demographics, laboratory test results within 24 h after ICU admission, complications occurring during the ICU stay, past medical history, ICU interventions, and disease severity scores. (1) Demographic variables included age, gender, and race; (2) Laboratory parameters encompassed white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelets, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, sodium, chloride, bicarbonate, potassium, and anion gap; (3) Complications during the ICU period included acute respiratory failure, sepsis, and acute kidney injury (AKI); (4) Past medical history covered hypertension, diabetes, congestive heart failure, severe liver disease, malignant tumor, chronic lung disease, and pneumonia; (5) Interventions after ICU admission included mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, propofol, midazolam, nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), and the use of benzodiazepines; (6) Severity scores included the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPSII), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). For the identification of delirium, patients were first required to have a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score ≥ −3 to ensure sufficient arousal for assessment of attention, thinking, and consciousness [21]. Delirium was then assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU), which includes four core features: Feature 1 refers to acute changes or fluctuation in mental status, Feature 2 is inattention, Feature 3 is altered level of consciousness, and Feature 4 is disorganized thinking [22]. A positive CAM-ICU diagnosis was established if the patient met the combination of Feature 1 and Feature 2, along with either Feature 3 or Feature 4.

Outcome

The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of delirium during the ICU stay. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, length of ICU stay, and total length of hospital stay.

Data analysis

For this observational study using MIMIC-IV data, SQL was used to extract relevant patient information. Patients were categorized into the ND group and the non-ND group based on whether a diagnosis of ND was present. To minimize bias from missing data, variables with more than 15% missing values were excluded, and multiple imputation was applied to the remaining missing entries using the most appropriate dataset. Continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR), and group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage, and comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. To minimize baseline imbalances and control for confounding, PSM was conducted to match ND and non-ND patients in a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.05. Matching effectiveness was evaluated using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance between groups. To further reduce confounding, four Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to assess the association between ND and ICU delirium before and after PSM: (1) an unadjusted model; (2) Model I, adjusted for age, gender, and race; (3) Model II, additionally adjusted for comorbidities; and (4) Model III, fully adjusted for all covariates. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The association between ND and secondary outcomes was examined using multivariate Cox regression and linear regression models. Considering ICU mortality as a competing risk, Fine and Gray competing risk regression was used to compare the incidence of delirium between the two groups, both before and after PSM. Univariate and multivariate competing risk models were employed, adjusting for all covariates. To assess the robustness of the results, three sensitivity analysis methods were used to control for confounding: multivariate Cox regression adjusted for the propensity score (PS), inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), and standardized mortality ratio (SMR) weighting. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), gender (male vs. female), sepsis, pneumonia, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, and benzodiazepine use, both before and after PSM. To explore differences in delirium incidence by diagnostic coding system, patients in the ND group were further stratified by ICD version (ICD-9 vs. ICD-10), and results were visualized using bar plots. Finally, we analyzed the incidence of delirium in ICU patients who received NRT prior to its onset. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this study, a total of 24,043 patients were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database for analysis and stratified based on whether they had nicotine dependence. Among them, 2,662 patients (11.07%) were in the ND group, and 21,381 patients (88.93%) were in the non-ND group. The flowchart of patient selection is shown in Fig. 1. Table 1 presents the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups before and after PSM. Before PSM, the incidence of delirium in the ND group was higher than that in the non-ND group (30.8% vs. 25.1%, P < 0.001), and both the median ICU length of stay (56.9 h vs. 54.0 h, P < 0.001) and total hospital stay (197.3 h vs. 183.1 h, P < 0.001) were longer. However, the ICU mortality rate (2.6% vs. 2.0%, P = 0.075) and in-hospital mortality rate (6.1% vs. 4.7%, P < 0.001) were higher in the non-ND group compared to the ND group. Patients in the ND group were younger, with 60.1% being male. After ICU admission, they had a higher usage rate of midazolam, NRT, and benzodiazepines. In addition, they exhibited a higher incidence of acute respiratory failure during the ICU stay, and a higher prevalence of severe liver disease, chronic lung disease, and pneumonia in their medical history. After PSM, 2,653 patients were retained in each group. The SMD of all covariates was less than 0.1, indicating satisfactory covariate balance after matching (Supplementary Information: Figure S1). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, gender, race, most laboratory parameters, ICU complications, medical history, and severity scores. The incidence of delirium remained significantly higher in the ND group than in the non-ND group (30.8% vs. 27.2%, P = 0.004). However, no significant differences were found between the two groups with respect to hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, ICU mortality, or in-hospital mortality.

Correlation analysis between ND and pre-PSM delirium

Before PSM, univariate logistic regression analysis (Table 2) indicated that ND was positively associated with the incidence of delirium (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.17–1.36, P < 0.001). The results of multivariate Cox regression analysis assessing the relationship between ND and delirium incidence are provided in Table S1 of the supplementary materials. After adjusting for age, gender, and race in Model I, ND remained positively associated with delirium (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.21–1.40, P < 0.001). In Model II, which further adjusted for comorbidities, ND was still positively associated with delirium (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.15–1.34, P < 0.001). Incidence of delirium treated with NRT

In the full sample, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Supplementary Table S1) showed that NRT use was significantly associated with a reduced risk of delirium after adjusting for confounding factors (HR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.39–0.62; P < 0.001). Descriptive analysis (Table S4) further indicated that among ND patients, the incidence of delirium was significantly lower in NRT users (6.8%) compared to non-users (17.9%) (P < 0.001). To further assess the association between NRT and delirium, we conducted subgroup analyses among ND patients (Table S5). Results showed that NRT use remained significantly associated with a reduced risk of delirium both before and after propensity score matching (pre-PSM: HR = 0.365; 95% CI: 0.278–0.479; P < 0.001; post-PSM: HR = 0.367; 95% CI: 0.280–0.481; P < 0.001). However, interaction analyses in both the full and matched samples revealed no statistically significant difference in delirium risk between NRT users and non-users among ND patients.

Discussion

Our study has shown that compared with patients without ND, those with ND have a 27% increased risk of delirium in the ICU (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.18–1.37, P < 0.001). After PSM, ND patients still exhibited a 17% increased risk (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.06–1.30, P = 0.002). This conclusion was consistently supported by the results of competing risk models, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses. Although the absolute difference in delirium incidence between ND and non-ND groups may appear modest, in the context of ICU patient complexity and in light of the substantial impact of delirium on resource utilization, clinical outcomes, and healthcare costs, this difference remains clinically meaningful in large populations. Furthermore, the presence of ND may reflect an increased vulnerability to neurocognitive dysfunction, suggesting that ND could be regarded as a potential high-risk characteristic in clinical management. Its association with ICU delirium indicates that proactive monitoring and early intervention strategies may be warranted, although prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Smoking is a well-established risk factor for ICU admission. Compared with non-smokers, smokers face significantly higher risks of infection and pneumonia [23]. Evidence indicates that each additional cigarette smoked increases the risk of respiratory diseases (such as pneumonia, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, pleurisy, atelectasis, respiratory failure, etc.) by 5% to 33%, and raises the risk of circulatory diseases (including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, and arterial embolism) by 5% to 23% [24]. Currently, smoking is also associated with an elevated risk of severe illness, with a reported relative risk (RR) of 1.3 (95% CrI 0.90–1.40) [25]. In our study, ND patients had significantly longer hospital and ICU stays compared to non-ND patients, but lower in-hospital and ICU mortality. This may be attributed to the fact that ND patients were generally younger, while non-ND patients had higher rates of AKI, hypertension, diabetes, congestive heart failure, and higher severity scores. Notably, after PSM, the differences in hospital stay duration, ICU stay duration, in-hospital mortality, and ICU mortality between the two groups were no longer statistically significant.

Nicotine, and indeed nicotine withdrawal, may increase the risk of delirium. Acute nicotine withdrawal during hospitalization may lead to a sudden decrease in acetylcholine (ACh) levels. This is because long-term smoking causes desensitization and upregulation of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), which disrupts ACh homeostasis once exogenous nicotine input is abruptly removed. This leads to cholinergic neurotransmission dysfunction, which is regarded as one of the core pathophysiological processes of delirium [26–28]. Additionally, nicotine broadly influences several neurotransmitter systems, including dopamine, glutamate, GABA, serotonin, and opioid peptides. These neurotransmitters jointly regulate arousal and cognitive function, and any imbalance caused by nicotine exposure or withdrawal may increase an individual’s susceptibility to delirium [29, 30]. Chronic smoking may also lead to microvascular damage and cerebral atherosclerosis, which in turn impairs cerebral perfusion. In elderly individuals, these vascular changes may persist even after smoking cessation, further elevating the risk of delirium [31, 32]. Moreover, pharmacological studies have shown that even low-dose or non-traditional forms of nicotine exposure can induce long-term and potentially irreversible alterations in neural structure and function during brain development [33]. A national prospective cohort study conducted in the United States found that individuals with ND were more likely to develop mental disorders within three years, and for the first time confirmed ND as an independent risk factor for both the onset and persistence of such conditions [34]. Other studies have also demonstrated that ND has a heritable component, with a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) heritability estimate of 8.6%, and is genetically correlated with 18 traits including other smoking behaviors, alcohol dependence, depression, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, schizophrenia, lung cancer, and coronary heart disease [35].

Existing studies have demonstrated a strong association between delirium and adverse clinical outcomes. The BRAIN-ICU study reported that 74% of critically ill ICU patients experienced delirium during hospitalization, and 40% of survivors continued to suffer from severe cognitive impairment three months post-discharge [36]. Delirium is typically multifactorial, with predisposing factors such as advanced age, dementia, depression, substance dependence, and stroke, and precipitating factors including infection, the use of sedatives, and diabetes [37]. To better isolate the relationship between ND and delirium in this study, we excluded patients with prior diagnoses of schizophrenia, dementia, depression, alcohol use disorder, and cerebrovascular disease during the design phase, thereby reducing confounding effects from neuropsychiatric and vascular comorbidities as much as possible.

Moreover, since the United States officially transitioned from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM/PCS on October 1, 2015, as part of a nationwide reform led by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) [38], the classification of ND has become more refined. Under ICD-9-CM, ND was uniformly coded as 305.1 (Tobacco use disorder), without distinctions based on usage patterns. In contrast, ICD-10-CM uses the term “Nicotine dependence” and provides more detailed classifications according to the mode of use and associated comorbidities [39]. The MIMIC-IV database includes ICU inpatient data spanning from 2001 to 2022, covering the transition period between ICD-9 and ICD-10. Prior to 2015, most records used ICD-9 coding, with a gradual shift to ICD-10 after 2016 [40]. Therefore, when investigating ND using MIMIC data, the potential impact of coding system differences must be carefully considered. In this study, we further analyzed the association between ICD coding versions and delirium incidence among ND patients. The results revealed a significant difference in delirium rates between the two coding systems (P < 0.001). Specifically, the incidence of delirium among ND patients coded under ICD-10 was 34%, significantly higher than the 26% observed under ICD-9. This difference may reflect the greater diagnostic sensitivity of ICD-10 in identifying patients with more complex clinical conditions and a higher risk of delirium, thereby contributing to a higher reported incidence.

NRT is a commonly used first-line treatment for preventing nicotine withdrawal in smokers. In 2024, NRT chewing gum and patches were included in the World Health Organization (WHO) Prequalification List of Medicines [41], and have been recommended as first-line treatments for smoking cessation by WHO and several other authoritative guidelines, including those from the U.S. Public Health Service [42, 43]. However, the current body of research presents considerable controversy regarding the association between NRT and delirium in the ICU. One prospective study reported that patients receiving NRT had more delirium-free, sedation-free, and coma-free days by day 20 (16.6 days vs. 12.6 days, p = 0.03) [44]. A case observation study in neuro-ICU patients also indicated that NRT may alleviate psychiatric symptoms in certain individuals experiencing withdrawal-induced delirium, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in specific contexts [45]. This study found that NRT use was significantly associated with a reduced risk of delirium, particularly among patients with ND, where the incidence of delirium was lower in users. These results remained consistent after propensity score matching. Although interaction analyses did not show statistically significant differences, the overall trend suggests that NRT may have a potential protective effect in the ND population. Given the inconsistency in current findings and the variation across studies in terms of population selection, timing of intervention, and NRT formulation, further large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify the safety and efficacy of NRT in ICU patients. In clinical practice, NRT should be considered a beneficial component of comprehensive treatment strategies, based on thorough individualized assessment, to balance potential benefits and risk control.

Although there are relatively few studies investigating the association between smoking and the risk of delirium in ICU inpatients, existing literature has reported inconsistent findings. Some studies suggest that smokers have a significantly increased risk of developing delirium during their ICU stay [16], while others have found no statistically significant association [17]. Still, some studies remain inconclusive on this relationship [18]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in study populations, definitions of smoking behavior, delirium diagnostic tools, and methods for adjusting for confounding factors. To date, research specifically examining whether ND independently increases the risk of delirium remains limited, particularly in terms of providing clear quantitative evidence. High-quality, systematic investigations on this topic are still lacking. Most current studies have focused on nicotine withdrawal treatment, which is commonly associated with symptoms such as restlessness, dizziness, insomnia, anxiety, and sweating. Recent case reports suggest that nicotine withdrawal may be an under recognized cause of delirium in patients with acute brain injury. If not promptly identified and managed, it could worsen the clinical condition and negatively impact long-term prognosis [46]. Therefore, future studies should focus more on characterizing delirium in nicotine-dependent patients, developing effective screening tools, and optimizing prevention and intervention strategies.

This study has several strengths. First, it utilized a large-scale public database with a substantial sample size, thereby improving the generalizability of the findings. Second, the use of propensity score matching effectively controlled for intergroup heterogeneity and minimized selection bias. Third, a stepwise modeling strategy was employed, which began with univariate screening and was followed by multivariate Cox regression, to confirm the statistical significance of ND as an independent risk factor for ICU delirium. Furthermore, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to enhance result robustness, and critical confounders such as comorbidities and sedative medication use were rigorously adjusted for. Finally, the identification of ND was based on a standardized ICD coding framework, ensuring diagnostic consistency and enhancing the reproducibility and cross-center applicability of the results.

This study also has several limitations. First, because the MIMIC-IV database contains de-identified and time-shifted data, the exact transition point from ICD-9 to ICD-10 coding cannot be determined, making it difficult to assess any potential temporal bias introduced by this coding change. Additionally, the database is based solely on the ICD diagnostic system and lacks information related to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), preventing classification of nicotine use disorders by historical diagnostic criteria (DSM-IV vs. DSM-5) and assessment of severity. Second, the database does not clearly distinguish the primary diagnosis at ICU admission and lacks key timestamps to determine the chronological order of clinical events, which may affect the accuracy of comorbidity identification and confounder control. Third, although delirium identification in this study relied on standardized assessment tools such as the CAM-ICU and the RASS, and the structure of these variables remained consistent across the study period, variations in clinical implementation (e.g., assessment frequency and staff training quality) could not be quantified, leaving the possibility of temporal or procedural bias. Fourth, the database does not contain key indicators of ND severity, such as daily cigarette consumption, pack-years, or details on withdrawal symptom management, which limits the ability to explore dose-response relationships. Lastly, nicotine withdrawal symptoms such as anxiety and agitation may confound delirium assessments in the ICU environment, potentially increasing the risk of misclassification.

Conclusion

Based on a systematic analysis of a large-scale public critical care database, this study found that ND is associated with an increased risk of delirium in ICU patients. These findings highlight the importance of actively assessing the risk of delirium in patients with ND and developing targeted intervention strategies within the intensive care setting.
