Reply: Clarifying the role of severe obesity in pediatric heart transplant outcomes
Authors: Joshua D Sparks, Bahaaldin Alsoufi
Published: 2025

Reply to the Editor:

We thank Lohano and colleagues1 for their thoughtful commentary on our recent article.2 We are encouraged by their recognition of the importance of this topic and appreciate the opportunity to further clarify our findings. Lohano and colleagues1 raise several points that we would like to address:

•Age group considerations: The authors highlight that children younger than age 8 years were not included in our primary analysis. As we noted, the incidence of severe obesity in this group was exceedingly low (<1.5%), and their exclusion minimized statistical noise and potential confounders. We agree that future studies employing more precise measures of adiposity in early childhood could provide additional insights.

•Body mass index as a measure of adiposity: We concur that body mass index (BMI), although widely used, is an imperfect measure of adiposity in children, particularly in younger age groups. Nonetheless, BMI remains the only standardized and practical measure available in large national registries such as United Network for Organ Sharing. In our study, BMI was also evaluated as a continuous variable, and this analysis confirmed its predictive value for adverse outcomes, reinforcing its clinical utility despite its limitations.

•Donor-related factors: We agree that donor variables such as ischemic time, hormonal treatment, and body surface area are important in pediatric heart transplantation. In our multivariable analysis, severe recipient obesity remained a significant independent risk factor for graft loss and mortality even after adjusting for donor characteristics. Of note, although one might hypothesize—by analogy to native coronary artery disease—that hearts from larger donors (ie, higher BMI) would be more prone to transplant coronary allograft vasculopathy (CAV), our data showed that donor size itself was not an independent risk factor, whereas recipient obesity was. This underscores the intrinsic metabolic and inflammatory risks conferred by obesity in the recipient, beyond donor influences.

•Pathophysiology of death and association with CAV: We appreciate the emphasis placed by Lohano and colleagues1 on the link between obesity and CAV. Our findings similarly suggested that late mortality in obese recipients was often attributed to chronic rejection and cardiac arrest, which we suspect reflect progression of CAV. This reinforces the need for vigilant long-term surveillance and preventive strategies in this vulnerable population.

We value these insights and agree that future research should refine adiposity assessment in children; examine outcomes across all pediatric age groups; and evaluate novel interventions, including pharmacologic weight-loss therapies, to mitigate risk. Our analysis, the largest to date in this field, demonstrates that severe obesity in children is not merely a perioperative concern but an ongoing, independent risk factor for long-term graft survival after pediatric heart transplantation (Figure 1). We thank Lohano and colleagues1 for engaging in this important discussion and for contributing to a deeper understanding of this complex issue.
