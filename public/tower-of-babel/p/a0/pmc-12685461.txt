Impact of Prevertebral Fascia Suture and Relevant Risk Factors on Postoperative Dysphagia Following Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion
Authors: Chengyi Huang, Majiao Jiang, Tingkui Wu, Junbo He, Yiwei Shen, Xiaqing Sheng, Dingke Wen, Li He, Jin Xu, Hao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe prevertebral fascia (PVF), which constitutes part of the deep layer of the deep cervical fascia, is routinely incised during anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) surgery. Suturing the PVF could serve as an optimal barrier between the surface of the fusion device and the posterior esophagus and may alleviate postoperative dysphagia. Thus, this retrospective study was aimed to (1) evaluate the impact of prevertebral fascia (PVF) suturing and (2) perform a multivariate analysis of relevant risk factors for postoperative dysphagia following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF).MethodsA total of 197 patients who underwent ACDF and had at least 1 year of follow‐up between June 2020 and February 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. To compare baseline data and incidence of dysphagia, the patients were divided into two groups on the basis of whether they had undergone PVF suturing during the operation (suture group, N = 83; nonsuture group, N = 114). The incidence and severity of dysphagia were evaluated using the Bazaz grading system. The patients were further categorized into a dysphagia group (N = 56) and a nondysphagia group (N = 141) to conduct a multivariate analysis of dysphagia.ResultsCompared with the nonsuture group, the suture group presented a significantly lower incidence and severity of dysphagia at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively (p < 0.05). A binary logistic regression analysis revealed that advanced age (odds ratio [OR], 1.034; 95% confidence interval [95% CI, 1.006–1.063]), greater ΔC2‐7A (OR, 1.141; 95% CI, 1.056–1.232), minor ΔTS‐CL (OR, 0.890; 95% CI, 0.842–0.941), and nonsutured PVF (OR, 0.329; 95% CI, 0.146–0.740) were significantly associated with higher rates of dysphagia (p < 0.05).ConclusionSuturing of the PVF during ACDF can significantly decrease the incidence and severity of transient postoperative dysphagia in patients. The incidence of postoperative dysphagia is also significantly associated with advanced age, greater ΔC2‐7A, and minor ΔTS‐CL.Keywords: ACDF, cervical degenerative disc disease, dysphagia, multivariate analysis, prevertebral fascia

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) has been the most widely accepted surgical treatment for patients with cervical myelopathy or radiculopathy refractory to conservative treatment [1]. As one of the most common complaints following ACDF, postoperative dysphagia has a reported incidence as high as 83% and is potentially associated with increased hospital length of stay, higher readmission rates, and postoperative morbidities such as malnutrition, aspiration pneumonia, and airway obstruction [2, 3]. The underlying mechanisms responsible for the development of dysphagia following anterior cervical surgery are multifaceted and remain incompletely understood. The extensive adhesions caused by mechanical irritation to the esophagus have been demonstrated to affect patients following anterior cervical surgery [4]. To reduce foreign body stimulation‐induced postoperative dysphagia, many measures have been implemented, including the use of smaller and smoother profile plates or zero‐profile fusion devices (Zero‐P) in ACDF [3, 5]. However, a considerable proportion of patients (range, 3%–76%) continue to suffer from dysphagia following ACDF [6, 7, 8].

The prevertebral fascia (PVF) originates at the base of the skull, terminates at the third thoracic vertebra, and contributes to the deep layer of the deep cervical fascia in conjunction with the superficial alar fascia [9]. Owing to its close attachment to the anterior muscles of the cervical spine and vertebrae, the PVF necessitates an incision to expose the anterior aspect of the cervical vertebrae during ACDF surgery. The PVF is characterized by its thinness and high flexibility, facilitating the passage of needles during surgery without being susceptible to tearing from suture tension. Thus, suturing the PVF could serve as an optimal barrier between the surface of the fusion device and the posterior esophagus [9]. However, following implantation of the fusion device, few surgeons suture the PVF. Furthermore, few studies have addressed the efficacy of PVF suturing in reducing the incidence of dysphagia.

Thus, the purpose of this retrospective study was to (1) evaluate the impact of PVF suturing and (2) perform a multivariate analysis of relevant risk factors for postoperative dysphagia following ACDF. Practical references are provided for future surgeries to further reduce the incidence of dysphagia after ACDF.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, China (Ethics Approval Number: 2019946). All of the patients provided informed consent before recruitment for analysis of their clinical data.

Patient Enrollment

The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① patients with a diagnosis of radiculopathy or myelopathy from cervical degenerative disc disease (CDDD) with correlating magnetic resonance imaging findings and conservative treatment proven to be ineffective for more than 6 weeks; ② patients aged 18 years or older who underwent one‐level or continuous multi‐level ACDF (C3–C7) using the Zero‐P VA Implant System (Synthes GmbH, Oberdorf, Switzerland); ③ patients who underwent a minimum of 1 year of postoperative follow‐up with complete data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① patients with dysphagia before surgery; ② patients with a history of esophageal disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease, prior head and neck surgery, or revision surgery; ③ patients with spinal deformity, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis, ankylosing spondylitis, malignant tumors, infection, uncontrollable metabolic disorders (such as hypertension or diabetes), neurological disorders, or poor general condition.

Between June 2020 and February 2024, 216 patients with CDDD were enrolled according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Among them, 4 patients declined to participate in this study, 2 patients temporarily rejected surgical treatment, 9 patients were lost to follow‐up, and 4 patients had their surgical plans altered by the surgeon during the operation. Ultimately, 197 patients completed all follow‐ups successfully and were retrospectively reviewed. The patients were divided into two groups on the basis of whether they had undergone PVF suturing during the operation (Suture group and Nonsuture group). For further multivariate analysis of dysphagia, the patients were categorized into a dysphagia group and a nondysphagia group (Figure 1).

Surgical Technique

All surgical procedures were performed by a senior surgeon with the right Smith‐Robinson approach after general anesthesia [1]. After exposure, the PVFs were meticulously dissected, and the incised edges of the PVFs were marked on both sides with sutures in the suture group. Next, complete discectomy was performed at the affected segments to achieve thorough decompression by removing the disc tissue, posterior longitudinal ligament, and osteophytes. After the endplates were well polished, a convex standard Zero‐P VA implant of appropriate height was implanted into the intervertebral space. The final fluoroscopy procedure was performed after 2 locking head screws were inserted bilaterally into the vertebrae.

For the suture group, the longus colli was sutured with 4–0 Mersilk Silk braided nonabsorbable suture (Ethicon US LLC, Somerville, New Jersey, USA) at the central position of the vertebra; the PVF was tightly sutured using a simple interrupted suture technique with a stitch spacing of approximately 5 mm (Figure 2). Notably, the thinness of the PVF often presents challenges in achieving proper alignment during surgery, necessitating precise incisions and marking via sutures during the exposure step to facilitate easier suturing subsequently. The cervical longus muscle should be sutured closely to minimize difficulties due to excessive tension during PVF suturing, particularly in multi‐level surgery. In addition, suturing the longus colli muscles with appropriate tension can effectively reduce the risk of postoperative bleeding from the severed nutrient vessels. During the suturing process, it is crucial to ensure that the esophagus is fully retracted using the retractor to avoid injury. Additionally, the PVF should be incised and sutured along the midline to prevent potential damage to the nutrient vessels located bilaterally [10]. No sutures were applied to the longus colli or PVF in the nonsuture group. After the incision was closed layer by layer, a drainage tube was placed.

In the suture group, the longus colli was tightened and sutured (A), followed by meticulous suturing of the prevertebral fascia (PVF) (B). PVF, prevertebral fascia.

Dysphagia Evaluation

The Bazaz grading system was selected to evaluate postoperative dysphagia because it is the most commonly used dysphagia symptom detection tool for patients with cervical diseases [11]. The system is utilized to assess patients' subjective experience of dysphagia, encompassing both liquid and solid intake, on the basis of personalized data. In this survey, patients are graded as having none/mild/moderate/severe dysphagia according to the Bazaz grading system, and a numerical scoring system ranging from 0 (none) to 3 (severe) is implemented to facilitate further analysis. Dysphagia that resolved within 3 months after surgery was categorized as transient, whereas dysphagia lasting for more than 3 months postoperatively was classified as persistent [3, 12, 13]. Two researchers conducted the Bazaz scale assessment. In cases of disagreement, the two raters were required to re‐evaluate the data. If consensus could not be reached, a senior surgeon was consulted to make the final decision.

Radiographic Assessment

PACS version 4.0 (GE Healthcare Milwaukee WI) was used to measure the O‐C2 angle (O‐C2A) C2–7 angle (C2–7A) T1 slope and prevertebral soft‐tissue thickness (PSTT) at a magnification rate of 150% based on the methodology reported by previous studies [3, 14] (Figure 3). The T1 slope minus cervical lordosis (TS − CL) was defined as the T1 slope minus C2–7A. The alterations in the aforementioned values (ΔO‐C2A ΔC2–7A ΔT1 slope ΔTS – CL and prevertebral soft‐tissue swelling (PSTS)) were determined by subtracting the preoperative value from the postoperative value. The measurements were performed 1 week postoperatively. The parameters were measured with standing X‐ray images in the neutral position. The kappa value (κ) was used to assess interobserver reliability. Generally κ < 0.20 indicates poor agreement; 0.21–0.40 fair; 0.41–0.60 moderate; 0.61–0.80 good; and 0.81–1.00 excellent. All κ values between the two observers for the radiographic parameters exceeded 0.81.

Diagram of the radiographic assessment. C2–7A = the angle between the inferior margin of the C2 vertebra and the inferior margin of the C7 vertebra; O‐C2A = the angle between McGregor's line and the inferior margin of the C2 vertebra; T1 slope = the angle formed by the T1 superior endplate and the horizontal line; PSTT = the length of the extended line of the AP diameter from C2 to C7, and the AP diameter was calculated between the center of the posterior and anterior cortices of the vertebral body. O‐C2A, O‐C2 angle; C2–7A, C2–7 angle; PSTT, prevertebral soft‐tissue thickness; AP, anteroposterior.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed with SPSS 23.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Independent sample t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were applied for continuous variables at each follow‐up. The chi‐square test was used to analyze categorical variables. The variables with a significance level of p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were chosen for inclusion in multiple regression models via binary logistic regression analysis. The time point for univariate analysis and multivariable logistic regression was 1 week postoperatively. The level of statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and Baseline Data

Among the 197 patients reviewed in this study, 83 patients were in the suture group, whereas 114 patients were in the nonsuture group. Furthermore, a total of 56 postoperative patients presented with dysphagia, while 141 patients remained asymptomatic for dysphagia. The preoperative PSTT in the suture group (10.31 mm±1.95 mm, median = 10.16) was significantly lower than that in the nonsuture group (11.04 mm±2.38 mm, median = 10.38) (U = 3918.000, p < 0.05), and the postoperative PSTT in the suture group (16.53 mm±2.38 mm) was also significantly lower than that in the nonsuture group (17.41 mm±2.63 mm) (mean difference = −0.87 mL, 95% confidence interval [CI], −1.59 mL – −1.56 mL, t = −2.40, p < 0.05). The suture group presented a significantly lower incidence and severity of dysphagia at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively than did the nonsuture group ( p < 0.05). Multivariate Analysis of Postoperative Dysphagia

All patients were divided into two groups on the basis of whether they experienced postoperative dysphagia at 1 week after surgery (dysphagia group, N = 56; nondysphagia group, N = 141). There was a significant difference in TS‐CL between the postoperative dysphagia group (10.52 mm±5.33 mm, median = 10.72) and the nondysphagia group (14.51 mm±5.66 mm, median = 14.33) (U = 2328.000, p < 0.001) (Table 3). The demographic parameters, including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, number of surgical segments, involvement of C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, and C6/7, intraoperative time, length of hospital stay, and estimated blood loss, were included in the univariate analysis. Additionally, ΔO‐C2A, ΔC2–7A, ΔT1 slope, ΔTS − CL, PSTS, and PVF suturing were included in the univariate analysis (Table 4). A binary logistic regression analysis revealed that advanced age (odds ratio [OR], 1.034; 95% CI, 1.006–1.063), greater ΔC2‐7A (OR, 1.141; 95% CI, 1.056–1.232), minor ΔTS‐CL (OR, 0.890; 95% CI, 0.842–0.941), and nonsutured PVF (OR, 0.329; 95% CI, 0.146–0.740) were significantly associated with higher rates of dysphagia ( p < 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

Main Findings

The main findings of this article are: (1) Suturing PVF in ACDF can significantly reduce the incidence and severity of dysphagia in patients within the first 3 months after surgery; and (2) The incidence of postoperative dysphagia is also significantly associated with advanced age, greater ΔC2‐7A, and minor ΔTS‐CL.

Risk Factors in Previous Studies

According to previous studies, numerous risk factors for the development of dysphagia following ACDF surgery have been identified. Age [15], frailty [16], sex [17], long intraoperative time [18], multisegmental surgery [19, 20], high surgical level [15], excessive traction of the esophagus [5], refractory pain following surgery [21], cervical hematoma [22], thickness of the anterior cervical plate [17], massive blood loss [23], esophageal intubation [19], postoperative topical infection [15], impaired recurrent laryngeal nerve [24], scar hyperplasia [25], PSTS [3, 23], ΔO‐C2A [24], ΔC2–7A [23], ΔTS − CL [16], and surgery at C3/4 and C4/5 [15, 16] have all been shown to be closely associated with postoperative dysphagia after ACDF. The discrepancies between studies may be due to differences in sample size, method of dysphagia assessment, and type of implant.

The Impact of PVF Suturing on Postoperative Dysphagia

In this study, we implemented a novel PVF suturing technique, which significantly mitigated post‐ACDF surgery dysphagia. A prospective, single‐center clinical study conducted by Deng et al. [26] used an absorbable collagen biomembrane made of type I collagen placed in the anterior cervical region during anterior cervical spine surgery, which yielded satisfactory results in improving the patient's postoperative swallowing function. The application of absorbable collagen biomembranes separates the implant from the esophagus to prevent physical irritation. Similarly, PVF, as an autologous tissue, does not pose the risk of rejection‐induced inflammation or edema; moreover, it can block the titanium alloy plate of the implant from directly contacting the esophagus. Additionally, owing to close attachment to the cervical vertebrae and muscles, the sutured PVF and the internal graft exhibit minimal relative motion during cervical movement, which can further reduce the stimulation caused by physical friction. Furthermore, the alar fascia and PVF collectively constitute the anterior and posterior boundaries of the “danger space,” serving as crucial barriers against the spread of infection to the thoracic cavity and thereby mitigating the risk of pleuritis, necrotizing fasciitis, and empyema [16, 27]. The reconstruction of PVF may play a certain role in preventing postoperative infections. It is important to note that not all patients are eligible for PVF suture during the surgical procedure. During the exposure, retraction, or suturing processes, PVF damage may occur, potentially hindering the successful completion of the suture. Therefore, meticulous surgical technique is essential to minimize this risk. In addition, for certain patients, we observed that the PVF was tightly adhered to the anterior longitudinal ligament, thereby precluding its dissection and consequently making it difficult to achieve a precise incision and suture.

The results indicated that PVF suturing affects only the incidence and severity of transient dysphagia. The incidence and severity of dysphagia in the first 3 months after ACDF were found to be influenced by the number of surgical segments, as demonstrated in a retrospective study involving 208 patients [3]. Lee et al. [5] demonstrated that early postoperative dysphagia can be mitigated through the utilization of a smaller and smoother profile plate following ACDF. Furthermore, a retrospective study demonstrated that overcorrection of TS‐CL and surgery at C3/4 impacted only the occurrence of dysphagia in the 2–6 weeks following anterior cervical spine surgery [16]. Surgical‐related factors may predominantly influence the occurrence and severity of transient dysphagia. Persistent dysphagia may result from neuromuscular dysfunction or psychosocial factors. In addition, the incidence and severity of postoperative dysphagia in our study improved gradually over time with increasing follow‐up.

Multivariate Analysis Results

In the present study, advanced age, greater ΔC2‐7A, and minor ΔTS‐CL were also identified as factors influencing the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia following ACDF. Smith‐Hammond et al. [15] reported a correlation between postoperative dysphagia and patient age of > 60 years. We hypothesize that the additional anatomic and physiologic changes related to aging, as well as decreased tolerance for dysphagia, may also contribute to these factors [16]. In terms of ΔC2‐7A and ΔTS‐CL, a previous study suggested that there is a significantly greater likelihood of developing postoperative dysphagia when the ΔC2‐7A angle exceeds 5° [28]. Additionally, a retrospective analysis conducted by Asada et al. revealed a significant correlation between dysphagia in the early postoperative period and overcorrection of TS‐CL [16]. These findings indicate the importance of carefully avoiding excessive correction of cervical lordosis during surgical procedures. Some surgeons apply greater traction to the intervertebral space and select larger implants to restore the physiological curvature of the cervical spine. However, overcorrection of cervical lordosis may lead to alterations in the anatomical relationship between the cervical spine and the anterior esophagus, causing the protrusion of the pharyngolaryngeal wall and, ultimately, postoperative dysphagia [29]. Hence, exercising caution when correcting the curvature and selecting the optimal implant height during surgery is imperative to prevent excessive changes in the cervical curvature that may result in physical narrowing of the oropharynx and postoperative dysphagia.

In this study, however, PSTS, the number of surgical segments, and involvement of C3/4 were excluded from the multivariate analysis. The improved exposure techniques employed may have protected the anterior soft tissues of the cervical spine, thereby mitigating the postoperative inflammatory and edema response [6]. Monitoring the retractor tension during surgical procedures and intermittently releasing it might be effective in reducing the temporary paralysis of the superior laryngeal nerve induced by multilevel surgery and surgery at C3/4 [23].

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this study lies in being the first to introduce a novel PVF suturing technique that significantly reduces postoperative dysphagia following ACDF surgery. The present study is subject to several limitations. First, although the Bazaz grading system is widely utilized and has demonstrated accuracy, notably, its assessment relies on the subjective sensation of patients and can introduce reporting bias. The combination of videofluoroscopic or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation should be employed in future studies. Second, despite conducting a comprehensive multivariate analysis, our study did not encompass all pertinent risk factors associated with dysphagia. Third, given that all the researchers involved in this study are affiliated with a single center, there is a possibility of investigator bias in the evaluation of dysphagia. While the consistency of the baseline data should not impact the results and trends of group comparisons, it may influence the overall assessment of dysphagia incidence. Additionally, the inherent nature of a retrospective study has the potential to introduce bias. Relevantly, future research should prioritize the implementation of multicenter randomized controlled trials and also focus on the interventions to control long‐term dysphagia. Fourth, the precise mechanism through which PVF suturing mitigates the occurrence and intensity of dysphagia remains incompletely understood, necessitating future animal studies and histological evaluations for further exploration. Alternatively, magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography scan can be employed to assess the correlation between PVF suturing and local soft tissue alterations.

Conclusions

The suturing of PVF in ACDF can significantly decrease the incidence and severity of transient postoperative dysphagia in patients. The incidence of postoperative dysphagia was also significantly associated with advanced age, greater ΔC2‐7A and minor ΔTS‐CL. Therefore, it is advisable for spine surgeons to consider suturing the PVF during ACDF whenever feasible in order to mitigate the risk of postoperative dysphagia. Reconsideration of the patient's age is crucial, and caution should be exercised to avoid excessive correction of cervical lordosis to prevent potential postoperative dysphagia.
