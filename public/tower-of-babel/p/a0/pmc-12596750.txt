The effect of using intra-oral camera as an educational aid for oral health promotion in children
Authors: KSS Pratyusha, Penmatsa Chaitanya, Chandrappa Vinay, KS Uloopi, Peddi Ravigna, Spandana Cheeli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of using an intraoral camera (IOC) as an educational aid for promoting oral health among 7–10-year-old children in India.Materials and methods:A randomized trial was conducted involving 260 children from private schools. The intervention group (n = 130) received IOC-based oral health education, while the control group (n = 130) received verbal education. Both groups were assessed for knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) towards oral health, as well as the DMFT/deft index at baseline and after 12 months. Statistical analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the Mann–Whitney test. Additionally, regression analysis was conducted to predict DMFT and deft scores using KAP scores as predictors.Results:A significant decrease (p<0.001) in the mean number of decayed teeth (dt + DT) was observed in both groups. However, the intervention group showed an increase in filled teeth (ft + FT) scores after 12 months. The experimental group also exhibited a significant increase in attitude scores (p<0.01) and practice scores (p<0.001). Despite these improvements, KAP scores did not predict the total caries experience, as no correlation was found between total caries experience (deft + DMFT) and KAP scores.Conclusion:The use of the IOC as an educational tool significantly improved children’s oral health status, as well as their attitude and practice scores. However, since KAP scores did not predict changes in oral health, a more comprehensive approach is required for effective oral health promotion among children.

Introduction

Health promotion is a two-faceted approach that involves environmental changes and health education (1). Oral Health Education (OHE) programs are specifically designed to enhance awareness and encourage the adoption of a healthy lifestyle, including appropriate dietary habits, positive attitudes, and beneficial oral health practices (2).

Schools play a pivotal role in promoting oral health among children. They serve a dual purpose: imparting education and shaping health-related behaviors. When oral health interventions are integrated into the school environment, they can significantly improve children’s dental health and related habits (3). Parents, teachers, and dental professionals all contribute to educating children about dental health (4).

Oral health education in schools is typically delivered by professionals through various methods. These include conventional approaches, suchas classroom lectures, flashcards, charts, and posters, as well as more interactive and experiential learning techniques (5). Research shows that health education using integrated modes such as pamphlet distribution, demonstrations, and audio-visual aids, has a significantly greater impact on dental health practices among rural Indian schoolchildren compared to demonstrations and pamphlets alone (6).

The development of innovative technologies is revolutionizing the way oral health care providers and clinicians deliver care. One such advancement is the intraoral camera (IOC), a user-friendly, affordable, and lightweight device capable of capturing high-resolution videos and images. The IOC plays a crucial role in boosting patient adherence and motivation, improving patient understanding and awareness of various prevalent oral conditions, and promoting effective communication between patients and dentists (7).

Despite the potential benefits, limited studies have been conducted where IOC has been used as an educational tool in oral health promotion. Hence, the current study is planned to evaluate the effectiveness of IOC as an education tool for oral health promotion among children in India. The null hypothesis of this study is that there is no significant difference in oral health status, knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) scores between children who receive intraoral camera (IOC)-based oral health education and those who receive verbal oral health education.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The present study is a double-blinded, randomized controlled trial conducted between September 2022 and October 2023. The clinical trial and its methodology were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Scientific Review Board (VDC/ RP/2022/34) and the Ethics Committee (IECVDC/2022/PG01/ PPD/IVV/34). The trial design was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI REG NO: CTRI/2023/05/052332) in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study sample

The sample size was determined based on the results of a pilot study conducted among 40 children. A power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software indicated that a sample size of 194 children (97 per group) was required. However, to account for potential loss to follow-up, a total of 260 children (N=260) was selected, with 130 participants in each group (n=130). Children aged 7 to 10 years who were in the mixed dentition period, had at least one decayed tooth, and demonstrated receptiveness and the ability to learn about oral health and maintain good oral hygiene were included in the study. Exclusion criteria included failure to provide consent, participation in another trial, or the presence of systemic illnesses or special health care needs. Each step of the trial was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines for randomized clinical trials (Figure 1).

Study design

School children were screened, and 260 children were selected based on the inclusion criteria. After obtaining written informed consent from parents, the children were randomly assigned to two groups of 130 each using cluster randomization: the Experimental Group (Group 1 – IOC) and the Control Group (Group 2 – Verbal). Demographic details of the children were recorded in the presence of their parents. Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) toward oral health were assessed using a pre-validated questionnaire (8), and the DMFT/deft index was recorded for children in both groups. In the experimental group, the oral health status of each child was explained to both the child and their parents’ using images captured by the IOC (Waldent Innovations Pvt Ltd., New Delhi, India). To prevent contamination while handling the IOC, disposable gloves were worn. Additionally, the camera, handpiece, and cable were wiped with a disinfectant solution (Bacillol 25, Raman and Weil Pvt. Ltd., India), and disposable protective sleeves were replaced for each child. In the control group, oral health status was explained verbally to parents and children without the use of the IOC. Children were re-evaluated after 12 months for changes in their DMFT/deft index and KAP scores.

Statistical analysis

The study results were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Since the data were not normally distributed between the groups, quantitative variables were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The Mann-Whitney test was used for intergroup comparisons of DMFT, deft, and knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) scores related to oral health. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while a p-value less than 0.01 was considered highly significant. Additionally, a linear regression analysis was performed to predict DMFT and deft scores using knowledge, attitude, and practice as predictor variables.

Results

At the end of 12 months, 36 children were lost to follow-up and were excluded from the analysis. Among the 224 children evaluated, 116 were male, and 108 were female. In the experimental group, there was a highly significant difference in total caries experience (deft + DMFT) (p = 0.000), with a reduction in the mean number of decayed teeth (dt + DT) and missing teeth (et + MT) scores, along with an increase in filled teeth (ft + FT) scores after 12 months. Additionally, knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) scores related to oral health significantly improved among children in the experimental group over the same period (Table 1).

In the control group, there was a highly significant difference in total caries experience (deft + DMFT) scores at the end of 12 months (p = 0.000), with a significant decrease in decayed teeth (dt + DT) scores (p = 0.000). However, there was no significant increase in the number of filled teeth. Similar to the experimental group, knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) scores related to oral health showed a significant improvement in the control group over the 12-month period (Table 2). On intergroup comparison, there was a highly significant difference (p = 0.000) in the mean decayed (dt + DT), missing (et + MT), filled (ft + FT), and total caries experience (deft + DMFT) scores at the 12-month interval. Additionally, there was a significant improvement in attitude and practice scores. However, no significant change was observed in the knowledge score (Table 3). Both groups exhibited weak negative correlations between dental caries experience and KAP scores, except for knowledge and practice scores in the experimental group. However, neither group demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between dental caries experience and KAP scores. This suggests that KAP may not have a substantial impact on changes in the oral health status of children in both the experimental and control groups (Table 4). The results of linear regression analysis on factors affecting oral health status in children is shown in Table 5. Predictors in the model were KAP scores, and the dependent variable was the total (deft+DMFT) score. Knowledge, attitude scores in experimental group, and knowledge score in control group showed positive coefficients, while other predictors showed negative coefficients with dependent variable However, none of the coefficients were statistically significant. This suggests that knowledge, attitude and practice scores do not predict changes in the oral health status of children in either the experimental or control groups (Table 5).

Discussion

Intraoral cameras (IOCs) facilitate communication between oral health providers and patients, motivating them to adhere to treatment recommendations. Improved patient compliance and acceptance ultimately contribute to treatment success (9). Additionally, IOCs play an integral role in tele dentistry by enabling screening and capturing images for diagnosis and ongoing assessment of oral diseases (10).

An IOC enhances patient compliance and oral hygiene by visually demonstrating oral health conditions and practices, empowering patients to actively engage in their care and improve oral health outcomes (9).

In the present study, there was a significant reduction in total caries experience (deft + DMFT), with a decrease in the mean number of decayed (dt + DT) and missing (et + ET) teeth, along with an increase in filled (ft + FT) teeth after 12 months in the experimental group, where the IOC was used for oral health education. These findings align with those of Gupta et al. (11), who reported similar improvements in oral health when audio-visual aids were used for oral health education in children aged 5–10 years.

Conversely, Bhardwaj et al. (12) conducted a study among 12–15-year-old children, using traditional teaching resources such as chalk, blackboards, models, posters, and charts for oral health education. However, they observed no significant reduction in caries status after four months. Similarly, Jamie R et al. (4) implemented a school-based oral health education program in Brazil for 5–7-year-old children, incorporating lectures for teachers, parents, and children, but found no significant decrease in caries incidence after three years. These findings highlight the effectiveness of IOC as a tool for oral health promotion.

In the present study, the questionnaire used to assess knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) regarding oral health included various aspects of oral hygiene. Assigning weight to different questions was challenging; therefore, a score of 1 was assigned for every correct answer and 0 for an incorrect response. A significant improvement in KAP scores was observed in both groups post-intervention (p < 0.005), emphasizing the importance of implementing oral health education programs specifically tailored for children. Supporting this, a study by Sarayuthpitak et al. (13) in Thailand demonstrated that the use of a smartphone endomicroscope in an oral hygiene promotion program significantly enhanced KAP among 10–12-year-old school students.

Parental knowledge has been positively correlated with children’s dental caries status in previous literature (14, 15, 16, 17). However, Basil et al. (18) found no significant association between children’s oral health and their parents’ KAP levels. Notably, limited studies have examined the direct correlation between children’s knowledge and their dental caries status. In the present study, a positive correlation was found between children’s knowledge and dental caries status in the experimental group, whereas a weak negative correlation was observed in the control group.

While oral health promotion initiatives can enhance children’s knowledge of oral health, their impact on behaviour and clinical disease indices remains uncertain, as noted by Kay and Locker (19) and Nakre et al. (20). Oral health education (OHE) programs have been shown to improve oral health status, knowledge, and behaviour, particularly in increasing brushing frequency, duration, and fluoride toothpaste use among 6–12-year-old children following schoolbased interventions (21). In the present study, oral health behaviours improved in both groups from baseline. Educating children alongside their parents enhances self-perception and self-improvement; however, parental involvement is crucial in fostering lasting behavioural changes and instilling lifelong oral health habits in children.

A study conducted in Malaysia by Tom (22) reported that participants who underwent oral health screening using a digital intraoral camera expressed high satisfaction with the camera’s role in improving their understanding of their oral health status. The IOC facilitated better comprehension of treatment plans and enhanced interactions with dentists. This underscores the potential of such technology in promoting effective oral health education. Additionally, Pate et al. (23) observed that both children and parents preferred intraoral cameras over traditional visual dental examinations. Caruso et al. (24) further suggested that intraoral scanners could serve as valuable visual aids and communication tools, fostering better interactions between parents, dentists, and children.

Most parents take their children to the dentist only when the child experiences tooth pain. In our study, this approach was specifically addressed by reminding parents about their child’s oral health status and recommending preventive and interceptive treatments. Notably, the control group exhibited a significant reduction in the decay component of the caries index, indicating a shift in dental visit behaviour.

Beyond its role as an educational tool, an IOC has various other potential applications. It helps patients visualize areas with higher biofilm accumulation that are difficult to clean, thereby improving their understanding, attitudes, and oral hygiene practices. This encourages them to adopt appropriate oral hygiene techniques and use proper interdental aids as demonstrated by their oral health provider (25). The use of an IOC has been shown to improve clinical, behavioural, and psychological aspects associated with gingival health in patients diagnosed with gingivitis. Incorporating an IOC during dental visits has been an effective strategy in motivating patients to regulate their toothbrushing and flossing habits and to sustain these behaviours over time (26).

The reliability of photographic examination using an intraoral camera was compared to standard clinical examination techniques for detecting caries in the primary teeth of 5–6-year-old children in a study by Mehta et al. (27). The photographic method demonstrated a sensitivity of 77% for one examiner and 86% for another, with a specificity of 98% for both, suggesting that the intraoral camera is a valuable tool in dental caries research (27). Furthermore, enamel caries was more accurately detected by examiners using an IOC compared to smartphones (28). IOC technology also serves as a supplementary tool for detecting occult or hidden dentinal lesions (29).

However, this study had certain limitations. The small sample size, narrow age range, and limited follow-up period may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data, such as KAP scores, introduces potential bias, as parents and children may provide socially desirable responses. The loss of participants during follow-up may also introduce bias. To enhance the generalizability of future research, studies should consider larger, more diverse populations, longer follow-up durations, and more frequent reinforcements at closer intervals, which could yield better outcomes. Future studies could explore the integration of captured IOC images to remind parents about treatment needs and emphasize early intervention. These images could also be incorporated into dental education software to create interactive simulations, enabling students to practice diagnosis and treatment planning in a controlled environment. Moreover, artificial intelligence integration with IOCs could enable automated image analysis, allowing for earlier and more accurate detection of caries and other oral health issues. Assessing children’s behaviour in relation to oral health is essential for understanding their habits and overall development. Through comprehensive assessments, targeted interventions can be developed to create more effective strategies for improving children’s oral health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study found that using an intraoral camera as an educational aid significantly improved oral health status and enhanced attitude and practice scores among children. However, knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) did not predict changes in oral health status in either group.
