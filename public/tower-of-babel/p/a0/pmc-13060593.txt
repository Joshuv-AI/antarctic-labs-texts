Scalable generalized meta-spanners enabling parallel multitasking optical manipulation
Authors: Tianyue Li, Wenyu Gao, Boyan Fu, Tianhua Shao, Yuchao Fu, Siarhei Zavatski, Jeeban Kumar Nayak, Shaohui Yan, Xiaohao Xu, Shuming Wang, Baoli Yao, Zhenlin Wang, Shining Zhu, Olivier J. F. Martin, C. T. Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Optical manipulation techniques offer exceptional contactless control but are fundamentally limited in their ability to perform parallel multitasking. To achieve high-density, versatile manipulation with subwavelength photonic devices, it is essential to sculpt light in multiple dimensions. Here, we overcome this challenge by introducing generalized optical meta-spanners (GOMSs) based on metasurfaces, which generate speckle-free, customizable optical vortices that suppress diffractive losses. As a result, several advanced functionalities are simultaneously achieved, including longitudinally varying manipulation and in-plane spanner arrays, which outperforms the same operations realized by conventional donut-shaped orbital flows. Furthermore, the particle dynamics are reconfigurable by simply switching the input and output polarizations, facilitating robust multichannel control. We experimentally validate our approach through the stable manipulation of both single particle and particle ensembles, demonstrating scalable multitasking stability for numerous simultaneous optical spanners, and advancing metadevices from wavefront sculptors to particle manipulators. We envision that the GOMS will catalyze innovations in cross-disciplinary fields such as targeted drug delivery and cell-level biomechanics.

INTRODUCTION

The transfer of momentum from light to matter gives rise to optical forces, a phenomenon that is fundamental to the principles of optical tweezers (OTs) (1–3). OTs use the intensity gradient of a tightly focused Gaussian beam to generate a three-dimensional restoring potential, enabling the noncontact trapping and precise manipulation of micro- and nanoscale objects (1–6). Beyond simple trapping, the exchange of linear momentum can facilitate other intriguing effects such as particle pulling (7–9), optical binding (10–13), and laser cooling (14–16). The diversity of optical forces expands with more complex light fields. For instance, Laguerre-Gaussian beams carry orbital angular momentum (OAM), which provides a rotational degree of freedom (DoF) to optical manipulation (17–25). These beams are characterized by an optical vortex (OV), a helical phase front and a null intensity core, around which the Poynting vector spirals (26–28). This beam profile exerts an optical torque or azimuthal force on particles (29), forming the basis of optical spanners (OSs; Fig. 1A) that are applied in optical sensing (30, 31) and microfluidics (32).

Precise optical manipulation with OSs requires subtle control over the phase and amplitude of the optical field. However, conventional devices for generating OVs face substantial limitations. For example, early in the 21st century, the proposed Q-plate (33, 34) was primarily used to generate conjugate topological charges, while spatial light modulators (35) introduce system complexity and are typically limited to a single hologram per configuration. In the past decade, metasurfaces (MSs) have emerged as a miniaturized solution, enabling streamlined OV generation (36). One prominent example is the ultrathin J-plate. It can integrate multiple independent OV patterns into a single element. As a result, the distinct OV pattern can be obtained by simply selecting the corresponding circular polarization (CP) channel (37, 38). More recently, control over OVs has further extended from the J-plate to the total angular momentum (TAM) plate (39–41), providing spatially varying topological charges along the optical field propagation direction via output polarization selection, thereby unlocking a previously unknown DoF for parallel, multitasking information processing (42–45).

Although MSs have been found helpful in overcoming the issues of the bulk systems based on discrete optics, their efficiency in advanced applications of OSs remain limited. This is because the primary function of OS-oriented MSs has long been focused on the force magnitude rather than controlling the particle trajectory (46, 47). As a result, OS operations typically appeared visually and physically monotonous. Only after the recent advent of generalized optical vortices (GOVs) has this paradigm been broken, enabling the shaping of OAM mode profiles into customized, noncircular geometries (48–52). Unlike conventional OAM modes, which typically exhibit a ring-shaped intensity maximum whose radius is determined by the topological charge ℓ, with larger ℓ values corresponding to intensity peaks farther from the center, the intensity distribution of a GOV varies with radial distance from the center. Theoretically, the topological charge of a GOV is a mixture of different ℓ values. Previous implementations of GOVs have relied on azimuthal phase modulation in Fourier space, requiring a lens for projection into real space. However, this approach inevitably introduces diffraction losses at the imaging center (50, 51), fundamentally limiting its potential as general OSs (GOSs). Similarly, spatial caustics fields, another method for engineering propagation-variant light fields, are also unsuitable as GOS candidates due to their uneven intensity distribution and lack of a phase gradient along closed paths (53).

To concurrently address the limitations of bulky systems with fixed patterns, and the growing demand for parallel multitasking micromanipulation, a portable GOS platform is required. As depicted in Fig. 1B, such a device, named the G-plate, would serve as an advanced alternative to J-plates and TAM plates. It can serve to encode OV pattern into arbitrary intensity contours, while preserving multifunctionality through controls such as incident-light switching and propagation-variation with mixtures of district ℓ values, thereby enabling complex and dynamic optical manipulation.

In this work, we demonstrate two classes of MS-based GOSs, each driving distinct rotational motions in closed trajectories. The first class generates multiple GOVs along the longitudinal direction, while the second does this within the same transverse plane. Unlike prior GOV plates, our complex-amplitude MSs transcend phase-only modulation in Fourier space by superimposing multiple Bessel rings to directly generate structured light fields, having phase-gradient forces to concurrently supply the intensity-gradient forces essential for trapping particles in polygonal trajectories during rotation. Furthermore, we use CP as the switching key, using two incident and two output CP states to produce three independent optical patterns. Both ultracompact metadevices enable on-chip particle delivery under configurable GOV profiles, showcasing customized micromanipulation capabilities. Crucially, this platform is scalable via arbitrary light-field engineering of the MS. The number and types of GOSs operating concurrently can be readily expanded beyond those demonstrated here.

RESULTS

Concept of generalized optical meta-spanner

Figure 1C presents a schematic of a generalized optical meta-spanner (GOMS), actuated by a propagation-varying generalized OV (PVGOV). The system features two rotating wavefronts, represented by different colors along the optical axis, which enable particle delivery across varying longitudinal depths. Under CP plane wave illumination, the metadevice operates in three distinct modes. When arranged to transmit the incident LCP or RCP light in cross-polarized configuration, the device produces an output with reversed chirality (RCP or LCP, respectively), each associated with a unique GOV at a different transverse plane. In addition, filtering the same handedness of incident CP (LCP or RCP) yields a cohanded output channel, thereby generating an additional GOV mode. Thus, these polarization-selection operations together give rise to a total of six distinct GOV types. The rotational capability arises because the GOV has a helical phase φ, introduced via the OV flux relation r × g⊥ with g ∝ I(r)∇φ (23), which provides both an intensity-gradient force and a phase-gradient force. The intensity-gradient force confines microscopic objects near the GOV, while the phase-gradient force drives their rotation around it. This fundamental GOV structure underpins the particle-rotation capability of the GOS, which can be selectively observed at distinct planes in free space.

PVGOV generation

Differing from conventional optical-spanner systems, our approach directly projects the sculpted GOV field into a propagation-varying framework, necessitating full complex-amplitude modulation. In practice, this operation is performed in two sequential steps: (i) acquisition of a phase-only GOV in the Fourier domain and (ii) superposition of the resulting Bessel rings to ensure intensity mapping.

According to the generalized Snell’s law, the abrupt phase change in the azimuthal direction at the air-interface under normal incidence must satisfy the condition ntsin(θt)=1k0r·dφ(θ)dθ, where nt is the refractive index of the medium after transmission. In Fourier space (k-space), where a position is described by the transverse wave vector k⊥, this phase gradient corresponds to a local change in the transverse wave vector. In cylindrical coordinates, the phase gradient is described as ∇⊥φ=1rdφdθeˆθ, and its magnitude defines the additional momentum ∆k as ∣∆k∣=∣∇⊥φ∣=k⊥sinθt≈ntk0sinθt, where the azimuthal angle of this wave vector in k-space is θ′=θ+π2 with the unit vector is eˆθ′. When a plane wave with intensity I0 impinges on this phase-discontinuous interface, the intensity distribution in k-space I0′ can be related to the real-space intensity through power conservation. For a differential area rdrdθ on the MS, the corresponding energy in k-space is I0′∆kd(∆k)dθ′=I0rdrdθ. Using the relation ∆k=dφ(θ)rdθ (for a fixed θ, treating dφ/dθ as constant), the differential dr can be expressed as dr=−1∆k2dφdθd(∆k). Substituting and solving for I0′ gives the intensity profile in k-space (50)I0′={I0∆k4(dφ(θ)dθ)2,∆k (θ)≥∆kmin(θ)0,∆k (θ)<∆kmin(θ)(1)

Here, ∆kmin(θ) represents the minimum change in wave vector corresponding to the scattered light at angle θ, which occurs ∆kmin(θ)=dφ(θ)rmdθ at the outermost radius of the MS rm. This equation reveals that under the ray-optics approximation, the intensity peaks at ∆k=∆kmin and decays with the fourth power of ∆k. Consequently, the most intense contour in k-space defined by ∆k=∆kmin has an intensity profile proportional to the phase gradient. Using the coordinate correspondence θ′=θ+π2, we find the modulated intensity k⊥(θ′)∝dφ(θ)dθ. This simple relation provides a direct design rule for generating arbitrary closed-loop intensity profiles, one can inversely engineer GOV with the azimuthal phase profile φ(θ) accordingly. The optical force governing particle motion comprises both a phase-gradient force and an intensity-gradient force. At sharp turns or corners of the contour, the phase profile dφ(θ)dθ undergoes rapid changes, effectively resulting in a drastic phase change over an extremely short azimuthal range, and therefore leading to a localized intensity gradient induced trapping force. As a result, the intensity-gradient force can dominate over the phase-gradient force in these regions, trapping the particle and preventing continuous orbital motion. To avoid this and ensure smooth rotation, the phase gradient must be kept uniform along the entire closed path. Therefore, to drive a particle into continuous rotation with a GOV, it must interact with the field along a trajectory of uniform arc length. This is achieved through equal-arc-length sampling: one first selects the total number of sampling points, computes the total contour length via arc-length integration, and then assigns phase values uniformly according to the arc-length coordinate rather than the azimuthal angle. This ensures the sampling points are distributed evenly along the trajectory, and the intensity-gradient force exerted on the particle also becomes more uniform, thereby preventing the particle from being trapped or pinned at localized points of excessive intensity. A comparison between equal-arc-length and non–equal-arc-length sampling is illustrated in fig. S2 (54). After uniformly distributing the total arc length of the target contour among the chosen number of sampling points, we calculate the cumulative arc length from the initial sample to each subsequent point. By evaluating the difference between each actual sampled length and its corresponding target length, while locating the position in space where this difference vanishes, we recover the precise radial coordinate k⊥ and azimuthal angle θ’ for each point in the Fourier space, thus enforcing a uniform azimuthal phase gradient via k⊥(θ′)∝dφ(θ)dθ.

Multichannel MS implementation

On the basis of the above conceptual procedure for generating a PVGOV, we implement a Jones-matrix–engineered MS to perform the required GOV transformation. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the predefined azimuthal phase gradient can be mapped into a U by V matrix for sampling. The matrix is subsequently integrated with the amplitude modulation, ultimately producing a complex field composed of interleaved pixels. The detailed pipeline for phase-map design is provided in Supplementary Text Section C and fig. S2. To integrate multiple, distinct phase patterns onto an individual MS, each nanopillar is designed to exhibit a strong refractive-index contrast across four CP channels; this behavior is captured by the Jones-matrix formalism (54, 56, 57)J(x,y)=R(θ)[Toeiϕo(x,y)00Teeiϕe(x,y)]R(−θ)(6)where ϕo and ϕe denote the pillar’s phase shifts for the ordinary and extraordinary axes, respectively, and R(θ) is the in-plane rotation matrix by the pillar’s orientation angle θ. Because of the unitarity of the Jones matrix, the co-CP channels simply replicate each other without polarization conversion, while the cross-CP channels exploit the birefringent difference between the nanopillar’s ordinary and extraordinary axes. The MS is assumed to perform as a pure phase shaper with equal transmittance (To = Te = T) for both axes to achieve maximum CP conversion efficiency, therefore the Jones matrix defined in the CP basis [eL eR]T can be simplified to a purely diagonal form J(x,y)=Teiϕr(x,y)·[cos(δ2)isin(δ2)e−i2θisin(δ2)ei2θcos(δ2)](7)where the phase difference δ governs the polarization state of both the incident and transmitted beams (see Supplementary Text Section C for a full derivation). To implement three independent GOV channels simultaneously, we decompose the overall Jones matrix as J=J~1+J~2, where J~1(xi,yi,θi) and J~2(xj,yj,θj) correspond to two different δ, These submatrices are then interleaved across the MS as shown at the end of the encoding flowchart. As the unit cell, we use amorphous silicon (α-Si) nanopillars with specific height (H), length (L), width (W), and in-plane rotation angle (θ) grown on fused-quartz substrates; the pillars stand freely in air on their top faces. Their refractive index dispersion is provided in Supplementary Text Section D and fig. S3. Figure 2B shows the simulated CP conversion efficiencies across our phase-library of nanopillars. From this library, we select two sets of nanopillars optimized for the cross-CP and co-CP channels, respectively. Their geometric parameters and the corresponding ordinary and extraordinary phase shift are listed in Fig. 2 (C and D). The detailed phase diagram as a function of geometric dimensions is provided in fig. S4. Furthermore, we simulated the supercell composed of two types of meta-atoms (described by J~1 and J~2). The underlying mechanism for decoupling incident CP light is explained in detail, with quantitative simulation results presented in Supplementary Text Section E and fig. S5.

The measured overall transmission and polarization efficiency of this MS sample are provided in Supplementary Text Section G.

We note that, while polarization multiplexing can also be extended to other polarized bases or cascaded in multilayer MSs for channel scalabilities, a fundamental constraint exists for single-layer MSs generating multichannel GOV-based fields. Unlike conventional holograms, GOV profiles are defined by concentric azimuthal phase distributions and cannot be optimized for noise suppression via iterative methods (58). This inherently limits the number of fully independent polarization channels achievable in a single planar device. It is precisely this limitation that motivated our development of propagation-multiplexed GOVs along the longitudinal direction, which provides a viable pathway to expand the number of usable channels without increasing the planar footprint or layout complexity.

Micromanipulation demonstration for GOMS

We next demonstrate the micromanipulation capability of our system by using three distinct GOV shapes and gold particles with random diameters ranging from 0.8 to 1.5 μm. The optical setup used for these experiments is schematically illustrated in Fig. 4A, and its detailed description can be found in Materials and Methods. Figure 4B depicts the microfluidic trapping chamber, which consists of a pair of microscope slide and coverslip separated by the gap of hd = 140 μm and filled with the suspension of gold particles (refractive index ngold = 0.26 + 6.97i) in water (ns = 1.33). The forces acting on each particle are the scattering force Fsca due to radiation pressure, the intensity-gradient force Fgrad, the gravitational (G) and buoyancy, and the drag force and the reaction force Fcover from the coverslip, which together establish mechanical equilibrium. Note that to achieve smooth particle transport, one must carefully align the incident light beam to ensure near-normal incidence and full illumination of the MS sample.

The measured orbital velocities for the triangular, quadrilateral, pentagonal, hexagonal, heptagonal, and circular trajectories corresponding to Fig. 4 are 26.8, 21.2, 25.5, 21.4, 18.2, and 24.4 μm/s, respectively. Notably, while the polygonal trajectories in our work were specifically chosen, our approach is fundamentally capable of encoding phase profiles for virtually any geometric shape. In practice, however, realizing smooth and complete particle transport along an arbitrarily designed closed path requires careful consideration of intensity uniformity in relation to the phase gradient. Optimizing this balance is essential to ensure homogeneous optical forces and reliable particle delivery.

Necklace rotation via MS-enabled GOSA

Beyond longitudinal multiplexing, our MS can simultaneously encode multiple GOV fields in the same plane to form a transverse, multiplexed generalized OS array (GOSA). As illustrated in Fig. 5A, the left panels show optical microscopy and SEM images of the fabricated GOSA sample, while the overall phase-encoding workflow is depicted on the right side of Fig. 5A. The principle is to independently encode each GOV phase profile and superimpose it with a unique deflection phase φjd=kpsinχj, where p is the deflected direction of light field and χ is the deflect angle. This produces an in-plane GOSA capable of parallel, multitarget micromanipulation. The composite phase distribution loaded onto the MS is φGOVA=∑j=1Narg[ei(φjGOV+φjd)]. For each individual GOV, we superimpose a pair of deflection angles (χjx,χjy) onto the off-axis coordinates (x, y), the sum of these deflections and the basic GOV phase yield the total phase profile. The three encoded total phases are shown in fig. S13. Figure 5 (B to D) shows the measured focal-plane intensity patterns for these three GOSA channels after elimination of zeroth order. Each GOVA is addressable independently, and switching channels reshapes the field distribution. To demonstrate parallel manipulation, we simultaneously trap four arrays of 3.3-μm-diameter SiO2 beads at the focal plane and recorded their motion (Fig. 5, E to G). The entire micromanipulation process is demonstrated in movies S7 to S9, and the corresponding force metrics are shown in fig. S14 and table S1. As can be seen from the figures, the particles in the trapping regions are arranged into chains resembling necklace shapes. Using blue-dye–labeled beads as fiducials, we observe that the upper-left and lower-right necklaces, which have opposite topological charges, rotate CCW, whereas the other two necklaces (tracked via red-dye beads) rotate clockwise (CW). It should be noted that the slight deviation of the particle from the intended polygonal trajectory is due to multipolar gradient forces, which confine the particle to a region where the optical intensity is neither maximum nor minimum, a regime known as gray optical trapping (59). However, since multipole effects are generally negligible for low-index dielectric particles, the trajectories of the SiO2 spheres closely follow the GOV contours, as shown in Fig. 5 (E to G). Crucially, all four necklaces move without mutual interference, confirming the possibility to simultaneously and independently control the particle orbital movement with the MS-enabled GOSA.

To further integrate both PVGOV and GOSA to support simultaneous longitudinal and transverse GOV multiplexing, this can be achieved using a MS array (60–62) in which each MS generates a set of PVGOVs by arranging them laterally during fabrication, a PVGOV array can be realized. This parallel processing requires no additional optical elements, offering an avenue for multitasking manipulation.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we report a MS-based GOS that combines PVGOVs with full complex-amplitude control, overcoming the field-shaping and single-function limitations of conventional optical micromanipulation. Our platform leverages both the inherent polarization degrees of freedom and the propagation direction to achieve an on-axis varying GOV, while simultaneously generating multiple GOV arrays in the focal plane, enabling the creation of six effective on-axis and 12 off-axis OSs. The maximum number of usable channels can be estimated based on the minimum optical power required for each GOV to perform its intended micromanipulation task. Moreover, we introduce a unified complex-amplitude design paradigm that enables arbitrary-shaped OSs with both trapping and rotation capabilities, thereby resolving the inherent trade-off between particle transport diversity and trapping stability in phase-only devices. An orthogonal circular-polarization channel-switching scheme provides ultraefficient, multitask, reconfigurable control. This ultracompact platform not only obviates the complexity and limited functionality of bulky system and integrates with optical fibers or wave guides (63–66) but also extends the scope of optical micromanipulation through space-programmable GOV landscapes.

The broader impact of our GOMS lies in other disciplines, offering distinct capabilities for fundamental science and advanced manufacturing. For instance, it provides a unique testbed for investigating fluctuation theorems through simulated entropy variation dictated by trajectory winding numbers (67) and for conceiving continuous-time crystals with customized looped lattice through force field (68). Practically, its parallel manipulation scheme enables previously unknown microfabrication paradigms, such as using multiple trapped particles that are self-assembled and dynamically reconfigurable into micronozzles for mask-free polymer extrusion (69, 70).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Principle of PVGOV generation

Numerical simulation

Our MS sample was designed by positioning α-Si nanopillars on a fused-silica substrate. The modulation phase generated was associated with the structural dimensions of the nanopillar. To find the optimal parameters conducive to the intended phase modulation, arrays of nanopillars with distinct geometric configurations were modeled using the finite-difference time-domain method. These nanopillars were organized in a square lattice having a lattice constant of Λ = 600 nm and a uniform height of H = 800 nm (Fig. 2A). The refractive indices of α-Si used were within the near-infrared wavelength range, determined from ellipsometry assessments of an α-Si thin film. The boundary conditions were designated as periodic in the in-plane directions to model the periodic nanopillar arrays, while for transmission situations, the boundary conditions were identified as perfect match layers in the light incidence direction. A plane wave with a wavelength λ = 1064 nm was used to discern the transmission coefficients and phase shifts along the x and y axes, corresponding to the lateral and vertical dimensions of nanopillars.

Sample fabrication

The silica-based wafer underwent ultrasonic cleansing using acetone, alcohol, and deionized water. This was succeeded by the application of an 800 nm layer of amorphous silicon (α-Si) onto the pristine silica wafer via Plasma-Enhanced Chemical Vapor Deposition (Oxford PlasmaPro 100 PECVD). Afterward, the silicon surface received a spin-coated layer of conductive, positive electron-beam resist (ZEP520A, Zeon) approximately 200 nm thick. The intended MS designs were then etched onto the CSAR-6200 resist using an Electron Beam Lithography system, specifically the Elionix ELS-F125-G8. Postexposure, the patterns were developed in a photoresist developer, transferring them onto a chromium (Cr) mask. Subsequent steps involved depositing a patterned chromium (Cr) layer onto the sample via electron beam evaporation, creating a Cr mask on the thin film for further etching processes. The etching was performed using an inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etcher with a mixture of O2 and CHF3 gases. Last, the remaining Cr mask was removed using a cerium ammonium nitrate solution, completing the fabrication of our MS. The visualized workflow refers to fig. S15.

Micromanipulation experiment

In this experiment, the light source was a continuous-wave fiber laser with a wavelength of 1064 nm (VFLS-1064-B-SF-HP, Connet Laser Technology Co. Ltd., China; rated power: 5 W). The laser beam was first modulated into horizontally linear-polarized light using a half-wave plate (HWP) and a polarizing beam splitter (PBS). It was then converted into circularly polarized (CP) light by passing through a quarter-wave plate (QWP1). The handedness of the incident CP light was flexibly controlled by adjusting the orientation of QWP1; this combination of HWP, PBS, and QWP1 served as the polarization generator for the CP state input. Subsequently, the beam passed through a collimating and beam-reducing system (lenses L1 and L2), was steered by mirrors M1 and M2, and was lastly compressed to a size precisely covering the MS), which was mounted on a manual translation stage. The light transmitted and modulated by the MS was collected by an objective lens O1 [40×, numerical aperture (NA) 0.95, CFI Plan Apo, Nikon Inc., Japan] and then processed by a spatial filtering system consisting of lenses L3 and L4 and a pinhole. At the Fourier plane of lens L3, a diffraction pattern was formed, which allowed effective filtering of either the higher-order diffraction components (for MS sample 1) or the zero-order component (for MS sample 2) in k-space. For output polarization analysis, a combination of a quarter-wave plate (QWP2) and a linear polarizer (P) was used as an analyzer to selectively extract the copolarized or cross-polarized component of the transmitted field. This selected component was then reflected by a mirror M3 toward a dichroic mirror (DM, DMR-1000SP, 1000 nm, LBTEK, China). The infrared portion reflected by the DM was directed by mirror M4 into the back aperture of an oil-immersion objective lens O2 (100×, NA = 1.45, CFI Plan Apo, Nikon Inc., Japan) to form the desired optical trap at its focal plane. For imaging, the sample chamber was illuminated by a light-emitting diode light source focused through a condenser lens. The sample image was formed by objective O2 and lens L5. The entire manipulation process was monitored and recorded in real-time by a CCD camera (Point Grey GS3U3-41C6M-C, FLIR Systems Inc., USA).

Considering the transmission loss and the extended beam profile, we use a relatively high optical power in experiments. For the longitudinal multiplexing scheme (Fig. 4), particle manipulation was operated at an output laser power of 2 W, and a power of 5 W is used for the transverse multiplexing (Fig. 5). However, it should be noted that the lowest power required for stable trapping should vary with the particle size, material and the field profile one wants to create.
