Factors associated with the willingness to accept tuberculosis preventive treatment among close contacts in Yantai, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xiaohua Ren, Yi Yang, Haifeng Hou, Lili Zhen, Jing Zhou, Huihui He, Yuelei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health threat. Understanding the determinants of willingness to accept TB preventive treatment (TPT) among close contacts is essential for improving prevention coverage and achieving the “End TB Strategy” targets.MethodsA cross-sectional survey using a consecutive sampling strategy was conducted in 2024 among close contacts of TB patients across four districts/cities in Yantai (Mouping, Penglai, Haiyang, and Longkou). The questionnaire collected data on sociodemographic, exposure history, lifestyle habits, health status, and TB-related knowledge. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with willingness to accept TPT.ResultsA total of 565 close contacts were surveyed. The overall willingness to receive TPT was high at 86.40% (n = 488), though the core TB knowledge awareness rate was 62.30%. Multivariate analysis revealed that willingness was significantly higher among those in the 25–44 age group (OR = 11.641; 95% CI: 2.1–64.541; P = 0.005) and the 45–64 age group (OR = 7.258; 95% CI: 1.379–38.214; P = 0.019). Significant positive associations were found with the frequency of fruit intake (e.g., daily consumption: OR = 17.708; 95% CI: 3.871–81.014; P < 0.001) and, most strongly, with better knowledge of latent TB infection and TPT (all correct answers: OR = 30.159; 95% CI: 11.24–80.919; P < 0.001). Conversely, various occupational groups (e.g., farmers, self-employed, retired individuals) and certain residential relationships (different rooms in the same dwelling) were negatively associated with willingness.ConclusionsClose contacts demonstrated generally high acceptance of TPT. Age, occupation, living arrangements, lifestyle behaviors, and TB-related knowledge were identified as significant factors associated with willingness to accept treatment. Strengthening targeted education and improving awareness of latent TB infection may enhance uptake of TPT and support TB control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26875-7.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), remains a major global infectious disease [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2024, TB has re-emerged as the leading cause of mortality from a single infectious agent, surpassing COVID-19 [2]. It is estimated that approximately one-quarter of the global population harbors a TB infection [3]. In 2023, China recorded an estimated 741,000 new TB cases, ranking third globally [4]. As a Class B legally notifiable infectious disease [5], the reported incidence of pulmonary TB is the second highest among all Class B infectious diseases in China [6], posing a persistent threat to public health security. The vast majority of individuals infected with MTB transition into an asymptomatic and non-contagious state known as Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI). Studies indicate that the lifetime risk of LTBI progressing to active TB disease is 5–10%, with half of these cases occurring within the first two years following infection [7, 8]. Previous research has demonstrated that TB preventive treatment(TPT) for individuals with LTBI provides a protective efficacy ranging from 60% to 90% [9]. Consequently, in the absence of an effective prophylactic TB vaccine, this intervention has become the cornerstone measure for arresting the progression of latent infection [10]. In 2015, the WHO launched “The End TB Strategy,” which explicitly identifies the screening and TPT of high-risk populations as one of its key pillars [11, 12].

Despite a gradual increase in global TPT coverage (a significant rise from 3.9 million people in 2022 to 4.7 million in 2023 [2]), the scale of implementation remains critically insufficient. The number of people initiating treatment in 2023 reached only 10.44% of the ambitious 2023–2027 global target of 45 million individuals [2]. In China, a total of 226,000 people received TPT between 2018 and 2022, representing merely 12.8% of the commitment outlined in the UN High-Level Meeting target [13]. These gaps highlight the urgent need to identify key determinants of TPT uptake to inform more effective intervention strategies.

Previous studies [9, 14–17] have identified multiple factors associated with acceptance of tuberculosis preventive treatment, including age, education level, perceived disease severity, health literacy, and concerns regarding drug safety and cost. However, most existing studies focus on specific subpopulations (e.g., college students) [16, 18] or emphasize general TB knowledge [18, 19] without distinguishing between core TB knowledge and specific understanding of LTBI and TPT.

Guided by established health behavior theories, including the Health Belief Model (HBM) and socio-ecological framework, health-related decision-making is understood as being shaped by individual characteristics, cognitive perceptions, and environmental context [20, 21]. Accordingly, this study integrates sociodemographic factors, health-related behaviors, cognitive variables (including knowledge and risk perception), and contextual exposure characteristics to examine factors statistically associated with willingness to accept TPT among household contacts. In this framework, willingness is conceptualized as a behavioral intention shaped by individual, cognitive, and contextual influences.

This study surveyed close contacts of pulmonary TB patients across four districts/cities in Yantai during 2024 to assess their willingness to accept TPT. By analyzing and comparing data on close contacts’ basic sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, health status, and core knowledge awareness, the research aims to identify the factors associated with acceptance, clarify the underlying motivations for treatment refusal, and ultimately provide evidence-based strategies for enhancing TPT coverage and reducing the overall incidence of TB.

Subjects and methods

Participants

This study employed a multi-stage sampling strategy. Four districts/cities in Yantai Municipality (Mouping, Penglai, Haiyang, and Longkou) were purposively selected [22] to represent different epidemic levels. Consecutive sampling was used to enroll household close contacts of newly diagnosed pulmonary TB patients identified between January 1 and December 31, 2024. Eligible contacts were identified through routine contact investigation records maintained by designated TB control institutions. All individuals meeting the eligibility criteria during the study period were invited to participate. The definitions of “household close contact” and related variables are provided in Sect. 2.2 (Relevant definitions). Individuals who were unreachable, declined participation, or were unable to complete the questionnaire were excluded.

The selection of these sites was based on two criteria: (1) representativeness: From 2015 to 2023, the average annual reported TB incidence rates in Longkou City, Mouping District, Penglai District, and Haiyang City were 31.19/100,000, 30.96/100,000, 27.78/100,000, and 26.50/100,000, respectively, representing different TB epidemic levels within Yantai. Furthermore, these sites exhibit diversity in geographical environment (coastal/inland), population structure, and socioeconomic characteristics, thereby allowing the sample to better represent the overall situation in Yantai and enhance the generalizability of the findings; (2) feasibility: Local TB prevention and control institutions possess a solid operational foundation and high staff cooperation, which provided crucial assurance for the identification, enrollment, and follow-up of new cases and their household contacts, facilitating smooth implementation and stringent data quality control.

Relevant definitions

Index case: an active pulmonary TB patient diagnosed by a designated TB medical institution according to the diagnostic criteria outlined in WS288—2017 Diagnosis of Pulmonary Tuberculosis [23].Household close contact: an individual who lived in the same residence with the index case for more than seven days during the period spanning from three months prior to diagnosis until 14 days after treatment initiation, or an individual who was continuously exposed to the index case in the same confined space for 8 hours or cumulatively for ≥ 40 hours [23, 24].

Questionnaire and quality control

This study utilized a self-designed questionnaire, the 2024 Survey of Close Contacts of Pulmonary Tuberculosis Patients in Yantai City, to conduct the cross-sectional survey. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study. An English version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1. The questionnaire was developed based on relevant domestic and international literature and similar studies, and its content validity was ensured through expert consultation. The survey was administered by staff from designated TB hospitals. After fully explaining the study’s significance and obtaining informed consent, subjects were guided to complete the online questionnaire.

The questionnaire items operationalized HBM constructs to measure individual perceptions (susceptibility, benefits), modifying factors (sociodemographics), and cues to action (exposure history). The content primarily covered seven aspects: (1) basic demographic information (age, sex, marital status, occupation, education level, household economic status, etc.); (2) lifestyle habits (smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise, and dietary habits); (3) core knowledge of TB prevention and control (including five core items: transmission routes, suspicious symptoms, treatment providers, subsidies for examination and treatment, and prognosis; ≥ 4 correct answers were defined as “good knowledge level”, and ≤ 3 correct answers as “poor knowledge level“ [18, 25]; (4) knowledge of LTBI and related facts (diagnosis and progression indicators of LTBI, and the importance of TPT); (5) exposure details (prior TB contact history and residential relationship with the index case); (6) willingness to accept TPT; and (7) reasons for refusing TPT.

Study quality and data reliability were ensured by strictly adhering to the defined criteria for screening study subjects. Before implementation, all staff involved in data collection at the designated TB medical institutions received uniform training to standardize the survey procedure. During data collection, subjects received health education, provided informed consent, and were guided to complete the online questionnaire, with on-site assistance provided for any questions. After questionnaire retrieval, researchers conducted logical checks and completeness reviews of all responses. Any identified missing items or logical errors were immediately followed up and clarified using the contact information provided by the subjects to guarantee data authenticity and integrity.

Statistical analysis

Data were managed and cleaned using Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages. Univariate analysis was conducted using the Chi-squared test (χ2 test), and multivariate analysis was performed using a Binary Logistic Regression model. Before conducting multivariate logistic regression, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). All VIF values were below 5 (ranging from 1.08 to 1.20), indicating no significant multicollinearity. A P-value of < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. Results

Basic information

This study included 565 household contacts of TB patients, with 565 valid questionnaires collected. Among them, 488 individuals agreed to receive TPT, yielding an acceptance rate of 86.37% (488/565). Among the 565 contacts, the male-to-female ratio was 0.60:1 (211/354); The predominant age group was 45–64 years (43.90%); The highest educational attainment was high school/vocational school or below (80.40%); Occupations were primarily farmers, employees of enterprises/institutions, or self-employed individuals (62.30%); The highest proportion had a household annual income ≤ 50,000 yuan (46.70%); 86.40% of respondents perceived their income level as middle or below compared to their peers; the highest proportion (45.30%) resided in separate residences from the index case; weekly physical exercise was primarily 1 time or less (62.50%); weekly fruit intake was highest at 1–2 times (38.20%); 92 close contacts (16.30%) had underlying conditions (including but not limited to hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease); core TB knowledge awareness rate was 62.30%. Regarding understanding of latent infection and preventive treatment, over 85% of close contacts answered one or more questions correctly, as shown in Table 2.

Univariate Analysis of TPT Willingness Among Yantai TB Contacts

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of willingness to undergo TPT

Variables with statistical significance in the univariate analysis were incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression model. Age, educational attainment, occupation, perceived economic status, residential relationship with index cases, weekly physical exercise frequency, weekly fruit consumption frequency, and knowledge of TB-related information were significantly associated with the likelihood of receiving TPT. Occupation [ farmer (OR = 0.026; 95%CI: 0.002–0.442; P = 0.011), self-employed (OR = 0.036; 95%CI: 0.002–0.621; P = 0.022), commercial/service industry employees (OR = 0.024; 95%CI: 0.001–0.483; P = 0.015), retire (OR = 0.042; 95%CI: 0.002–0.953; P = 0.047)], residence relationship with the index case [different rooms within the same residence (OR = 0.361; 95%CI: 0.134–0.973; P = 0.044)] were more likely to refuse prophylactic treatment. Age groups [25–44 (OR = 11.641; 95%CI: 2.1–64.541; P = 0.005), 45–64 (OR = 7.258; 95%CI: 1.379–38.214; P = 0.019)], fruit intake [1–2 times weekly among TB contacts (OR = 4.166; 95%CI: 1.263–13.739; P = 0.019), 4–5 times weekly (OR = 6.237; 95%CI;1.667–23.332; P = 0.007), daily fruit consumption (OR = 17.708; 95%CI: 3.871–81.014; P < 0.001)], and responses to questions about LTBI and TPT [1 correct answer (OR = 12.485; 95%CI: 5.358–29.089; P < 0.001), all correct (OR = 30.159; 95%CI: 11.24–80.919; P < 0.001)] were more willing to accept TPT (Table 4).

Discussion

Compared with the general population, close contacts of patients with infectious pulmonary TB have a significantly higher risk of infection with MTB and progression to active TB [26]. They represent a high-risk group for TB prevention and control. Systematic screening and preventive interventions for this population are key measures to achieve the strategic goal of “ending the TB epidemic.” This study focused on household contacts as a core group. Results showed a TPT acceptance rate of 86.40%, higher than the 70.34% (306/435) reported by Mayisha Daken in Urumqi [14], and 53.70% (58/108) reported by Jinying Wen [27]. This discrepancy may stem from factors such as differing population mobility patterns, economic development levels, social mobilization efforts, and the degree of emphasis placed on related work by local authorities across study regions.

The multivariate analysis revealed that age, occupation, residential relationship with the index case, dietary habits, and knowledge level regarding TB were significant factors associated with the willingness to accept TPT. Specifically, close contacts working in the commercial/service sector, farming, self-employment, or retirement, and those living in the same residence but separated from the index case, demonstrated a greater propensity to refuse TPT. These findings may be statistically associated with factors such as the nature of their work, economic status, cultural background, and psychosocial considerations within these groups.

By synthesizing the refusal factors identified in this study with prior research, several aspects emerge as playing critical roles: (1) Time costs and economic burden. The duration of TPT, typically lasting 2.5 to 9 months [10], combined with the necessity for regular follow-up visits, poses a severe challenge for occupational groups (such as service staff, farmers, and the self-employed) whose income is directly tied to attendance or who maintain non-fixed working hours. This burden can interrupt their business or productive work, leading to subsequent financial loss for the household [2]. The Global Tuberculosis Report 2024 [2] indicates that approximately 50% of people face catastrophic costs (exceeding 20% of annual household income) during diagnosis and treatment. Although this data primarily originates from the active TB patient population, the severe economic burden it highlights remains a crucial reference for understanding the trade-offs faced by close contacts when deciding to initiate long-term preventive treatment. (2) Concerns over drug safety and efficacy. Previous studies have indicated that TPT regimens can cause adverse drug reactions, such as drug-induced hepatitis, gastrointestinal upset, and hypersensitivity reactions [28, 29]. Such perceived risks significantly reduce the treatment willingness among close contacts. (3) Social stigma remains a concern. We found that contacts who lived in the same residence but in separate rooms were more likely to refuse TPT, consistent with findings by Ren et al. [30]. This may stem from concerns that accepting TPT would lead others to mistakenly believe they have active TB disease, resulting in discrimination or even jeopardizing their employment.

Therefore, it is essential to innovate relevant clinical service models, leveraging flexible solutions such as telemedicine, mobile health services, and community drug delivery to reduce the time cost of care for mobile working populations. To address widespread barriers like drug concerns, social discrimination, and knowledge gaps, we must utilize the cost-effectiveness of health education and promotion as core public health measures, continuously advancing initiatives like the “Hundreds of Thousands of Volunteers TB Knowledge Dissemination and Enhancement Action,” and actively promoting social mobilization [31]. Furthermore, tiered and segmented precision health education should be implemented: on one hand, focusing on clearly explaining the safety of shorter-course regimens and explicitly differentiating between “latent infection” and “active TB disease”; on the other hand, adopting differentiated communication strategies tailored to specific demographics. For example, traditional interpersonal methods, such as “physician-led lectures” or “neighborhood/village committee outreach,” should be prioritized for the elderly and agricultural workers [32], while enhanced online education is necessary for those who frequently use mobile devices, thereby comprehensively elevating public health literacy [33, 34]. Simultaneously, policy support systems must be further improved, gradually promoting free TB diagnosis and treatment through multi-channel financing to reduce the financial burden on both LTBI carriers and active TB patients [35].

Regarding positive factors, middle-aged and elderly individuals, those who regularly consume fruit, and those with better knowledge of LTBI and TPT exhibited a higher willingness to accept TPT. This could be associated with their health awareness and knowledge base. Unlike the conclusions drawn by Ren et al. [15], our study found that the 25–44 and 45–64 age groups were more willing to accept treatment than younger cohorts. This discrepancy may stem from our sample being predominantly concentrated in the middle-to-older age groups. For this cohort, the risk of severe disease and death from progression to active TB is substantially higher than for younger adults [8, 36, 37], meaning their perceived benefit of TPT outweighs the potential inconvenience. Close contacts who consume fruit weekly showed higher TPT acceptance, potentially due to their increased focus on personal health [38], where maintaining a healthy state translates into a greater inclination to use TPT as a safeguard against infection risk. However, it is noteworthy that some studies suggest good lifestyle habits may paradoxically reduce TPT acceptance willingness [39], implying that the link between lifestyle and acceptance may be modulated by other factors, warranting further exploration. Additionally, while some research found that BCG-vaccinated close contacts were more likely to refuse TPT [24, 40], many individuals are unaware if they were vaccinated [41, 42], thus this variable was excluded from our analysis. Previous studies [9, 16, 17, 19] have consistently shown that individuals with good core knowledge are more likely to accept TPT than those with poor knowledge. Although core knowledge awareness was not a statistically significant independent predictor in our multivariate model, the observed difference in treatment willingness between groups with differing awareness levels suggests that core knowledge remains an important factor. Our addition of specific questions on LTBI and TPT knowledge found that targeted dissemination of LTBI knowledge is particularly effective in boosting acceptance [39]. This suggests that the focus of health education should shift from general health promotion toward precise deployment of core messages, concentrating resources on conveying key information such as “latent infection is preventable and manageable” and “TPT is crucial for blocking disease progression“ [31].

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the findings reflect statistical associations only. Second, the purposive selection of study districts and inclusion of only reachable and consenting participants may have introduced selection bias, potentially leading to overestimation of willingness; therefore, generalizability beyond Yantai or similar contexts should be interpreted cautiously. Third, most variables were self-reported and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias, particularly given the stigma surrounding tuberculosis. Fourth, the study measured willingness to accept TPT rather than actual treatment uptake, and intention may not necessarily translate into behavior.

Additionally, some odds ratios were relatively large with wide confidence intervals, possibly due to uneven subgroup distributions, small reference group sizes, or sparse data bias, which may affect estimate stability. Thus, the magnitude of certain associations should be interpreted with caution. Although multicollinearity was within acceptable limits, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Future longitudinal studies with larger and more balanced samples are needed to validate these findings, clarify causal pathways, and examine the translation of willingness into actual treatment behavior.

Conclusion

In summary, household close contacts of pulmonary TB patients in this study demonstrated a high willingness to accept TPT, but this willingness is complexly associated with multi-dimensional factors spanning socioeconomic status, health cognition, and psychological behaviors. To achieve the “End TB Strategy” goal and effectively increase TPT coverage, a multi-layered intervention system is required: at the policy level, focus on building a supportive environment and alleviating socioeconomic barriers; at the community level, advance precise health education to dispel cognitive misconceptions and social discrimination; and at the individual level, strengthen risk communication to enhance the perceived benefit of TPT. These findings provide evidence to inform targeted public health strategies, although further longitudinal research is required to confirm causal relationships and evaluate intervention effectiveness.
