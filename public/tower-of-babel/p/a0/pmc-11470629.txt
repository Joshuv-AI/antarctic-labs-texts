Propensity score matching analysis of early vs. delayed surgery for intertrochanteric fracture in older patients: a retrospective multicenter cohort study of 7414 patients with a mean 4-year follow up
Authors: Junfei Guo, Binfei Zhang, Hui Yu, Jiale Xie, Tao Wang, Yubin Long, Ying Pan, Ke Xu, Zheng Li, Wensen Jing, Zhiyong Hou, Xin Xu, Peng Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe widespread acceptance of early surgery as a treatment for acute intertrochanteric fracture (ITF) has been accompanied by ongoing controversy due to conflicting conclusions presented in previous studies. This study aims to compare the occurrence of perioperative complications and mortality, as well as functional outcomes in older patients with ITF who underwent either early or delayed surgery.MethodsA retrospective multicenter cohort study involving 7414 patients with ITF between Jan. 2017 and Dec. 2021 was conducted. After predefined participants selection inclusion and exclusion criteria, 2323 surgically treated ITF patients were included and analyzed utilizing propensity score matching (PSM) method. Their demographics, injury-related data, surgery-related data, and perioperative adverse outcomes during hospitalization were collected and compared between the early or delayed surgery groups by PSM with a 1:4 ratio. All participants received a minimum of two-year follow-up and perioperative outcomes, functional outcomes, and survival analyses were conducted and compared.ResultsAfter adjustment for potential confounders, there were no significant difference in surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion rate, mortality rates, functional outcomes, and perioperative complications rates including severe complications, cardiac complications, pulmonary complications, and neurological complications regardless of whether the patient was treated with early or delayed surgery (all P > 0.05). Although length of hospital stay (mean, 11.5 versus 14.4 days, P < 0.001), total hospital costs (mean, 39305 versus 42048 yuan, P < 0.001), and minor complications rates including hematological complications (31.7% versus 41.2%, P = 0.007) and nutritional/metabolic complications (59.3% versus 66.4%, P = 0.039) were lower in the early surgery group, our result indicated patients with early surgery were more inclined to receive more blood transfusion (mean, 2.8 versus 2.2 units, P = 0.004).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that a 48-hour delay in surgical intervention for older patients with an ITF does not result in a higher mortality rate, worse functional outcomes, and increased incidence of major perioperative complications when compared to early surgery. While expedited surgery is undoubtedly necessary for suitable patients, a reasonable preoperative delay of 48 h may be justified and safe for those with severe conditions, rather than strictly adhering to the current guidelines.

Introduction

Due to the increase in population aging and the growing prevalence of osteoporosis among older individuals, there has been a rise in the number of patients with hip fractures (HFs) seeking surgical intervention [1]. Despite a decline in the HF rate over the past 15 years, it has been observed that the absolute number of HF and associated comorbidity burden has continued to increase due to the expanding population of older individuals [2]. Intertrochanteric fracture (ITF), being a significant orthopedic clinical issue pertaining to the hip region, is a frequent cause of illness, disability, and mortality among older adults, thereby imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden on society [3, 4].

The existing literature indicates that patients with severe comorbidities, delayed surgery, anemia, and reactions induced by anesthesia or blood transfusion are unlikely to achieve complete recovery following surgery. These factors are associated with a heightened risk of perioperative complications and mortality, potentially leading to a rapid deterioration in health and independence [5–7]. The impact of surgical delay on HF has been a prominent area of research for the past two decades, yielding varying results. A comprehensive analysis of 52 studies encompassing a total of over 290,000 participants revealed conflicting outcomes in relation to mortality. However, it was concluded that the occurrence of complications and mortality can be mitigated if surgical intervention is conducted within 48 h of admission [8]. An additional meta-analysis [9], which examined 16 observational studies, demonstrated that regardless of the specific surgical time frame (24, 48, or 72 h), expedited surgery (within the designated time frame) was associated with a decreased risk of mortality, as well as reduced incidences of postoperative pneumonia and pressure sores among older patients with HFs. Currently, contemporary guidelines derived from observational studies advocate for the performance of HF surgery within 48 h following the incident [10–12]. Contrasting studies [13–18] have failed to establish a correlation between early surgery and decreased mortality rates, however, supporting the conclusion that surgical delays correlate with longer length of hospital stay (LOS) and higher total hospital costs (THC).

It is worth noting that expedited HF surgery has been extensively implemented in hospital settings. However, it has been brought to attention that a significant confounding factors in these contentious studies is that patients with severe comorbidity who require comprehensive optimization prior to the surgical intervention are more prone to receive delayed surgery [19–23]. Therefore, we conducted this real-world study (RWS) designed retrospective multicenter cohort study using real-world data (RWD) and utilizing propensity score matching (PSM) with the aim of mitigating selection bias and potential confounding factors. In addition, to investigate the real relation between surgical timing and adverse outcomes, we selected exclusively older patients who experienced ITF (as a common and specific type of HF) and underwent identical type of internal fixation with proximal femoral nail anti-rotation (PFNA). The aim of this study was to compare adverse outcomes including perioperative complications, in-hospital outcomes, functional outcomes, and long-term mortality rates between patients with early surgery within 48 h from the initial injury and those who received delayed surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and population

A RWS designed retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted at three Level I trauma centers including Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University, Baoding First Central Hospital, and Honghui Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University in China between Jan. 2017 and Dec. 2021. The three institutions all serve as teaching hospitals and as three of the largest medical centers for orthopedic trauma in China, totally carrying out approximately 160,000 to 180,000 orthopaedic surgeries each year. All three institutions have specialized geriatric orthopedics wards and follow a standardized treatment protocol with multidisciplinary teams that evaluate the patients twice a day, consisting of at least two orthopedists, one internal medicine consultant who manages patients’ perioperative care, an attending anesthesiologist, and nurses. The main goal of this research was to assess perioperative results following hip fracture surgery by employing medical supervision during hospitalization and arranging follow-up telephone or outpatient appointments post-discharge. Since this was an observational study, investigators did not interfere with clinical decision-making during treatment. Perioperative clinical data were collected using pre-designed collection forms, and routine follow-ups were provided every 3–6 months through telephone interviews or outpatient reviews, which contains more precise information compared to the administration database. This study was approved by the institutional internal review boards of the three participating institutions (Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University, IRB number: 2021-070-1; Baoding First Central Hospital, IRB number: 2022-064; and Honghui Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University, IRB number: 202312020) in compliance with the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments, and the informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. All collected patient data were anonymously recorded to protect patient confidentiality. The current study followed the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and the work has been reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of cohort, cross-sectional and case-control studies in Surgery (STROCSS) criteria [24].

The study investigated the correlation between surgical timing and adverse outcomes in patients who were monitored for a minimum of two years after treatment. The study population consisted exclusively of patients aged 65 years or older, with an admission delay of less than 48 h, and a minimum of two years of follow-up. Patients received conservative treatment or surgical intervention beyond 7 days, with multiple fractures or injuries, with open or pathological hip fractures, with insufficient medical records, or loss of follow-up were excluded due to their potential impact on the accuracy of the results. Patients were retrospectively categorized into two groups based on the timing of their surgery. Group A consisted of individuals who underwent surgery within 48 h from injury, whereas Group B included those who received delayed surgery beyond 2 days but within 7 days. To improve the analysis, we then categorized surgical delay into distinct groups categories: early delay (2–3 days), intermediate delay (4–5 days), and prolonged delay (6–7 days). In total, 2323 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, out of which 262 (11.3%) received early surgery, while 2061 (88.7%) received delayed surgery (including 391 in early delay group, 1019 in intermediate delay group, and 651 in prolonged delay group for the following sensitivity analyses).

Data collection, definition, and outcome assessment

To minimize the information bias, standard data collection forms were obtained from the institutions’ electronic in-hospital patient records, which were extracted regarding patients’ demographics including age, gender, body mass index (BMI); injury-related data including fracture type (stable or unstable according to the AO/OTA classification [25], injury mechanism, time from injury to admission, and hemoglobin (Hb) at admission; surgery-related data including type of anesthesia (general or regional), transfusion before surgery, and general health status based on the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade and modified Elixhauser comorbidity method (mECM); in-hospital outcomes including surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, LOS, THC, and the volume/ rate of transfusion. We collected patient comorbidity profiles using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) codes, as well as mortality and complications data. In the event that the conclusions significantly deviate from those drawn by the two orthopedic surgeons who were not involved in the patients’ care and were unaware of the study’s hypothesis, a discussion is warranted. During the follow-up, the survival and functional status and date of death of participants were also collected.

The patients were further stratified by age into the following groups: 65–69, 70–79, 80–89, 90–99, and 100 years old and above. Additionally, BMI was classified as normal, overweight, or obese (BMI < 24 kg/m2, 24 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m2, BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2, respectively). For the purpose of our analyses on LOS and mortality, we employed the time from admission as our timescale. The surgical delay was specifically defined as the duration between injury and the performance of definitive surgery. In this particular investigation, the mECM was employed to detect comorbidities in patients upon admission utilizing 30 unique medical conditions and screened and further stratified into groups 1–5 representing < 0, 0, 1–5, 6–13, ≥ 14, respectively. Prior research has illustrated that this technique is significantly more effective at accounting for comorbidities than other approaches, resulting in a more accurate forecast of complications and mortality [16, 26]. Furthermore, the ASA grade has been demonstrated to serve as a predictive factor for both LOS and mortality in elective orthopedic surgery [20, 27], as evidenced by previous studies conducted by the authors, who have made a deliberate effort to maintain transparency by incorporating these variables in their previously published works [16, 28].

Patient follow-up commenced upon enrollment in the cohort and persisted until either death or the most recent available data, whichever transpired first. Initially, the timing and primary cause of death were documented, followed by the recording of mortality rates at 30 days, 90 days, 180 days, 1 year, 2 years, 3 years, and 4 years. In-hospital outcomes including surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, LOS, THC, and the volume/ rates of transfusion were collected. Furthermore, a comprehensive examination was carried out to detect and contrast the perioperative complications experienced by the patients, encompassing the major complications including severe complications (consisting of pulmonary embolisms, sudden cardiac death, stroke, acute myocardial infarction, acute cerebral infarction, heart failure, respiratory failure, and stress ulceration), cardiac complications (consisting of new-onset cardiac arrhythmia, ischemic heart disease, hemodynamic instability, low cardiac output, and cardiac dysfunction), pulmonary complications (consisting of pulmonary infection, respiratory insufficiency, pleural effusion, and atelectasis), and neurological complications (consisting of transient ischemic attack and delirium), and minor complications including hematological complications (consisting of deep vein thrombosis and anemia) and nutritional/metabolic complications (consisting of stress hyperglycemia, electrolyte disorder, hypoproteinemia, and metabolic or endocrine disturbances), and each patient’s functional outcomes including independent walking, use of walking aids, use of wheelchair, bedridden status, and death were also recorded, which were described in detail in our previous research [28, 29].

Statistical analysis

A statistical power analysis was conducted, employing a two-sided 5% significance level and 80% power to establish reliability and significance. The determination of sample size and power analysis was accomplished using NCSS-PASS V11.0.7 software (https://www.ncss.com/software/pass/). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the data followed a normal distribution, it was reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Alternatively, it was reported as the median (interquartile range). To compare the distribution of patient numbers across groups based on categorical data presented in absolute numbers (percentages), a chi-square test was employed. For normally distributed samples, Students’ t-tests were utilized, whereas for non-normally distributed groups, Mann-Whitney U tests were employed. The PSM technique was employed to mitigate selection bias and potential confounding effects by adjusting for differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups. The PSM was conducted using a 1:4 ratio and the caliper matching method, with logistic regression used to calculate the propensity scores. Covariates such as gender, age, BMI, fracture type, injury mechanism, time from injury to admission, Hb at admission, ASA grade, mECM, anesthesia type, and transfusion before surgery were included in the propensity score calculation. Given the relatively low percentage of missing data (5%), imputation was deemed unnecessary. Survival analyses were conducted for each individual using Kaplan-Meier methods, and log-rank tests were used to compare Kaplan-Meier survival curves between groups. In order to test the robustness of the results, we conducted sensitivity analyses on the various data sets by: (I) all participants; (II) all participants without men; (III) all participants without age ≥ 90; (IV) all participants without BMI ≥ 28; (V) all participants without violent injury; (VI) all participants without general anesthesia; (VII) all participants without unstable fracture; (VIII) all participants without ASA ≥ 4; (VIIII) all participants without mECM ≤ 0; (IV) all participants without prolonged delay group. All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The level of significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Between Jan. 2017 and Dec. 2021, a study was conducted involving a total of 7414 consecutive patients who presented with ITFs. Among these patients, 5091 individuals were excluded based on predetermined criteria. There were 702 patients with age < 65 years; 1164 patients had an admission delay > 48 h; 389 patients received conservative treatment; 974 received surgical intervention beyond 7 days; 668 patients had multiple fractures or injuries, pathological or open hip fractures; 932 with insufficient medical records; and 262 patients were lost to follow-up. Consequently, a total of 2323 patients (with the number of patients undergoing surgery each day following injury depicted in Fig. 1), comprising 262 in group A (who underwent surgery within 2 days from injury) and 2061 in group B (who experienced a surgical delay of more than 2 days but less than 7 days), met the inclusion and exclusion criteria before PSM. Following PSM at a 1:4 ratio, the final sample consisted of 246 patients in group A and 984 patients in group B (Fig. 2).

The delayed surgery group exhibited higher mean LOS and THC compared to the early surgery group following PSM (mean, 14.4 versus 11.5 days, and 42048 versus 39305 yuan, respectively, P < 0.001). Regarding perioperative complications, our findings indicate that the overall rates of minor complications, including hematological and nutritional/metabolic complications, were elevated in the delayed surgery group before PSM. Notably, these differences were still statistical significantly after PSM (P = 0.007 and P = 0.039, respectively). For cardiac complications, the differences did not reach a significantly statistical after PSM whereas the higher rate statistically exists in delayed surgery group before PSM (Table 2).

In the current investigation, a minimum follow-up period of two years and a mean follow-up duration of 48.8 months were implemented for all participants. Among the total of 2323 patients, 558 individuals (24.0%) had deceased by the conclusion of this study, with 24.4% (64/262) belonging to the early surgery group and 24.0% (494/2061) to the delayed surgery group, respectively. Over the course of 7 years following the injury, both cohorts exhibited a gradual decrease in cumulative survival rates. Nevertheless, the Kaplan-Meier survival curve did not reveal any significant disparities in cumulative survival between the two groups, whether in the pre-matching or post-matching groups (Figs. 4 and 5, P = 0.906 and P = 0.098 log-rank). In general, the findings demonstrated resilience in additional sensitivity analyses. Significant statistical findings were still not observed in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, even after excluding participants with men, age ≥ 90, BMI ≥ 28, violent injury, general anesthesia, unstable fracture, ASA ≥ 4, mECM ≤ 0, and prolonged delay group (Table 3).

Discussion

In our investigation involving a cohort of 2323 older patients who underwent IF surgery, we discovered that, following PSM, the delayed surgery group exhibited higher rates of LOS, THC, and minor complications, specifically hematological and nutritional/metabolic complications, compared to the early surgery group. Furthermore, the delayed surgery group demonstrated a significantly lower volume of transfusions in comparison to the early surgery group. However, surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion rate, mortality rates, and perioperative complications rates including severe complications, cardiac complications, pulmonary complications, and neurological complications did not exhibit significant differences between patients receiving early or delayed surgery.

The timing of surgery following an early hip injury has been a subject of debate due to conflicting conclusions in previous studies, despite its widespread acceptance among clinical surgeons. This controversy arises from the varying findings regarding improved functional outcomes [30, 31], increased overall survival [5, 9, 22, 32–35], reduced hospital stay [8, 36], and decreased risk of complications [5, 8, 9, 22, 30, 36–38] associated with early surgical intervention. The existing literature exhibits considerable heterogeneity in the recommended timing for surgical intervention. Multiple studies [5, 8, 9, 22, 31, 32, 38] have indicated that a delay in surgery ranging from 24 to 48 h can lead to an increase in mortality. Advocates of early surgery have emphasized the potential for reduced complication rates. Conversely, other studies [13, 14, 36, 37, 39] have found no significant impact on outcomes when surgery is delayed beyond two days. Furthermore, certain researchers have advocated for even shorter timeframes, such as 12 or even 6 h, arguing that ultra-early surgery may enhance survival rates, decrease the likelihood of major peri-operative complications, and expedite the initiation of mobilization [33–35]. However, other researchers have not observed any detrimental consequences when patients delay surgery for a maximum of 4–5 days after admission [20, 22, 23, 40].

In this particular study, it was discovered that delayed surgery was quite prevalent, accounting for 88.7% (2061 out of 2323 cases). The outbreak of COVID-19, which commenced towards the end of 2019 and worsened throughout 2020, has had a devastating impact on global demographics, resulting in over 300 million infections and more than 5 million deaths worldwide. Consequently, it has emerged as the most significant global public health crisis to date. In response, the Chinese government has implemented a range of measures to mitigate the risk of the epidemic escalating into a nationwide outbreak, resulting in delays in hospital admissions. Furthermore, our study deviates from previous literature by defining surgical delay as the time elapsed from injury to definitive surgery, rather than from admission as defined by previous literature [17]. If we exclude the impact of pre-hospital delay, the rate decreases to 33.2% (771 out of 2323), which is lower than the range of 40.0–50.7% reported in previous studies [20, 41, 42] and is similar with other countries in Asia such as India and Korea [43, 44]. From an academic perspective, it is widely acknowledged that the timely initiation of surgery within 48 h poses a challenge for clinical surgeons due to the imperative need to manage comorbidities and ensure the stability of geriatric patients. Buse et al.‘s research [30] indicates that the majority of medical institutions globally adhere to a surgical intervention timeline exceeding 24 h for patients with femur fractures. Additionally, White et al.‘s study [41] reveals that in the United Kingdom, more than 48 h elapsed between admission and surgery for 42% of surgical cases, primarily due to organizational reasons (51%), medical reasons (44%), and anesthetic reasons (4%). Furthermore, it is widely agreed upon that expediting the surgical procedure is preferable [9]. However, it should be noted that early intervention may not always be feasible, particularly for patients with compromised health at the time of injury, as it has been associated with increased mortality rates [45].

Consistent with prior research, this study emphasizes that factors such as gender, concurrent ailments, and overall medical condition of the patient exert a greater influence on mortality outcomes compared to the timing of the surgery [17, 28, 46]. The primary finding of this study suggests that there were no statistically significant disparities observed in mortality rates at various time intervals, cumulative survival rate, functional outcomes, and major complications such as severe complications, cardiac complications, pulmonary complications, and neurological complications between the early and delayed surgery cohorts. This outcome aligns with previous research, indicating that patients experiencing medically necessary delays may have a reduced likelihood of experiencing adverse outcomes when undergoing delayed surgery compared to those who undergo early surgery [22, 47]. Furthermore, the observed disparities in mortality rates among care providers can be attributed primarily to disparities in staffing levels, facilities, infrastructure, and admission procedures. Multiple explanations are plausible and may have a cumulative effect. To mitigate potential biases and confounding factors, particularly in cases where patients with severe comorbidities may have experienced surgical delays in previous studies, we employed a retrospective approach utilizing real-world data and the PSM method. This approach aims to minimize selection bias and account for confounding effects, ultimately resulting in a more accurate assessment of the high incidence of severe complications and significant morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, our institutions, in its capacity as prominent tertiary referral centers in China, offers comprehensive trauma care to older patients in collaboration with our geriatric wards. This care is delivered through a multidisciplinary team approach, ensuring optimal preoperative preparation and subsequently leading to a reduction in postoperative complications and improved prognosis.

Another significant explanation is that the surgical delay in this study, consistent with the findings of the HIP ATTACK Investigators [48], was defined as the time elapsed from the moment of injury to the definitive surgery, whereas previous studies have defined it as the time calculated from hospital admission to surgery. It is important to note that although the time between fracture occurrence and arrival at a hospital is beyond the control of the hospital organization and cannot be considered a modifiable quality marker, we have concluded that longer time to admission is associated with one-year mortality in our previous research [49]. Therefore, it is unreasonable to use the time of hospital admission as the starting point, especially when considering the continuous and evolving nature of geriatric patients’ condition. Neglecting pre-hospital times during surgery may lead to an inaccurate assessment of patient differentiation in real-time, resulting in an overestimation of the advantages of early surgery.

Our findings also align with the research conducted by Kempenaers et al. [17], as they indicate that prolonged surgical delays are associated with increased hospital stay and total hospital charges, as well as decreased transfusion volume. Although the delayed surgery group exhibited a statistically significant rise in minor complications such as hematological and nutritional/metabolic issues, major complications were not observed. A recent high-quality study of global randomized clinical trial conducted by the HIP ATTACK Investigators in The Lancet [48] systematically evaluated the mortality rates, major complications, and other secondary outcomes between the accelerated-surgery group and the standard-care group (median time from hip fracture diagnosis to surgery: 6 h vs. 24 h). They concluded that compared to standard care, accelerated surgery did not significantly reduce mortality or a composite of major complications.

Taking all our findings together suggests that, although delayed surgery of more than two days causes minor complications and increases THC and LOS, it can serve as a non-inferior solution to the problem without increasing mortality or major complications, especially in patients with frail conditions. In essence, our assertion is that only a small proportion of hip fracture patients can undergo surgery within 24–48 h, while the majority are unable to avoid a delay in surgery due to the presence of comorbidities. However, this initial two-day delay presents an opportunity to optimize patients prior to the operation, including addressing issues such as anemia correction [50, 51] and stress hyperglycemia [52].

Our study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the data collection was limited to one country in Asia, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other regions or countries. Secondly, our assessment of perioperative complications only encompasses the hospital stay, excluding any complications that may occur after discharge. Additionally, while our analysis focused on mortality, functional outcomes, and perioperative complications, the around 48 months. Future studies with extended follow-up data will explore these outcomes to provide a more comprehensive understanding of patient outcomes. Thirdly, as this is a retrospective study based on real-world data, unmeasured factors such as smoking, perioperative laboratory values, patient preferences, surgeon practices, and other unknown confounders could potentially impact our results. However, we employed propensity score matching and sensitivity analyses to mitigate these potential biases. Additionally, several studies [53, 54] have demonstrated that frailty, which encompasses vulnerability due to cumulative declines in physiological systems, is associated with worse outcomes in hip fracture patients. Unfortunately, despite its clinical relevance, we were unable to adjust for frailty as a confounding factor due to data limitations. While our analysis used the multimorbidity index to account for comorbid conditions, future studies should consider frailty-specific indices to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its impact on outcomes. Fifthly, we acknowledge that the reasons for surgical delay, whether medical or administrative, were not specifically explored in this study. Future research should focus on detecting the reasons for surgical delay (medical vs. administrative), which will provide critical insights into the factors influencing outcomes and further inform clinical decision-making. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that this study is the first to investigate the combined effects of patient, fracture, surgical, anesthetic, and transfusion factors. Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study possesses notable strengths. Firstly, it encompasses a thorough preoperative evaluation of comorbidities that represents frailty utilizing mECM. Secondly, the study involves a substantial sample size, consisting of 2323 participants, and a prolonged follow-up period exceeding 4 years on average. Thirdly, the retrospective nature of our study, coupled with the utilization of electronic medical records for data collection, ensures the precision of in-hospital patient information, in contrast to previous studies that relied on a local government database with inherent limitations, such as underreporting or uncertain comorbidities. Lastly, the present study employed distinct patient cohorts with single internal fixation surgery, thereby mitigating the influence of potential confounding factors.

Finally, this investigation represents the inaugural utilization of real-world data and the PSM technique to examine mortality, functional outcomes, and perioperative complications within a specific subset of orthopedic patients, namely those with an ITF, with or without early surgical intervention. In a prior publication [51], we demonstrated that patients who underwent surgery within 7 days exhibited a significantly superior cumulative survival rate compared to those who underwent delayed surgery (> 7 days). Although controversy persists regarding the appropriate timeframe for surgical intervention in cases of acute hip fractures, it is evident that prioritizing expedited surgery alone may not suffice to guarantee improved functional outcomes at present. The advantages of accelerated surgery reported in previous literature should be critically evaluated in future cost-effectiveness investigations, taking into account the necessity to allocate additional hospital resources and the associated urgency it entails.

Conclusions

In this multicenter cohort study involving older patients with ITFs, we found that a surgical delay of up to 7 days did not significantly increase mortality rates, functional outcomes, major perioperative complications, surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, and transfusion rates when compared to early surgery within 48 h. However, it was associated with an increased incidence of minor complications such as hematological and nutritional/metabolic complications, THC, and LOS. Additionally, delayed surgery was found to potentially reduce the overall transfusion volume, thereby decreasing the risk of perioperative blood loss. An imperative for the establishment of a multi-disciplinary management intervention arises to facilitate prompt surgical intervention for suitable patients, while a reasonable preoperative delay of 48 h is deemed justifiable for individuals with severe conditions, in order to fully optimize their preoperative physical condition. Our results contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the optimal timing for hip fracture surgery and underscore the importance of individualized patient care.
