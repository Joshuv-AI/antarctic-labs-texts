A Single-Center Review of Clinical Outcomes After Transcatheter Relief of Superior Vena Cava Stenosis in a Pediatric and Young Adult Population
Authors: Sarah Pradhan, Sarosh P. Batlivala, Luisa Raga Pombo, Nicholas Ollberding, Zhiqian Gao, Nicholas Szugye, Shabana Shahanavaz, Russel Hirsch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe suspected etiology of superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction is predominantly iatrogenic. Transcatheter interventions relieve stenosis with good long-term results, although there is a paucity of published data in pediatrics. We aim to describe patient characteristics and clinical outcomes after transcatheter SVC intervention at a single-center pediatric quaternary care center.MethodsSingle-center, retrospective study of all pediatric and young adult patients who underwent transcatheter SVC intervention from December 2006 to January 2020. SVC gradients and changes in vessel diameter were compared using paired-sample t-tests. Balloon and stent cohorts were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test or Fisher exact χ2 test. Reintervention was compared between balloon and stent cohorts using the McNemar test to determine if initial procedural type was associated with reintervention. Time to reintervention was compared between balloon vs stent cohorts using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with a log-rank test.ResultsA total of 42 patients (median age, 1.5 years; IQR, 0.31-15; 64.2% male) underwent 81 procedures with 1 minor complication and no mortality attributed to the procedure. Most (71.4%) patients were asymptomatic. Affected patient subgroups include those with a history of central venous line (92.9%), congenital heart disease (76.2%), postorthotopic heart transplant (42.9%), post-ECMO (31.0%), and history of surgical baffling or SVC surgery (9.5%). Those who underwent initial balloon angioplasty were significantly younger and smaller than those with an initial stent. Significantly lower pressure gradients and larger SVC dimensions were achieved after stenting. The majority of patients (54.8%) resolved after initial balloon angioplasty and reintervention rates were similar between initial balloon angioplasty and stent, although reintervention trended toward being earlier after the balloon.ConclusionsTranscatheter SVC intervention in pediatrics is acutely effective and safe in relieving SVC obstruction in all subgroups. Reintervention rates are similar between balloon and stenting, with balloon tending to be earlier, with the majority resolving after initial intervention. Further research is necessary to investigate the most effective transcatheter intervention for each patient subgroup.

Introduction

Superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction or occlusion has significant hemodynamic and clinical implications if unrecognized. SVC syndrome has been related primarily to iatrogenic entities, including surgical manipulation1 (eg, extracorporeal membranous oxygenation), orthotopic heart transplant, congenital heart surgery (eg, superior cavo-pulmonary and cavo-atrial anastomoses), or indwelling lines.2,3 Surgical baffle stenosis is a known complication of atrial switch procedures and has been found to be safely relieved by transcatheter intervention as an alternative to surgery.4 Pediatric patients with congenital heart disease often undergo multiple procedures involving instrumentation of the SVC during their lifetime, demonstrating the importance of adequate evaluation and treatment of SVC obstruction in this population.

As many as 50% of patients with hemodynamically significant SVC stenosis may be asymptomatic5; thus, it is important to provide at-risk patients with appropriate surveillance to identify these lesions. Early endovascular treatment and restoration of physiologic venous flow may improve patient outcomes and prevent complications associated with progressive venous obstruction.

Transcatheter interventions have been found to effectively relieve SVC stenosis with good long-term results and symptom relief at follow-up.5 However, there is a paucity of recent data on the pediatric population, warranting further investigation into understanding outcomes in this vulnerable patient population.

We aim to identify patient and clinical characteristics of affected pediatric groups and assess clinical outcomes of transcatheter relief of SVC stenosis. We hypothesize that transcatheter intervention is acutely successful, safe, and durable in children and that age, weight, and body surface area at initial intervention are associated with reintervention.

Methods

We conducted a single-center, retrospective study of all pediatric and young adult patients who underwent transcatheter SVC intervention between December 2006 and January 2020 at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center. The institutional review board approved this study and waived the requirement for informed consent. Patients were excluded if there were insufficient procedural data accessible in the electronic medical record (EMR).

Data extraction

Patient characteristics gathered from the EMR (Epic Systems) included age, sex, height, weight, body surface area, and mortality. Clinical data included presence of congenital heart disease, history of orthotopic heart transplant, central venous line, and extracorporeal membranous oxygenation. Central venous line was defined as the history or presence of a peripherally inserted central line in the upper extremity at the time of cardiac catheterization as documented in the EMR. Symptoms included head or neck swelling, headaches, visual changes, or complaints of exercise intolerance. Indications for cardiac catheterization included presence of symptoms in the setting of SVC stenosis and progression in SVC stenosis detected by echocardiogram. Criteria for procedural success included subjective angiographic improvement or decrease in SVC gradient to less than 2 mmHg. The presence of venous collaterals was qualitatively assessed by a single reviewer (S.B.).

Procedural data gathered included date of catheterization, SVC pressure gradient pre-intervention and post-intervention, balloon and stent type used, number of stents deployed, and complications. Angiographic data were gathered from Syngo Dynamics (Siemens Healthcare GmbH) and procedural data from Mac-Lab hemodynamic software (GE Medical Systems). Data included the following: pre-intervention and post-intervention mean gradients and SVC dimensions. Angiographic measurements were performed by 2 researchers (S.P. and S.B.), with pre-intervention measurements obtained at the narrowest area of SVC stenosis. There is no published angiographic classification of SVC stenosis in the adult or pediatric populations. Yu et al6 has proposed a grading system in the adult population for SVC stenosis based on the clinical severity of symptoms rather than angiographic appearance. In this study, SVC stenosis was arbitrarily classified as mild if >60% of normal vessel size, moderate if 30% to 60%, and severe if <30%. Angiographic venous collaterals were qualitatively assessed.

SVC intervention

The practice at our institution is to start with balloon angioplasty and determine if angiographic and gradient improvement can be achieved. If inadequate after initial angioplasty, stenting is considered, factoring in patient age and size. In certain populations, the decision to intervene in SVC stenosis was shared between the transplant cardiologist or referring cardiologist and interventionalist. In general, balloon size reflected normal dimensions of the SVC surrounding the stenotic area. The same applied to choice of balloon size for any of the stenting procedures.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics and clinical data are described using median with range for continuous variables and frequency with percent of the total for categorical variables. Changes in SVC gradient or vessel diameter from baseline (ie, pre-intervention and post-intervention measurements) were compared using paired-sample t-tests. For those with reintervention, the first and second procedure types were compared using the McNemar test to see if there was a significant difference in the type of procedural reintervention types (balloon and stent). Differences between cohorts (ie, balloon angioplasty vs stenting were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test or Fisher exact χ2 test. Time to reintervention was compared between balloon angioplasty and stenting using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with a log-rank test. The reintervention rate was expressed as the number of reintervention per 10 person-year follow-up time and compared by Poisson regression. P-values <.05 were used to determine statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Forty-two patients underwent 81 procedures, including reinterventions, where 19 of 42 patients (45.2%) underwent reintervention. The vast majority (97.6%) underwent intervention on the right SVC, and 12 patients (28.6%) were symptomatic. None of the patients had complete SVC occlusion at the time of intervention. It was most common for patients to have moderate SVC stenosis (22, 52.3%), followed by severe (15, 35.7%), and the minority with mild (5, 11.9%) stenosis. The majority of patients (28, 66.7%) had angiographic evidence of venous collaterals at the time of catheterization. There was no significant difference in mortality between stent vs balloon groups at the time of data collection. Procedural outcomes

All patients who were symptomatic at the time of catheterization had improvement in symptoms pos-tintervention. There was a significant decrease in SVC gradient measured at catheterization immediately postintervention in all comers, with median pre-intervention gradient of 5 mm Hg (0-22) and median post-intervention gradient of 1 mm Hg (0-8) (P < .01). There was a significant percent increase in SVC dimension post-intervention by 63% (0-386) (P < .01). Clinical characteristics and procedural data at initial SVC intervention are provided in Table 2. Those who underwent balloon angioplasty at initial intervention were significantly younger at a median age of 0.5 years (0.08-21) vs 11.5 years (0.08-36) (P < .01), and smaller at 8.3 kg (2.7-74.8) vs 30.3 kg (3.9-107) (P < .01) when compared to those who underwent stenting. A lower median pressure gradient was achieved after stenting when compared to balloon angioplasty (1 mm Hg [0-3] vs 2 mm Hg [1-8], P < .01). Pre-intervention SVC dimensions were not significantly different between groups, but a larger percent change in SVC dimension was achieved after stenting (103% [23-386] vs 26.3% [0-189], P < .01). Both closed and open-cell stents were used to treat SVC obstruction. Fifty-three percent of stents were closed-cell, including Palmaz (Cordis) (5/15, 33.3%) and Covered CP (NuMED) stents (3/15, 20%). The remaining (46.7%) stents were open-cell including IntraStent (Medtronic) (6/15, 40%) and Formula 418 (Cook Medical) (1/15, 6.7%). One minor complication occurred (1.2%) where a pre-existing large innominate vein thrombus was aspirated prior to SVC stent deployment. Total cohort: Reintervention characteristics

Patient characteristics comparing those who did and did not undergo reintervention are outlined in Table 3. Of those who underwent reintervention, indications were predominantly (16, 84.2%) due to restenosis with gradients noted on echocardiogram, and few (3, 15.7%) were due to symptoms of SVC obstruction. Indications for reintervention were similar between initial balloon angioplasty (10/12, 83.3%) vs stenting (6/7, 85.7%), due to echocardiographic gradient, and the minority had symptoms of SVC syndrome (balloon: 2/12, 16.7%, stent: 14.3%). The overall median time to reintervention was 131 days (1-2725). There was no significant difference in number of patients requiring reintervention between those with initial balloon angioplasty vs stent, although time to first reintervention trended toward being earlier in those with initial balloon angioplasty (109 days [1-502] vs 162 [99-2725], P = .05) when compared to the initial stent. Follow-up time was not significantly different between groups (balloon: 1731 [16-5474]; stent: 1455 [10-4858]; P = .76). Survival analysis was performed, and although patients who underwent initial balloon angioplasty appeared to have earlier reintervention, no statistically significant difference was found between groups (P = .39), shown in Figure 1. The rate of reintervention for the total cohort was 1.84 (95% CI, 1.35-2.51) reinterventions per 10 patient-years of follow-up. This was higher in the balloon group at 1.92 (95% CI, 1.30-2.84) than the stent group at 1.72 (95% CI, 1.04-2.86), but did not meet statistical significance (P = .74). Initial balloon angioplasty cohort: Reintervention characteristics

Twelve of the 26 patients (46%) underwent reintervention after initial balloon angioplasty over a median follow-up time of 1731 days (16-5474). Of the reinterventions, 5 (41.7%) had repeat balloon angioplasty and 7 (58.3%) had future stenting. There was no significant difference in patient characteristics within the angioplasty cohort who did or did not undergo reintervention. Further, there was no significant difference when comparing the initial procedural characteristics between these 2 groups (balloon diameter to narrowing ratio, pre-intervention or post-intervention SVC pressure gradient, pre- or post-SVC dimension) (Table 4).

Initial stent cohort: Reintervention characteristics

Seven of the 16 patients (43.8%) who underwent primary stenting underwent reintervention over a median follow-up time of 1455 days (10-4858). Most (6, 86.7%) underwent stent dilation, and 1 patient (14.3%) underwent stenting. Similar to the balloon angioplasty group, there was no significant difference in patient characteristics or initial procedural parameters between the 2 groups (mean pre-intervention or post-intervention SVC pressure gradient, pre- or post-SVC dimension) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our single-center experience demonstrates that transcatheter relief of SVC stenosis with balloon angioplasty and stenting in children is acutely safe and effective (Central Illustration), with no mortality attributed to the procedure. Symptomatic improvement occurred in all patients who initially presented with symptoms of SVC obstruction. As expected, stenting acutely achieved a larger post-intervention SVC dimension with a lower gradient. Reintervention rates are of particular interest in the pediatric population. The findings from our study are consistent with prior literature5 where freedom from reintervention did not differ between initial stent and angioplasty groups. However, time to first reintervention trended toward being earlier in those with initial balloon angioplasty, and although the rate of reintervention was also higher in this group, it did not meet statistical significance. Reintervention indications were primarily due to restenosis diagnosed on echocardiogram, which were similar between initial balloon angioplasty vs stenting. Few patients were symptomatic, emphasizing the necessity of close interrogation of the SVC in this patient population. Decisions regarding aggressiveness of balloon angioplasty are complex and involve consideration of time since surgical intervention and interventionalist discretion. Reassuringly, over half of the patients resolved after the initial intervention, whether balloon angioplasty or stent, thus affirming that a successful initial dilation may be the only procedure required in patients. However, certain lesions such as baffle obstruction may not respond to initial angioplasty. Thus, expansion of future research to specifically assess how each cause of SVC obstruction responds best to each type of transcatheter intervention is necessary.

Affected patient subgroups are consistent with those described in prior literature. Heightened suspicion for SVC stenosis and close interrogation of the SVC is necessary during every echocardiogram performed. When interrogating the SVC by echocardiogram, one must be cognizant that the distal SVC may appear to have flow if there is obstruction cranially with venous collateral flow back into the proximal SVC. Specific subgroups were not predictive of the need for reintervention; thus we cannot recommend more frequent surveillance in one affected patient group over another. Importantly, only 32% of patients in our study presenting for SVC intervention were symptomatic, lower than that described in other surveys5, within the limitations of retrospective chart review. The absence of symptoms is consistent with the indolent and slowly progressive nature of SVC stenosis, although most patients (67%) had decompressing venous collaterals present at the time of cardiac catheterization, which can result in the absence of symptoms of SVC syndrome. However, our patient population was younger, and unable to participate in routine cardio-pulmonary stress testing to evaluate objective abnormalities in exercise capacity. The intervention was thus based on the absolute presence or progression of stenosis even in the absence of overt symptoms. This study highlights the diversity in causes of SVC obstruction in the pediatric and young adult population. Although the mechanism of SVC obstruction between groups varies, including surgical patch obstruction or suture-related stenosis versus chronic vascular changes from thrombogenic vascular lines, response to initial balloon angioplasty may differ. Our retrospective study suggests that transcatheter interventions were effective and safe in relieving obstruction in all subgroups. Nonetheless, SVC obstruction in the pediatric population is rare and subgroups are small, limiting further statistical analysis to tailor treatment strategies specific to each subgroup. Further prospective studies to investigate the most effective transcatheter intervention to relieve SVC stenosis in each subgroup are necessary.

Limitations of this study include those related to retrospective data acquisition and operator discretion. This report thus reflects real-world experience. The individual clinical scenario often cannot be clearly understood in a retrospective manner and may also have impacted therapeutic choice. This review describes treatment practices limited to a single institution, affected by institutional practices including specific thresholds and timings to stent. When comparing reinterventions and outcomes of balloon angioplasty and stenting groups, one must acknowledge the age gap between these patient cohorts, reflecting differences in patient history extending past age alone. This includes the cause of obstruction, clinical status, vessel pathology, and size which may be a confounder in these comparisons. The presence of decompressing venous collaterals may obscure signs and symptoms of SVC syndrome leading to delay in diagnosis even if flow in the SVC is detected echocardiographically. The low volume of various causes of SVC obstruction renders inadequate statistical power to further compare outcomes between angioplasty and stenting between patient subgroups. Additionally, although unable to predict the need for reintervention based on size, weight, postintervention residual gradients, and SVC dimension, further expansion of this study to a larger cohort is prudent to investigate these relationships.

Conclusion

This single-center study confirms that transcatheter intervention successfully alleviates SVC stenosis in the pediatric population. Reintervention rates were similar between the balloon and stenting groups, with the balloon tending to be earlier, although the majority resolved after the initial intervention. Affected patient subgroups are mostly iatrogenic and warrant close surveillance. The findings represent our institution’s practice, and expansion to a multicenter study in the future can further explore these findings.
