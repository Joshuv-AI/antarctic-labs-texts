Prognostic stratification with composite insulin resistance–inflammation biomarkers in patients with chronic kidney disease and coronary artery disease across glycemic statuses
Authors: Zixiang Ye, Enmin Xie, Chenxi Song, Rui Zhang, Haoyu Wang, Chao Wu, Kefei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and coronary artery diseases (CAD) have a poor long-term prognosis. Although insulin resistance (IR) and systemic inflammation are well-established drivers of cardiovascular risk, the prognostic value of their composite assessment across the glycemic spectrum in patients with CKD and CAD remains undetermined. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic utility of composite IR-inflammation biomarkers for predicting mortality in patients with CKD and CAD stratified by glycemic status.Methods1353 patients with CKD and CAD were enrolled from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data (1999–2018). Composite biomarkers (TyG-hsCRP, TyG-CRP, and C-reactive Protein–Triglyceride Glucose Index [CTI]) were calculated. Patients were categorized by glycemic status (normoglycemia, prediabetes, diabetes) based on WHO/IEC criteria. The endpoint was all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) death. Statistical analyses included Cox regression, Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard plots with Log-rank test, restricted cubic splines, ROC curves, and reclassification metrics, adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, and treatments. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses ensured robustness.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 63-months, 744 all-cause and 323 CVD deaths occurred. Adjusted models showed elevated composite indices linked to higher mortality (e.g., CTI HR 1.43 [95% CI 1.24–1.65] for all-cause; HR 1.32 [1.06–1.64] for CVD). CTI provided good discrimination (AUC 0.700) and reclassification (IDI 0.010; NRI 0.196 for all-cause). The predictive utility of all three composite biomarkers was most pronounced in patients with diabetes, whereas CTI retained the strong association with all-cause mortality in normoglycemic and prediabetic patients. Risk stratification using both CTI and glycemic status identified patients with diabetes and high CTI as having the highest all-cause (HR 1.63 [1.22–2.17]) and CVD (HR 1.37 [0.88–2.14]) death risk.ConclusionComposite biomarkers integrating IR and inflammation, particularly CTI, significantly enhance mortality prediction in patients with CKD and CAD. The predictive utility is modulated by underlying glycemic status, enabling refined risk stratification and potentially guiding tailored management strategies for this complex patient population.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03108-0.

Research insight

What is currently known about this topic?

Both insulin resistance and systemic inflammation are established risk factors in CAD, yet their combined prognostic value in high-risk patients with CKD and CAD across different glycemic states remains unquantified.

What is the key research question?

Can biomarkers integrating insulin resistance and inflammation improve risk stratification for mortality in CKD and CAD patients, and does their predictive utility vary across the glycemic spectrum?

What is new?

We demonstrate that the CTI provides good prognostic value, with its predictive strength being significantly modulated by underlying glycemic status—revealing distinct risk patterns in normoglycemic, prediabetic, and diabetic subgroups.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

This research enables refined risk stratification using readily available biomarkers, potentially guiding personalized management strategies by identifying high-risk CKD and CAD patients who may benefit from more intensive metabolic and anti-inflammatory interventions tailored to their glycemic status.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a leading cause of death worldwide, placing a heavy burden on healthcare systems [1, 2]. Renal impairment strongly correlates with cardiovascular diseases. Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) face a higher risk of cardiovascular events [3, 4]. This link may stem from insulin resistance (IR) and disrupted glucose metabolism due to kidney dysfunction [5]. Once CKD patients develop CAD, their prognosis worsens, with increased risks of adverse outcomes and stress-induced hyperglycemia [6]. Thus, early interventions and secondary prevention are essential to reduce long-term cardiovascular risks and improve outcomes in this group.

However, due to the high heterogeneity of renal disease combined with cardiovascular conditions, traditional statistical methods often perform inadequately in developing predictive models, which limits their effectiveness [7]. Single biomarkers also have drawbacks: the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a surrogate for IR, links to CAD severity and events but overlooks inflammation’s role [8, 9]. Similarly, inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) or high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) predict atherosclerotic burden and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), yet they ignore IR [10–12].

IR and inflammation interact synergistically in CKD and CAD patients. IR promotes endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, amplifying inflammation. Meanwhile, chronic inflammation worsens IR by impairing insulin signaling pathways. Together, they accelerate atherosclerosis, plaque instability, and vascular calcification—key drivers of poor outcomes in this population [13]. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index (CTI) integrates these factors, offering a better tool to capture their combined effects [13]. Yet, the predictive value of IR and inflammation together, especially across glycemic statuses (normoglycemia, prediabetes, diabetes), remains underexplored in CKD and CAD patients.

This study aimed to assess the predictive value of composite indices (TyG-hsCRP, TyG-CRP, and CTI) for long-term mortality in CKD and CAD patients, stratified by glycemic status (normoglycemia, prediabetes, and diabetes). We also compared these biomarkers’ performance and their added value for risk stratification and evaluate their integrated stratification value alongside glycemic status. These insights could refine risk assessment and guide clinical decisions in this high-risk group.

Methods

Study population and design

A pooled analysis was performed using data from NHANES survey cycles spanning 1999 to 2018. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) continuously conducts the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a project that prospectively evaluates the health, diet, and lifestyle of individuals in the U.S., and this research made use of its data [14, 15]. DataDryad (10.5061/dryad.d5h62) provides access to the datasets. The CDC’s institutional review board for research involving human subjects granted permission for this study.

Our study incorporated CKD and CAD participants who were 20 years or older at the time of registration. We utilized exclusion standards to eliminate persons with incomplete data on vital parameters, such as triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), diabetes condition, creatinine concentrations, and life expectancy. Definition of glycemic status

Glycemic status was defined according to World Health Organization/International Expert Committee (WHO/IEC) criteria. Prediabetes was defined as an HbA1c level of 6.0–6.5% or an FPG level of 6.1–7.0 mmol/L [16]. Diabetes was defined based on a self-reported physician’s diagnosis, current use of antidiabetic medication, or newly detected hyperglycemia (defined as FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%). Individuals with glycemic levels below the threshold for prediabetes were classified as having normoglycemia.

Measurement of inflammation biomarkers

Inflammation biomarkers, including CRP, hsCRP, were measured under ambulatory stable conditions. Blood samples were collected from non-institutionalized participants during routine visits to mobile examination centers in a community-based setting. hsCRP was quantified using high-sensitivity latex-enhanced nephelometry or immunoturbidimetric assays (e.g., on Beckman Coulter analyzers) for precise low-level detection. CRP measurements followed similar immunoturbidimetric protocols, with assay details ensuring reproducibility across NHANES cycles as per CDC laboratory guidelines.

Clinical presentations and outcomes

The endpoints were all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. The National Center for Health Statistics paired NHANES data with death records from the National Death Index to acquire mortality data [17]. Participants who could not be linked to death data during the follow-up period were considered alive. The evaluation was conducted on both general death rates and those related to cardiovascular diseases, with the latter being categorized according to the International Classification of Diseases10th Revision (ICD-10) codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69.

The presence of CAD was confirmed through a self-declared doctor’s diagnosis of coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, or myocardial infarction. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation was utilized to compute the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [18], where a CKD diagnosis was determined by an eGFR value was lower than 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2.

Statistical analysis

Patient baseline characteristics are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. After confirming that the data were missing at random, multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) with the random forest method, implemented in the R mice package, was employed to handle missing data. The missing data patterns and details were showed in the Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to examine the linearity of the relationship between the various biomarkers and clinical outcomes. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between the biomarkers and mortality risk. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Participants were stratified into two groups using pre-specified cut-off values for the inflammatory biomarkers based on the median value (TyG-hsCRP: 11.48; TyG-CRP: 9.40; CTI: 8.45). Subsequent analyses were further stratified by glycemic status (Normoglycemia, Prediabetes, and Diabetes). Two analytical models were constructed: a crude model without adjustments, and a fully adjusted model that controlled for age, gender, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes status, hypertension, dyslipidemia, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride and use of antidiabetic agents, antihypertensive medication and lipid-lowering medication. The variables included for adjustment were selected based on collinearity testing using the variance inflation factor (VIF), in conjunction with prior literature and clinical expertise [19, 20].

Cumulative hazard plots were generated using the Nelson-Aalen estimator to visualize risk accumulation, with group differences tested via Log-rank statistic. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed, and the areas under the curve (AUC) were compared using the DeLong test. Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) and Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) are used to evaluate the enhancement in disease risk prediction accuracy provided by biomarkers. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (male vs. female), BMI (< 30 kg/m2 vs. ≥ 30 kg/m2), smoking status (yes vs. no), hypertension (yes vs. no), and hypercholesterolemia status (yes vs. no). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding variables associated with the TyG index and blood glucose levels (including diabetes status, triglyceride and hemoglobin A1c) and repeating the analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3) and Stata (version 17.0). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1353 participants with CKD and CAD were included in the final analysis. The median age was 77 years (IQR 69–80), 47.1% were female. Glycemic status distribution was as follows: normoglycemia (34.6%), prediabetes (18.8%), and diabetes (46.6%). The median follow-up time was 63 months. During the follow-up, 744 all-cause deaths and 323 CVD deaths were recorded. Biomarkers and mortality

Higher levels of TyG-hsCRP, TyG-CRP, and CTI were associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality (Table 2). In fully adjusted models, the strongest association was observed with CTI (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.24–1.65, P < 0.001), followed by TyG-CRP (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.12–1.22, P < 0.001). When dichotomized at medians, elevated levels showed consistent risks (e.g., CTI > 8.45: HR 1.44, 95% CI 1.20–1.74, P < 0.001). Cumulative hazard curves demonstrated significantly higher cumulative incidence of all-cause death in groups with elevated composite indices compared to low levels (log-rank P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). RCS analyses revealed TyG-hsCRP had linear dose–response relationships for all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear > 0.05), while other biomarkers had non-linear dose-response relationships for all-cause mortality with accelerating risks at higher biomarker levels for composites and a U-shaped pattern for TyG (Fig. 3). Joint biomarkers and glycemic status interactions

Using combined stratification based on biomarker levels and glycemic status, patients were divided into six groups (Fig. 5). The highest risk of all-cause death was observed in patients with high CTI and diabetes. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses excluding TyG- and glucose-related variables (diabetes status, triglyceride and HbA1c) yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. The associations between composite indices and both all-cause (e.g., CTI HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.18–1.43, P < 0.001) and CVD (e.g., TyG-CRP HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.08–1.23, P < 0.001) mortality remained statistically significant and similar in magnitude (Supplementary Table 7, 8). Stratified sensitivities by glycemic status yielded similar results, with preserved significance in dysglycemia (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study of 1353 patients with CKD and CAD, composite indices integrating IR and inflammation—namely TyG-hsCRP, TyG-CRP, and CTI—demonstrated good prognostic value for all-cause and CVD mortality. Elevated levels of these indices were independently associated with increased mortality risk, with CTI exhibiting the strongest adjusted HR. Stratified analyses revealed amplified risks in prediabetes and diabetes subgroups, where joint effects with dysglycemia conferred the highest mortality hazards. Reclassification metrics confirmed enhanced predictive performance, with CTI yielding the highest integrated discrimination improvement and net reclassification improvement for all-cause mortality. Risk stratification integrating the composite biomarkers with glycemic status successfully identified patient subgroups at the highest all-cause and CVD death risk. Sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of glucose-related variables, affirmed the robustness of these associations across glycemic statuses.

IR plays a central role in the pathogenesis of CAD [21]. Its detrimental effects extend beyond the promotion of traditional risk factors to directly drive the process of coronary artery disease and kidney diseases [8, 22]. IR can promote active vascular calcification through multiple pathways, including the induction of endothelial dysfunction, activation of the renin-angiotensin system, and the promotion of an osteogenic phenotypic switch in vascular smooth muscle cells [23, 24]. Consequently, IR is not merely a causative factor in CKD and CAD but also a pivotal biological marker for assessing its activity and prognosis.

Operating in parallel with IR, chronic inflammatory states constitute another cornerstone throughout the continuum of atherosclerosis [12]. In advanced lesions, persistent inflammation contributes to plaque destabilization by weakening the fibrous cap via actions of substances like matrix metalloproteinases, while simultaneously exacerbating a pro-calcific microenvironment within the intima [25, 26]. For patients with CKD, a high inflammatory burden often heralds more rapid plaque progression, a greater risk of microvascular dysfunction, and more frequent cardiovascular events, which explains why inflammatory markers carry independent prognostic value in this high-risk population [10, 11, 27, 28].

The novel composite biomarkers examined in this study—TyG-hsCRP, TyG-CRP, and the CTI—offer the distinct advantage of concurrently capturing the dual pathological processes of metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory activation. Our finding that these composite biomarkers demonstrated good predictive performance aligns with the pathophysiological concept that IR and inflammation synergistically contribute to cardiovascular risk. While previous studies have established the prognostic value of either the TyG index or inflammatory markers alone in CAD populations, few have specifically examined their combination in CKD and CAD patients. The CTI is not a simple linear addition of the TyG index and CRP; instead, it optimizes their combined contribution to risk by assigning a specific weight to CRP in its algorithm [29–32]. This mathematical refinement likely underlies its marginally superior predictive performance in our cohort. Furthermore, the significant associations observed for TyG-hsCRP suggest that the core “metabolism-inflammation” axis is a robust predictor, regardless of the specific inflammatory marker used, though the strength of association may vary. Our findings provide new clinical evidence supporting the application of these composite biomarkers in the CKD and CAD population. Their potential mechanism for enhancing prognostic prediction lies in their ability to provide an integrated assessment of this core axis, thereby yielding pathophysiological information beyond that offered by traditional risk factors.

In this study, we compared and validated various composite indices of IR and inflammation (TyG-hsCRP, TyG-CRP, and CTI). It is important to note that their underlying algorithms differ; for instance, CTI incorporates logarithmic transformations, whereas TyG-CRP is a simple summation. The primary objective of this research was to validate the predictive value of these novel indices in patients with CKD and CAD, rather than to develop new biomarkers, which justified this comparative approach.

The primary clinical significance of our study lies in providing a more precise risk stratification tool for patients with CKD and CAD—a complex CAD subgroup characterized by high procedural risk and poor long-term outcomes. The heterogeneous associations across glycemic status offer refined insights for personalized management. The good prognostic value of CTI and TyG-CRP in the non-diabetic subgroup may be explained by the fact that in these patients, death risk is less dominated by severe hyperglycemia, allowing the more subtle interplay between IR and inflammation to emerge as a dominant risk driver. In contrast, in established diabetes, the metabolic milieu is so profoundly disturbed that multiple pathways, reflected by various composite indices, contribute more uniformly to adverse outcomes [33, 34]. By utilizing readily available composite indices like the CTI in conjunction with a patient’s glycemic status, clinicians can readily identify those “very high-risk” individuals within an already high-risk population. For these patients, more aggressive management strategies—including intensive lipid-lowering, stringent glycemic and inflammation control, and closer long-term follow-up—can be implemented.

In the subgroup analysis, a notable interaction was observed between TyG-CRP/glycemic status and age. Although variations in CRP levels across age groups may partially explain this interaction, the findings in our study are more likely attributable to reduced statistical power due to insufficient sample sizes in certain subgroups.

Several limitations of our study warrant careful consideration when interpreting the results. First, the observational design may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability to other ethnic populations. Future multi-ethnic studies are essential for validation and should also aim to establish clinically applicable cut-off values for these composite indices to guide decision-making. Second, using WHO/IEC criteria for prediabetes resulted in a smaller cohort (18.8%) compared to ADA standards, possibly underrepresenting this group and attenuating subgroup associations. Additionally, inflammatory markers such as hsCRP and CRP may be influenced by acute infections, intercurrent illnesses, or transient factors, potentially affecting biomarker interpretation. However, in the NHANES cohort, blood samples were collected under non-emergency conditions, which may mitigate such interference to some extent. Furthermore, for CKD patients, the etiology of CKD, staging based on eGFR and proteinuria levels, and the use of medications such as SGLT2 inhibitors or ACEI/ARBs—which can improve prognosis in this population—were not analyzed due to database limitations; we anticipate further exploration in future clinical studies. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as dialysis status or the severity of coronary lesions, cannot be entirely excluded despite extensive adjustment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that composite biomarkers integrating insulin resistance and systemic inflammation, particularly the CTI, provide significant prognostic value for mortality in patients with CKD and CAD. The predictive utility of these biomarkers varies across glycemic status. The integration of these biomarkers with glycemic status enables enhanced risk stratification, potentially guiding more personalized management strategies for this high-risk population.
