Graph neural networks in the nephropathological diagnosis of antibody-mediated rejection
Authors: Israel Mateos-Aparicio-Ruiz, Anibal Pedraza, Nicola Altini, Lucia Gonzalez, Gloria Bueno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody-mediated rejection (AMR) is a leading cause of kidney transplant failure, requiring accurate histopathological assessment for diagnosis. This study evaluates graph-based deep learning models for AMR classification using periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)-stained whole slide images (WSIs), with the aim of improving diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility. A multi-institutional dataset of 1193 WSIs from 348 patients was used, where glomeruli, arteries, and cortical tubulointerstitial regions were segmented via deep learning and represented as nodes in graph-structured data. Feature extraction was performed using both supervised and self-supervised methods, and classification was conducted with four graph neural network (GNN) architectures: Graph-Transformer, and the novel SimpleGCN, DenseGCN and SimpleGAT. Patch-wise convolutional and transformer-based classifiers served as baselines. All models were evaluated at both the WSI and biopsy levels using stratified five-fold cross-validation. GNN-based models consistently outperformed patch-wise baselines, with the best glomeruli-only GNN achieving a 5.34 % improvement in WSI-level accuracy (71.00 %) over the strongest baseline. Incorporating additional compartments (arteries and cortex) further improved accuracy to 86.97 % at the WSI level and 89.53 % at the biopsy level, with statistically significant gains confirming the additive value of multi-compartment modeling. Performance varied across feature extractors and graph configurations, underscoring the complexity of optimizing computational pipelines for AMR diagnosis. Overall, graph-based modeling substantially enhances AMR diagnostic performance over conventional approaches, enabling scalable, low-cost and reproducible workflows with minimal expert input. These findings demonstrate the potential of GNNs to support nephropathologists in delivering more consistent and reliable diagnoses, with future work needed to refine feature representations and integrate multimodal data for broader clinical utility.Keywords: Graph neural networks, Antibody-mediated rejection, Whole slide imaging, Digital pathology, Deep learning, Nephropathology

Introduction

Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) remains a leading cause of late kidney allograft failure [1], [2]. Its diagnosis, as defined by the dominant [3] Banff Classification, involves a complex set of rules integrating histopathological findings in the current transplant biopsy, previous biopsies, and immunogenetic test data for donor-specific anti-human leukocyte antigen (HLA) antibodies and antibodies against non-HLA antigens expressed on donor endothelium [4]. Relevant histopathological criteria for AMR include glomerulitis, peritubular capillaritis, transplant glomerulopathy, severe peritubular capillary basement membrane multilayering, endarteritis, C4d positivity and thrombotic microangiopathy after exclusion of any other cause. Although acute tubular damage is also among the Additional Diagnostic Parameters (ADPs) used for the diagnostic subcategories of antibody-mediated damage (Diagnostic Category 2), it is not used for any AMR diagnostic subcategory in a strict sense [4]. Unfortunately, inter-observer variability remains substantial even among expert nephropathologists [5]. Although the complex set of diagnostic rules for AMR might contribute to this variability, the main problem is likely the variability in scoring the crucial lesions and ADPs.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and computational pathology have opened new possibilities for more accurate, reproducible and efficient diagnostic methodologies. Computational pathology leverages machine learning and deep learning algorithms to analyze digitized histopathological images, known as whole slide images (WSIs), thereby automating diagnostic workflows through advanced image analysis. Beyond diagnostic classification, digital pathology facilitates telepathology, education and research data sharing [6].

Several studies have applied deep learning to classify renal biopsy images into rejection and non-rejection categories. For example, [7] used data augmentation and DenseNet-121 [8] for binary classification, and later leveraged set transformers to distinguish AMR from non-AMR in glomerular images [9], [10]. In a broader classification setting, [11] applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to full biopsies stained with various techniques to categorize them as normal, rejection (without subclassifying into Borderline, AMR or T cell-mediated rejection) or other pathologies. These studies highlight the versatility of deep learning approaches for nephropathological image analysis. Compared to transcriptomics, image-based approaches are more cost-effective and can be applied directly to routine paraffin sections and their corresponding WSIs.

Despite these advances, most existing methods operate on isolated sub-images or patches, which are processed independently due to the gigapixel resolution of WSIs and corresponding hardware constraints. However, this patch-wise analysis neglects the complex tissue architecture and global spatial relationships that are essential for accurate diagnosis. In contrast, expert nephropathologists evaluate biopsies by integrating findings from multiple WSIs (serial sections), immunostaining, transmission electron microscopy and clinical information (including prior biopsies) to provide a comprehensive diagnosis. This process considers the complex relationships between various tissue compartments, such as glomeruli, arteries, arterioles and peritubular capillaries (PTCs).

To overcome the limitations of patch-based approaches, graph-based tissue representations have emerged as a powerful alternative. While traditional methods model local relationships using grid-based representations with fixed neighborhood topology, graphs offer a flexible data structure where the neighbors of a node are unordered and variable in size [12]. This ability to model unstructured data has gained attention in computational pathology, where cell-, patch- or region-level features are used as nodes, and edges are defined based on distance or similarity measures [13]. This advanced representation allows for the integration of both local and global spatial contexts, and inter-compartment relationships.

Accurate AMR diagnosis, per the Banff guidelines, often requires a combination of staining techniques and imaging modalities, such as immunofluorescence for C4d detection, immunohistochemistry on paraffin-embedded tissue and electron microscopy for detailed ultrastructural analysis [14]. Additional stains such as hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), trichrome-elastic, Jones or methenamine silver, and Congo red may be necessary to identify specific pathological features or rule out concurrent conditions. However, such techniques are often unavailable in routine practice due to cost, limited infrastructure or technical expertise. To address this challenge, our study focuses solely on periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)-stained WSIs, a widely available and cost-effective staining technique that highlights key histological structures relevant to AMR diagnosis.

The objective of this study is to evaluate the efficacy of deep learning and graph-based models for AMR diagnosis using only PAS-stained WSIs. We compare conventional CNN and transformer-based architectures with graph neural networks (GNNs) that incorporate glomeruli, arteries and cortical tubulointerstitial (TI) tiles as nodes. Rather than proposing new GNN architectures, our contribution lies in adapting existing models to clinically meaningful graph representations aligned with Banff-relevant compartments, and systematically assessing their impact on diagnostic accuracy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of graph-based deep learning to AMR classification using multi-compartment tissue graphs derived from histopathology. By exploring alternative graph construction strategies and GNN variants, we demonstrate the translational potential of graph-based models to improve diagnostic performance and inter-observer reproducibility in AMR classification.

Materials

A cohort with 1,193 PAS-stained WSIs, originating from 348 biopsies, was used for this study. These images were originally acquired for clinical purposes at four institutions: the Cologne University Hospital, the Lille University Hospital, the Milan Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico and the Istanbul Koç University School of Medicine. The authors were not involved in data collection and received the dataset in fully anonymized form, with no associated patient identifiers or metadata. This cohort includes adversarial samples such as accommodation, defined as a stable transplant without rejection despite the presence of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs), transmitted, recurrent and de novo diseases. The biopsies without AMR (non-AMR) were balanced for Banff Category 3 and 4 diagnoses (Borderline and T cell-mediated rejection infiltrates) which often accompany AMR. Each WSI was assigned the biopsy-level label of its source biopsy. Biopsies were classified according to the Banff 2022 criteria [4].

Glomeruli were automatically segmented using the GNCnn QuPath extension [15], [16], which employs a Cascade Mask R-CNN [17] with a ResNet-50 [18] backbone. On the test set, this model achieved an AP50 of 0.720 and a Dice coefficient of 0.807. Post-processing involved filtering with two Swin-T (Tiny) [19] models: one distinguishing glomeruli from non-glomeruli (balanced accuracy 0.999 on the test set), and another classifying glomeruli as sclerosed or non-sclerosed (balanced accuracy 0.985 on the test set). Only non-globally sclerosed, verified glomeruli were retained, yielding 15,430 glomeruli. Each was cropped at the highest magnification with 300 pixels of padding.

To investigate whether incorporating additional histological structures improves classification, we also segmented arteries and cortical TI tissue. Arteries were segmented using a U-Net [20] with a ResNet-34 backbone, achieving a Dice coefficient of 0.716 on the test set. Cortex regions were extracted via DeepLabv3+ [21], also using ResNet-34, achieving a Dice coefficient of 0.891 on the test set. Both models were trained on 526 WSIs from 79 biopsies and validated on 158 WSIs from 26 biopsies, all comprising multiple stainings (PAS, Jones, trichrome-elastic, H&E). Arteries were cropped with 300-pixel padding, and cortex regions were divided into non-overlapping 300-micron tiles, both at the highest magnification. Methods

Several deep learning classification architectures were used, following two approaches. First, CNNs and transformers were used to classify glomerular crops individually. Second, WSIs were represented as graphs with glomeruli as nodes, and GNNs were used to classify each WSI. Finally, the graphs were enhanced by incorporating arteries and cortical TI tiles as additional nodes alongside glomeruli, and the same GNN classifiers were used to evaluate their impact on classification performance.

Graph construction

To apply graph-based methods, data must be formalized as a graph. A graph is a pair G=(V,E), where V is the set of nodes and E⊆V×V is the set of edges. Given a node vi∈V, the edge eij=(vi,vj)∈E connects it to node vj. The neighborhood of vi is defined as N(vi)={vj∈V|(vi,vj)∈E}. The adjacency matrix A∈{0,1}n×n satisfies Aij=1 if eij∈E, and Aij=0 otherwise.

In the context of WSI analysis, graphs are typically constructed by dividing images into patches or downsampled feature maps, with each region treated as a node, as in Vision GNN [27]. This patch-based design is also common in transformer-based vision models. Our approach departs from this paradigm by explicitly modeling each WSI as a graph of biologically meaningful regions. Rather than assigning the WSI-level diagnosis to individual crops (a strategy that may introduce noise by mislabeling structures), we classify the entire WSI graph directly. This strategy is designed to more closely mimic the diagnostic workflow of a pathologist, who evaluates the entire slide rather than isolated regions.

Constructing such a graph, however, introduces new challenges. Representing each node as a full image crop (e.g., a glomerulus) increases input dimensionality and computational complexity. Moreover, GNNs are inherently expensive due to message-passing operations that aggregate features from neighboring nodes [28]. To mitigate this, we reduce the dimensionality of each crop before graph construction.

By modeling the spatial and morphological interactions among distinct tissue compartments, our method supports a more comprehensive and context-aware classification. In contrast to previous work that relies on dense tiling of the entire WSI [29], [30], we construct graphs using only regions of interest (glomeruli, arteries, and cortical TI tiles). These were chosen because they are diagnostically relevant to AMR and can be segmented with sufficient reliability. This targeted selection also improves inference efficiency, which is critical for real-world deployment.

In contrast to previous work that relies on dense tiling of the entire WSI [29], [30], we construct graphs using only regions of interest (glomeruli, arteries, and cortical TI tiles). These compartments are central to Banff scoring and can be segmented with sufficient reliability.

Glomeruli and arteries are represented in several AMR-related Banff Lesion Scores and ADPs. Although AMR-related lesions may also occur in arterioles, they contribute comparatively little additional diagnostic information for AMR. PTCs, while diagnostically important, are diffusely distributed across the cortex and can be difficult to annotate in cases of interstitial fibrosis or dense infiltrates, which are sometimes observed in AMR biopsies.

We therefore represented PTCs indirectly by including cortical TI tiles, which capture both PTCs (Banff ptc Lesion Score) and interstitial lesions (i score) in context. This choice is also consistent with the Banff Classification, which explicitly discounts some peritubular capillaritis toward a diagnosis of AMR in the presence of tubulointerstitial infiltrates (Banff i and t), represented in the cortical TI tiles rather than in PTCs themselves. Finally, explicitly modeling PTCs would introduce hundreds of additional nodes per WSI, which would come at a substantial computational cost. This targeted compartment selection therefore balances diagnostic relevance with segmentation reliability and computational efficiency, which is critical for real-world deployment.

On average, each WSI includes 13 glomeruli, 3 arteries and 56 cortical TI tiles. We first constructed graphs using only glomeruli, where all nodes are fully connected. We then extended the graph to include arterial crops and cortical TI tiles, providing additional contextual information. Since arteries are comparatively sparse and segmented with lower reliability, and cortical TI tiles are abundant but often contain diffuse or uninformative tissue, we employed two distinct graph construction strategies to stabilize information and reduce noise, described below. While these decisions were primarily computational, they are also consistent with real-life diagnostics, where the diagnosis of AMR, including its activity and chronicity, relies heavily on glomerular pathology because of the high prevalence of alterations in this compartment and the diagnostic algorithms of the Banff Classification [4].

Hub-based graphs

Hubs are usually defined as high-degree nodes in the literature, although they are not formally defined in graph theory. An alternative characterization by Walsh [31] defines a hub set U⊂V in a graph G such that any two nodes outside U are connected by a path whose internal vertices lie in U.

In GNNs, node embeddings are updated by aggregating features from neighbors. We hypothesize that structuring graphs with hubs as intermediaries can concentrate information flow through the hub set. Consistent with the rationale outlined above, we designated glomeruli as hubs, since they are relatively scarce compared to cortical TI tiles, more reliably segmented than both arteries and cortical TI tiles, and diagnostically central. By routing message passing through glomeruli, this strategy aims to reduce the risk that noisy or redundant nodes dominate the graph, while still allowing contextual information from arteries and cortex to contribute. Moreover, GNNs often perform better on high-degree nodes than low-degree nodes [32], which further supports this design choice.

To test for statistical significance, we first evaluated normality of ΔA(i) using the Shapiro–Wilk test [34]. The Shapiro–Wilk test calculates a statistic that compares the sample distribution of ΔA(i) to an ideal normal distribution. The null hypothesis Ho is that ΔA(i) is normally distributed; if p>0.05, we fail to reject H0 and proceed with a parametric test. Otherwise, a non-parametric test is used. These models were also retrained on the expanded dataset including arteries and cortical TI tiles, with the number of training epochs reduced to 50 to accommodate the increased dataset size.

Graph classification models

The deep learning architectures employed in this study are Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which generalize classical neural networks to graph-structured data. The basic intuition behind GNNs is to iteratively aggregate the information of each node from its local neighborhood. After k iterations, each node embedding captures information from its k-hop neighborhood [40].

The specific graph classification models used in this work are based on GCN and GATv2 layers. Four architectures were used for this task: Graph-Transformer [30], which had already been applied to WSIs by its original authors, and SimpleGCN, DenseGCN and SimpleGAT, which were newly designed as part of this work. A graph convolutional layer is followed by a pooling operator, specifically the minimum cut pooling operator (MinCutPool). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that graph-based methods outperform traditional patch-wise CNN and transformer-based classifiers in binary AMR diagnosis using PAS-stained WSIs. The best-performing glomeruli-only graph-based model achieved a 5.34 % accuracy gain over the best patch-wise model. At the biopsy level, where predictions from multiple WSIs are aggregated, this gain rose to 11.85 %, highlighting how patient-level assessment can mitigate inconsistencies from individual WSIs. This superior performance suggests that graph-based tissue representation effectively captures spatial context and inter-compartment relationships, which are often overlooked by patch-wise models that treat each patch as an isolated region.

Beyond overall accuracy, a clinically relevant advantage of these models lies in their high true negative rates. The best-performing multi-compartment GNN achieves a specificity of 85.56 % at the WSI level and 88.70 % at the biopsy level, compared with 61.65 % for the best patch-wise baseline (Δ=+27.05%). These results suggest that graph-based integration of PAS-derived structures can materially increase the confidence to exclude AMR at the initial PAS-screening stage. Confirmatory tests such as C4d immunostaining and DSA assays are resource- and time-intensive, but remain indispensable when AMR cannot be confidently excluded from PAS findings alone. By raising specificity, our PAS-only models could provide practical value by reducing the need for these additional tests in clearly negative cases, simplifying the diagnostic process and lowering costs.

While these gains are clinically promising, it is also important to consider how dataset composition influences model performance. As shown in Supplementary S1, all models performed better on the mini-dataset, likely due to its homogeneity, as it comprises micrographs from a single center and lacks adversarial samples such as accommodation, transmitted, recurrent and de novo diseases. This reduced variability simplifies the classification task. However, robust performance on such a limited dataset does not guarantee similar results when applied to more complex, multi-center datasets [50]. These results underscore the importance of evaluating models on diverse datasets to ensure clinical applicability.

Our dataset already spans four independent institutions, which strengthens robustness across centers. However, we did not have access to additional data beyond this cohort. We therefore relied on stratified five-fold cross-validation, where each fold included a fully unseen test set, to provide unbiased estimates of generalization. Prospective validation on an independent cohort would further confirm robustness, but is beyond the scope of the present study given current data availability.

Beyond dataset variability, the selection and combination of tissue compartments also play a decisive role in shaping performance. The best-performing multi-compartment graph compositions (glomeruli and arteries, and glomeruli and cortical TI tiles) using Swin as the feature extractor, show a 14–15 % WSI-level accuracy gain over glomeruli-only graphs, resulting in an accuracy of 86.97 %, with a value of 89.53 % at the biopsy level. However, when all three structures are combined, the best-performing model configuration (using SimCLR as the feature extractor) achieves a more modest 2.25 % increase in WSI-level accuracy compared to glomeruli-only graphs. Moreover, GNN classifiers that use Swin-extracted features perform worse with all three structures than their glomeruli-only counterparts. This reflects a complex interplay between the number of different tissue compartments and the specific feature extractor used to compose the graphs. Swin benefits considerably from the addition of a single compartment, whereas MAE generally deteriorates with any added structure and SimCLR handles the full combination better but without large gains relative to simpler graph configurations.

These large accuracy variations likely stem from how each feature extractor manages the increased embedding space complexity when multiple tissue compartments are introduced. Specifically, the Swin model, which was re-trained under a multi-label classification approach to distinguish among artery, cortex and glomerulus for both AMR and non-AMR diagnoses, appears to prioritize compartment-level distinctive features over the AMR diagnosis. When all three structures are combined, this increased structural diversity may dilute the more relevant AMR-related features. As both spectral and non-spectral GNNs can be approximated as solving a graph denoising problem [51], [52], the increased tissue heterogeneity might introduce noise in the graph signal, complicating the implicit denoising and making it harder for GNNs to focus on AMR-specific features. Conversely, SimCLR may better integrate the full graph composition, although with only modest performance gains. MAE performs consistently worse, possibly due to its reconstruction-based pretraining being less effective at distinguishing AMR from non-AMR within multiple tissue compartments. While the supervised Swin model achieved the strongest results in this study, its dependence on labeled data may reduce transferability across cohorts compared with self-supervised or multimodal approaches, which limits scalability. Overall, these differences underscore the impact of feature choice on performance in multi-compartment graphs.

Our comparison of multi-compartment graph construction techniques indicates that neither the hub-based nor the edge-weighted approach consistently outperforms the other across different configurations. For graphs comprising glomeruli and arteries, as well as those including all three structures, the best-performing models use hub-based graphs. In contrast, for graphs composed of glomeruli and cortical TI tiles, the best-performing configuration, which also achieves the best overall accuracy across all experiments, employs edge-weighted graphs. The same patterns are reflected at the biopsy level, suggesting that the effectiveness of a graph construction technique depends on the specific tissue compartments included. Further investigation is needed to determine how construction strategies can be tuned to balance contextual integration with noise reduction when combining heterogeneous compartments in AMR diagnosis.

Alongside graph construction strategies, scalability represents another practical consideration when deploying these models. While graph sizes in our dataset remained manageable (on the order of 70–80 nodes per WSI), scaling to larger cohorts or incorporating additional compartments would increase both node counts and edge density. This growth could raise GPU memory demands and training times, since message passing scales with the number of edges. Our current design mitigates this partly by reducing crop dimensionality before graph construction and by focusing on compartment-level nodes instead of exhaustive tiling, which limits graph size compared to patch-based approaches. However, scalability remains a potential limitation, and handling larger, denser graphs may require pooling, sampling or sparsification strategies in future work.

Beyond such technical concerns, patient-level biological variability further complicates the challenge of robust AMR diagnosis. For example, one individual could exhibit AMR through vascular lesions, e.g., intimal arteritis, while another’s AMR presentation might be predominantly glomerular. This patient-to-patient variability in the relevance of each tissue compartment for AMR diagnosis helps explain why adding arteries or cortex to glomerular graphs may greatly enhance classification accuracy for certain WSIs but act as noise in others. Furthermore, other criteria such as C4d staining of PTCs and medullary vasa recta or evidence of DSAs, both of which are not evident in light microscopy images, can also be the determining factors. Therefore, two graphs with the same morphological features could correspond to different diagnoses, which also increases the complexity when training the classifiers. Another limitation of our study is the absence of patient-level clinical metadata such as DSA status or comorbid allograft diseases, which were not available in the anonymized dataset. This precludes subgroup analyses but can also be viewed as an advantage for scalability: our pipeline requires only raw WSIs and biopsy-level labels, making it broadly applicable to larger cohorts where detailed clinical data may not be consistently available.

At the biopsy level, aggregating multiple WSIs reduces the impact of individually misclassified slides, leading to overall higher classification performance. This performance gap between WSI- and biopsy-level predictions likely reflects the focal nature of some allograft pathologies, where not every WSI from a biopsy necessarily contains the diagnostic features of AMR. In such cases, biopsy-level aggregation provides a more reliable representation of the disease than any single WSI, making biopsy-level assessments more clinically useful by mitigating variability from WSI-level misclassifications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the advantages of graph-based models for AMR diagnosis using PAS-stained WSIs. By representing WSIs as graphs, GNNs capture spatial relationships and inter-compartment interactions, leading to improved performance. The best glomeruli-only graph model outperformed the strongest patch-wise classifier by 5.34 % in accuracy. Incorporating arteries and cortical tiles further improved classification, reaching accuracies of 86.97 % at the WSI level and 89.53 % at the biopsy level (a 21.31 % gain over baseline on a challenging multi-institutional dataset).

The entire pipeline is fully automated, combining deep learning-based segmentation, feature extraction and classification. This design enables scalable deployment on large WSI repositories with minimal expert input, making it suitable for cost-effective, reproducible decision support in routine pathology workflows.

Despite these improvements, AMR diagnosis remains inherently complex, particularly when relying solely on PAS-stained sections. Identical PAS-stained WSIs can correspond to different diagnoses depending on complementary information, such as C4d staining or DSAs, which are absent in PAS-stained images. Additionally, performance variability across different feature extraction methods and graph construction choices highlights the challenge of designing an optimal computational approach.

These findings emphasize the need for refined, domain-informed feature extraction techniques, as well as multimodal strategies that integrate complementary histological and immunogenetic data. Future work should also explore novel algorithmic approaches tailored to nephropathology to build upon the translational foundation established here. By incorporating spatial context and structural information, graph-based methods already provide reliable and scalable decision support for AMR diagnosis. Looking forward, external validation on independent cohorts and integration with patient-level clinical metadata will be essential to ensure broader robustness and clinical utility.
