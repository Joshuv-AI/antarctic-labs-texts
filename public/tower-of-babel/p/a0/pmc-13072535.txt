Incidence, management, follow-up and long-term outcomes of spinal dysraphism in a cohort of diverse origins and cultures: a 17-year cohort study
Authors: Tachrifa Bacar, Ny Ary Tsiky Lalasoa, Simon Lorrain, Brahim Bouhmani, Sébatien Freppel, Pierre-Yves Robillard, Silvia Iacobelli, Etienne Suply
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal dysraphism is a congenital condition requiring lifelong management to prevent long-term complications. Our institution is a referral center for congenital malformations in the Indian Ocean, particularly serving Réunion Island, Mayotte, Madagascar, and the Comoros. The aim of this study was to describe a cohort of children with spinal dysraphism followed at our center.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients diagnosed with spinal dysraphism who were born between January 1, 2006 and December 31, 2022, and followed at our institution. Data were extracted from electronic records and covered the antenatal period, delivery, initial management and follow-up.ResultsOver the 17 years of the study period, 121 patients were included. The annual mean incidence was 2.1/10,000 live births and remained stable throughout the study period. Among the 121 patients, 82 had a prenatal diagnosis, and 69 underwent a proactive management. Proactively managed patients originated from Réunion Island (n = 41), Mayotte (n = 12), the Comoros (n = 12) and Madagascar (n = 3). Surgical closure was performed in all patients with open defects, and a ventricular shunt was used in half of the patients. The median follow-up duration was 4.2 years. The mortality rate was 4.3%, and 42% of patients were lost to follow-up. At least one clinical review was conducted by a neurosurgeon in 85% of cases, a urologist in 71%, or an orthopedic specialist in 49%. Neurodevelopmental delay was observed in all patients who required a shunt placement. Ambulation was achieved in 39% of the patients.ConclusionsThe incidence of spinal dysraphism remained stable over time in our population. The monitoring of patients was not optimal, but the remote origin of some of our patients must be taken into account. Further studies should be performed to evaluate continence and long-term complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08885-w.

Background

Spinal dysraphism (SD) results from failure of neural tube closure during embryogenesis [1]. Several factors have been associated with SD, including folic acid deficiency, maternal diabetes and, less frequently, a family history of neural tube defects [2]. The prevalence of SD varies considerably across countries [3, 4], ranging from 3.7 per 10,000 live births in North America to 8.1 per 10,000 in Asia. Notably, the incidence in Réunion Island was reported to be 13.1 per10,000 births between 2011 and 2015 [5].

Réunion Island is a French overseas department located in the Indian ocean. Its university hospital provides a level of medical care comparable to that of Western countries and serves as a referral center for patients from neighboring islands and countries, including Madagascar, Mayotte, Mauritius and the Comorian Islands. As a result, healthcare professionals at our institution routinely manage patients from diverse linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic backgrounds.

The management of SD extends beyond surgical closure of the neural placode closure. Clinical manifestations vary widely depending on the location and the extent of the defect, as well as on the level of care and the timing of the clinical management. Published guidelines have been made available, particularly in the United States, to assist in care planning and long-term follow-up [6–8].

Long-term management of patients with SD presents multiple challenges and requires a multidisciplinary approach involving pediatricians, neurosurgeons, orthopedic surgeons, urologists, and rehabilitation specialists. Anticipation and aggressive management of walking disability, hydrocephalus, pressure sores, and neurogenic bladder are essential. Clinical deterioration with aging is also a challenge in patients with SD. For example, a tethered spinal cord may have a negative consequence on walk ability. Bladder dysfunction represents another major concern, not only for dryness purposes, but also for the preservation of renal function, while urosepsis is a frequent cause of morbidity in this population [9].

In low- and middle-income countries, however, published guidelines are rarely applicable. This raises important questions regarding the initial and long-term management of patients referred from abroad who may lack access to structured follow-up programs after returning to their home countries.

The aim of this study was to describe the incidence of SD in our single-center university hospital over a 17-year period, and to report patterns of initial management and follow-up.

Methods

This single-center retrospective study included all consecutive patients with open or closed spinal dysraphism who were born between January 1, 2006 and December 31, 2022, and whose mothers were referred to Réunion Island University Hospital.

Patients were identified through the French hospital stay database (Programme de Médicalisation des Systèmes d’Information, PMSI) with a diagnosis of SD (international classification of diseases 10th edition codes used: Q05 – SD and Q76.0 – SD occulta). Patients with a filum terminale lipoma, caudal regression or Currarino syndrome were not included.

Data were collected from the electronic record program system (Crossway®) used in our hospital and from the hospital perinatal database.

Maternal demographic characteristics included age at delivery, nationality, residence during pregnancy, and employment status. Medical history data included prior pregnancies affected by neural tube defects, obesity, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and alcohol consumption.

Pregnancy-related variables included SD antenatal diagnosis, timing of diagnosis, in- utero transfer, and whether medical termination of pregnancy was proposed or performed. Neonatal data included date of birth, sex, gestational age, and birth weight. Palliative care at birth was decided jointly by the parents and the medical team and consisted of non-surgical management followed by death within the first month of life. Patients who underwent surgery were classified as receiving proactive management. Surgical data included age at operation, defect location and characteristics, postoperative complications, cerebrospinal fluid shunting procedures, associated complications, and length of hospital stay.

Long-term follow-up in the proactive management group included documentation of specialist consultations (pediatric neurologist, neurosurgeon, orthopedic surgeon, urologist, and rehabilitation physician).

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians and ranges. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and results are presented descriptively in tables and figures. This study was conducted in accordance with French legislation.

As per French law applicable to research involving human subjects (Jardé Act), specific approval of an ethics committee is not required for this non-interventional study based on retrospective, anonymized data of authorized collections. All parents are provided with oral information (no-opposition form), and written parental consent is not needed.

This study was registered under French Health Data Hub under ID F20231121130604.

Results

Over the 17-year period, 121 cases of SD were managed at our institution (Fig. 1). Antenatal diagnosis was established in 67.8% of cases. The reason for death before birth was either unexplained, or a result of a medically assisted abortion (n = 43). A neonatal palliative care pathway was implemented in 9 patients including 4 with a postnatal diagnosis and 5 with a postnatal diagnosis. Ultimately, 69 patients were proactively managed, including 35 postnatally diagnosed.

Figure 2 illustrates the annual number of referrals and incidence for 10,000 live births. The mean number of referred patients was 7 per year, with substantial interannual variability, ranging from 3 patients in 2010 and 2016 to 19 patients in 2013. On average, 2 in-utero transfers and 1.7 postnatal transfers occurred annually (supplemental data). The number of patients receiving proactive management remained relatively stable at approximately 4.4 per year. Postnatal transfers involved 26 foreign patients (Mayotte, n = 12; Madagascar, n = 3; The Comoros, n = 11), and 7% of mothers did not speak French and required interpreter assistance for medical explanations and consents.

Walking ability was analyzed after 18 months of age and varied according to the lesion level. Among the patients with a proactive management, 37.7% were able to walk, including 60.0% with a sacral defect, 43.6% with a lumbo-sacral defect, 33.0% with a thoracic malformation.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the care pathway for patients with SD managed at our institution over a 17-year period. Although the total number of SD cases increased between 2011 and 2020, the number of patients receiving proactive management remained stable due to a combination of pregnancy terminations and neonatal palliative care. Our analysis focused on the 69 patients who underwent proactive management, making this the first cohort study to describe both incidence and outcomes of SD on Réunion Island. In our cohort, 32 out of 121 patients (26.4%) had a medical evacuation, all of whom were foreign nationals. This factor may contribute to the relatively high prevalence of SD observed in Réunion Island. Notably, no case outside Réunion Island ended with a medical abortion, whereas 43% of SD cases were prenatally diagnosed on Réunion Island. In addition, 3 miscarriages and 8 cases of neonatal palliative plans were noted in patients from Réunion Island, in contrast with only one palliative case from Mayotte. Cultural, religious, and referral biases likely explain these differences.

Only patients with a presupposed proactive plan were referred and transferred for initial management. In addition, 68% of the 121 patients with SD had a prenatal diagnosis; in other words, 93.9% of patients with a prenatal diagnosis were born in Réunion Island. This percentage appears to be consistent with that of a study performed in the Netherlands, reporting 88% of antenatal diagnosis and 69% of medical abortion for SD in a cohort of 85 pregnancies [10].

Among Réunion Island patients with a proactive program, there was a predominance of lumbosacral locations (54%), followed by sacral locations (15%), lumbar locations (13%), and finally thoracic locations (8%). This distribution aligns with data from the United States National Spinal dysraphism Patient Registry (NSBPR), which comprises the first 10 clinics with a focus on SD. Among the 1,763 SD patients in this study, one-third had sacral lesions, 44.8% had lumbosacral lesions, and 24.9% had lumbar and upper thoracic lesions [11]. All patients, with the exception of those who had spina occulta (n = 3), underwent surgical closure. Because of the correlation between Chiari condition and brainstem functional disorders, the severity and clinical implications of which can differ greatly, this malformation is important for children with open SD [6]. In our study, 47% of patients were diagnosed with this disease highlighting the need for careful monitoring due to potential psychomotor and neurological complications [12]. In our cohort, developmental impairment was observed in half of our patients.

The management of patients with SD requires a multidisciplinary approach [13]. Our study demonstrated that even if our institution is able to provide all kinds of specialist reviews, our data showed suboptimal completeness with the neurosurgeon (85%), the urologist (71%), the orthopedic surgeon (49%), and the rehabilitation physician (43%). Although these data indicate the engagement of various healthcare professionals, evaluating their impact on health outcomes and patient quality of life remains challenging. One explanation for the lack of specialist review is the proportion of patients who returned from their native land. Telemedicine may help improve continuity of care in the future.

Functional outcomes varied according to the location area of SD. With respect to mobility and ambulation in patients with myelomeningocele in our study, more than half of the patients (60%) with a sacral lesion were able to walk, followed by those with an occipital meningocele (50%), patients with a lumbosacral lesion (43%), and those with a thoracic lesion (33%). A study conducted by James W. Roach et al. [14], which analyzed 84 patients with SD, reported slightly different findings. In this study, no patient with a thoracic lesion was ambulatory; 14% of patients with a thoracolumbar lesion were able to walk; 22% of those with a lumbosacral lesion ambulated; and 100% of patients with a sacral lesion were ambulatory. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that only 49.3% of our patients were reviewed at least once by an orthopedic surgeon. This type of management is, however, very important and aims to prevent skeletal deformations, pressure sores, and back pain and promotes ambulation when hip dislocation is found, for example.

Loss to follow-up remains a key limitation of this study and may affect reported outcomes, including mortality and functional status. Follow-up issues may also have been encountered due to cultural diversity and language barriers; in our study, 7% of families with children with SD did not speak French and required specific translators. Future works could establish a prospective registry and standardized patient information tools to improve follow-up and long-term care.
