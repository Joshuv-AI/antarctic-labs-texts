Spatiotemporal plasticity of the gut microbiome facilitates redbelly tilapia (Coptodon zillii) invasion in China
Authors: Shoujie Tang, Ying Xing, Zhiyi Yan, Kenneth Nyirenda, Jinliang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe redbelly tilapia (Coptodon zillii) is a highly invasive fish species posing serious ecological risks to freshwater systems in China. To uncover mechanisms behind its invasion success, this study investigated spatiotemporal plasticity of its gut microbiome and explored the drivers of such plasticity. Using high-throughput 16 S rRNA sequencing, gut microbiota of C. zillii from five water bodies (two rivers, three reservoirs) and from Shuikou Reservoir across four seasons were compared.ResultsAmong populations from different habitats, 6 of the top 10 abundant phyla and 17 of the top 30 genera varied significantly (P < 0.05). Gut microbiota differed significantly (P < 0.05) in all pairwise population comparisons. Alpha diversity indices also showed significant differences among some populations (P < 0.05). Of 24 digestion-related KEGG pathways identified, 23 exhibited significant abundance variations across populations. Relative gene abundances of beta-glucosidase (P = 0.0025) and chitinase (P = 0.022) also differed significantly. Water temperature, dissolved oxygen, and conductivity were key environmental drivers of gut microbiota variation. Notably, microbial community assembly was predominantly governed by deterministic processes (> 60%), with heterogeneous selection contributing 42%–63%. In seasonal samples from Shuikou Reservoir, 6 of the top 10 phyla and 15 of the top 30 genera showed significant seasonal variation (P < 0.05). PERMANOVA indicated significant differences (P < 0.05) in all pairwise seasonal comparisons. Of 19 digestion-related KEGG pathways, 13 exhibited significant abundance variations across seasons. Chitinase-coding gene abundance varied significantly among populations (P < 0.0001). Key factors driving seasonal differentiation were pH, conductivity, turbidity degree, and total nitrogen.ConclusionsThe gut microbiome of invasive C. zillii displays significant spatiotemporal variation in structure, diversity, and functional gene composition. This pronounced plasticity—rather than a stable, specialized microbiota—likely serves as a key adaptive strategy, enabling the species to thrive in heterogeneous environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s42523-026-00604-2.Keywords: Coptodon zillii, Gut microbiome, Spatiotemporal variation, Invasive species, Microbial plasticity, Environmental adaptation

Background

Biological invasions represent a leading cause of global biodiversity decline [13], imposing significant pressures on terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems, with profound ecological and economic consequences [80]. Freshwater ecosystems, which harbor the highest species richness per unit area globally [26], are among the most vulnerable to exotic species invasions [72]. Globally, 277 invasive alien freshwater species have been documented, with fishes constituting the dominant taxonomic group (161 species) [21]. Among invasive fishes, tilapias are recognized as particularly detrimental and highly invasive [74]. Belonging to the family Cichlidae, tilapias comprise several genera, including Coptodon, Oreochromis, Pelmatolapia, and Sarotherodon, with notable species such as C. zillii, Oreochromis mossambicus, O. niloticus, O. aureus, and Sarotherodon galilaeus. Of these, C. zillii is regarded as one of the most widespread and ecologically harmful tilapias worldwide [15]. Coptodon zillii (formerly Tilapia zillii) is native to northern half of Africa and parts of the Middle East [75]. Since the early 20th century, it has been introduced globally—both intentionally for aquatic weed control and aquaculture, and unintentionally—and has successfully naturalized in numerous countries owing to its broad environmental tolerance and distinct life-history traits [33]. Coptodon zillii is a cold-tolerant tilapia species and a potential competitor of native fishes for food and spawning resources [22]. In recent decades, several countries—including the United States [18], Iran [7], Iraq [69], Japan [48], and China [35]—have reported adverse ecological impacts of C. zillii, such as water quality deterioration and declines in native fish communities. It is listed as an invasive alien species in the Global Invasive Species Database (https://www.iucngisd.org/gisd/) (2024).

China is among the countries most affected by C. zillii invasion. The species was introduced from Thailand in 1978 by the Guangdong Food Branch of the China National Cereals and Oils Import and Export Corporation [14] and cultured in coastal provinces including Guangdong, Fujian, and Guangxi during the 1980–1990 s. Due to its small size and slow growth, C. zillii was primarily used for hybridization with Mozambique tilapia to enhance cold tolerance [54], and was subsequently abandoned by farmers. Escaping from aquaculture facilities, it has established populations across multiple river systems, including the Pearl River (e.g., Hongshui, Beijiang, Dongjiang, and Xijiang rivers), southeastern coastal rivers (e.g., Hanjiang, Minjiang, and Qiantang rivers), Hainan Island rivers (e.g., Nandujiang, Wanquan, and Changhua rivers), and the Yangtze River system (e.g., Jinsha and Xiangjiang rivers), exhibiting continued expansion. Given its environmental adaptability, thermal tolerance, and high fecundity, C. zillii threatens the structure and function of Chinese aquatic ecosystems. It was listed among China’s 59 most invasive alien species in the List of Invasive Species for Priority Management on December 20, 2022 [37]. Under ongoing globalization, climate change, and intensified human activities, further spread and population outbreaks are likely without proactive management. Understanding the biological mechanisms behind C. zillii’s invasion success is essential for developing effective prevention and control strategies.

Successful fish invasions are influenced by several key factors, including propagule pressure, genetic variation [25], pre-adaptation to novel environments [95], and life-history traits such as environmental tolerance [19], rapid growth [12], reproductive capacity [71], and trophic plasticity [86]. As an integral component of life-history strategies, fish gut microbiota reflect host-environment interactions across developmental stages, offering insights into host-microbe coevolution [68]. The gut microbiome, a dynamic microbial community, plays essential roles in host digestion, metabolism, immune development, and pathogen defense [100]. Gut microbes possess a greater gene repertoire than their host and contribute to metabolic processes through the synthesis of essential nutrients—such as amino acids, vitamins, and short-chain fatty acids—and the enzymatic breakdown of complex carbohydrates via amylases, proteases, lipases, chitinases, and phytases [77].

Recent recognition of the microbiome’s fundamental role in host biology and fitness [64] has highlighted its potential contribution to adaptation in novel environments, which may determine invasion success [90]. While the role of symbiotic bacteria in invasive species has been widely studied in terrestrial ecosystems [61]—with insect research showing that microbiota can influence traits such as competitor suppression [92], nutrient acquisition [3], stress resilience [11], and increased fecundity and survival [44]—studies in aquatic systems remain limited. For example, gut microbiomes of invasive hybrid carps in the Mississippi River Basin were shown to aid local adaptation through enhanced resource utilization [105], and Cyanobacteria-dominated gut communities in silver carp reflected their algal-based diet [101]. Research on tilapia gut microbiomes is particularly scarce. Liu et al., [56] identified gut microbial biomarkers in invasive Nile tilapia in China’s Pearl River basin, while Liu et al., [57] demonstrated that spatial variation in gut microbiome composition, diversity, and function in invasive C. zillii populations facilitated local adaptation and invasion in the Pearl River system.

Fish gut microbiota composition is influenced by multiple factors, including host genetics [53], diet [66], trophic level [55], disease prevalence [43], and environmental conditions such as habitat type and husbandry practices [23]. For instance, Gaikwad et al., [32] observed significant differences in the gut microbiota of Mozambique tilapia (O. mossambicus) from river versus lake habitats, underscoring the role of habitat in shaping microbial diversity. Invasive fishes often encounter substantial environmental shifts—such as altered food resources and climatic conditions—in newly colonized areas, which may drive adaptive restructuring of their gut microbiomes, reflecting the community’s inherent plasticity. However, current research on invasive fish microbiomes remains largely descriptive, emphasizing static inter-population comparisons within invaded regions. A systematic analysis of microbial structure and function across continuous spatiotemporal gradients—encompassing diverse habitat types (e.g., rivers, reservoirs), seasonal variations, and environmental characteristics—is still lacking. This study therefore addresses the following central question: How does the gut microbiome of the invasive C. zillii respond to spatiotemporal environmental variation across its invaded range, and does such plasticity represent a key mechanism facilitating its invasion success?

Early investigations of fish gut microbial diversity primarily relied on culture-dependent methods and microscopy [99], which are limited by the inability to culture many microorganisms [47]. The advent of next-generation sequencing has enabled the acquisition of large volumes of sequence data from specific DNA biomarkers [65]. Among high-throughput platforms, Ion Torrent semiconductor sequencing is widely used in microbial community studies targeting the 16 S rRNA gene, offering relatively long read lengths and low error rates [8].

To comprehensively assess spatiotemporal plasticity in the gut microbiota of the invasive fish C. zillii across its introduced range in China, we sampled gut contents from individuals across five water bodies spanning 9.86° latitude and 10.47° longitude. These sites represented two distinct habitat types—rivers and reservoirs—encompassing both lotic and lentic environments. Additionally, we conducted a longitudinal survey over four seasons (spring, summer, autumn, winter) in a typical large subtropical reservoir to evaluate intra-annual variation, hypothesizing that seasonal shifts in food resource availability may alter host-microbiome interactions and reshape microbial community structure. Using Ion Torrent semiconductor sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene, we characterized gut microbial composition and inferred functional potential. We further applied neutral community models [82], beta nearest-taxon index (βNTI) [103], and a taxonomically modified Raup–Crick metric [84] to quantify the contributions of stochastic and deterministic processes to microbial assembly across habitats and seasons. Our objectives were to: (1) evaluate spatiotemporal variation in the gut microbiota of invasive C. zillii; (2) disentangle the roles of stochastic and deterministic processes in structuring the gut microbiome of C. zillii across habitats and seasons; and (3) identify how environmental, seasonal, and climatic variables influence microbial community composition, alpha diversity, beta diversity, and functional profiles. We predicted that: (1) the gut microbiota would exhibit considerable spatiotemporal plasticity, driven by environmental selection and reflected in dynamic diversity and functional profiles; (2) deterministic processes dominate microbial community assembly across habitats, whereas stochastic processes prevail across seasons; and (3) a portion of inter-population variation would correlate with local environmental and climatic conditions. To our knowledge, this study presents the first comparative analysis of gut microbiota in invasive C. zillii across broad geographic, habitat, and seasonal gradients.

Methods

Study area

The five sampling sites selected for this study encompass major water bodies in China severely invaded by C. zillii, representing its principal distribution range from north to south: Qiandao Lake (QDH), Shuikou Reservoir (SKSK), Letan Reservoir (XJ), Dongjiang River (DJ), and Nandujiang River (NDJ). Among these, QDH, SKSK, and XJ represent lentic (reservoir) habitats, while DJ and NDJ represent lotic (riverine) habitats. Detailed geographical and environmental information of the sampling sites is provided in Fig. 1.

Map of the sampling sites of C. zillii in China. The locations of QDH, SKSK, DJ, NDJ and XJ were indicated with yellow, pink, purple, red and light blue dots, respectively

Qiandao Lake, situated in Chun’an County, Zhejiang Province, is a subtropical valley-type reservoir in the upper Qiantang River basin, with an average depth of 30 m and a surface area of 580 km2. It represents the northernmost recorded invasion extent of C. zillii in China. Although the precise introduction pathway of C. zillii into the lake remains unclear, Nile tilapia (O. niloticus) were cultivated in hapas within the reservoir as early as 1987 by the local aquaculture authority [102]. We hypothesize that C. zillii may have been accidentally introduced alongside Nile tilapia fry and subsequently escaped into the reservoir from these enclosures.

Shuikou Reservoir, situated in Yanping District, Nanping City, Fujian Province, is a river-type reservoir in the middle reaches of the Minjiang River, with a surface area of approximately 99.6 km2 and an average depth of about 65 m. Following its impoundment in March 1993, C. zillii has been cultured extensively in earthen ponds around the reservoir. It is believed that escapes occurred due to inadequate aquaculture management, leading to the establishment of a self-sustaining population in the reservoir, with numbers increasing markedly since 2010 [42].

Letan Reservoir is situated in Xincheng County, Laibin City, within the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. This large hydropower facility lies on the lower section of the Hongshui River, part of the Pearl River Basin, with an average depth of 112 m and a total storage capacity of 9.5 × 108 m3. Coptodon zillii was first introduced for hapa-based aquaculture in the upper Hongshui River in 2007. Following escapes from culture systems, the species formed self-sustaining populations upstream. It is hypothesized that C. zillii recorded in Letan Reservoir originated through dispersal from these upstream areas.

Located in Huizhou City, Guangdong Province, the DJ sampling site lies within the middle Dongjiang River, a major tributary of the Pearl River system. The Dongjiang River spans 562 km in length and drains a basin area of 35,340 km2. Coptodon zillii was introduced to Guangdong from Thailand in 1978 for aquaculture purposes. Insufficient management led to its escape into local watercourses, and the species rapidly colonized the Dongjiang River via the interconnected Pearl River network. In recent years, C. zillii has expanded significantly, becoming a dominant and widespread species in this river system [36].

The NDJ sampling site is situated in Ding’an County, Hainan Province, along the lower Nandujiang River—the largest river on the island, with a mainstream length of 334 km and a drainage area of 7,033 km2. Coptodon zillii was introduced into Hainan for aquaculture in 1986, but cultivation was soon discontinued due to unsatisfactory growth performance. Escaped individuals subsequently colonized natural waterways, establishing populations in the Nandujiang River [35]. In recent years, the species has undergone rapid expansion, becoming a dominant component of the local fish assemblage [36].

Environmental and climatic data for the five sampling sites (Table S1) were obtained from two national databases. Nine water quality parameters—water temperature (TEMP), dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, conductivity (COND), turbidity degree (TD), permanganate index (PI), ammonia nitrogen (AN), total phosphorus (TP), and total nitrogen (TN)—were sourced from the China National Environmental Monitoring Centre (https://www.cnemc.cn/). Climatic variables, comprising daily mean, maximum, and minimum temperatures, as well as daily precipitation totals, were acquired from the Public Meteorological Service Center of the China Meteorological Administration (https://www.weather.com.cn).

Field sampling and laboratory procedures

Sample collection and preparation

Field sampling was performed in two phases. Phase 1 took place from October to November 2022 at five sites (QDH, SKSK, XJ, DJ, and NDJ). A total of 347 live specimens were captured using multi-mesh gillnets (mesh sizes: 3–8 cm), with sample sizes of 110 (QDH), 63 (SKSK), 68 (XJ), 51 (DJ), and 55 (NDJ). This phase aimed to examine geographic variation in gut microbiota and their association with environmental factors (Table S1). Phase 2 was conducted monthly from March 2023 to February 2024 at SKSK, using gillnets of the same specifications. A total of 1,079 individuals were obtained, with monthly sample sizes ranging from 80 to 100. To evaluate seasonal effects, the sampling period was categorized into four seasons: spring (March–May), summer (June–August), autumn (September–November), and winter (December–February). Due to the invasive nature of gut content sampling, which requires euthanasia of the fish, different individuals were collected at each time point (cross-sectional design). All fish were randomly sampled from the reservoir using standardized multi-mesh gillnets to minimize sampling bias. All sampling was carried out by licensed local fishermen under approval from relevant fisheries authorities. Gillnet deployment occurred at dawn, coinciding with the peak feeding activity of C. zillii.

Upon collection, fish were first sexed based on genital pore morphology. Each specimen was then anesthetized by immersion in an overdose of MS‑222 (0.4 mg/mL) for 15 min. Standard length (SL, to 0.1 cm) and body weight (to 0.1 g) were measured using digital calipers and an electronic balance, respectively. Following measurement, individuals were aseptically dissected in a laminar flow cabinet. The gastrointestinal tract was excised, and gut contents were gently extruded from the mucosal lining. These samples were immediately flash‑frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C in sterile cryotubes. In Phase 1, a total of 24 gut content samples were obtained—five from each site except QDH, from which four were collected. In Phase 2, 58 samples were secured, with 15 collected per season except winter (13 samples). This sample size (13–15 individuals per season) was determined based on a power analysis using pilot data, which indicated that n ≥ 12 per group provides > 80% power to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) at α = 0.05 for alpha diversity comparisons. Details on SL, body weight, and sex for specimens from each site are provided in Tables S1 and S2.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Upon arrival at the laboratory on dry ice, gut content samples were suspended in 2–20 ml of TE buffer and homogenized using a TissueRuptor. A 200 µl aliquot of the homogenate was subjected to DNA extraction with the QIAamp Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), incorporating the following modifications: incubation in InhibitEx Buffer was extended to 30 min at 72 °C, and a 10‑min bead‑beating step with 0.70 mm garnet beads (MOBIO, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was performed after adding lysis buffer and proteinase K. DNA concentration was quantified using an ND‑1000 spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA), and samples were diluted to 20 ng/µl with sterile water. For quality control, extraction blanks were included as negative controls, and three randomly selected controls were sequenced. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341 F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’) [17], with sample‑specific barcodes incorporated for multiplexing (Table S3). PCR reactions (20 µl) contained 10 µl Phusion MasterMix (2×), 1 µl each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 1 µl DNA template (20 ng/µl), and 7 µl ddH2O. Thermal cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplification products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, excised, and purified using the Gene JET Gel Recovery Kit (Thermo Scientific). Libraries were prepared with the Ion Plus Fragment Library Kit (Thermo Fisher) and sequenced on an Ion S5TMXL platform (Thermo Fisher) after quality assessment by Qubit quantification.

DNA sequence processing

Raw sequencing reads were processed using Cutadapt v1.9.1 [49] to remove low-quality regions and trim barcode and primer sequences. The resulting reads were aligned against a reference database using Vsearch [76] for chimera detection and removal, yielding high-quality clean reads. Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% sequence similarity using Uparse v7.0.1001 [63], with the most abundant sequence within each OTU designated as the representative sequence. After removing host (C. zillii) derived sequences, taxonomic assignment of OTU representatives was performed using the RDP Classifier v2.2 [89] against the Silva 132 database [38], with a minimum match threshold of 98%. All assignments were further verified by BLASTN against the GenBank database using a sequence identity cutoff of ≥ 97% and an e-value threshold of 10− 5. Microbial community composition was analyzed across all standard taxonomic ranks (kingdom to species). Multiple sequence alignment was conducted with MUSCLE v3.8.31 [27] to infer phylogenetic relationships among OTUs. Finally, data from all samples were normalized to ensure comparability for downstream analyses.

Data analyses

Analysis of basic gut microbiota data

Species rarefaction curves were generated using the vegan and ggplot2 packages in R (version 4.0.3) to evaluate sequencing depth adequacy across samples. Sequences that could not be classified (i.e., < 97% identity via BLASTN against GenBank) were excluded from downstream analyses. The relative abundance of each microbial taxon was calculated as the percentage of its reads relative to the total reads in a sample, reflecting its approximate contribution to the community biomass. Relative read abundance (RRA) was computed as: RRA (%) =×100, where T is the total number of taxa, N is the number of samples, and Si, j represents the read count of taxon i in sample j. Bar charts illustrating RRA at the phylum and genus levels were generated using Perl 5.26.2. Taxa not ranking among the top ten at the phylum level or the top thirty at the genus level were grouped as “Others”. Differences in RRA among groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test at a significance level of α = 0.05. When a significant overall difference was detected, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test, and p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Taxon codes used in the visualizations are provided in Table S4.

We confirmed that sex had no significant effect on alpha diversity in our preliminary analyses (P > 0.05); therefore, it was not included as a covariate in the final models.

A species abundance profile was generated by aggregating feature sequences according to their taxonomic classifications. Beta diversity between samples was assessed using both Weighted UniFrac and Bray–Curtis distances, computed in QIIME v1.9.1 [16]. Heatmaps visualizing the resulting distance matrices were generated using Perl 5.26.2. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Weighted UniFrac distances was performed using the ade4 and ggplot2 packages in R (v4.0.3) to evaluate similarities among microbial communities across samples, populations, or seasons. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations, applied to Bray-Curtis distances using the adonis2 function in the vegan package in R, was employed to test for significant differences among groups (five populations or four seasons). To identify microbial taxa (from phylum to genus) that differed significantly between populations, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was conducted via the Galaxy module (http://galaxy.biobakery.org/). Taxa with LDA scores exceeding the threshold of 4 were considered statistically discriminative.

Influence of environmental, geographic, and climatic factors on gut microbiota structure across populations

Selection of environmental and climatic predictors

To mitigate potential multicollinearity among environmental and climatic predictors, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was applied. Variables exhibiting VIF values exceeding 10 were considered highly collinear and subsequently excluded. This procedure retained predictors with greater independence and relevance to gut microbiota variation.

Spearman’s correlation analysis

Associations between microbial taxa and environmental variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, implemented via the corr.test function from the psych package (R v4.0.3). Significant correlations were visualized in a heatmap generated with the pheatmap package.

Analysis of multiple linear regression models

To identify environmental variables significantly influencing H’, J, and B, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted. Predictor variables were standardized, and normality of the response variables was assessed; non-normally distributed responses were subjected to logarithmic or inverse transformation. All statistical procedures were performed using SPSS 22.0.

Redundancy analysis (RDA)

Gradient-based ordination analysis was conducted using CANOCO 4.5 to evaluate how gut microbial taxa respond to environmental drivers across habitats and seasons. Microbial relative abundance data were Hellinger-transformed prior to detrended correspondence analysis (DCA). The length of gradient (LGA) from the first DCA axis determined the choice of ordination method: redundancy analysis (RDA) for LGA < 3, canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) for LGA > 4, and both if LGA fell between 3 and 4 [88]. Based on LGA values below 3 in all analyzed scenarios (Table S5, Table S6), RDA was selected. In these models, microbial taxa served as response variables, while environmental factors were included as predictors without transformation, as RDA does not assume specific distributional properties for explanatory variables [87]. Rather than including a binary ‘habitat type’ (river vs. reservoir) as a separate covariate, we treated each of the five sampling populations as an independent group in the RDA models. This approach allowed us to capture site-specific environmental heterogeneity (e.g., dissolved oxygen, temperature, conductivity) beyond the simple river–reservoir dichotomy, as our primary objective was to understand the continuous environmental drivers of gut microbiome variation across the invaded range. Significance of RDA axes was evaluated using Monte Carlo permutation tests (5000 iterations), and the first two axes were visualized in ordination plots depicting sample–environment relationships.

Mantel test

To assess the relationship between geographic distance and gut microbial dissimilarity, pairwise geographic distance matrices derived from GPS coordinates were compared with Bray-Curtis distance and Schoener’s overlap index matrices using the Mantel test in the ade4 package (v1.7-15) with 10,000 permutations. The reported R2 values represent the squared Mantel correlation coefficients (coefficients of determination).

Analysis of Microbial Community Assembly Mechanisms

To investigate the assembly mechanisms of gut microbial communities in invasive C. zillii, this study quantified the relative influences of stochastic and deterministic processes using a multidimensional statistical framework. The Sloan neutral community model [82] was fitted in R v3.5.1 to evaluate the role of random dispersal. A null model approach was applied within each group to assess phylogenetic and taxonomic turnover via the beta nearest taxon index (βNTI) and the taxonomically modified Raup–Crick metric (RC) [103]. Pairwise comparisons with |βNTI| > 2 indicated heterogeneous (βNTI > + 2) or homogeneous (βNTI < − 2) selection. When |βNTI| ≤ 2, RC values were used to distinguish between dispersal limitation (RC > + 0.95), homogeneous dispersal (RC < − 0.95), and undominated processes including drift (|RC| ≤ 0.95) [84, 103]. These analyses were conducted separately across five water bodies (DJ, XJ, NDJ, QDH, SKSK) and four seasons (Spring–Winter) in SKSK. By integrating neutral model fits and βNTI/RC distributions, we identified the predominant ecological processes shaping microbial assembly over space and time.

Functional profiling of gut microbiota

Functional profiles of gut microbial communities were predicted using Tax4Fun [2] by mapping 16 S rRNA-based OTU data to KEGG orthologs (KOs). Only orthologs associated with level one KEGG pathways were retained; missing entries were replaced by the next most abundant ortholog. Relative abundances of functional pathways and enzymes were inferred accordingly. We focused on digestive pathways to compare populations across habitats and seasons. Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap and RColorBrewer packages in R (v4.0.3), with two-way hierarchical clustering (average linkage). Pathway abundances were log10-transformed and converted to z-scores for visualization. Based on known dietary habits of redbelly tilapia [86], we analyzed key digestive enzymes: beta-glucosidase and endoglucanase (cellulose degradation), chitinase (chitin digestion), and carboxypeptidase T (protein breakdown). Differences in gene abundances across populations were tested using one-way ANOVA in SPSS 22.0, with Dunn–Sidàk correction [1] applied for post-hoc comparisons when significant effects were detected.

Results

DNA sequencing

Effective sequencing tags from gut content samples averaged between 85,864 and 105,177 across five populations from two habitat types, with effective tag percentages ranging from 72.72% to 84.69% (Table S7). For seasonal samples from SKSK, tag counts spanned 66,791 to 91,400, with efficiencies of 76.53% to 88.21% (Table S7). All sequencing data met quality thresholds for downstream analyses. Rarefaction curves of the Shannon–Wiener index approached saturation with increasing sequencing depth (Figure S1), suggesting adequate sampling effort. Good’s coverage values exceeded 0.99 for all samples (Figure S2), confirming near-complete representation of the bacterial communities.

Gut microbial composition

A total of 11,293 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified across gut content samples from five populations spanning two habitats (Table S7). Taxonomic annotation revealed 42 phyla and 978 genera. The most abundant phylum and genus were Proteobacteria (35.04%) and Cetobacterium (18.16%), respectively (Table S8, Table S9, Figs. 2a and 3a). The ten predominant phyla, based on relative abundance, comprised Proteobacteria (35.04%), Firmicutes (26.53%), Fusobacteriota (18.18%), Actinobacteriota (12.30%), Bacteroidota (4.84%), Verrucomicrobiota (0.82%), Chloroflexi (0.72%), Cyanobacteria (0.40%), Desulfobacterota (0.17%), and Planctomycetota (0.16%). In seasonal samples from SKSK, 22,162 OTUs were delineated (Table S7), annotated to 52 phyla and 1,153 genera. Proteobacteria (26.44%) and Cetobacterium (23.67%) dominated at the phylum and genus levels, respectively (Table S10, Table S11, Figs. 2b and 3b). The top ten phyla by relative abundance included Proteobacteria (26.44%), Fusobacteriota (23.68%), Firmicutes (19.18%), Actinobacteriota (16.10%), Bacteroidota (4.69%), Euryarchaeota (2.48%), Chloroflexi (1.95%), Cyanobacteria (1.66%), Verrucomicrobiota (1.31%), and Desulfobacterota (0.60%).

Gut microbiota variation with habitat

Gut microbial phyla counts across the DJ, NDJ, XJ, QDH, and SKSK populations were 35, 27, 28, 27, and 27, respectively. Six of the top ten most abundant phyla exhibited significant variation among sites (P < 0.05) (Table S8). Proteobacteria relative abundance was significantly elevated in NDJ and QDH compared to XJ (P < 0.05), while Fusobacteriota was more abundant in XJ than in QDH (P < 0.05). Among the 30 dominant genera, 17 differed significantly across locations (P < 0.05) (Table S9). Core genera included Cetobacterium, Ralstonia, Romboutsia, and Bacillus. Cetobacterium was more abundant in XJ than QDH (P < 0.05); Romboutsia showed higher abundance in DJ, XJ, and NDJ relative to QDH (P < 0.05); and Bacillus was enriched in DJ compared to QDH (P < 0.05). Alpha diversity indices (H’, J) were significantly higher in SKSK than in QDH and XJ (P < 0.05; Table 1, Figure S3a, Figure S3c). Niche breadth index (B) was also greater in SKSK than in QDH (P < 0.05; Table 1). Bray–Curtis dissimilarity among populations ranged from 0.6593 to 0.9785 (Table S12). NMDS ordination yielded a stress value of 0.062 (Fig. 4a), and PERMANOVA confirmed significant community differences among all population pairs (P < 0.05; Table 2). Schoener’s niche overlap indices at the phylum level spanned 0.1734–0.6650, with three pairs (NDJ–DJ, NDJ–SKSK, NDJ–QDH) exceeding 0.6 (Table S13). Linear discriminant analysis highlighted Fusobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, Proteobacteria, and Firmicutes as key phyla driving inter-population differences (Fig. 5a).

Temporal variation of gut microbiota

To assess population-level seasonal dynamics, 13–15 randomly collected individuals were analyzed per season. Gut microbial phyla identified in seasonal samples from the SKSK population numbered 42 (spring), 34 (summer), 43 (autumn), and 35 (winter) (Table S10). Significant seasonal variation was observed in six of the ten most abundant phyla (P < 0.05) (Table S10). Proteobacteria exhibited higher relative abundance in spring compared to summer (P < 0.05), while Bacteroidota was more abundant in spring and winter than in fall (P < 0.05). Among the top 30 genera, 15 differed significantly across seasons (P < 0.05) (Table S11). The core genera present in nearly all samples were Cetobacterium and Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1. No significant seasonal differences were detected in alpha diversity (H’, J) or niche breadth index (B) (Table 1, Figure S3b, Figure S3d). Bray–Curtis dissimilarity between seasonal pairs ranged from 0.5721 to 0.6854 (Table S14). NMDS ordination indicated a stress value of 0.1 (Fig. 4b), and PERMANOVA confirmed significant compositional differences across all seasonal comparisons (P < 0.05; Table 2). Schoener’s overlap indices at the phylum level ranged from 0.6735 to 0.8687, all exceeding the 0.6 threshold (Table S15). Linear discriminant analysis identified Proteobacteria, Euryarchaeota, and Chloroflexi as the primary phyla contributing to seasonal differences in gut microbiota (Fig. 5b).

Factors influencing gut microbiota variation across habitats and seasons

Three environmental variables with variance inflation factor (VIF) values below 10 were identified across habitats: TEMP (VIF = 1.52), DO (VIF = 1.11), and COND (VIF = 1.59). In seasonal samples from SKSK, six factors met the same criterion: pH (VIF = 3.47), COND (VIF = 4.00), TD (VIF = 4.00), PI (VIF = 7.43), TP (VIF = 3.66), and TN (VIF = 6.23).

Spearman correlation analysis identified significant relationships between environmental variables and the top ten gut microbial phyla across five populations from two habitats (Figure S4a). Notably, Proteobacteria and Desulfobacterota were positively correlated with DO, whereas Chloroflexi showed a negative association with COND (P < 0.05). At the genus level, Bacillus and Romboutsia exhibited significant positive correlations with TEMP (P < 0.05), while Cetobacterium was significantly negatively correlated with DO (P < 0.05) (Figure S5a). Among the dominant gut microbial phyla in seasonal SKSK samples (Figure S4b), Firmicutes exhibited significant negative correlations with pH, COND, and TN (P < 0.05), while Proteobacteria showed significant positive associations with TP and TN (P < 0.05). At the genus level, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 was significantly positively correlated with pH, and Stenotrophomonas displayed a significant positive relationship with TN (P < 0.05) (Figure S5b).

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed significant associations between alpha diversity indices and environmental factors across the studied populations. For the five populations from two distinct habitats, both the J and B indices showed significant positive relationships with DO (P < 0.05; Table S16). In seasonal samples from SKSK, Shannon diversity index (H’) exhibited a significant correlation with COND (P < 0.05; Table S17).

RDA was performed to evaluate the relationship between environmental variables and the gut microbiota of C. zillii from five sampling sites, analyzed at both phylum and genus levels (Tables 3 and 4; Figs. 6a and 7a). Significant explanatory power was detected along the first two RDA axes at the phylum level (RDA1: F = 7.9, P = 0.002; RDA2: F = 4.0, P = 0.002). These axes collectively accounted for 79.25% of the variance in the microbiota–environment relationship, with RDA1 and RDA2 explaining 51.06% and 28.19%, respectively (Fig. 6a). DO and COND were identified as key drivers shaping gut microbial community structure across habitats. DO exhibited the strongest influence (r2 = 0.8269), followed by COND (r2 = 0.3385) (Table 3).

RDA was applied to assess associations between environmental variables and the gut microbiota of C. zillii across seasonal samples from SKSK, analyzed at the phylum and genus levels (Tables 3 and 4; Figs. 6b and 7b). At the phylum level, both the first (RDA1: F = 8.5, P = 0.004) and second (RDA2: F = 2.7, P = 0.002) ordination axes were statistically significant. Together, these axes explained 55.59% of the variation in the microbiota–environment relationship, with RDA1 and RDA2 contributing 34.96% and 20.63%, respectively (Fig. 6b). TN, pH, COND, PI, and TD were identified as influential factors driving seasonal shifts in gut microbial composition. Mantel tests indicated no significant association between gut microbiota overlap and geographic distance (Table S18) across the five populations from two habitat types, either at the phylum (R2 = 0.0036, P = 0.8684) or genus (R2 = 0.0639, P = 0.4811) level (Figure S6a, Figure S6b). Note that the reported R2 values represent the squared Mantel correlation coefficient (i.e., the coefficient of determination). Similarly, the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity of microbial communities was not significantly correlated with spatial distance among populations (R2 = 0.2335, P = 0.1571) (Figure S6c).

Gut microbial community assembly processes across different habitats and seasons

A neutral community model (NCM) was applied to evaluate the role of stochastic processes in structuring gut microbiota across habitat and seasonal groups of C. zillii (Figure S7, Figure S8). In populations from five water bodies, NCM yielded negative R2 values and minimal migration rates (Nm), indicating a strong deviation from neutral expectations (Figure S7). β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) analysis revealed that mean values exceeded + 2 for all populations except DJ (βNTI = 1.9), reflecting dominant deterministic assembly across sites (Fig. 8a). Heterogeneous selection contributed more than homogeneous selection to community assembly, with the QDH population exhibiting the strongest heterogeneous selection signal (Fig. 8b). In seasonal samples from SKSK, stochastic processes prevailed throughout the year (Figure S8). Except for winter (βNTI = -2.77), βNTI values in other seasons ranged from 0.28 to 1.22, further supporting stochastic dominance (Fig. 8c and d). All |RC| values remained below 0.95, suggesting that ecological drift and other weak nondeterministic processes primarily drove community variation (Fig. 8e).

Functional divergence of gut bacterial communities across habitats and seasons

A total of 24 digestion-related pathways were identified across the five populations from two habitat types, with major categories including carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism, digestive system, and membrane transport. Of these, 23 pathways exhibited significant abundance differences among populations (Fig. 9a). Notably, the XJ population showed significantly higher enrichment (P < 0.05) than NDJ and SKSK in specific carbohydrate metabolism pathways (glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, citrate cycle, and galactose metabolism). Additionally, the XJ population displayed significantly elevated levels (P < 0.05) in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism relative to NDJ, SKSK, and QDH. In seasonal samples from SKSK, 19 digestion-associated pathways were identified, spanning carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism, digestive system function, and membrane transport. Among these, 13 pathways exhibited significant abundance variations across the four seasons (Fig. 9b). Summer samples showed markedly higher enrichment (P < 0.05) than spring and winter in two carbohydrate metabolism pathways—glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and butanoate metabolism. Conversely, the winter population demonstrated significantly elevated levels (P < 0.05) in the energy metabolism pathway (oxidative phosphorylation) relative to the summer population.

Discussion

Phenotypic plasticity—the adaptive adjustment of life-history traits to environmental variables such as food availability, temperature, and population density—is essential for organismal adaptation [60]. Gut microbial communities, as shown by metagenomic and meta-transcriptomic analyses, rapidly alter their structure and function in response to shifts in host diet or habitat, thereby enhancing host metabolic versatility and phenotypic plasticity [62]. This microbial adaptability may underpin vertebrate phenotypic flexibility, particularly in invasive species confronting novel ecological pressures such as resource scarcity or extreme climates [4]. For instance, gut microbiome plasticity facilitated Mediterranean invasions by Siganus rivulatus and S. luridus [30] and supported the establishment of Cyprinella lutrensis in southeastern U.S. rivers [40]. In China’s Pearl River, invasive Nile tilapia exhibited gut microbial traits—including high alpha diversity, reduced interspecific competition, and enhanced dietary niche breadth—fostering local adaptation [56]. These findings support the hypothesis that gut microbiota plasticity is a determinant of invasion success in alien species [104]. In the present study, the gut microbiome of invasive C. zillii displayed marked spatiotemporal variation in structure, diversity, and functional potential. Rather than maintaining a fixed composition, this high plasticity appears to be a central adaptive mechanism for coping with environmental heterogeneity, as further elaborated in subsequent sections.

Spatial plasticity of the gut microbiome and its underlying driving mechanisms

Gut microbiome spatial plasticity has been identified as a key adaptive mechanism facilitating invasion success in non-native fishes [30, 40, 56, 57]. For instance, comparative analysis of C. zillii from the Dongjiang (DJ) and Xijiang (XJ) tributaries in China’s Pearl River revealed marked differences in gut microbial composition, diversity, and function, enhancing local adaptation and invasion progression [57]. Our study further demonstrates pronounced spatial plasticity in C. zillii’s gut microbiome across structural, diversity, and functional levels. Structurally, 6 of the top 10 phyla and 17 of the top 30 genera differed significantly among sites (P < 0.05). In diversity, alpha diversity indices (H’, J) and niche breadth (B) varied between populations—SKSK exhibited higher values than QDH/XJ (P < 0.05). Beta diversity was consistently high (Bray–Curtis: 0.6593–0.9785), with PERMANOVA confirming significant interpopulation distinctions (P < 0.05). Functionally, 23 of 24 digestion-related pathways showed differential abundance; the XJ population had significantly elevated enrichment in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, citrate cycle, and galactose metabolism versus NDJ/SKSK (P < 0.05). Beta-glucosidase and chitinase gene abundances also differed significantly (P = 0.0025 and P = 0.022, respectively). Notably, the XJ population exhibited significantly elevated beta-glucosidase gene abundance compared to the NDJ and SKSK populations (P < 0.05). Mantel tests indicated no significant correlation between microbial dissimilarity and geographic distance, suggesting adaptive divergence rather than dispersal limitation. These findings confirm extensive spatial plasticity in C. zillii’s gut microbiota, underpinning its invasive success.

The functional capacity of gut microbiota is governed by community structure, which is shaped by host genetics [28], habitat [50], environmental conditions [85], diet [55], physiology [20], and geographic setting [9]. These factors likely underpin the observed spatial plasticity in the gut microbiome of C. zillii.

Host genetic background is a key determinant of gut microbiota composition, as evidenced by intra- and interspecific variations across fish species [28]. Reduced microbiome diversity in non-native populations, such as in Clavelina oblonga, has been linked to genetic bottlenecks [34]. Similarly, greater genetic divergence among threespine stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus) populations correlates with distinct microbial profiles, mediated by host genotype via immune regulation [83]. Genomic resequencing of C. zillii revealed severe bottlenecks in the QDH and XJ populations, with QDH exhibiting significantly lower nucleotide diversity (π) (our unpublished data). This reduced genetic variability likely explains their lower gut microbial alpha diversity (H’ and J). Elevated genetic differentiation (FST) between QDH and other populations further corresponds to higher microbial beta diversity (Bray–Curtis distance), underscoring the role of host genetic structure in shaping microbiota variation.

Abiotic environmental conditions, particularly temperature, constitute another major driver of gut microbiome variation. Physicochemical parameters (e.g., temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, pH) significantly influence fish gut microbiota composition and function through direct microbial selection and indirect host-mediated pathways [24]. Temperature exerts host- and environment-specific effects, shaping microbial communities by impacting microbial survival, host metabolism, and diet interactions. For instance, temperature fluctuations modulate the abundance and metabolic activity of Bacillus and Romboutsia in fish guts [98]. In this study, temperature (TEMP, r2 = 0.2756) was the second strongest predictor of genus-level microbial structure. Bacillus and Romboutsia—two of the four most abundant genera—showed significant positive correlations with temperature (P < 0.05). The QDH population exhibited significantly lower abundances of both genera compared to warmer sites (DJ, XJ, NDJ; P < 0.05). As the northernmost invasive site in China, QDH has a lower mean annual surface temperature; winter temperatures averaged 12.19 °C in 2022, notably colder than other sites (SKSK: 16.65 °C, DJ: 17.77 °C, XJ: 17.97 °C, NDJ: 22.80 °C). These thermal differences likely contribute to the distinct gut microbiome profile observed in QDH.

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is a critical environmental determinant of fish gut microbiota [39], driving microbial differentiation [83] and co-varying with community shifts across aquatic systems [91]. In this study, DO exerted the strongest influence on microbial structure at both phylum (r2 = 0.8269) and genus (r2 = 0.8079) levels. Proteobacteria abundance correlated positively (P < 0.01), while Fusobacteriota correlated negatively (P < 0.05) with DO, consistent with QDH (higher DO: 9.11 mg/L) exhibiting elevated Proteobacteria and reduced Fusobacteriota compared to XJ (7.15 mg/L; P < 0.05). Aerobic Proteobacteria (e.g., Hydrogenophaga, Acidovorax) thrive under high DO in freshwater systems, whereas low DO may suppress them via host stress and immune alterations [67]. Similarly, the dominant genus Cetobacterium showed a negative correlation with DO (P < 0.05), being more abundant in XJ than QDH (P < 0.05). As an anaerobic taxon involved in nutrient metabolism (e.g., vitamin B12 synthesis), Cetobacterium benefits from low-oxygen intestinal conditions, while high DO may inhibit it [5]. Thus, DO variation likely underpins the distinct gut microbiota between QDH and XJ populations.

Dietary variation represents a third key factor shaping gut microbiota, as the five study regions differ substantially in available food resources. Fish gut microbial composition is strongly influenced by diet, with distinct nutrient inputs (e.g., proteins, fibers) selectively enriching specific bacterial groups such as cellulose-degrading or protease-producing taxa [57]. Diet is widely recognized as a major predictor of microbiome structure [6]. For instance, threespine sticklebacks in larger lakes with diverse diets exhibit greater microbial individuality [41], and dietary diversity is positively correlated with microbial niche width [57]. Similarly, a study on Nile tilapia indicated that greater gut microbiome alpha diversity is associated with enhanced dietary resource heterogeneity in the environment [56]. In this study, the SKSK population showed significantly higher gut microbial α-diversity (H’ and J) and niche width (B) than QDH (P < 0.05). This aligns with findings that SKSK’s diet encompassed more prey phyla (17) compared to QDH (11) [86], supporting the role of dietary diversity in driving microbial community differences.

Aquatic food resources markedly shape the taxonomic and functional composition of fish gut microbiota. For example, herbivorous fish often harbor anaerobic Firmicutes (e.g., Clostridia) [70], while carnivorous species exhibit dominance of genera like Vibrio and Photobacterium [24]. In this study, the XJ population showed significantly higher abundance of Fusobacteriota (P < 0.05) and greater enrichment in the alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism pathway (P < 0.05) compared to QDH. Fusobacteriota abundance is strongly associated with animal-based diets (e.g., crustaceans, mollusks), which promote their proliferation, whereas plant-based resources reduce it [46]. Gut microbiota also facilitates amino acid metabolism, aiding in the digestion of animal-derived foods [78]. Consistent with this, Tang et al., [86] reported that animal prey constituted a significantly higher proportion of the diet in XJ (RRA = 30.73%) than in QDH (RRA = 3.86%) (P < 0.05), supporting the hypothesis that dietary differences drive the observed microbial and metabolic variation between populations.

Gut microbes in omnivorous fish contribute to cellulose digestion by producing enzymes like endoglucanases. For instance, Actinobacteria and Bacteroidota aid plant cell wall degradation in Oplegnathus punctatus [97]. Here, endoglucanase gene abundance was significantly higher in QDH than SKSK (P < 0.05), corresponding to a greater dietary proportion of Chlorophyta in QDH (RRA = 32.98%) versus SKSK (RRA = 2.74%) ([86]; P < 0.05), suggesting diet-driven enzymatic adaptation.

Temporal plasticity of the gut microbiome and its underlying driving mechanisms

Fish gut microbiota exhibits temporal dynamics across daily to interannual scales [31], with long-term shifts often linked to seasonal environmental variation—particularly in freshwater ecosystems—highlighting microbial plasticity and adaptive evolution [101]. Seasonal and temperature changes significantly influence gut microbial composition [85], often elevating bacterial abundance in summer and autumn [73]. Invasive species further demonstrate microbial adaptation to seasonal shifts [52], as seen in Pomacea canaliculata, where diversity peaks in summer [52]. This study reveals pronounced seasonal plasticity in the gut microbiome of invasive C. zillii in Shuikou Reservoir, China, across structural, diversity, and functional dimensions. Structurally, significant compositional differences occurred in 6 of the top 10 phyla and 15 of the top 30 genera (P < 0.05). Beta-diversity varied markedly among seasons (Bray–Curtis distance: 0.5721–0.6854), with PERMANOVA confirming significant inter-season differences (P < 0.05). Functionally, 13 of 19 digestion-related pathways showed seasonal variation: glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and butanoate metabolism were significantly elevated in summer (P < 0.05), while oxidative phosphorylation increased significantly in winter (P < 0.05). Chitinase gene abundance also differed seasonally (P < 0.0001), rising in spring, summer, and winter relative to autumn (P < 0.05). These findings underscore the temporal adaptability of gut microbiota in response to seasonal environmental cues.

Dietary variation across seasons is a primary driver of gut microbial plasticity in fish, often outweighing environmental influences [83]. In C. zillii, seasonal shifts in food resources likely induce microbial restructuring, as diet profoundly shapes piscine microbiota [29]. Similar dietary-dependent microbial shifts occur in Fejervarya limnocharis [45], supporting the role of host diet in facilitating temporal microbial adaptation [81].

The gut microbiota of fish is strongly shaped by dietary resources, with herbivorous and omnivorous species exhibiting greater microbial diversity than carnivorous counterparts [94]. Key taxa such as Clostridium, Citrobacter, and Cetobacterium dominate across feeding guilds [29]. In the present study, Bacteroidota was significantly more abundant in spring and winter populations of C. zillii than in autumn (P < 0.05), accompanied by significantly elevated enrichment of pyruvate and galactose metabolism pathways (P < 0.05). Bacteroidota contributes to plant-fiber digestion via cellulases and xylanases, generating short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that acidify the gut and inhibit pathogens [10]. Microbial support for plant-based diets involves polysaccharide degradation, metabolic regulation, and SCFA production, with carbohydrate pathways acting as functional cores influenced by diet and environment [58]. In black Amur bream (Megalobrama terminalis), seasonal shifts in water temperature and salinity elicit adaptive responses in gut microbiota, primarily through modulation of amino acid and carbohydrate metabolic pathways [58]. Notably, Tang et al., [86] reported significant higher intake of plant prey (Chlorophyta, Bacillariophyta, Haptophyta) in spring (RRA = 24.92%) and winter (RRA = 10.77%) compared to autumn (RRA = 3.22%) (P < 0.05) in the same reservoir. Thus, seasonal dietary variation likely drives Bacteroidota abundance and associated carbohydrate metabolism, enhancing host adaptation to fluctuating resources.

A notable observation in this study was the significantly higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the gut microbiota of the spring population of C. zillii (RRA = 38.59%) compared to the summer group (RRA = 11.34%) (P < 0.05). As a dominant phylum in fish intestines, Proteobacteria contributes to nutrient metabolism and organic matter decomposition, including the silica-based cell walls of diatoms [96, 101]. Diatoms (Bacillariophyta) constituted a substantially larger portion of the diet in spring (RRA = 22.89%) than in summer (RRA = 0.48%) ([86]; P < 0.05). Proteobacteria, particularly Deltaproteobacteria, may facilitate diatom digestion via silicase secretion, aiding nutrient release [96]. This supports the hypothesis that seasonal dietary shifts in diatom intake drive temporal variation in intestinal Proteobacteria abundance.

Deterministic dominance in gut microbiome assembly underpins the invasion success of C. zillii

Gut microbiome assembly is influenced by both deterministic and stochastic processes [59]. While neutral theory attributes community structure to random birth, death, and dispersal [103], niche theory emphasizes deterministic factors like environmental conditions and species interactions [84]. Analytical tools such as the neutral community model (NCM), βNTI, and Raup–Crick index based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (RCBray) help quantify their relative contributions [103]. In this study, integrated βNTI and NCM analyses indicated that deterministic processes dominate the gut microbial assembly of invasive C. zillii across habitats, comprising ≥ 60% of assembly mechanisms. Heterogeneous selection was particularly prominent (βNTI > 2) in sites including Xijiang River, Nandujiang River, Qiandao Lake, and Shuikou Reservoir. Habitat variability has been shown to mediate deterministic-stochastic balances, enabling microbial adaptation to environmental gradients [57]. Supporting this, Tang et al., [86] observed significant dietary plasticity in Chinese invasive C. zillii, and RDA analysis revealed marked differences in water temperature, dissolved oxygen, and conductivity among sites. We infer that diet and abiotic heterogeneity are key deterministic drivers of gut microbiome differentiation. The dominance of heterogeneous selection indicates that C. zillii adapts to new environments through microbial plasticity rather than a conserved core microbiota. Its gut microbiome rapidly assembles in response to local conditions, enabling flexible resource use across diverse aquatic systems. This adaptive strategy likely underpins the species’ successful invasion throughout China’s major river basins.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the seasonal sampling employed a cross-sectional design, in which different individuals were collected at each time point rather than tracking the same individuals longitudinally. This design was necessitated by the invasive nature of gut content sampling, which requires euthanasia. Consequently, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that individual genetic variation contributes to some fraction of the observed seasonal differences. However, several lines of evidence suggest that seasonal effects are the primary driver: (i) the substantial overlap in standard length and body weight ranges across seasons (Table S2) minimizes size- or age-related confounding; (ii) the consistent seasonal patterns observed across multiple independent samples within each season (as reflected by within-season standard errors in Tables S10 and S11) indicate that seasonal signal exceeds individual noise; and (iii) the neutral community modeling results (Fig. 8c–e) show that stochastic processes (drift) dominate seasonal assembly, arguing against strong host genetic filtering. Second, we used OTU clustering at 97% sequence similarity rather than ASV-based methods (e.g., DADA2). This choice was necessitated by the Ion Torrent sequencing platform, whose error profile is less compatible with the error models underlying DADA2. While OTU-based methods may collapse closely related taxa and provide lower resolution than ASVs, our conclusions regarding community composition, diversity, and functional predictions are robust to this choice, as these metrics are primarily driven by higher-rank taxonomic shifts (phylum to genus level). Third, we used LEfSe to identify differentially abundant microbial taxa across populations and seasons. While LEfSe is a widely accepted method for biomarker discovery in microbiome studies, we acknowledge that it does not directly incorporate covariates (e.g., sex, environmental parameters) into its statistical framework. Future studies employing covariate-adjusted differential abundance methods (e.g., DESeq2, ANCOM-BC, MaAsLin2), longitudinal tracking of individually marked fish, ASV-based inference, and updated reference databases are needed to further validate the findings of this study and to better disentangle the effects of group identity from environmental covariates.

Conclusion

This study provides multidimensional evidence of the high plasticity of the gut microbiome in C. zillii from both temporal and spatial perspectives. This plasticity is primarily driven by two mechanisms: environmental filtering and host-microbe synergistic functional adaptation. We propose that the invasion strategy of C. zillii involves a “dual adaptability”: its inherent broad physiological tolerance, combined with its highly plastic gut microbiome as an “extended adaptive organ,” enables it to rapidly decode and adapt to the complex challenges of various freshwater ecosystems in China. This is likely one of the core mechanisms underlying its successful invasion. Future studies could further validate the causal role of specific microbial taxa in helping the host adapt to particular environmental stressors through controlled microbial colonization experiments.
