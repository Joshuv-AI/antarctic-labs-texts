Alveolar Ridge Preservation Using Low Dosage Bone Morphogenetic Protein 2 Incorporated Biomimetic Calcium Phosphate
Authors: Wenjing Zhang, Jie Liang, Dedong Yu, Zhonghao Liu, Yuelian Liu, Lingfei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Adequate bone quantity and quality in the alveolar ridge are essential for the successful placement of dental implants and the achievement of desirable aesthetic outcomes. Following tooth extraction, irreversible bone resorption occurs especially at the buccal site, resulting in a significant reduction in alveolar ridge dimensions compared to its original state.1, 2, 3, 4 This resorption affects both the ridge’s width and the height, with the width reduction up to 60% and the height reduction approximately 40%.3,4 These dimensional changes compromise the available bone for implant placement, hinder the orientation of implant, and adversely affect the final aesthetic outcomes and long-term stability of the implant restoration.

Alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) in which bone substitutes were grafted into the extraction sockets to promote new bone formation and reduce ridge resorption has become a widely adopted strategy to preserve the alveolar ridge dimensions in clinical practice. Various bone substitutes have been used for ARP, including autogenous, allogeneic, xenogeneic, and synthetic materials.5,6 These bone substitutes can promote osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, thereby enhance bone formation and preserve alveolar bone integrity.1,7 Autogenous bone is considered the gold standard due to its inherent osteogenesis, osteoconduction, and osteoinduction.8, 9, 10 However, its clinical application is limited by restricted availability and donor site morbidity. Allogeneic bone substitutes also have challenges in supply and potential disease transmission.11,12 Xenogeneic bone substitutes, particularly deproteinised bovine bone mineral (DBBM), which were widely used due to their slow degradation rate and good space maintenance, have demonstrated relative acceptable efficacy in ARP. However, these bone substitutes double the socket healing period compared with natural healing, which may reduce patient compliance.5,8,13 Moreover, although xenogeneic bone substitutes exhibit exceptional oseoconductivity, they lack osteoinductivity and have excessively slow degradation rate. The bone formation may be hindered by the residual particles by occupying valuable space.14 Synthetic bone substitutes, especially calcium phosphate-based bone substitutes, are commonly used in bone augmentation due to their excellent biocompatibility and osteoconductivity. However, their limited osteoinductivity and mismatch between degradation rate and new bone formation compromised the ARP outcome. Therefore, endowing bone substitutes with osteoinductivity while ensuring an appropriate degradation rate is of great significance for enhancing the efficacy of ARP.

Bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) has been identified as one of the most osteoinductive growth factors and widely applied in bone regeneration.15,16 However, current delivery systems often require high dosage BMP-2 to achieve therapeutic effects, raising concerns on their side effects due to excessive dosage and burst release. Studies have shown that incorporating low dosage BMP-2 into sustained-release system can enhance its efficacy while minimising risks.17 Previously, our research group has developed a novel bone substitute—BMP-2-incorporated biomimetic calcium phosphate (BMP-2/BioCaP)—using a layer-by-layer self-assembling coating method.18,19 In this system, BMP-2 is embedded within the lattice of a biomimetic calcium phosphate coating, which has appropriate degradation rate and serves as the BMP-2 sustained-release carrier.20 Animal studies have shown that BMP-2/BioCaP degrades in cell-mediated manner, allowing for the sustained release of BMP-2, thereby promoting continuous bone formation in ectopic tissues.21 Besides, BMP-2/BioCaP has been shown to effectively regenerate critical-sized calvarial defects in rats and horizontal periodontal defects in beagle dogs.22,23 Our preliminary clinical trial further confirmed that the low dosage BMP-2 delivered via biomimetic calcium phosphate coating can promote rapid bone formation in human’s extraction sockets within six weeks.24,25 Given its favourable properties, BMP-2/BioCaP holds significant potential as bone substitutes for ARP.

This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of BMP-2/BioCaP in ARP in a canine model. The efficacy was assessed by measuring vertical and horizontal changes in ridge dimensions using dual CBCT superimposition immediately and 12 weeks after surgery. Besides, micro-CT and histomorphometric analyses were conducted at 12 weeks postoperatively to assess bone regeneration within the sockets.

Methods

Synthesis of BioCaP granules and surgical establishment of canine mandibular critical-sized defects

Preparation of BioCaP and BMP‐2/BioCaP granules

BioCaP and BMP‐2/BioCaP granules were synthesised in a sterile environment using the layer-by-layer self-assembling coating method. Briefly, amorphous calcium phosphate layer was prepared by incubating simulated body fluid (684 mM NaCl, 12.5 mM CaCl₂·2H₂O, 5 mM Na₂HPO₄·2H₂O, 21 mM NaHCO₃, and 7.5 mM MgCl₂·6H₂O) at 37 °C for 24 hours. Then a supersaturated calcium phosphate solution (40 mM HCl, 4 mM CaCl₂·2H₂O, and 2 mM Na₂HPO₄·2H₂O) was prepared and incubated at 37 °C for 48 hours to deposit a crystalline calcium phosphate layer on the surface of amorphous calcium phosphate layer. BioCaP particles were obtained through a three-cycle, layer-by-layer self-assembly process involving alternating amorphous and crystalline calcium phosphate coatings. To produce BMP-2-loaded BioCaP (BMP-2/BioCaP), 2 mg/L recombinant human BMP-2 (Medtronic) was added during the final crystalline calcium phosphate coating step. The BioCaP and BMP-2/BioCaP granules ranged in size from 0.25 to 1 mm were filtered through a sieve and used in subsequent animal experiments. Preliminary testing revealed that the BMP-2 dosage in BMP-2/BioCaP granules was 182.6 ± 34.6 μg/g.

Establishment of mandibular furcation defect model in beagle dogs

The study included six healthy male beagles, average weighing 12kg and aged 18-24 months. During the study period, the dogs were housed individually and fed a soft diet. Oral hygiene was maintained with twice-weekly toothbrushing and daily rinsing with 0.5% chlorhexidine solution to control plaque accumulation.

Prior to surgery, general anaesthesia was induced via intravenous sodium pentobarbital (20-30 mg/kg), followed by local infiltration anaesthesia using articaine with epinephrine at the mandibular surgical sites. The second, third, and fourth mandibular premolars were hemisected, with flapless extraction of the distal roots and subsequent root canal treatment of the mesial roots (Figure 1A–C).

Surgical implantation procedures of bone graft materials

Under general and local anaesthesia, each dog received four different treatment conditions, randomly assigned to extraction sockets: (a) no bone substitute material (blank control), (b) deproteinised bovine bone matrix (DBBM, Bio-Oss, Geistlich, particle size 0.25-1 mm), (c) biomimetic calcium phosphate (BioCaP), and (d) BMP-2-loaded biomimetic calcium phosphate (BMP-2/BioCaP). All grafted sites were covered with a collagen matrix (CM, Mucograft, Geistlich) and sutured. Two additional extraction sockets in each dog were grafted with DBBM and covered with different collagen membranes for unrelated studies and were excluded from the analysis. (Figure 1D–F) Postoperatively, pain and infection were managed with oral ibuprofen (10 mg/kg, every 12 hours for 48 hours) and daily broad-spectrum antibiotics for 7 days. The researchers who performed measurements and analyses were completely blinded to the allocation.

Radiographic and histomorphometric evaluation of bone regeneration

CBCT measurement analysis

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans (11*8 cm field of view, 0.15 mm resolution; Newton) were performed immediately after surgery and at 12 weeks postoperatively. CBCT datasets were imported into Romexis software (Planmeca), and dual-timepoint scans were superimposed using stable mandibular landmarks. Registration accuracy was maintained at < 0.1 mm for nonoperated hard tissues. As shown in (Figure 2), sagittal sections passing through the centre of each extraction socket were selected to measure vertical buccolingual ridge height loss and horizontal width changes at 1 mm, 3 mm, and 5 mm below the crest.

Micro-CT measurement analysis

At 12 weeks postoperatively, animals were euthanised via anaesthetic overdose. Mandibular specimens were harvested, rinsed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 3 days, followed by overnight rinsing in running water. Samples were trimmed and subjected to micro-CT scanning using a SkyScan 1176 system (Bruker, Belgium) under the following conditions: 90 kV voltage, 270 μA current, and 17.4 μm resolution. For each extraction site, the mesial root region was scanned in the sagittal plane and mirrored to define the region of interest (ROI) at the distal root site. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined at horizontal planes located 1 mm, 3 mm, and 5 mm apical to the alveolar crest. (Figure 2). Quantitative analyses were performed using proprietary Bruker software to measure, including: bone mineral density (BMD); bone volume fraction (BV/TV [%]); Trabecular number (Tb.N [1/µm]); Trabecular thickness (Tb.Th [µm]); Trabecular separation (Tb.Sp [mm]).

Histological observations and histometric analyses

Samples were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in methyl methacrylate. Serial sagittal sections were obtained at 1mm intervals, polished to 50 μm thickness, and stained with McNeal’s trichrome solution. Three representative sections were selected from the central region of each extraction socket. Two experienced, unbiased examiners independently conducted these analyses. Histomorphometric analysis was performed using a Leica DC 200 microscope and Leica QWin software (Leica Microsystems), along with Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA). For each parameter in every section, two measurements were made, one by each examiner. The following parameters were measured including: new bone volume density (%); residual material volume density (%); unmineralised tissue volume density (%).

Statistic analysis

All statistical values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (SPSS16, IBM, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for analysis. When significant, t-tests were performed for post hoc comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at P < .05.

Results

Intraoral observations

Throughout the entire experimental period, all beagle dogs remained healthy, and intraoral wounds demonstrated excellent healing without signs of wound dehiscence or significant inflammation. Decreases in surface contour alterations were observed in all groups after 12 weeks of socket preservation.

Radiographic observations and analysis

Twelve weeks after ARP, CBCT analyses (Table 1) revealed significantly reduced buccal bone height loss in the BMP-2/BioCaP + CM group (1.31 ± 1.07 mm) and the DBBM + CM group (1.33 ± 0.42 mm) compared to the blank control group (2.28 ± 0.68 mm). However, the lingual bone height loss values were consistently lower than buccal values across all groups, with no statistically significant differences among them. For horizontal bone width resorption, both the BMP-2/BioCaP + CM (1.63 ± 0.62 mm) and DBBM + CM (1.78 ± 1.1 mm) groups exhibited significantly less resorption than the blank control group (3.38 ± 1.42 mm) at 1 mm below the alveolar crest. Although the BMP-2/BioCaP + CM group showed the lowest horizontal resorption values at 3 mm and 5 mm below the crest, these differences were not statistically significant compared to the other groups.

Histological observations and histometric analyses

Histomorphological analysis (Figure 4) showed that in the blank control group, new bone formation was primarily observed at the crest and near the existing alveolar bone, with limited bone formation in the central socket region, which remained predominantly occupied by marrow. The buccal bone wall exhibited considerable resorption and collapse. In the DBBM + CM group, new bone was primarily distributed around DBBM + CM particles near the original alveolar bone, while the central socket region was largely occupied by fibrous tissue encapsulating DBBM + CM particles, leaving minimal osteogenic space. Nonetheless, the buccolingual bone walls were well preserved. In the BioCaP + CM group, bone formation occurred near the native alveolar bone, similar to the DBBM + CM group, but with significantly less residual substitute material. The BMP-2/BioCaP + CM group exhibited the most extensive bone regeneration. Abundant newly formed trabecular bone was observed both centrally and peripherally within the socket, with most of the residual substitute material surrounded by uniformly distributed bone. Only a few particles were encapsulated by connective tissue. Furthermore, the BMP-2/BioCaP + CM group effectively preserved buccolingual bone contours, and new mature alveolar ridge formation was evident even at the resorbed buccal crest.

Discussion

This preclinical study demonstrated that BMP-2/BioCaP combined with a collagen matrix revealed superior alveolar ridge preservation outcomes compared to DBBM + CM group and BioCaP + CM group. The bone regeneration was significantly promoted by the incorporation of low dosage of BMP-2, consistent with previous studies.26, 27, 28 CBCT superimposition analysis showed that both the BMP-2/BioCaP + CM and DBBM + CM groups exhibited minimal buccal height and width changes at 1 mm below the crest compared to the blank control, highlighting the effectiveness of BMP-2/BioCaP in preserving the alveolar ridge contour. Micro-CT findings further confirmed that BMP-2/BioCaP enhances bone formation and mineralisation, resulting in appropriate bone architecture favourable for implant placement and primary stability. Moreover, the histological observations further demonstrated that the BMP-2/BioCaP + CM group gained higher bone volume and reduced nonmineralised tissue volume, indicating synchronised material degradation and bone regeneration.

Bone Morphogenetic Protein 2 (BMP-2) is a well-established osteoinductive factor that promotes osteogenic cell proliferation and differentiation. The principal aim of ridge preservation is to maintain alveolar morphology to facilitate future implant placement. BMP-2 not only enhances bone regeneration but also minimises bone resorption, contributing to ridge stability.15,29,30 In clinical practice, alveolar ridge preservation is often performed in sockets with pre-existing bone loss. BMP-2 use in such cases not only conserves existing bone but also aids in defect repair, optimising conditions for subsequent implantation. Moslemi et al.16,31 reviewed BMP-2 applications and concluded that BMP-2 is particularly beneficial in preserving ridge contours in sockets with ≥ 50% buccal bone loss. When defects are minimal, no statistical difference is observed between BMP-2 and traditional grafts. Our results align with this, as flapless extractions were performed in intact sockets using low-dose BMP-2/BioCaP. While no significant differences were found between BMP-2/BioCaP and DBBM in ridge height and width preservation, both outperformed the blank control.

Fiorellini et al.32 reported that higher BMP-2 doses (1.50 mg/ml) were effective in maintaining ridge contours, but lower doses (0.75 mg/ml) yielded results similar to controls. In contrast, our study utilised a layer-by-layer self-assembled BioCaP scaffold containing an ultralow dose of BMP-2 (182.6 ± 34.6 μg/g), which still demonstrated robust osteogenic effects. Although no significant difference in new bone volume was observed compared to the control, the BMP-2/BioCaP group showed significantly better performance than the DBBM group, with extensive new bone formation closely resembling native bone. Consistent with prior research,6,33 our results suggest that a more natural bone structure within the socket may enhance future implant osseointegration. Notably, the BMP-2/BioCaP group exhibited uniform bone regeneration in both peripheral and central regions of the socket, unlike the DBBM and BioCaP groups, where central bone formation was limited. Fahmy et al.34 reported that BMP-2-loaded calcium phosphate composites accelerated bone healing and vertical height gain. Our BioCaP scaffold, proven to provide sustained BMP-2 release, addresses the need for controlled delivery to maximise osteogenic potential while minimising adverse effects.7,23,35 Our previous work showed early-stage bone formation in tooth extraction sockets using BioCaP loaded with 126.2 μg/g BMP-2.24 In this study, a slightly higher concentration (182.6 ± 34.6 μg/g) also proved effective in ridge preservation.

This study has several noteworthy limitations that should be taken into account. The healing response in beagle models does not entirely reflect the biological characteristics observed in humans, which may affect the generalisability of the findings. Regarding the observation period, 12 weeks was selected instead of an earlier time point such as 8 weeks, as it allows a more complete evaluation of graft remodelling, new bone maturation, and dimensional stability in the beagle dog extraction socket model.36 At 8 weeks, the healing process may still predominantly reflect early bone formation and ongoing graft substitution, whereas 12 weeks better represents a more mature stage of regeneration. Nevertheless, even at 12 weeks, the relatively limited duration may still be insufficient to determine the long-term stability and safety of alveolar ridge preservation. Another drawback is the absence of sequential monitoring during the early healing stages, which restricts the understanding of the temporal dynamics of tissue repair. Although notable differences were detected in the average values of certain parameters, the limited sample size of large animals likely contributed to considerable variability, reducing the statistical strength of the conclusions. To further validate the preclinical efficacy of this therapeutic strategy, future studies should incorporate larger experimental cohorts and extended observation periods. Moreover, the inclusion of a BMP-2/DBBM combination group would allow direct comparative evaluation with the current BMP-2/BioCaP and DBBM groups, thereby providing deeper insight into the osteogenic performance of BMP-2 delivered through different graft carrier systems.

Conclusions

The use of BMP-2/BioCaP in combination with a collagen matrix effectively minimises ridge resorption after extraction and stimulates substantial bone formation. These findings highlight its potential as a promising osteoinductive bone substitute for alveolar ridge preservation.
