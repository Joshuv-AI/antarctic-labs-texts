Developing and Designing an Evidence‐Informed Serratus Anterior Plane Block Education Program for Emergency Medicine
Authors: Christopher Partyka, Timothy Manion, Daniel Gaetani, Anthony Delaney, Kate Curtis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe aim of this paper is to describe the methods and processes used to develop a theory‐informed, dedicated serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) education and training program for Emergency Medicine clinicians.MethodsThis program was informed by Kern's six‐step approach to curriculum development. Firstly, expert consensus on education and training was developed using a modified Delphi process. Secondly, contemporary medical literature was incorporated with educational pedagogical theoretical frameworks including Bloom's taxonomy of educational objectives, backwards design, and constructive alignment, scaffolded learning, and segmented learning. Finally, these were aligned with existing national policies around regional anaesthesia in the Emergency Department.ResultsThe resultant SAPB evidence‐based education package is supported by the most current evidence for using the SAPB as a regional anaesthesia procedure for patients with rib fracture pain. It serves as an educational tool for clinicians already proficient in the principles of plane blocks with additional guidance on establishing local workshops for practical education as well as ongoing supervision and governance for this procedure.ConclusionsThe development of a theory‐informed, dedicated SAPB education and training program for Emergency Medicine clinicians required a balanced combination of clinical science and procedural expertise with educational theory. This provides a framework for future works when developing educational resources to introduce new clinical procedures into routine Emergency Medicine practice.

Introduction

The serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) is an ultrasound‐guided, regional anaesthesia technique that provides analgesia and anaesthesia to most of the chest wall [1]. It is now being used in Emergency Medicine and trauma care as an analgesic adjunct for rib fracture associated pain, leading to both an early, significant reduction in pain scores and a marked reduction in opiate consumption following a single depot infiltration of local anaesthetic (referred to as a ‘single shot’ block) [2, 3]. This block is feasible and quick to perform in the emergency department setting [4]. As a result, the SAPB is becoming a skill valued by Emergency Medicine clinicians who are seeking to add this to their procedural armamentarium.

As a new procedure to the field of Emergency Medicine, the SAPB is not yet taught in a formal manner and is largely organised by local clinical leads who upskill their colleagues and trainees within their own facility. Therefore, the development of an open‐access, sharable educational program that is both evidence‐based and built from educational theory could provide the solution to rapidly upskilling a large number of Emergency Medicine clinicians in this procedure in a standardised manner.

In medical education, the development of educational resources is frequently guided by Kern's six‐step approach to curriculum development [5]. This framework has been successfully applied across a variety of medical education programs across multiple specialities [6, 7, 8]. In the case of SAPB education, steps one and two had been previously accomplished as growing evidence justifies the use of the SAPB in early rib fracture pain management from the Emergency Department [2, 3]. The aim of this paper is to describe the methods and processes used to develop a theory‐informed, dedicated SAPB education and training program for clinicians in Emergency Medicine.

Part 1: Developing Expert Consensus for Education, Training, and Credentialing of the SAPB Procedure

As a new procedure to the field of Emergency Medicine, the SAPB is not yet taught in a formal manner. Additionally, there is little evidence or universal standards to guide the education, or the credentialing required to define independent practice to undertake this procedure. A modified Delphi study was completed to develop expert consensus in this domain and has previously been published [9]. This study recruited 30 expert clinicians from Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand with backgrounds in Anaesthesia and Emergency Medicine who completed three online rounds using a web‐based questionnaire which was divided into the three key components: (1) theoretical knowledge, (2) training and practical skills, and (3) credentialling and how to assess competency. Consensus was established if ≥ 70% of panellists provided a score of seven or greater on the 9‐point Likert scale for any given statement. This resulted in the generation of 59 consensus statements for the education, training, and credentialing requirements for the use of a ‘single shot’ SAPB in the management of rib fractures [9].

Part 2: Review of Educational Pedagogical Theoretical Frameworks

The second phase of developing this educational training program was to review and incorporate established educational pedagogical theoretical frameworks known to enhance learning outcomes. The known theoretical frameworks were reviewed, as was existing medical education literature. Following this review, consensus was reached by two authors (C.P. and K.C.) and the following frameworks were adapted for SAPB training including Blooms taxonomy of educational objectives, backwards design, constructive alignment, scaffolding, and segmented learning.

Blooms Taxonomy of Educational Objectives

Blooms taxonomy of educational objectives provides a framework for developing the goals and objectives for an educational program. It categorises these objectives into six major cognitive domains including knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation [10]. The intention here is to build a program where learners move through each of these domains taking them from lower to higher order thinking. Additionally, this taxonomy has the additional dimension of layered knowledge types that can be addressed by a learning activity [11]. The four dimensions include factual (terminology and discrete facts); conceptual (theories, principles, and models); procedural (knowledge of a technique, process, or methodology); and metacognitive (including self‐assessment and knowledge of various learning skills and techniques) [12] with learning objectives and lessons being tailored accordingly.

Backwards Design and Constructive Alignment

Backward education design in medicine, also known as ‘backward planning’, is a curriculum development approach that firstly identifies the end‐goal. It prioritises the development of learning outcomes and assessments before selecting the specific academic content and learning activities that fulfil the curriculum itself [13]. The initial task here is to establish the overall goals of the syllabus through the identification of what learners need to understand, and what they need to accomplish.

Constructive alignment is another outcomes‐based approach to teaching [14, 15]. Here, the learning objectives and what students need to achieve are clearly stated with the educational resources and lessons developed with those goals in mind [16].

Scaffolding

In educational terms, scaffolding is an individualised learning approach that provides students with temporary supports whilst they learn new concepts or skills. These supports, which can be instructional, technological, cognitive, or environmental, are gradually reduced over time as the student becomes more skilled or proficient, allowing them to progressively develop independence. This approach allows students to progress their knowledge level to perform complex tasks [17]. The innate complexity of medical education has justified the use of scaffolding to support students in becoming competent health professionals [18].

Segmented Learning

Segmented learning is an educational strategy that divides specific content or processes into several stages or segments. This method focuses on gradually advancing learners by deconstructing larger objectives or skills into smaller, more achievable components. In healthcare and medical education, segmented learning has been demonstrated repeatedly to improve the efficiency of training in a variety of practical and procedural skills [19, 20, 21].

Part 3: Alignment With Existing Policies Around Regional Anaesthesia in the Emergency Department

Within healthcare, especially in the critical care specialities, the credentialing of many core procedures is largely embedded within formal hospital‐based or health‐network Fellowship training programs. It is typically assumed that competency and independence are achieved by the time training is complete. Some core skills such as point of care ultrasound have additional logbook‐based targets as well as formative and summative assessments [22]. Similarly, the fascia iliaca block (for hip and femoral fractures) is a routinely performed procedure in the emergency department and is technically very similar to the SAPB. As established practice, it does have a detailed and accepted training pathway and accreditation process [23, 24].

In addition to this, the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care recommends that new clinical procedures being introduced to a scope of practice have a credentialling committee that seeks out specific centres of excellence for advice on defining standards and minimum competency requirements [25].

These existing procedures and national recommendations were reviewed for guidance and relevance to the current SAPB education program.

Results

The SAPB evidence‐based education package has been completed and is available for widespread, open‐access education and training [26]. This 29‐page resource covers the principles of integrated rib fracture management and regional anaesthesia as well as detailed instructions on how to perform a SAPB. It also provides recommended timetabling for training workshops and the requirements for procedural credentialing. The education package and the combined face‐to‐face workshop were purposefully structured to take participants through asynchronous to synchronous learning with increasing levels of skill complexity and associated feedback. This package and workbook equip emergency medicine clinician participants with requisite knowledge and core concepts to complete the skill acquisition and transition into clinical practice efficiently.

Expert Consensus

The 59 consensus statements developed during the modified Delphi process were all incorporated into the final education package. These were treated as mandatory items and have essentially formed a backbone for which the program was built around. The manner with which each of these statements was incorporated into the education program was influenced by the applied education theory, which is detailed below.

Backwards Design

The crucial component of this project design was to start by defining the outcomes and objectives of the project itself; namely, to upskill emergency medicine clinicians in the SAPB procedure. The minimum required knowledge for this procedure was firstly defined with our modified‐Delphi study reporting what elements of this procedure (and the surrounding knowledge) should be taught. It also allowed the team to highlight what was important or crucial to the training. Additionally, this body of work defined what acceptable independent practice is, and how it can be reproducibly assessed. Evidence‐based content was used to complete the lessons identified in the Delphi, based on contemporary, up to date literature. This process is summarised in Figure 2.

Scaffolding

Scaffolding has been incorporated into the clinical skills phase of this program through staged observation and supervision of their serratus block performance. The training sequence is intentional with this regard; to use direct observation of a clinically indicated SAPB followed by the completion of a clinically indicated SAPB under direct supervision until the participant is deemed capable of independent practice. If further education and adjustment to practice is required, remote supervision and review of cases can take place.

Segmented Learning

It has been recognised that, within the clinical environment of a busy, time‐poor Emergency Department, new procedures such as the SAPB can be seen as complicated with too many steps to achieve. To overcome this, the SAPB education package has broken down the procedure into more manageable, smaller sub‐tasks (Figure 3). Additionally, the layout of the educational document incorporates specifically designed, spaced short videos to reinforce learning.

Alignment With Existing Policies Around Regional Anaesthesia in the Emergency Department

The recommended implementation of the SAPB in emergency medicine practice mirrors that of the fascia iliaca block used for neck of femur fractures. As a result, the recommended local anaesthetic dosing, monitoring & post‐procedural care used in this education package has been adopted from the existing fascial iliaca block guidelines [23] and supported by the New York School Of Regional Anaesthesia recommendations [28].

Discussion

In this paper, the methods and processes used to develop a theory‐informed, dedicated SAPB education and training program for clinicians in Emergency Medicine are reported. The program has taken contemporary scientific evidence and incorporated educational pedagogical theoretical frameworks known to enhance learning outcomes which now serve as a deliverable, open‐access training resource for clinicians and facilities whilst also providing a framework for future works when adopting new clinical procedures into routine medical practice.

The existing literature reporting educational program implementation describes similar themes, especially around the view that theory‐informed approaches are overly prescriptive with frequently long implementation times [27, 29]. Longer implementation times are consistent with our experience designing this package. Whilst patient‐facing clinicians seek rapid change and immediate application to improve outcomes and overall care, this must be balanced against wider considerations such as patient safety, development of appropriate governance systems and supervision networks, as well as allowing other systems to adapt or adopt these changes into the wider multidisciplinary hospital network.

The representation of the educational frameworks described in this paper can appear to be overly linear with learners required to move in a unidirectional path from start to finish, with growing complexity throughout the progression. The pathway detailed in this package specifically needs to be considered as guidance or a set of recommendations, especially for learners who are not completely naïve to the process (e.g., clinicians with some SAPB or regional anaesthesia experience). In such cases the individual needs of a learner must be considered and their placement in the program should be customised.

Education is just one aspect of ensuring successful implementation—clinicians additionally need the capability, opportunity and motivation to enact change (the COM‐B model [30]). This is a behaviour change framework that postulates that for a behaviour to occur, each of these three components must be present. Capability can be both psychological and physical. Psychological capability includes having the necessary knowledge, skills, and decision‐making. Our document and the subsequent training serve to provide clinicians with the necessary knowledge and appropriate skills required through both evidence‐based justification for adopting the SAPB and education‐theory informed methods for learning it. Both physical and social opportunity can also impact implementation. Physical opportunity refers to the external environment that enables a behaviour (e.g., facilities or resources). We provide recommendations for the development of equipment ‘grab bags’ to expedite the collation of required materials to perform a SAPB whilst also demonstrating optimal ways of setting up this equipment at the patients' bedside. It is hoped that through development of agreed accepted practice and the widespread adoption of the SAPB as routine care at a departmental or hospital level that clinicians will also see a perceived value for time spent undertaking this procedure on a busy, time‐poor shift. Additionally, the intrinsic clinician motivations may drive this change. Finally, the recommendation of these changes by medical colleges and other specialty organisations would also serve to lower the barriers which restrict the wider acceptance and implementation of these programs.

For this specific project, with a goal of widespread implementation and training of the SAPB procedure, steps five and six of Kern's approach remain incomplete. These are the focus of future works by the authors with the intention of evaluating both the clinical success and efficacy of the training program, but also studying the barriers and facilitators to the widespread use of the SAPB itself in Emergency Medicine.

Limitations

There are several important limitations which need to be discussed. Firstly, this paper and the accompanying education package have been designed by educators and clinicians with established expertise in the SAPB procedure. SAPB‐naïve clinicians (the learners) were not involved in the development of this program. Secondly, the program is yet to include participant feedback, nor has it been trialled in real‐world clinician education. The intention is to collect and synthesise the clinician experience with future planned research and adapt the package accordingly. Thirdly, there is potential bias in this work through the authors' own interpretation and application of the theoretical frameworks. Whilst additional, independent expert opinion was sought throughout the development process, it does remain an important consideration. Finally, this education package itself represents an evidence‐based, idealistic program for SAPB education and has not been developed to incorporate or consider individual departments, facilities and networks. Each adoption of this package will need to consider unique local requirements and make modifications accordingly during the real‐world implementation.

Conclusion

The development of a theory‐informed, dedicated SAPB education and training program for clinicians in Emergency Medicine required a balanced combination of clinical science and procedural expertise with educational theory. This paper provides a framework for future works when adopting new clinical procedures into routine medical practice. Moving forward, the efficacy and effectiveness of this educational package must be assessed in the real world and adapted according to the feedback from the clinicians who have undertaken the training.
