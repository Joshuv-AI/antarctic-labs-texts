Contemporary use of fibula resection compared to traditional medial approach in peroneal artery bypass
Authors: Elisabeth Brontë Jenkins, Kristy Wrana, Ya-Huei Li, James Gallagher, James Gallagher, Elizabeth Aitcheson, Thomas Divinagracia, Edward Gifford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditionally, approach to the peroneal artery for lower extremity bypass is performed from the medial calf (MED), where the artery's depth may limit access to mid/distal segments. Lateral resection of the fibula (FR) offers an alternate approach, albeit one less often utilized. In this single-center retrospective cohort study of 56 patients undergoing peroneal artery bypass, use of FR (n = 13) demonstrated similar outcomes to MED (n = 43), with 1-year primary patency 45.5% vs 38.5% (P = .736), respectively. Of note, FR was more likely than MED to have higher body mass index (P = .015). Contemporary use of fibula resection is an adjunct for lower extremity bypass.

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects over 200 million people worldwide, and chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) represents its most severe form, occurring in approximately 11% to 20% of patients with known PAD.1 Historically, CLTI carries a high 1-year mortality rate of 25% to 35% and a 1-year major amputation rate of up to 30%.1 Lower extremity bypass surgery is a critical intervention for patients with CLTI, providing durable revascularization that minimizes tissue loss, relieves ischemic pain, and reduces risk of major amputation.1 Randomized trials, such as BEST-CLI, demonstrate that in patients with suitable anatomy and available great saphenous vein (GSV), surgical bypass is associated with lower rates of major adverse limb events and major amputation compared with endovascular therapy.2 Among revascularization options, the peroneal artery is a reliable target for autogenous vein bypass, achieving outcomes comparable to other tibial and pedal arteries.3

Although lower extremity bypass is an essential treatment option for limb preservation, some drawbacks include morbidity from surgical incisions and dissection. These complications are notably higher for more complex and anatomically deep targets, such as the peroneal artery.4 The most common approach to performing a peroneal artery bypass is to access the peroneal artery from the medial calf, where it runs behind the tibia.5 However, this approach requires deep dissection of the tissue, creating procedures that are potentially longer and require larger incisions. A less commonly utilized approach to the peroneal artery, albeit one that has been described since 1977, is via lateral resection of the proximal fibula.6 As the tibia bares a majority of the axial load throughout the lower extremity, fibula resection has minimal impact on functional outcomes and is overall well-handled by patients.7 Advantages to fibula resection include direct access to the artery and performing the anastomosis in a more superficial tissue plane, which may mitigate technical complications and improve graft patency.6 Previous studies have also demonstrated that lateral approaches to popliteal and tibial vessels may have some advantages to the traditional medial approach, particularly for cases of revision surgery.8 However, many prior studies examining this approach predate the contemporary endovascular era, or lack a comparison of fibula resection for peroneal artery bypass to the traditional medial approach.8 Despite its advantages, the lateral approach to the peroneal artery is rarely utilized. Within our institution, multiple surgeons occasionally perform fibula resection for lateral peroneal bypass. Given the varied approach to the peroneal artery, this study aimed to evaluate contemporary outcomes of lateral peroneal bypass with fibular resection compared with the more traditional medial exposure.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study examined patient demographics, preoperative medications and laboratory test results, procedures during admission, perioperative outcomes, discharge disposition, and follow-up outcomes among patients undergoing peroneal artery bypass at a tertiary center from October 2011 through October 2024 based on whether lateral fibula resection (FR) for peroneal artery bypass was performed. Institutional review board approval was granted for this study.

Study population

Adult patients undergoing lower extremity bypass to the peroneal artery for occlusive peripheral arterial or aneurysmal disease were included. Patients who underwent a tibial bypass to a vessel other than the peroneal artery, or those who obtained a lower extremity bypass at a different hospital or outside of the study period, were not included.

Variables and outcomes

Both institutional Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) data and electronic medical records (EMRs) were used to collect preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative information. Preoperative factors included patient demographics, comorbidities, medications, and laboratory tests. Perioperative and postoperative variables included procedure details, immediate outcomes, discharge information, 30-day readmission, and 1-year outcomes from the index intervention. The primary outcome measure of the study was 1-year primary patency (a patent graft with no secondary intervention). Secondary outcomes included primary-assisted patency (a patent graft with stenosis that required revisions to maintain patency), secondary patency (a patent graft but required revision or a new graft for occlusion), and subsequent major amputation. Additionally, the follow-up notes of patients with FR were scrutinized for any reports of complications regarding fibula resection, orthopedic referral, or imaging to document adjacent fracture.

Operative technique

Our technique to lateral peroneal bypass with FR is as follows: Patients who were evaluated for FR typically underwent diagnostic angiogram with possible adjunct computed tomographic angiography (CTA) prior to surgery. This imaging allowed evaluation of the peroneal artery in its mid-portion for suitability as a distal target, with CTA being useful to assess for heavy medial wall calcific burden that may limit clamping. On the day of surgery, the leg was prepped circumferentially, making sure that it could be adequately flexed to allow for manipulation during FR (Fig 1, A). A longitudinal incision approximately 10 to 15 cm in length was made directly overlying the fibula, centered along the mid-portion of its length along the lateral calf. Cautery was used to dissect down to the fibula, taking care in the lateral compartment to avoid injury to the superficial peroneal nerve. Once the fibula was identified, all muscular attachments were then freed using cautery (Fig 1, B). An unprotected bovie tip was curved to be able to release all fibrinous muscular attachments along the lateral, superior, and inferior borders of the fibula. After the fibula was freed on three sides, a large penetrating towel clamp or large kocher clamp was used to grasp the fibula. Using a pneumatic sagittal saw, the fibula was transected superiorly and inferiorly while gently retracting the fibula away from deeper structures (Fig 1, C). The fibrinous muscular attachments were then resected with cautery from the posterior portion of the fibula and the bone was removed. The peroneal artery was sharply dissected in the neurovascular bundle. Statistical analysis

Identified qualified patients were reviewed using the approaches for bypass (FR vs traditional medial approach [MED]) and examined using an independent t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Fisher’s exact test, or Pearson χ2 test, whichever fit the best. A multivariate logistic regression of FR was developed by including potential predictors (P ≤ .1 in univariate analysis). The odds ratio (OR) of each predictor with a known reference was reported along with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Kaplan-Meier curves presented probabilities of events (graft revision, major amputation, and occlusion) occurring over time by interventions and tested with the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test. All hypotheses were tested with a two-sided alpha level of .05, and statistical results with a P-value < .05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis procedures were carried out in SPSS version 29 (Released 2023. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows).

Results

From October 1, 2011, to October 31, 2024, a total of 56 patients were identified, comprising 13 FR (30.2%) and 43 MED (76.8%). Indication for procedure was CLTI in 100% of FR patients and 74.4% of MED patients (P = .118). Popliteal aneurysms were present in 12.5% of patients (0% FR vs 16.3% MED; P = .182). Operative details, in-hospital complications, and outcomes at follow-up were similar between groups (Table III). Procedure time (mean, 315; standard deviation [SD], 65 vs 292; SD, 88 minutes; P = .383), estimated blood loss (300; interquartile range [IQR], 275-400 vs 300; IQR, 200-500 mL; P = .868), and return to operating rooms (15.4% vs 23.8%; P = .709) did not significantly differ. Patients with FR were less likely to receive P2Y12 inhibitors at discharge compared with patients with MED (7.7% vs 56.1%; P = .002). Discussion

In this retrospective study of 56 patients at a tertiary institution undergoing peroneal artery bypass utilizing either the common MED approach or the less common FR technique, the researchers found relatively equivalent operative details, rates of perioperative complications, and limb-related outcomes at 1 year. In addition, with the exception of one patient that reported pain at the site of fibula resection, there were no obvious orthopedic consequences to fibula resection. As expected for a complex lower extremity revascularization, the majority of the patients presented with CLTI. There were a few differences in patients who underwent FR, including a higher BMI in this cohort and higher rate of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), even though they were nonsmokers at the time of bypass. Despite need for bone resection, FR did not add to either operative time or blood loss. The only noteworthy difference at discharge in the FR cohort was lower utilization of a P2Y12 inhibitor such as clopidogrel. As expected with this less utilized approach, the study sample size with FR likely impacted our ability to make any significant determinations with regard to patency and subsequent major amputation, but the primary-assisted patency was >90% following FR at 1 year.

The previous available literature on FR is limited to small case series and technical reports, and they do not make a direct comparison to MED, as done in this study. In a study comparing surgical peroneal artery bypass with endovascular intervention, the 1-year primary patency rate was 47.9% in bypass patients with chronic limb ischemia and isolated peroneal runoff, which is similar to our results with improved 1-year primary-assisted patency.4 In the aforementioned study, the incidence of major amputation was 18.5% at 1 year for bypass patients, similar to our overall rate of 22.9%.4 As expected, patients undergoing bypass to the peroneal artery compared with endovascular peroneal intervention had a higher rate of incisional complications (13% vs 4.4%; P = .008).4 Additional studies have documented similar limb salvage rates with peroneal artery bypass, around 79% to 81%.9,10 The majority of studies of peroneal artery bypass do not indicate the operative approach, or when specified, highlight the rarity of FR. In a series of 159 bypasses, only two utilized a lateral approach.11 In fact, among a series of only lateral leg bypasses, where all bypasses were approached through an unconventional lateral approach, only three of the bypasses were lateral peroneal bypasses with FR.8

Although FR is not frequently utilized, certain scenarios where it may be favorable to MED include obesity, prior medial leg interventions, or infection or dehiscence of a previous incision, as well as difficulty in accessing the peroneal artery at a medial portion of the vessel. Our results found that BMI in patients with FR was higher than that of patients with MED, possibly indicating the role that body habitus, obesity, and vessel depth have on choosing an optimal exposure. Additionally, BMI was a significant predictor of FR, with an increased BMI being associated with a higher likelihood of FR. It has previously been shown that MED can be technically challenging in patients with high BMI due to increased adipose tissue, deeper vessel location, and more difficult exposure, which may increase operative time and risk of wound complications.12 Furthermore, high BMI is associated with increased rates of surgical site infection and respiratory complications after lower extremity bypass.12

One concern regarding the use of FR is the potential impact on long-term gait or adjacent orthopedic complications. In one of the largest series of lateral peroneal bypasses (18 patients), two patients had subsequent tibia fracture on long-term follow-up.7 Both patients were noted to be elderly women with osteoporosis, which may have contributed to their tibial fractures.7 In our study, one of 13 FR patients reported persistent localized pain, but no major orthopedic complications were observed, although the median follow-up in the study was 815 days (IQR, 393-1678 days). There have also been two studies that have proposed methods for lateral or latero-anterior access to the peroneal artery without the need for FR.13,14 These approaches may serve as useful adjuncts in cases where there is concern for postoperative orthopedic complications.

We found that patients who underwent FR were less likely to receive discharge P2Y12 inhibitors compared with those who underwent MED (P = .002). The researchers attribute this finding to a clustering of FR cases with two surgeons who less often utilize P2Y12 inhibitors after lower extremity bypass. A multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 851 patients undergoing below-knee bypass found that the combination of clopidogrel (P2Y12 inhibitor) and aspirin did not improve outcomes in patients with PAD after infrainguinal bypass.15 Similarly, a retrospective cohort study of 1812 Medicare-linked patients undergoing infrapopliteal bypass for CLTI compared discharge prescription of aspirin or clopidogrel, aspirin and clopidogrel, and anticoagulation plus any antiplatelet.16 Their study found that use of aspirin and clopidogrel together did not have improved outcomes compared with aspirin or clopidogrel alone.16

Although our study demonstrated equivalent perioperative and 1-year outcomes between FR and MED for peroneal artery bypass, it possessed significant limitations. These include limited information regarding why FR was chosen as the initial bypass strategy. Approximately two-thirds of the FR cases were performed prior to adoption of our current EMR. Although we have access to some clinic notes and the operative reports, not all clinical decision-making both pre- and postoperatively is available. Additionally, due to reliance on manual chart review and the lack of uniform completion angiogram studies, we were unable to definitively determine which segment of the peroneal artery was used as the bypass target. Consequently, target site could not be included as a variable in the analysis comparing FR vs MED. Another limitation is that this study did not interrogate whether peripheral vascular intervention (PVI) or prior bypass was conducted to a more proximal tibial target, which would serve as a meaningful point of comparison between the FR and MED group. This study is also constrained by the small sample size, which may limit the statistical power or generalizability of our findings. A multi-institutional study would potentially provide a larger sample, allowing for a more robust comparison between the FR and MED approaches with regard to preoperative patient factors and postoperative outcomes.

Conclusions

This study reviewed perioperative and long-term outcomes of patients undergoing FR vs MED for peroneal artery bypass. The results of the study showed that contemporary use of FR displays equivalent perioperative and 1-year outcomes compared with MED and therefore, can serve as a useful adjunct for lower extremity bypass. FR may be particularly helpful in patients with larger BMI, where exposure of the peroneal artery through a medial approach results in a more difficult exposure of the artery and more wound to heal.
