Characterization of novel cofactorless self-cleaving RNAs with activity at low pH
Authors: Nae Sakimoto, Emily Matsusaka, Natsuki Doi, Tell Biwata, Taku Ishigaki, Tomoka Akita, Elisa Tomita-Sudo, Shigenori Iwai, Junji Kawakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Several diseases abnormally lower cellular pH owing to metabolic pathway changes. Developing drugs based on genetic material that act only in target tissues may reduce side effects by exploiting the differences between diseased and normal tissue environments. Most naturally occurring ribozymes—ribonucleic acids with catalytic activity—are active at a neutral pH and inactive at lower pH. Ribozymes that cleave RNA only in acidic solutions require a general acid with a sufficiently low pKa and a general base with a pKa closer to the acidic region. As the rational design of such a ribozyme is difficult, we performed an in vitro assay for the selection of self-cleaving RNAs under acidic conditions. We attempted to develop a self-cleaving RNA that only acts in acidic solutions without requiring divalent metal cations, as the activity of natural ribozymes is affected by the presence or absence of these ions. To obtain RNA molecules with high activity under any conditions, in vitro selection was performed in the absence of divalent metal ions and cofactors. Using this method, we obtained a new functional RNA with self-cleavage activity only at an acidic pH. This RNA also exhibited high activity in the absence of divalent metal ions. This study derived a novel functional RNA. The obtained RNAs were shortened to a 53-nucleotide RNA with the original properties, and the sequence required for the catalytic activity was identified.

Introduction

Ribozymes (Rzs) are ribonucleic acids with catalytic activity. The cleavage mechanism of RNA-cleaving Rzs is a general base-acid catalysis;1–15 the 2′-OH of the cleavage nucleoside is deprotonated by a general base, followed by a nucleophilic attack on the phosphate. The general acid protonates the resulting 5′-OH via a trigonal bipyramidal transition state. Following the cleavage reaction, RNA fragments with a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and a 5′-hydroxyl group are generated.15–18 The combinations of general base and general acid employed by RNA-cleaving Rzs derived from self-cleaving RNAs can be broadly classified into three categories: G + M, G + A, and M + C. In this notation, the general base is provided either by a G or by a metal ion–derived species, whereas the general acid is contributed by a nucleotide (A or C) or by a metal ion–coordinated component.19–21 The general bases for Hepatitis delta virus (HDV)-like Rz,22 hammerhead Rz (HHRz),23,24 and hairpin Rz25 are a Mg-coordinated water molecule, G, and G, while the general acids are C, metal-coordinated O2′ of guanosine, and A, respectively.26–28 For the cleavage activity of Rzs, the protonation and deprotonation of the catalytic residues are critical.19,29 The proton concentration increases by a factor of 10 for each 1-unit decrease in pH; therefore, the deprotonated active form of a general base decreases, while the protonated active form of a general acid increases. The pH profile of a ribozyme depends on the pKa values of its general base and general acid, and as a result, it often follows a bell-shaped curve.16,30 Most naturally occurring Rzs are active near neutral pH and inactive at low pH (Fig. 1).1–9,16,30 As one of the key factors in ribozymes′ catalytic reactions, divalent metal ions also play a vital role. One catalytic role of divalent metal ions is to facilitate proton transfer through coordination to proton donors or acceptors. Some other Rzs do not use divalent metal ions as catalysts in their reactions but instead use them as stabilizers for their active structures. For example, Varkud Satellite and Twister Rz interact with divalent metal ions, which stabilize the active site, suppress electrostatic repulsion between phosphate groups, and stabilize higher-order and transition structures, thereby promoting cleavage reactions.31–34 Thus, the role of divalent metal ions in ribozymes includes stabilizing the active site and directly influencing the chemical steps of cleavage by altering the pKa of substrates. Therefore, divalent metal ions, such as Mg2+, or high concentrations of monovalent metal ions are required to induce Rzs activity. Additionally, the activities of Rzs vary significantly with metal ion type and concentration.1,35 As the types and concentrations of metal ions within cells are not necessarily optimal for ribozyme reactions, functional nucleic acids with high activity, without metal ions, are desired for use as biological tools to control gene expression.

When considering practical applications of functional nucleic acids, such as ribozymes, ligand-gated or pH-responsive activation strategies are particularly suitable for environment-dependent systems that are either physiologically or pathologically specific—for example, the Warburg effect in the tumor microenvironment, where glycolysis predominates and the extracellular pH decreases to 6.3–6.8.36,37 Alternatively, such strategies can be applied in spatiotemporally restricted contexts, such as intracellular compartments, including endosomes (pH 5.5–6.5) or lysosomes (pH 4.5–5.5).38,39

In this study, we aimed to obtain functional nucleic acids with cleavage activity that remain active at low pH, regardless of the presence or absence of metal ions and cofactors. In our previous study,40 we obtained a self-cleaving functional RNA with histidine dependency. We concluded that histidine, lacking the ability to alter pKa as significantly as divalent metal ions, functions not as a catalytic residue during the chemical step but rather in inducing the formation of the active conformation. This suggests that the functional nucleic acids that express activity via histidine in the absence of divalent metal ions likely use their own bases as catalytic residues during the chemical step. Furthermore, they exhibit activity at acidic pH. Therefore, we expected that the desired RNA molecule would be obtained as derivatives of the histidine-dependent ribozymes.40 First, we prepared a library derived from the sequence that was found to be active under acidic conditions in our previous study.40 In the selection process, we isolated RNA that exhibits self-cleavage under acidic conditions (pH 5.0) in the absence of divalent metal ions. We excluded molecules that exhibit cleavage activity at near-neutral pH and in the presence of divalent metal ions as a negative selection. After obtaining the desired RNA molecules, we shortened their sequences to identify the core sequence responsible for the cleavage reaction. Finally, we determined the predicted pKa of the shortened RNA from the pH activity profile and assumed the active secondary structure.

Experimental

In vitro selection

Under the selection conditions, molecules exhibiting cleavage activity in transcription reactions (pH 7.0) were excluded, while molecules undergoing cleavage only under acidic conditions containing monovalent metal ions were acquired. This enabled the acquisition of molecules possessing activity under acidic conditions, regardless of the presence of metal ions. To obtain functional nucleic acids with activity at low pH, in vitro selection was performed using an RNA library containing a random region of 70 bases constructed in our laboratory. The RNA library contained a T7 promoter sequence at the 5′ terminus and a primer extension at the 3′ terminus (Fig. S1a). The following cycle was repeated for the selection: (1) cDNA generation from the RNA cleavage products by reverse transcription, (2) amplification of the cDNA by PCR, (3) synthesis of RNA by transcription, (4) recovery of the uncleaved RNA (negative selection), (5) self-cleavage reaction at low pH (pH 5.0), and (6) recovery of cleavage products (Fig. S1b). The PCR products were transcribed using the T7-Scribe Standard RNA IVT Kit (CELLSCRIPT, Madison, USA) to synthesize the RNA. Following RNA purification, an RNA self-cleavage reaction was performed using a selection buffer, and the cleavage products were separated using 6% denaturing PAGE (Fig. S1b and c).

RNA samples for electrophoresis were mixed with a 2 × formamide + dye mixture (99% formamide, 0.1% xylene cyanol, 0.1% bromophenol blue, and 1 mM EDTA), heated at 94 °C for 2 min, quenched, and dispensed into the appropriate wells. The recovered RNA was reverse-transcribed using SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., USA) to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). DNA was amplified using PCR (KOD-plus; Toyobo Co., Ltd, Osaka, Japan). The RNA band was excised after transcription or selection, and the gel was illuminated under UV light to confirm its position. To extract RNA from the gel, 500 µL RNA extraction buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, and 0.005% SDS) was added and thoroughly mixed using a vortex mixer to facilitate elution. RNA was obtained from the supernatant using the ethanol precipitation method. After drying, the resulting RNA pellet was dissolved in 20 µL RNase/DNase-free water (NIPPON GENE Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). The selection conditions are listed in Table S1.

The construction of the RNA library was based on the selection of the histidine-dependent RNA molecules from our previous study.39 Functional RNAs were active at pH 5.0; therefore, we aimed to determine whether the histidine specificity could be eliminated. To enrich more active RNA molecules, the concentration of histidine in the selection buffer was reduced in successive selection experiments, and the reaction time was gradually shortened. As transcription was performed under neutral conditions (pH 7.0), RNA molecules active at neutral pH and in the presence of divalent metal ions were eliminated. Therefore, transcription was employed as a negative selection method to obtain RNA molecules active in the absence of divalent metal ions.

Cloning and sequencing

Cloning was performed using the TOPO TA Cloning Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K.K., Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, the PCR product of G5 obtained by selection (9 µL) was mixed with 1 µL 10 × A attachment mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd, Osaka, Japan) and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. The dA-tailed PCR product was integrated into the TOPO vector by incubating the sample at 25 °C for 30 min to create a plasmid for cloning. The constructs with the inserts of the DNA fragments were mixed with competent cells (TOP10; One Shot ®INVαF′ Chemically Competent E. coli; Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., Tokyo, Japan) and incubated as follows: 5 min on ice, at 42 °C for 30 s, and at 0 °C for 5 min. Following incubation, 250 µL SOC medium was added, and the sample was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Thereafter, 50 µL of the transfected competent cell solution was transferred onto LB plates (1% w/v Bacto™ Tryptone, 0.5% w/v Bacto™ Yeast Extract, 0.5% w/v NaCl, and 1.5% w/v Bacto™ Agar) and incubated at 37 °C. Single colonies were picked up and cultured in the LB liquid medium containing 50 µg mL−1 ampicillin. Plasmid DNA was recovered from cultured E. coli by the alkaline mini-prep method. The recovered plasmids were sequenced at FASMAC Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan).

Truncation of the functional RNAs

After the selection cycles, the rate of mutation introduction at each base in the random region was determined by calculating the variation index (V.I.).41,42 A higher V.I. value reveals the introduction of more mutations into the RNA base, indicating that the sequence is likely not important for the catalytic activity. Using the V.I. values, the obtained RNAs could be shortened to reveal the active core sequence without losing the catalytic activity. The V.I. was calculated using the following equation:5V.I. of the RNA base = mutation rate of each base in the clone/initial mutation introduction rateUsing eqn (5), we compared the sequences of the 27 RNA molecules obtained and calculated the mutation rate at each base in the clone. If the original base in this library was C, a sequence should contain 85% C, 5% A, 5% T, and 5% G in the calculation. Therefore, a 15% mutation was introduced at each of the 70 bases in the random region as the initial ratio of the mutation introduction.

RNA synthesis and purification

RNA synthesis for LC-MS analysis was performed by synthesizing double-stranded DNA as the template, rather than using a vector as the template. The primers used are shown in Table S2. To the annealing solution, SuperScript™ III reverse transcriptase (200 U) was added and subjected to a reaction at 37 °C for 120 min to synthesize 139-bp pre-template DNA. This is not a reverse-transcription reaction but an unusual application of reverse transcriptase to prepare dsDNA. Using a 25 × diluted 139-bp pre-template DNA solution as a template, 156-bp DNA was synthesized by PCR using T7 plus primer (5′- TAATACGACTCACTATAGGAAATAAACTGCGACGACCC-3′) and reverse primer (minus 15). The 40 µM RNA solution (10 µL) was subjected to a reaction at 25 °C for 33 h in 2 × His (+) buffer. After the reaction, full-length RNA and cleaved RNA fragments were desalted by ten ZipTip pipette tips, according to the manufacturer‘s protocol (Merck, Germany). Then, 100 µL water was added to the eluate (100 µL 50% aqueous acetonitrile), and the solution was freeze-dried. The freeze-dried samples were dissolved in 40 µL of water.

Chromatography

A SCIEX ExionLC system (SCIEX, USA) was used with 10 mM triethylamine (TEA) and 100 mM hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP) aqueous solution as mobile phase A, and 10 mM TEA and 100 mM HFIP aqueous solution in MeOH (1 : 1 v/v) as mobile phase B. A gradient of 25–35% B for 22 min with a 2 min wash step at 90% B was used at a flow rate of 0.2 mL min−1. The column was an ACQUITY Premier Oligonucleotide BEH C18 (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 µm, 130 Å) (Waters Corporation, USA) kept at 80 °C. The injection volume was 1 µL.

Mass spectrometry

A SCIEX Triplet TOF 6600+ system (SCIEX, USA) was used in negative polarity mode. The mass parameters were 500–3000 m/z for the mass range, 450 °C for temperature, and −4500 V for ion spray voltage floating. The analysis was performed using SCIEX OS analysis software.

Point mutation assays

For introducing single-nucleotide mutations into RNAs, a plus primer containing the commonly available T7 promoter (T7 plus 52) and a minus primer introducing a single-nucleotide mutation were synthesized (Table S3). These primers have a 7-base double-strand formation site. The double-stranded DNA was synthesized by Super Script™ III reverse transcriptase. The plus and minus primers (100 pmol each) were added, and the reaction mixture without the enzyme was prepared and subjected to annealing (90 °C × 1 min, 60 °C × 1 min, 37 °C × 3 min, then held at 4 °C). Reverse transcriptase (1 µL) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 50 min. The RNAs with single-nucleotide mutations were transcribed onto a template of the synthesized double-stranded DNA.

Results

Sequence and activity analyses of the clones obtained by the selection

The cleavage activity of the library was analyzed, and the resulting RNA sequences were classified into groups based on sequence similarity to evaluate their catalytic activity. The RNA library (139 nt) was designed to have a central region (70 bases) composed of random nucleotides, which contained an equal probability of each of the four nucleotides at each position. This RNA library was subjected to five cycles of in vitro selection. These were then analyzed using 6% denaturing PAGE with 7 M urea, which showed that G5 exhibited high self-cleavage activity (Fig. 2). Highly active RNAs were obtained by shortening the reaction time in each cycle (Table S1). Cloning yielded 27 clones, the sequences of which are shown in Table S4. Lower- and upper-case bases indicate the primer regions and random sequences of 70 bases, respectively. Bases shown in red in the 70-base random sequence region indicate the mutated bases. The pH dependence of the self-cleavage reaction was evaluated for all the clones. The clones were incubated for 2 h at 25 °C in reaction solutions of pH 4.5, 5.0, and 7.0 (Fig. 3a). The cloned RNAs exhibited a low activity at neutral pH (pH 7.0) and a high self-cleavage activity at low pH values (pH 4.5 and 5.0). Notably, the reaction mixtures contained no divalent metal ions or cofactors. Therefore, we successfully obtained functional nucleic acids with a self-cleavage activity under low pH conditions without divalent metal ions. The sequences of the obtained RNA molecules with cleavage rates >20% are listed in Table S5. A comparison of the sequence similarities of these molecules revealed that they could be classified into six groups: group 1: N24, N26, N27, N36, N41, and N44; group 2: N12, N29, and N47; group 3: N28; group 4: N34; group 5: N37; and group 6: N39. For each group, N24, N28, N29, N34, N37, and N39 were used as representative samples to determine the reaction rate constants at pH 4.5. The rate constants were determined by curve fitting of the data shown in Fig. 3b, and the obtained values are listed in Table 1.

Analysis of N29 activity in the presence of divalent and monovalent ions

Because divalent metal ions, particularly magnesium ions, enhance the catalytic activity of HHRz, cleavage activity was examined at different pH values in the presence of 10 mM Mg2+ and at high concentrations of monovalent metal ions. To probe metal ion-dependent contributions, the N29 variant with relatively low intrinsic activity was selected to enhance sensitivity to both structural and catalytic effects. Cleavage reactions were performed across a range of pH values with or without 10 mM Mg2+.

As shown in Fig. 3c, the cleavage yield of N29 was the highest at pH 4.5 in the presence of 10 mM Mg2+. The pH profile of the reaction in the absence of the divalent metal ion (250 mM K+) was very similar to that in the presence of Mg2+, suggesting that the underlying chemical step is not substantially altered by Mg2+. However, the amount of the cleaved product was greater in the presence of Mg2+. Although it cannot be completely ruled out that divalent metal ions are involved as catalytic residues in catalytic reactions, these results indicate that the Mg2+ ion probably stabilizes the active structure required for the self-cleavage reaction.

To further evaluate the role of metal ions, the rate constants for the reaction in the presence of different divalent metal ions were determined at pH 4.5 (Fig. 3d). The acceleration effect that divalent metal ions have on natural Rzs was observed (Table S6). Acceleration effects of N29 were observed even with the addition of 250 mM Li+ (Fig. 3e and Table S7), suggesting that higher ionic strength leads to faster reaction. The ionic radii of the metal ions (K+, Li+, Mg2+, Mn2+, and Ca2+) are 1.37, 0.74, 0.82, 0.96, and 1.18 Å, respectively,43 and the rate constant of HHRz had a log-linear relationship with the ionic radii.35 However, as shown in Fig. S2, unlike HHRz, N29 did not exhibit a clear dependence of activity on ionic radius.

Determination of V.I. for shortening functional RNAs

The enhanced activity in the presence of metal ions, regardless of ion radius, is consistent with the possibility that formation of the active structure is the rate-limiting step. The chain length of the obtained RNA was 139 nt; thus, it is difficult to determine the catalytic residues or elucidate the reaction mechanism. In addition, shorter chain lengths are preferred for the biotechnological applications of this functional RNA. Therefore, the shortest core sequence with the catalytic reaction was determined. The sequences of the 27 RNA molecules were compared, and the mutation rate at each base was evaluated using V.I. (eqn (5)). As each of the 70 bases in the random region of the library had a 15% mutation rate, the V.I. was calculated using 15% as the initial mutation introduction rate (Fig. 4a). A smaller V.I. indicates a highly conserved and important base, and a V.I. ≥ 1 indicates that the bases are highly variable (i.e., of low importance).

Secondary structure prediction was performed using mfold,44 and the merged results with V.I. values are shown in Fig. S3a. Similarities were observed in the predicted secondary structures of the obtained molecules, with three main types being predicted. The stem-loop region containing highly-conserved bases was isolated (red square in Fig. S3b), and the resulting RNA was further truncated (Fig. S3c). Since N29-53 showed the highest activity, it was determined that 53 bases are the optimal length.

Other molecules were also shortened to 53 bases. Shortening was performed using six samples: N24, N28, N29, N34, N37, and N39. The RNA molecules shortened to 53 nt according to the V.I. results are denoted as N24-53, N28-53, N29-53, N34-53, N37-53, and N39-53. At pH 4.5, these RNAs were incubated for 2 h at 25 °C (Fig. S4). Consequently, four molecules, N28-53, N29-53, N34-53, and N39-53, retained the activity after the shortening. The sequences of the active molecules are listed in Table S8. Sequence comparison revealed similarities between N28-53 and N39-53 and between N29-53 and N34-53. These sequences are thought to represent the core sequence required for the activity at low pH. Moreover, the rate constants of the truncated RNAs were determined at pH 4.5 (Table S9). Compared with the rate constants obtained for the original 139-nt RNAs (Table 1), the values increased for N29 and N34 after truncation. In contrast, the kobs values of N28-53 and N39-53 were smaller than those of N28 and N39. In all cases, the change in the rate constant was within one order of magnitude.

Identification of the cleavage site by LC-MS

To determine whether the cleavage mechanism differs between the 139- and 53-nt RNAs, we performed mass spectrometric analysis of the cleavage products. In the mass spectra, high intensities of tetravalent and divalent ions were observed for the short products from the 139-nt and 53-nt RNAs, respectively. Therefore, the mass spectra of these ions were used to identify the products. The obtained monoisotopic-ion masses for the short products from the 139-nt and 53-nt RNAs were 2574.3 and 1118.6 Da, respectively (Fig. S5 and S6). Based on the results in our previous study using the same library,40 the 5′-end products obtained from 139-nt and 53-nt RNA were presumed to be 5′-TP-GGAAUAAACUGCGACGACCCGCUUGGAGUA-CP-3′ and 5′-TP-GGAAUA-CP-3′ (TP, triphosphate; CP, cyclic phosphate), respectively. The theoretical masses calculated for the corresponding ions of these RNA fragments are 2575.31 and 1118.58 Da, which agreed well with the measured values. This analysis confirmed that the cleavage site remained unchanged upon truncation, indicating that the active site architecture should be preserved. Furthermore, characterization of the product termini provided insights into the underlying cleavage mechanism.

Determination of the pKa values of the truncated RNAs

To confirm that the properties of the functional RNAs were not altered by the shortening, the pH dependence of the activity for the shortened RNAs, N28-53, N29-53, N34-53, and N39-53, was measured in the same manner as 139-nt RNA. Two pairs of RNAs—N28-53 and N39-53, and N29-53 and N34-53—were similar in sequence and had similar pH-dependent activities; N29-53 and N34-53 became active at pH 3.5, reached maximum activity at pH 4.5, and lost most of their activity at pH 5.5. In contrast, N28-53 and N39-53 exhibited almost constant activity in the pH range of 3.5 to 4.5, decreased activity at pH 5.0, and no activity at pH 5.5. As shown above, these RNAs retained their pH dependency after shortening. Using N39-53—which was highly active even at the acidic pH of 3.5—the rate constants at each pH were determined to obtain the pH profile. The rate constants of the N39-53 reaction were 0.06, 0.04, 0.03, and 0.02 min−1 at pH 3.5, 4.0, 4.5, and 5.0, respectively (Fig. 5 and Table S10). The curve-fitting results revealed that the estimated pKa of the residues important for the activity of N39-53 was 4.1. Figure S7 shows a comparison of the pH profile of N39-53 obtained in this study with those of the naturally occurring Rzs. We successfully obtained the core sequence of a functional nucleic acid with high activity only in the low pH region, which does not exist in natural Rzs.

Identification of the essential bases by point mutation assays

To conduct a more in-depth investigation of the activity, we performed point mutation assays to identify the bases essential for the activity. The pKa values of the nucleobases are as follows: 3.5, adenosine; 1.6 and 9.2, guanosine; 9.2, uridine; and 4.2, cytidine.45–48 As there was a high possibility that a cytosine base is involved in the catalytic reaction from the results in Fig. 5, we hypothesized that, to clarify the results, the introduction of a single-nucleotide mutation into N39-53—which contained continuous cytosine residues—should be avoided. Therefore, we performed single-nucleotide mutagenesis of N34-53 to identify the essential bases. The primers used for this experiment are listed in Table S3. The cleavage activities of RNAs with single-nucleotide mutations were evaluated by determining the cleaved fraction after 30 min of reaction at pH 4.5 and 7.0 (Fig. S8). The sample name C30A indicates that the 30th base was converted from the original C to A. A41A is a control sequence that is the same as unmutated N34-53. No activity was observed at pH 7.0 for any mutant. Single-nucleotide mutations rendered some sequences inactive at pH 4.5. Several of the identified bases may play a key role in the activity; the 32nd, 33rd, 34th, and 36th bases significantly reduced or almost completely lost the RNA self-cleavage activity when they were replaced with any other base. These bases are most important for the activity of the functional nucleic acids obtained in this study. For the other bases, the 31st and 39th were significantly active with the C and U bases, respectively. It is thus important that the 31st and 39th are pyrimidine bases; the 41st base remained highly active when this A was changed to G, implying that this position prefers a purine base. The 40th base maintained the cleavage activity with all bases.

Discussion

Nucleic acid therapies leverage genetic information from DNA and RNA, can target genetic information such as mRNA, and provide an alternative and versatile strategy for curing diseases.49–52 Recently, modified nucleic acids, such as locked nucleic acids, have been synthesized.53,54 Antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) that use these synthetic nucleic acids induce enzyme tolerance and exhibit high binding ability to the target mRNA.55,56 However, current antisense nucleic acids face difficulties in distinguishing mRNA produced in target tissues from that produced in normal tissues. ASOs target disease-causing genes, which, unlike regular genes, contain mutations. To suppress the expression of a gene that is commonly expressed in normal and target tissues without any mutations, the characteristic features should be utilized. For example, diseases such as cancer induce a lower cellular pH than that of normal cells, as their metabolic pathways differ from those of normal cells.38,39 We hypothesized that nucleic acid-based therapies that only function in the target tissues and cause fewer side effects than other treatments, such as chemotherapy, could be developed by exploiting the environmental differences between diseased and normal tissues. To recognize only the target genes and control their expression levels, we focused on Rzs with a specific cleavage activity.

Developing nucleic acid medicines that can discriminate between different tissue environments could facilitate drug discovery with fewer side effects. In this study, we focused on differences in pH and sought to obtain novel functional nucleic acids with cleavage activity at low pH. However, ribozymes exhibit the same pH profile activity (Fig. 1). Naturally occurring Rzs are active near neutral pH and require divalent metal ions. Therefore, we obtained a new functional RNA that does not require divalent metal ions for self-cleavage and is active only in the acidic pH range through in vitro selection.

The obtained functional RNA, consisting of 139 nt, was inactive at pH 7.0 but active in the acidic region below pH 5.0 (Fig. 3a). Based on sequence similarity, the sequences could be divided into six groups. Among these, the sequences in group 1 exhibited high cleavage activity and high-rate constants.

The role of divalent metal ions was investigated using the less reactive N29 to sensitively assess their effects on both structure and catalysis. Both the product yield and reaction rate of N29 increased in the presence of divalent metal ions (Fig. 3c and d). The increase in the rate constant in the presence of divalent metal ions might suggest that these ions are also involved in the chemical steps of the catalytic reaction. However, N29 exhibited enhanced activity even in the presence of 250 mM K+ + 250 mM Li+ (Fig. 3e), and the pH profile did not change in the presence or absence of 10 mM Mg2+ (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, no correlation was observed between N29 activity and ionic radius. This observation differs from the behavior reported for HHRz35 and therefore does not provide positive evidence for an inner-sphere catalytic role of metal ions in N29. Instead, in this system, the metal ion is likely to contribute primarily through electrostatic stabilization or facilitation of proper folding. Although the precise role of metal ions in the N29 reaction remains unclear and a contribution to catalysis cannot be excluded, the lack of ionic radius dependence in the rate constant argues against a primary role for metal ions as general acid/base catalysts. Notably, the RNA constructs characterized here retain catalytic activity in the absence of divalent metal ions, demonstrating an intrinsic capacity for self-cleavage without external cofactors.

In previous studies, DNAzymes41,42,57,58 and Rzs,59 which cleave RNA in the low-pH range, have been obtained using in vitro selection methods. In the case of these Rzs,59 divalent metal ions, spermine, and high concentrations of monovalent metal ions inhibit the activity. In contrast, the newly obtained Rzs in this study exhibited cleavage activity at low pH, and their activity was not inhibited by divalent metal ions in the reaction mixture. The functional RNA obtained through this process is likely to interact with cations and other cationic molecules within the cell, thereby increasing its activity. Although it is presumed that the pH range in which it exhibits activity will remain unchanged, this possibility requires further investigation.

The obtained RNA was truncated to identify the catalytic core sequence (Fig. 4, Fig. S3). Sequences considered to have little relevance to the catalytic reaction by V.I. were eliminated and shortened to 53 nts. Among the six sequences tested, four shortened RNAs exhibited self-cleavage activity (Fig. 4b–e and Fig. S4). The pH profile of N39-53 was analyzed by determining kobs at each pH, yielding an estimated pKa (N39-53) of 4.1 (Fig. 5). The pKas of the conjugate acids of guanosine, adenosine, and cytidine are 3.2–3.3, 3.5–3.9, and 4.0–4.6, respectively.45–48 Cytosine is important for the cleavage activity: the bases in the cytosine-rich region (30th to 41st) away from the cleavage site, which are highly conserved, are considered important for the cleavage reaction or forming active folding. However, the position of the catalytic residue remains unclear. Therefore, we identified the bases required for this activity using single-nucleotide mutagenesis of N34-53 (Fig. S8). By evaluating the N34-53 mutants, U32, C33, A34, and C36 were identified as essential bases. We re-predicted the secondary structure of the truncated N34-53 fragment using mfold.44 We incorporated V.I. values into the predicted structure and reflected the results of the single-nucleotide mutagenesis (Fig. 6a). The V.I. values and the results of single-nucleotide mutagenesis did not fully agree. For example, while the 32nd base had a large V.I. value (Fig. 4a), the activity was lost when it was replaced with any base other than U (Fig. S8a). On the other hand, the 37th base was conserved in terms of the V.I. value, but it retained activity regardless of the base introduced in the single-nucleotide mutagenesis. The results of the two analyses were consistent at position 33. The C base at this position was highly conserved, and this C was the only base that retained activity.

The results in Fig. 5 suggest that the cytosine base is important for activity. In the sequence of N34-53, the 33rd and 36th bases must be C, and the 31st and 39th bases should be pyrimidine, as shown in Fig. 6b. N29-53, which has a similar sequence to N34-53, contains all of the important bases. N28-53, N29-53, N34-53, and N39-53 share the common feature (Table S8). Although further analysis is required, it appears to play a key role in the formation of the active structure or in the chemical steps of the catalytic reaction.

The spatial distance between the cleavage site and the essential base in the secondary structure suggests that the essential bases may approach the cleavage site and form an active structure through loop–loop interactions, which are often observed in the formation of higher-order RNA structures. Therefore, we examined potential regions of continuous base pairing between the important sequence identified in this study and the surrounding sequence at the cleavage site. The activity observed under low pH conditions suggests that protonation of cytosine would be indispensable for self-cleavage. The correlation between sequence and activity obtained in this study was analyzed, and the structure was inferred based on sequence constraints on the function. The results suggested that the sequence from position 30 to 36 constitute a highly-constrained catalytic core. Taking into account the base pairs involving protonated cytosine, it is assumed that the 30th to 36th bases of the sequence in the cytosine-rich loop interact with the 11th to 17th bases of the loop containing the cleavage site, as shown in Fig. 6b. Though there is a discrepancy in the sequences of N34-53 and N39-53, the loop-loop interaction could be formed in the same way (Fig. S9) using a non-canonical base pair shown in Fig. 6c. The important point is that this interaction pattern could be formed for all molecules: N28-53, N29-53, N34-53, and N39-53. In summary, the functional nucleic acid obtained in this study was active in the acidic region, and the active structure may be induced by the loop–loop interactions involving the 30th and 36th nucleobases, which are protonated under the low pH conditions where the self-cleavage reaction occurs (Fig. S9). The free cytosine, which is not involved in base-pair formation, presumably acts as a catalytic residue (general acid). Following the loop-loop interaction described above, the cytosine residues at positions 8, 9, and 21 appear to be free. Based on their proximity to the cleavage site, the cytosines at positions 8 and 9, or 21, are likely candidates as a general acid. In the case of the Twister ribozyme, it is important to note that the N + 1 adenine (i.e., the base immediately following the cleavable phosphate group) functions as an acid, and this point requires careful consideration. Although nucleobases adjacent to the cleavage site can participate directly in catalysis, our current model does not place C8 or C9 in a geometry favorable for catalysis. It is thought that the cytosine at position 21 is the most likely candidate for the general acid.

In the structures shown in Fig. 6b, the essential bases of single-nucleotide mutagenesis at positions 32, 34, and 39 in N34-53 do not match the bases at positions 32, 34, and 39 in N39-53. This suggests that the differences in these bases may account for the variations in the pH dependence of activity (Fig. 4b–e).

As an application of the obtained RNAs, challenges exist in utilizing them as a nucleic acid drug specific to cancer tissue by exploiting differences in pH environments. This is because the pH of cancer tissue is known to be around 6.8,36,37 and N39-53 exhibits activity in regions that are too acidic. Therefore, re-selection is necessary to optimize the estimated pKa of N39-53. For example, under re-reaction conditions, the reaction is conducted at pH 6.8, while the negative selection reaction is performed at pH 7.2. The RNA obtained this time exhibits significant changes in activity when the pH changes by 0.5 units. This further supports our belief that by meticulously setting the selection conditions, it is possible to create molecules that exhibit activity only within the target pH range.

Additionally, it is necessary to confer nuclease resistance and binding affinity. Previous studies have reported introducing modified nucleic acids into DNAzymes to enhance nuclease resistance and binding affinity for gene expression control.60,61 In future analyses, we plan to utilize the results obtained from point mutations to introduce modified nucleotides into bases that are not critical for activity and evaluate the resulting activity. By using techniques such as X-ray diffraction-based structure determination and molecular dynamics simulations, it is possible to elucidate the catalytic mechanism in greater detail.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully obtained novel cofactorless functional nucleic acids possessing unique properties not found in nature. These RNAs exhibit high self-cleavage activity at pH 5.0 or below and retain activity regardless of the presence or absence of divalent metal ions. The estimated pKa of 4.1 suggests that cytosine is involved in the self-cleavage reaction. Regarding the interaction pattern of N39-53, this activity profile suggested that cytosine may play a crucial role as a catalytic residue. The ability to form an active structure under acidic conditions suggests that protonated bases may be used for base pairing in the active structure. Furthermore, the successful shortening to a 53-base length while maintaining activity resulted in a size suitable for application as a tool for gene-expression control. These findings expand the new possibilities of functional nucleic acids.
