Polypeptide encoded by the −1 PRF signal of SARS-CoV-2 controls multiple enzymatic activities of the viral NiRAN domain
Authors: Hsiu-Ting Hsu, Che-Pei Cho, Chia-Liang Lin, Kung-Yao Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Nidovirus RdRp-associated nucleotidyl transferase (NiRAN) domain is located amino-terminally to the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) in nonstructural protein (nsp)12 of SARS-CoV-2. The GDP-polyribonucleotidyltransferase (GDP-PRNT) and UMPylation activities of NiRAN contribute to viral RNA biogenesis by forming an RNA capping precursor and an RNA synthesis initiation primer, respectively. Translation of nsp12 is mediated by −1 programmed ribosomal frameshifting (−1 PRF), via an RNA signal composed of a slippery sequence and a downstream stimulator structure. The −1 PRF signal encodes a polypeptide, yet its function remains unexplored. Here, we show that the −1 PRF signal-encoded polypeptide is required for NiRAN activity. Mutational analysis reveals that a phenylalanine encoded by part of the slippery sequence, as well as two specific amino acids encoded by the stimulator, are essential for the UMPylation and GDP-PRNT activities of NiRAN. Thus, in addition to affecting the expression level of nsp12 through RNA-mediated frameshifting efficiency, variation in the −1 PRF signal influences functional viral RNA production through polypeptide-mediated modulation of NiRAN activity. This dual role likely constrains the allowable sequence variation of the −1 PRF signal during viral propagation, suggesting that the entire −1 PRF signal is a better antiviral target than the stimulator alone.

Introduction

COVID-19 is an acute respiratory illness caused by SARS-CoV-2, one of the seven known human coronaviruses (HCoVs). It is a positive-stranded RNA virus, with its genomic RNA also serving as its mRNA by bearing a 5′-N7-methylated guanosine base (cap) modification to facilitate translation initiation. Viral nonstructural proteins (nsps) 12–16 contain the enzymatic activities responsible for viral RNA biogenesis: nsp12 harbors RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) activity in its carboxyl-terminal portion; nsp13 contains both RNA helicase and triphosphatase (TPase) activities; and nsp14 and nsp16 are methyltransferases required for cap methylation [1–6].

One nsp12 protein associates with one molecule of nsp7 and two molecules of nsp8 to form the RdRp holoenzyme (holo–RdRp), capable of synthesizing RNA in vitro [7–9]. An amino-terminal (N-terminal) nsp12 domain, termed nidovirus RdRp-associated nucleotidyl-transferase (NiRAN), which constitutes a kinase-like fold, was recently shown to be involved in viral RNA capping by collaborating with nsp9 [6, 10]. NiRAN catalyzes the transfer of a 5′-monophosphorylated RNA from a 5′-triphosphorylated viral RNA to the N-terminal primary amine of nsp9 (termed RNAylation), forming RNAylated nsp9 in vitro [11, 12]. The 5′-monophosphorylated RNA is then transferred to a NiRAN-bound GDP by the GDP-polyribonucleotidyltransferase (GDP-PRNT) activity of NiRAN to form G-5′ppp5′-RNA, the cap precursor and substrate of nsp14/16, with the release of nsp9 [11, 12]. Moreover, NiRAN can transfer an NMP (N stands for G, A, U, or C) to the N-terminal primary amine of nsp9 (termed NMPylation), forming nsp9-NMP via a phosphoramidate linkage. Because the viral RdRp is not a de novo RNA polymerase, evidence suggests that the nsp9–UMP is used as the primer by viral RdRp to initiate negative-stranded viral RNA synthesis [13–15]. Thus, NiRAN possesses multiple enzymatic activities crucial for viral RNA biogenesis.

The 5′ two-thirds of a coronavirus genome constitutes ORF1, which is translated as fused polyproteins (ORF1a and ORF1ab) and then processed into 16 nsps (nsps 1–16). The genes for nsps 12–16 reside in the −1 reading frame of ORF1, and their translation requires a −1 ribosomal frameshifting (−1 PRF) mechanism because of a 0-frame stop codon within the nsp12 ORF [1, 2]. Efficient −1 PRF requires an RNA signal, the frameshifting stimulation element (FSE), which consists of a slippery sequence and an optimally spaced downstream stimulator structure [16, 17]. When ribosomes pass through the slippery sequence, some of them move backward by one nucleotide toward the 5′ end of the mRNA (entering the −1 frame) due to the influence of the downstream stimulator. A slippery sequence needs to match an XXXYYYZ consensus (X can be any nucleotide, Y is either A or U, and Z is usually A, C, or U) to facilitate the anticodon of tRNAs in the 0-frame (pairing with XXY and YYZ in the ribosomal P and A sites, respectively) to re-pair with the −1 frame codons (XXX and YYY) upon frameshifting [18, 19]. Thus, ORF1a is a 0-frame translation product harboring nsps 1–10, and ORF1ab is a translation product harboring nsps 1–16 and containing polypeptides encoded by both the 0 and −1 frames fused at the slippery site junction. Accordingly, the expression level of nsps 12–16 is determined by the efficiency of −1 PRF.

All seven known HCoVs have a slippery sequence of UUUAAAC. In the cases of SARS-CoV-1 and -2, a three-stem pseudoknot was identified as the stimulator, which has been intensely studied for its role in the −1 PRF mechanism and as a drug target [20–28]. By contrast, no study has been reported on the −1 PRF signal encoded-polypeptide (-1 PRFSEP), which is also located at the N-terminus of nsp12 (residues 7–34). This study demonstrates that the −1 PRFSEP is essential for multiple enzymatic activities of NiRAN. Key amino acid residues impacting NiRAN activity have been characterized and found to be defined by both the slippery sequence and the stimulator. Critical positions in the stimulator that are sensitive to mutation have been revealed, due to its dual role in maintaining pseudoknot structure and encoding essential amino acids. In addition to explaining why the unique UUUAAAC sequence is used as the slippery sequence, these findings facilitate the prediction of possible stimulator variants and support the design of drugs targeting the −1 PRF of SARS-CoV-2.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and recombinant DNA mutagenesis

The following plasmids were obtained from Addgene: pIA1414 expressing 10 × His-SUMO-nsp9 (Addgene #172402) [14]; pCDB302 expressing SUMO protease containing a C-terminal 10 × His tag (Addgene #113673) [29]; RSFDuet-1 expressing (14 × His-nsp8/nsp7) (nsp12) holo–RdRp (Addgene #165451) [30]; pET28a-SARS-CoV-2 NSP13 expressing 6 × His-nsp13 (Addgene #176492) [31]; pSGDluc (Addgene #87323) [32]. A nucleotide fragment containing the slippery sequence, spacer, and pseudoknot element of SARS-CoV-2 was inserted between the PspXI and HindIII sites of pSGDluc to generate pSGDluc-CoV2 as the −1 PRF reporter. Mutagenesis of the −1 PRFSEP or slippery sequence was performed using the QuickChange mutagenesis kit from Stratagene according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The FLAG-tagged nsp9 used in immunoprecipitation was obtained by inserting nucleotide sequences encoding the FLAG tag at the C-terminus of nsp9 via mutagenesis, using pIA1414 as the template. After plasmid isolation, all clones were verified by DNA sequencing.

Protein expression and purification

SUMO protease: The plasmid pCDB302 encoding SUMO protease was overexpressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) CodonPlus-RIL cells. The cells were grown in lysogenic broth (LB) containing kanamycin (50 µg/ml), to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8 at 37°C. Expression of SUMO protease was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and the culture was grown at 18°C for an additional 16–20 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in 50 ml of lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.02% Tween 20, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM DTT, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma–Aldrich, S8830)]. After being rotated for 10 min at 4°C, the mixture was sonicated for 10 min (5-s on, 5-s off) on ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane filter and loaded onto a HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo). The resin was washed with buffer A (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 50 mM imidazole). The SUMO protease was eluted by a step gradient of imidazole (100, 150, and 350 mM). The SUMO protease-containing fractions were collected and passed through an Amicon Ultra 15 ml 100K MW cutoff filter by centrifugation to remove higher-molecular-weight proteins. The purified SUMO protease was dialyzed against storage buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 50% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM PMSF) and stored at −70 °C.

Nsp9: 10 × His-SUMO-nsp9 and 10 × His-SUMO-nsp9-FLAG were overexpressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. The cells were grown in LB medium containing kanamycin (50 µg/ml) and cultured at 37°C to an OD60 of 0.6–0.8. The culturing temperature was then lowered to 16°C, and expression was induced with 0.1 mM IPTG for 18 h. Induced cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the cell pellets were stored at −70 °C.

The frozen cells were resuspended in lysis buffer and sonicated to lyse the cells. The lysate was centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane filter and loaded onto HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo). The resin was washed with buffer A1 [50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM DTT, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME)]. The 10 × His-SUMO-NSP9 or 10 × His-SUMO-nsp9-FLAG was eluted using a step gradient of imidazole (100, 150, and 350 mM) in buffer A. Fractions were collected and analyzed by 12% SDS–PAGE. The fractions containing 10 × His-SUMO-nsp9 or 10 × His-SUMO-nsp9-FLAG were pooled and treated with SUMO protease. The protein sample was dialyzed against buffer B (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF, and 1 mM DTT) at 4°C overnight to remove imidazole and then loaded onto HisPur Ni-NTA resin to bind and remove the cleaved 10 × His-SUMO and 10 × His-tagged SUMO protease. The fractions containing nsp9 were dialyzed against storage buffer and stored at −70 °C.

RdRp holoenzyme and its variants: pRSFDuet-1 or its derived plasmid containing a specific nsp12 mutant was transformed into E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells to overexpress and purify holo–RdRp variants via 14 × His-nsp8 mediated protein–protein interactions as reported [30], and described briefly below. Cells were grown in LB medium containing kanamycin (50 µg/ml) and cultured at 37°C to an OD60 of 0.6–0.8. Expression of 14 × His-nsp8/nsp7/nsp12 recombinant proteins was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG. Flasks were placed at 4°C for 15 min and then incubated for 20 h at 20°C with shaking at 150 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the cell pellets were stored at −70°C.

Frozen cells were resuspended in lysis buffer, rotated for 10 min at 4°C, and lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing the RdRp holoenzyme complex was filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane filter and loaded onto HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo). The resin was washed with buffer A. The complex was eluted using a step gradient of imidazole (100, 150, and 350 mM). Fractions were collected and analyzed by 12% SDS–PAGE. Holo–RdRp-containing fractions were pooled and 3-fold diluted using 50 mM Tris–HCl of pH 8.0. The diluted protein complex was loaded onto a DEAE Sepharose™ Fast Flow anion exchange column (GE Healthcare), pre-equilibrated in buffer B (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl). The column was washed with 5 ml of buffer B. The holo–RdRp was eluted by a step gradient of NaCl in buffer B (100, 150, and 300 mM). Fractions were collected and analyzed by 12% SDS–PAGE. The RdRp complex eluted in the 150 mM NaCl fraction was collected, dialyzed against storage buffer, and stored at −70 °C.

Nsp13: The plasmid pET28a-SARS-CoV-2 nsp13 was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). The cells were grown, harvested, lysed, and purified in the same way as those of the SUMO protease above. Fractions were collected and analyzed by 12% SDS–PAGE. The nsp13-containing fractions were pooled and then dialyzed against storage buffer.

RdRp activity assay

To test RdRp activity, an RNA hairpin serving as both the primer and the template was used [7]. RNA stock containing 250 nM RNA oligonucleotide (5′-FAM-UUUUCAUGCUACGCGUAGUUUUCUACGCG-3′) (Genomics Inc., Taiwan) in 10 μl of 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, and 50 mM KCl was heated to 75°C and then cooled on ice to form the RNA hairpin prior to use. The RdRp assay reactions, each containing 1 μl of RNA sample, nsp12/7/8 complex (with 500 nM nsp12 or its variant), 250 µM NTPs, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 15 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT, were incubated at 37°C for 40 min. The reactions were stopped by adding gel loading buffer (95% formamide, 18 mM EDTA, 0.025% SDS, 0.025% xylene cyanol, and 0.025% bromophenol blue) and then analyzed by loading into an 18% denaturing polyacrylamide gel (19:1 acrylamide: bisacrylamide ratio) in 0.5 × TBE buffer. The RNA products were visualized using the Gel Doc EZ Gel Documentation System with Blue Tray (Bio-Rad) and Bio-Rad Image Lab Software 6.1.

NMPylation of nsp9

NMPylation reactions were carried out as reported [11, 14] in 15 µl of buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 15 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, RdRp holoenzyme or variant containing an nsp12 mutant, and nsp9. The reactions were incubated at 37°C for 5 min before the addition of 660 nM α-³²P-GTP or α-³²P-UTP. After incubation at 37°C for 10 min, the reactions were stopped by adding 5 µl of 5 × SDS–PAGE sample buffer containing 10% β-ME. The reaction products were separated by Tricine SDS–PAGE of 16% total acrylamide with a 3% crosslinker (bisacrylamide) [33] and then stained with Instant Blue (Abcam 11 921). Radiolabeled proteins were detected using a Typhoon FLA 7000 phosphorimager (GE).

RNAylation assay and generation of P32-radiolabeled GpppA-RNA LS10

RNAylation reactions were carried out in a 10 μl buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.5 µl yeast inorganic pyrophosphatase (NEB), RdRp holoenzyme or variant (with 1 µM nsp12-WT or mutants), 6 µM nsp9, 2 mM MnCl2, and 400 µM 5′-ppp LS10 RNA (5′-pppAUUAAAGGUU-3′) (ChemGenes). The reactions were incubated at 37°C for 60 min and terminated by adding 5 µl of 5 × SDS–PAGE sample buffer containing 10% β-ME. The reaction products were separated by Tricine SDS–PAGE and then stained with Instant Blue.

GDP-PRNT activity assay was performed by combining the GTPase reaction and RNAylation. The GTPase reaction was carried out in a 10 µl of buffer containing 2 µM [α-³²P] GTP, 4.5 µM nsp13, 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, and 2 mM MgCl2. The reaction was first incubated at 37°C for 30 min to generate [α-³²P] GDP and then boiled at 95°C for 5 min to inactivate the nsp13 [11]. To produce P32-radiolabeled GpppA-RNA LS10, 2 μl of nsp13-inactivated GTPase reaction sample and a final concentration of 1 mM MgCl2 were added to 10 µl of RNAylation reaction. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The reactions were stopped by adding gel loading buffer (95% formamide, 18 mM EDTA, 0.025% SDS, 0.025% xylene cyanol, and 0.025% bromophenol blue). The reaction products were separated by a 20% denaturing polyacrylamide gel and stained with toluidine (Sigma–Aldrich, 89640). Radiolabeled RNA was detected using a Typhoon FLA 7000 phosphorimager (GE)

Immunoprecipitation

The RdRp holoenzyme or its variant was incubated with nsp9-FLAG protein and left on ice for 10 min before being mixed with ANTI-FLAG® M2 Affinity Gel (Millipore Sigma) in binding buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 15 mM KCl, and 5 mM MgCl2). The mixtures were rotated at 4°C for 3 h. The beads were washed twice with binding buffer. The protein complex was eluted with 0.1 M glycine (pH 3.5). The proteins were separated by 10% or 15% SDS–PAGE and detected by western blotting using anti-nsp12 (GeneTex GTX135467) or anti-FLAG (Sigma–Aldrich, F1804) antibodies.

Dual luciferase assay

HEK293T cells were grown in a 24-well plate (1 × 105 cells per well) with 0.5 ml of DMEM at 37°C overnight. The cells were transfected with 500 ng of the −1 PRF reporter plasmid using jetPRIME® transfection reagent (Polyplus) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated at 37°C overnight. After washing once with 0.5 ml of 1 × PBS buffer, the cells were resuspended in 60 µl of 1 × PBS buffer. The luciferase measurements were performed by using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay (Promega). A 10 µl volume of resuspended cells was added to a 96-well plate and then mixed with 10 μl of Dual-Glo® Luciferase Reagent. After measurement of firefly luminescence, 10 μl of Dual-Glo® Stop & Glo® Reagent was added to each well of the plate, and Renilla luminescence was then measured using a PerkinElmer1420 Multilabel Counter. Frameshifting efficiency was calculated as (Fluc/Rluc)/(Fluccontrol/Rluccontrol) × 100% [32].

Quantification and statistical analysis

Radiolabeled nsp9 (Figs 4C, 5C, and 6A), RNA–nsp9, apo–nsp9 (Fig. 6C), and radiolabeled LS10 RNA (Fig. 6E) bands from the gel images were quantified using ImageQuant™ TL software for quantitative analysis. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean from three independent experiments (Figs 4D, 5D, 6B, 6D, and 6F).

Conservation analysis

Clustal Omega [34] was used for sequence alignment of the N-terminal regions of nsp12 for five Human Betacoronaviruses: SARS-CoV (Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus; NC_004718.3); SARS-CoV-2, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (NC_045512.2); MERS-CoV, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (NC_019 843); HCoV-HKU1, Human coronavirus HKU1 (PP356908.1); HCoV-OC43, Human coronavirus OC43 strain ATCC VR-759 (NC_006213.1).

Results

−1 PRFSEP removal impacts GMPylation activity but not RdRp activity of nsp12

Comparison of the −1 PRF signals of SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV-1 revealed a single nucleotide difference between their pseudoknot stimulators. By contrast, we observed more profound sequence variation downstream of the pseudoknot (Supplementary Fig. S1). Interestingly, all these differences occur at the wobble positions of codons belonging to the −1 reading frame, implicating functional conservation at the protein level. This hint is consistent with a structural study showing that some amino acids encoded by the downstream sequences are involved in the binding of nsp9 by NiRAN [12]. The N-terminus of nsp9 is presented to the NiRAN active site for modification by forming specific contacts with residues (Asp36 and Tyr38) of the nsp12 β2 strand, which constitutes a β hairpin with the neighboring β3 strand (Fig. 1A) [8]. By contrast, the −1 PRFSEP spans residues 7–34 in the N-terminal region of nsp12 and is embedded in a α1β1β2 secondary structure context, with residues 31–34 encoded by the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot stimulator to form part of the β2 strand (Fig. 1B and C). The proximity and overlap of the −1 PRFSEP with the β hairpin prompted us to investigate its involvement in NiRAN activity.

To this end, we constructed a deletion mutant of nsp12 (mutant D4-28), which leaves the last six amino acids encoded by the −1 PRF signal intact. In this way, D4-28 lacks most of the −1 PRFSEP while retaining an intact β hairpin. We then compared the GMPylation activity of D4-28 with wild-type nsp12 (WT) and two negative control constructs. The first control lacks the nsp9-contacting part of the β hairpin (mutant D33-40), and the second control has a key active site residue, D218 of NiRAN, mutated to alanine (mutant D218A) (Fig. 2A and B). Because GMPylation of nsp9 requires a free amino-end starting with an NNE tripeptide motif, we prepared authentic nsp9 through a SUMO-tagged/de-SUMO scheme (Supplementary Fig. S2) via expressing nsp9 as an N-terminal SUMO-tagged fusion protein and removing the SUMO-tag by SUMO protease treatment after fusion protein purification [14]. Nsp12 variants were co-expressed with nsp7 and His-tagged nsp8 for holo–RdRp purification [30]. During purification, nsp8 levels varied, but nsp12 variants were adjusted to comparable amounts, with RdRp activity serving as a control since both NiRAN and RdRp domains reside in nsp12. RdRp activity of the mutants remained similar to that of WT, as shown by elongation RNA products from a FAM-labeled primer (Fig. 2C) [7]. By contrast, the GMPylation activity of all three mutants was abrogated (Fig. 2D). Importantly, nsp9–NiRAN association assay by immunoprecipitation showed that mutants D218A and D4-28 retained nsp9-binding efficiency similar to WT, whereas mutant D33-40 lost most nsp9-binding ability (Fig. 2E), consistent with the role of the β hairpin in nsp9-binding. The fact that D4-28 retains nsp9 binding capacity similar to WT and D218A argues that its deficiency in GMPylation activity is not due to an unfolding effect caused by polypeptide removal. Thus, amino acids within residues 4–28 of −1 PRFSEP are involved in the GMPylation activity of NiRAN.

GMPylation activity maintenance of NiRAN requires amino acids residing in α1 and β2 structural elements of −1 PRFSEP

To systematically map the −1 PRFSEP residues involved in maintaining GMPylation activity, we replaced residues in each secondary structural element (mutant ss5A: α1 substitution and mutant 4A: β2 substitution), including a threonine/serine-rich linker between β1 and β2 (mutant TTA: threonine/serine substitution), with alanine to perturb potential functions (Fig. 3A). Because the resulting β1 substitution mutant expressed poorly (data not shown), single alanine mutations were introduced into its constituent residues one by one (R18A, L19A, T20A, and P21A). A T20E mutant was also constructed due to its reported role in viral RNA synthesis [35]. Results from the GMPylation assay indicated that mutations in β1 (Supplementary Fig. S3) and the T/S linker (data not shown) retained WT-like GMPylation activity. By contrast, mutants ss5A and 4A, which are respectively encoded by nucleotides covering the slippery site and 12 nucleotides on the 3′ side of the pseudoknot, showed dramatically reduced GMPylation activity without perturbing RdRp activity (Fig. 3B and C). Thus, residues located in α1 and β2 of −1 PRFSEP participate in maintaining GMPylation activity.

Specific amino acids encoded by the pseudoknot sequence and a potential π–π interaction mediated by phenylalanine 7 contribute to −1 PRFSEP-mediated GMPylation activity

To pinpoint specific residues involved, we performed alanine scanning of four β2-constituent residues encoded by nucleotides spanning stem 2 and stem 3 of SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot (Fig. 1B and C). Among these mutants, V31A and R33A showed a substantial reduction in GMPylation efficiency on nsp9, whereas mutants V30A and Y32A retained WT-like activity, implicating a role for V31 and R33 (Fig. 3D). Consistently, a double mutant (V31A/R33A) lost almost all GMPylation activity, echoing the result of the 4A mutant. Interestingly, two recent structural studies indicated that V31 and R33 constitute part of a GDP-binding site, with R33 forming a potential π–cation interaction with the guanine base [12, 36].

Reduced GMPylation activity in mutant ss5A was interesting, due to the unique sequence requirement of a slippery site for −1 PRF. Replacement of five residues with alanine could destabilize the α helix, disrupt specific interactions mediated by residues in the helix, or both. Structural analysis of the nsp9-bound holo–RdRp complex [8] showed that residues at positions 8 and 9 form intra-helical hydrogen bonds with residues located at the two ends of α1. Specifically, two side chain-mediated hydrogen bonds could form between N9 and Q5, while the backbone of L8 could couple with the thiol group of C12 (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Helical wheel analysis revealed that the side chain of phenylalanine 7 (F7) points toward the core of NiRAN. Additionally, a potential π–π interaction could exist between the aromatic ring of F7 and either the aromatic ring of Phenylalanine 104 in β6 or the imidazole ring of Histidine 113 in β7 of the NiRAN core (Fig. 4A). Moreover, F7, F104, and H113 are conserved in five HCoVs that belong to the Betacoronavirus genus (Supplementary Fig. S5). We hypothesized that specific residues displayed on α1 may be required to maintain GMPylation activity. An alanine replacement of Q5 (mutant Q5A) or C12 (mutant C12A) to disrupt potential side chain-mediated hydrogen bonds within the helix had little or no effect on GMPylation activity (Supplementary Fig. S4B–D). By contrast, a point mutation of phenylalanine 7 to alanine (mutant F7A) resulted in more than a 95% loss of GMPylation efficiency relative to nsp12-WT without impairing RdRp activity (Fig. 4B–D). Finally, a phenylalanine 104 to alanine mutation (mutant F104A) resulted in WT-like GMPylation activity in contrast to a 70% reduction in GMPylation activity in a histidine 113 to alanine mutation (mutant H113A). These results are consistent with the potential π–π interaction between F7 and H113 being crucial for GMPylation activity. Thus, three residues of −1 PRFSEP are essential for the GMPylation activity of NiRAN, two located at β2 (V31/R33) and one located at α1 (F7), and the codons encoding them are contributed by the stimulator and slippery sequence, respectively.

UUUAAAC ensures decoding of a key phenylalanine embedded within a α-helical conformation for NiRAN activity maintenance

F7 is encoded by a UUU codon composed of two uridines 5′ to the UUUAAAC slippery sequence and the first U of the slippery sequence. F7 is followed by an LN dipeptide encoded by the remaining slippery sequence before ribosomes frame-shift into the −1 reading frame. To further assess the role of F7 and its impact on slippery sequence identity determination, we replaced the FLN tripeptide in nsp12-WT with specific amino acids that can be encoded by other XXXYYYZ-compatible sequences when combined with the two 5′-UU (Fig. 5A and B). Replacement with an LKN tripeptide encoded by the UUAAAAAAC sequence resulted in an 80% loss of GMPylation activity relative to WT. Strikingly, an LNF tripeptide encoded by the UUAAAUUUC sequence lost 90% of GMPylation activity even though it possesses the same amino acid composition as the FLN in WT, emphasizing that the order of specific side chains displayed along the helix is crucial (Fig. 5C and D). Since UUC is the alternative codon for phenylalanine, CCCYYYZ could be a potential choice for slippery sequence selection while keeping F7 unchanged during viral propagation, because the cytosine in the wobble position determines the identity of the first nucleotide in the XXX slippery sequence consensus. Interestingly, an FPL tripeptide encoded by the UUCCCUUUA sequence also lost 90% of GMPylation activity, consistent with a structural role provided by α1, as proline acts as a helix breaker.

To evaluate the relative contributions of −1 PRF efficiency and GMPylation efficiency in determining the identity of the slippery sequence, we inserted four −1 PRF signals—each containing one of the four aforementioned slippery sequences and an identical downstream SARS-CoV-2 stimulator, into the pSGDluc dual-luciferase reporter [32] to measure −1 PRF efficiency in HEK293T cells. The −1 PRF efficiencies of the three non-wild-type constructs all dropped to ~60% of that of the wild-type sequence (Fig. 5E). Thus, among the compatible slippery sequences tested, the UUUAAAC sequence led to both the highest −1 PRF activity and the most efficient GMPylation activity when combined with the two upstream uridines. Together, these results suggest that the presence of F7 and the maintenance of a helical conformation, encoded by the UUUUUAAAC sequence, may be determinants, in addition to −1 PRF efficiency, for the selection of UUUAAAC—but not CCCAAAC or other tested sequences—as the slippery sequence in the five human Betacoronaviruses.

−1 PRFSEP impacts viral replication–relevant UMPylation and GDP-PRNT activities of NiRAN

Although GMPylation was used above to monitor the NMPylation activity of NiRAN, its relevance to viral replication remains unclear [13–15]. To confirm that the identified residues in the −1 PRFSEP are also involved in viral replication–relevant NiRAN activities, the LNF and V31A/R33A mutants were examined for their UMPylation efficiency. Similar to the GMPylation results, the UMPylation activity of each mutant was severely impaired compared with that of the WT (Fig. 6A and B). Next, the NiRAN activities required for in vitro viral RNA capping—RNAylation of nsp9 and the subsequent GDP-PRNT activity, which replaces conjugated nsp9 from the 5′ end of viral RNA with GDP [11]—were also measured. An RNAylation reaction that results in a linkage between the free amino end of nsp9 and the 5′-monophosphate of a viral 5′-UTR-derived 5′-triphosphorylated RNA oligonucleotide, LS10 [12], was monitored by observing the formation of a covalently conjugated LS10 RNA–nsp9 complex in a Tricine SDS–PAGE. Interestingly, RNAylation activity was only slightly reduced in the LNF mutant and reduced by 40% in V31A/R33A relative to WT (Fig. 6C and D). By contrast, the GDP-PRNT activities of LNF and V31A/R33A were decreased by 70% and 90%, respectively, compared with WT (Fig. 6E and F).

To better understand the underlying mechanisms, an immunoprecipitation assay was used to examine the nsp9-binding efficiency of mutants with substitutions in the α1 or β2 regions of the −1 PRFSEP. While ss5A retained WT-like nsp9-binding levels, the 4A and V31A/R33A mutants showed substantially reduced nsp9 binding (Fig. 6G), suggesting a correlation with the observed reductions in RNAylation and GDP-PRNT activity for V31A/R33A. However, the nsp9-binding deficiencies caused by α1 or β2 mutations do not fully explain the observed UMPylation and GDP-PRNT impairments in LNF and V31A/R33A, indicating that other prerequisites for NiRAN activity are affected. Therefore, to efficiently form two crucial intermediates—an RNA synthesis primer and the cap precursor of the viral genome—specific amino acid residues encoded by the −1 PRF signal are essential.

Discussion

Being part of the mRNA, a −1 PRF signal encodes a polypeptide. However, the function of the −1 PRFSEP has not been explored, although the −1 PRF signal—specifically the RNA stimulator—has been the subject of investigation [37]. In this study, we demonstrated that deletion or specific amino acid mutations in the −1 PRFSEP of SARS-CoV-2 severely impaired two key viral replication–relevant enzymatic activities of the NiRAN domain in nsp12, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1, underscoring its essential role in maintaining NiRAN function. Rather than directly participating in catalysis, we propose that the −1 PRFSEP influences overall catalytic efficiency by modulating the β hairpin, a versatile structural element that facilitates the docking of various biomolecules and ligands to the NiRAN active site. Additionally, a potential tertiary interaction between the N-terminal α1 helix of the −1 PRFSEP and the NiRAN core likely helps orient the geometric arrangement of key active site residues for efficient catalysis. However, this work raises several interesting questions that warrant further investigation.

Distinct mechanisms of NiRAN activity impairment by α1-residing F7 and β2-residing residues

The three NiRAN enzymatic activities under investigation all involve modification of the N-terminus of nsp9. In our mutagenesis analyses, alanine substitutions at three residues within the −1 PRFSEP resulted in significant impairment of two NiRAN activities. Because both nsp9-binding efficiency and RNAylation were substantially reduced in the V31A/R33A mutant (Fig. 6D and G), this suggests that the decreased RNAylation level may be a direct consequence of impaired nsp9 binding. The further reduction in GDP-PRNT activity can likely be attributed to disrupted GDP binding, in addition to the nsp9-binding defect. Both interpretations are consistent with the proximity of V31 and R33 to the nsp9-binding site and the involvement of their side chains in contacting the guanine ring of a GTP analog, as revealed in the structure of the GMPRNP-bound holo–RdRp–nsp9 complex [12, 36]. However, the severe impairment in UMPylation cannot be fully explained by reduced nsp9 binding alone. Alternatively, additional effects—such as misorientation of the NNE motif of nsp9 within the active site—may contribute to compounding the deficiency.

By contrast, the involvement of F7 in UMPylation and GDP-PRNT activity lacks a clear structural rationale. Experimental results showed that the nsp9-binding efficiency of the ss5A mutant was nearly intact, ruling out nsp9-binding interference as the primary cause of F7-mediated effects and aligning with its WT-like RNAylation levels (Fig. 6D and G). Findings from the FPL mutant further support that both the specific phenylalanine side chain of F7 and its α helical context are required for −1 PRFSEP-mediated maintenance of NiRAN activity (Fig. 5C). The α1 helix and its resident F7 are separated from the NiRAN active site by a set of antiparallel β strands (β4–β7), which form the core of NiRAN along with three flanking helices. Structural analysis revealed that F7 may form a tertiary interaction with the heterocyclic imidazole ring of H113, consistent with experimental data (Fig. 4C and D). Three nucleotide-binding modes have been observed within the NiRAN active site. Importantly, in each mode, the 5′-phosphoryl group of the nucleotide points toward a shared network that encompasses the nsp9-binding β hairpin and a subset of conserved residues, including D218, which coordinate phosphate and metal ion organization during catalysis [12]. We propose that the α1 helix is anchored to the β4–β7 wall of the NiRAN active site via F7-mediated tertiary interactions, thereby aligning the intricate network of active site residues required for catalysis across different reactions. Substitution of F7 with alanine or disruption of the helical conformation could decouple the F7–H113 interaction, leading to misalignment of the key catalytic arrangement necessary for UMPylation, GMPylation, and GDP-PRNT activity. Interestingly, H113A retained some GMPylation activity, which may be due to its proximity to the active site. Further structural studies of the F7A- and H113A-containing holo–RdRp–nsp9 complex are required to test this hypothesis, although such mutations may destabilize specific interactions and preclude structural analysis.

Virus-specific slippery sequence defined by an amino acid–mediated function

A variety of viruses utilize −1 PRF to regulate viral gene expression. Interestingly, specific viruses tend to possess unique slippery sequences, even though distinct XXXYYYZ-compatible candidate sequences are available [38]. One possible explanation is that the specific slippery sequence defines the optimal frameshifting efficiency window necessary for the propagation of a particular virus. Additionally, simultaneous substitution of three consecutive nucleotides in the slippery sequence with another compatible sequence would be challenging; errors in such attempts could result in the loss of −1 PRF and failure of viral propagation. However, our finding that F7 must be embedded within a helical conformation to maintain NiRAN activity provides an alternative explanation for the use of UUUAAAC as the slippery sequence in SARS-CoV-2. Among the three other compatible slippery sequences examined, the −1 PRF efficiencies were lower than that of UUUAAAC. Importantly, the resulting NiRAN activities were severely impaired in all cases. Therefore, the levels of the primer for initiating RNA synthesis and the cap precursor would be significantly reduced if any of the other three slippery sequences were used. Since the −1 PRFSEP of SARS-CoV-2 represents the first example linking this element to viral enzyme activity, it remains to be seen whether a similar role exists for −1 PRFSEPs in other viruses.

A full −1 PRF signal is a better drug target than the stimulator alone

The −1 PRF stimulator of SARS-CoV-2 has been suggested as a promising antiviral target due to its pseudoknot structure and critical role in −1 PRF-mediated translation of ORF1ab [39]. Several small molecules requiring concentrations higher than the micromolar range have been reported to target the SARS-CoV-1/2 pseudoknots and inhibit −1 PRF in vitro or in vivo [40–43]. However, the error-prone nature of viral RdRp makes targeting viral RNA challenging. Our finding that the −1 PRFSEP is required for NiRAN activity suggests that maintaining a functional pseudoknot may not be the only constraint on sequence variation of the −1 PRF signal during viral propagation, as demonstrated by the critical role of F7 in maintaining the UUUAAAC slippery site. Thus, a mutation that preserves −1 PRF efficiency might still fail to meet the NiRAN activity requirement. This is particularly likely for nucleotides spanning stems 2 and 3 of the pseudoknot, which encode four amino acids constituting part of the pivotal β hairpin essential for NiRAN activity. Interestingly, two independent studies have identified antisense oligonucleotides targeting this region of SARS-CoV pseudoknots, resulting in −1 PRF attenuation and antiviral activity [44, 45]. Finally, the codon requirement imposed by the critical role of a helical conformation-embedded F7 in NiRAN activity suggests that targeting the complete −1 PRF signal, beyond just the stimulator, could be a better RNA target for drug design to prevent further viral escape. Given that the dual-function restricted UUUAAA sequence provides specific base moieties for sequence-specific recognition, the efficiency of pseudoknot-targeting molecules could be further enhanced by conjugation with a UUUAAA-specific molecule, such as an antisense oligonucleotide or a mimetic.
