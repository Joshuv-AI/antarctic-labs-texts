Consensus-based disease definitions for endocrine immune-related adverse events of immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Ling Zhu, Yee-Ming M Cheung, Cherie Chiang, Emily J Gallagher, Ole-Petter R Hamnvik, Jennifer Mammen, Zoe Quandt, Renisha Jones, Jon McDunn, Huda Al-Bahadili, Trevor Angell, Irina Bancos, Allison Betof Warner, Susan Blackwell, Cassandra Calabrese, Tracey A Cho, Michael Dougan, Michelle Ferreira, Pauline Funchain, Justine Herndon, Douglas B Johnson, Randol Kennedy, Meenal Kheterpal, Michel Khouri, Melissa G Lechner, Annette M Lim, Alina Markova, Alexa Meara, Laura Overton, Ana Luisa Perdigoto, Nirupa Sachithanandan, Elad Sharon, Lavinia Spain, Katy Tsai, Mark Yarchoan, Kerry L Reynolds, Afreen Shariff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are an important class of cancer treatment. Endocrine immune-related adverse events (E-irAEs) account for a significant proportion of irAEs in ICI-treated patients. Diagnosing E-irAEs accurately and timely can be challenging in the absence of clear categorization and standardization across oncology, endocrinology and other specialties. While existing guidelines provide some broad directions to diagnosis and management, they lack clarity on irAE-specific symptom evaluation and work-up, as well as assessment of severity. These limitations can then impact triage, time-sensitive management and care escalation to specialists such as oncoendocrinologists. The objective of this study was to develop consensus-based statements on disease definitions for E-irAEs.A core working group of endocrinologists with expertise in the oncoendocrinology field drafted a survey with statements outlining the general approach to E-irAEs, disease definition and management approach of six specific diagnoses: ir-thyroiditis thyrotoxic phase, ir-thyroiditis hypothyroidism, ir-Graves’ disease, ir-hypophysitis, ir-adrenalitis and ir-type 1 diabetes mellitus. Severity grading tables were drafted for three disease categories—“thyroid”, “pituitary and adrenal”, and “diabetes mellitus”. A two-round modified Delphi process, using the RAND/University of California Los Angeles (RAND/UCLA) Appropriateness Method, was employed. In this process, a 25-member voting panel consisting of endocrinologists, oncologists and other specialists and healthcare providers with experience in management of ICI-treated patients and irAEs was recruited. The panel rated anonymously on usability, accuracy, appropriateness or agreement of 41 items on a 9-point scale in Survey 1, after which a meeting was convened. Statements were modified based on voting results from Survey 1 and Meeting 1, and the process was repeated in Survey 2 and Meeting 2. At the end of this process, consensus was achieved for all statements.Our study findings address the gap in standardized nomenclature, clinical, laboratory and radiological evaluations, and management principles of E-irAEs. With consensus achieved from a panel of experts from a variety of disciplines, we anticipate that the statements can form the basis for standardization of the diagnostic process and improvement of patient care.

Introduction

The use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has had a great impact on cancer care since the approval of the first ICI ipilimumab in 2011.1 ICIs are now approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment of at least 20 different types of cancers. In 2011, only 1.54% of patients with cancer in the USA were eligible for ICI treatment although this increased to 55.47% in 2023.2 ICIs currently approved by the FDA include monoclonal antibodies targeting cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4, eg, ipilimumab), the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1, eg, pembrolizumab, nivolumab), the programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1, eg, atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab) and the lymphocyte activation gene-3 (eg, relatlimab).3 4

ICIs, given as monotherapy or in combination, work by blocking immune cell surface receptors that would normally exert an inhibitor effect on the immune response. Blocking these natural regulatory signals enhances the immune response to target cells, but can also cause or augment an autoimmune response.5 Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) are therefore bystander effects of activation of immune system, and may impact one or more organ systems. Up to approximately 40% of patients in clinical trials and 30% of those in real-world settings have been noted to stop treatment with ICIs due to irAEs.68 In addition to cancer type and ICI treatment regimen, other factors that influence the risk of irAEs include age, body mass index, pre-existing autoimmune disease, chronic use of drugs such as proton pump inhibitors.9 10 While the risk for irAEs is significant with ICI treatment, the benefit of overall survival is generally considered to outweigh the risk. Furthermore, there is a marginal, but reproducible relationship between irAEs and improved cancer response.11 12 Studies evaluating survival in ICI-treated patients with melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer have shown improved survival in those who developed irAEs, even after accounting for immortal time bias.13 14 In addition, the timing of irAEs (early vs late) appears to influence treatment response and survival, with a more favorable outcome observed in patients with late-onset irAEs.15 However, specific to endocrine irAEs (E-irAEs), the relationship between toxicity and survival outcomes remains inconsistent across studies.1618

Guidelines have been developed to aid clinicians in the diagnosis and management of irAEs, but many challenges remain.1922 E-irAE is among the most common irAEs in ICI-treated patients.23 While most E-irAEs can be managed supportively, without the need for cessation or interruption of ICIs, their diagnoses require vigilant monitoring of clinical and biochemical changes, some of which may not be clinically apparent during routine oncology consultations due to the intrinsic dynamic nature of hormone secretion (eg, when a morning blood sample is required for hormones that are secreted in a diurnal rhythm). Given the barriers impeding the diagnosis of E-irAEs, there is an unmet need for improved identification and reporting of irAE data, for both clinicians and researchers.24 25 Recognizing the challenges that oncologists, endocrinologists, internists and other members of the healthcare team may face when diagnosing, reporting, and managing E-irAEs, a core working group was formed in 2022 to undertake the E-irAE Disease Definitions Project. This project is part of the “irAE Program”, in partnership with Project Data Sphere, an independent initiative of the CEO Roundtable on Cancer. The irAE program has successfully led to publications of neurologic and dermatologic Delphi-based disease definitions consensus.26 27 Here, we describe the execution and results of the “modified Delphi” consensus study, and provide discussions based on the latest evidence from the literature. We strive to provide standardized and clear recommendations on the diagnosis and management of E-irAEs for clinicians and researchers, thereby enabling timely and accurate reporting of these events. While the primary objective of the present initiative is to provide clear and practical directions to aid diagnosis of E-irAEs, the core working group has also included outlines on management principles. Decisions on detailed management of specific endocrinopathies, however, should be made in reference to the latest local, regional and international guidelines, and following consultation with endocrinologists as required.

Methods

The RAND/University of California Los Angeles (RAND/UCLA) Appropriateness Method (RAM), with adaptations made in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and logistic restrictions, was used in the development of the disease definitions and consensus guidance on E- irAEs.28 29 The RAM begins with a review of the latest literature and development of a survey. To achieve this, a core working group consisting of eight endocrinologists (AS, LZ, Y-MMC, CCh, EJG, O-PRH, JMa, ZQ) from the USA, Singapore and Australia, with expertise in management of E-irAEs (including thyroid, adrenal and pituitary dysfunction, and diabetes mellitus related to ICI treatment) was convened in November 2022. The objectives of the core working group were to draft the disease definitions survey, whose scope included the approach to E-irAEs, as well as diagnosis and management of core diagnoses. The core working group conducted an extensive literature review and communicated through virtual meetings over the subsequent 12 months. The list of conditions to be included as core diagnoses was proposed based on factors including incidence and disease burden.30 31 For each core diagnosis, sections on clinical presentation, evaluation, diagnostic criteria, and treatment were included. In addition, a severity grading system aligned with the CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events) V.5.0 was proposed.32

Following the development of proposed statements, an international expert voting panel was formed. Members of the expert voting panel were identified through their clinical and research expertise, as well as peer recommendation. The expertise of the voting panel was intended to represent the make-up of multidisciplinary teams involved in cancer care. An invitation outlining the objectives of the study and roles of panel members was sent to all prospective participants in October 2023. Participation in the voluntary survey was deemed to constitute informed consent. The expert voting panel underwent a two-round “modified Delphi” process.28 The first round commenced on October 23, 2023, and involved completion of an anonymous 77-item electronic survey hosted on Qualtrics, a secure web-based platform (Survey 1). It comprised 4 questions on demographics, 1 question on unmet needs in management of E-irAEs, 41 statements for which participants provided ratings on usability, accuracy, appropriateness and agreement of survey items on a 9-point scale, and 31 questions on further clarifications and free-text comments. Consensus of each item was determined by the attainment of both “appropriateness” and “agreement”.28 “Appropriateness” was achieved when the median score was in the 7–9 range (1–3 inappropriate; 4–6 uncertain; 7–9 appropriate). “Agreement” was achieved when less than one-third of the participants voted outside the 3-point range containing the median. After collating responses from Survey 1, a virtual meeting (Meeting 1) was conducted on November 14, 2023. It was facilitated by the core working group, with the objectives of enabling the panelists to achieve a common understanding of the survey items, presenting of results, clarifying doubts, and discussing/resolving areas of disagreement. Based on feedback from Survey 1 and Meeting 1, statements that did not achieve consensus and/or underwent major revision were included in a second round of survey (Survey 2, commenced on December 18, 2023). Other items that were not revised or underwent no more than minor revisions were also included for completeness. On collation of responses from Survey 2, Meeting 2 was conducted on January 9, 2024, to allow further comments and discussions as required.

Results

Invitation to participate in the disease definitions survey was sent to 53 international content experts, of whom 31 based in the USA and Australia accepted the invitation and joined the expert voting panel. 25 participants completed the first survey; 19 completed the second survey. The panel consisted of 11 endocrine healthcare providers, 8 oncology healthcare providers, 2 dermatologists, 1 gastrointestinal immunologist, 1 neuroimmunologist, 1 rheumatologist, and 1 internal medicine physician.

The first survey comprised 41 statements, for which participants rated on usability, accuracy, appropriateness or agreement of each survey item on a 9-point scale. The scope of these statements included general guidance on a spectrum of E-irAEs, timing of diagnosis, as well as symptoms, diagnostic tests, diagnostic criteria, treatment and severity grading for each condition (online supplemental table 1). Additionally, there were 31 further questions relating to these statements, allowing free-text responses for clarification and elaboration.

Consensus was achieved for all 41 statements in Survey 1. Items that generated a greater number of free-text feedback responses and/or received relatively greater number of responses outside the “appropriate” range (7–9 points) of rating were prioritized for discussion in Meeting 1. Following Meeting 1, statements that underwent major revision (list of core diagnoses; appropriateness of diagnostic work-up, accuracy and usability of diagnostic criteria for ir-hypophysitis and ir-adrenalitis; severity table for adrenal insufficiency (AI)) were listed for repeat voting in Survey 2. Survey 2 consisted of eight statements for 9-point scale voting, and 12 questions for free-text feedback. Statements that achieved consensus in Survey 1 were also presented to provide an overview of the complete document, though no voting was required from the panel for these statements. Medians, ranges and levels of agreement (percentage of panel voting within the 3-point range containing the median) from the two surveys are presented in the following consensus statements and summarized in online supplemental table 2.

Approach to endocrine irAEs

The recommended approach to patients with suspected E-irAEs is illustrated in figure 1, with key areas of evaluation and decision-making being the types of E-irAEs to suspect, presentation and initial management, time to onset (TTO), and considerations of alternative diagnoses.

Spectrum of E-irAEs

E-irAEs are frequently seen in patients treated with ICIs, typically in the first 6 months following ICI initiation.36 The incidence depends on the type of ICI used. ir-Thyroiditis and ir-hypophysitis are more commonly reported, particularly in combination therapy.30 Their management is largely supportive, including timely replacement of hormone deficiencies, and control of symptoms. While ir-T1DM, ir-adrenalitis and ir-Graves’ disease are less common, they may be associated with severe clinical manifestations if not diagnosed and managed promptly.

Presentation and initial management

The diagnosis of E-irAEs requires a thorough assessment of symptoms, including the temporal pattern of presentation following ICI initiation, as well as review of relevant laboratory and radiological investigations. In patients with cancer, symptoms of E-irAEs can sometimes overlap with those resulting from cancer itself, or non-ICI-related treatment effects. Sometimes, one or more hormone deficiencies may be apparent clinically or biochemically (eg, hypothyroidism, AI), but the etiology of the deficiencies cannot be conclusively established due to a lack of clinical data at the time of diagnosis. In these cases, we suggest management based on clinical judgment and endocrine consultation. Replacement of hormones that are deficient should be prioritized, as delayed treatment can be associated with rapid clinical deterioration and potential progression to endocrine emergencies (such as diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in ir-T1DM and adrenal crisis in ir-hypophysitis or ir-adrenalitis).

Unlike irAEs affecting other organ systems, E-irAEs can generally be managed without cessation of ICI therapy.37 High-dose glucocorticoid is not routinely administered, given the limited evidence supporting its efficacy in prevention or reversal of hormone deficiencies.16 Conversely, high-dose glucocorticoids may impede immunity, leading to tumor progression or in cases of ir-T1DM, exacerbate hyperglycemia further.38 39 The only exception to high-dose glucocorticoid use is in the setting of acute ir-hypophysitis when the patient has severe headache and/or optic chiasm compression whereby it can potentially rapidly reduce inflammation.19 40

Time to onset

The time periods from initiation of ICI to onset of E-irAEs vary by condition, but generally fall within the first 6 months of ICI initiation.40 To enable better appreciation of the clinical profiles published in the literature, irAE-specific TTO durations are included in the relevant sections of this article. In the drafting of the consensus statements, the core working group aimed to propose a duration of TTO for consideration of E-irAEs that would be sufficiently long to capture the majority of cases of true irAEs, while retaining an accurate reflection of typical temporal pattern of presentation. It should be noted that in the research setting, a majority of the published durations of TTO refer to the time between initiation of ICI to diagnosis of the irAE. Recognizing that in the real-world setting, there is great heterogeneity in the frequency of hormone assessments of ICI-treated patients, and that ICI treatment regimens can sometimes be interrupted or changed, the core working group opted to adopt a broad approach and expanded the initial algorithm to include patients on concurrent ICI therapy or less than 12 months from last dose of ICI. This recommendation achieved consensus at Survey 1 (median=8, range 1–9, 76% of votes within the range containing the median). Through Survey 1, Meeting 1 and Survey 2, there were panelists who advocated for either a longer or shorter duration than 12 months. Following extensive discussion and further review of literature, a decision was made to keep the suggested timeframe to 12 months. Although there are published reports of E-irAEs occurring more than 12 months after the last ICI dose,41 the core working group considered that the longer the interval from ICI exposure, the greater the number of confounding factors—such as disease progression or the introduction of other treatments that may also affect endocrine function. In such cases, alternative etiologies of the endocrine dysfunction should be considered with equal or even greater weight.

Consideration of alternative diagnoses

The suggested algorithm in figure 1 highlights the importance of ongoing review and revision of diagnosis, as the clinical course often becomes apparent with time. Furthermore, any patient receiving ICI treatment may develop one or more E-irAEs, or develop two phases of a single E-irAE. For example, an ICI-treated patient with newly diagnosed thyrotoxicosis may be considered for a variety of possible etiologies at the first instance, but the demonstration of self-limiting nature of thyrotoxicosis and subsequent development of hypothyroidism would point towards a diagnosis of ir-thyroiditis.

Core diagnosis disease definitions

Thyroid irAEs

The evaluation, diagnosis and management of thyroid irAEs are presented in tables13 respectively. Thyroid-irAEs are common side effects of ICIs. In clinical trials, the incidence of thyroid-irAEs has been reported to be 4.7%, 8.8% and 15.6% in ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4), nivolumab and pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1)-treated patients respectively.42 The incidence is higher in patients treated with combination or sequential ICIs.43 Data from prospective studies reveal that the incidence of thyroid dysfunction in the real-world setting is even higher—9.9% to 33.5% in patients on anti-PD-1, and 37 to 42% in patients on combination anti-PD-1/CTLA-4.4446

The current understanding of pathophysiology of ir-thyroditis suggests a destructive inflammatory process, which is characterized by a transient subclinical or overt thyrotoxic phase, followed by resolution of thyrotoxicosis, with or without progression to hypothyroidism.47 48 Diagnosis of thyroid-irAEs is sometimes challenging because of the high prevalence of thyroid disorders and autoimmunity in the general population.49 50 Furthermore, thyroid function tests (TFTs) in individuals with cancer may be confounded by acute illness, frequent use of iodine-based contrast for CT scans, surgery, external beam radiation, and concomitant administration of other thyroid-interfering pharmacological agents such as glucocorticoids and vascular endothelial growth factor-associated multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (eg, sunitinib, axitinib).51 Nonetheless, thyroid-irAEs have some unique clinical characteristics. The understanding of these features would allow managing healthcare providers to make a timely diagnosis.

The biochemical assessment of thyroid function involves measurement of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free thyroxine (T4) level and sometimes triiodothyronine (T3) levels.52 53 Interpretation should be made based on local laboratory reference ranges, and with awareness of local assay limitations. Limitations of free T3 assays include a lack of validation and robustness, when compared with free T4 assays. The measurement of total T3 is therefore preferred to free T3 in most settings.52 Specific assays for thyroid hormones and TSH may be susceptible to biotin interference, thereby leading to increased thyroid hormone levels and/or decreased TSH levels measured, depending on the assay used. To avoid biotin interference in susceptible assays, patients should be advised to stop biotin for a minimum of 72 hours prior to blood collection.54 55 Certain biomarkers such as antithyroid antibodies have been shown to be associated with higher risk of developing thyroid irAEs.56 The prevalence of baseline positivity of antithyroid antibodies such as anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody, however, is high in the general population.50 As the study of biomarkers in thyroid irAEs remains an evolving field at present, the role of measurement of antithyroid antibodies in the diagnosis of thyroid irAEs is currently unknown.

Given the high incidence of thyroid irAEs, a proactive screening approach is recommended for the diagnosis of ir-thyroiditis. Most guidelines suggest assessing thyroid function at baseline and subsequently every 4–6 weeks, or with each cycle of ICI treatment.19 20 22 57 The frequency of testing should be tailored to the individual patient’s clinical course. For example, patients in the thyrotoxic phase of ir-thyroiditis may require more frequent monitoring of both clinical symptoms and TFT, whereas those with established ir-thyroiditis and hypothyroidism, on a stable dose of thyroid hormone replacement months into ICI treatment, may be considered for less frequent monitoring.

ir-Thyroiditis–thyrotoxic phase

The thyrotoxic phase of ir-thyroiditis typically occurs early, within 8 weeks of initiation of ICIs. This can manifest as subclinical or overt thyrotoxicosis, and symptoms are frequently mild or absent.42 44 58 Antithyroid medications are not indicated, but supportive measures such as beta-blockers can be used for symptom relief. The thyrotoxic phase is usually self-limiting and resolves rapidly. Up to 75% of these patients subsequently progress to develop hypothyroidism.45 46 59 ir-Thyroiditis may also occur in patients with pre-existing thyroid disorders. In the context of pre-existing hypothyroidism in patients who still retain their thyroid glands (eg, hypothyroidism due to Hashimoto thyroiditis or previous radioiodine therapy, but not total thyroidectomy), the thyrotoxic phase of ir-thyroiditis may be characterized by a reduction or cessation in requirement of thyroid hormone replacement. There is limited literature describing the disease course of patients with well-controlled Graves’ disease who subsequently receive ICI. The trajectory of thyroid hormone levels may then guide decision making.

ir-Thyroiditis–hypothyroidism

ICI-induced hypothyroidism, which may or may not occur with a preceding clinically notable thyrotoxic phase, has a reported onset of 5.7–14 weeks after initiation of ICI.48 59 60 If hypothyroidism is overt (defined in this survey by a high TSH level with low free T4 level, or a TSH level >10 mIU/L regardless of free T4 level), thyroid hormone replacement should be commenced. A recent large retrospective cohort study demonstrated that 74% of patients who developed hypothyroidism as the initial thyroid-irAE needed to remain on long-term thyroid hormone replacement.48 Thyroid hormone replacement dose has been reported to be slightly lower than that for athyreotic individuals, at 1.45 mcg/kg/day versus 1.54 mcg/kg/day.61

ir-Graves’ disease

In addition to ir-thyroiditis, there are reports of Graves’ disease occurring after initiation of ICI. This is rare, and accounts for only 2% of cases of thyrotoxicosis in ICI-treated patients.62 Nonetheless, it is important to look for clinical features that distinguish Graves’ disease from the thyrotoxic phase of ir-thyroiditis, such as presence of diffusely enlarged goiter and Graves’ orbitopathy in all patients with thyrotoxicosis. In patients with significant thyrotoxic symptoms, assessment of severity and likelihood of thyroid storm should be made using the Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale or the Japanese Thyroid Association Criteria.63 64 TSH receptor antibody should be considered in patients with suspected ir-Graves’ disease, as the condition is managed differently from ir-thyroiditis. Clinically, ir-Graves’ disease is often associated with persistent thyrotoxicosis, and therefore may only be diagnosed when it is shown to not resolve with the time expected in the thyrotoxic phase of ir-thyroiditis. Rarely, evolution from thyrotoxicosis to hypothyroidism in patients with ir-Graves’ disease has been described.62 However, in patients without convincing features of Graves’ disease, whose thyrotoxicosis is self-limiting without need for antithyroid drugs, ir-thyroiditis should be the primary consideration. Of note, Graves’ orbitopathy can present in isolation following ICI initiation, without thyroid dysfunction, in patients with or without a prior history of Graves’ disease.6568

Pituitary and adrenal irAEs

The evaluation, diagnosis and management of pituitary and adrenal irAEs are presented in tables46 respectively. AI, predominantly in the form of glucocorticoid deficiency, is a common sequela of both ir-hypophysitis and ir-adrenalitis. Suspicion for AI is usually based on clinical features and this diagnosis is confirmed with a low morning cortisol, with or without the use of cosyntropin stimulation test. The adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) value differentiates between ir-hypophysitis and ir-adrenalitis. The timing of the blood draw is a key determinant of the interpretability of cortisol levels, as the secretion of cortisol follows a diurnal rhythm. Morning cortisol level should ideally be measured between 06:00 and 10:00 hours.69 As there is significant interindividual variability in morning cortisol levels in healthy adults, the cut-off value for diagnosis of AI is controversial and assay-dependent.69 Furthermore, minor discrepancies exist between published cortisol values in mcg/dL and nmol/L due to variations in measurement methodology and rounding. It has been shown that a morning cortisol level of <5 mcg/dL (138 nmol/L) best predicted AI, and a value of >13 mcg/dL (365 nmol/L as published in this paper) best predicted normal hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis function.70 Similarly, a study evaluating basal morning cortisol of three different immunoassays reported values of above 12.2 mcg/dL (336 nmol/L) to 18.3 mcg/dL (506 nmol/L) as predictors of normal response to cosyntropin stimulation, depending on the assay used.71 The applicability of these findings to ICI-treated patients with cancer has yet to be established. The Endocrine Society suggests a morning cortisol of <5 mcg/dL (138 nmol/L) in combination with ACTH may be used to make a preliminary diagnosis, and a peak cortisol response of <18 mcg/dL (500 nmol/L) following cosyntropin stimulation to be used to confirm the diagnosis of AI.69 These recommendations, however, were made in the context of suspected primary AI, and based on literature which included studies using older assays.72 In the context of recent-onset ir-hypophysitis, the cosyntropin stimulation test can be falsely normal. Furthermore, cortisol cut-off values have since been reevaluated and debated.72 To facilitate more efficient diagnosis and treatment, we have proposed cut-offs for consideration of ICI-related AI as morning cortisol level of <5 mcg/dL (138 nmol/L), or between 5 and 13 mcg/dL (138–359 nmol/L) with suboptimal response to cosyntropin stimulation. These values are by no means absolute and should not be regarded as the sole criterion for diagnosis of AI. In patients who are acutely unwell with clinical features suggestive of AI, a random cortisol measurement can be considered prior to urgent glucocorticoid replacement. There is, however, limited evidence supporting its use in diagnosis of AI.69 Likewise, a morning cortisol above 13 mcg/dL (359 nmol/L) should not be regarded as a definitive test to exclude AI, although it does decrease the likelihood substantially. Testing should be repeated if clinical suspicion remains. Shift workers may have an altered diurnal rhythm, and their cortisol levels should be interpreted with consideration of their usual sleep–wake hours.73 Current or recent use of glucocorticoid, regardless of route of administration, may also interfere with the accuracy of cortisol measurement.69 Whenever the clinical suspicion of AI remains, initiation of empirical glucocorticoid therapy, with a view to repeat cortisol level assessment, should be considered.

ir-Hypophysitis

ir-Hypophysitis is characterized by inflammation of the pituitary gland. The incidence of ir-hypophysitis is dependent on the type of ICI therapy used. It is more common in patients treated with anti-CTLA-4 agents (incidence 5.6%) and combination therapy (incidence 8.8–10.5%), than those treated with anti-PD1/PD-L1 monotherapy (incidence 0.5–1.1%).74

It should be noted that ir-hypophysitis and ir-adrenalitis are two distinct conditions and affect the HPA axis at different levels. ACTH deficiency leading to secondary AI is the most common endocrine consequence of ir-hypophysitis, and the effect is nearly always permanent.75 76 Less commonly, other pituitary hormones may be deficient, but AVP (arginine vasopressin) deficiency, previously known as central diabetes insipidus, is rare.76 ir-Adrenalitis causes primary AI, in which there is a deficiency in both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid. ACTH in these patients is expected to be high. It is important to distinguish between the two causes of AI in ICI-treated patients, as they involve distinct pathologies, have different clinical features, and hence require different management considerations.

The median time to diagnosis of ir-hypophysitis is 9–26 weeks, depending on the type of ICI used.76 Compared with screening of glucose and thyroid function, routine screening of adrenal and pituitary function can be challenging to execute. Many patients with ir-hypophysitis are diagnosed after presenting with suggestive symptoms or biochemical abnormalities.36 The most common symptoms reported include fatigue, headache and nausea.77 The diagnosis of ir-hypophysitis should only be made after careful considerations of potential drug exposures which may affect the HPA axis (eg, exogenous steroids including dexamethasone-based mouthwashes or peri-chemotherapeutic glucocorticoids used as antiemetics).75 76 78 In addition, laboratory and imaging results should be reviewed in the context of oncological history. Imaging of the pituitary gland may be useful to exclude the presence of pituitary metastasis, but an absence of pituitary enlargement or atrophy does not exclude the diagnosis of ir-hypophysitis. Radiological changes in ir-hypophysitis have been reported to be highly variable, and can also resolve with time.79 Abnormalities such as pituitary enlargement are more commonly seen in anti-CTLA-4-treated patients with ir-hypophysitis (up to 80%) than those treated with anti-PD/PD-L1 (less than 20%).80

The diagnostic process of ir-hypophysitis or ir-adrenalitis versus other etiologies of AI may be a dynamic one. The cause of a low cortisol level may not be immediately apparent without further testing such as ACTH, ancillary tests and imaging. Once AI has been diagnosed (or is suspected in unwell patients), regardless of the certainty of its association with ICI, treatment should not be delayed and glucocorticoid replacement should be initiated promptly. Endocrinology consultation can help guide additional confirmatory testing to determine the underlying etiology. If ir-hypophysitis is diagnosed, thyroid and gonadal function should also be assessed, but the accuracy of these may be confounded by acute illness and therefore should be followed over time.

ir-Adrenalitis

ir-Adrenalitis, characterized by inflammation of the adrenal gland, is a rare side effect of ICI treatment, with reported incidence of less than 1%.30 81 ir-Adrenalitis causes primary AI, in which there is a deficiency in both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid. In the setting of newly diagnosed AI in ICI-treated patients, ir-hypophysitis is a much more common cause than ir-adrenalitis.82 Based on limited case reports, the median time to diagnosis for ir-adrenalitis is 10 weeks.83 Patients with ir-adrenalitis would require both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement. Imaging is not needed to make a diagnosis of primary AI, but may be useful to exclude other adrenal pathologies.

Pancreatic endocrine-irAEs

Pancreatic irAEs can present in endocrine (ir-T1DM) and/or exocrine (ir-pancreatitis) dysfunction.84 85 The subsequent section outlines the evaluation, diagnosis and treatment of ir-T1DM (boxes13, table 7).

ir-Type 1 diabetes mellitus

ir-T1DM (also known as ICI-associated autoimmune diabetes mellitus, checkpoint inhibitor associated diabetes mellitus/ CPI-DM or checkpoint inhibitor associated diabetes mellitus/ CIADM), is a subtype of diabetes characterized by beta-cell destruction and insulin deficiency.86 ICI is a rare cause of diabetes in ICI-treated individuals, with a reported incidence of 0.2–1.4%.87 Though termed as “ir-T1DM” in the current consensus statement, ICI-related diabetes mellitus is a distinct entity from classical T1DM. ir-T1DM has been reported to have a higher rate of DKA at first presentation, a lower C-peptide level at initial diagnosis and a lower rate of islet autoantibody positivity when compared with classical T1DM.35 The diagnosis of ir-T1DM can be challenging as hyperglycemia is commonly seen in patients with cancer due to the high prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), side effects of anticancer treatments and exposure to glucocorticoids. High-dose glucocorticoids are often used as part of chemotherapy regimens, or to treat non-endocrine irAEs from ICIs, thereby further increasing the risk of hyperglycemia. As approximately 70% of patients with ir-T1DM present with DKA, timely diagnosis and insulin initiation are crucial.87

The median TTO of ir-T1DM has been reported to be 2–4.8 months after ICI initiation, with an earlier onset in patients who are glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65) autoantibody positive.33 88 89 A history of pre-diabetes or T2DM does not preclude the diagnosis of ir-T1DM.33 88 GAD65 autoantibody has been reported to be positive in 40–43% of patients with ir-T1DM, compared with approximately 1% in general population, 4.2% in T2DM, and 70–80% in classical T1DM.358992 Given the limited sensitivity and specificity of islet autoantibodies for ir-T1DM, a greater emphasis should be placed on the clinical presentation and beta cell function of ICI-treated patients with hyperglycemia. ir-T1DM has been described to have a fulminant clinical course in the majority of cases, where greater than two-thirds of patients present with life-threatening ketoacidosis at diagnosis.87 In addition, 91.6% of patients have very little insulin production, as demonstrated by a low C-peptide level (<0.4 nmol/L) in spite of hyperglycemia, which necessitates the use of exogenous insulin therapy.35 87 Patients with ir-T1DM generally do not have the “honeymoon” phase that is seen with classical T1DM during which there is some endogenous insulin production in the early years after diagnosis.35 The administration of glucocorticoids for other concurrent irAEs can delay the diagnosis of ir-T1DM, as hyperglycemia may be initially attributed to glucocorticoid effects and patients are treated with insulin prior to assessment of islet cell function or islet autoimmunity. Ketoacidosis can manifest as glucocorticoids are weaned and insulin is ceased. Compared to pancreatic endocrine insufficiency, exocrine dysfunction is a rarer irAE, which may lead to fat malabsorption, weight loss and steatorrhea.93

To enable prompt identification of ir-T1DM, ICI-treated patients should undergo routine metabolic panels including glucose measurement prior to each infusion, as well as glycated hemoglobin at ICI initiation. ir-T1DM should be suspected in any patient with emergent or worsening hyperglycemia (>200 mg/dL or 11.1 mmol/L) following ICI treatment, especially in those with no history of diabetes and no new prescription of glucocorticoids, and in those with previously well-controlled T2DM. Careful interpretation of paired glucose and C-peptide levels provides information on beta cell function and can be useful in ir-T1DM diagnosis.94 95 C-peptide reflects the endogenous pancreatic beta cell reserve, an inappropriately low or low-normal C-peptide in the setting of hyperglycemia would point towards a diagnosis of ir-T1DM. A C-peptide of ≤0.3 nmol/L in the setting of hyperglycemia is consistent with reduced beta cell reserve; a proposed cut-off of <0.5 nmol/L takes into account the inter-assay performance of C-peptide and the imprecision at low level.89 96 Where possible, interpretation should be made based on local laboratory reference ranges and the corresponding glucose levels. As the decline in beta cell function is a dynamic process, the biochemical signature of ir-T1DM may not be apparent if assessed early in the course of the disease. A detectable C-peptide should not be regarded as a reassuring result to exclude the diagnosis of ir-T1DM, as C-peptide level can decline rapidly in ir-T1DM.35 Conversely, transiently low C-peptide may be seen in patients with glucotoxicity, whereby chronic hyperglycemia leads to beta cell dysfunction and impaired insulin secretion.94 97 As such, patients with suspected ir-T1DM should undergo repeated glucose and C-peptide testing within a month, and again after at least 1 month of euglycemia as required, to allow longitudinal assessment of beta cell function.87

Once ir-T1DM is suspected, or if the etiology of hyperglycemia is unclear, the patient should be considered for urgent insulin initiation, to minimize the risk of hyperglycemic crisis.34 After the patient has been stabilized, the diagnosis of ir-T1DM can then be reviewed and revised at a later time as required. Given the insulin-deficiency nature of ir-T1DM, intensive glucose monitoring and insulin titration, along with a multidisciplinary team are key components of comprehensive management. Patients with suspected or confirmed ir-T1DM should therefore be referred promptly to an endocrinologist with expertise in T1DM management.

Other endocrine irAEs

We recognize that other E-irAEs have been described, such as ir-hypoparathyroidism, ir-orchitis and ir-lipodystrophy.98100 The core working group felt that due to the rarity of these irAEs, the Delphi consensus methodology would not be suitable for development of a consensus statement on the diagnosis and management of these conditions at present.

Discussion

This consensus-based disease definitions initiative for E-irAEs is an international collaboration undertaken by eight endocrinologists from a core working group and 25 specialists and healthcare providers who participated as panelists. Through a two-round modified Delphi process, consensus was achieved for all statements on the initial approach, list of core diagnoses and the usability, accuracy, appropriateness and agreement of symptoms, diagnostic tests, diagnostic criteria, treatment and severity grading of each diagnosis. This methodology has been successfully used in the development of consensus-based disease definitions of neurologic and dermatologic irAEs.26 27 The key strength of the present study is the involvement of a large panel of experts with extensive experience in management of irAEs. We were able to maintain a high response rate throughout the study—81% of the 31 invited participants took part in Survey 1, and 76% of panelists from Survey 1 took part in Survey 2. With a comparable representation of endocrinologists and oncologists in the panel (44% and 32% respectively), the proposed disease definitions attempted to address the needs to balance accuracy of diagnosis, and feasibility of incorporating endocrine testing in the care of patients with cancer who often have complex medical issues.

Areas of controversy remain in the diagnosis and management of E-irAEs, some of which have been discussed in the sections on individual E-irAEs. TTO is often the first factor to be considered when diagnosing E-irAEs. We proposed that any patient on concurrent ICI therapy, or within 12 months from the last dose of ICI, to be considered for E-irAE. This was not intended as an absolute criterion—data from both clinical trials and retrospective studies have suggested that E-irAEs are most likely to occur in the first 6 months from the commencement of ICI.101 A recent retrospective study further supported these findings, reporting that 94% of E-irAEs requiring hospitalization occurred within 12 months of ICI initiation.102 For most E-irAEs, the diagnosis is made based on a combination of assessment of clinical context and features, and biochemistry. There is no single diagnostic test that would confirm the diagnosis and etiology. Another area of contention was the cut-off values for hormone measurements. For instance, there is a lack of universally accepted morning cortisol cut-offs for the diagnosis of AI. Cortisol values at extreme ends have been proposed to suggest that AI is likely or unlikely.69 While a cosyntropin stimulation test traditionally provides clearer reflection of adrenal function in the context of primary AI, especially in patients with intermediate morning cortisol values, it can be cumbersome and logistically challenging to perform in non-endocrine centers. Its major limitation in diagnosing recent onset secondary AI is most pertinent in ICI-treated patients with cancer. We acknowledge these limitations and have hence de-emphasized the reliance on cosyntropin stimulation testing, and instead suggested morning cortisol values to enable prompt diagnosis of ir-hypophysitis or ir-adrenalitis. We also recognize that there are centers which use a different morning cortisol cut-off to identify patients with possible AI, likely due to the availability of validated local reference ranges. We have highlighted the importance of consideration of empirical glucocorticoid coverage and repeat biochemical assessment based on clinical judgment. A similar biochemical test that generated discussion during the meetings was C-peptide level, in ir-T1DM. While C-peptide values at extreme ends can indicate insulin deficiency (when low) and insulin resistance (when high), the levels can be confounded by glucotoxicity, and can change along the clinical course of ir-T1DM. Both these conundrums highlight the value of repeating hormone measurements as part of dynamic assessment for all patients with a suspected E-irAE.

In addition to recognition of current areas of controversy, we also acknowledge the limitations of the Delphi process.103 There is a variety of “modified” Delphi Methods being used in generating clinical recommendations. Panelists remained anonymous during the voting process to encourage fair contribution and incorporation of results. The drawback then was the difficulty in tracing the origin of comments that required further clarifications and discussion, though two live meetings were conducted to delve deeper into the comments in the presence of a multidisciplinary panel. Given the limited scope of this document, it is not possible to address all variations of clinical characteristics and differing practices in global institutions. Clinical judgment and discretion should always be exercised, and multidisciplinary care is recommended in the approach to ICI-treated patients with suspected irAEs, with early referral to the endocrinologist as needed. Additionally, while our recommendations are based on the literature and guidelines available prior to the conclusion of our study in January 2024, we advise that they be interpreted in conjunction with the most recent society statements. For instance, a joint consensus on the management of hyperglycemic crises was published in August 2024, revising the glucose cut-off for diagnosis of DKA to ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L).104 These updates underscore the evolving nature of clinical practice and the importance of applying our findings within the context of current recommendations.

Moving forward, there has been a growing interest in risk stratification and prognostication of ICI-treated patients who may be susceptible to irAEs. For instance, antithyroid antibodies, antipituitary antibodies, islet autoantibodies, anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies, human leukocyte antigen etc have been described as potential biomarkers for irAEs.10 However, these biomarkers are not unique to irAEs and may be present in other endocrine disorders. While it is beyond the scope of the present study to make recommendations on the routine use of biomarkers in management with E-irAEs, future research may clarify and enhance their clinical utility.

Although the association between E-irAEs and improved survival has not been consistently demonstrated across all studies, emerging evidence suggests that ir-thyroiditis, ir-hypophysitis, and ir-T1DM appear to have a correlation with more favorable outcomes in ICI treatment for certain cancer types.10 12 16 18 105 106 Recognizing that patients who develop E-irAEs may experience longer survival therefore strengthens the case for timely and appropriate management of these toxicities. Optimizing care in this context not only supports treatment continuation but may also contribute to better overall quality of life for affected patients.

As the role of ICI in cancer treatment continues to expand, we expect that this set of consensus-based E-irAE disease definitions can aid both clinicians and researchers in timely diagnosis and management of E-irAEs. By bringing together an international group of endocrinologists, oncologists and physicians and healthcare providers with experience in managing irAEs, we have worked to optimize the clinical applicability and safety of the consensus statements. Importantly, consensus definitions not only play a critical role in paving the way for accurate diagnosis and management in clinical care, but also provide the basis for robust and standardized data collection in clinical research. This would potentially accelerate our understanding of irAE disease models and outcomes. A similar model can be extrapolated and applied to other oncological toxicities, thereby streamlining clinical decision-making, and improving patient care.
