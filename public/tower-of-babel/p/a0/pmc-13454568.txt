Explainable ensemble machine learning for predicting diabetes mellitus and identifying key risk factors: a population-based study in northern Bangladesh
Authors: Most Nusrat Jahan Resma, Md Abdul Kayum, Pankaj Bhowmik, Md. Earfan Ali Khondaker, Md. Kaderi Kibria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus (DM) is an escalating global public health concern, with a rapidly increasing burden in low- and middle-income countries, including Bangladesh. Despite its growing prevalence and associated complications such as cardiovascular disease, kidney failure and stroke, comprehensive evidence on its determinants and predictive modeling at the population level remains limited. This study aimed to predict the DM and identify its associated risk factors using ensemble machine learning (EML) approaches among adults in northern Bangladesh. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 1408 adults in Dinajpur district between March 25 and June 5, 2025, using structured and pilot-tested questionnaires administered through face-to-face interviews. Feature selection was performed using Recursive Feature Elimination, Random Forest importance and Best First Search methods. Six machine learning models were developed, followed by a stacking ensemble model to enhance predictive performance. Model evaluation was based on accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP analysis, and findings were validated using multivariable logistic regression. The prevalence of DM was 15.1% in the study population. Among individual models, LightGBM demonstrated the highest performance (accuracy: 89.44%; AUC: 0.958 [95% CI 0.945–0.973]), followed by XGBoost (accuracy: 88.69%; AUC: 0.955 [95% CI 0.945–0.972]). The stacking ensemble model outperformed all base learners, achieving an accuracy of 91.67% and an AUC of 0.967 (95% CI 0.957–0.981). SHAP analysis identified age, family history of diabetes, BMI, weight, dietary behaviors (particularly low vegetable intake and added salt/sugar), family income, and gender as key predictors. Multivariable logistic regression confirmed these findings, showing that advancing age especially 51–60 years, female gender, family history of diabetes, hypertension, kidney disease and low vegetable consumption were independently associated with DM. Therefore, stacking-based ensemble learning significantly improves the predictive accuracy of DM while enabling robust identification of key risk factors. The consistency between machine learning and traditional statistical approaches strengthens the validity of the findings. These results highlight the importance of integrating advanced analytical methods into public health research to support early detection, targeted prevention, and evidence-based decision-making in resource-constrained settings such as northern Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55482-0.

Introduction

Diabetes milletus (DM) is a major non-communicable disease and a leading contributor to global morbidity and mortality1. It is characterized by chronic hyperglycemia and is associated with severe long-term complications, including cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, neuropathy, stroke, and vision impairment2,3. The growing burden of DM poses substantial challenges to health systems worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where healthcare resources are often limited4. Globally, the prevalence of diabetes has risen sharply over recent decades. According to the WHO, the number of individuals living with diabetes increased from approximately 200 million in 1990 to over 800 million in 20225. Similarly, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reports that nearly one in ten adults worldwide is affected by diabetes, with projections indicating continued growth6. The burden is particularly pronounced in Asia, driven by rapid urbanization, population aging, and lifestyle transitions7. In Bangladesh, diabetes has emerged as a critical public health concern. National estimates suggest that approximately 8–10% of adults are living with diabetes, with a significant proportion remaining undiagnosed8. Rural and semi-urban populations are especially vulnerable due to limited access to healthcare, low health literacy, and insufficient awareness of preventive behaviors9. These disparities underscore the need for region-specific investigations to better understand the epidemiology and determinants of T2D. Previous studies in Bangladesh and elsewhere have primarily relied on traditional statistical approaches, such as logistic regression, to estimate prevalence and identify risk factors10–12. While these methods are valuable, they may be limited in capturing complex, nonlinear relationships among predictors. In recent years, machine learning (ML) techniques including decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, and gradient boosting have been increasingly applied to diabetes prediction, demonstrating improved predictive performance13–18. Recent studies have also incorporated explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques such as SHAP to improve model interpretability and identify clinically relevant risk factors16. Several studies have reported strong predictive accuracy using ML models. For instance, artificial neural networks have achieved accuracy up to 88.6%19, while k-nearest neighbor models have demonstrated accuracy exceeding 96% with high sensitivity and specificity. Advanced approaches such as generative adversarial networks and ensemble tree-based models have further improved classification performance, achieving area under the curve (AUC) values as high as 0.9720,21.

Despite these advancements, most existing studies have focused on single-model approaches, with limited exploration of EML techniques that combine multiple models to enhance predictive robustness. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no prior community-based study in northern Bangladesh has integrated stacking-based EML, SHAP-based explainability and traditional multivariable logistic regression for diabetes prediction and risk factor identification using real-world population-level data. This represents a critical research gap, as ensemble approaches are particularly effective in improving generalizability and capturing complex interactions among socio-demographic and behavioral variables. The northern region, particularly Dinajpur district, is characterized by distinct socio-demographic and economic challenges, including lower educational attainment, limited healthcare infrastructure, and suboptimal lifestyle practices, which may contribute to an elevated risk of DM22. However, comprehensive community-based studies integrating advanced analytical techniques in this region remain scarce. Therefore, this study aims to predict the DM among adults in northern Bangladesh, identify key socio-demographic and behavioral risk factors using EML approaches. Then finally, validate these findings through traditional multivariable logistic regression analysis. By integrating advanced predictive modeling with conventional statistical approaches, this study seeks to generate robust, interpretable, and generalizable evidence for diabetes risk stratification. The findings of this study have important implications for public health policy and practice. They can inform targeted screening, early detection, and prevention strategies, particularly among underserved populations. Furthermore, this research aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being), contributing to efforts aimed at reducing premature mortality from non-communicable diseases through evidence-based and data-driven interventions in Bangladesh23,24.

Results

Demographic and behavioral characteristics of the respondents

A total of 1408 adults participated in the study, with the majority residing in rural areas (77.9%) and a slightly higher proportion of males (52.3%) than females (Table 1). Most participants were married (88.4%), and the largest age group was 31–40 years (25.6%), followed by 41–50 years (22.2%) and 51–60 years (18.5%). Educational attainment varied, with 25.1% having no formal education and 15.9% completing higher education (honors/diploma). The predominant occupations included housewives (41.8%), agriculture (12.4%), and employment-based jobs (11.2%). Nearly half of the participants (47.4%) reported a monthly family income below 20,000 BDT, while only a small proportion (5.2%) earned above 55,000 BDT; additionally, 58.2% reported having outstanding loans. Regarding nutritional status, 61.8% of participants had normal body mass index (BMI), whereas 28.3% were overweight and 6.6% were obese. Most respondents identified as Muslim (74.4%).

Behavioral characteristics of the participants revealed several lifestyle patterns associated with potential health risks (Table S1 and Table 2). Approximately 20.1% of respondents reported ever smoking, of whom the majority (87.3%) were current smokers, indicating a high continuation of smoking behavior; among these, nearly half (47.4%) had been smoking for 3–5 years, while 29.9% reported a duration exceeding six years. Similarly, 31.4% of participants reported consuming betel leaf or gul, with 86.2% continuing current use, and most users (62.0%) reporting consumption duration of 1–5 years. Tea or coffee consumption was highly prevalent, with 57.3% reporting ever use and 96.4% of them currently consuming these beverages; most participants consumed 1–2 cups daily (60.2%), followed by 3–5 cups (35.4%). Dietary behaviors indicated that 52.4% of participants consumed sweets regularly, while meat intake was generally moderate, with 69.8% consuming meat 1–2 days per week and only a small proportion consuming it daily. Fruit consumption was relatively low, with 56.0% reporting intake on only 1–2 days per week and 10.6% reporting no fruit consumption at all. In contrast, vegetable intake was comparatively adequate, with 81.0% consuming vegetables 6–7 days per week. Regarding physical activity–related behaviors, a large majority of participants (83.2%) relied on engine vehicles for transportation, whereas only 10.0% used bicycles and 6.8% traveled on foot, indicating limited routine physical activity. Additionally, 30.8% of respondents reported adding extra salt to their meals.

Prevalence of DM and distribution of health conditions

Figure 1 presents the overall prevalence of diabetes and the distribution of related health conditions among the study participants. The prevalence of diabetes was 15.1%, while 84.9% of participants were non-diabetic, with approximately one-third reporting a family history of diabetes. The figure also highlights the substantial burden of comorbid conditions and risk factors within the population, including hypertension (26.6%), cardiovascular disease (8.5%), and kidney disease (4.3%). Figure S2 illustrates the age-specific prevalence of diabetes. The proportion of individuals with diabetes increased progressively with age, from 7% in the 25–30 age group to 26% among participants aged 51–60 years, followed by a slight decline to 18% in those older than 60 years. Conversely, the proportion of non-diabetic individuals was highest in the 31–40 age group (93%) and lowest in the 51–60 age group (74%), indicating a clear age-related trend in diabetes risk. Figure S3 depicts diabetes prevalence by gender. Among females, 18% were diagnosed with diabetes, whereas a majority of males (87%) were non-diabetic.

Risk factor identification for DM prediction

Fifteen variables were consistently identified as key risk factors for DM in the study population. Across the feature selection methods applied, RFE suggested 19 features, BFS identified 17, and RF highlighted 18 important predictors (Table S2). Fifteen features were common to at least two methods, indicating their robust association with DM. These key risk factors encompass socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, education, family income, occupation), anthropometric measures (height, weight, BMI), lifestyle and dietary behaviors (fruit and vegetable intake, sweet consumption, tea/coffee intake, added salt use), and family medical history (diabetes and cardiovascular disease), as summarized in Table 3.

Among the individual models, LightGBM and XGBoost demonstrated the highest predictive capability. LightGBM achieved 89.44% accuracy, 89.85% F1-score, and an AUC of 0.958 (95% CI 0.945–0.973), while XGBoost reached 88.69% accuracy, 87.18% F1-score, and an AUC of 0.955 (95% CI 0.945–0.972). Random Forest also performed robustly, yielding 85.65% accuracy, 85.47% F1-score, and an AUC of 0.939 (95% CI 0.923–0.954). SVM showed moderate but balanced performance across metrics, with 79.39% accuracy and 79.73% F1-score, whereas Decision Tree and Logistic Regression displayed comparatively lower predictive effectiveness. The stacking ensemble model outperformed all individual algorithms, achieving the highest accuracy (91.67%), precision (94.79%), recall (86.07%), F1-score (90.02%), and AUC of 0.967 (95% CI 0.957–0.981). The ROC curves (Fig. 2) demonstrate that the stacking model provides superior discriminative capability compared with all baseline models. This confirms that integrating multiple classifiers in a stacking framework enhances DM prediction by leveraging the complementary strengths of individual models. However, the ensemble approach confirms that combining multiple ML models can substantially enhance DM prediction in the study population.

Key risk factors identification for DM

To interpret the contributions of individual predictors to diabetes risk, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was applied, providing a robust framework for understanding feature importance in ML models. In the SHAP summary plot (Fig. 3), the x-axis represents SHAP values, indicating both the magnitude and direction of each feature’s influence on DM risk. Positive SHAP values denote increased risk, while negative values indicate a protective effect. Feature values are color-coded, with red representing high values and blue representing low values, enabling simultaneous visualization of feature intensity and impact. The analysis identified multiple socio-demographic, behavioral, and anthropometric predictors of DM. Age, family history of diabetes, weight, family income, and adding salt/sugar in tea emerged as the strongest positive predictors of DM risk, highlighting their critical role in disease predisposition within the study population. Lifestyle factors such as vegetable intake and adding extra salt showed moderate negative associations, while other factors including BMI, height, education, family size, gender, fruit intake, and occupation contributed smaller but statistically significant effects.

Table 5 quantifies these findings by integrating correlation coefficients, feature importance scores, IRS, SHAP values, and statistical significance. Age demonstrated the strongest overall influence, with a correlation of 0.208, importance score of 0.151, IRS of 2.33, and SHAP value of 1.28. Family history of diabetes also had a pronounced effect (correlation 0.240, SHAP 0.802), reflecting the hereditary contribution to DM risk. Behavioral factors such as vegetable intake (correlation − 0.090, IRS 1.35, SHAP 0.354) and adding extra salt (correlation − 0.202, IRS 0.897, SHAP 0.263) exhibited inverse associations with moderate contributions. Among anthropometric variables, BMI (SHAP 0.464), weight (SHAP 0.529), and height (SHAP 0.368) showed modest effects, while socio-demographic variables including gender (SHAP 0.471), family income (SHAP 0.521), education (SHAP 0.454), and family size (SHAP 0.582) demonstrated meaningful but comparatively lower contributions. Collectively, these results indicate that age and family history are the dominant risk factors, whereas behavioral, anthropometric, and socio-economic variables exert moderate yet statistically significant influences (p values mostly < 0.001).

Age-specific trends in DM prevalence

Given that age emerged as a key risk factor for DM in the SHAP and feature importance analyses (Sect. 3.5, Table 6), we further examined diabetes prevalence across specific age groups. Figure 4 illustrates that the prevalence of DM increased progressively with age, reaching its peak in the 51–60 years group before declining slightly in participants over 60. Gender-specific patterns were also observed: females exhibited higher prevalence in younger age groups, whereas males predominated among older adults. Conversely, non-diabetic cases were most frequent in the 31–40 years age group and gradually decreased with advancing age. These findings underscore the critical influence of age on diabetes risk and highlight the importance of targeted screening and preventive strategies in older adults, particularly in high-risk populations identified in the current study.

Validation of EML-based findings using multivariable logistic regression

To validate the EML–based results, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of DM (Table S3). Consistent with EML findings, age emerged as a strong and graded risk factor for DM (p < 0.001). Compared to participants aged 25–30 years, the odds of DM increased progressively across older age groups: 31–40 years (AOR = 2.042; 95% CI 1.011–4.121), 41–50 years (AOR = 3.360; 95% CI 1.671–6.756), 51–60 years (AOR = 6.380; 95% CI 3.115–13.053), and > 60 years (AOR = 4.014; 95% CI 1.812–8.889). Females demonstrated a higher likelihood of DM than males, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.642 (95% CI 1.169–3.102; p = 0.005). Family history of diabetes was strongly associated with DM (AOR = 4.173; 95% CI 2.864–6.081; p < 0.001), confirming hereditary influence. Comorbid conditions, including hypertension (AOR = 2.306; 95% CI 1.616–3.290; p < 0.001), kidney disease (AOR = 2.470; 95% CI 1.306–4.672; p < 0.001), and cardiovascular disease (AOR = 1.895; 95% CI 1.302–2.756; p < 0.001), were independently associated with higher DM risk. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as lower vegetable consumption were linked with increased odds of DM (AOR = 1.719; 95% CI 1.164–2.537; p = 0.007). Overall, the multivariable logistic regression analysis corroborates the EML-derived results, emphasizing that advancing age particularly 51–60 years, family history of diabetes, comorbid conditions and certain dietary habits are robust predictors of DM in this population. These findings highlight the cumulative impact of demographic, hereditary, and lifestyle factors on diabetes risk.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify key risk factors and predict DM among adults in Dinajpur district, Bangladesh, using EML algorithms. Our analysis revealed a T2D prevalence of 15.1%, highlighting a substantial burden of this non-communicable disease in both urban and rural settings. Identifying current risk enables targeted interventions for susceptible individuals, while predictive modeling supports proactive screening and lifestyle modification to mitigate disease progression1,2. The prevalence observed in our study is consistent with a growing trend in Bangladesh. According to the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2022, the age-standardized prevalence of diabetes has risen to 20.4%, up from 10.7% in 2011 and 12.8% in 2017/18, representing a nearly 90% relative increase over the past decade25–27. Other local studies reported a wide prevalence range: 9.2% of adults with diabetes and 13.3% with prediabetes (BDHS 2017–18)28. while age-adjusted prevalence estimates ranged from 9.7% to 9.9% in other Bangladeshi cohorts29,30. Comparatively, DM prevalence in the Middle East is substantially higher, reaching 33.6% in Bahrain and 29.1% in Saudi Arabia31–36, whereas Iran and Yemen reported lower rates (~ 9–10%)35. Our findings underscore a particularly high DM burden in northern Bangladesh, necessitating region-specific preventive measures.

Using SHAP analysis, this study identified several key socio-demographic, behavioral, and anthropometric risk factors associated with T2D, including age, family history of diabetes, BMI, weight, height, family income, dietary behaviors (fruit and vegetable intake, and addition of extra salt or sugar), occupation, and gender. Among these, age and family history of diabetes emerged as the most influential predictors, reflecting the combined effects of genetic susceptibility and age-related metabolic changes37–39. Consistent with this, T2D prevalence increased progressively with age, peaking in the 51–60 years group, with a relatively higher burden among females at younger ages and males at older ages40. These findings align with both regional and global evidence identifying age as a dominant determinant of diabetes risk41,42. In addition to non-modifiable factors, several modifiable risk factors were identified. Obesity, hypertension, and dietary practices showed significant associations with T2D43,44. Notably, SHAP results indicated that low vegetable intake and the addition of extra salt or sugar were linked to increased diabetes risk, underscoring the role of unhealthy dietary patterns45. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting BMI and dietary behaviors as major contributors to T2D development43,44. The robustness of these findings was further supported by multivariable logistic regression analysis, which confirmed that advancing age (particularly 51–60 years), female gender, family history of diabetes, hypertension, kidney disease, and low vegetable consumption were independently associated with DM. The magnitude of association was substantial, with adjusted odds ratios ranging from 1.642 for females (95% CI 1.169–3.102) to 6.38 for individuals aged 51–60 years (95% CI 3.115–13.053). This strong agreement between ML outputs and traditional statistical modeling enhances the validity and interpretability of the study findings.

ML approaches have been widely applied for disease prediction due to their ability to capture complex, nonlinear relationships within high-dimensional data46,47. Previous studies have reported strong predictive performance using different algorithms. For instance, ANN achieved an accuracy of 88.6%48, while RF models reported accuracy as high as 94.25% with an AUC of 0.95 under cross-validation settings49. Logistic Regression has also demonstrated moderate predictive capability, with reported accuracies around 0.7748,50,51. Similarly, studies using the PIMA dataset reported exceptionally high accuracy (98.35%) under controlled conditions52, although such performance may reflect dataset-specific characteristics rather than real-world applicability. Ensemble approaches, particularly stacking models, have shown improved predictive performance compared with single classifiers, with reported AUC values ranging from 0.832 to 0.90053–55. In comparison, the present study achieved superior performance, with the stacking ensemble model reaching an accuracy of 91.67% and an AUC of 0.96, outperforming all individual models. Among base learners, XGBoost and LightGBM demonstrated the strongest performance, which is consistent with their ability to effectively handle nonlinear relationships and feature interactions56. Variations in model performance across studies may be attributed to differences in sample size, population characteristics, feature selection strategies, and data quality. The improved performance observed in this study can be attributed to the integration of diverse predictors, robust feature selection techniques, and advanced optimization strategies. By incorporating socio-demographic, behavioral, and clinical variables, the models were able to capture the multifactorial nature of DM more effectively than traditional approaches. A key strength and unique contribution of this study lies in the integration of explainable EML with conventional statistical validation. By combining stacking-based predictive modeling with SHAP interpretation and multivariable logistic regression, the study not only achieved high predictive accuracy but also ensured transparency and clinical interpretability of identified risk factors57. However, this study highlights the combined influence of modifiable and non-modifiable factors on DM risk in a semi-urban and rural population in northern Bangladesh. These findings emphasize the importance of early screening, lifestyle modification, and targeted prevention strategies. The application of EML models provides a promising tool for risk stratification and decision support, enabling healthcare providers to identify high-risk individuals and implement timely interventions.

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference between identified risk factors and DM. Self-reported behavioral data may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. In addition, the study was conducted in a single district of northern Bangladesh, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Although robust ML approaches were applied, external validation using independent datasets was not performed. Furthermore, some potentially relevant clinical and biochemical predictors were unavailable and could not be incorporated into the models. Future research should include larger nationally representative cohorts, longitudinal study designs, external validation procedures, and additional predictive variables to further improve model generalizability and predictive performance.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among adults in the northern region of Bangladesh between March 25 and June 5, 2025. The study focused on Dinajpur District, a predominantly rural area characterized by limited healthcare access, lower educational attainment and suboptimal preventive health practices. Those participants had undergone blood glucose testing at a certified diagnostic or medical center within the past one month and were able to provide valid medical reports were included and those have not any recent medical report are excluded.

Sampling technique and sample size determination

A multistage sampling strategy was employed to ensure representativeness. Initially, four upazilas (Dinajpur Sadar, Birganj, Parbatipur, and Nawabganj) were randomly selected from the district. Subsequently, three unions were randomly chosen from each upazila, followed by systematic selection of households within each union. One eligible adult participant (≥ 18 years) was interviewed per household. Participants who had undergone blood glucose testing at a certified diagnostic center within the previous month and could provide valid medical reports were included (Fig. S1). Individuals without recent diagnostic records were excluded. The minimum sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula (95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% assumed prevalence), yielding 384 participants 58,59. To enhance statistical power and representativeness, a total of 1,408 individuals were ultimately included.

Questionnaire preparation and validation

A structured questionnaire was developed on DM and its associated risk factors after a comprehensive literature review59–63. The questionnaire primarily improved in English and subsequently interpreted into Bengali for ease of understanding64. To ensure the validity of the study, a pilot study involving 50 individuals was conducted that prompted minor revisions to enhance the clarity and comprehensibility. Cronbach’s alpha (0.79), representing an acceptable reliability level by confirming the internal consistency. The finalized questionnaire consisted of six key sections: (i) demographic and socio-economic information, (ii) behavioral factors, (iii) history of diabetes and comorbid conditions, (iv) current diabetes status and family history, (v) physical measurements (height and weight), and (vi) diabetes management and treatment practices.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome of this study was the presence or absence of DM. Diabetes status was determined based on participants’ previously documented diagnostic reports from certified health facilities. Only participants who had a valid medical report from the past month were included. This approach ensures that the outcome reflects clinically confirmed diagnoses according to standard diagnostic criteria (fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L). For analytical purposes, the outcome was coded as a binary variable, 0 for absence of DM and 1 for presence of DM.

Predictor variable

A comprehensive set of predictors was selected based on prior literature10,14,65,66. These predictors included demographic and socio-economic factors (such as sex , age, marital status, occupation, education level and household income), behavioral factors (including smoking status, betel-leaf/gul use, tea/coffee consumption, dietary habits such as intake of fruits, vegetables, sweets, and meats, and the use of added salt), physical activity (type, frequency, and duration of exercise, labor, or walking), mode of transportation, and relevant medical history (such as family record of DM, HTN, cardiovascular disease and kidney disorders). Physical measurements like height (in inches) and weight (in kg) were carefully recorded and BMI was calculated following WHO67 classification guidelines to assess overweight and obesity as potential risk feature for DM. Several behavioral variables in the questionnaire followed a conditional structure. For example, questions regarding current smoking status and duration of smoking were only applicable to participants who reported ever smoking. Similar conditional dependencies existed for betel-leaf/gul consumption and tea/coffee intake. Responses that were conditionally inapplicable were not treated as true zero values; instead, they were coded as a separate “Not Applicable” category during preprocessing to preserve the logical hierarchy of the questionnaire and avoid introducing artificial information into the ML models. Subsequently, categorical variables were numerically encoded prior to model development.

Data preprocessing

Data were initially coded in Microsoft Excel and subsequently processed using SPSS (version 26). Data quality assessment was conducted prior to feature selection and model development to evaluate missing values, conditional responses, outliers, and multicollinearity. No missing observations were identified in the finalized dataset. Several questionnaire items followed a conditional structure; therefore, responses that were not applicable were encoded separately and were not considered missing values. Outliers among continuous variables were assessed using the interquartile range (IQR) method, and no extreme values were detected68. Multicollinearity among predictor variables was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all variables falling below the threshold of 10, indicating no evidence of significant collinearity69. Following these procedures, a complete and high-quality dataset comprising 1408 observations was retained for subsequent analysis.

Feature scaling standardizes the range of input variables, facilitating faster model convergence and improving predictive performance. Various scaling techniques such as standardization, normalization, robust scaling, and Min–Max scaling can influence the performance of ML algorithms70,71. Feature selection was performed to reduce dimensionality, minimize noise and improve model interpretability by identifying the most relevant predictors. The dataset initially included 45 variables comprising both categorical and numerical features. To ensure robust and unbiased feature identification, three complementary feature selection techniques were applied. RF feature importance was used to rank variables based on their contribution to reducing impurity across multiple decision trees72,73. RFE was employed as a wrapper method, which iteratively removes the least important features and evaluates model performance at each step to identify an optimal subset of predictors70,74. BFS, a heuristic-based search algorithm, was applied to explore different combinations of variables and select those that maximize predictive performance while minimizing redundancy. To ensure robustness and reduce selection bias, features identified by at least two out of the three methods were retained for final model development. This consensus-based approach ensured that only consistently important predictors were included in the ML pipeline.

Data partitioning and balancing

The study dataset consisted of 1408 participants, as described earlier. To maintain class proportions, stratified random sampling was used to divide the data into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. The training set contained 150 diabetic (15.2%) and 835 non-diabetic (84.8%) participants, while the testing set included 62 diabetic (14.7%) and 361 non-diabetic (85.3%) participants. To address class imbalance and improve model sensitivity toward the minority class, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied; specifically, SMOTE was used only on the training data, where it increased the number of diabetic cases from 150 to 835 by generating synthetic minority samples, resulting in a balanced training set of 835 diabetic and 835 non-diabetic observations. Importantly, to avoid information leakage and ensure unbiased evaluation, all preprocessing steps including feature scaling, feature selection, and SMOTE were performed exclusively within the training data under the cross-validation framework75, while the testing dataset remained completely untouched until final model evaluation, thereby providing an independent and reliable assessment of model performance.

Machine learning algorithm and evaluation

To predict DM and identify associated risk factors, six supervised ML algorithms were developed and evaluated. Logistic Regression (LR)76, Decision Tree (DT)77, Random Forest (RF)78, Support Vector Machine (SVM)79, XGBoost80 and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM)81,82. Logistic Regression estimates the probability of diabetes based on input features using a logistic function, while Decision Trees classify outcomes by recursively partitioning the dataset. RF, an ensemble of multiple decision trees, mitigates overfitting by aggregating predictions from individual trees, and gradient boosting methods, including XGBoost and LightGBM, sequentially combine weak learners to enhance predictive accuracy. SVM utilizes kernel functions to project input data into high-dimensional feature spaces for effective classification83. These algorithms were selected for their complementary strengths, ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships, and demonstrated efficacy in previous diabetes prediction studies76–81. To further improve predictive performance, reduce bias and variance, and leverage the strengths of individual models, a stacking ensemble approach was implemented81. In this framework, the six base models generated out-of-fold predictions, which were then used as input features for the meta-learner. Logistic Regression was employed as the meta-classifier to integrate these predictions and learn the optimal combination weights automatically from the training data through its model coefficients, without any manual weighting scheme81. The ensemble model is particularly advantageous in handling high-dimensional and heterogeneous datasets, as it balances the limitations of single classifiers while enhancing robustness and accuracy81,84. Hyperparameter optimization for the base models was performed using Bayesian optimization combined with k-fold cross-validation on the training dataset, ensuring optimal model selection while avoiding data leakage from the test set. Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and error rate, to identify the most effective classifier for DM prediction and to interpret the relative importance of contributing risk factors.

Cross-validation and hyper-parameter tuning

To enhance predictive performance and ensure model robustness, hyperparameter tuning was systematically performed for all ML models85. Model generalizability and stability were evaluated using fivefold cross-validation, which reduces the risk of overfitting by validating model performance across multiple data partitions86. Hyperparameter optimization for all base models was performed using Bayesian optimization combined with fivefold cross-validation on the training dataset to ensure optimal model selection while preventing data leakage87. During each fold of cross-validation, SMOTE balancing was applied only to the training portion of the fold, while the corresponding validation fold remained unchanged, thereby preserving the original data distribution and preventing information leakage during model training and evaluation. During each optimization iteration, the model was trained using a candidate set of hyperparameters and evaluated through fivefold cross-validation, after which the mean cross-validation error was returned to the Bayesian optimizer to iteratively identify the optimal hyperparameter configuration. This integrated optimization-validation procedure ensured robust model selection while preventing data leakage and overfitting. Compared with conventional grid search and random search approaches, Bayesian optimization offers faster convergence and improved computational efficiency88,89. This pipeline ensured that no information from the testing set was used during preprocessing, feature selection, or hyperparameter tuning, thereby preventing data leakage and ensuring unbiased model evaluation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that DM poses a substantial public health burden among adults in northern Bangladesh, with a prevalence of 15.1%. By integrating ML techniques with traditional statistical approaches, the study provides robust evidence on both the prediction and determinants of DM. The stacking ensemble model achieved the highest predictive performance, outperforming individual algorithms, thereby highlighting the effectiveness of combining multiple classifiers to capture complex relationships within the data. Key risk factors identified included age, family history of diabetes, BMI, hypertension, dietary behaviors, and socioeconomic characteristics. Among these, advancing age particularly 51–60 years and family history emerged as the most influential predictors, while modifiable factors such as diet, obesity, and comorbid conditions further contributed to increased risk. Importantly, the consistency between ML-based findings and multivariable logistic regression reinforces the validity and reliability of the results. The integration of stacking ensemble learning with explainable AI and traditional epidemiological analysis represents a novel and practical framework for diabetes risk prediction in low-resource community settings. Furthermore, the study underscores the value of combining advanced analytical methods with conventional epidemiological approaches for improved risk stratification and prediction of DM. These findings have important implications for public health practice, emphasizing the need for targeted screening, early detection, and context-specific interventions focusing on lifestyle modification and management of comorbidities. The adoption of EML models in healthcare settings may further support evidence-based decision-making and contribute to reducing the growing burden of diabetes in Bangladesh and similar resource-limited settings.
