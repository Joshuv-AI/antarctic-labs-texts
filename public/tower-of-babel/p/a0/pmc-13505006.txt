Surgical management of massive calcium silicate-based sealer extrusion into the maxillary sinus with palatal necrosis in an adolescent
Authors: Zeynep Afra Akbiyik Az, Abdulkadir Tiftik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough minor apical extrusion of calcium silicate-based sealers is relatively common, massive extrusion into the maxillary sinus is rare and may lead to severe complications when vital anatomical structures are involved. This case is notable for presenting palatal bone necrosis associated with calcium silicate-based sealer extrusion into the maxillary sinus in an adolescent, requiring staged surgical management.Case presentationA 14-year-old female was referred one week after root canal treatment with severe spontaneous pain, palatal bone exposure, swelling, and purulent discharge. Radiographic and cone-beam computed tomography examinations revealed extensive extrusion of calcium silicate-based sealer into the maxillary sinus. Initial medical management included antibiotics, chlorhexidine mouthwash, saline irrigation, and analgesics. After symptomatic improvement, necrotic palatal bone was removed via local debridement. A lateral window sinus surgery was subsequently performed to eliminate the extruded material and associated granulation tissue while preserving the Schneiderian membrane. Two months later, nonsurgical endodontic retreatment was completed. Follow-up imaging demonstrated gradual resorption of the residual sealer and complete symptom resolution.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this case report, it can be concluded that massive extrusion of calcium silicate-based sealers—despite their recognized biocompatibility—may cause destructive complications when displaced into the maxillary sinus. Early diagnosis, appropriate surgical intervention, and careful endodontic retreatment are essential for successful outcomes. Preventive measures, including accurate working-length determination and controlled obturation pressure, remain critical to avoiding such adverse events.Keywords: Bioceramic sealer, Case report, Maxillary sinus, Palatal necrosis, Root-canal treatment, Surgical management

Introduction

The primary objective of endodontic treatment is to remove diseased pulp and dentin, followed by thorough cleaning, shaping, and three-dimensional obturation of the root canal system, thereby providing a hermetic seal and maintaining periapical health [1]. The obturation phase is technically critical; while an adequate seal prevents microleakage and reinfection, procedural errors such as overfilling or apical extrusion can injure periapical tissues and involve adjacent anatomical spaces including the maxillary sinus or the inferior alveolar canal [2].

Accurate working-length determination and controlled obturation pressure are essential to minimize apical over-instrumentation and tissue injury. Contemporary endodontic standards recommend establishing working length with an electronic apex locator in conjunction with verifying periapical radiographs, rather than relying on radiography alone [3]. In maxillary posterior teeth, where root apices may be close to the sinus floor, this combined strategy is particularly important; radiographic confirmation is especially indicated when electronic apex locator readings are inconsistent, when apical constriction is altered, when canal anatomy is complex, or when roots are adjacent to critical anatomical structures [4, 5].

Although the composition and biocompatibility of modern sealers have improved, all endodontic materials exhibit a certain degree of cytotoxicity and neurotoxicity when extruded beyond the root apex. Contact of these substances with vital tissues may initiate inflammatory reactions, cellular damage, or even necrosis. Consequently, sealer extrusion can result not only in periapical tissue irritation but also in severe complications such as persistent paresthesia, foreign body reactions, or maxillary sinus pathologies [1, 6, 7]. In addition to chemical irritation, extrusion may transport microorganisms and dentinal debris, sustaining inflammation and delaying healing [8].

Calcium silicate-based sealers (CSBS), often marketed as “bioceramic sealers”, are hydraulic root canal sealers with material properties that depend on their formulation [9, 10]. Although many CSBS exhibit favorable bioactivity and biocompatibility, the umbrella term “bioceramic” should not be used as a synonym for a single sealer chemistry or as a guarantee of uniform biological behavior [11]. Therefore, in this report we use the term CSBS consistently and interpret clinical behavior in a material-specific manner.

Material extrusion during root canal treatment should be viewed as an anatomically driven complication that is not limited to the posterior maxilla [12]. In maxillary teeth, close root–sinus relationships may facilitate sinus mucosal involvement and foreign-body related sinonasal sequelae [13]. In mandibular posterior teeth, the proximity between root apices and the mandibular canal creates a parallel risk corridor for inferior alveolar neurovascular injury. Therefore, extrusion events should be contextualized within a region-specific risk model that includes both maxillary sinus and mandibular canal anatomy [12].

The biological impact of extrusion is multifactorial and may include mechanical compression, chemical toxicity/irritation, and neurovascular compromise [1]. Clinically, this may range from transient paresthesia to persistent neurosensory dysfunction, and management urgency is emphasized when nerve contact is suspected [6]. Recent literature on calcium hydroxide extrusion has further highlighted rare but potentially severe vascular-ischemic complications, including Nicolau syndrome, reinforcing that prevention and early recognition are central to risk mitigation [14, 15].

Although calcium silicate–based sealers are widely adopted for their bioactivity and favorable biological profile, “bioceramic” should not be interpreted as an absolute safety label [16, 17]. Experimental and clinical evidence indicates that these materials are heterogeneous in chemistry and radiopacifier systems, and composition-related differences can influence physicochemical behavior and clinical response after extrusion [17, 18]. Accordingly, adverse tissue or neurosensory reactions remain possible in specific anatomic contexts, which justifies cautious interpretation of extrusion events in the present case [1, 6, 16].

This case report presents and discusses the surgical management of a rare complication in which massive extrusion of a calcium silicate-based root canal sealer into the maxillary sinus during endodontic treatment led to palatal bone necrosis in an adolescent patient. The clinical, radiographic, and surgical findings are evaluated to emphasize the importance of careful operative technique and anatomical awareness, particularly because palatal necrosis secondary to calcium silicate-based sealer extrusion has not, to the best of our knowledge, been previously documented. To verify this, a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted using the terms ‘calcium silicate sealer,’ ‘bioceramic sealer,’ ‘maxillary sinus,’ ‘sealer extrusion,’ and ‘palatal necrosis,’ with no date restriction and no language filter. No case combining calcium silicate-based sealer extrusion into the maxillary sinus with concomitant palatal bone necrosis was identified in the retrieved literature.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old female was referred one week after root canal treatment performed at another clinic, complaining of pain in the treated tooth and an ulcerative lesion on the palatal mucosa. Her medical history was unremarkable. Her legal guardian provided consent for all procedures.

On presentation, the left maxillary first molar exhibited severe percussion tenderness with spontaneous pain. On the palatal aspect adjacent to the tooth, there was an area of exposed bone with a centrally ulcerated surface and an erythematous rim, accompanied by purulent discharge. Palatal soft tissue swelling/edema was present; the buccal aspect appeared intact (Fig. 1). At initial presentation, the differential diagnosis of the palatal lesion included iatrogenic chemical necrosis secondary to sealer extrusion, necrotizing sialometaplasia, invasive fungal infection, and ulcerative malignant lesion. The clear temporal relationship with the recent endodontic procedure, the radiographic evidence of massive sealer extrusion, and the absence of systemic risk factors collectively supported a diagnosis of iatrogenic palatal necrosis secondary to calcium silicate-based sealer extrusion as the most likely etiology.

Preoperative intraoral photograph showing a large area of necrotic palatal bone exposure with yellowish necrotic debris and surrounding erythematous, inflamed mucosa adjacent to the left maxillary first molar

A panoramic radiograph revealed a well-defined radiopaque mass extending from the apical region of the left maxillary first molar toward the maxillary sinus floor, raising a suspicion of material extrusion during obturation. CBCT demonstrated sinus membrane thickening and increased mucosal edema relative to the contralateral side.

According to information obtained from the clinician who performed the initial treatment, after access cavity preparation, working length was determined using an electronic apex locator, and canal shaping was completed with rotary instruments up to size 30/0.04. Irrigation was performed with saline and sodium hypochlorite, followed by drying with paper points. Obturation was then attempted using CeraSeal (Meta Biomed Co., Ltd., Cheongju-si, Chungcheongbuk-do, South Korea), a premixed flowable calcium silicate-based root canal sealer delivered under pressure. No gutta-percha cone or other core material was used, and the tooth was sealed with a temporary coronal restoration at the end of the session. At presentation to our clinic, the temporary restoration was clinically intact and appeared to provide an adequate coronal seal, with no obvious marginal leakage. The patient subsequently presented to our clinic with pain and palatal ulceration (Fig. 2).

Radiographic assessment of calcium-silicate based sealer extrusion (A) Panoramic radiograph showing a radiopaque extruded material extending from the apices of the left maxillary first molar into the maxillary sinus. B Coronal CBCT view demonstrating sealer accumulation within the sinus with associated mucosal thickening. C Sagittal CBCT view confirming the extent and location of the extruded sealer within the sinus cavity

After obtaining guardian consent, medical management was initiated: amoxicillin/clavulanic acid 1000 mg twice daily for 7 days, chlorhexidine mouthrinse three times daily and paracetamol 500 mg twice daily for analgesic control. Patient was instructed to perform saline irrigation at home. At 1-week review, symptoms had subsided; antibiotics were paused, saline irrigation/debridement continued, and chlorhexidine was replaced with benzydamine hydrochloride mouthrinse to provide local anti-inflammatory/analgesic support during mucosal epithelialization while avoiding unnecessary prolonged chlorhexidine exposure.

At the 2-week visit, after clear demarcation of necrotic tissue and resolution of acute inflammation, surgical debridement was performed under local anesthesia. The exposed necrotic palatal cortical bone adjacent to the left maxillary first molar was conservatively curetted under copious saline irrigation until bleeding vital margins were reached. No deep palatal involvement or oroantral communication was observed. The site was allowed to heal by secondary intention with continued postoperative irrigation. A new course of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid 1000 mg twice daily was prescribed (Fig. 3).

Intraoral view following mechanical debridement of the necrotic palatal bone. Complete soft-tissue epithelialization is observed prior to sinus surgery

Preoperative CBCT revealed minimal bone thickness between the molar apices and the sinus floor, with radiopaque material extending directly into the sinus cavity. These findings suggested extrusion through the thin periapical bone and Schneiderian membrane, without evidence of pre-existing sinus pathology.

One month later, following complete mucosal healing, a lateral window approach was performed. After elevation of a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap, a bony window was created in the lateral sinus wall. Granulation tissue surrounding the extruded material was observed, without purulent exudate (Fig. 4). The Schneiderian membrane was intact but mildly thickened. The sealer fragments were carefully removed using sinus curettes while preserving membrane integrity, and the cavity was irrigated with sterile saline. The flap was repositioned and sutured primarily.

Intraoperative view of the lateral window approach to the maxillary sinus

Postoperatively, healing was uneventful and the patient remained asymptomatic. Follow-up imaging confirmed substantial clearance of the extruded material. Two months later, nonsurgical endodontic retreatment was performed under local anesthesia. The temporary coronal restoration was removed, initial canal irrigation with sterile saline was performed, and working length was re-established using an electronic apex locator. Canals were then re-instrumented with a rotary NiTi system up to size 40/0.04 (VDW.ROTATE; VDW GmbH, Munich, Germany). Final irrigation was carried out using sterile saline and 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Wizard, Istanbul, Türkiye), and irrigant activation was performed using a sonic activation device (EndoActivator, Dentsply Sirona Endodontics; Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) After drying with paper points, obturation was completed with gutta-percha and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany) using the cold lateral condensation technique, followed by definitive direct composite coronal restoration in the same session.

The staged management strategy was deliberately structured based on biological and surgical considerations. Initial medical therapy was instituted to control acute inflammation and reduce infectious burden prior to surgical manipulation. Palatal debridement was performed only after clear demarcation of necrotic tissue to avoid unnecessary removal of viable bone. Maxillary sinus surgery was intentionally delayed until satisfactory palatal epithelialization was achieved, thereby minimizing the risk of oroantral communication and ensuring optimal surgical field conditions. Finally, nonsurgical endodontic retreatment was completed after sinus healing to reduce the risk of persistent intracanal contamination and recurrent sinus irritation.

In summary, the sequence was as follows: medical management was initiated at presentation (week 0); palatal debridement was performed at week 2; lateral window sinus surgery was performed at week 6; and nonsurgical endodontic retreatment was completed at month 4.

A follow-up panoramic radiograph showed that previously unreachable distal-sinus remnants of the extruded sealer had undergone resorption. Patient remained asymptomatic thereafter (Fig. 5). At the 1-year follow-up, the panoramic radiograph demonstrated continued radiographic improvement with further reduction of the residual radiopaque sealer and no recurrence of symptoms (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This case highlights that even calcium silicate-based sealers, despite their acknowledged bioactivity and sealing ability, can cause destructive sequelae when extruded into vital spaces. Although minor overextension of sealers is often clinically asymptomatic and may not compromise the long-term outcome, extensive extrusion into vital anatomical spaces can lead to significant tissue damage, secondary infection, and the need for surgical intervention.

Calcium silicate-based sealers are widely recognized for their high biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, calcium and silicate ion release, and their ability to stimulate mineralized tissue formation [8, 19], though these biological properties are material-specific and should not be applied uniformly across all formulations. Several clinical studies have reported that minor extrusion of calcium silicate-based sealer is usually well tolerated by periapical tissues and often followed by complete healing [8]. However, inappropriately performed treatments -such as inadequate canal debridement, over instrumentation, or excessive obturation pressure- may lead to the simultaneous extrusion of sealer and residual infected debris beyond the apex. In such cases, the extruded material may act as a carrier for microorganisms, resulting in persistent inflammation or secondary infection despite the material’s inherent biocompatibility [8]. Recent in vitro studies have clarified that the cytotoxicity of calcium silicate-based sealers is concentration dependent; high concentrations of fresh sealer extracts can still significantly reduce cell viability despite the material’s general biocompatibility [20].

In the present case, the extruded material was CeraSeal. CeraSeal is a calcium silicate-based sealer formulated as a premixed injectable material. During hydration, calcium silicate sealers generate hydroxyl ions and calcium ion release, producing an alkaline microenvironment [21]. Previous in vitro studies have shown sustained alkalizing behavior and calcium-ion release for CeraSeal, with physicochemical performance reported within ISO-based evaluations. These features are generally associated with bioactivity and mineralization potential under controlled conditions; however, in a sinus-adjacent overfilling scenario, persistent foreign material burden together with local alkalinity may contribute to tissue irritation and delayed healing [21, 22].

It should also be noted that the initial obturation was performed using sealer alone, without a gutta-percha core material. This represents a significant deviation from standard endodontic practice, as the absence of a solid core material eliminates the physical resistance that normally limits apical sealer displacement. Sealer-only obturation under hydraulic pressure — particularly with a premixed injectable calcium silicate-based sealer — markedly increases the risk of apical extrusion. This procedural factor likely contributed substantially to the volume of extruded material observed in this case and should be recognized as an independent risk factor for extrusion-related complications.

From an operator-related perspective, several procedural factors in this case merit critical analysis. The use of a pressure-driven injectable delivery system without a solid core material, combined with the absence of radiographic confirmation of working length prior to obturation, likely created conditions favorable for uncontrolled apical extrusion. Premixed injectable calcium silicate-based sealers, while clinically convenient, generate significant hydraulic pressure during delivery that can overcome apical resistance, particularly in teeth with open or poorly defined apical constrictions. Clinicians should be aware that electronic apex locator readings alone may be insufficient in teeth with close root-sinus proximity, and that radiographic confirmation, conservative obturation pressure, and the use of a gutta-percha master cone as a physical barrier are essential safeguards against massive extrusion.

The pathogenesis of tissue injury in extrusion cases is therefore multifactorial. Besides bacterial contamination, the physical pressure of the extruded material and the high alkalinity of calcium silicate-based sealers may aggravate local ischemia and cytotoxic effects, leading to necrosis and delayed healing [23]. Moreover, large extrusion into maxillary sinus has been reported to act as both chemical and mechanical irritants, causing severe pain and necessitating surgical removal [24].Similar findings were described by Alves et al., who reported permanent labiomandibular paresthesia following the extrusion of a calcium silicate-based material, suggesting that direct tissue contact and chemical irritation can cause irreversible neurovascular damage [6].

Large volume extrusion should be interpreted as a combined chemical-mechanical-biological injury rather than a purely material related event. Beyond high-pH chemical irritation, the bulk effect of displaced material in confined tissues may amplify pressure-related ischemia, while concomitant microbial carry-over and persistent foreign body response can sustain inflammation and delay repair [25]. This broader model is consistent with evidence showing less favorable healing in extrusion cases overall and with persistent apical periodontitis frameworks that include both microbial and non-microbial drivers. In patients presenting with palatal necrosis, the differential diagnosis should therefore explicitly include iatrogenic ischemic injury, necrotizing sialometaplasia, invasive fungal disease, and ulcerative malignant lesions; when the clinical course is atypical or progressive, early tissue diagnosis is warranted [15, 26].

Compared with published endo-sinus communication (ESC)/sinus-extrusion reports, observation is reasonable only when symptoms are mild, imaging is stable or improving, and communication defects appear limited with early tendency to close; in contrast, persistent pain, necrosis, non-healing communication, progressive sinus pathology, or retained foreign material support timely surgical intervention within a multidisciplinary pathway (27–30]. This threshold based approach aligns with the recently published case series, classic gross-sinus extrusion reports where surgery was indicated despite limited initial symptoms, and contemporary odontogenic sinusitis recommendations emphasizing joint dental-ENT decision making when disease does not resolve with initial conservative/dental measures [29, 30].

In posterior maxillary endodontics, iatrogenic ESC should be recognized as a clinically relevant event, especially in teeth with close root-sinus proximity [13, 31]. Importantly, ESC must be distinguished from established odontogenic sinusitis (ODS) [32]. Contemporary literature emphasizes that ODS is a distinct disease entity from non-odontogenic rhinosinusitis and often unilateral, with diagnosis generally requiring dual confirmation: sinusitis by the otolaryngologist and odontogenic source by the dental team [33].

In this context, ESC can be considered an early interface event on the dental-sinus continuum, whereas ODS reflects persistent or established sinonasal infection/inflammation [32]. The recently published 15-case ESC series is especially useful for framing this distinction: when ESC was recognized intraoperatively and managed promptly, communications resolved within one week and no persistent sinonasal pathology was observed during follow-up [30]. These findings support conservative initial management in selected, limited ESC presentations, but they do not eliminate the need for escalation in clinically progressive cases [34].

Therefore, management should be stepwise and multidisciplinary: early detection, close clinical/radiographic monitoring, and coordinated decision making [34]. The staged surgical strategy employed in the present case was guided by these principles and is consistent with current multidisciplinary consensus recommendations, which advocate individualized, stepwise management based on symptom burden, extent of sinus involvement, and dental pathology. Current consensus-based ODS literature supports individualized selection of dental treatment and endoscopic sinus surgery according to symptom burden, dental pathology, and sinus disease extent. In cases with significant foreign material displacement, persistent symptoms, or objective sinus involvement, a more active surgical strategy is justified to prevent chronic sequelae and optimize outcomes [34].

The decision to defer endodontic retreatment until after sinus healing was based on the premise that instrumentation and irrigation of an actively inflamed periapical environment could introduce additional microbial load and chemical irritants into a surgically recovering sinus. While an alternative approach — performing retreatment concurrent with or prior to sinus surgery — could theoretically eliminate the intracanal source of contamination earlier, we considered this sequence to carry a higher risk of recurrent sinus irritation in the acute phase. We acknowledge that no high-level evidence directly comparing these sequencing strategies exists, and the optimal timing of endodontic retreatment in relation to sinus surgery in such cases remains to be established.

Anatomical factors play a key role in determining the severity of such complications. In the posterior maxilla, particularly in the first and second molar region, root apices are often in close proximity to the maxillary sinus and in some cases are separated from it only by Schneiderian membrane. Consequently, even minimal over instrumentation or excessive obturation pressure can result in the displacement of endodontic materials into the sinus cavity [35, 36]. Once inside the sinus, these materials can provoke chronic sinusitis, foreign body reactions, or cause fungal infections such as aspergillosis [37]. The case reported here presented an extensive sealer extrusion that not only occupied the sinus floor but was also associated with palatal necrosis. Although several reports have described maxillary sinus complications caused by the extrusion of endodontic materials—including foreign-body reactions, fungal infections, or the need for Caldwell–Luc surgery—none have documented concomitant palatal bone necrosis. For example, Saruhan et al. described the surgical removal of foreign endodontic material from the maxillary sinus following root canal treatment, yet no associated palatal tissue destruction was observed [19]. In contrast, the present case features a uniquely aggressive presentation in which calcium silicate-based sealer extrusion into the sinus coincided with extensive palatal osteonecrosis, a finding not previously reported in the literature.

Management of such complications depends on the severity of clinical symptoms and the extent of material displacement. Conservative approaches may be considered in asymptomatic cases with limited extrusion; however, when extensive extrusion causes persistent pain, necrosis, or sinus involvement, surgical intervention becomes necessary to remove the foreign material and debride necrotic tissue [35]. In the present case, surgical removal of the extruded material led to complete resolution of symptoms. It should be acknowledged that complete removal of all extruded sealer was not achieved during sinus surgery, as residual fragments in the distal sinus were inaccessible without risking Schneiderian membrane perforation or extending the surgical field unnecessarily. The decision to leave these remnants was based on the clinical judgment that the risks of more aggressive removal outweighed the potential benefits, given the absence of purulent exudate and the intact membrane observed intraoperatively. This approach is supported by the favorable outcome observed at follow-up, where residual sealer underwent gradual resorption without recurrence of symptoms, consistent with the known solubility characteristics of calcium silicate-based sealers over time.

The intraoperative finding of a macroscopically intact but thickened Schneiderian membrane, despite extensive sealer extrusion into the sinus cavity, warrants brief pathophysiological consideration. It is likely that the sealer was displaced through a pre-existing or procedure-induced microscopic defect in the periapical bone and membrane, rather than causing gross membrane rupture. The viscoelastic and self-sealing properties of the Schneiderian membrane, combined with the flowable nature of the premixed injectable sealer under pressure, may have allowed material to penetrate through a pinpoint breach that subsequently sealed around the extruded bolus. This would explain the absence of oroantral communication and the membrane continuity observed intraoperatively.

This report has several limitations. As a single-case observation, the findings cannot be generalized. Histopathological confirmation of the necrotic palatal bone was not performed. Microbiological investigation was not performed despite the presence of purulent discharge, and therefore the specific contribution of infection to the observed tissue pathology cannot be fully determined.

In addition, pre-operative periapical radiographs from the initial treatment were unavailable, as the procedure had been performed in another clinic. Consequently, direct radiographic evaluation of the obturation technique, working length control, and the precise initial extent of extrusion was not possible. Radiographic assessment was descriptive rather than quantitative; CBCT findings were not systematically analyzed using standardized measurements such as volumetric assessment of extruded material or precise extent of membrane involvement, which limits the radiological rigor and objective evaluation of treatment outcomes. Furthermore, procedural details of the initial treatment were obtained from the treating clinician rather than direct observation and are therefore subject to recall bias. The postoperative medication protocols employed, including antibiotic and analgesic regimens, were not critically evaluated against current evidence-based guidelines, and their potential influence on healing outcomes cannot be determined from this single case observation. Follow-up assessment relied on symptom resolution and radiographic appearance rather than standardized outcome measures, limiting the objectivity of the reported treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights that even calcium silicate-based sealers, despite their excellent biological and sealing properties, can cause destructive complications if extruded beyond apical foramen. Awareness of maxillary sinus anatomy, accurate working length determination, radiographic confirmation prior to obturation, and controlled use of pressure-driven delivery systems are crucial for preventing such adverse outcomes. Clinicians should remain vigilant, as prevention through careful technique remains the most effective approach to managing extrusion related complications.
