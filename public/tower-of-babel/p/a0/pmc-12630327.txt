Genetic Testing and Counselling for Hereditary Renal Carcinoma: What Do Clinicians Need to Know?
Authors: Chiara Re, Angela Pecoraro, Grant D. Stewart, Anna Caliò, Selcuk Erdem, Tugba Kalayci, Zehra Oya Uyguner, Alessandro Larcher, Riccardo Campi, Daniele Amparore, James Whitworth, Laura Marandino, European Association of Urology Young Academic Urologists Renal Cancer Working Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageGenetic testing for hereditary renal cell carcinoma remains underutilised in clinical practice. However, testing crucial for early disease detection, targeted surveillance, individualised surgical planning, and cascade testing of at-risk relatives. In this setting, multidisciplinary management is essential to ensure comprehensive and effective care.

Introduction

It is estimated that hereditary renal cell carcinoma (H-RCC) accounts for 5–8% of all newly diagnosed kidney cancers [1,2]; however, this approximation may be significantly underestimated particularly in patients with advanced non–clear-cell RCC [3]. Although it has been well established that several variants are associated with higher risk of RCC [3], genetic counselling is often underutilised as a component of the work-up for patients with kidney cancer [4]. Accurate and prompt diagnosis allows for an appropriate surveillance programme that can reduce morbidity and mortality. Identification of H-RCC can guide tailored surgical management and, when available, specific systemic treatment. Recognition of H-RCC also allows “cascade testing” for early identification of other potentially affected family members and supports prenatal genetic management [5]. Various barriers at the patient, clinician, and health-care system levels may complicate identification of individuals suitable for referral to cancer genetics services [6]. Because of the significant implications for clinical practice, enhanced clinical guidelines on genetic testing and counselling are needed [6].

The aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive overview of the most well-characterised H-RCC syndromes and to offer a practical step-by-step clinical guide for diagnosis and management to assist clinicians in the care of patients with suspected or diagnosed H-RCC.

Methods

We performed a nonsystematic, narrative review on H-RCC with a focus on genetic risk assessment, counselling, testing, and clinical aspects of RCC syndromes. A PubMed/MEDLINE search was conducted to identify scientific guidelines, consensus statements, original studies, and review articles on H-RCC. Keywords included “kidney cancer”, “kidney neoplasm”, “renal cancer”, “renal cell carcinoma”, “genetic testing”, “genetic counselling”, “germline variants”, “germline mutations”, “hereditary RCC”, “hereditary syndromes”, and “recommendations”, along with free-text and related and derivative terms. The search was conducted in June 2024 and updated in February 2025. To focus on the most up-to-date guidelines and recommendations, we initially included articles published in the last 20 yr. Additional relevant manuscripts were subsequently identified via a manual search of the reference lists of the articles retrieved. The resulting review provides a summary of the most well-characterised H-RCC syndromes, and a step-by-step clinical guide based on existing recommendations that covers all relevant aspects, from patient identification and counselling to the uro-oncological management of confirmed cases in clinical practice. Supplementary Table 1 provides a practical vocabulary of the genetics terminology used in the text.

Results

H-RCC syndromes: key features

More than a dozen genes (including VHL, FH, FLCN, MET, SDHB, BAP-1, and PTEN, among others) for which germline alterations significantly increase the risk of RCC and possibly other benign/malignant manifestations are currently recognised [7]. Many of these represent the underlying cause of monogenic syndromes related to germline alterations in tumour suppressor genes, except for hereditary papillary RCC syndrome (HPRC), which is characterised by activating variants in the MET proto-oncogene. Although these syndromes have overlapping features, they vary significantly in histological characteristics, aggressiveness, penetrance, and associated clinical manifestations [7,8]. Von Hippel-Lindau disease

Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease is a rare autosomal-dominant hereditary disorder caused by germline pathogenic variants in the VHL tumour suppressor gene located on the short arm of chromosome 3. VHL disease represents the most common hereditary disorder associated with RCC; prevalence rates range from 1/36 000 to 1/91 000 individuals worldwide [9,10]. Affected individuals are predisposed to the development of lesions in multiple organs, such as retinal and central nervous system (CNS) haemangioblastomas, RCC, phaeochromocytoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, endolymphatic sac tumours, and renal, pancreatic, and epididymal cysts [9]. While the risk of RCC varies among different VHL subtypes, it has been reported that the lifetime risk for the most common forms reaches up to 70% [11]. VHL-associated RCC is typically bilateral and multifocal, exhibits clear-cell histology, and presents a more indolent course than its sporadic counterpart [12]. The expression of VHL disease is variable. VHL pathogenic variants are highly penetrant, with nearly all individuals showing symptoms by the age of 61–70 yr [13]. De novo variants are relatively common and account for approximately 20% of cases [13]. Therefore, individuals may lack a family history of VHL disease [14].

Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome

Birt-Hogg-Dubé (BHD) syndrome is caused by germline variants in the FLCN tumour suppressor gene, which encodes the protein folliculin. Although the commonly reported prevalence of BHD syndrome is 1/200 000, it is believed that the condition is underdiagnosed [15,16]. BHD syndrome is associated with fibrofolliculomas/trichodischomas (raised pale or skin-coloured papules that predominantly occur on the face and upper trunk) and multiple pulmonary cysts, which may lead to spontaneous pneumothorax [8,17]. Approximately one-third of affected individuals develop bilateral and multifocal renal tumours with variable histology, including hybrid oncocytic tumours containing features of both chromophobe RCC and oncocytoma. These tumours are characterised by indolent behaviour and lower metastatic potential in comparison to sporadic RCC [16]. There has been a recent proposal to rename this neoplasm as FCLN-mutated tumour and use an immunohistochemical panel for screening [18].

FH tumour predisposition syndrome

FH tumour predisposition (FHTP) syndrome, previously known as hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC (HLRCC) syndrome, is caused by germline variants in the FH gene, which encodes the Krebs-cycle enzyme fumarate hydratase. This syndrome is associated with higher risk of cutaneous leiomyomas, multiple uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) in women, and approximately 15–20% risk of RCC [19,20]. Phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma have also been reported. The prevalence of FHTP syndrome is unknown. While previous estimates suggest 1/200 000 individuals, the increasing availability of genetic panel testing that includes the FH gene suggests a higher prevalence and lower RCC penetrance than previously thought [21]. FHTP syndrome–associated RCC can be highly aggressive [19]. Advanced disease at presentation with nodal involvement or distant metastases can occur even when renal tumours are small [22].

From a pathology point of view, these tumours are characterised by a mixed architectural pattern, including papillary, solid, and cribriform tubulocystic patterns and high nucleolar grade [23]. Sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation and tumour necrosis are frequently seen and reflect the intrinsic aggressiveness of the tumour. FH deficiency can be detected as abnormal negative immunohistochemical staining for FH in approximately 80–90% of FH-deficient tumours. However, this marker might not be sensitive enough to exclude FH-deficient RCC, considering that there are many different FH variants and some pathogenic variants exhibit normal expression despite loss of heterozygosity, which could potentially lead to false-negative results [24,25]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) remains the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis, but it is time-consuming and expensive. In this scenario, S-(2-succino)-cysteine (2SC) protein (derived from the reaction between fumarate and cysteine sulfhydryl groups of proteins) has emerged as a promising biomarker [26].

Hereditary papillary RCC

HPRC is a very rare autosomal-dominant syndrome caused by germline variants in the MET proto-oncogene, located on chromosome 7. Prevalence has been estimated as <1/100 000 [27]. HPRC is characterised by the development of slow-growing, bilateral, multifocal papillary RCC with no known extrarenal manifestations [28]. The disease is mostly detected after the age of 30 yr and the risk of RCC increases with age, with penetrance close to 100% by the age of 80 yr [8].

Hereditary SDH-deficient RCC

SDH is an enzyme composed of multiple subunits, including SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, and SDHD [29]. SDH takes part in both the Krebs tricarboxylic acid cycle and the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Germline variants in the genes encoding all subunits have been associated with paragangliomas, phaeochromocytomas, and, less frequently, gastrointestinal stromal tumours and RCC [30]. SDH-deficient RCC is most commonly associated with variants in subunit B [29]. Individuals with an SDHB pathogenic variant have 4.7% risk of developing RCC by the age of 60 yr [31]. SDH-deficient RCC has specific histological features; most commonly, it presents as an oncocytic low-grade tumour with cytoplasmic vacuoles and occasionally flocculent inclusions, which should prompt evaluation of SDH on immunohistochemistry [32]. Loss of SDHB on immunohistochemistry is a sensitive and specific marker and should prompt genetic referral [33]. The prognosis of SDH-deficient RCC is variable; a few cases with aggressive behaviour have been observed in the presence of high nuclear grade, sarcomatoid differentiation, and necrosis [29,32,33].

Tuberous sclerosis complex

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is an autosomal-dominant genetic disorder caused by germline loss-of-function mutations in either the TSC1 or TSC2 gene, which lead to dysregulation of the mTOR pathway [34,35]. TSC incidence is approximately 1 in 6000 births worldwide, with approximately two-thirds of cases arising from de novo mutations [36]. TSC is characterised by the development of hamartomas in multiple organs, including the brain, skin, heart, and lungs. A subset of patients also presents with cognitive impairment and neurological manifestations [34,35]. The most common abdominal lesions include bilateral multifocal angiomyolipomas, renal epithelial cysts, and RCC, which represent a major source of morbidity and mortality in TSC [37]. Although angiomyolipomas and their variants are typically indolent tumours, some epithelioid angiomyolipomas and pure perivascular epithelioid cell neoplasms exhibit aggressive behaviour, with a non-negligible risk of embolisation or surgery. Malignancy criteria have been proposed to aid in their identification [[38], [39], [40]].

While guidelines assist clinicians in identifying high-risk cases, the evidence for these recommendations sometimes lacks support from the literature. For instance, a cohort study of 254 patients with advanced RCC by Carlo et al [3] revealed that 36% of individuals with high-penetrance RCC-associated variants would have been missed if ACMG referral criteria had been strictly followed. Their findings suggest the need for a broader genetic testing approach, particularly for patients with advanced non–clear-cell RCC [3].

Regarding the age cutoff for genetic testing [46], analysis by Shuch et al [47] of the RCC age distribution in the general population (SEER-17 programme) and in their institutional hereditary kidney cancer population revealed a median age at diagnosis of 37 yr for H-RCC, and the authors recommended ≤46 yr as the onset cutoff for genetic testing. Truong et al [48] retrospectively analysed data for 232 patients with early-onset RCC (≤46 yr). P/LP germline variants were detected in 17.7%, with 9.1% in RCC-associated genes and 8.6% in non-RCC genes. P/LP variants were associated with bilateral/multifocal tumours, non–clear-cell histology, and extrarenal malignancies. In solitary clear-cell RCC, the P/LP variant prevalence was 0% in RCC-associated genes and 9.9% in non-RCC genes [48]. Other studies questioned age as an independent criterion for genetic testing, and the NCCN guidelines emphasise its limited sensitivity as a sole criterion for risk evaluation [49,50]. In the first consensus conference on genetic risk assessment for H-RCC [4], there was general agreement on age alone as a sufficient criterion, with 67% of participants favouring a cutoff of ≤46 yrs, although uniform consensus was not reached.

At the same conference, there was uniform consensus on offering genetic risk assessment to patients with bilateral or multifocal tumours. However, discussions highlighted that individuals aged >60 yr may not require testing or are likely to test negative [4]. Agreement was reached on assessment for individuals, with or without RCC, with a personal or family history of classic syndromic features associated with H-RCC [4]. The panel also considered whether genetic risk assessment is necessary for patients who have undergone somatic tumour testing. Most participants agreed that if a variant is found in a gene associated with hereditary cancer syndromes, the decision to proceed with further genetic evaluation should be based on the specific gene involved. Meanwhile, parallel testing of tumour and normal tissues, which allows distinction between somatic and germline variants, is increasingly being integrated into clinical oncology. Emerging evidence indicates that 8–18% of patients undergoing parallel testing harbour clinically actionable germline variants, and many of these patients would not meet traditional criteria for germline testing [51].

What is the appropriate timing for genetic testing?

For patients with a new diagnosis of RCC, genetic testing results may influence the management strategy for their kidney cancer [52]. In the first consensus conference on RCC genetic risk assessment, the panel agreed (93%) on genetic testing before initiation of management for individuals with a localised renal lesion <3 cm and strong suspicion of a H-RCC syndrome [4]. There was also agreement (79%) that patients with multifocal or bilateral disease should undergo genetic risk assessment before treatment. In patients with metastatic RCC, systemic therapy is typically initiated on confirmation of the pathological diagnosis. The consensus panel did not reach agreement on whether genetic risk assessment should precede management in metastatic disease not requiring urgent treatment when a H-RCC form is suspected [4].

The timing of genetic testing often intersects with the availability and interpretation of pathological findings, typically obtained via core needle biopsy or renal surgery. There are currently no guidelines regarding the use of renal mass biopsy in the H-RCC setting, and the role of pathology in guiding genetic assessment remains a matter of debate. Nonetheless, pathological features can be highly informative—sometimes distinctive—even in the absence of other syndromic manifestations. In such cases, histology may support targeted or even single-gene testing. The utility of testing must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

Who should request genetic testing?

Genetic testing regulations differ across countries and health care systems. Medical geneticists traditionally hold a central role in prescribing tests, providing pretest counselling, and delivering results [53,54]. Genetic counsellors are highly skilled health care professionals with training in genomic medicine and counselling skills [55].While the role of genetic counsellors is well defined in many countries, especially in USA [56], it is still debated in other countries, including countries in Europe [53,54].

The increasing number of patients eligible for genetic testing and the limited capacity of genetics departments to accommodate this growing demand have recently paved the way for mainstream genetic testing. In a mainstream genetic testing pathway, health care professionals who are not genetic specialists provide pretest counselling and order the genetic test [57]. The first consensus panel on RCC genetic risk assessment agreed (92%) that physicians such as urologists and oncologists with expertise in H-RCC syndromes may offer counselling before genetic testing [4]. However, while specialist physicians might have the expertise to integrate genetic test results into treatment decisions, they may find themselves in unfamiliar situations. A structured pathway for implementation of mainstream genetic testing, including dedicated training, is needed, as well as close collaboration between genetics and other clinical departments [57,58].

Pretest counselling: what patients should expect

Genetic counselling is a process that helps individuals to understand and adapt to the medical, psychological, and familial implications of genetic contributions to their disease [59]. As part of pretest genetic management, a multisystem evaluation of the patient and a thorough pedigree analysis are essential for identifying syndromic RCC and at-risk family members with subtle phenotypes [5], particularly given the variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance of these conditions. Before proceeding with genetic testing, individuals should be informed about the purpose, scope, potential outcomes, and implications of the test results [60], and informed consent must be obtained. In the context of pretest counselling for multigene panel testing, high-penetrance syndromes under evaluation should be clearly outlined and particular attention should be paid to the implications of less well-understood or less-penetrant genes and possible variants associated with syndromes not suggested by the personal or family history. In addition, the high rate of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) should be acknowledged [60]. It is crucial to address the potential implications of genetic testing, including the risk of new primary tumours, the preventive or therapeutic options available, surveillance pathways, psychological aspects, and identification of familial risk [60]. In addition, it should be clearly emphasised that further genetic tests may be necessary following the initial test. Patients should also be informed that rigorous clinical follow-up remains necessary, regardless of the genetic testing results.

What genetic testing should be requested and how to interpret the results

When deciding on which genetic test to use, if the molecular genetic diagnosis has already been established within the family, targeted Sanger sequencing of the associated variant identified is sufficient [43]. This approach is cost effective and allows for rapid diagnosis. However, while genetic analysis via Sanger sequencing is excellent at detecting small genetic variations such as single-nucleotide variants and small insertions or deletions, it cannot detect large deletions or insertions.

In the absence of a known familial P/LP variant, genetic testing using a kidney cancer–focused multigene panel or clinically guided single-gene testing is recommended. With NGS, multiple genes can be tested together in a larger panel. If a molecular genetic diagnosis has not been established but the clinical diagnosis is suspected, sequencing of the relevant gene(s) to identify the pathogenic variant is more appropriate. This is consistent with clinical guidelines recommendations [43] and the first consensus conference on RCC genetic risk assessment [4], at which 83% of experts endorsed single-gene testing over multigene panel testing for suspected syndromes with a defined gene. For patients with suspected H-RCC without syndromic features, multigene panels are recommended [4,60]. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) has increased in popularity in oncology but has some limitations, particularly in detecting mosaicism. In addition, expanded genetic testing often results in identification of VUS in a considerable number of cases, which can complicate diagnosis [59]. Finally, the interpretation of genetic test results is typically carried out by diagnostic laboratories in collaboration with clinicians and adheres to the ACMG standards and guidelines for sequence variant classification [61].

Positive genetic testing: what happens next?

Post-test counselling

Post-test genetic counselling involves the delivery of genetic testing results along with comprehensive information about the associated condition. This includes an evaluation of organ-specific penetrance risks and a discussion on follow-up care, preventive strategies, and the therapeutic options available. Cascade testing, which involves offering genetic counselling and testing to at-risk relatives of an individual who has been diagnosed with a genetic condition, depends on the age of renal cancer onset for the specific syndrome, and other disease manifestations [52]. Psychological support should be made available to assist patients and their families in managing the emotional and practical implications of the genetic findings. For complex conditions, centralisation of care via referral to a comprehensive clinic is useful in ensuring care coordination and optimal patient outcomes [5].

Impact of genetics on clinical management of patients

Patients with H-RCC syndromes require a comprehensive multidisciplinary care model involving urologists, oncologists, geneticists, and other specialists, depending on the specific syndrome. Whenever feasible, patients should be under the care of physicians with expertise in managing each specific rare condition, ideally at specialised centres such as a VHL Clinical Care Centre (https://www.vhl.org/) for patients diagnosed with VHL disease [62]. Surveillance and nephron-sparing approaches are usually recommended in most H-RCCs [2,16,43,63]. Ablative treatment options may be considered for those with significant risk factors for surgery, including patients with VHL disease, for which multiple resections can potentially increase the risk of chronic renal failure [62]. Nonetheless, exceptions exist, such as SDH-deficient RCC, cases with larger tumours or aggressive histological features (eg, high-grade or sarcomatoid differentiation), and FH-deficient RCC, for which immediate surgical intervention and radical nephrectomy are recommended owing to the aggressive nature of these tumours [2,43].

Active surveillance of multiple organs is crucial for individuals at risk of VHL disease who do not yet have symptoms, or who are known to have VHL disease but do not yet have clinical manifestations [63]. Renal tumours are managed with active surveillance and then surgical intervention when the largest renal tumour reaches 3 cm in diameter to potentially reduce the risk of metastatic disease while minimising the need for repeat surgeries and preserving renal function [64,65]. Care for patients with VHL disease has been transformed by belzutifan, an HIF2α inhibitor, for which an objective response rate of 49% was observed in a phase 2 trial in VHL-associated RCC without tumours >3 cm requiring immediate surgery and no metastatic disease [66]. Responses to belzutifan were also observed in patients with pancreatic lesions (77%), CNS haemangioblastomas (30%), and retinal haemangioblastomas [66]. Belzutifan was first approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in August 2021 [67], with a more recent conditional approval from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) [68].

In HPRC, renal tumours tend to be slow-growing and are often managed with nephron-sparing surgery when the largest tumour dimension reaches 3 cm [69]. For advanced disease, identification of activating MET variants in patients with HPRC and sporadic papillary RCC has led to trials of MET pathway inhibitors [70]. There are currently no systemic therapy options specifically approved for patients with HPRC-associated metastatic RCC [71]. As with VHL-associated RCC and HPRC, current recommendations for managing FCLN-mutated renal tumours include active surveillance until the largest diameter reaches 3 cm, at which time nephron-sparing surgery is recommended [72]. Metastases are uncommon, and the best systemic treatment approach for metastatic tumours with FCLN mutation is unknown.

Considering the aggressive behaviour of FHTP syndrome–associated RCC, radical nephrectomy should be considered first [2,43]. Favourable outcomes with immune checkpoint inhibitor/tyrosine kinase inhibitor combination therapy among patients with advanced disease have been observed in retrospective series and for the small numbers of patients with FH-deficient RCC included in prospective trials [73,74]. The NCCN guidelines currently recommend the combination of cabozantinib + nivolumab in certain circumstances [43]. The combination of bevacizumab + erlotinib has shown promising results in advanced disease, achieving a response rate of 64% and median progression-free survival of 21.1 mo, compared to 37% and 8.7 mo in sporadic papillary RCC, in a phase 2 trial [75]. Bevacizumab + erlotinib is considered a treatment option for this population according to the ESMO and NCCN guidelines, despite lacking EMA and FDA approval [43]. Both the EMA and FDA have approved everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, for abdominal manifestations of TSC on the basis of its demonstrated efficacy in reducing the volume of renal angiomyolipomas by >50% and delaying disease progression [76]. Everolimus has also been approved for the treatment of subependymal giant-cell astrocytomas and TSC-associated seizures, with promising clinical results [77,78].

Negative genetic testing: what happens next?

Post-test counselling remains essential following a negative result, as it should still cover the possible genetic, clinical, and emotional implications. Individuals must understand that general population cancer risks remain, and that personal risk may still be influenced by other factors.

In cases in which genetic testing yields negative results despite strong clinical suspicion of H-RCC, three possible paths can be taken:•Expand the scope of genetic testing: If initial testing was limited to a single gene or panel testing, consider broader approaches such as WES or whole-genome sequencing.•Evaluate for somatic mosaicism: In cases with no family history but features suggestive of a H-RCC syndrome, somatic mosaicism should also be considered [79].•Account for unknown genetic aetiologies: The possibility of currently unidentified genetic causes of RCC remains. Therefore, it is essential to reanalyse genetic test results annually and incorporate insights from the latest advances in the literature [5].

Conclusions

Several guidelines recognise genetic assessment as a key component of RCC management. However, gaps and controversies exist regarding certain referral criteria. Specific findings may aid a clinician’s assessment and prompt consideration of H-RCC. Nevertheless, physicians may be misled by incomplete information for family history, isolated cases with de novo variants, or unrecognised subtle clinical features. Identification of H-RCC is essential, as it may influence both surgical and, in some cases, systemic management. Therefore, enhancement of education and awareness is essential for health care professionals. While the variety of professionals authorised to provide pretest counselling and request genetic testing is expanding, adequate training and multidisciplinary collaboration with genetic specialists remain paramount.
