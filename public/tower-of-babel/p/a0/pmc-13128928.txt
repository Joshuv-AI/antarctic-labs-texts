Study on the genesis mechanism of geothermal resources in the Yunkai area, South China based on geophysical data
Authors: Yike Zhou, Nansheng Qiu, Chuanqing Zhu, Keyan Liao, Qianqian Feng, Jialin Song, Kefu Li, Zhanwen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Yunkai area is located in the southwest of South China Block and is characterized by widespread granite distribution, dense hot spring occurrences, and well-developed deep faults, indicating significant geothermal resource potential. However, the establishment of a comprehensive geothermal genesis mechanism for this region has been hindered by limited geological data. In this study, geophysical data are employed to constrain the depths of faults and intrusion. Regional granite heat production rates are systematically compiled, and a typical geological profile is modeled using a two-dimensional heat conduction equation to elucidate the geothermal resource genesis mechanism of the Yunkai area. The research indicates that most faults in the study area extend to depths of 0–4 km, with only a few faults in the southwestern region exceeding 8 km. The depth of intrusions are mostly distributed at depths of 6–8 km, while those in the central region are significantly deeper, exceeding 10 km. The heat generation rate of granite ranges from 1.87 to 6.00 µW/m³, with an average value of 3.42 µW/m³, serving as a thermal source to heat underground fluids. Based on these findings, the geothermal genesis mechanism of the Yunkai area is interpreted as follows: atmospheric precipitation originating from surrounding high-altitude mountains infiltrates downward along fracture networks into deeper layers, where it is heated by the surrounding rock. Driven by pressure, this heated fluids migrate upward along deep-seaked faults, mixes with shallow cold groundwater, and undergo further heating due to the radiogenic heat production rate of granite at the surface. The fluids ultimately converge at fault intersections and discharge at the surface, forming hot spring clusters. The southeastern part of the study area shows favorable conditions for the development of high-temperature geothermal resources. This study clarifies the genesis mechanism of geothermal resource in the Yunkai area and provides a scientific basis for future geothermal exploration and development.

Introduction

With the accelerating global energy transition and increasing urgency to mitigate climate change, geothermal resources have emerged as a strategically important component of sustainable energy systems worldwide. Owing to their abundant reserves, widespread distribution, and near-zero carbon emissions, geothermal resources represent a critical foundation for achieving global carbon neutrality and long-term socioeconomic sustainability. Consequently, understanding the geological controls on geothermal systems has become a central focus in both academic research and resource development. Subsurface fault structures play a fundamental role in geothermal systems by acting as conduits for heat and fluid transport and by regulating the thermal regime of geothermal reservoirs. Deep and large-scale fault zones, which are widely developed in tectonically active regions such as plate boundaries, are commonly associated with intense processes including plate subduction, collision, and lithospheric extension. These processes promote large-scale faulting and magmatic upwelling, providing sustained heat sources and efficient pathways for heat transfer from depth to the shallow crust, thereby exerting primary control over geothermal fluid circulation and reservoir temperature distribution1–4. Numerous studies from regions such as the central Mexican Volcanic Belt, northeastern Italy, eastern Elba Island, and Nevada have demonstrated that fault-controlled geothermal systems often give rise to economically viable geothermal anomalies5–8.

The Yunkai region is located along the western segment of the southeastern coastal zone of China, at the junction between the Qinzhou–Hangzhou suture zone and the South China Block. This region is characterized by intense tectonic activity, widespread development of deep-seated faults, and dense occurrences of hot springs, indicating considerable geothermal resource potential9,10. However, despite these favorable geological conditions, geothermal resources in the Yunkai region remain largely undeveloped, and their full potential has yet to be realized. Meanwhile, the region is economically developed and densely populated, resulting in high energy demand and an urgent need for clean and renewable energy sources, such as geothermal energy, to supplement the regional energy supply. Previous studies based on magnetotelluric investigations, geochemical analyses, and forward numerical modeling have inferred the presence of concealed granitic bodies beneath Guangdong Province and revealed interconnected fluids and partial melts within the crust and upper mantle of the Yunkai–Wuyi orogenic belt11–13, while isotopic tracing methods have been applied to estimate the circulation temperatures and depths of geothermal reservoirs in western Guangdong10,14. Nevertheless, most existing studies have focused on large-scale investigations of the South China region, with limited high-resolution research targeting the Yunkai area specifically. Given the pronounced heterogeneity of subsurface geological structures and the scarcity of detailed geological data, the characteristics of fault systems in the Yunkai region remain poorly constrained. In addition, the radiogenic heat production of granitic rocks and its relationship with regional geothermal resources are still not well understood.

In recent years, geophysical methods have been increasingly applied to the study of complex crustal structures due to their sensitive response to deep physical property parameters15–18, becoming an important means to analyze the genesis mechanisms of geothermal resources. Among these methods, gravity and magnetic techniques are currently the most widely used for identifying subsurface fault structures, as filtering and derivative procedures can effectively enhance geological lineaments such as faults and intrusive bodies. Compared with traditional approaches, including seismic surveys and field observations, gravity data can be extracted from global gravity field models and allow for the integrated delineation of fault systems and estimation of fault depths, thereby providing higher-resolution constraints for geothermal exploration19–21. For example, gravity-based studies in the northern Kenya Rift3 and the Nigerian Benue Trough22 have successfully identified and interpreted fault systems, offering valuable insights into fault-controlled geothermal fluid migration. Accordingly, this study applies gravity methods to constrain the depths of fault structures and intrusive bodies in the Yunkai region15–18, addressing a critical gap in regional research. Furthermore, by integrating geophysical data with the thermal properties of rocks, thermal simulations are conducted along representative profiles to comprehensively assess the genesis mechanism of geothermal resources in the Yunkai area. The results of this study provide a scientific basis for future geothermal exploration and development in the Yunkai region.

Geological settings

The South China Block is a significant Precambrian terrane in East Asia, formed through the amalgamation of the Yangtze Block in the northwest and the Cathaysia Block in the southeast along the Jiangnan Orogen during the Neoproterozoic(Fig. 1a)23–27. The South China Block has experienced multiple phases of orogeny from the Neoproterozoic to the Mesozoic, accompanied by extensive magmatism, strong deformation, and metamorphism and migmatization26–31. During the Hercynian-Indosinian period, the South China Block underwent intense intracontinental orogeny along the NW-trending Bobai–Luoding–Guangning Fault Zone with the Xianggui Block, characterized by intense intracontinental compression and the development of extensive granites28,32. Since the early Yanshanian, the tectonic setting of the South China Block has involved intracontinental lithospheric extension and asthenospheric upwelling, resulting in the emplacement of Mesozoic granites over a large area of South China13,33.

The Yunkai area is situated in the southwestern segment of the South China Block, at the intersection of the Qinzhou-Hangzhou boundary and the South China Block. It extends from Sihui in Guangning to the south of Lianjiang, and is bounded by the WuChuan-Sihui fault zone and the LingShan-Tengxian fault zone, forming a NE- to NNE-trending structural domain34,35. The Yunkai area constitutes a key exposure zone of the ancient basement within the South China Block. This basement comprises Precambrian metamorphic rocks exhibiting a dual-layer structure. The deeper section consists of the Xinyi-Gaozhou Complex, which has undergone amphibolite- to granulite-facies metamorphism. The shallower section is characterized by micaceous schists, metamorphosed volcanic rocks, and weakly metamorphosed sedimentary rocks36,37. Under the influence of multiple phases of tectonic and magmatic activity, this region has developed a series of NE- and NW-trending fault zones.Major regional faults include the Bobai-Cenxi fault zone, the Licun-Wendi fault zone, the Xinyi-Lianjiang fault zone, and the WuChuan-Sihui fault zone(Fig. 1b)38,39. These deep-seated faults exert significant control over the topography and hydrogeological conditions. They serve as efficient conduits for the ascent of deep thermal energy, thereby favoring the development of geothermal water reservoirs.

The thermal flow in the Yunkai area is primarily influenced by the tectonic setting. Tectonically proximal to plate boundaries and characterized by intense tectonic activity, this region exhibits a high heat flow40. Based on the magnitude of the terrestrial heat flow, the study area is divided into four thermal flow zones (Fig. 1d). The regions of Gaozhou, Maoming, Xinyi, Yangjiang, and Yangchun are classified as high-value thermal flow areas, with thermal flow exceeding 80mW/m241. According to the Compilation of Terrestrial Heat Flow Data in Continental China (5th edition)42 and field measurements by Wang et al.10, the terrestrial heat flow in Yunkai region is 60-98.2mW/m2. This exceeds both the average heat flow of 61.5 mW/m² for the continental region of China and the global continental average of 67 mW/m243. The distribution of hot springs and thermal flow reveals that hot springs are predominantly distributed within areas of high terrestrial heat flow. These are closely associated with magmatic-tectonic activity and are controlled by NE-trending deep faults. They are primarily hosted within highly fractured zones along the fault belts, forming convective low-to-medium temperature hydrothermal systems that circulate along these deep fault zones. However, the thermal background characteristics of the hot spring geothermal resources in this region remain poorly constrained, hindering the exploration and utilization of geothermal resources.

Data and methods

Gravity data

The gravity field data used in this study are derived from the XGM2019e model (https://icgem.gfz-potsdam.de/home), which combines satellite models GOCO06s and NGA’s 15’ ground gravity anomaly dataset, along with 1’ enhanced data sets derived from ocean altimetry and continental topography35. The spatial resolution of gravity data is 2’ (~ 4 km). This study focuses on the region between 109.5°E and 112°E, and 21.5°N and 23.5°N, extracting Bouguer gravity anomalies to serve as the foundation for the research (Fig. 2). Power spectrum analysis

Gravity anomalies arise from lateral variations in subsurface density or from density contrasts between geological bodies and their surrounding rocks. In this study, the average depth to the top of density sources is estimated using the power spectrum analysis method, which is based on the Fourier transformation of gravity anomaly data46. This method provides a quantitative means to analyze potential-field data in the frequency domain and to infer the depth distribution of subsurface density interfaces. The power spectrum analysis enables the separation of regional (low-pass) and residual (high-pass) components, allowing for the estimation of major crustal structural parametres, including Moho depth, crustal thickness, and basement interface depth47–50. The method proposed by Spector et al.51 derives the power spectrum of anomalies in the frequency domain. The spectral slope observed in the low-frequency range serves as a statistical assessment of the mean depth to the upper boundary of density anomalies within the study region, corresponding to the average depth to crustal density boundaries.

A plot is constructed with the natural logarithm of the spectral values against the wavenumber. The slope of each segment provides the proportional average depth of the each density interfaces52:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h = K/4\pi$$\end{document}

where h represents the mean estimated depth (km) to the upper boundary of the primary density anomaly, while K corresponds to the gradient of the fitted linear segment. The boundary wavenumber distinguishing distinct slope regions serves as the cutoff wavenumber for the deeper components of the density structure. By calculating the critical wavelength through dividing 2π by this cutoff wavenumber, it becomes possible to implement low-pass or high-pass filtering techniques on potential field datasets. This methodology facilitates the distinction of gravitational anomaly contributions arising from density variations at different subsurface depth ranges.

Derivative calculations

Following the separation of regional and residual gravity anomalies, it is necessary to calculate the derivative of the local gravity anomalies, because the spatial derivatives of gravity data are usually used for qualitative and semi-quantitative interpretation, and the horizontal and vertical gradients can indicate the edge position of the anomaly source53,54. The Total Horizontal Derivative (THD) can highlight the density contrast of local fault systems. On nearly vertical and isolated fault surfaces, the maximum THD of gravity is located above the top edge of the fault surface. In the case of inclined faults, the maximum value shifts to the top of the fault plane, making it useful for identifying the location of faults. When the density source is deep, both THD and VD are relatively low. Power spectrum calculation

The power spectrum analysis of the Bouguer gravity anomaly in the Yunkai area is shown in Fig. 4, and the corresponding parameters are shown in Table 2. Based on previous studies of Moho depth, crustal structure, and granitic intrusions in South China10–13,67−69, together with the regional geological background, four distinct slope segments were identified in the power spectrum, representing four prominent density interfaces. The average estimates of its interface depth are 28.67 km, 20.79 km, 9.39 km, 4.31 km, respectively. In light of previous investigations of crustal structure, the interface at 28.67 km is interpreted as the average Moho depth inferred from gravity data in the study area, which is consistent with the results reported by Huang70 and Cheng13. The interface at 20.79 km is inferred to represent the boundary between the middle and lower crust, while the interface at 9.39 km corresponds to the boundary between the upper and middle crust and is comparable to the crustal layering indicated by the the Crust1.0 model. The interface at 4.31 km is interpreted as the basal surface of granitic intrusions within the upper crust, which is broadly consistent with previous estimates by Liao18. Therefore, we believe that the four interfaces identified by the power spectrum are reliable.

By applying a high-pass filter with a cutoff wavelength of 263.64 km to separate the Bouguer gravity data, we obtained the local gravity anomaly (Fig. 5a) mainly generated by the crust in the depth range of 0–28.67 km and the regional gravity anomaly (Fig. 5b) with a depth greater than 28.67 km. The local gravity anomaly eliminates the scattered gravity field caused by regional effects, allowing us to characterize density anomaly that are shallowly buried and have limited horizontal extent. The local gravity anomaly in the Yunkai area range from-22 to 17 mGal. Significant positive anomaly is found in the northern region and the southern Wuchuan-Gaozhou belt, indicating a relatively shallow Moho depth and suggesting the presence of mantle-derived intrusions in the crust. Significant negative anomaly is observed in eastern Xinyi, reflecting a deeper sedimentary basement with greater thickness10. The regional gravity anomaly reveals anomalies originating from deeper gravity sources, ranging from − 40 to -17 mGal. A negative gravity anomaly center is located in the central part of the region, and the anomaly values gradually increase as they extend outward, contrasting with the topographic relief of the region. From the land to the ocean, the anomaly values gradually increase, indicating a gradual increase in the overall density of the mantle, which may be related to the lithospheric extension and thinning since the Cenozoic71.

Derivative calculations

To enhance the contrast of gravity anomaly variations, the local gravity anomalies were upward continued to heights of 5 km and 10 km, respectively. The Total Horizontal Derivative (THD) of the upward-continued local gravity anomalies is shown in Fig. 6a,b. The THD calculated from the 5 km upward continuation still exhibits relatively strong noise interference. In contrast, the 10 km upward continuation more effectively suppresses shallow-source effects, resulting in clearer and more coherent anomaly patterns. Consequently, the THD derived from the 10 km upward-continued gravity anomalies was selected for fault identification (Fig. 6b). The NE-trending main faults are more easily identified on the THD map, with the LingShan-Tengxian fault, the Bobai-Wuzhou fault, the Xinyi-Lianjiang fault, and the Wuchuan-Sihui fault being the most typical examples. In the southern part of the study area, between the main fault zones of the Xinyi-Lianjiang Fault and the Wuchuan-Sihui Fault, numerous small-scale faults with intersecting NE and NW-trending are densely distributed. This area exhibits the highest THD values, indicating strong density contrasts associated with these faults within the crust. Additionally, a series of NE-trending faults are also present in the central region, and a NW-trending fault traverses both the Bobai-Wuzhou Fault and the Licun-Wendi Fault, displaying distinct fault characteristics. Comparison with surface-identified faults reveals that the THD with local gravity anomaly can effectively identify both NE- and NW-trending faults, which largely match those identified on the surface. However, the fault identification results in the northeastern part of the Xinyi-Lianjiang Fault differ from those identified on the surface. We infer that complex surface structures obscure the signatures of the deeper faults, and only faults with a distinct density contrast at the fault plane will show a significant change in the THD.

The identified faults were projected onto the VD and TD maps (Fig. 6c,d). In the VD map, the faults are generally distributed near the zero-value lines. The TD map provides an in-depth estimate of the density source causing gravity anomalies, with the distance between the tilt angles − 45° and 45° contour lines is approximately twice the depth of the density source15. The positive tilt angles point towards the position of the high-density sources. In the central and southern parts of the study area, the fracture network shows that high-density sources are located in the lower crust. Based on the Moho morphology, basaltic intrusions may exist in this region’s lower crust. The density bodies between the southwestern Bobai-Wuzhou fault zone and the Licun-Wendi fault zone, as well as those between the Lingshan-Tengxian fault zone and the Baojiu-Wuzhou fault zone, are estimated to be located at the base of the upper crust. These are inferred to result from the upward of mantle-derived material, indicating high-density sources18.

Discussion

Structural control of deep-seated faults on hot springs distribution

The results of gravity derivative analysis and Euler deconvolution collectively indicate that the NE-trending deep-seated faults represent the dominant heat-conducting structures in the Yunkai area. These faults penetrate deep into the crust and provide efficient pathways for upward heat and fluid transport, thereby exerting a first-order control on the spatial distribution of geothermal anomalies. Although some surface-mapped faults cannot be directly resolved by gravity data, gravity-derived interpretations effectively reveal concealed fault structures that are not exposed at the surface. The integration of surface geological observations with gravity-based fault identification therefore provides a more comprehensive understanding of the regional fault architecture(Fig. 8). The spatial coincidence of hot spring clusters with fault intersections—particularly at the junction of the Wuchuan–Sihui Fault Zone and NW-trending faults, as well as within the southern fault intersection network—highlights the critical role of fault connectivity in geothermal system development. Areas with a high density of hot spring occurrences are generally associated with relatively low Bouguer gravity anomalies. Furthermore, comparison with the elevation data and hot spring distribution shown in Fig. 1c indicates that geothermal anomaly zones, hot spring outcrops, and pronounced mountainous relief consistently correspond to gravity low regions. This fault-controlled geothermal pattern is consistent with previous studies in the Yunkai region and adjacent areas, which have emphasized the significance of NE-trending faults in governing geothermal heat anomalies and fluid migration pathways11,39. These insights provide important implications for geothermal resource exploration, indicating that fault intersection zones associated with gravity lows represent favorable targets for future geothermal prospecting.

The genesis and distribution of hot springs are predominantly governed by tectonic-thermal events since the Meso-Cenozoic, exhibiting a diminishing trend with increasing distance from plate boundaries73. Influenced by the Paleo-Pacific Plate subduction, the South China Block has undergone extension since the Middle Jurassic.This process has generated an upper mantle upwelling zone at depth, resulting in localized Moho uplift, lithospheric thinning, and asthenospheric upwelling13,74–76. The higher density of the basement compared to surrounding areas results in high gravity anomalies in rift basins. Basaltic magma surges to the bottom of the lower crust, serving as a heat source that causes high-temperature melt to intrude into the upper crust, leading to extensive medium-acidic magmatic-tectonic activities. This has resulted in the formation of large-scale ductile shear zones and a series of NE-trending regional active faults16,77,78. These deep faults serve as excellent conduits for the upwelling of deep thermal fluids, promoting the exposure of deep hydrothermal fluids at fault intersections. The development of fracture zones facilitates the formation of geothermal reservoirs, leading to the formation of hot spring clusters.Independent support for this interpretation is provided by isotopic tracer studies. Hydrogen and oxygen isotope (δ ²H and δ ¹⁸O) data indicate that regional geothermal waters are of meteoric origin, recharged by precipitation from the Yunkai Mountains. After infiltrating downward along deep fault zones, the fluids are heated at depth and subsequently mix with shallow cold waters during ascent, accompanied by water–rock interaction. Variations in lithology and heat sources among different fault zones influence the depth of fluid circulation, highlighting the critical role of fault activity in controlling fluid migration pathways and hydrothermal circulation patterns72,79.

Radiogenic heat contribution of granitic intrusions

The shallow high-heat-production granitic intrusions constitute a crucial thermal source for geothermal anomalies in the Yunkai area, primarily associated with widespread Paleozoic-Mesozoic granites. These high-heat-production granite significantly elevate regional heat flow29,80. Granites with heat production rate greater than 3µW/m³may contribute over 80% to crustal heat flow81. Surface heat flow records in the Yunkai area range from 60 to 98.2 mW/m², exceeding the Chinese continental average of 60.9 mW/m²43. On one hand, the Yunkai area exhibits a relatively thin crust70,82, with granitic intrusions in the upper crust.The average depth of radioactive elements (U, Th, K) in the crust is 10 km83, leading to a relatively high heat generation rate. In contrast, the middle and lower crust have a lower heat generation rate, with the crust contributing significantly to the total heat flow. On the other hand, high terrestrial heat flow anomalies correlate with deep-seated fault activity. The Wuchuan-Sihui fault zone, an important regional active fault zone in the Yunkai area, is over 800 km long. The most recent activity occurred about 0.4-0.3 Ma ago during the mid-Middle Pleistocene10,39. Multiple high-value points with large-scale heat flow values greater than 80mW/m2 are distributed along this fault in a beaded distribution patterns41.

Calculations of heat production rates for major granitic intrusions in the study area (Table 1) reveal values ranging from 1.87 to 6.00 µW/m³, with an average of 3.42 µW/m³, exceeding the Chinese continental crustal average of 2.53 µW/m³. Notably, the Shicun granitic intrusions exhibits the highest heat generation rate, averaging 5.01 µW/m³, followed by Guangping granitic intrusions, averaging 3.68 µW/m³. During Indosinian-Yanshanian magmatic upwelling, radiogenic elements migrated upward with the magma activity and enriched in shallow crustal levels84, forming high-heat-production granites that thermally control spring distribution39. Zhou83 found that the peak of radioactive heat generation corresponds to the period of magma activity, indicating that radioactive elements migrated to the upper crust in a single event during the peak of magma activity, rather than through multiple cycles of magma remelting. Most springs emerge near large batholith margins, exhibiting strong spatial correlations with granite intrusions and heat production rates. These granites act as a heat source for the shallow surface, providing additional heat to the hot springs.

Representative cross-section thermal modeling

Based on magnetotelluric profiles, this study performed thermal simulations for the L1 and L2 sections using COMSOL Multiphysics under the following constrained parameters: a mean Moho temperature of 695 °C39; the densities of the crust and mantle of 2600 kg/m3 and 2900 kg/m385, and the thermal conductivities of the upper and lower crust and mantle of 3.1 W/(m·k), 2.7 W/ (m·k) and 2.0 W/(m·k)85,86. The density of the granitic intrusions were set at 2800 kg/m3, with a thermal conductivity of 3.7 W/(m·k)87,88. The surface temperature was set to the region’s annual average temperature of 22 °C, and the boundaries on both sides were treated as adiabatic conditions, with no lateral heat exchange. Profile L1 comprises a total of 15,716 grid cells, with an average grid area of 0.8739 km², while Profile L2 consists of 27,470 grid cells, with an average grid area of 0.8855 km². Due to the lack of detailed information on subsurface fluid occurrence, fluid flow, and heat transport mechanisms in the study area, it is difficult to reliably simulate coupled fluid–heat transfer processes. Therefore, this study considers only conductive heat transfer in the subsurface temperature field, and the numerical modeling is restricted to a two-dimensional heat conduction equation.

The Curie isotherm represents the depth–temperature interface at which crustal temperatures approach the demagnetization temperature of ferromagnetic minerals and can be regarded as the basal boundary of the crustal magnetic layer. Estimates of the Curie isotherm depth provide important constraints on deep crustal temperatures and can therefore be used to evaluate the reliability of thermal modeling results. Zhang et al.39 adopted a Curie temperature of 550 °C as the representative average Curie point temperature for the coastal regions of Guangdong Province. Accordingly, this temperature was used in this study as a benchmark for model validation. The 550 °C isotherm extracted from the simulated temperature field indicates depths ranging from 17.0 to 22.3 km along the two profiles. These values are generally consistent with Curie depth estimates of 16.9–24.9 km for the Guangdong coastal region derived from magnetic anomaly spectral analysis by Zhang et al.67 and Xiong89. This agreement suggests that the thermal modeling results presented in this study are reliable.

To facilitate observation and comparison, we established two simulated wells (XN1 and XN2) to analyze spatial temperature variations. In the depth-temperature profile of L1 section for well XN1 (Fig. 9a), the area with granite shows a significant increase in temperature, indicating that the high-heat-producing granitic intrusions is one of the key heat sources in the region. For well XN2, a turning point begins around 18 km, where the temperature rises significantly. This thermal anomaly results from advective heat transport by deep-derived fluids migrating along deep-seated faults, indicating active hydrothermal circulation proximal to major fault zones—consistent with findings by Zhang39. In the depth-temperature profile of L2 section for well XN1 (Fig. 10a), thermal convection occurs at ~ 5 km depth due to fault-induced fluid circulation, while high heat-production granites at depths initiating from ~ 13 km drive significant temperature increase. At ~ 23 km depth, within the mantle uplift zone, the Moho interface exhibits localized shallowing, facilitating the upward geological emplacement of deep-seated high-temperature melts13,74–76, thereby leading to higher temperatures compared to the western part. For well XN2, thermal convection develops near faults at ~ 4 km depth, and a granitic intrusion at ~ 8 km depth with a high radiogenic heat production rate, which can serve as a heat source to heat the fluid in the fault, resulting in an increase in temperature.

Depth-temperature results from simulated wells across both profiles consistently reveal thermal convection anomalies proximal to fault zones, indicative of advective heat transfer by ascending deep-sourced materials. This confirms that deep-seated faults function as efficient conduits for deep thermal fluxes, controlling the migration of underground fluids90. Magmatic activities provide both thermal energy and material sources for hydrothermal systems, The heat released during the cooling of magma heats the underground fluids, forming high-temperature hydrothermal fluids. These fluids discharge preferentially at fault intersections, ultimately emerging as thermal springs at the surface10,91–93.

According to China’s geothermal resource assessment methodology and estimation procedures, geological resources with temperatures exceeding 150 °C are classified as high-temperature geothermal resources, where shallower burial depths correspond to higher exploitation value.The 150 °C contour lines of both sections correspond well to the boundary between the upper crust, indicating that the shallower the upper crust is buried, the greater the geothermal resource potential. In the southeastern study area, temperatures reach 150 °C at ~ 4.5 km depth, consistent with 3D modeling results of 150 °C thermal distribution by Liao18. Considering development costs, only areas with temperatures exceeding 150 °C and depths less than 5 km possess viable potential for high-temperature geothermal resource exploitation. Concurrently, the region’s favorable topography and high population density ensure effective resource utilization.

Genesis mechanism of hot spring geothermal resources

The occurrence conditions of geothermal water are controlled by geological structures and magmatic rocks94. Hydrogen and oxygen isotopic results from geothermal water in the Yunkai area indicate an origin from meteoric precipitation79. Considering the control of groundwater by faults, it is inferred that this is a typical deep-circulation fracture-controlled hydrothermal system10,95–97. The hydrochemical facies of cold groundwater in the study area are predominantly HCO3-Ca type、HCO3·Cl-Na·Ca type. Geothermal water exhibits a transition in facies from HCO₃·Cl-Na·Ca type inland to Cl-Na in coastal areas. Both cold and geothermal waters are recharged by meteoric precipitation infiltrating from nearby low mountains14,98, percolating downward through fault fractures and granite weathering fractures into deeper zones. As the reservoir rock is granite, silicate-aluminate minerals within the granite undergo accelerated decomposition under high temperature and pressure conditions. This process increases the concentrations of potassium (K⁺) and sodium (Na⁺) ions in the water, forming HCO₃-Na type water. Thermal exchange occurs between the percolating meteoric water and the hotter surrounding rock, causing the water to acquire the mineral characteristics of the host rock. This phenomenon is the result of water-rock interaction, typically associated with the emplacement of Caledonian and Yanshanian intermediate-acidic granites and diorite intrusions94,98,99. The circulation depths of thermal springs vary across different regions within the study area.In the Xinyi, Gaozhou, Maoming, and Lianjiang areas, estimated reservoir temperatures range from 63.2 °C to 122.5 °C, corresponding to circulation depths of approximately 1.45 km to 3.43 km.Near Yangchun, the reservoir temperatures are between 148 and 162℃, with circulation depths ranging from 3.9 to 4.7 m10,100. This spatial distribution correlates with the observed gravity anomalies: areas characterized by lower gravity anomalies generally exhibit higher reservoir temperatures and greater circulation depths, whereas areas with higher gravity anomalies show lower reservoir temperatures and shallower circulation depths.

The primary thermal conductive structure in the Yunkai area is a deep NE-trending major fault, which intersects with a NW-trending secondary fault zone, forming a region with frequent hydrothermal activities. This indicates that fault structures significantly influence hydrothermal activities10,73. The deep heat source primarily originates from the uplift of the upper mantle, the influx of energy from the asthenosphere into the crust, and high-temperature hydrothermal activities near the Curie isotherm69,76. Euler deconvolution analyses confirm these deep faults penetrate the crust, potentially connecting to high-temperature hydrothermal reservoirs13,39. Hot springs often emerge near large-scale faults and are closely related to the scale of pluton and their radiogenic heat production rates. A portion of the heat generated by radiogenic processes in pluton is dissipated through thermal conduction or groundwater transport. The heat produced by an intrusive body depends on its morphology, and the formation of highly radiogenic granite requires larger-volume intrusions enriched in radioactive elements9,101,102. According to the previous understanding and the conclusions of this study, the genesis mechanism of hot spring geothermal resources in Yunkai area is shown in Fig. 11, meteoric precipitation from surrounding high-altitude mountains percolates downward through fractures to variable depths dictated by subsurface structures, undergoes thermal exchange with surrounding rocks under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions, and heats to 63.2–162 °C by granite. The fluid then moves upward along the NE-trending deep fault, mixes with shallow groundwater during upflow, and is reheated by heat-producing granites at shallow levels, and ultimately discharge at the intersection of the NE- and NW-trending faults, emerges to the surface, and forms a cluster of hot springs.

Research limitations and future prospects

Owing to the limited availability of subsurface data in the Yun–Kai region, the rock thermal physical parameters used in the two-dimensional thermal simulations were adopted from representative values of the Pearl River Delta and adjacent areas, rather than from in situ measurements within the study area. In addition, subsurface fluid circulation and its contribution to heat transport were not considered in the numerical modeling. As a result, discrepancies may exist between the simulated temperature field and the actual geothermal conditions. Future studies should incorporate in situ measurements of rock thermal properties and employ three-dimensional coupled thermo–hydraulic modeling to achieve a more refined characterization of the regional geothermal structure. Furthermore, this study primarily relies on geophysical data, with gravity observations providing an effective means of resolving deep-seated density structures. However, interpretations based solely on gravity data are inherently non-unique. Future studies should integrate multi-source geophysical datasets with diverse geochemical and isotopic tracing techniques. For instance, δ¹⁸O and δ²H can be employed to identify the origin of geothermal fluids and to infer reservoir temperatures and circulation depths. He isotopes are effective in constraining the nature of the heat source and quantifying contributions from deep-seated materials. Sr isotopes can be utilized to trace fluid–rock interactions and to determine the provenance of dissolved constituents. Through such an integrated approach, the fault-controlled fluid circulation patterns and genesis mechanism of geothermal resources in the Yunkai area can be comprehensively elucidated.

Conclusions

The fault depths in the Yunkai area range from 0 to 4 km, with a few deep major faults located in the southwestern part of the region. The depths of intrusive bodies exhibit a pattern of being deeper in the center and shallower around the periphery, characterized by central intrusive bodies generally exceeding 10 km in depth, while those in the surrounding areas mostly range between 6 and 8 km. The fault depths in the Yunkai area range from 0 to 4 km, with a few deep major faults located in the southwestern part of the region. The depths of intrusive bodies exhibit a pattern of being deeper in the center and shallower around the periphery, characterized by central intrusive bodies generally exceeding 10 km in depth, while those in the surrounding areas mostly range between 6 and 8 km. The central intrusive bodies are likely derived from mantle upwelling.
