Modelling the time to cervical cancer elimination in England: strategies for achieving elimination by 2040
Authors: Cody Palmer, Olga Ovcinnikova-Hutchings, Kayla Engelbrecht, Giampiero Favato, Alexandra Stewart, Susan Lalondrelle, J Chatterjee, Dionysios Ntais, Vincent Daniels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNational Health Service (NHS) England has committed to eliminating cervical cancer as a public health problem by 2040, defined as an incidence of below 4 per 100 000 woman-years. This study aims to estimate the contributions of currently available interventions to cervical cancer elimination and assess how improvements in human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination coverage, cervical cancer screening and treatment following an abnormal screen would impact the timeline for achieving this elimination target.MethodsUsing a previously published mathematical model that captured historical changes in HPV vaccination and screening programmes in England, cervical cancer incidence was forecasted from 2024 through 2124. Various scenarios were explored to assess the impact of resolving issues in disparities of screening coverage, low adherence to follow-up of abnormal screens and increasing vaccination coverage over time. Consistent with other cervical cancer elimination models in high-income countries, we also considered a more ambitious cervical cancer incidence threshold of below 1 per 100 000 woman-years.ResultsWith current screening and vaccination levels, it was estimated that England could achieve cervical cancer elimination (incidence of below 4 per 100 000) by approximately 2050. Achieving this target by 2040 would necessitate improved follow-up of abnormal screens and increased cervical cancer screening uptake in under-screened populations. The aspirational threshold can only ever be achieved with both improved screening (in follow-up and coverage disparities) and high vaccination rates. These results are consistent with other UK-specific model results.ConclusionsImprovements in follow-up of abnormal screens and decreasing disparities in screening coverage will be necessary if England is to attain the 2040 elimination goal. Meanwhile, consistent high vaccination coverage will ensure future population protection and enable more aspirational thresholds to be achieved.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is the 14th most prevalent cancer and 19th most common cause of cancer mortality among females in the UK, with approximately 3300 new cases every year and 850 deaths annually.1 It is widely accepted that infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common cause of developing CC, accounting for an estimated 90–99% of CC cases. CC is a vaccine-preventable disease that would relieve a significant financial burden, estimated at £406 million annually, on the UK healthcare system and wider economy were elimination to be achieved.2

The WHO has defined elimination as reaching, and maintaining, an incidence rate of below 4 per 100 000 women-years and has set three key targets that countries must aim to achieve by 2030 in order to meet CC elimination goals within the next century3:

90% of girls fully vaccinated with the HPV vaccine by the age of 15.

90% of girls fully vaccinated with the HPV vaccine by the age of 15.

70% of women screened using a high-performance test by the age of 35, and again by the age of 45.

70% of women screened using a high-performance test by the age of 35, and again by the age of 45.

90% of women with pre-cancer treated and 90% of women with invasive cancer managed.

90% of women with pre-cancer treated and 90% of women with invasive cancer managed.

The UK has implemented successful HPV vaccination and CC screening programmes that have contributed to the historical decline in CC incidence through high levels of coverage and uptake. A routine school-based HPV national immunisation programme (NIP) was introduced in 2009 for girls aged 12–13 years of age and extended to include boys as part of gender-neutral vaccination in 2019.4 Recently, the UK shifted from a 2-dose to a single dose schedule for those eligible under the routine adolescent and universal HPV immunisation programme and gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men under the age of 25 years commencing in the 2023 academic year.5 Meanwhile, the National Health Service Cervical Screening Programme, a national call and re-call CC screening programme consisting of primary HPV testing since 2019, offers women and people with a cervix aged 25 to 64 screening once every 5 years.6

The success of these prevention strategies has been demonstrated with an 83.9% decline in age-adjusted standardised incidence rate of CC and stage 3 cervical intraepithelial neoplasia in a cohort offered routine HPV vaccination compared with women never offered vaccination in England.7 Meanwhile, in an observational study of the population that received bivalent HPV vaccination at 12 to 13 years of age in Scotland, no CC cases were reported at the most recent study follow-up.8 While historical vaccination coverage rates (VCRs) have been high but falling short of the 90% target set by the WHO, the COVID-19 pandemic saw a sharp decline in coverage that has yet to recover to pre-pandemic levels. This is compounded by frequently reported inequalities in vaccination and screening uptake. Screening uptake has historically been lower in the more deprived regions where women are less likely to engage with the healthcare system, among Black, Asian and women of minority ethnic backgrounds, areas with the largest Muslim population and individuals with learning or physical disabilities, or with severe mental health issues.9 10 All these inequalities are evidenced through disparities in CC incidence across the UK.7

In 2023, NHS England (NHSE) made a pledge to eliminate CC by 2040, making it one of the first countries to make a public commitment.11 The NHS England plan for CC elimination (2025) outlines comprehensive measures to achieve this goal, including increasing HPV vaccination rates, improving cervical screening coverage and ensuring treatment for cervical disease.12 This is supported by the NHS 10 Year Health Plan.13 In addition, CC elimination aligns with cancer strategies in the remaining three devolved nations of the UK (Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland). A Scottish government expert group report highlighted that CC elimination could be achieved in the next 25 years; however, if stark inequalities remain unaddressed, then this target may never be reached for those living in the most deprived areas.14 None of the devolved nations to date have followed in the steps of England to make a public commitment to elimination by a target date.

The objective of this study is to understand how current prevention strategies are contributing to the elimination of CC in England and to forecast the year that elimination will be met if current efforts are maintained. In addition, the study will model the impacts on achieving elimination through improvements in vaccination coverage, screening uptake and treatment of pre-cancerous and cancerous cervical disease.

Materials and methods

Model overview

A previously developed model of HPV transmission and progression to disease was adapted to the UK, and specifically to England. This model has been used in previous assessments of cost-effectiveness in the UK, in which all HPV-related diseases were considered.15 16 We will briefly summarise the model structure here and discuss some of the relevant changes to the model since the previous publication.

The model is a compartmental model that captures the main demographic and epidemiological features that govern HPV in England, summarised below with full details available in online supplemental file 1. In addition to modelling the primary epidemiological features of HPV, the model also captures the primary interventions against cervical diseases, in particular, CC screening and adolescent HPV vaccination. Implementation of these interventions has changed substantially over the years, including the move to 25 years of age for the initiation of screening, the expansion of the girls-only school-based HPV vaccination programme to include boys, the use of higher valency vaccines, and changes in the number of vaccine doses given, all changes which are accounted for in the model. Given the limited trial data currently available to inform long-term protection in reducing the number of doses, the model also accounts for this uncertainty by investigating outcomes for a range of possible durations of protection.

Since it is a large model, a substantial amount of data was required to adapt the model to England’s situation. CC disease burden is sourced from the Office for National Statistics,1 17 screening coverage and screening programme performance is sourced from NHS England1 and historical vaccine coverage from the UK Health Security Agency.41820 Parameter ranges and values were sourced from the literature where available. More details on these inputs are available in online supplemental file 1.

The population of the model is divided by age, sex, sexual activity and cervical screening intensity. The proportion of the population in each class is computed following various methods described in online supplemental file 1. Sexual mixing between these subpopulations is based on sexual behaviour data: average number of new partners in the last year, and the proportion of partners by age group. Mixing by sexual activity is computed according to an assumed level of assortative and random mixing.

HPV infections in the model can be transient or persistent. Transient infections are those that can clear. Persistent infections can progress to cervical precancerous disease, with 3 levels of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and two stages of carcinoma in situ prior to invasive cancer. Recovery from HPV infection of a particular type can provide some protection against a subsequent infection of that type if seroconversion occurs.2123 The rate of seroconversion and the degree of protection imparted are calibrated (online supplemental file 1).

Vaccination is modelled with a degree of protection and duration of protection against transient infection, persistent infection and disease. Thus, breakthrough infections have a decreased chance of becoming persistent, and breakthrough persistent infections have a much smaller chance of progressing to disease. The vaccinated population is also subdivided by the number of doses they receive, which is important when we consider scenarios where a single dose may not have lifetime duration of protection. Given the limited trial data currently available to inform long-term protection from reduced number of doses, the model also accounts for this uncertainty by investigating outcomes for a range of possible durations of protection. For example, we consider a range of durations of protection for types 16 and 18 of between 3.8 and 122 years (effectively lifetime protection) for the single-dose regimen (current UK recommended dosing). The single-dose degree and duration assumptions were derived by Daniels et al24 from the 36-month KEN SHE single-dose clinical trial data.25 Vaccination rates are set to match historical data and the full-series vaccine efficacy degree of protection is derived from clinical trial data and assumes lifelong protection (see online supplemental file 1 for details).

The division of the population based on screening intensity assumes that the overall average coverage of screening results from some subpopulations being over-screened while the other subpopulations are under-screened. The relative sizes of these populations and the difference in screening intensity are calibrated. We also assumed that some identified cases of precancers, 3% to 7% depending on stage,26 were not followed up, and thus left untreated, but could be picked up in a subsequent screen.

Uncertain parameters were calibrated by minimising the mean-squared error accounting for model outputs related to cancer incidence data and HPV infection prevalence. The results of this calibration, along with measures of goodness of fit, are presented in online supplemental file 1.

Model validation

To validate the model, we estimated the decrease in HPV prevalence from the model for comparison with data from sexually transmitted infection screening from 2010 to 2020.27 However, it was not immediately clear from the outset that the population from the data was representative. To allow for comparison with the model, it was necessary to control for differences in baseline HPV prevalence and vaccination coverage. To account for differing vaccine coverages, an adjusted prevalence from the model was used, with weights selected so that the vaccine coverage is the same. To account for the difference in baseline HPV infection, proportionate reductions in HPV prevalence were considered. CIs for the proportionate reductions given by the data were computed via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (methods). See online supplemental file 1 for more information.

Results

Model validation

The model was validated against real-world data by comparing the reduction in age-specific female HPV16 and HPV18 prevalence over time among 16–18 year olds and 19–21 year olds in the UK27 (figure 1). Among 16–18 year-olds, the modelled reduction in prevalence of HPV16 and HPV18 over time fit within the real-world data CIs at each time point. Very wide CIs were observed in the real-world data in this age category in 2020. Modelled reduction HPV16 prevalence closely tracked the real-world data among 19–21 year-olds, only overestimating the proportion reduction in the 2012–2013 period above the calculated CIs. The real-world data collected from STI clinics showed an observed spike in the proportion reduction of HPV18 prevalence in 2016–2017 in the 18–21 year age category that is not reflected in the modelled data but still falls within the CIs indicating no significant difference.

Discussion

If current efforts of HPV vaccination, cervical screening and follow-up for cervical disease are maintained, England is modelled to achieve the 4 per 100 000 women target for CC elimination in approximately 2052. Addressing the heterogeneity in cervical screening uptake by increasing uptake in the under-screened population in England is estimated to have the single greatest impact, reducing the time to CC elimination by 11–13 years. Meanwhile, vaccination is demonstrated to be an important pillar for maintaining reduced CC incidence rates in the long term, in addition to the prevention of other HPV-related cancers and diseases which are not covered in this study. Other high-income countries, such as Australia, are targeting a more aspirational goal to reach CC incidence of 1 per 100 000 women.28 This study estimates that such an aspirational target can only be achieved in England through combined improvement and maintenance in both HPV vaccination and cervical screening uptake. While this study looks at England specifically, being the only UK nation to have pledged to the elimination of CC, achieving elimination is a wider goal that requires efforts across all nations.

The results of this study are consistent with the literature and previous modelling studies. In a global modelling study assessing the impact of HPV vaccination and cervical screening on the elimination of CC in 181 countries, Simms et al29 estimated the UK to reach the WHO elimination threshold between 2045 and 2050 assuming 70% coverage of at least two lifetime screenings and 100% coverage of nonavalent HPV vaccination; this would predict the UK to be the ninth country globally to achieve elimination.29 Similarly, in a simplified model based on historical rates of CC incidence in the fUK, the Office for Health Economics predicted elimination of CC in 2046 and estimated an associated cost saving were this to be achieved.2 Gilham and Peto30 examined CC mortality and incidence trends within birth cohorts and age groups in England. They concluded that elimination may be reached by 2040 in the cohorts that have been vaccinated. However, the trends in age-specific incidence rates among women born before 1991 suggest that their rate is unlikely to fall below 10 per 100 000 unless the effectiveness of the NHS Cervical Screening Programme improves.30 To date, there are no studies looking specifically at strategies for CC elimination in England.

Meanwhile, in other high-income countries with comparable national HPV vaccination and cervical screening programmes, Hall et al28 used the Policy1-Cervix dynamic model for HPV to estimate that Australia is on track for achieving a CC incidence rate of 4 per 100 000 women and 1 per 100 000 women by 2028 (range, 2021–2035) and 2066 (range: 2054–2077), respectively.28 A comparative modelling study in the US used two independent HPV microsimulation models to demonstrate that under current vaccination efforts the US would be expected to achieve a CC incidence of 4 per 100 000 women or below by approximately 2036 and 2046.31 In addition, Burger et al showed the up-scaling of CC screening in line with the WHO target had the greatest impact in reducing the time to elimination. The comparison to other high-income countries indicates that England, and the UK as a whole, is potentially lagging behind countries of similar economic status and history of public health measures for CC prevention.

Achieving CC elimination requires a unified effort across all three pillars of vaccination, screening and treatment to overcome the barriers to uptake and access. UK HPV vaccination coverage rates saw a large decline during the 2019–2021 academic years18 32 and, up to the most recent 2023/24 school year, remained below pre-COVID pandemic levels.19 The reason for the lack of recovery in vaccination coverage remains unclear, but likely results from multiple confounding factors including reduced vaccine acceptability and uptake across routine immunisation programmes in the UK,33 widening socioeconomic and ethnic inequalities, and NHS workforce capacity with recent figures indicating a decline in qualified school nurses from 1135 to 809 between September 2009 and August 2024.34

While England consistently reaches the WHO target of at least two cervical screens in a lifetime, socio-economic and ethnic inequalities in uptake remain evident.9 10 Several studies are ongoing looking at the use of self-sampling in the UK to improve uptake among non-attenders for GP-based cervical screening35 36 and allow potential savings to NHS.37 The UK National Screening Committee made a recommendation for all four nations that HPV self-sampling becomes an available option for under-screened women in cervical screening programmes.38 Following declining rates of screening uptake, a survey of over 2400 women aimed to better understand screening hesitancy and service barriers demonstrated 73% support for home testing, if it was offered for free on the NHS.39

Furthermore, addressing inequalities to vaccination and screening is called out specifically in the NHS England CC elimination plan including better understanding of barriers and perceptions in low uptake groups, developing inclusive and culturally sensitive materials and tailored initiatives to targets unvaccinated or unscreened individuals.12 This mirrors the National Cancer Plan; calling out uptake of HPV vaccination and cervical screening with a commitment to roll out HPV vaccination in pharmacies for those who missed vaccination in school, and a national roll-out of self-testing by 2029 to all women who have not taken up the screening offer.40 The timely implementation of these strategies will be critical to achieving elimination targets. Better data infrastructure and reporting of diversity in screening attendance will allow greater understanding of the true inequities and enable the success of current programmes to be quantified. This is the first model to estimate the time to CC elimination in England and project the impact of prevention strategies on CC incidence. The modelled data closely predicts the impact of HPV vaccination compared against real-world drops in HPV prevalence. In addition, this is the first HPV model to explicitly account for heterogeneity in cervical screening uptake between under-screened and over-screened populations. However, there are limitations. First, a common limitation for all infectious disease modelling is the need for assumptions due to insufficient available data. This study does not take into account migration (both documented and undocumented) in and out of England, generating uncertainty in the HPV vaccinated population and subsequently on the projections of HPV prevalence and CC incidence in the population. The status quo HPV VCR and screening uptake is based on pre-pandemic levels and subsequently may under or overestimate vaccination coverage and HPV prevalence in the population. Finally, heterogeneity in screening uptake is inferred from the data and requires further validation from real world data.

Conclusion

Through the past successes of its CC control programmes, England is estimated to eliminate CC as a public health problem by around 2050, 10 years later than the target set by NHS England, if HPV vaccination, cervical screening and treatment uptake rates remain as they are. Given the historical high HPV vaccination coverage, improvements to the cervical screening programme through reducing heterogeneity in uptake is estimated to have the biggest impact on the time to CC elimination. HPV vaccination remains a key component of this strategy and high coverage is essential to ensure elimination targets are maintained in the long term and for future generations. A more aspirational target of CC at 1 per 100 000 women can only be achieved, and sustained, by combining screening improvements with an increased coverage of vaccination.
