Research in context

Evidence before this study

Health system resilience refers to the capacity to absorb, adapt, and transform in response to shocks, such as policy changes, refugee migration surges, or pandemics. Refugee health systems, which often function separately from broader healthcare systems, are particularly vulnerable to these disruptions, especially during rapid migration caused by humanitarian crises. While such crises provide valuable opportunities to study major health system shocks, existing frameworks largely focus on changes in care levels following these events. However, they often overlook the operational costs and burdens—such as financial strain, staff burnout, and resource shortages—imposed by these shocks. Currently, limited evidence exists on how refugee-serving health systems or specialized health centres adapt to and recover from repeated shocks over time.

Added value of this study

This 10-year longitudinal analysis is the most comprehensive study of a specialized refugee health centre in Canada, one of the world's leading refugee resettlement countries. It includes over 107,000 appointments for 10,661 refugees from 106 countries and examines the centre's adaptation to four distinct shocks. Using a mixed-methods approach, the study revealed how shocks can catalyze resilience—absorptive, adaptive, and transformative capacities—while imposing significant operational burdens. These burdens, including burnout, vicarious trauma, and resource strain, highlight the human and systemic costs of resilience. Our findings extend existing resilience frameworks by introducing “operational burden” as a critical concept, encompassing financial, material, and human resource impacts.

Implications of all the available evidence

Humanitarian-related shocks are increasing due to conflict and climate change (i.e., South Sudan, Gaza, North American wildfires). Refugee health systems offer valuable insights for preparing broader health systems to face future shocks related to rapid population displacements. This study helps inform other displaced population serving health systems to maximize resilience while minimizing operational burdens, protect healthcare workers and maintain service quality despite escalating global challenges.

Introduction

Global forced displacement has reached historic levels, with 122.6 million people displaced as of mid-2024, the highest on record.1 This increase, driven by conflicts, persecution, disasters, both natural and climate-related, marks the twelfth consecutive year of rising displacement.1 Refugees face unique health vulnerabilities, including exposure to violence, poverty, and limited healthcare access that can strain health systems that receive large refugee influxes or face rapid policy shifts.2 The resilience capacity of health systems to address refugees’ complex needs remains critically underexplored.

Blanchet et al. developed a health system resilience framework that defines resilience through four dimensions: (1) Knowledge, the system's ability to gather and apply diverse information; (2) Uncertainties, readiness to anticipate and manage unexpected events; (3) Interdependence, effective coordination within and beyond the health sector; and (4) Legitimacy, the ability to uphold norms and gain community trust.3 These dimensions shape a health system's absorptive, adaptive, and transformative resilience capacities enabling it to maintain performance levels—defined by quantity, quality, and equity—under similar or reduced resources, or to restructure and enhance performance.3 Health system shocks, such as sudden policy shifts, rapid refugee influxes, and pandemics expose vulnerabilities by generating sudden demands or straining resources.4 Systems lacking resilience and adequate resourcing risk disruption when exposed to shocks, with system performance levels falling, failing to recover afterwards, or collapsing entirely under such pressures.5,6

Canada is a global leader in refugee resettlement, leading in annual formal resettlements from 2018 to 2022.1 However, unlike countries with formalized national refugee health systems, Canada utilizes a decentralized approach, with most care delivered by primary care providers.7,8 Across Canada, specialized refugee 'beacon clinics' developed organically, acting as hubs of expertise, that provide culturally sensitive, interdisciplinary care tailored to refugees' unique needs.9,10 These centres often integrate primary and specialty care, mental health, public health, and social services to efficiently manage resettled refugees' and asylum claimants’ health needs.9,10 Beacon centres also provide hubs of clinical expertise and medical education that supports local health systems.8,9 The WHO classifies refugee health system responses into four models—mainstream, specialized-focus, gateway, and limited—with Canada employing a mixed model. Beacon clinics function as a specialized-focus component within this hybrid, providing tailored care immediately post-arrival and before refugees fully transition into the mainstream provincial health systems.8 Since 2012 these centres have faced multiple health system shocks including, refugee health policy changes, rapid refugee influxes, and COVID-19 pandemics. Despite their critical role, their resilience capacities and adaptations to successive system shocks remains poorly understood.

Between 2011 and 2020, Canada's refugee health system faced four major shocks.11, 12, 13, 14 In 2012, cuts to the Interim Federal Health Program (IFHP), a national insurance program for refugees and asylum claimants, left many without essential services, especially medications, increasing healthcare access barriers and forcing provincial systems to absorb emergency care costs for untreated conditions.11 The IFHP was reinstated in time to support the Syrian Resettlement Initiative, when Canada rapidly resettled 40,000 refugees between November 2015 and January 2017, creating a surge that strained local systems.12,13 From February 2017 to February 2020 the Survivors of Daesh program resettled 1300 Yazidi refugees, survivors of genocide and horrific violence to four major Canadian cities. They arrived with complex trauma and physical health conditions, further straining refugee health services.15 In March 2020 the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted global health systems, adding strain and exacerbating health disparities between resettled refugees and host populations.14

Subsequent humanitarian crises in Afghanistan (August 2021)16, Ukraine (January 2022),17 Sudan (April 2023), Gaza (October 2023), and Lebanon (October 2024) illustrate the urgent need for resilient health systems responsive to sudden forced displacement and other shocks.18 Despite multiple shocks, the impact on beacon refugee health centres remains unexamined, particularly their resilience capacities, adaptive strategies and impacts on healthcare providers and staff.

This study investigates utilization, provider supply, staff experiences and adaptations at the Calgary Refugee Health Clinic. Between 2013 and 2024, it operated as the Mosaic Refugee Health Clinic, with multidisciplinary support through the Mosaic Primary Care Network (PCN). It is among Canada's longest-operating specialized refugee health centres, functioning as a transitional beacon clinic for recently resettled refugees that typically provides care for up to two years post-arrival before patients transition into mainstream primary care. Embedded within Alberta's health system, its structure is similar to other provincial clinics. Physician services are funded through an Alternative Relationship Plan (ARP), aprovincially administered model, while care for eligible patients without provincial coverage is billed fee-for-service through the IFHP. Additional funding sources have included physician overhead contributions and external grants. The health centre contributes to broader system capacity medical education, consultative support to other clinicians, facilitates transition of care from the clinic to family physicians in host communities, and advocates for reforms to strengthen primary care responsiveness to refugee health needs.19,20

We hypothesized that monthly appointment volume level and rates of change would increase sharply at the onset of each shock but ultimately return to baseline levels as the system adapted. Additionally, we expected each shock would lead to high levels of perceived provider stress and burden, but also innovative adaptations and resilience factors developed across shocks. Together, these methods allowed exploration of resilience capacities, adaptive strategies and impacts on healthcare providers and staff through repeated shocks.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a sequential explanatory mixed methods retrospective study to investigate changes in utilization, provider supply, and perceived impacts associated with health system shocks at a specialized beacon refugee health centre established in 2002 in Calgary,21 Alberta, Canada, between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2020. This beacon clinic functions as a centralized hub for refugee healthcare in the region, providing interdisciplinary, culturally responsive care. Based on available data, the centre provided care for between 45% and 75% of resettled refugees and asylum claimants in the area during the study period.22 During resettlement surge periods, such as the Syrian or Afghan resettlement programs, the centre expanded its care to temporary satellite clinics co-located within settlement housing.23 The study followed the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist (Table S1) and the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) checklist (Table S5) and was organized into three stages.

Stage I (quantitative data collection and analysis)

We extracted electronic medical record (EMR) data from all patients who attended one or more centre appointments including sociodemographic, utilization, biometric, diagnostic testing, treatment, and clinical data. Sociodemographic information included age, sex, and country of origin, aggregated into World Health Organization (WHO) regions (i.e., African, Eastern Mediterranean, Americas, European, South-East Asia, Western Pacific) to ensure confidentiality.24 We analyzed patient appointments by provider type—family physicians, specialist physicians, and multidisciplinary team (MDT) members—using EMR scheduling software. We defined specialist physicians as non-primary care providers (e.g., internal medicine, pediatrics, obstetrics, psychiatry, infectious diseases, and hepatology) and MDT members as nurses, pharmacists, dietitians, and psychologists.

Statistical model

We used interrupted time series analysis to examine associations between shocks (our exposure variable) and monthly appointment volumes and rates of change (our primary outcomes of interest). We assembled segmented linear regression models (ITS) to estimate level and slope changes for four provider types: Family physicians, MDT members, and specialist physicians, and all providers combined as a total. Quantitative analyses were conducted using Stata (version 18.0).25

For each study period, we modeled the monthly number of appointments (Yi) as a function of the intercept (β0j), slope (β1j), and seasonal fixed effects (θs) for the j-th period. The general model for time point i in period j is:

We used a linear combination of the intercept (β0j) and slope (β1j) coefficients to test for differences in the mean monthly appointments (level) and mean rate of change of monthly appointments during each study period (the slopes of the regression lines) in appointments per month between adjacent periods, and overall change from baseline to COVID-19 period using a two-tailed alpha of <0.05. We generated data visualizations to facilitate qualitative interviews.

To evaluate whether residual autocorrelation might bias these estimates, we assessed each model using the Durbin–Watson statistic and Breusch–Godfrey test for first-order serial correlation. Upon detecting evidence of autocorrelation, we re-estimated all models using Prais–Winsten regression with an AR(1) correction, which assumes each monthly observation is correlated with its immediately preceding value. In all final models, we additionally adjusted for seasonality using a four-category fixed-effect variable for Winter (Dec–Feb), Spring (Mar–May), Summer (Jun–Aug), and Fall (Sep–Nov).

Supply analysis

We conducted an exploratory-descriptive analysis of healthcare provider work supply where we collected work hours for each family physician, specialist physician, and MDT member and categorized them by provider type. We calculated mean monthly averages and standard deviations for supply metrics for the baseline, each shock and over the entire study period.

Stage II (qualitative data collection and analysis)

We conducted semi-structured interviews with current and former centre leaders including medical directors, executive directors and managers to explore changes in utilization, adaptations and operational burden across shock periods. Participants were purposively selected based on their leadership roles during one or more of the shock periods, ensuring they were directly involved in decision-making and could speak to the challenges, successes, and adaptations implemented at the health centre. Interviews were conducted in person or online via Zoom between May and August 2022, based on participant preference.26 We presented participants with graphs summarizing the quantitative data and prompted them to reflect on utilization patterns, staff experiences, and adaptations during each shock. We anonymized all materials and replaced participant names with pseudonyms to ensure confidentiality while preserving the context of their responses.

Thematic analysis

Two researchers (EN and LH) independently coded interview transcripts using deductive thematic analysis, then combined via consensus. Any remaining coding disputes were resolved by a third senior researcher (GEF). We adapted Blanchet et al.’s framework to examine resilience dimensions—knowledge (the system's ability to generate, integrate, and apply information effectively), uncertainties (the challenges related to unpredictable events), interdependence (the collaborative relationships that support system functionality), and legitimacy (the recognition and acceptance of the health center's role in the community).3 Studies of health system resilience often seek to understand health system vulnerabilities, where aspects of a system reduce its resilience capacities.27,28 We also examined vulnerabilities by coding text where participants described limited resilience during a shock and the contributing factors they identified. Emerging themes were verified through iterative discussions and cross-checking to ensure analytic consistency and rigor.

Stage III (integration)

To synthesize findings from our quantitative and qualitative stages, we developed a joint display to integrate results across each shock period. A joint display is a mixed methods tool that allows researchers to align quantitative slopes with qualitative insights in a structured format, facilitating meta-inference and drawing conclusions that neither data type could provide alone. This approach enabled us to identify points of convergence (when quantitative and qualitative results aligned), divergence (when they contradicted one another), and expansion (where one form of data added nuance or extended the meaning of the other).29

Each row of the display corresponded to one shock period, integrating findings to assess resilience capacities—absorptive, adaptive, and transformative—as well as evidence of disruption or collapse. We also identified instances of operational burden, a novel concept we define as the human and final cost incurred through shocks. These burdens were categorized across five domains: financial strain, material shortages, provider wellbeing, quality of care, and infrastructure limitations.

Interrupted time series analyses

Over the ten-year period, total appointments at the health centre rose substantially, increasing nearly fivefold from 455 per month at baseline to 2208 during the COVID-19 period (Table S1). This included an adjusted total level difference of +1656.06 and a slope difference of +95.68 appointments per month, both statistically significant after correcting for first-order autocorrelation (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1a).

Monthly appointments by provider type, March 2011–December 2020. Monthly appointment counts are shown with black circles, and Prais–Winsten regression lines indicate appointment levels and rates of change across shock periods after adjustment for autocorrelation. Panels display: (a) all providers, (b) family physicians, (c) multidisciplinary team (MDT), and (d) specialists. Bolded labels denote statistically significant changes in level or slope (p < 0.05).

Monthly family physician and MDT appointments rose substantially over the study period. Family physician appointments nearly tripled, increasing from 399.1 to 1144.40 per month (Table S1), with an adjusted level difference of +587.08 and a significant slope difference of +41.73 appointments per month (p < 0.01; Fig. 1b). MDT appointments rose even more dramatically, from 31.90 to 1029.70 per month, with an adjusted level difference of +1007.65 and a slope difference of +46.41 (both p < 0.0001; Table S1, Fig. 1c). Specialist appointments, in contrast, showed a different pattern. A small but statistically significant drop occurred between baseline and the IFHP Cuts period (−8.58, p = 0.01), followed by a gradual, non-significant increase through the Syrian Surge and Yazidi periods. This growth was ultimately reversed, as specialist appointment volumes collapsed to near-baseline levels during the COVID-19 period (Yazidi–COVID difference −34.58, p < 0.0001), yielding an overall adjusted level difference of just +4.72 (p = 0.52) and no significant change in slope (p = 0.91; Fig. 1d). All reported values reflect results adjusted for first-order autocorrelation using Prais–Winsten regression.

While total provider appointments showed a consistent rise in both level and slope across shock periods, a secondary analysis of intake and transition appointments (Table S4) revealed more variability. Monthly intake appointments declined significantly from a baseline mean of 244.70 per month to 54.50 during COVID-19 (p < 0.01), despite the COVID-19 period representing the highest overall appointment volume. Monthly transition appointments were more stable, but their rate of change shifted over time, with a significant deceleration during the COVID-19 period (p < 0.0001).

Provider supply

Mean monthly provider work hours increased for family physicians and MDT members to accommodate the rising health centre utilization. Family physician hours rose from 80.00 h per month at baseline to 184.00 h per month during the COVID-19 period (2.30-fold increase). MDT supply increased 14.94-fold, expanding from 58.0 h to 871.40 h per month. In contrast, specialist physician supply initially rose from 37.19 h at baseline to 78.48 h during the Yazidi Resettlement period, but declined nearly 50% during COVID-19, dropping to 41.90 h per month (Table S4). These findings are descriptive only, but they demonstrate that changes in utilization coincide with shifts in provider supply, suggesting that observed slopes reflect true changes in service delivery rather than administrative or scheduling artifacts.

During the baseline and IFHP cuts periods integrating and applying different forms of knowledge was crucial, though finances emerged as a key vulnerability. During the Syrian surge period interdependence with health and resettlement partners was critical for handling the rapidly increased patient volume. During the COVID-19 pandemic, participants emphasized adaptability, building on lessons from previous shocks to navigate the uncertainties of COVID-19.

Integration

The health centre demonstrated absorptive, adaptive, and transformative capacities, but these came with operational burdens as described in our joint display (Table 3). Over the 10-year study period, the health centre demonstrated transformative resilience, as evidenced by a 4.85-fold increase in mean monthly appointments from 455 at baseline to 2208 by the COVID-19 period (Table S1), with an adjusted level difference of +1656.06 and a slope difference of +95.68 appointments per month (both p < 0.0001; Fig. 1a), and notable expansions in provider hours, with family physician hours more than doubling, and MDT hours increasing 14.66-fold (Fig. 2). These dramatic expansions of system performance reflect the health centre's capacity to adapt and transform in response to recurring shocks.

During the IFHP Cuts period, care levels were maintained despite policy-driven financial strain on patients and moral distress among providers who worked and personally donated funds to fill care gaps, demonstrating absorptive capacity. The Syrian Surge may represent a transformative period, with total provider appointments rising from 610.80 to 937.90 per month (Table S1). While the adjusted level difference was +77.51 and not statistically significant (p = 0.39; Fig. 1a), leadership described this period as one of unprecedented chaos, necessitating rapid adaptations to manage the surge in arrivals. Qualitative data supported the interpretation of this period as transformational: collaborative adaptations were developed across sectors, provider supply expanded, and new partnerships emerged. Although the ITS analysis adjusted for autocorrelation did not detect statistically significant changes in level or slope during this period, the combination of a marked increase in utilization (as seen in unadjusted data) and stakeholder accounts of system-wide change support the interpretation that the Syrian Surge may have served as an inflection point in the clinic's development. During the Yazidi Resettlement, service levels were maintained despite high mental health needs that created additional burdens on staff, many of whom reported vicarious trauma. The centre adapted to these mental health needs through advocacy for family reunification, which built community trust, and a vicarious trauma training program for staff and providers. The COVID-19 period demonstrated both absorptive and adaptive capacity, with a steep increase in all provider monthly appointment change rate (adjusted slope +102.05, p < 0.0001). However, this period also featured a collapse in specialist care, with adjusted appointment levels falling by −34.58 (p < 0.0001) from the Yazidi period, returning to near-baseline levels (Table S1, Fig. 1d). While these adaptations highlighted the centre's flexibility and resilience, they came with accumulated operational burden, including expanded infrastructure, increased human resources, financial costs, and challenges to provider wellbeing, such as burnout and change fatigue. Despite these challenges, the health centre's ability to navigate these shocks demonstrate its role as a model for transformative resilience in refugee health systems.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest 10-year longitudinal study of a specialized refugee health centre that cares for resettled refugees and asylum claimants in Canada, offering insights into how the centre absorbed, adapted, and transformed in response to multiple health system shocks. Through mixed-methods, we observed major increases in utilization and provider supply, and identified resilience capacities—absorptive, adaptive, and transformative—alongside operational burdens. These periods also revealed a complex funding scheme, where stable provincial funding for physicians coexisted with reliance on community and institutional support for operation and donations for patient needs, often due to tight budgetary constraints. These findings demonstrate the centre's operational resilience and the continuous demands imposed by external shocks.

Appointments and provider hours increased substantially over the decade, with family physician and multidisciplinary team appointments rising by 2.9-fold and 32.3-fold, respectively, reflecting adjusted level increases of +587.1 and + 1007.7, and slope increases of +41.7 and + 46.4 appointments per month (both p < 0.01, Fig. 1b–c). This reflects the health centre's transformation into a beacon centre, expanding services through specialized programs and increased provider hours. The centre's broader role included outreach, education, and service delivery for marginalized populations.30,31 During COVID-19, appointment growth peaked, with an adjusted monthly slope increase of +102.1 appointments (p < 0.01), culminating in a total increase from 455 to 2208 appointments per month by the end of the study (Fig. 1a; Table S1).32 Nonetheless, despite the collapse in specialist care, the health center's resilience grew over time but was not an assured outcome of each shock.

Integrated analyses assessed resilience capacities and operational burdens across shock periods. During IFHP Cuts, family physician visits were maintained—demonstrating absorptive capacity—but financial strain on patients, the need for increased organizational support, and stress among providers, who donated to cover medication gaps, revealed significant burdens. Budget pressures required trade-offs including staff reductions. The Syrian Surge showed transformative capacity: appointments and provider hours rose and new partnerships formed. During Yazidi Resettlement, the centre showed adaptive capacity, sustaining care while addressing high mental health needs and provider vicarious trauma.33 The early COVID-19 period showed absorptive and adaptive capacity through rapid appointment growth and virtual care. However, significant initial drops in specialist and family physician appointments mirrored broader declines in healthcare utilization across Canada.34 These findings suggest that refugee health system resilience cannot be assessed in isolation but must be understood within the national context. Our mixed-methods approach also revealed that qualitative insights were especially valuable when quantitative trends appeared stable. For instance, during the IFHP Cuts period, family physician appointment levels remained steady, yet qualitative data uncovered substantial strain on clinic operations, staff morale, and patient access. These discordant findings demonstrate how qualitative data can reveal critical operational burdens and adaptive processes that remain invisible in routine health metrics.

Our findings also emphasize the financial underpinnings of resilience. Although we did not collect formal budget data, qualitative interviews revealed a multi-level funding structure. Provincial funding for family physicians through an Alternative Relationship Plan (ARP) was cited as both a challenge and a strength. Notably, during the Syrian Surge, ARP support increased from 2.0 to 4.0 full-time equivalents (FTEs), representing a sustained increase of approximately $500,000 per year. This investment, reflected in a 2.3-fold rise in family physician hours and a 14.9-fold increase in multidisciplinary team hours, signals long-term system commitment. Conversely, during the IFHP Cuts period, the clinic established a donation fund to fill medication access gaps, illustrating how financial adaptations became critical to maintaining care.

These findings suggest that resilience was supported by both human adaptability and structural and financial resource shifts. However, refugee health funding remains uncertain amid major provincial health system restructuring. In 2025, the Calgary Refugee Health Clinic secured physician funding for three years, however without dedicated MDT and other operational funding.35

The WHO Building Blocks framework outlines core system components but lacks guidance for shock response.36 Blanchet et al.’s resilience framework, applied here, helped identify capacities like knowledge, interdependence, uncertainty management, and legitimacy.3 While others have used it to study COVID-19 and the Syrian Surge, it omits structural components like the Building Blocks and resilience outcomes.28,37 Other published frameworks integrate shock preparedness with elements of the Building Blocks, or include bottom-up perspectives on everyday resilience that highlight the critical role of frontline actors, but also fail to explicitly address resilience outcomes.38,39 Our study contributes to this evolving discussion by incorporating dimensions such as disruption, collapse, and operational burdens—including financial strain, material shortages, provider well-being, and infrastructure challenges.

Our integrated findings demonstrate that resilience can be understood as both a process and an outcome.4,40 While some caution against viewing resilience as an outcome, our mixed-methods design supports both interpretations.4,40 Qualitative data captured adaptive processes among frontline staff; quantitative data showed sustained system performance despite shocks. Importantly, system resilience likely emerges from both structural capacities and individuals’ adaptive responses—shaped by prior stress exposure. Recognizing frontline perspectives avoids misattributing resilience solely to systems and highlights how individuals co-construct resilience over time.

Our findings align with the Johns Hopkins Center for Humanitarian Health – Lancet Commission's call for critical research into resilient health systems and directly address the WHO Department of Migration Health's Global Research Agenda, which prioritizes understanding refugee health systems and their capacity to adapt to shocks.41 Our decade-long analysis of over 10,000 refugee patients from 106 countries, contextualized by refugee health leaders' firsthand experiences, provide actionable insights. Consistent with studies of health systems in fragile and conflict-affected settings, our results emphasize the importance of cross-sector interdependence and proactive preparation to address unforeseen challenges.37 Moreover, our results reflect the cumulative impact of consecutive shocks, such as rapid Syrian and Yazidi resettlement programs and COVID-19, interact to compound health system demands over time.37 This compounding effect creates cascading burdens on health systems, demonstrating that such shocks cannot be effectively understood or managed in isolation.28

Limitations include a qualitative focus on clinic leadership, excluding frontline staff and refugees. Patient voices are essential for understanding how shocks impact care, access, and experience. Refugees may also have sought care outside the clinic, limiting generalizability, however, this health centre was the only specialized refugee primary care available in the city. EMR data reliably captured appointment counts but not durations. Most appointments were scheduled for 30 min (family physicians) or 1 h (specialists and MDT), however, actual lengths varied with patient complexity and interpretation needs. Interviews in 2022 may have introduced recall bias for earlier shocks. COVID-19 analysis was limited to its first nine months. Finally, our outcomes were operational rather than clinical, limiting our ability to assess changes in patient health or well-being. Future research should integrate health outcomes to evaluate resilience more fully.

Finally, specialized refugee health centres risk the perception of operating outside general health systems, potentially reducing shared accountability and investment. Several factors mitigate this risk: the centre studied is embedded within the provincial health system, with physicians funded through the ARP model; it serves as a transitional entry point, stabilizing newly arrived refugees before their transition into mainstream care; and it builds system capacity through teaching, training, community engagement, and advocacy. Lastly, consistent with existing literature, our study shows that newly arrived refugees have distinct health and social needs, and addressing these early in primary care, coordinated with public health, is more cost-efficient than responding later through preventable emergency visits or hospitalizations.42

Despite limitations, this study provides valuable insights into how specialized refugee health centres may build resilience while responding to consecutive health system shocks. While focused on a single clinic, our findings may be applicable to other refugee health settings in high-income countries that centralize services for newly arrived refugees. The model of a beacon clinic—offering interdisciplinary care, outreach, and coordination with settlement agencies—may resonate in jurisdictions with similar organizational structures. Further, this study illustrates that while refugee beacon clinics can absorb, adapt, and transform in response to external shocks, sustaining these capacities requires adequate resources and comes with operational burdens, particularly to the well-being of frontline providers. As global geopolitical instability continues to drive displacement, proactive strategies to mitigate operational burdens will be critical to sustain resilient refugee healthcare systems.
