Biomechanical evaluation of acromion and scapular spine fracture repair techniques systematic review
Authors: John W Moore, Maxwell A Northrop, Matthew E Guareschi, Brandon L Rogalski, Richard J Friedman, Josef K Eichinger

Total shoulder arthroplasty, more specifically reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA), has steadily gained popularity across the United States and the incidence is projected to continue to rise.1 Acromion and scapular spine fractures (ASFs) are a well-known complication following rTSA with an incidence rate of approximately 5%, though it may be under-reported.10,15,20 The increase in ASFs following rTSA has been attributed to a number of factors, including changes to the biomechanics of the shoulder resulting in increased strain on the deltoid and its origin.3 There is still no consensus on whether to treat ASFs operatively or nonoperatively.13 Nonoperative treatment has been consistently shown to lead to inferior outcomes when compared to rTSA patients without fractures, but operative treatment has yielded inconsistent results in the literature and nonoperative treatment is often found to be noninferior.5,10,12,13,18,19,22,24 Several studies have identified factors associated with a worse prognosis following ASFs, though the literature is somewhat conflicted on these as well.2,10,12,14,20,26 Medial fractures are generally more likely to be treated with operative management than lateral fractures, largely because of prior research showing medial fractures do worse than lateral fractures when both are treated nonoperatively.4,11

Studies have yet to identify a satisfactory treatment for post-operative ASFs following rTSA.3 Identifying a gold standard of treatment would significantly improve the standard of care for patients with ASFs, who continue to do worse regardless of treatment. Biomechanical studies offer a unique ability to test different fixation techniques by removing confounding variables. This study sought to perform a systematic review of all the biomechanical literature published on operative management of ASFs following rTSA to identify which techniques provide biomechanically superior fixation.

Methods

A systematic review was conducted of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from January 1, 2000 to August 1, 2025, using the keywords (“acromion” OR “scapula”) AND (“fracture”) AND (“biomechanical” OR “cadaveric” OR “synthetic bone”) AND (“fixation” OR “plating” OR “treatment” OR “management”) AND (“reverse shoulder arthroplasty” OR “RTSA”). Two authors (JM and MN) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts using the Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Inclusion criteria were predefined and stated the studies must be biomechanical studies, must specifically examine treatment of ASFs following rTSA (ie, no os acromiale studies), and they must be published after January 1, 2000. Conference proceedings, editorial comments, and any non–peer-reviewed data were excluded.

Data extraction included author, year, specimen type, sample size, fracture classification according to Levy et al,11 fixation techniques, failure load, and mechanical outcomes.

Results

The initial literature search yielded 202 studies. However, 3 studies were found to be duplicates or prior versions of updated articles. Out of the remaining 199 studies, 194 did not meet the inclusion criteria and were excluded, while 5 studies met the criteria and were included. The studies included are presented in Table I.

The 5 biomechanical studies included tested a total of 89 shoulders. Fifty-three were synthetic scapulae (Sawbones), while 40 were human cadaveric scapulae. The most common fracture type was Levy type III, which accounted for 4 out of 5 studies. One study (Ting et al23) examined Levy type II fractures. Three out of 5 studies compared some variation of double plating to single plating. The 3 methods of dual plating studied included a 3.5-mm small fragment locking plate with a 2.7-mm small fragment locking plate in a 90-90 configuration (Katthagen et al), 2 3.5-mm small fragment locking plates positioned dorsally and caudally to the spina scapulae (Hollensteiner et al), and a 3.5-mm small fragment locking plate and a 3.5-mm one-third tubular locking plate in a 90-90 configuration (Pastor et al).6,7,16,17 Of these studies that compared single plating to double plating, only Hollensteiner et al outright showed double plating to be superior to single plating in a statistically significant manner.6 Although Katthagen et al7 failed to reach significance in their initial study of n = 16 human cadavers, they later showed double plating to be statistically superior to single plating when only looking at scapulae with an osteoporotic bone mineral density (n = 12). Pastor et al was not able to show double plating with a 3.5-mm small fragment locking plate and a one-third tubular locking plate to be superior to single plating with a 3.5-mm small fragment locking plate.16,17

Of the 2 other studies, Kicinski et al8 compared a 3.5-mm small fragment locking plate to a 3.5-mm clavicle plate and a 3.5-mm reconstruction plate and showed the 3.5-mm small fragment locking plate to provide significantly better fixation. Ting et al23 compared dorsal plating ± a hook to supraspinous fossa plating ± a hook in Levy type II fractures. It is the only study in this review that examined Levy type II fractures and found dorsal plating to be superior.23

Discussion

There were only 5 biomechanical studies identified in the last 25 years that examined fixation of ASFs following rTSA. All 5 of these studies tested different fixation methods and all had different methods of applying biomechanical stress. Due to the paucity of research on the topic and the high degree of heterogeneity in the existing studies, it is difficult to draw conclusions about treating ASFs from these 5 studies.

Three point five mm small fragment locking plates are preferred to other types of plates and show superior fixation when compared to clavicle plates or reconstruction plates.8 This finding from Kicinski et al3 influenced both clinical practice and further biomechanical research, as seen in subsequent systematic reviews for clinical studies of fixation techniques for ASFs following rTSA.

Previous clinical studies cite Katthagen et al and then Hollensteiner et al to support claims that dual plating outperforms single plating for fixation of ASFs.3,9,24,25 However, there is hardly definitive evidence suggesting that dual plating is superior to single plating. Katthagen et al failed to demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the failure point of single and double small fragment locking plating in their original study. It was only after examining only those human cadaver scapulae in their study with osteoporotic bone mineral density (n = 12 of 16) that they reached a significant difference. This study, like 4 out of the 5 in this review, only examined Levy type III fractures and the results are not necessarily generalizable across all fracture types. While it is true that osteoporosis or poor bone quality is a risk factor for developing ASFs after rTSA, readers need to be careful about applying these results too broadly.21 Hollensteiner et al was able to able to show that double plating was superior to single plating on the caudal edge of the spina scapulae (350 N vs. 217 N, P = .003) but was unable to show a statistically significant benefit for double plating compared to single plating on the dorsal aspect of the scapular spine (350 N vs. 292 N; P = .160) when examining Levy type III fractures. The final study that examined single vs. double plating, Pastor et al, examined a small fragment locking plate vs. a small fragment locking plate with a one-third tubular locking plate in Levy type III fractures and found no difference between single and double plating. Their conclusion was that the tubular plate was not a sufficient fixation technique.16,17

Every clinical study will cite some combination of those 3 papers as justification for why double plating is superior to single plating, but closer analysis reveals that none of the studies were able to show double plating to be superior to dorsal single plating in bone of normal density. Double plating makes intuitive sense, and all these studies show trends favoring double plating over single plating that would more than likely reach statistical significance with a larger sample size. Still, with the current evidence available, there is little to no justification in a biomechanical study for double plating over dorsal single plating in patients with normal bone quality.

Ting et al23 was the only study to examine Levy type II fractures. They found that dorsal plating was significantly less likely to fail under a maximum cyclical load than plating in the supraspinatus fossa. They also found the presence of a lateral hook on the plate was significantly protective at preventing failure of the lateral acromion.

Conclusion

There exists a great need for further biomechanical exploration of fixation techniques for ASFs following rTSA. There is not currently enough research to draw many true conclusions to guide treatment for clinicians. Small fragment locking plates are superior to clavicle plates or reconstruction plates. The current biomechanical evidence suggesting double plating is superior to dorsal single plating is weak for those with poor bone quality and nonexistent for those with normal bone quality or those without Levy type III fractures.
