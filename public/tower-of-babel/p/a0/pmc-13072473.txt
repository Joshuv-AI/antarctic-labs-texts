Moving beyond cancer: longitudinal changes in 24-hour movement behaviors and their association with quality of life in breast cancer survivors
Authors: Iris Willems, Marieke De Craemer, Lore Dams, Elien Van der Gucht, Aaron Miatke, Mira Meeus, Nele Devoogdt, An De Groef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany breast cancer survivors experience persistent symptoms after treatment, impairing quality of life (QoL). At the same time, maintaining healthy levels of 24-hour movement behaviors (24h-MBs) i.e. engaging in 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (PA) a week, several hours of light PA a day, limiting sedentary behavior during the day, and achieving restorative sleep, remains challenging. While these behaviors influence QoL individually, little is known about the combined impact of 24h-MBs in breast cancer survivorship. Therefore, this study aimed to 1) examine longitudinal changes in 24h-MBs across 1 week, 4 months, and 12 months post-surgery, 2) compare 24h-MBs of breast cancer survivors at 12-month post-surgery with healthy controls and 3) investigate associations between 24h-MBs and QoL in breast cancer survivors.MethodsThe 24h-MBs were measured by a hip-worn Actigraph GT3X-BT + , and QoL by the McGill QoL questionnaire at 1 week, 4 months, and 12 months post-surgery. Compositional data analysis was used to account for the time-use interdependence of 24h-MBs. Multivariate linear mixed models assessed longitudinal changes in 24h-MBs, a MANOVA explored the group differences and regressions models examined associations between 24h-MBs and QoL at each timepoint.ResultsResults from 184 breast cancer survivors (54 ± 15 y/o) showed that sedentary behavior decreased while light and moderate-to-vigorous PA increased over 12 months (p < 0.001). Compared to 135 healthy women (44 ± 10 y/o), breast cancer survivors at 12 months post-surgery showed 24h-MBs with more sleep (> 9h/night) and less low and moderate-to-vigorous PA (p < 0.001). At 4 months post-surgery, light PA (relatively against the other behaviors) was associated with a better overall QoL, whereas longer sleep duration (relatively against the other behaviors) was associated with a worse overall QoL and less perceived support. No significant associations were found at 1 week and 12 months post-surgery. However, at all three timepoints, the most commonly self-reported impairing symptoms related to their QoL were fatigue, insomnia and pain.ConclusionBreast cancer survivors gradually improved their 24h-MBs in the first year after surgery, but their 24h-MB profiles remained less favorable than those of healthy controls. More optimal post-surgery 24h-MBs were associated with a better QoL, emphasizing their relevance for recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44167-026-00098-y.Keywords: 24-h movement behaviors, Physical activity, Sedentary behavior, Sleep, Breast cancer survivors, Quality of life, Cancer-related symptoms

Background

In Belgium, breast cancer represents the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women, with 11,636 new cases reported in 2023 [1]. Advances in early detection and treatment have greatly improved survival rates, yet many breast cancer survivors continue to experience persistent challenges long after treatment ends [2]. Commonly reported physical symptoms include fatigue, pain, lymphedema, musculoskeletal problems (particularly affecting the arm and breast region), and weight gain [3, 4]. These post-surgery effects can impair physical, mental, and social functioning, thereby diminishing overall quality of life (QoL) [3, 4].

Evidence suggests that optimizing movement behaviors, i.e. engaging in 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (PA) a week, several hours of light PA a day, minimizing sedentary behavior (SB), and achieving restorative sleep may improve QoL [5–11]. Higher levels of PA have been linked to a better overall QoL, while poor subjective sleep quality is associated with worse outcomes [5–7]. These associations may reflect multiple physiological and psychosocial mechanisms related to more optimal movement behaviors, including for example improved cardiometabolic health, reduced fatigue, mood enhancement, and cognitive benefits [5–11]. For example, a 10-year longitudinal study found that breast cancer survivors engaging in low levels of PA reported greater fatigue and pain (subscales of QoL) compared to those meeting PA guidelines [8]. Similarly, sleep problems such as insomnia and poor sleep quality have been associated with lower QoL at 12 and 15 months after diagnosis [7, 9]. Moreover, a meta-analysis reported small but significant effects of PA interventions on global health-related QoL and physical functioning [10]. Qualitative studies further support these findings, with survivors reporting perceived physical and psychological benefits of PA on their QoL [11].

However, many breast cancer survivors struggle to maintain healthy levels of movement behaviors [12–14]. The steepest decline in PA typically occurs around one month post-surgery, with a gradual recovery thereafter; however, some individuals never regain their pre-surgery activity levels [14]. Another study showed that over a 10 year follow-up post diagnosis, the majority (60%) of breast cancer survivors did not meet the PA guidelines, with this group also displaying insufficient activity levels prior to diagnosis [8]. Sleep problems are reported by more than 50% of cancer survivors, with 20–60% experiencing poor sleep quality and approximately 20% suffering from excessive daytime sleepiness [2, 13, 15, 16].

Building on this evidence, it becomes clear that an optimal balance of movement behaviors might be promising in improving QoL of breast cancer survivors. However, to date, most studies have examined these movement behaviors in isolation, which overlooks the inherent interdependence of a finite 24-h day consisting of PA, SB and sleep. Increasing time in one behavior necessarily reduces time in another [17]. To address this, the 24-hour movement behaviors (24h-MBs) paradigm has been proposed, emphasizing the interrelated nature of movement behaviors throughout a 24-h day [17].

Although the 24h-MBs paradigm has gained attention in general and other clinical populations, its application among cancer survivors remains limited [18]. Few studies have examined time-use models in cancer populations (including breast, colorectal, and others) [19–21]. These studies explored cross-sectional non-compositional isotemporal substitution models to investigate the association between 24h-MBs and QoL, but the results have been inconclusive [19–21]. For example, Hidde and colleagues [19] and Mazzoni and colleagues [20] reported no significant associations between reallocating time across behaviors and QoL subscales, although Mazzoni and colleagues [20] did observe that substituting 30 min per day of SB with PA was associated with reduced fatigue. Similarly, Van Roekel [21] found that reallocating one hour of SB into light PA or moderate-to-vigorous PA was linked to improved physical functioning, reduced disability, and decreased fatigue. These mixed results may be partly explained by methodological limitations, as isotemporal substitution models rely on linear assumptions and absolute values that do not fully capture the inherently relative nature of 24h-MBs [22]. To address this issue, researchers recommend analyzing 24h-MB compositions with compositional data analysis, i.e. CoDA, which more appropriately accounts for the interdependence of 24h-MBs [17, 23].

Until now, no research exists on 24h-MBs of breast cancer survivors, using a CoDA approach, to explore the longitudinal changes in time spent in 24h-MBs as well as associations between 24h-MBs and QoL. Therefore, this study aims to 1) examine the longitudinal evolution of 24h-MB compositions across 1 week, 4 months and 12 months post-breast cancer surgery, 2) compare the 24h-MB composition of cancer survivors 12 months post-surgery with the 24h-MB composition of a group healthy controls, and 3) investigate the association between 24h-MB compositions and QoL among breast cancer survivors at 1 week, 4 months, and 12 months post-surgery.

Methods

Study design and participants

Breast cancer survivors

Study protocol

This study is a secondary data analysis of the EduCan-trial which is a randomized controlled trial that was conducted from November 2017 to February 2021 at the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation of the University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University Hospitals Leuven (s60702) and registered at ClinicalTrails.gov (NCT03351075). The main objective of the randomized controlled trail was to examine the effectiveness of pain neuroscience education program in addition to a standard best evidence physical activity program. A detailed description of the protocol has been published elsewhere [24]. Participants were recruited from the Multidisciplinary Breast Center of the University Hospitals Leuven (Belgium) between November 2017 and March 2020. An informed consent was signed prior to inclusion. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of either invasive or non-invasive primary breast cancer and were scheduled to undergo a mastectomy with sentinel node biopsy, axillary lymph node dissection, or breast-conserving surgery with axillary lymph node dissection. Eligible individuals were women aged 18 years or older who had sufficient proficiency in Dutch, including reading, writing, listening, and speaking skills. For this study, we used cross-sectional and longitudinal observational data from the EduCan-trial including data collected at 1 week post-surgery, 4 months post-surgery and 12 months post-surgery.

Participant characteristics

The following participant characteristics were collected: age (years), Body Mass Index (BMI; kg/m2) and education level (primary or no education, lower secondary education, upper secondary education, higher education professional bachelor, higher education academic bachelor or master). The following clinical characteristics were collected from the patient’s medical file: type of surgery, tumor size, lymph node stage, type of adjuvant treatment (e.g. radiotherapy, hormone therapy, chemotherapy or endocrine therapy).

Quality of life

The valid and reliable Dutch version of the McGill Quality of Life Questionnaire was used to assess QoL among breast cancer survivors [25]. The questionnaire starts with Part A consisting of a single item that evaluates the respondent’s overall self-perceived QoL, covering physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and financial aspects over the previous two days (from 0 "very bad" to 10 "excellent"). In Part B, the participants were asked to identify the three most burdensome physical symptoms/problems they experienced during the past two days and to rate the severity of each on an 11-point Likert scale (from 0 “No problem” to 10 “Tremendous problem”) (items 1–3 of the questionnaire). Additionally, they filled in a question on physical wellbeing with a higher score indicating better physical wellbeing (0 “Physically terrible” and 10 “Physically well”; item 4 of the questionnaire). Part C questions different subscales related to psychosocial functioning over the past two days rated on a 11-point Likert scale, including:, psychological symptoms (0 “Not at all” and 10 “Completely agree”; items 5–8 of the questionnaire; scores were transposed so higher score indicating better psychological functioning), existential well-being (0 “Not at all” and 10 “Completely agree”, items 9–14 of the questionnaire, higher score indicating better existential wellbeing), and perceived support (0 “Not at all” and 10 “Completely agree”; items 15–16 of the questionnaire, higher score indicating better perceived support).

Device-based measured time-use features

Time-use data were collected using a tri-axial Actigraph wGT3X-BT + at 1 week, 4 months and 12 months post-surgery. Participants were instructed to wear the device on the right hip during seven consecutive days during their waking hours and for at least 12 h (except water-based activities) [26]. The durations of waking up and going to bed were recorded in a diary. When participants removed the device for water-based activities (e.g. swimming) or for other reasons (e.g. contact sports), the duration was also recorded in the diary. The accelerometer was initialized via ActiLife (Version 6.13.4 Full Edition) software and was set up to measure at a frequency of 90 Hz with 60-s epochs [26].

Control group

Study protocol

Because the randomized controlled trial did not include a control group, and in line with the second research objective, an external healthy control group was included. This comparison was considered valuable for better interpreting the findings, as it provided a reference for typical 24h-MB compositions. Therefore, a group of healthy women was selected from an existing dataset of 24h-MBs collected among Belgian adults. These data were derived from a cross-sectional observational study conducted between April 2022 and March 2023 at the Department of Rehabilitation Sciences, Ghent University, Belgium. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ghent University Hospital (ONZ-2022_0013), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. A detailed description of the study protocol has been published elsewhere [27]. Participants were eligible to participate if they aged 25 to 64 years, employed at least 50% of a full-time schedule per week, and free from any physical (e.g., amputations, paralysis, recent stroke), cognitive (e.g., dementia, psychological disorders), or major medical (e.g., chronic respiratory diseases, heart failure) conditions that could impair daily functioning. For the analysis of this paper, only female participants were selected from the dataset.

Participant characteristics

The following participant characteristics were retrieved out of the secondary dataset: age (years) and BMI (kg/m2).

Device-based measured time-use features

Time-use data were collected using a tri-axial Actigraph wGT3X-BT + . Participants wore the accelerometer during the day on their right hip and at night on their nondominant wrist during seven consecutive days [26]. The durations of waking up and going to bed were recorded in a diary. When participants removed the device for water-based activities (e.g. swimming) or for other reasons (e.g. contact sports), the duration was also recorded in the diary. The accelerometer was initialized via ActiLife (Version 6.13.4 Full Edition) software and was set up to measure at a frequency of 100 Hz with 60-s epochs [26, 28].

Data analysis

Processing of time-use features

Time-use features (i.e. mean time spent in the 24h-MBs, overall activity volume and intensity) were derived from raw accelerometer signals using the open source R package GGIR [28]. The GGIR package uses an autocalibration algorithm that checks and corrects for calibration errors in triaxial accelerometer signals [29]. Non-wear time was defined by GGIR as a period of 60 min during which less than 13 mg for at least two out of three axes was noted or the range of accelerations accumulated to less than 50 mg [28].

For both breast cancer survivors and the control group, all non-wear periods recorded in the diary—when participants removed the device—were classified as non-wear time. Among breast cancer survivors, sleep time was estimated using the GGIR package based on the sleep diary, as the accelerometer was not worn overnight (GGIR guider: NotWorn).). For the control group, sleep time was also determined using the same GGIR package and relied on sleep diary data. However, this group had the advantage when sleep diary information was invalid or missing, the HDCZA algorithm was applied to identify the sleep period due to the wear time protocol of 24h a day (GGIR guider: HDCZA) [30].

For both breast cancer survivors and the control group, the non-sleep time was classified based on the Mean Amplitude Deviation (MAD) metric accompanied by cut-points provided by Vähä-Ypyä et al. (2018, 2023): SB (< 22.5 mg), light PA (22.5–93.9 mg), moderate PA (94–396 mg) and vigorous PA (≥ 396 mg) [31, 32]. This combination of metric and cut-points was recommended above other metrics (e.g., ENMO, counts per minute) by Willems and colleagues (2024) [27]. All behaviors were represented in minutes a day (min/day) and weighted to represent an average day (i.e. ((weekdays*5) + weekend days*2))/7). Movement behaviors were considered valid if the accelerometer provided data for at least four days with a minimum of 10h valid wear-time hours during the waking day supplemented with a completed sleep diary [33]. Additionally, cut-point independent metrics such as average acceleration and intensity gradient were calculated by GGIR [34]. The average acceleration represents the overall activity volume represented in mg [34]. The intensity gradient refers to the intensity distribution over a 24h day [34]. A smaller intensity gradient (more negative) reflects a more sedentary profile [34].

Compositional data analysis

To account for the relative nature of movement behaviors throughout a 24-h period, the time spent in each behavior category (based on the Vähä-Ypyä cut-points [31, 32] and sleep analysis) was transformed using Compositional Data Analysis (CoDA) [23]. This approach considers the interdependent nature of 24h-MBs. Specifically, a four part composition including sleep, SB, light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA, was expressed as three isometric log-ratio (ilr) coordinates at each timepoint [23] using the compositions package in R [35]. Given that the 24h-MB composition consists of four behaviors, we generated four unique sets of three ilr coordinates. Each ilr coordinate captures the relative contribution of one behavior compared to the geometric mean of the remaining behaviors. For example, if moderate-to-vigorous PA is the numerator in ilr1, it reflects the ratio of time spent in moderate-to-vigorous PA relative to the geometric mean of light PA, SB, and sleep. Variation matrices were created to explore the variance–covariance of the 24h-MBs [see Additional File 1].

Statistical analyses

Participant characteristics are presented as means and standard deviations (± SD), and medians and interquartile range. Proportions (%) were used to report categorical data.

For the first research aim which tries to explore the longitudinal changes of 24h-MB composition over 1 week, 4 months and 12 months post-surgery, multivariate linear mixed models were used. To use the 24h-MB composition as the dependent variable in the models, ilrs were used in long ‘stacked’ format with a dummy variable indicating the corresponding ilr response (ilr1, ilr2 or ilr3) [36]. Fixed effects were then specified for the indicator variable and interaction terms between the indicator variable and other fixed predictors of interest (i.e. timepoint, age, education, BMI). Random intercepts were specified to account for repeated measurements on participants, with separate random intercepts for each ilr response. An unstructured covariance matrix was used for both the between-person and within-person matrices that allowed for heterogeneous variance for each ilr response, along with flexible covariance between each ilr. This structure allowed the entire 24h-MB composition to be modelled simultaneously as a multivariate-related outcome, while ensuring consistent results regardless of the basis used to construct the ilr coordinates. To determine whether there was a significant difference in 24h-MB compositions between timepoints, a type III MANOVA F-test was conducted on the fixed effects, focusing on the interaction between the indicator variable and timepoint to assess whether the 24h composition differed significantly across follow up. Post-hoc testing was used to explore the differences in 24h-MB compositions between each timepoint (Posthoc testing, Bonferroni correction 0.05/3 = 0.016). Non-parametric cases bootstrapping with 1000 replicates were used to generate 95% percentile intervals, exploring which behavior in the 24h-MB composition is significantly different.

Due to no pre-diagnosis data, we decided for the second research aim to compare the 24h-MBs of breast cancer survivors at 12 months post-surgery with healthy control women. A MANOVA-test was conducted to test if the 24h-MB compositions (ILRs’ coordinates as dependent variable) differed between the two groups (independent variable). Additionally, mean differences in behaviors within the 24h-MB composition were bootstrapped with 1000 replicates to generate 95% confidence intervals for identifying significant differences.

For the third research aim, linear regression models were used to examine the cross-sectional association between 24h-MB composition and overall QoL, as well as its subscales at each timepoint separately (1 week, 4 months and 12 months post-surgery). The analysis began by assessing whether the overall 24h-MB composition was significantly associated with each QoL outcome. If a significant association was found, four separate models were then constructed for each outcome, with each behavior (e.g. moderate-to-vigorous PA, light PA, SB, or sleep) sequentially placed in the numerator of the first ilr transformation. This allowed the behavior in the numerator to be compared relatively against the remaining behaviors. The direction and significance of the regression coefficient for ilr1 indicated whether spending more or less time in that specific behavior (relatively against the other behaviors) was associated with changes in QoL outcomes. Moreover, the McGill QoL questionnaire questions the most impairing symptoms related to reduced QoL. These were categorized into symptom categories based on the problem list defined in the Distress Thermometer [see legend Additional File 2] [37]. The most frequently reported symptoms at each timepoint were summarized with the number and percentage of individuals affected, along with the mean and ±SD of symptom severity. The number of participants reporting each symptom at each timepoint can be found in the Additional File 2.

A dropout analysis was conducted among breast cancer survivors who discontinued participation at 12 months post-surgery. No significant differences were found between participants who remained in the study and those who dropped out (see Additional File 3). All models were adjusted for confounding factors, i.e. age, BMI at pre-surgery level and educational level. All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 [38].

Longitudinal changes in 24h-MB compositions

There was a significant change in 24h-MB composition over time among breast cancer survivors (p < 0.001). Specifically, the composition at 1 week post-surgery differed significantly from both 4 months (p = 0.002) and 12 months post-surgery (p < 0.001). More specifically, within the 24h-MB composition, SB decreased over time by approximately 20 to 25 min (1w vs 4m -19.2 min [− 34.7; − 3.2]; 1w vs 12m -25.2 min [− 43.0; − 4.7]), alongside increases in light PA of approximately 20 to 30 min (1w vs 4m 19.8 min [9.2; 31.4]; 1w vs 12m 30.1 min [16.9; 44.3]) and increases of moderate-to-vigorous PA of approximately 9 min (1w vs 12m 8.6 min [2.0; 12.3]). However, there was no significant difference in the 24h-MB composition between 4 and 12 months post-surgery (p = 0.11), indicating relative stability in 24h-MBs during this period. Associations between 24h-MBs and QoL among breast cancer survivors

At 4 months post-surgery, the 24h-MB composition of breast cancer survivors was significantly associated with overall QoL, the physical well-being domain, and the existential well-being domain (p < 0.05) and showed a borderline significant association for perceived support (p = 0.08) (Table 5). Exploration of each behavior within the 24h-MB composition relative to the other behaviors in relation to the QoL subscales can be found in Table 6. Time spent in light PA, relative to the other behaviors, was positively associated with overall QoL, meaning that more time spent in light PA might reflect in a better overall QoL. In contrast, sleep, when considered relative to the other behaviors, was significantly and negatively associated with overall QoL and perceived support (Table 6). This might indicate that longer sleep durations are associated with worse overall QoL and less perceived support. A descriptive analysis of the most frequently self-reported symptoms impairing their QoL at 4 months post-surgery revealed that the most commonly reported symptoms were fatigue (n = 71, 38.6%), pain in regions other than the breast/arm (n = 42, 22.8%), insomnia (n = 29, 15.8%), and pain in the breast/arm region (n = 14, 7.6%). The severity scores of these symptoms ranged between 4.5 and 6.5 out of 10 [See Additional File 2].

Discussion

This study presents the first longitudinal compositional data analysis of 24h-MBs among breast cancer survivors over the course of one year post breast cancer surgery. Our findings show that the 24h-MB composition of breast cancer survivors changed significantly from 1 week and 4 months post-surgery up to 12 months post-surgery, with sleep duration remaining stable, decreases in time spent in SB, and increases in both time spent in light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA. Between 4 and 12 months post-surgery, the 24-MBs did not change significantly. Importantly, at 12 months post-surgery, breast cancer survivors showed 24h-MBs characterized by more sleep and lower light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA as compared to healthy women. At 4 months post-surgery, the 24h-MBs of breast cancer survivors were linked to different domains of QoL, with light PA (relative to other behaviors) showing positive associations and sleep (relative to other behaviors) showing negative associations. The most impairing symptoms impacting QoL were fatigue, insomnia, pain at breast/arm region and pain in other regions. No significant associations between 24h-MBs and QoL were found at 1 week and 12 months post-surgery.

Regarding aim 1, the breast cancer survivors showed improvements in 24h-MBs over one-year post-surgery resulting in less SB and more light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA over time. The more balanced 24h-MBs over time likely reflect recovery of daily functioning as breast cancer survivors regain physical capacity, and resume daily routines after treatment [39]. These behavioral improvements are often most pronounced in the first few months, which is not surprising given that this period follows the peak of treatment burden (post-surgery) and offers the greatest window for recovery [14]. However, in this study a plateau in 24h-MBs was observed between 4 and 12 months among the breast cancer survivors possibly suggesting the establishment of a “new normal” of activity patterns. As this is the first study to examine longitudinal 24h-MBs, previous research investigating movement behaviors in isolation has shown that cancer survivors generally fail to return to their pre-diagnosis levels of PA or remain insufficiently active both before and after diagnosis [40]. This is further supported by longitudinal evidence indicating that PA levels among breast cancer survivors often remain below the recommended guidelines over time [8, 41, 42]. For example, one study found that approximately 60% of breast cancer survivors did not meet PA guidelines even 10 years after treatment [8], while others documented declines in PA and increases in SB between 10 and 24 months post-diagnosis [41, 42]. Moreover, the prolonged sleep duration of the breast cancer survivors in the present study is noteworthy. At 12 months post-surgery, survivors averaged 9.3 h of sleep per night, slightly above the recommended 7–9 h for adults [43]. However, our study only captured sleep duration and not indicators of sleep quality, latency, or wakefulness during the night. Given that in general > 60% of breast cancer survivors experience poor sleep quality, with 3 to 32% reporting insomnia and 24% excessive daytime sleepiness [13], the longer sleep duration may reflect compensatory rest in response to potential fragmented or poor-quality sleep. Future studies should therefore incorporate more sleep parameters such as sleep quality and sleep/wake regularity which have been shown to be more predictive of QoL outcomes than sleep duration alone [13, 44].

As mentioned in the previous paragraph, a popular way to describe movement behaviors in research is often in relation to guideline compliance [8, 41, 42]. The American Cancer Society and the American College of Sports Medicine have issued evidence-based PA guidelines for cancer survivors, which align with the general recommendations for adults with chronic conditions (including cancer survivors) set forth by the World Health Organization resulting in 150 to 300 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous PA and limiting SB during the day [45, 46]. However, until now, these guidelines do not fully incorporate the total 24h day. Therefore, the 24h-MBs guidelines for adults established by Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology are often used in research within time-use epidemiology [43]. Comparing the 24h-MBs of breast cancer survivors at 12 months post-surgery to the 24h-MB guidelines, the breast cancer survivors had on average 9.3 h of sleep per night (slightly above 7–9 h/night), 10.3 h/day of SB (exceeding the recommended ≤ 8 h/day), 3.7 h/day of light PA, and 41 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous PA, surpassing the weekly threshold of 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous PA. These findings should, however, be interpreted with caution. Direct comparisons of mean 24h-MB values to guideline thresholds are limited by methodological inconsistencies, including the choice of accelerometer cut-points (e.g. Hildebrand and colleagues cut-points vs. Freedson and colleagues cut-points [26]) and differences in data processing protocols [27]. Such variability restricts comparability across studies as shown in a paper of Willems and colleagues (2024) [27]. Consequently, within-group changes over time (aim 1) and group comparisons (e.g. breast cancer survivors versus healthy controls) (aim 2) provide more reliable insights.

While the 24h-MBs of breast cancer survivors in this study improved over one year of time compared to the first week post-surgery, aim 2 showed that the 24h-MBs at 12 months post-surgery still remained less balanced compared to the 24h-MBs of healthy controls. At 12 months post-surgery, breast cancer survivors accumulated approximately 50 min more sleep per night, 40 min less light PA per day, and 30 min less moderate-to-vigorous PA per day compared to controls. These differences are clinically meaningful, as recent evidence indicates an additive interaction between insufficient PA and both short (< 7 h/night) and long (> 9 h/night) sleep durations in relation to increased all-cause mortality risk among cancer survivors [47]. Moreover, due to cancer treatment, breast cancer survivors have a higher risk to develop comorbidities (e.g., a 10% decline in exercise capacity during treatment and an increased long-term risk of cardiovascular problems), making it even more crucial for them to strive for optimal movement behaviors [40, 48].

Last, regarding aim 3, our findings indicated that higher levels of light PA, relative to the other behaviors, were associated with better overall QoL. In contrast, excessive sleep relative to the other behaviors, was associated with poorer overall QoL or unmet support needs. However, these associations were observed only at 4 months post-surgery and not at 1 week or 12 months post-surgery. This might be explained by the fact that the first months post-surgery often represents a transition phase for cancer survivors, passing by the acute treatment effects and the gradual uptake of lifestyle behaviors [39]. Compared to other research exploring associations between QoL and 24h-MBs among adults diagnosed with cancer and cancer survivors, results remain inconclusive. Some cross-sectional studies have reported positive associations between theoretically reallocating time from SB to moderate-to-vigorous PA and improvements in overall QoL, physical functioning, and physical well-being subscales [21, 49], whereas others found no association with overall QoL or its subscales [19, 20]. Another longitudinal study showed that within person increases in light PA over time were related with a better QoL [50]. Although evidence for associations between 24h-MBs and QoL is mixed, symptom-related outcomes appear more consistently linked to 24h-MBs. For example, theoretical reallocating time from SB to light PA or moderate-to-vigorous PA was not found to be associated with overall QoL but showed a decrease in cancer-related fatigue [20]. Similarly, studies examining behaviors in isolation suggest that meeting recommended PA levels is linked with fewer symptoms such as fatigue, pain, and anxiety [6, 8]. Moreover, within-person increases in light PA over time was related with less fatigue [50], whereas sustained changes in sleep duration (either shorter or longer compared to pre-diagnosis) were linked to lower vitality scores and greater fatigue among cancer survivors [51]. These cancer-related symptoms may contribute to reduced energy availability and, together with limited awareness of the benefits of PA, create barriers to sustained PA engagement [52]. Longitudinal evidence further shows that while QoL tends to improve gradually over a 5-year period and even approaching QoL levels seen in healthy controls, survivors often experience persistent adverse effects, including fatigue, sleep problems, pain, and impaired physical performance [39]. This evidence highlights the importance of examining symptom trajectories alongside QoL. Our findings align with this perspective: although only limited associations between 24h-MBs and overall QoL were observed at 4 months post-surgery and even no associations at 1 week and 12-months post-surgery; pain, fatigue, and insomnia consistently emerged as the most impairing self-reported symptoms affecting QoL across all timepoints.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this secondary data analysis is the application of compositional data analysis, representing the first study to employ this method among breast cancer survivors and emphasizing the need to account for the interdependence of all daily behaviors within a 24-h day [18]. Another strength is the longitudinal design, allowing for comparisons across multiple timepoints in the first year post-surgery (1 week, 4 months and 12 months). However, aim 3 explored the associations between 24h-MBs and QoL at different timepoints which reflects cross-sectional designs, from which causality cannot be inferred. Next, the absence of pre-surgery 24h-MBs data limited our ability to assess true recovery trajectories. Due to their absence, this study relied on a control group drawn from a separate dataset on 24h-MBs among healthy Belgian adults who were 10 years younger. Moreover, the median age of the breast cancer survivor sample was about 10 years younger than the median age of female breast cancer survivors in Belgium, which may limit the generalizability of the findings [1]. Additionally, data for the control group were collected after the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas the final assessment of the breast cancer survivor cohort occurred during the pandemic, which may have affected comparability between the two groups. Although the control group consisted of general working adults (≥ 50% employed), the comparison with breast cancer survivors at 12 months post-surgery remains meaningful, as more than 50% of the women in the survivor group had also returned to work by that time [53]. This enhances the functional comparability between both groups despite differences in recruitment criteria. Another limitation relates to chemotherapy in the BCS group: 50 participants received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and 68 received adjuvant chemotherapy, which likely influenced their PA levels and should be considered when interpreting the results. Nevertheless, this reflects the real-world variability of the breast cancer population. Regarding the weartime protocol, accelerometer data in the breast cancer survivor group were collected only during waking hours, with sleep estimated using sleep diaries. This approach may introduce inconsistencies in wear time, particularly when reported sleep and wake times do not fully align with actual device use. In contrast, a continuous 24-hour wear protocol would likely improve measurement accuracy by reducing uncertainty related to removing and reattaching the device. Last, a limitation of this study is that device removal periods during waking hours were not consistently documented. Most healthy controls did not record reasons, and breast cancer survivors mainly noted routine activities such as personal hygiene, with occasional mentions of swimming but without details on duration or intensity. Future studies should aim to collect more detailed information on device removal or include waterproof devices.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first longitudinal compositional analysis of 24h-MBs in breast cancer survivors, highlighting significant changes during the first year post-surgery. Survivors increased light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA and reduced SB early post-surgery while a plateau was found after 4 months-post-surgery. At 12 months post-surgery their 24h-MB profile showed longer sleep duration and less time spent in light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA compared to healthy controls. Notably, only at 4 months post-surgery more time spent in light PA within the total day was associated with a higher overall QoL, whereas longer sleep durations were associated with a worse overall QoL and less perceived support. Future research should further examine how 24h-MBs relate to symptom burden and QoL within- and between- days, to better understand how modifying movement behaviors can improve long-term QoL and symptom management in breast cancer survivors.
