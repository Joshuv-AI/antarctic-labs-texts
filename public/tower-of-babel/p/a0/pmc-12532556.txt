Longitudinal insights into the industrial IoT–green intellectual capital Nexus in the post-COVID era using SEM‑ANN approach
Authors: Yang Cao, Lin Xinwen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the post-COVID-19 era, the tourism industry faces urgent sustainability challenges stemming from carbon-intensive operations and inefficient resource use. While the industrial internet of things has the potential to drive green transformation, how it reconstructs green intellectual capital to foster green innovation remains underexplored. This study addresses this critical gap by adopting a three-wave longitudinal survey of 543 Chinese star-rated hotels and applying a hybrid SEM-ANN model to analyze both linear and nonlinear dynamics among industrial internet of things, green intellectual capital, and green innovation. The results reveal that industrial internet of things integration directly promotes green innovation (β = 0.214, p < 0.001) and indirectly drives green innovation by enhancing green intellectual capital dimensions: green human capital (β = 0.324) through digital skill upgrading, green structural capital (β = 0.450) via improved digital governance, and green relational capital (β = 0.360) by enabling cross-sectoral partnerships. Mediation analysis confirms that green intellectual capital partially mediates the IIoT–GI relationship (p < 0.05). Notably, ANN analysis shows that green relational capital exerts the strongest nonlinear influence on green innovation. By extending dynamic capabilities theory, this research proposes a novel IIoT–GIC–GI integration model, offering a strategic roadmap for green digital transformation in hospitality. Practically, the findings inform China’s tourism policy goals for 2023–2025 and provide actionable insights for enhancing low-carbon development in star-rated hotels.Keywords: Post-Covid-19, Industrial internet of things, Green innovation, Sustainable development, Digital transformation, Green intellectual capital, Longitudinal study, SEM-ANN modelSubject terms: Environmental economics, Sustainability

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has had a profound impact on the global tourism industry, bringing business travel to a near standstill and revealing underlying structural weaknesses such as low resilience, poor information integration, and inadequate digital infrastructure1,2. These disruptions have further intensified environmental pressures, particularly in developing countries where tourism’s ecological footprint was already significant3–5. In response, countries like China have accelerated their digital transformation strategies to promote industrial and economic recovery. With strong network infrastructure and strategic government support, the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) has emerged as a key enabler for rebuilding tourism operations with higher efficiency, safety, and environmental responsiveness6. However, despite its potential, IIoT deployment in small and medium-sized (SME) tourism enterprises remains uneven due to digital resource fragmentation6, capability gaps4, and unclear governance standards7. The challenges have brought sustainability concerns to the forefront. Tourism businesses are not only under pressure to recover economically but also to address long-standing ecological vulnerabilities2,3,8. While aligning digital transformation with sustainability goals requires more than technological investment1,9. It demands organizational capacity, cross-sector knowledge, and trust based collaboration10,11. This highlights the critical role of green intellectual capital (GIC), which encompasses green human capital, green structural capital, and green relational capital, as a strategic mediator in enabling corporates to convert IIoT capabilities into sustainable outcomes12–14.

While existing literature confirms the positive link between IIoT and green innovation15, little is known about how IIoT affects the three dimensions of GIC to enhance sustainable performance. Furthermore, the unique conditions of SMEs limited resources, context-specific constraints, and uneven digital maturity remain underexplored in this relationship. Addressing this gap, this study constructs a mediation model that investigates how IIoT capabilities influence green performance through GIC in Chinese SMEs, thereby contributing to the emerging discourse on post-COVID digital sustainability transitions in tourism. On this basis, GIC as a strategic resource for the green transformation of the tourism industry, empowers enterprises to develop low-carbon products (such as eco-tourism routes) and services (such as carbon tracing systems) by integrating environmental knowledge and technological capabilities4,12,14,16. This not only meets the mandatory requirements of policies for low-carbon development but also aligns with consumers’ preferences for sustainability13, thereby establishing differentiated competitive advantages18. Meanwhile, GIC-driven resource integration optimizes supply chain efficiency and reduces the costs of green innovation17. Furthermore, green product innovation (such as renewable energy facilities) and green process innovation (including IIoT-based (Industrial Internet of Things) intelligent waste sorting and energy consumption optimization) form a dual-driving force: the former enhances brand premium and market competitiveness through eco-friendly products19, while the latter achieves resource recycling and cost control through technological means, thereby fulfilling corporate social responsibility18. The synergy between the two not only constructs an ecological value chain from “design to recycling” but also promotes the construction of an innovative ecosystem in the tourism industry through cross-enterprise collaboration20, accelerating resilient recovery in the post-Covid-19 era21.

In the post-Covid-19 era, the green transformation of the tourism industry urgently required the deep integration of green intellectual capital (GIC) and the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT). GIC, by integrating green human capital (green skills of employees)8, green structural capital (low-carbon operational processes)18, and relational capital (cross-sector collaboration), directly drives improvements in environmental performance (e.g., hotel energy optimization) and green technological innovation (e.g., intelligent emission reduction systems)4,8,19–21. However, the empowerment of GIC through IIoT is hindered by three critical structural contradictions: data silos (disconnected systems between hotels and scenic areas), skill gaps (employees’ lack of data analysis and smart technology capabilities), and collaboration barriers (absence of effective cross-departmental data sharing mechanisms)22.

The key issue is that, if these contradictions remain unresolved, IIoT would be reduced to an ineffective technological artifact, sensor-generated data might fail to inform real-world emission reduction efforts, thereby exacerbating the disconnect between tourism development and global carbon neutrality targets23. Conversely, by constructing a synergistic model centered on skill development, data integration, and collaborative governance, IIoT could become a true lever for sustainable transition7. For example, the pressure of job displacement can be transformed into green employment opportunities—such as training digital energy managers—while smart grids can be integrated into regional carbon networks to achieve systemic decarbonization22. Thus, clarifying the coordination logic between IIoT and GIC is not only pivotal in addressing the tourism sector’s high-carbon growth dilemma, but also constitutes a decisive challenge for aligning Industry 4.0 technologies with the objectives of the Paris Agreement.

Although existing literature has acknowledged the positive impact of the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) on green innovation (GI)17, three critical research gaps remain. First, the mechanisms through which IIoT influences the multidimensional structure of green intellectual capital (GIC) are still unclear. Current studies primarily focus on the direct relationship between IIoT and GI, without exploring how IIoT technologies such as intelligent sensing and data sharing specifically enhance green human capital (e.g., skill restructuring), green structural capital (e.g., low-carbon process optimization), and green relational capital (e.g., ecological collaboration networks)24. Second, there is a lack of an integrated framework that connects IIoT-enabled capabilities, GIC resource reconfiguration, and the transformation of GI outcomes. Existing studies tend to investigate either the technological driver role of IIoT on GI17 or the mediating role of GIC in sustainable development25, while overlooking the nonlinear interactions among these three elements. Third, the misalignment between IIoT transformation and existing GIC remains unresolved. The realization of the environmental benefits of Industry 4.0 technologies requires the dynamic alignment of institutional capabilities. However, there remains a challenge in achieving such alignment between technology deployment and institutional capacity during transformation practices26. Although technical studies have discussed hardware limitations in IIoT implementation27, there is a lack of empirical investigation into how existing GIC (such as employees’ digital capabilities and cross-departmental coordination mechanisms) matches the requirements of IIoT-driven transformation. This leads to an unaddressed imbalance between technological potential and institutional inertia. This study aims to bridge these gaps by applying dynamic capability theory to examine the linear and nonlinear relationships among IIoT, GIC, and GI.

This study makes significant contributions to the literature on green intellectual capital in several ways: (1) It focuses on how the IIoT promotes the development of GIC in the tourism industry, addressing a gap in existing research. (2) A new framework model is developed, highlighting IIoT as a crucial driver for enhancing green intellectual capital and its impact on green innovation. (3) This study adopts a strategic perspective at the overall organizational level. Unlike previous studies where respondents were primarily frontline employees8,28, the respondents in this study are senior managers who are more representative of the hotel industry. (4) Most importantly, this study employs a hybrid SEM-ANN (Structural Equation Modeling-Artificial Neural Network) model for analysis in two phases. Compared to a single model, the SEM-ANN model provides more insightful research results29. Traditional SEM is mainly suitable for linear relationships, while ANN can compensate for this limitation by capturing more complex nonlinear relationships30, improving prediction accuracy and model robustness31. This study explores the intrinsic connections between IIoT, green intellectual capital, and green innovation in the post-pandemic context. As the core of digital transformation in the tourism industry, IIoT reshapes the industry ecosystem by enhancing tourist experiences, optimizing operations, and promoting sustainable development in tourism. Therefore, it not only has academic value but also provides practical guidance for achieving sustainable social and economic development, making a significant contribution to creating an eco-friendly future.

Theoretical background and hypotheses development

Dynamic capacities theory

The dynamic capacities theory examines how businesses sense, seize, and restructure internal and external resources to stay competitive in fast-changing environments32. Businesses gain competitiveness by recognizing environmental shifts, grabbing market opportunities, and dynamically shifting resources33. Dynamic capabilities play a very important role in green innovation, allowing enterprises to integrate GIC with innovative practices, fostering green innovation and enhancing environmental performance33. Through real-time data analysis and resource integration, IIoT enables enterprises to sense market demands for green products and low-carbon production. By enhancing green human capital (green skills training), green structural capital (knowledge management and process optimization), and green relational capital (supply chain collaboration transparency), it helps enterprises dynamically capture green opportunities. Furthermore, star-rated hotels utilize IIoT to optimize resource allocation, promoting technological and process-related green innovation, thereby achieving a win-win outcome for environmental and economic benefits. The rapidly changing post-pandemic environment highlights the critical role of dynamic capabilities theory in supporting enterprises to flexibly adapt to green transformation, providing a vital pathway for sustainable development.

Based on the sensing–seizing–reconfiguring framework of dynamic capabilities theory, this study constructs a mechanism model linking IIoT, GIC, and GI. In the sensing stage, IIoT enables real-time monitoring of environmental changes such as carbon emissions and resource efficiency. In the seizing stage, technologies like digital twins are used to identify and capture green innovation opportunities. In the reconfiguring stage, organizations restructure their green intellectual capital by enhancing employees’ data analysis skills (green human capital), developing intelligent decision-making systems (green structural capital), and building ecological partnership networks (green relational capital). The sensing stage is enabled by IIoT technologies such as real-time environmental sensors and data acquisition tools, which allow firms to monitor energy consumption, waste generation, and tourist behaviors15,27. These data streams improve awareness of emerging sustainability risks and opportunities. Seizing is supported by predictive analytics and intelligent decision-making systems that help tourism SMEs prioritize green investments and redesign processes for efficiency23,34. Finally, reconfiguring is driven by machine-to-machine (M2M) communication and edge computing, which enable the dynamic coordination and transformation of internal processes, organizational routines, and stakeholder relationships in response to green imperatives15,34.

Relationship between IIOT and GI

As a transformative technology, IIoT is profoundly impacting environmental sustainability. Through applications such as smart sensors and energy-efficient data centers, IIoT technology significantly enhances energy efficiency and resource optimization. This has not only enabled real-time monitoring, automation, and green production methods in industrial fields17,35, but also promoted energy conservation, emissions reduction, and efficient resource management systems in smart cities36. Moreover, IIoT supports the circular economy by enhancing waste management and optimizing supply chains, thereby promoting sustainable practices37. However, the widespread application of IoT has also brought challenges, such as high energy consumption, electronic waste, and carbon emissions38,39. To address these challenges, green IIoT technologies have emerged, striving to mitigate environmental impacts through energy-efficient designs, green production and recycling practices, the deployment of low-energy sensors, and the integration of renewable energy38.

According to dynamic theory, the effect of IIoT on green innovation in the tourism sector is a complex, interconnected, and dynamic process. IIoT technology combines physical equipment, data systems, and human decision-making to help luxury hotels and tourism facilities save energy and resources37. Smart cities and data-driven frameworks promote green tourism by integrating green buildings, energy, and trash management into tourism infrastructure8,33. Tourism is more sustainable and adaptable to environmental changes due to these interdependencies. In addition, the Green IIoT encourages industrial actors to collaborate on sustainable production and circular economy practices, displaying interdependence17.

On the other hand, the sensing, seizing, and transforming mechanisms of the dynamic capabilities theory are crucial for enhancing green practices in the tourism industry32. The adoption of the IIoT has accelerated green innovation, while the integrated services of smart tourism destinations have improved experiences and reduced environmental burdens33. Additionally, the success of smart grids has promoted technological advancements and investments in clean energy40,41. Attention to the carbon footprint and electronic waste of IIoT devices has driven the industry to adopt green design principles and recycling programs38.

Tourism IIoT can adapt to external challenges, such as pandemics, through the application of the adaptability principle of dynamic theory. IIoT technology increases supply chain efficiency and transparency while cutting emissions and waste42, and offering robust support for the development of resilient supply chains23. Moreover, IIoT-enabled remote monitoring is pivotal in fostering green technologies in agriculture, water management, and energy consumption, providing sustainable solutions for the tourism sector37.

Therefore, we posit that:

H1: IIOT positively affects green innovation in the tourism industry.

Relationship between IIOT and GIC

Real-time data platforms from IIoT enable organizations to train employees to optimize resource use and eliminate waste43,44. IIoT devices give employees real-time feedback on their environmental impact23, boosting their environmental responsibility45. Privacy and carbon footprint concerns encourage organizations to streamline logistics and minimize energy use with IIoT technology46, boosting green structural capital. In addition, IIoT allows tourism companies to track and analyze energy, water, and waste in real time to adapt to market and environmental changes37. It provides dynamic environmental management by helping companies develop green products and processes47. IIoT and blockchain improve supply chain transparency, allowing organizations to build greater relational capital with eco-friendly partners48.

Early IIoT adoption creates technology path dependency32,49. Initial investments in hotel and tourism facility smart management systems accelerate the transition to completely intelligent and green technologies18,40,49. Corporate investments in IIoT technology and green intellectual capital are boosted by environmental policies33,43. IIoT improves supply chain collaboration efficiency, providing organizations with dynamic integration capabilities in green supply chains45. It also strengthens partnerships with eco-friendly partners48, increasing environmental protection18,33.

Therefore, we posit that:

H2: IIOT positively affects green intellectual capital in tourism industry.

Relationship between GIC and GI

Green intellectual capital drives green innovation by providing knowledge and intangible assets conducive to innovation, with varying impacts across its different dimensions20,21. Research indicates that green human capital and green structural capital have significant effects on green innovation performance35,50. whereas green relational capital, although supportive of innovation in small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), has a less pronounced impact23. Additionally, green innovation acts as a mediator in the influence of green intellectual capital (GIC) on corporate sustainability and environmental performance24. Dual-adept green innovation, which combines exploratory and exploitative strategies, serves as a key channel through which green intellectual capital impacts performance51. However, research on leveraging green intellectual capital to promote green innovation in the tourism industry remains insufficient, with most studies focusing on the manufacturing and agricultural sectors35.

The dynamic capabilities theory posits that tourism enterprises develop unique green innovation capabilities by integrating internal processes, policies, and knowledge management systems34,52, along with employees’ environmental awareness and skills, and dynamically integrating resources53. Collaboration with stakeholders helps in acquiring green resources and market data, thereby fostering innovation35. Hotels innovate through green supply chain practices, with green knowledge capital aiding enterprises in rapidly responding to policy changes and consumer demands36, as well as promoting green technologies and practices through collaboration54. Dynamically integrating green relational capital is particularly crucial in the tourism industry, requiring coordination among various stakeholders such as suppliers, communities, and governments20. Specifically, green human capital provides the capacity for innovation (perception layer), green structural capital establishes institutional safeguards for innovation (acquisition layer), and green relational capital expands the boundaries of innovation resources (reconfiguration layer)33.

Therefore, we posit that:

H3: Green intellectual capital positively affects green innovation in the tourism industry.

Mediating role of GIC between IIoT and GI

The role of GIC between IIoT and GI essentially embodies the transformation process from technological capabilities to organizational routines13. Specifically, data assets generated by IIoT are transformed into structural capital through knowledge encoding, human-machine collaborative work models reshape employees’ green skill sets, and digital platforms expand the boundaries of ecological cooperation networks17. This transformation mechanism enables the conversion of technological potential into sustainable innovation capabilities50. Although the role of driving factors cannot be overlooked, GIC struggles to achieve outstanding green innovation independently without IIoT support. Current research on the mediating role of GIC remains insufficient. As a combination of sustainable development knowledge and capabilities, GIC converts IIoT into green productivity by integrating resources, not only enhancing innovation effectiveness but also identifying green market opportunities and facilitating organizational adaptation to sustainable development policies.

Therefore, we posit that:

H4: Green intellectual capital mediates the relationship between industrial Internet of Things and green innovation in the tourism industry.

Methodology

The comprehensive process described in Fig. 2 outlines the research methodology employed in this study. Initially, an in-depth literature review is conducted to expand the dynamic capabilities theory, thereby constructing a conceptual framework. Subsequently, the validity and reliability of the questionnaire are assessed, and a questionnaire from previous research is adapted for use. Then, using convenience sampling, questionnaires are distributed to top managers of star-rated hotels to collect data, which is then divided into training and testing sets. During the data analysis phase, a hybrid approach is utilized, including the structural equation modeling (SEM) for measuring model evaluation, and artificial neural networks (ANN) for model validation. Ultimately, research hypotheses are explored through multi-criterion hypothesis testing.

Research process.

Sample and data collection

The sample for this study consists of star-rated hotels listed by the China Tourist Hotel Association (CTHA) in 2024. These hotels follow strict national and industry standards, such as the Classification and Rating of Tourist Hotels (GB/T 14308 − 2023), which ensures consistency in management practices and service quality, laying the groundwork for research comparability and reliability.

Star-rated hotels, as a key component of Chinese hotel business, not only cover a wide geographic range, from first-tier cities to smaller cities and villages, thoroughly covering the landscape of China’s hotel industry, but also hold a significant position in the worldwide hotel market. Globalized management and customers characterize these hotels, which meet international standards. Additionally, they lead service innovation, customer relationship management, and market strategy. Star-rated hotels also prioritize sustainable development, such as green hotel certifications, making them excellent for researching corporate green intellectual capital and innovation. Research on Chinese star-rated hotels fills a geographical void in worldwide hotel industry research, highlighting emerging economies’ unique characteristics, and provides case studies for other developing countries.

This study collected data through survey. A pre-test was undertaken with three scholars experienced in green innovation and sustainability research and two industry practitioners using green practices to guarantee the assessment validity of scales. A pilot test was then carried out by sending the questionnaire to frontline personnel engaged in green activities at star-rated hotels, with 42 responses collected to assess reliability.

According to China Tourist Hotel Association, China has 7,973 star-rated hotels in 2024. The finalized questionnaire was delivered to top executives of star-rated hotels via convenience sampling method. Although the CTHA list of star-rated hotels was used as a reference sampling frame, many listed hotels were either unresponsive or unable to commit to a three-wave survey due to ongoing post-pandemic recovery challenges. As such, convenience sampling was adopted to select hotels with established IIoT applications and managerial willingness to participate in the full data collection process. This approach ensured data quality while maintaining alignment with the study’s scope. The respondents all adopted green practices, knew their hotels’ green innovation performance, and had at least two years of hotel work experience. Prior to data collection, the study guaranteed that participant data was confidential and anonymous. According to G*power 3.1.9.7, the minimum sample size of this study is 129. Because the proposed framework includes mediated connections a three-wave approach was used for two months to distinguish cause from effect. At time 1, IoT and demographics were collected as independent variables. At time 2, GIC was collected as a mediation variable, and at time 3, GI was collected as a dependent variable. The duration between each wave was two months. 543 valid surveys were received. This interval was chosen based on both theoretical and methodological grounds. Specifically, it was intended to help confirm the causal relationships among IIoT capability, Green Intellectual Capital (GIC), and green performance. A time lag allows sufficient duration for changes in GIC dimensions to materialize in response to IIoT implementation while mitigating common method bias.

Measures

This study adapts previous research by categorizing green intellectual capital into three dimensions for measurement18 : green human capital (three items), green structural capital (five items), and green relational capital (three items)18,33,51. Each dimension is a reflective first-order latent construct.

The scale of the industrial Internet of Things (5 items) was adopted based on previous research15,41. First-order latent construct is used.

The scale of green innovation is adopted from previous literature52, which is green product performance (four items) and green process performance (four items) respectively, and set as second-order reflective latent construct.

All measures were scored on a seven-point Likert scale, with 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Data analysis

In the first phase, this study employs PLS-SEM (Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling) using SmartPLS. PLS-SEM is a statistical analysis method that is appropriate for complicated models and prediction-based research. It is especially useful for small sample sizes, non-normal data, and models with several latent variables and path links (Hair et al., 2021). In comparison to CB-SEM, PLS-SEM focusses on explaining variance and predictive capabilities. It uses iterative optimization to maximize the explained variance across variables, assuring model stability and practical outcomes53.

In the second phase, this study applied artificial neural network (ANN) analysis to examine the nonlinear relationships between predictor variables and the dependent variable as Fig. 3. Although ANN models have been applied in the fields of behavioral science and marketing29,30, their application in organizational management remains limited. Therefore, this study fills the gap in literature from a methodological perspective. The ANN model was implemented using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architecture with one input layer, one hidden layer, and one output layer. The number of neurons in the hidden layer was optimized using a grid search approach, with selection based on minimizing the root mean square error (RMSE) during the training process. The number of neurons was selected based on minimum RMSE. The sigmoid activation function was used for both hidden and output layers. Additionally, 10-fold cross-validation was performed to ensure the robustness and generalizability of the model.

Common variance Bias

By procedure technically this study guarantees that respondents are anonymous and confidential during the data collection process. In addition, the wording used in the questionnaire is simple and straightforward41. From a statistical perspective, this study tested CMB using two different approaches. Initially, the PLS methodology was used to evaluate the latent method component in the structural model. Given that the majority of the Rb2 values are insignificant and all the Ra values of the indicators are significantly higher than Rb values, indicating that CMB is improbably a main issue in the current data set. Second, researchers stated that if the VIF value is less than 3.3, there is no CMB in the mean model54. All relevant variables in this study exhibited VIF values below 3.3. Consequently, no multicollinearity issue exists in this context.

Notably, a difference arises when comparing SEM and ANN findings regarding GSC. While SEM confirms a significant positive linear relationship between GSC and Green Innovation (β = 0.176, p < 0.001), the ANN Model A reports very low normalized importance for GSC (0.183%) when IIoT is included. Interestingly, GSC becomes highly important (60.9%) in Model C, where GIC components alone predict Green Innovation. This divergence suggests that GSC’s contribution may be overshadowed by strong nonlinear effects from IIoT or GRC when present. It is also plausible that GSC acts as a suppressor variable in multivariate non-linear configurations, diminishing its relative contribution when stronger interactive predictors dominate. This reinforces the value of the SEM-ANN hybrid approach in uncovering differential effects that may remain hidden in linear-only models.

Discussion

This study employs a three-stage longitudinal design and a hybrid SEM-ANN model to explore how IIoT influences green intellectual capital (GIC) and promotes green innovation in the hospitality industry. The research advances dynamic capabilities theory in three key ways. First, it is the first to empirically verify how IIoT technologies empower the three dimensions of GIC through distinct pathways. Specifically, intelligent monitoring systems enhance employees’ green skills (GHC, β = 0.307). Blockchain platforms help build digital governance structures (GSC, β = 0.450). Cross-sector collaboration mechanisms foster ecological partnership networks (GRC, β = 0.232). These findings offer a nuanced understanding of how IIoT drives green transformation at the organizational level. These findings not only confirm existing hypotheses regarding the restructuring of global value chains by Industry 4.0 technologies26 but also empirically demonstrate the specific mechanisms by which IIoT breaks down “data silos” in the hospitality organizations. Compared to traditional manufacturing, where IIoT primarily optimizes production processes17,56, our research identifies a decisive nonlinear mediation effect of GRC underscoring the unique reliance of service industry green transformations on relational capital28. Tourism is inherently experiential, high-contact, and network-dependent, relying heavily on relationships with stakeholders such as eco-conscious customers, suppliers, communities, and regulatory bodies. In such a context, relational capital serves not merely as a support mechanism but as a strategic enabler of green innovation through trust-building, resource sharing, and reputation leverage. Furthermore, IIoT technologies amplify these dynamics by enabling real-time environmental monitoring, seamless M2M communication, and transparent reporting, which in turn enhance stakeholder confidence and co-creation potential. The nonlinear behavior of GRC suggests that its influence might only manifest beyond a certain relational threshold, once a firm establishes sufficient trust and connectivity within its green ecosystem, its capacity to innovate and implement sustainable practices accelerates significantly. This insight deepens our understanding of how digital enablers interact with intangible assets in service-based sectors to drive transformation.

Implications

Theoretically, this study extends the application of dynamic capabilities theory by demonstrating how specific IIoT functionalities drive the sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring stages. For instance, the real-time sensor data enhances sensing capabilities by identifying ecological inefficiencies, predictive analytics strengthen seizing by enabling firms to allocate resources toward green innovation, and M2M communication supports reconfiguring by facilitating internal structural change and external stakeholder. These dynamic capabilities manifest concretely in the transformation of GIC dimensions. GHC through IIoT-driven skill realignment, GSC via the digitization of green processes and routines, and GRC through data-enabled collaborative partnerships. A notable temporal pattern emerging from the longitudinal analysis is the “structure-first, relation-led” sequence, wherein GSC plays a dominant role in early stages while GRC exerts increasing influence over time. This dynamic likely reflects the phased nature of organizational digital transformation. In the early adoption phase, IIoT deployment typically focuses on building internal data infrastructures, automation routines, and environmental monitoring systems, all of which contribute to enhancing GSC through improved governance frameworks and standardized green processes. These internal foundations subsequently enable organizations to engage more effectively with external stakeholders, including customers, regulatory bodies, and sustainability-focused partners. As digital maturity increases, firms are better positioned to leverage GRC for co-innovation, transparent communication, and ecosystem-based green initiatives. This supports the argument that internal digital capacity is a precondition for effective external collaboration, with relational capital exerting nonlinear impacts once a critical structural base is achieved. This finding reinforces the relevance of dynamic capabilities theory, highlighting how sensing and seizing phases might be GSC-driven, while reconfiguring capabilities are catalyzed through GRC engagement.

Methodologically, the comparative SEM-ANN analysis highlights limitations of traditional linear models. Although SEM initially confirms the significant mediating role of GSC (β = 0.066), ANN analysis reveals exponential increases in GRC’s contribution once accumulation surpasses a threshold of 0.87 standardized units, significantly reducing the RMSE from 0.687 to 0.135. This nonlinear dynamic characteristic revises Chen’s linear framework of GIC18, verifying that green innovation in the hospitality organizations follows complex adaptive system dynamics. Contrary to the incremental innovations driven predominantly by structural capital in manufacturing33, innovation breakthroughs more heavily depend on the synergistic effects triggered by relational capital. This finding extends the contextual theoretical boundaries of dynamic capability theory, demonstrating that the “sensing-seizing-reconfiguring” stages proposed by Teece (1997) exhibit unique evolutionary capital formation patterns13,32.

Temporal analysis of three-wave longitudinal data further reveals the dynamic coupling mechanisms between institutional capabilities and technological deployment. Initially, during early IIoT deployment (T1-T2), GSC contributes significantly (72.3%) through digital governance frameworks; however, in the subsequent T2-T3 phase, GRC’s mediation effect through ecological network effects rises notably to 58.9%. This “structure-first, relation-led” evolution, captured at a micro-temporal scale, elucidates the dynamic coupling of digital technologies with institutional capabilities, surpassing cross-sectional studies’ limitations that typically treat GIC elements as static25. Importantly, GHC consistently exerts stable influence throughout the innovative lifecycle (β = 0.307), indicating that digital skill restructuring is fundamental to green transformations, thereby providing direct evidence for tourism talent policymaking and supplementing current studies on the role of green human capital8.

At the practical level, the integrated IIoT–GIC–GI model proposed by this study provides an actionable framework supporting China’s “2023–2025 Domestic Tourism Enhancement Plan.” By identifying the threshold effect of GRC (0.87 standardized units), this research recommends setting cross-enterprise data-sharing platform coverage as a core criterion for green certification standards of star-rated hotels. Additionally, to address digital governance requirements related to GSC, regional tourism carbon footprint tracking systems can be developed based on the validated blockchain architecture (factor loading = 0.930). These initiatives effectively resolve the “technology-institution mismatch” identified in prior studies47, facilitating a shift from singular technological subsidies toward systematic capability building, aligning with the digital transformation strategy advocated by scholars10. Hotels should use IIoT to foster cross-sectoral partnerships (e.g., green data-sharing platforms, joint dashboards) to operationalize GRC benefits, enhancing trust, co-learning, and joint innovation on sustainability. This aligns with study showing inter-organizational cooperation boosts digital green supply chains’ impact on eco-innovations57. In hospitality organizations, embedding cooperation in IIoT strategies (e.g., alliances, cloud audits, AI systems) could strengthen GRC’s role in promoting green innovation.

Specifically, hotels adopting Industrial IoT-supported platforms (such as blockchain for carbon tracking and AI-driven energy management systems) can overcome data silos and integrate green skill development with real-time environmental monitoring. For instance, intelligent sensors could reduce energy consumption by 20–30%, while employee digital training programs (e.g., data analysis for waste reduction) enhance the competitive advantage of green hotels by strengthening GHC. Policymakers are encouraged to incorporate GRC indicators (such as cross-enterprise data-sharing compliance) into green certification criteria for star-rated hotels. Local governments can incentivize the development of blockchain-based carbon footprint tracking systems to complement tourism enhancement plans. Industry associations should prioritize “green digital literacy” initiatives to foster collaboration among hotels, technology providers, and NGOs, thereby amplifying GRC impacts, collaborative platforms for eco-tourism routes can reduce supply chain emissions and enhance market differentiation.

Limitations and direction for future research

This study has limitations, notably its sample restriction to the Chinese hotel industry, suggesting future comparative research could examine IIoT empowerment differences under varying institutional contexts. Furthermore, the GRC threshold effect identified by the ANN model requires further longitudinal validation. These limitations highlight directions for future research, particularly in the development of industrial symbiosis networks and agent-based simulation modeling. Through innovative methodological design and theoretical construction, this study offers a new analytical framework and empirical benchmark for sustainable hospitality organizations research in the digital economy era.

Future research could advance theoretical depth and practical innovation in sustainable tourism across multiple dimensions. First, it is essential to broaden the analytical framework by verifying the generalizability of the GRC threshold effect within the IIoT-GIC framework under diverse institutional contexts, such as EU circular economy demonstration zones and ASEAN tourism economic communities. Emphasis should be placed on analyzing how policy instruments, including carbon pricing, moderate the dynamic relationship between Industrial IoT and circular green innovation. Furthermore, future studies should expand beyond hotel contexts, adopting cross-sector IoT analytical approaches to fields such as eco-tourism accommodations and cultural heritage preservation, constructing tailored theoretical models based on industry-specific characteristics. In terms of technological integration, exploring the application potential of AI tools like ChatGPT in green skill training is recommended, with a focus on quantifying their enhancement mechanisms for greenhouse gas emission reductions. Additionally, future research could explore integrating generative artificial intelligence (e.g., large language models, generative design tools) with IIoT to shape GIC in tourism. Scholars found that combining IIoT’s real-time sensing with generative artificial intelligence generative abilities supports ESG performance and eco-innovation, offering opportunities for empirical testing and digital strategy development58. Generative artificial intelligence could enhance GHC through personalized training, GSC via scenario modeling, and GRC through stakeholder simulations. Furthermore, while this study offers important insights into the organizational mechanisms linking IIoT, GIC, and green innovation, it does not incorporate external contextual factors that may moderate these relationships. Variables such as government subsidies, environmental policy frameworks, or regional digital infrastructure quality can significantly shape the effectiveness of IIoT deployment and GIC development59. Future studies should consider integrating multi-level models or policy environmental interaction terms to capture these exogenous moderating effects, thus offering a more holistic understanding of digital sustainability pathways in tourism and beyond. Emerging quantum computing technology could amplify IIoT-enabled systems in tourism by solving complex optimization problems and accelerating AI training60, enabling better green resource allocation. Future research should explore how quantum-enhanced AI could integrate with IIoT to create more adaptive, intelligent sustainability frameworks.

Conclusion

In the post-Covid-19 era, where digital transformation and sustainability are inextricably linked, this study illuminates a transformative pathway: IIoT is not merely a technological tool but a catalyst for redefining how institutions think, collaborate, and innovate. By bridging the gap between dynamic capabilities theory and green intellectual capital, we provide a roadmap for turning data into actionable sustainability—one where smart sensors, cross-sector partnerships, and adaptive governance converge to build resilient, low-carbon futures. As the tourism industry navigates the dual imperatives of recovery and climate action, our findings urge stakeholders to embrace complexity, prioritize relational capital, and reimagine innovation as a collective endeavor. The journey toward sustainability is not a sprint but a marathon, and this research offers both the compass and the proof that collaboration, powered by technology, can light the way.
