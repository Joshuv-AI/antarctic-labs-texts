Frail to Fail: More Support for Less Reserve—Can Clinical Frailty Scale Predict Path to Liberation?

Frailty and Weaning Failure

The concept that weaning begins the moment a patient is intubated is the cornerstone of modern intensive care, aimed at reducing the risks of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), muscle atrophy, and prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays. This proactive strategy focuses on early weaning to reduce complications. However, 20–30% of mechanically ventilated patients are considered difficult to wean. Determining the reason for difficult weaning and subsequently developing a strategy requires a dedicated clinician with a detailed understanding of the pathophysiology of weaning failure.1

Frailty is widely recognized as a significant contributor to difficult weaning. It is not just associated with advanced age; it is a multidimensional syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve, loss of mobility, chronic systemic inflammation, impaired cardiopulmonary function, and reduced multisystem resilience. Weaning failure can also occur due to respiratory sarcopenia, diaphragmatic dysfunction, and ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (VIDD). These impairments limit the patient's ability to adopt or tolerate mechanical ventilation.1,2

Frailty is common in critically ill populations, affecting nearly 40% of ICU patients older than 75 years of age. Frailty, associated with diminished physiological reserves, not only delays the initiation of weaning, even if the patient meets the initial eligibility criteria, but also increases the likelihood of weaning failure. In critically ill patients, the effect of frailty on the weaning process often has a more consistent and substantial impact than chronological age alone. Studies show failure rates as high as 23–25% for frail cohorts, compared to approximately 13% for robust populations.

Hence, the frail population requires a longer total duration of mechanical ventilation—with a median of 8 vs 5 days in certain septic populations—and exhibit an increased rate of tracheostomy due to prolonged weaning difficulties. While airway failure is common across all groups, respiratory failure is the most frequent specific cause of weaning failure in frail patients.3

Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS): A Reliable Tool for ICU Decision-making

Multiple techniques may be employed to assess frailty; however, their use is uncertain in older adults, particularly in ICUs and emergency departments. Given the potential side effects, the incorrect application of scales can lead to hurried decision-making, especially under time constraints and with limited healthcare resources. The frailty phenotype is now actively being used by many physicians to evaluate older adults for ICU admissions, limitation of life-sustaining treatments, or prognostication.4,5 The CFS is a rapid substitute for the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA). The CFS is a 9-point scale used to grade the level of frailty, 1–3 indicates no activity limitations, 4–7 represents increased limitations of daily activities, and 8–9 indicates severe frailty or terminal decline. This CFS integrates mobility, baseline function, and comorbidity burden; it provides information not captured by conventional respiratory readiness parameters and may complement extubation decision-making.6,7

Evidence Added

The systematic review and meta-analysis in this issue of the Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine, which included seven observational studies involving 13,746 patients, examined the association between frailty and weaning outcomes among mechanically ventilated patients using CFS thresholds of ≥5 and ≥4.8 The study reports that frailty is strongly associated with weaning failure, with odds ratios ranging from 1.4 to 32.31, an average 2.5-fold increase in risk. This corresponds to an increase of approximately 73–334 cases of weaning failure per 1,000 patients.

This meta-analysis study used Hedges’ g; the pooled effect was 0.51 (95% CI: 0.21–0.81), which remained significant when restricted to continuous data (g = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.33–1.13). When the analysis was limited to studies with low risk of bias, the association was stronger (OR: 4.23, 95% CI: 1.65–10.8), while the inclusion of only large studies (n > 100) showed a more precise but moderate effect (OR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.34–1.70).

Excluding studies that defined weaning failure using spontaneous breathing trial criteria still showed a significant association (OR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.37–1.83). Similarly, restricting the analysis to studies defining failure as reintubation within 7 days yielded consistent results (OR: 1.94, 95% CI: 1.2–3.16), which states that this association is consistent across subgroup analyses. Furthermore, the study's methodology adheres to the PRISMA and MOOSE guidelines, with an extensive sensitivity analysis to ensure the robustness of the findings.8

Clinical Implications—A Tool, Not a Rule

The CFS is a prognostic tool rather than a rigid rule in the management of mechanically ventilated patients, helping clinicians to identify patients at risk of weaning failure and delayed extubation.

A CFS score ≥5 is an independent predictor of delayed weaning and the probability of weaning failure.7 However, the CFS is a global, clinician-judgement-based tool, which introduces subjectivity and potential observational bias, which can lead to ableism bias in the case of physical disabilities, where reduced mobility does not necessarily reflect physiological reserve. Distinguishing between intermediate frailty levels (e.g., CFS 4 vs CFS 5) remains difficult, making it challenging to determine intervention cut-offs. This meta-analysis evidence was rated as low certainty with substantial heterogeneity among the included studies (I² > 90%), indicating major inconsistency in the study effects. The CFS may be less accurate and is potentially inadequate for patients with dementia, and relies heavily on accurate premorbid functional assessment. Despite its prognostic utility, it is difficult to separate the effects of frailty from those of advanced chronological age in predicting poor outcomes.8–10

Future Directions

Future care should incorporate the CFS early in the course of the ICU management process as part of a structured risk assessment, rather than as a mandatory standalone tool, and further studies should be conducted to investigate frailty in specific subpopulations, such as sepsis, heart failure, and prolonged ventilation cohorts. The protocol should focus on individual nutrition and metabolic optimization to enhance respiratory muscle strength, use diaphragm ultrasound with dynamic tools that go beyond standard respiratory parameters, and measure muscle fatigue by assessing diaphragmatic excursion and thickening fractions. The development of a predictive model that combines traditional clinical factors, artificial intelligence, and machine learning to improve the prediction of weaning outcomes is essentially the need of the hour. Importantly, frailty should not be seen as a barrier to treatment but rather as a marker of the need for more attentive and individualized management. By integrating the CFS with traditional physiological parameters, clinicians will improve decision-making, reduce the risk of premature extubation, and enhance patient outcomes in complex situations.9,11,12

Conclusion

Frailty, as assessed by the CFS, is an important and practical marker of physiological reserve among critically ill patients and is more frequently associated with an increased risk of weaning failure. While current meta-analytic evidence supports its role in risk stratification, the available data remain of low certainty, and substantial heterogeneity limits its utility in directing clinical decision-making. Therefore, frailty should be interpreted as an adjunct rather than a replacement for established clinical parameters predicting weaning failure. Future research should focus on the standardized definitions, prospective validation, and targeted interventions to better understand the role of the CFS in optimizing weaning strategies and improving patient-centered outcomes in the intensive care settings.
