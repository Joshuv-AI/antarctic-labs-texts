Temperature effects on single cavitation bubble dynamics under the free field condition: Experimental and theoretical investigations on water☆
Authors: Hao Geng, Tairan Chen, Jiacheng Chen, Biao Huang, Guoyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper examines how water temperature affects the dynamics of a single cavitation bubble in free field conditions. Both experimental and theoretical approaches are employed to explore the bubble dynamics in water under different temperatures. A series of single bubble experiments are conducted in water using the capacitive discharge method, with water temperature ranging from room temperature to near boiling point under atmospheric pressure. A high-speed photography system is utilized to capture the bubble evolution during the experiments. The experimental results suggest that (1) At all temperatures, the bubble evolutions progress through expansion, shrinkage, and oscillation stages, with significant changes observed in bubble dynamics when temperatures are above 60 °C. (2) The maximum bubble radius and the oscillation period of the cavitation bubble increase with increasing temperatures. The minimum bubble radius remains almost constant at 1.00 mm for water temperatures below 60 °C, but a rapid increase occurs above 60 °C. Thus, the bubble shrinkage ratio (Rmin/Rmax) in the first cycle at 95 °C is 6 times more than that at 30 °C, corresponding to the weaker collapse. (3) Near the boiling point, the cavitation bubble hardly rebounds after the first cycle and the bubble breaks into multiple micro-bubbles which continue to oscillate instead of collapsing. Meanwhile, a theoretical model accounting for heat transfer, phase change, and compressibility has been used to quantify the vapor mass transfer rate, the bubble internal pressure, and the bubble internal temperature. It is found that the mass transfer rate at 30 °C is significantly higher than at 95 °C. As a result, the bubble boundary collapse velocity is dozens of times lower at 95 °C compared to that at 30 °C. Moreover, the bubble internal pressure and internal temperature significantly decrease with increasing temperature due to the weaker collapse. In general, high temperatures (above 60 °C) significantly reduce the non-equilibrium interphase mass transfer effect of the bubble, and the bubble boundary retraction speed is slower and the collapse is weaker. This investigation is essential for better clarifying and explaining how water temperature affects the single cavitation bubble dynamics.

Introduction

Cavitation is a hydrodynamic phenomenon involving gas–liquid phase transition which produces the generation of cavitation bubbles as the local pressure drops below the saturated vapor pressure [[1], [2], [3]]. Cavitation is widely observed in various fields such as hydraulic machinery [4,5], ocean engineering [6,7], and underwater explosions [7,8]. The creation of cavitation bubbles causes oscillation and collapse, which releases a considerable amount of energy [9]. This collapse produces intense shock waves along with high-speed micro-jets [10,11]. Hence, the collapse of cavitation bubbles causes structural noise [12], vibration [13,14], and damage [15] near various boundaries [16,17]. In recent decades, fluid machinery with high-temperature fluid as the main medium has been widely used in the chemical industry [18], nuclear energy [19], and solar energy [20]. Cavitation severely restricts the applications of high-temperature fluids in engineering. Therefore, clarifying the cavitation bubble dynamics at different temperatures, especially high temperature is extremely important for revealing the physical mechanisms of thermal cavitating flows.

For decades, experimental investigations have been conducted to explore the cavitation phenomenon induced by cavitation bubble and its mechanisms [21,22]. Florschuetz and Chao [23] introduced a dimensionless parameter Beff to study the factors affecting the bubble collapse. Beff evaluates the relative contributions of liquid inertia and thermal effects during the collapse stage. In addition, they found that the saturated vapor pressure significantly affects the bubble dynamics. Plesset [24] investigated the cavitation damage of various materials in water at different temperatures. It was found that the maximum rate of cavitation damage occurs in the temperature range of 40 °C to 50 °C. Ahmed [25] explored the impact pressure and cavitation erosion under different water temperatures. It was observed that temperature and radial distance strongly influence the impact pressure. Specifically, lower temperatures resulted in higher pressure, particularly in the central region. Hattori et al. [26] investigated the effect of the water temperature on cavitation erosion at different cavitation numbers using a cavitation injection device. They proposed a relative temperature based on ambient pressure to express the effect of temperature on cavitation erosion rate in a uniform way. The researchers found that the erosion rate initially increased with increasing relative temperature, peaked at approximately 45 °C, and then gradually decreased. Dular [27] conducted experiments using hydrodynamic cavitation to study cavitation erosion in water at temperatures ranging from 30 to 100 °C. They found out that the aggressiveness of cavitation is significantly influenced by water temperature, with maximum aggressiveness observed at 60 °C. Okawa et al. [28] and Zhang et al. [29] investigated the effect of temperature on the velocity profile and rise characteristics of the cavitation bubble. They observed that the lower the temperature, the more time to reach a constant velocity. Barbaglia and Bonetto [30] experimentally researched the dynamics of single cavitation bubble at different water temperatures and purity values. They indicated that for the nanopure water, the time of collapse increased with decreasing temperatures. Liu et al. [31,32] performed the oscillations of the cavitation bubble over multiple cycles at different temperatures. Their results showed that the bubble volume and the oscillation time both increase with temperature. Dular and Coutier [33] investigated the thermal effects during the growth and collapse stage of the bubble, focusing on the temperature gradient in the liquid surrounding the bubble. The experimental results show a 3 K temperature decrease during the growth stage and a 4 K temperature increase during the collapse stage. Zhang et al. [34] investigated the bubble dynamics under different conditions at various temperatures, including in the free field and near the rigid wall. The presented results showed that the bubble radius decreases as the temperature increases. However, both the bubble diameter and bubble wall velocity increase as the ambient temperature rises near the solid boundary, due to the decrease in liquid viscosity. Phan et al. [35] experimentally and numerically investigated the impacts of thermal effects on bubble dynamics in the free field. They used the laser method to induce the cavitation bubble in water at temperatures ranging from 20 to 80 ℃. The findings revealed that the intensity of bubble collapse decreased with increasing water temperature.

Due to the limitations of experimental techniques, experimental investigations could not reveal the bubble dynamics in depth, thus, theoretical analysis has been the focus of previous studies. As early as 1917, Rayleigh [36] established a dynamic equation for the spherical bubble in the free field. However, the equation neglected many key factors, including the surface tension and compressibility of the liquid, the interphase heat transfer, and mass transfer. Plesset [37] proposed the Rayleigh-Plesset equation by considering the surface tension based on the Rayleigh equation. The Rayleigh-Plesset equation is the basis of the subsequent theory of single cavitation bubble and is crucial for describing bubble oscillation and studying the bubble dynamics. Keller et al. [38,39] theoretically analyzed the dynamics of cavitation bubbles in compressible fluids and proposed the Keller model derived from the wave equation and the incompressible Bernoulli equation. Fujikawa and Akamatsu [40] proposed a new theoretical equation that considers the effects of liquid compressibility, non-equilibrium condensation of the vapor, heat transfer and phase interface temperature discontinuities. The results show that the evaporation and condensation have strongly influenced the bubble dynamics. Yasui [41] constructed single-bubble sonoluminescence model by taking into account the effect of the kinetic energy of the bubble. The results showed that the kinetic energy of the bubble is changed to heat at the end of the violent collapse of the bubble. Meanwhile, almost all the water vapor molecules undergo chemical reactions during the violent collapse. Recently, Zhong et al. [42] refined the mathematical formulation proposed by Fujikawa [40] using a new approximation of the bubble surface temperature gradient. They found that non-condensable air in the bubble reduces the collapse strength of the bubble, thus reducing the maximum pressure and temperature. Zhang et al. [43] proposed the initial condition and the equation of state (EOS) which are applicable for calculating the bubble dynamics of the different explosives underwater explosions, with reference to the Bubble Dynamics equations established by Prosperetti and Lezzi [44]. Zhang et al. [45] established a unified theory of cavitation bubble dynamics by formally unifying several classical bubble equations such as the Rayleigh–Plesset equation, the Gilmore equation, and the Keller–Miksis equation. This theory is available to predict the cavitation bubble dynamics for a wide range of conditions. Numerical methods have also been widely used in recent years to explore the thermal dynamics of single cavitation bubble. Yu et al. [46] used an integrated modified numerical model to investigate the thermodynamic effects of single cavitation bubble near the rigid boundary. The result showed that heat exchange is suppressed as the initial standoff distance increases. H. Peng et al. [47] and C. Peng et al. [48] used the double distribution function (DDF) thermal lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) to simulate the collapse process of cavitation bubble near the rigid boundary, which is a reliable method for studying the thermal effects of cavitation bubble. Toledo et al. [49] developed a generalized cavitation model (gPCM) by coupling the simplified Rayleigh-Plesset equation (inertial regime) and the Plesset-Zwick equation (thermal regime), which unifies the dynamics of cavitation across inertial, intermediate, and thermal regimes. This model shows better results in capturing the cavitation features and thermal effects of multiple bubble pulsations.

Although several studies on cavitation phenomena and cavitation damage in water at different temperatures have been carried out in the past few years, the dynamics of single cavitation bubble have rarely been analyzed at the microscopic scale to reveal the effect of temperature on the cavitation phenomenon. Besides, there are few experimental studies investigating the bubble dynamics in water near the boiling point. This is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of the physical mechanisms of bubble dynamics at different water temperatures and to provide a theoretical basis for anti-cavitation design of high-temperature hydraulic equipment. Hence, in this paper, the experimental platform capable of operating at high water temperatures is established to perform a series of experiments on the evolution of the single cavitation bubble at different temperatures to investigate the bubble dynamic behaviors, especially near the boiling point. Meanwhile, we further explore the dynamics in detail using the theoretical model which considers the compressibility, the heat transfer, and phase change. The effects of temperature on the single cavitation bubble dynamics are experimentally and theoretically investigated and the physical mechanisms are clarified.

Experimental setup

Experimental system operating over a wide temperature range

Due to the high temperature involved in the experimental process, traditional cavitation bubble experimental platforms are inadequate for these conditions. Additionally, it is essential to effectively minimize heat leakage in the experimental system to ensure the stability of temperature during multiple repeated experiments. Therefore, an experimental system capable of operating under high-temperature conditions has been designed. Fig. 1 shows the experimental system schematically, which mainly consists of a test platform, a bubble generator, a high-speed photography system, and a temperature controller. The test platform is the same as that used by Chen et al. [50] in cryogenic cavitation bubble experiments. The test platform is fully enclosed and consists of two coaxial cylindrical experimental tanks, the outer tank with a diameter of 460 mm and the inner tank with a diameter of 325 mm, with a vacuum insulation chamber between them. The inner tank height is approximately 450 mm. During the experiment, the distance between the bubble generation position and the free liquid surface exceeds 280 mm, and the distance between the bubble and the inner tank body exceeds 150 mm, both of which are much greater than the bubble radius (no more than 8 mm). Therefore, the bubble evolution occurs in a completely free field. On this basis, to achieve control and adjustment of water temperature, a temperature controller, which consists of a controller and a heating rod, is incorporated into the system. The heating rod is fixed to the interface on the top of the experimental platform, and the required temperature value is set in the controller. In this paper, spring water was used as the experimental medium. The water temperature range T∞ was from 30 to 95 ℃ in the experiment. The water temperature is measured using Pt100 thermometers (accuracy ±0.21 ℃ at 30 ℃). The ambient pressure inside the test tank is measured using an absolute pressure sensor. In this study, the ambient pressure p∞ is 1 atm. It should be noted that before the experiment, the spring water was first heated to the boiling point and then allowed to cool down. This step serves to mitigate the potential influence of undissolved gases present in the water on the experimental outcomes to a certain degree.Fig. 1Experimental setup for single cavitation bubble under different water temperatures. Bubble generator

The bubble generator is composed of a capacitor, a charge/discharge circuit, and a controller. In the experiment, the bubble was induced by Joule heating generated at the connection point of the electrodes upon discharging a capacitor charged to 120 V. The connect point of the electrodes consisted of two silver wires with a diameter of 0.3 mm in contact. During the discharge process, the high voltage caused localized melting of the silver wires at the connection point. This phenomenon generated extremely high temperatures and energy densities, resulting in a rapidly expanding bubble called spark-induced bubble. This approach was initially introduced by Turangan et al. [51]. The method for generating bubble by high voltage discharge utilized in this study is similar to that previously employed by Zhang et al. [52]. According to previous theory [52], the bubble contains not only vapor but also potentially includes electrolysis byproducts of water and evaporated silver. Since the duration of the experiment is very short, the thermometer reading at the time of discharge of the bubble generator is recorded as the water temperature in each experiment. Meanwhile, the temperature controller maintains the water within ±0.2 °C of the target value, e.g., 29.8 ℃–30.2 ℃ are regarded as 30 ℃.

Before the experiment, the calibration scale is placed in the focus plane of the camera to provide a reference for subsequent image dimensional measurements. The calibration scale is 10 mm, corresponding to 154 pixels. In the process of measuring the area, the bubble area is calculated by extracting the pixels occupied by the bubble image. The edges of the bubble image are deformed with a width of 1–2 pixels. Therefore, the measurement error of the bubble radius is 0.13 mm or less. This error is small enough compared to the bubble radius (approximately 5 mm at least). Five repeated experiments are performed for each experiment condition, and it is found that the maximum radius of each experiment is different, which may be caused by the instability of the discharge of the bubble generator, but fortunately, the deviation in the maximum bubble radius is approximately 5 % after five repeated experiments.

High-speed photography system

The temporal evolution of the single bubble is captured by the high-speed photography system which consists of an LED light, a frosted glass sheet, and a high-speed camera (Phantom V1612). The high-speed camera operates at a sampling frequency of 40,000 fps with an exposure time of 10 μs to ensure well-defined bubble boundaries. The maximum error in the time measurement is equal to the frame interval, approximately 0.025 ms, which is small enough compared with the period of bubble oscillation (typically not less than 3 ms), thus, the error can be ignored. The LED light and the frosted glass sheet are positioned on the other side of the experimental platform, opposite to the high-speed camera. This backlight illumination configuration enables to capture of bubble images clearly.

Results and discussion

Effect of temperature on the typical behavior of single cavitation bubble

In this study, the single cavitation bubble in water from 30 °C to 95 °C was experimentally investigated. The experimental results show that the overall evolution process consists of three stages: expansion, shrinkage, and oscillation. The expansion and shrinkage stages are the first cycle of oscillation of the cavitation bubble. The first cycle is also the longest duration and the largest bubble volume oscillation in the entire bubble evolution. In this section, we discuss the effect of the water temperature on the overall evolution according to the experimental results.

Fig. 3 illustrates the maximum and minimum bubble radius during the first cycle at different temperatures. The black and red solid points are the maximum radius and minimum radius, respectively. The results suggest that the maximum radius increases as the water temperature rises, which is highly consistent with previous experimental results [30,31]. For minimum bubble radius, when the water temperature is below 60 °C, it remains nearly constant at around 1.00 mm, and when the water temperature is above 60 °C, the minimum bubble radius rises sharply. This phenomenon indicates that high temperatures inhibit the shrinkage of the cavitation bubble. This is because as the water temperature changes, it causes significant alterations in the properties of water, especially saturated vapor pressure and density. This effect is particularly noticeable close to the boiling point [35], as shown in Table 1. The transformation of these properties at different temperatures is significant, especially near the boiling point. During this transition process, physical properties become increasingly sensitive as the temperature rises. This phenomenon is known as the thermal effect of thermos-fluids(such as cryogenic fluids and high temperature water) [55]. Thus, as the temperature rises, both the heightened saturated vapor pressure and the reduced density result in a larger overall bubble size at the same discharge voltage. The first cycle time and the shrinkage ratio in the first cycle (Rmin/Rmax) are shown in Fig. 4. The first cycle time increases with an increase in water temperatures. On the contrary, the shrinkage ratio remains essentially unchanged below 60 °C. At high water temperatures, it increases rapidly. The shrinkage ratio at 95°C is more than 6 times that at 30 °C. A higher shrinkage ratio means that the bubble is less compressed by the surrounding liquid. As shown in Fig. 5 and Fig. 6, the bubble dynamics throughout evolution exhibit significant variations in response to temperature changes, demonstrating temperature-dependent characteristics in expansion, shrinkage, and oscillation stages. The main differences are as follows: (1) During the expansion stage, both the maximum bubble volume and the duration of expansion increase significantly with increasing temperature. (2) Throughout the expansion stage, bubbles maintain a nearly perfect spherical morphology across all temperature ranges. However, during the collapse stage, while low-temperature conditions preserve the quasi-spherical shape, high-temperature conditions induce significant deformation, characterized by interface instabilities and asymmetric collapse patterns. (3) At temperatures close to the boiling point, many micro-bubbles separate at the edges of the bubble, and these micro-bubbles promote deformation and breakup of the main body of the bubble. (4) The amplitude of the bubble rebound during the oscillation stage decreases with increasing temperature. The ratio of maximum rebound radius to minimum collapse radius decreases from 2.42 at 30 °C to 1.99 at 60 °C, and further diminishes to 1.07 at 85 °C. (5) The bubble collapse time also increases with increasing temperature, and high temperature prolongs the collapse of single cavitation bubble.

The Rayleigh collapse time is used to explain the significant increase in the evolution time of the bubble with increasing temperature. The Rayleigh shrinkage time is defined as [36]:(7)ts=0.915Rmaxρ∞p∞-pvwhere Rmax is the maximum bubble radius, pv and p∞ are the bubble internal pressure and the water pressure, respectively. The internal temperature of the bubble at maximum volume is reduced to a temperature similar to that of the surrounding water due to thermal diffusion. As a result, the bubble internal pressure can be considered as the saturated vapor pressure corresponding to the surrounding water temperature [40]. With the increasing water temperature, the corresponding saturated vapor pressure also increases while the ambient pressure remains constant in the experiment, resulting in a decreasing pressure difference (p∞-pv). Therefore, the shrinkage time of the cavitation bubble at high water temperature is larger than that at normal water temperature.

In summary, bubble dynamics change significantly at high temperatures compared to low temperatures, particularly when the water temperature exceeds 60 °C. To systematically analyze these differences, the following study will focus on a comparative examination of bubble behavior at three strategically selected temperature points: T∞=30 °C, T∞=60 °C, andT∞ = 95 °C.

Comparative analysis of bubble dynamics at characteristic temperatures

Fig. 7 and Fig. 8 show the typical bubble shapes atT∞ = 30 °C andT∞ = 60 ℃, respectively. As shown in Fig. 7, at the beginning of the expansion stage, the high-voltage discharge from the bubble generator generates high-density energy. This leads to the internal pressure of the bubble being significantly greater than that of the surrounding liquid, which causes the bubble to expand rapidly (frames 1–5). At t1 = 0.675 ms (frame 5), the bubble reaches its maximum volume with a radius of Rmax = 5.70 mm. Subsequently, the bubble starts shrinking until t2 = 1.275 ms because the bubble internal pressure has dropped below the pressure of the surrounding water (frames 6–10). During the shrinkage stage, the gases within the bubble undergo violent compression by the surrounding liquid. After the bubble reaches its minimum radius, Rmin = 1.01 mm, it starts to oscillate, undergoing multiple expansions and shrinks (frames 11–15). It should be noted that the maximum bubble radius decreases gradually with each oscillation cycle, and the duration of each oscillation cycle is reduced because the bubble evolution is significantly affected by the phase transition processes, especially the condensation process [56,57]. The bubble radius reduction rates for the second, third, and fourth oscillation cycles are approximately 57.3 %, 67.4 %, and 73.2 %, respectively. For the bubble atT∞ = 60 ℃, the bubble dynamics during both expansion and shrinkage stages are similar to those at 30 °C, albeit with larger overall dimensions. Notably, the cavitation bubbles maintain a highly spherical morphology at both temperatures. The typical bubble shapes atT∞ = 95 °C are shown in Fig. 9. The bubble expansion persisted until t1 = 0.675 ms (frames 1–5), with surface roughening initiating in the late expansion stage (frames 4–5). According to the above experimental results and analysis, the effect of temperature on bubble dynamics is mainly reflected in the shrinkage stage and the oscillation stage. Meanwhile, it should be noted that the initial conditions of the oscillation stage are fundamentally determined by the shrinkage stage. Therefore, the main reason for the significant difference in bubble dynamics at different temperatures is the difference in the shrinkage stage. The subsequent study focuses on the shrinkage stage to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

It is concluded that the thermal effect is negligible at low temperatures but becomes pronounced and gradually dominates the bubble dynamics as the temperature rises. Thus, bubble dynamics are primarily influenced by liquid inertia at temperatures between 30 °C and 60 °C, while thermal effects dominate at 80 °C to 95 °C. The transitional states in intermediate temperatures are controlled by a combination of both factors. Thus, at high temperatures, the enhanced thermal effects prolong the bubble collapse time and inhibit the energy release within the bubble [47,49]. Further discussion of bubble dynamics through theoretical modeling

In this section, the theoretical model is utilized to analyze the bubble dynamics and the influencing factors during the shrinkage stage. Fig. 15 presents the bubble radius evolution during the shrinkage stage between the experimental results and predicted results at different temperatures. The solid dots are the experiment results and the dashed line is the radius predicted by Eqs. (2), (3), (4). It can be seen from Fig. 15 that the value of the evaporation/condensation coefficient αM significantly affects the predicted results. In particular, forαM = 0, the entire process is treated as an adiabatic process, with no non-equilibrium phase transitions and energy loss. With the increasing value of αM, the non-equilibrium phase transition process is enhanced, leading to a reduction in the rebound radius and a shortening of the shrinkage time. Therefore, altering the values of αM at different temperatures enabled the predicted results to be in better agreement with the experimental data. Based on the comparison between the experimental results and the predicted results of the theoretical model, it can be found that the evaporation/condensation coefficient αM is 0.065 at T∞=30 °C (black dashed line in Fig. 15 (a)), while it is 0.045 and 0.028 at 60 °C and 95 °C, respectively. A decrease in the evaporation/condensation coefficient represents a weakening of the mass transfer effect, indicating that the water vapor in the bubble condenses into water at a smaller percentage and a slower rate. This reduction suggests that non-equilibrium mass transfer between the vapor and liquid phases is suppressed at high temperatures, which directly weakens the driving force for bubble shrinkage.Fig. 15Comparison of the predicted dimensionless radius with experimental results.

Comparison of the predicted dimensionless radius with experimental results.

Further, to quantify the mass transfer rate of the bubble, Eq. (5) was solved. Fig. 16 shows the comparison of the mass transfer rate at different temperatures. As shown in Fig. 16, the mass transfer rate increases gradually with bubble shrinkage and then decreases with bubble rebound, reaching a maximum value around the bubble minimum radius. For the bubble at 30 °C, the maximum mass transfer rate reaches 330 Kg/(m3·s), and the peak of the rate occurs within a short period. When the water temperature is 95 ℃, as illustrated in Fig. 16 (c), the maximum mass transfer rate is only 1.7 Kg/(m3·s), which is two orders of magnitude different from that at 30 °C. It can be concluded that non-equilibrium phase change effects can have a significant impact on the shrinkage and rebound of the cavitation bubble, the decrease in αMindicates a weakening of the non-equilibrium nature of the phase change process at high temperatures, and the rate of mass transfer between the vapour and the liquid slows down. This is also related to the stronger thermal effect at high temperatures. The enhanced thermal effect weakens the temperature gradient between the bubble and the surrounding liquid, and the thermal effect inhibits heat transfer between the gas and liquid phases. Meanwhile, the condensation of the bubble during shrinkage also releases a large amount of latent heat, further raising the temperature of the surrounding liquid. These factors inhibit heat transfer between the gas and liquid phases, thereby weakening mass transfer and reducing the mass transfer rate. It is worth noting that although the mass transfer rate is much greater at low temperatures than at high temperatures, the total amount of mass transferred is greater at high temperatures over the entire evolutionary cycle, which correlates with the larger bubble size at higher temperatures.Fig. 16The bubble radius evolution and predicted mass transfer rate.

The black dashed line and the red solid line are the bubble radius and the boundary velocity, respectively. The velocity vectors point in the positive direction towards the outer normal of the bubble edge. As shown in Fig. 17 (a), at T∞=30 °C, the boundary velocity increases sharply towards the end of the shrinkage stage and reaches 180 m/s when the bubble shrinks to its minimum. Then, as the bubble rebounds, the velocity grows rapidly to a positive value. The boundary velocity at 60 ℃ shows the same trend as 30 °C, but the value significantly decreases, only 20 % of that at 30 °C. Meanwhile, the velocity does not grow to a positive value after entering the oscillation stage, which corresponds to the bubble not rebounding at 95 ℃. From the above analysis, we can find that: First, according to the Rayleigh shrinkage time (Eq. (7), the shrinkage time is proportional to ρ∞/p∞-pv. A sharp decrease in p∞-pv leads to a significant increase in the shrinkage time and minimum bubble radius at high temperatures. Second, as the temperature increases, the bubble dynamics gradually change from being controlled by liquid inertia at low temperatures to being controlled by thermal effects at high temperatures. The enhancement of thermal effects also significantly prolongs the bubble collapse time and energy release [47,49]. Third, the non-equilibrium phase transition effect is substantially higher at low temperatures than at high temperatures. The phase transition enhances the boundary velocity of the bubble and promotes its shrinkage and rebound. Fourth, the maximum boundary velocity of the bubble at high temperatures is only 1.2 % of that at low temperatures, which means that the bubble shrinks more slowly and gently. Therefore, it can be concluded that high temperature and thermal effects reduce the bubble shrinkage intensity.

To further quantitatively analyze the bubble shrinkage intensity at different temperatures, Eqs. (3), (4) are solved to get the bubble internal pressure and temperature. Fig. 18 and Fig. 19 illustrate the predicted bubble internal pressure and internal temperature, respectively. At 30 ℃, the initial pressure difference (p∞-pv) is large, leading to a high mass transfer rate and substantial boundary velocity. As a result, the bubble shrinkage rate is considerable. This causes the bubble internal gas to be compressed violently, leading to a rapid rise in both the bubble internal pressure and internal temperature. The highest peak internal pressure and temperature are observed when the bubble reaches its minimum radius. In particular, the maximum internal pressure reaches 21.15 MPa, corresponding to a maximum internal temperature of 5395 K, which agrees well with the conclusions of previous research [38,48]. With the increase of ambient temperature and the decrease of shrinkage strength, the pressure and temperature inside the bubble decay rapidly. The internal pressure is 2.8 MPa and the internal temperature is 1900 K at 60 ℃. Furthermore, as the surrounding temperature nears the boiling point, the pressure difference becomes significantly lower, resulting in a smaller mass transfer rate. Therefore, the bubble internal pressure and internal temperature are only 0.13 MPa and 420 K. Based on the experimental and theoretical analysis as mentioned above, it is concluded that bubble dynamics, particularly during the shrinkage stage, are affected by a variety of factors, including phase change, liquid inertia, thermal effects, and so on. High mass transfer rates and shrinkage boundary velocity lead to violent shrinkage and collapse of the bubble. For the bubble at high temperature, both the pressure difference and the rate of mass transfer are small, thus, the shrinkage intensity is much weaker. As a result, the bubble internal pressure and internal temperature at high temperatures are only 1 % of those at normal temperature. Meanwhile, the thermal effects gradually dominate the bubble dynamics with increasing temperature. The strengthening of the thermal effect prolongs the evolution time of the cavitation bubble, and the collapse intensity behaves less violently.

Conclusions

In this paper, the evolution and dynamics of single cavitation bubble at different temperatures are investigated. The experiments utilize the spark-induced method to create a single cavitation bubble and the evolution of the bubble is recorded in detail using a high-speed photography system, while the temperature controller is used to ensure the water temperature is stable. In addition, a theoretical model is used to further investigate the experimental results. The bubble dynamics are discussed based on the experimental and predicted results. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)The maximum radius and the minimum radius of the bubble both increase with an increase in water temperature. The minimum bubble radius grows faster, thus, the bubble shrinkage ratio in the first cycle at 95°C is more than 6 times that at 30 °C. Meanwhile, compared to the bubble at normal water temperature, the evolution time and oscillation cycles of the bubble at high water temperature are significantly larger.(2)The temperature makes a significant difference in the bubble evolution process, especially at water temperatures above 60 °C, where the intensity of the bubble rebound and oscillation decreases significantly. When the temperature is close to the boiling point, the bubble does not rebound during the oscillation phase and does not collapse completely, but rather the main body splits into several micro-bubbles that continue to oscillate.(3)The predicted results indicate that the evaporation/condensation coefficient αM decreases with temperature increases and the compressibility of the bubble is weaker at high temperature. Therefore, the mass transfer rate at normal temperature is much larger than that at high temperature, which, combined with the greater pressure difference between the inside and outside of the bubble, leads to greater bubble boundary velocity. As a result, the bubble internal pressure and temperature at high temperature are much lower than those at normal temperature.(4)During the expansion stage, the bubble dynamics at all water temperatures are governed by liquid inertia. With an increase in water temperatures, the thermal effects gradually dominate the bubble dynamics. In general, combining predicted results with experimental results, it is concluded that high temperature and thermal effects reduce the bubble shrinkage and collapse intensity.
