Lipid metabolism profiles as predictors of gestational diabetes mellitus risk: A retrospective study
Authors: Xiaoyan Xiu, Huale Zhang, Yingying Lin, Jianying Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most common metabolic disorders in pregnancy and is associated with significant maternal and fetal risks. The study aims to investigate lipid metabolism profiles associated with GDM to identify independent risk factors.MethodsClinical data from 17,452 women with singleton pregnancies who received regular prenatal care and nutritional assessment at Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital between December 2011 and December 2019 were analyzed. Participants were divided into GDM and control groups based on diagnosis. Gestational age was confirmed by first‐trimester ultrasound, and lipid profiles were measured in early (10–13 weeks) and late pregnancy (28–32 weeks). GDM was diagnosed using either a two‐step approach (2011–2014) or the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) one‐step 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test. Maternal characteristics, pregnancy outcomes, and lipid indicators—including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), LDL‐C, HDL‐C, and apolipoproteins (Apo‐A1, Apo‐B)—were extracted. Feature selection was performed using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm.ResultsAge, TG/HDL‐C ratio, TG, pre‐pregnancy BMI, and HDL‐C were identified as independent risk factors for GDM. Levels of ALT, HGB, Apo‐B, TC, TG, and TG/HDL‐C ratio were significantly higher in the GDM group, while HDL‐C was lower (P < 0.05). Threshold analysis indicated that first‐trimester HDL‐C and TG/HDL‐C ratio critical cut‐off points were 2.37 and 1.33, respectively.ConclusionAbnormal lipid metabolism is a hallmark of GDM and may serve as a predictive biomarker. Assessment of TG/HDL‐C ratio and HDL‐C in early pregnancy could improve risk stratification and facilitate early interventions.Keywords: gestational diabetes mellitus, lipid metabolism

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a common metabolic disorder in pregnancy, poses a significant risk to maternal and fetal health, as it raises the risks of preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, macrosomia, and long‐term maternal and neonatal metabolic dysfunction 1 , 2 , 3 . GDM imposes a significant economic burden on the health system, underscoring the need to improve early detection and management 4 .

Current GDM tests primarily include the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), which has been shown to have limitations in sensitivity and specificity for predicting individual risk 5 , 6 , 7 . Recently, there has been growing evidence of the potential value of dysregulated lipid metabolism as a biomarker of GDM. Alterations in triglycerides (TG), cholesterol profiles, and apolipoproteins were shown to serve as early indicators of GDM and predict adverse outcomes 8 , 9 , 10 . However, systematic analyses of lipid metabolism across gestational stages are lacking, and the results on individual lipid markers are inconsistent 11 , 12 , 13 . Identifying lipid biomarkers of GDM can help improve risk assessment, inform targeted responses, and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes 14 , 15 , 16 . This large retrospective cohort study aims to use machine learning techniques like the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and the Boruta algorithm to characterize trimester‐specific metabolic profiles linked to the risk of GDM. The results may elucidate the role of lipid dysregulation in the pathophysiology of GDM.

METHODS

Study population and design

This retrospective cohort study included clinical records of 17,452 women with singleton pregnancies who received regular prenatal care and underwent nutritional assessment at Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital between December 2011 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants with accurate gestational dating confirmed by first‐trimester ultrasound; (2) completed lipid profiling during early (10–13 (+6) weeks) and late pregnancy (28–32 weeks); (3) delivered at the same hospital; (4) complete outcome data. Exclusion criteria: (1) Pregestational diabetes; (2) Preexisting cardiovascular, immune, or hematologic disorders; (3) fetal anomalies; (4) stillbirth; (5) missing key data such as demographic information, clinical records, or laboratory results.

Diagnostic criteria

The data for this study were obtained from the hospital's electronic medical record system. All cases of GDM were strictly diagnosed in accordance with clinical guidelines 12 . The two‐step methods were used for women treated from 2011 to 2014. The approach included a 50‐g glucose challenge test (GCT) as the initial screening, and a 100‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) for those with positive results. The thresholds for fasting/1/2/3 h were 5.3/10.0/8.6/7.8 mmol/L, respectively. If two or more thresholds were exceeded, a diagnosis of GDM was made. The International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) one‐step method was used for women treated from 2015 to 2019. The method included 75 g OGTT with thresholds for fasting/1/2 h at 5.1/10.0/8.5 mmol/L, respectively. If anyone threshold was exceeded, a diagnosis of GDM was made. The 2014 data were corrected for the time window (the old standard used January to June). Additionally, the electronic medical record system underwent quality control through logical verification and manual review, excluding cases of pre‐pregnancy diabetes, data inconsistencies, and cases with missing key indicators to ensure the traceability of diagnoses.

Data collection and variables

The electronic medical records provided comprehensive data, which included maternal characteristics such as age, parity, pre‐pregnancy body mass index (Pre‐BMI), and prior preterm birth; pregnancy outcomes including GDM, preterm birth, and hypertensive disorders; and lipid profiles comprising total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), LDL‐C, HDL‐C, and apolipoproteins (Apo‐A1 and Apo‐B).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables (mean ± SD or median [IQR]) were compared using t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U‐tests; categorical variables (n [%]) with χ2 or Fisher's exact tests. LASSO Regression was done via glmnet in R (v4.3.1) with 10‐fold cross‐validation to identify predictors of GDM. Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation (mice package); outliers were winsorized. A random forest‐based approach (Boruta package) compared feature importance against shadow attributes to confirm robust predictors. For key lipid markers (HDL‐C, TG/HDL‐C ratio), generalized additive models (GAMs) explored nonlinear associations with GDM. Thresholds were identified using segmented regression, and piecewise logistic regression assessed risk gradients above and below cutpoints. Multivariable logistic regression was adjusted for age, BMI, and parity. Bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) was validated model stability. All analyses were two‐tailed. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. In cases of non‐linear data, the results were reported as the first‐order derivative of log (odds ratio [OR]), with 95% confidence interval (CI).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

After applying inclusion/exclusion criteria, a total of 17,452 participants, treated between December 2011 and December 2019, were enrolled. Of them, 3,153 (18.07%) pregnant women who were diagnosed with GDM comprised the GDM group, and 14,299 (81.93%) without GDM were assigned to the control group. The overall GDM prevalence was 18.07% (3,153/17,452). When stratified by diagnostic criteria period, the prevalence was 19.02% (830/4,363) during 2011–2014 (two‐step approach) and 17.75% (2,323/13,089) during 2015–2019 (IADPSG one‐step criteria). The difference in GDM prevalence between the two periods was not statistically significant (P = 0.061), indicating that the transition in diagnostic criteria did not result in a substantial systematic shift in GDM detection rates in our study population (Table S1). As shown in Table 1, women with GDM were older (median age: 31.0 vs. 28.0 years), had a higher pre‐pregnancy BMI (21.4 vs. 20.3 kg/m2), and higher parity (51.9% vs. 42.5% primipara) compared to non‐GDM counterparts. As shown in Tables 2 and 3, clinically, GDM was associated with significantly higher rates of cesarean delivery (42.1% vs. 31.1%) and low birth weight (4.6% vs. 3.7%). It was also associated with higher first‐trimester ALT (12.9 vs. 11.9 U/L) and hemoglobin levels (121.0 vs. 120.0 g/L). Additionally, first‐trimester lipid profiles showed a higher TG/HDL‐C ratio in the GDM group, whereas HDL‐C levels were slightly lower than in the control group (both P < 0.05).

Feature selection for GDM prediction

A total of 17 feature variables were included in the LASSO analysis of potential risk indicators for GDM in the first trimester. These variables included gravidity, parity, history of preterm birth, height, weight, pre‐pregnancy BMI, age, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin (ALB), white blood cell count (WBC), apolipoprotein B (Apo‐B), apolipoprotein A1 (APO‐A1), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), TG/HDL‐C ratio, and platelet count (PLT). All included variables were within the minimum ranges (Figure 2). Through further analysis using the 1‐se criterion, seven variables, including age, TG/HDL‐C ratio, TG, pre‐pregnancy BMI, TC, Apo‐B, and HDL‐C, were screened as independent risk factors for GDM prediction (Figure 3).

Multivariable logistic regression

The results of the multivariate logistic regression demonstrated that, after adjusting for potential confounders (including age, pre‐pregnancy BMI, parity, and other covariates), the TG/HDL‐C ratio remained strongly and statistically significantly associated with the risk of GDM. In the minimally adjusted model (Model A), each unit increase in TG/HDL‐C was associated with a 24% higher risk of GDM (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.18–1.31). This association remained virtually unchanged in the fully adjusted model (Model C: OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.17–1.30; P < 0.001), demonstrating the reliability of this metabolic marker independent of major confounding factors (Table 6).

Incremental predictive value of lipid markers

To quantify the incremental predictive contribution of the TG/HDL‐C ratio and HDL‐C, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed (Figure 7). The base clinical model, incorporating age, pre‐pregnancy BMI, and parity, yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.65–0.67). The extended model, which additionally included first‐trimester TG/HDL‐C ratio and HDL‐C, showed a modest improvement with an AUC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.66–0.68). Although the increment in discrimination was small, this finding is consistent with the established understanding that age and pre‐pregnancy BMI are dominant predictors of GDM and that a single or limited set of biochemical markers typically yields small marginal improvements in AUC when added to a strong base model. Importantly, the TG/HDL‑C ratio and HDL‑C are routinely available, inexpensive, and can be measured earlier in pregnancy than traditional glycemic screening. Their independent associations with GDM risk, as demonstrated in fully adjusted logistic regression and threshold analyses, support their potential utility in early risk stratification. This utility provides additional predictive value beyond the information already captured by demographic and anthropometric factors.

Early pregnancy HDL‐C and TG/HDL‐C ratio thresholds

Generalized additive models revealed a nonlinear relationship between early‐pregnancy high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and GDM risk (Figure 8). After adjusting for age, HDL‐C demonstrated a protective effect up to 2.7 mmol/L, with each unit increase associated with a 20.9% lower risk of GDM (OR = 0.791, 95% CI: 0.690–0.906; P = 0.001). Beyond this threshold, the association reversed, indicating an increased risk (OR = 1.662, 95% CI: 0.874–3.160; P = 0.121). There was a significant risk discontinuity at the cutoff point (OR = 2.298 for ≥2.7 vs. < 2.7 mmol/L; P = 0.022). Therefore, the log‐likelihood ratio test confirmed the superior fit of the segmented model (P < 0.01), supporting the presence of a threshold effect (Table 7).

The TG/HDL‐C ratio exhibited a biphasic association with GDM (Figure 9). Below 1.33, each unit increase conferred 74.4% higher risks (OR = 1.744, 95% CI: 1.486–2.047, P < 0.001) of GDM. Sensitivity analysis: 2015–2019 cohort (uniform IADPSG criteria)

To evaluate whether the diagnostic criteria transition during 2014 influenced the observed associations, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 2015–2019 cohort (n = 13,089), during which the IADPSG one‐step diagnostic criteria were uniformly applied. In this subgroup, generalized additive models and segmented regression confirmed threshold effects that were highly consistent with the full‐sample analysis. Specifically, the protective threshold for first‐trimester HDL‐C was identified at 2.37 mmol/L, identical to the full‐sample finding. The TG/HDL‐C ratio threshold was identified at 1.34 (vs. 1.33 in the full sample), with a nearly identical biphasic risk pattern (Figure 11). Below 1.34, each unit increase was associated with significantly elevated GDM risk (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.44–2.03, P < 0.001), while above this threshold the risk increment attenuated (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.23, P = 0.019). These findings confirm that the transition in diagnostic criteria did not materially affect the observed nonlinear associations between first‐trimester lipid parameters and GDM risk.

DISCUSSION

This large retrospective study of 17,452 pregnancies combined conventional statistics with machine learning and nonlinear modeling to provide a detailed, clinically meaningful interpretation of how lipid metabolism contributes to the pathophysiology of GDM. By combining LASSO regression and Boruta feature selection, the study identified age, TG/HDL‐C ratio, triglycerides, pre‐pregnancy BMI, and HDL‐C as independent risk factors for GDM. The results support the potential value of dysregulated lipid profiles during pregnancy as predictive biomarkers of GDM.

GDM is increasingly recognized as a significant public health concern, affecting approximately 2%–20% of pregnant women worldwide 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 . GDM is characterized by glucose intolerance that occurs during pregnancy and can lead to serious maternal and neonatal complications 18 , 19 , 20 . The rising prevalence of GDM has prompted the need for more effective screening and management strategies, particularly as traditional methods, such as oral glucose tolerance tests, may not adequately identify all at‐risk individuals 13 .

One of the key findings of this study is that abnormalities in lipid metabolism were already evident in the first trimester, well before the clinical diagnosis of GDM. Women who later developed GDM had significantly higher TG, TC, and Apo‐B levels; higher TG/HDL‐C ratios; and lower HDL‐C levels. These findings are consistent with earlier observational studies suggesting that dyslipidemia precedes glucose intolerance in pregnancy rather than being a mere consequence of hyperglycemia 21 , 22 , 23 .

Pregnancy is characterized by progressive physiological insulin resistance, particularly in the second and third trimesters 24 . However, lipid abnormalities in early pregnancy suggest underlying metabolic vulnerability: elevated TG levels reflect hepatic overproduction of very low‐density lipoproteins driven by insulin resistance, while reduced HDL‐C levels reflect impaired reverse cholesterol transport and altered lipoprotein remodeling 22 . Together, these changes worsen insulin sensitivity, thereby increasing the risk of GDM development 25 , 26 , 27 .

This study showed that the TG/HDL‐C ratio emerged as the most robust predictor of GDM, remaining strongly associated with GDM risk after extensive adjustment for confounders. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified TG/HDL‐C as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome in non‐pregnant populations 28 , 29 , 30 . Several cohort studies have also reported elevated TG or TG/HDL‐C ratios in women with GDM, but most were limited by smaller sample sizes or single‐time‐point measurements 31 , 32 , 33 .

The study demonstrated a dose–response, nonlinear relationship between the TG/HDL‐C ratio and the risk of GDM and identified a critical threshold at TG/HDL‐C around 1.33. Below this threshold, the risk of GDM increased sharply; above it, the risk of GDM continued to rise but at a slower rate. This suggests a saturation effect, whereby early increases in TG/HDL‐C reflect rapidly worsening insulin resistance, while extremely high values may represent an already established metabolic disturbance. It is plausible that an elevated TG/HDL‐C ratio reflects both excessive triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins and impaired HDL‐mediated lipid clearance. Such lipid imbalance may directly contribute to GDM pathogenesis by exacerbating lipotoxicity in skeletal muscle and pancreatic β‐cells, impairing insulin signaling, and promoting chronic low‐grade inflammation 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 .

This study also revealed a nonlinear association between HDL‐C and GDM risk. At lower concentrations, higher HDL‐C levels were clearly protective, consistent with its roles in enhancing insulin sensitivity, reducing oxidative stress, and modulating inflammation 38 . However, this protective effect diminished beyond a certain threshold. This phenomenon may reflect qualitative differences in HDL particles or altered HDL function in metabolic disorders, underscoring that HDL concentration alone may not fully account for its biological effects in pregnancy.

In the third trimester, GDM in this study was associated with progressive worsening of lipid abnormalities, including higher TG, Apo‐B, and TG/HDL‐C ratios and lower HDL‐C levels. While physiological hyperlipidemia is expected in late pregnancy 39 , the extent of dyslipidemia observed in GDM suggests a pathological amplification of normal gestational metabolic changes. This progressive lipid disturbance may contribute to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes and aligns with reports linking late‐pregnancy dyslipidemia to fetal overgrowth and long‐term metabolic risk 40 , 41 , 42 .

These findings have significant clinical implications. Lipid parameters such as TG and HDL‐C are routinely measured, inexpensive, and available early in pregnancy. Therefore, incorporating lipid‐based markers, such as the TG/HDL‐C ratio, into prenatal risk assessment could improve the timely identification of women at high risk for GDM and complement routine glucose‐based screening methods typically performed later in gestation 9 . This will allow early interventions to significantly improve maternal and neonatal outcomes and reduce the long‐term risk of metabolic disorders in offspring 43 , 44 . Furthermore, identifying specific thresholds further enhances the potential for individualized risk stratification 45 and enables the optimization of guidelines for GDM management. Although the improvement in AUC was modest, the independent and nonlinear associations of these lipid markers with GDM risk, combined with their early availability and low cost, support their integration into first‐trimester risk assessment strategies.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design limits causal inference and does not exclude residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle or genetic factors. This is a single‐center study, which limits generalizability. Lipid functionality and inflammatory mediators were not assessed in this study. Additionally, a transition in diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes mellitus, from a two‐step approach to the IADPSG one‐step criteria, was implemented institutionally during the study period. This change may have been associated with increasing GDM detection rates and may have introduced heterogeneity in disease severity, with later cohorts potentially including milder cases. Such differences could influence the observed associations and effect sizes between lipid parameters and GDM risk. Although cases were classified according to the diagnostic criteria in use at the time, the impact of this transition cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, key established risk factors for gestational diabetes mellitus, including family history of diabetes and history of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), were not available in the dataset and were therefore not included in the multivariable models. This may result in residual confounding and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations. First‐trimester fasting plasma glucose (FPG) data were not systematically captured in the electronic medical record system during the study window; therefore, the potential independent contribution of the TG/HDL‐C ratio beyond early dysglycemia could not be fully isolated, and residual confounding by glucose status remains possible.

Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and explore their underlying mechanisms 46 . Finally, although the GAM model identified a TG/HDL‐C threshold of approximately 1.33 for the overall cohort, we were unable to assess the stability of this cutoff point across different pre‐pregnancy BMI strata (e.g., normal weight, overweight, obese) due to limited sample sizes within certain subgroups, which precluded robust stratum‐specific threshold estimation. Future studies with larger, BMI‐diverse cohorts are warranted to validate whether this threshold is consistent across varying body types and to refine BMI‐specific risk stratification algorithms.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, this study identified abnormal lipid metabolism, particularly an elevated TG/HDL‐C ratio and abnormal HDL‐C levels, as an early and independent predictor of GDM. The identified nonlinear and threshold‐dependent associations provide new insights into GDM pathophysiology and support the integration of lipid metabolism assessment into early‐pregnancy risk stratification.
