Evaluating depression in Indonesian adolescents with epilepsy: Comprehensive validation and reliability assessment of the neurological disorders depression inventory‐epilepsy for youth Indonesian version (NDDI‐E‐Y[ID])
Authors: Setyo Handryastuti, Tjhin Wiguna, Novie Amelia Chozie, Bernie Endyarni Medise, Wahyuni Indawati, Cut Nurul Hafifah, Widdy Winarta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEpilepsy is a common chronic neurological disorder in pediatrics. Depression is an often underdetected comorbidity in childhood epilepsy. This study aimed to adapt the Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory‐Epilepsy for Youth (NDDI‐E‐Y) to the Indonesian language and population, as well as to validate the Indonesian version of NDDI‐E‐Y (NDDI‐E‐Y[ID]).MethodsThis three‐stage study comprised instrument translation, cultural verification, and content validity testing (first stage), pilot testing (second stage), followed by concurrent validity and reliability testing (third stage) of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). Validation was done against the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale – Revised (CESD‐R). Content validity, assessed by six experts, was quantified using the content validity index for items (I‐CVI) and scale (S‐CVI). Participants were adolescents aged 12–17 years diagnosed with any type of epilepsy who completed both instruments. Concurrent validity was evaluated using Spearman's correlation and reliability was measured using Cronbach's alpha.ResultsThe first stage produced a culturally appropriate NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). Thirty healthy adolescents and 10 adolescents with epilepsy participated in the second stage. In the third stage, another group of 30 adolescents with epilepsy took part. We obtained I‐CVI and S‐CVI values averaging 1. The NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) showed a positive and significant correlation with CESD‐R (Spearman's rho = 0.671, p < 0.001). A Cronbach's alpha of 0.928 reflected a high internal consistency.SignificanceBased on the results, the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) was found to be a valid and reliable screening instrument for detecting depression in youth with epilepsy.Plain Language SummaryDepression is an under‐recognized problem in youth with epilepsy. Currently available depression screening tools are in English, making it less suitable for detection purposes in Indonesia. This study developed and validated the Indonesian version of the NDDI‐E‐Y, a depression screening tool for youth with epilepsy.Keywords: concurrent validity, content validity, depression, epilepsy, reliability, youth

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a prevalent chronic neurological disorder among youth, with an incidence rate ranging from 0.5% to 1%. 1 , 2 This condition often leads to developmental and psychiatric disorders in 40% of cases, a rate significantly higher than the rate of 6% in the general population. 3 The prevalence of depression in youth with epilepsy has been reported to be as high as 10%–30%, 4 , 5 potentially attributed to shared biological mechanisms with epilepsy and psychosocial factors, such as disease stigma and academic challenges. 6 , 7 Depression in epilepsy is associated with increased suicide rates, lower quality of life, and increased mortality. 8 , 9 , 10

Given the substantial impact of depression, routine screening is essential for early detection. An ideal screening tool for youth should be brief, self‐administered, and sensitive. Existing instruments include Children's Depression Inventory (CDI), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale – Revised (CESD‐R). However, these tools may not suit individuals with epilepsy due to atypical symptom presentations and potential side effects from anti‐epileptic drugs (AEDs). 11 , 12 Furthermore, long‐term treatment with AEDs also causes potential side effects that mimic depressive symptoms, such as decreased concentration, fatigue, and sleep disturbances. 13 , 14 , 15

The Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory‐Epilepsy for Youth (NDDI‐E‐Y) was developed to screen for epilepsy in children and adolescents. The questionnaire was adapted from a similar instrument designed for adults and excludes overlapping symptoms and AED‐related effects. The questions in NDDI‐E‐Y target youth‐specific depressive symptoms, such as irritability or crankiness. Despite having been demonstrated as reliable in previous studies, it has not been adapted to the Indonesian language and culture. 16 , 17 This study aimed to adapt NDDI‐E‐Y to the Indonesian language and examine the diagnostic performance, validity, and reliability of the Indonesian version of NDDI‐E‐Y (NDDI‐E‐Y[ID]).

METHODS

Study design

This study consisted of three stages. The first stage entailed a qualitative phase comprising translation, cultural verification, and content validity study. The second stage involved pilot testing the instrument, while the third stage consisted of concurrent validity and reliability testing.

Setting and participants

In the first stage of the study, several translators and experts were involved in the process of translation, cultural verification, and assessment of content validity. In the second stage, 30 healthy high school students in Jakarta and 10 adolescents with epilepsy from the Pediatric Neurology Clinic of Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital (CMH), all aged 12–17 years old, participated in the cognitive debriefing study (pilot testing). Participants were included if they were able to read and write independently in Indonesian, gave consent, and obtained consent from their parents. Exclusion criteria were below‐average school marks or documented moderate/severe intellectual disability.

For the third stage assessing concurrent validity and reliability, 30 separate 12‐ to 17‐year‐old adolescents with epilepsy were consecutively recruited from the CMH Pediatric Neurology Clinic and a private neurology clinic in South Jakarta. Similar to the second stage, these adolescents with epilepsy were included if they were able to read and write independently in Indonesian, gave consent, and obtained consent from their parents. Exclusion criteria were below‐average school marks or documented moderate/severe intellectual disability. Sample size calculations used the formula for correlation between two numeric variables and reliability testing. 18

Measurement tools

The NDDI‐E‐Y(ID)

The NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) is a translation and cultural adaptation of the English version. NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) is a depression screening tool for youth with epilepsy that requires adequate neurocognitive ability for independent completion. This tool consists of 14 items, consisting of 12 items reflecting thoughts or feelings in the past 2 weeks, scored on a scale from 1 to 4 (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = always or often); one unscored “yes or no” question regarding the presence of anything bothering the respondent's mind in the past 2 weeks, and one unscored open‐ended question to elaborate on the answer to the previous question (Figure S1). The total score is the sum of all item scores, yielding a possible total score of 12–48. Based on a previous study validating the English NDDI‐E‐Y against the CDI‐2, scores of ≥32 were considered to indicate possible depression, with a sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) values of 79%, 92%, and 0.886, respectively. 16

CESD‐R Indonesian version

The CESD‐R, originally developed by Radloff and named CESD in 1977 and revised in 2004 by Eaton and colleagues, is a 20‐item scale. 19 This tool measures depressive symptoms across nine domains, including sadness, loss of interest, appetite, sleep, thinking/concentration, guilt, tiredness, movement, and suicidal ideation. In line with the DSM‐V criteria for a major depressive episode, CESD‐R used a 4‐point ordinal scale for each item indicating frequency of symptoms, namely, 0 = not at all or less than 1 day in the last week; 1 = 1–2 days in the last week; 2 = 3–4 days in the last week; 3 = 5–7 days or nearly every day for 2 weeks. 20

CESD‐R had already been validated in Indonesian youth with chronic diseases against the Mini‐International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI‐KID), a neuropsychiatric interview instrument for children and adolescents with psychiatric morbidity. The cutoff score of CESD‐R for screening of depression was >9. In the validation study, the CESD‐R had a sensitivity of 93.9% and specificity of 73.1%, with an AUC value of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.86–0.97). 21 The scale's reliability, shown by Cronbach's alpha, was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.84–0.91). 21

Study procedures

This study follows systematic guidelines for the translation, adaptation, and validation of instruments in healthcare research, as explained by Wild et al., 22 and Sousa et al. 23 in their publications. The process is summarized in Figure 1.

Overview of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) adaptation process.

First stage

The English NDDI‐E‐Y was forward‐translated by two independent medical translators and back‐translated by another two independent translators not involved in the forward translation. An expert committee discussion was conducted after each translation process. There were six experts involved, consisting of a child and adolescent psychiatrist, a pediatric neurologist, a developmental‐behavioral pediatrician, a psychologist, a general pediatrician, and a language expert. 24 , 25 The experts were invited to assess the appropriateness and discrepancies between translations and process cognitive debriefing (cultural verification) of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). During this stage, each expert was also invited to assess the content validity. Content validity entailed asking experts to rate each item's relevance and congruency on a scale of 1–4, with 1 being not relevant, 2 being somewhat relevant, 3 being quite relevant, and 4 being highly relevant. Rating valuations for each item were used as item content validity index (I‐CVI), which would subsequently be used to compute the scale content validity index (S‐CVI). 25

Second stage

The pre‐final instrument produced after the first stage was then subjected to final cognitive debriefing (pilot testing) in 30 healthy adolescents and 10 adolescents with epilepsy to assess comprehensibility and acceptability. 22 , 24 , 26 The initial testing was conducted on healthy adolescents who were assumed to have better cognitive function than those with epilepsy. After confirming comprehensibility with these participants, we tested the instruments on adolescents with epilepsy. Healthy participants were recruited from a high school in Jakarta, while adolescents with epilepsy were recruited from the Pediatric Neurology Clinic of CMH. The NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) that had been developed in the first stage was delivered using an online form. Following each scale item, we asked an open‐ended question requesting feedback on language comprehensibility and acceptability, as well as suggestions for changes. Interviews were then conducted by an independent physician through telephone or remote video conferencing to clarify the participant's understanding. The interviewer completed a summary sheet after each interview to show the level of comprehension among participants. The sheet also included information regarding items or questions that were confusing or misunderstood. 22 , 24

Third stage

This stage used a cross‐sectional diagnostic test study design to measure concurrent validity and the reliability of the instrument. For concurrent validity, we compared the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) to the CESD‐R. Participants were asked to complete both instruments, each administered through an online form. A gap of 1–3 days was imposed between administration of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) and the CESD‐R, to avoid bias resulting from participants synchronizing their answers for both instruments. Participants who attended the Pediatric Neurology Clinic completed the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) by filling out the online forms after being given explanation in person by a study staff. Those who could not attend the clinic in person due to the COVID‐19 pandemic completed the instrument remotely after receiving explanation by telephone. Afterward, all participants completed the CESD‐R remotely. Participants who were identified by the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) or CESD‐R as having suicidal thoughts or severe depression were referred to the Child/Adolescent Psychiatry Unit in CMH or any nearby hospital; their parents or guardians were notified by telephone and provided the contact details of study investigators facilitating the referral. Participants with mild–moderate depression, assessed based on the CESD‐R, were advised to seek psychiatric services in CMH or the nearest healthcare facility.

Data management and statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from medical records and subsequently recorded. SPSS version 20 (IBM, Armonk, New York) was used for data analysis. Content validity was determined by calculating the content validity index (CVI), comprising I‐CVI and S‐CVI. S‐CVI was computed through the average I‐CVI scores for all items on the scale (S‐CVI/Ave) and the proportion of items on the scale receiving a uniform 3 or 4 valuations from all experts (S‐CVI/UA). 25

Concurrent validity was assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation. A correlation coefficient (r) of 0.4 to 0.69 was considered as moderate correlation and an r of 0.7 to 1.00 as strong to very strong correlation. 27 A Cronbach's alpha value of 0.7 to 0.95, representing a good reliability, was considered acceptable. 28

We also performed receiver‐operator curve (ROC) analysis to measure AUC value and Youden's index (sensitivity + specificity – 1). 29 This analysis was done to provide initial data on the optimum cutoff level of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). An AUC value of 0.7 to 0.9 was considered as indicating moderate accuracy and 0.9 to 1 as indicating high accuracy. 30

Ethics approval

The protocol for this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia (No. KET‐468/UN2.F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2022, May 17, 2022).

RESULTS

First stage: Translation, cultural verification, and content validity testing

This study commenced with forward translation by two translators, followed by a focused discussion among experts to assess the appropriateness and discrepancies between translations. The most suitable translation was selected for each original wording and for some items of the instrument. The experts also proposed different wordings perceived to be more common and culturally appropriate for the target age group across the Indonesian archipelago. All experts achieved consensus during the discussion. No issues were identified in the ensuing back translation, and the results were sent to the original instrument developer, Wagner et al. 16 During this stage, an analysis of content validity was also conducted on 12 items of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID), excluding items 13 and 14 due to the open‐ended nature. In general, the instrument showed excellent content validity with mean I‐CVI, S‐CVI/UA, and S‐CVI/Ave values of 1 (Table 1).

Second stage: Pilot testing

Pilot testing was performed on 30 healthy high school students with a median age of 16 years. About three‐quarters were females (22/30). None of the healthy participants found the instrument confusing or difficult to understand.

Subsequent pilot testing was done on 10 adolescents with epilepsy with a mean age of 14.3 years. Of the adolescents in this group, half were females and most (9/10) had generalized epilepsy. None had refractory epilepsy or were prescribed more than two AEDs. Three adolescents in this group expressed uncertainty about the term “frustration.” After further discussion with the experts, it was decided that the wording of the instrument would remain unchanged to maintain the original construct and also because “frustration” is a common word in Indonesian. Third stage: Measuring concurrent validity and reliability

Characteristics of participants

After finalizing the instrument, a total of 30 adolescents with epilepsy were recruited. Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants are shown in Table 2. More than half of the participants (19/30) were female. The median age of participants was 14.8 years. Most participants (28/30) were junior‐ or senior high school students; two were in the final year of elementary school. The majority had generalized (19/30) epilepsy, with an average treatment duration of 6 years. Only five participants were prescribed more than two AEDs, and six had refractory epilepsy. Seven participants, who were all female, had NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) scores of ≥32, indicating depression.

Concurrent validity

Spearman's correlation coefficient between NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) and CESD‐R was 0.671 (p < 0.001), showing good concurrent validity (Figure 2). Additionally, the correlations between each item of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) and each item of the CESD‐R were examined (Table S2a,b). Notably, items 3 and 11 of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) showed poor correlations with most CESD‐R items; conversely, items 1, 10, 12, and 18 of the CESD‐R had poor correlations with most NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) items.

Receiver‐operator curve analysis

Using the CESD‐R as the gold standard, a ROC curve of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) was generated (Figure 3). The optimum NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) cutoff level based on the ROC curve was ≥27, resulting in the highest Youden's index value of 0.667. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) were 86.7% (95% CI: 59.5%–98.3%), 80% (95% CI: 51.9%–95.7%), 65% (95% CI: 39.8%–83.9%), and 93.3% (95% CI: 63.7%–93.6%), respectively. The AUC value was 0.822 (95% CI: 0.654–0.991).

DISCUSSION

This is the first study aimed at adapting NDDI‐E‐Y to Indonesian and assessing the performance of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). Our findings provide preliminary support for the reliability and validity of the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) for early and prompt detection of depression among youth with epilepsy in Indonesia. Depression, despite the strong association with increased mortality, morbidity, and suboptimal treatment adherence, remains under‐recognized in the population. During clinic visits, the majority of epilepsy patients undergo routine evaluation only; pediatricians often do not recognize the issue of depression. 7 , 8 To address this, a concise and suitable instrument such as the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) becomes crucial for recognizing, referring, and treating depression in adolescents with epilepsy. Guilfoyle et al. stated that adolescents who received treatment showed improvement in their depressive symptoms despite only being treated briefly. Therefore, pediatricians and pediatric neurologists should be engaged in actively screening for depression in this subgroup of the population during their routine care. 5

In line with Wagner et al.'s original study in English, 16 which compared the NDDI‐E‐Y to the CDI‐2 (r = 0.7; p < 0.001; Cronbach's alpha 0.92), the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) also showed similar traits, implying excellent internal consistency and strong correlation with the CESD‐R, affirming good concurrent validity. Comparable results were found by Viellard et al.'s study of the French version, 17 showcasing a strong correlation with CDI (r = 0.848, p < 0.001) and excellent internal consistency, denoted by Cronbach's alpha value of 0.862. This study also assessed content validity using I‐CVI, S‐CVI, and CVR scores, all showing excellent content validity. The assessment ensured the relevance and representation of the adapted instrument for targeted constructs, making it a sound choice for depression screening among youth with epilepsy. 25 , 31

The NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) and CESD‐R generally demonstrated good correlation, but some items did not show a statistically significant correlation. Certain NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) items, related to crying frequency and feeling cranky or irritated, showed a weak correlation with CESD‐R. Although some experts asserted a correlation between the frequency of crying and severity of depression, others observed that this association was not consistent. 32 The frequency of crying might increase in youth with a milder degree of depression, but individuals experiencing severe depression may lose the ability to cry. 32 The other item which read “I feel cranky or irritated” was a manifestation of depression frequently encountered in youth. This item was not specifically stated by CESD‐R. Thus, it is assumed that there is a poor item correlation between CESD‐R and NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). 16 , 19 , 33 These differences underscore the specificity of the instruments, measuring distinct dimensions of depressive symptoms. Furthermore, this condition applied to some CESD‐R items, representing appetite, anhedonia, and movements, which did not correlate well with NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). Adults typically show decreased appetite during a depressive episode. However, in youth, appetite level could increase especially if it coincides with an anxiety disorder, while in children, the appetite usually remains normal. 33 , 34 Additionally, in older populations, anhedonia and slow movements occur frequently, while in youth, depression might manifest as an increased level of activity, agitation, and restlessness. 33 The side effects of anti‐epileptic drugs (AED) can also induce decreased concentration, fatigue, and sleep disturbance, which overlap with the somatic symptoms of depression. The exclusion of these symptoms in the construct of NDDI‐E‐Y and NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) accounts for its difference and uniqueness compared to other instruments. 16

Another important result from this study was the optimum cutoff level and diagnostic performance of NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). Using a score of ≥27 as the cutoff level to detect depression, we found acceptable sensitivity and AUC values. Since the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) was developed for screening purposes, the good sensitivity result in this study indicate that this instrument can be used to screen depression symptoms in clinical setting. Despite possessing a satisfactory initial performance level, the cutoff level of NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) was quite different from the one produced by Wagner et al. 16 in the United States of America and Viellard et al. 17 in France, which were ≥ 32 and ≥ 23, respectively. The difference could be attributed to variations in the gold standard examination. A study by Marker et al. compared the diagnostic performance of several commonly used depression screening measures, including CESD‐R and CDI‐2, in adolescents with type 1 diabetes. They found that CDI‐2, both the long and short versions, performed better than CESD‐R. 35 While both measures were developed based on DSM‐V criteria, the CDI‐2 is specifically designed for children and adolescents, unlike CESD‐R, which is intended for adults and older adolescents. Additionally, the CDI‐2 has been extensively used in epilepsy clinical practice and research, assisting clinicians with the diagnosis and monitoring of depression severity. 5 , 36

Differences in cutoff level could also be attributed to the characteristic of the study participants. The majority of participants in this study were adolescents with well‐controlled epilepsy, that is, those requiring less than two AEDs to manage their epilepsy. Moreover, those with below‐average school performance were excluded. In contrast, Viellard et al. found that over 50% of participants in their French study reported still experiencing seizures. Adolescents with cognitive impairment or decline and those with refractory epilepsy may perceive the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) items differently. Novak et al. reported that cognitive decline in epilepsy is related to frequent, recurrent, and prolonged seizures. 37 Consequently, it may increase the risk for depression. 38 Further studies should be conducted to validate the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) in those with uncontrolled or refractory epilepsy.

Beyond participant characteristics, differences in culture, race, religion, and beliefs among populations could further contribute to the variation in cut‐off levels. These factors would affect the types and severity of depression as well as the variation in clinical manifestations. 39 , 40 Marker et al. found that the cutoff levels of several common screening measures for depression in adolescents need adjustment for use in Korean adolescents with type 1 diabetes. 35 Cultural differences between regions could cause varying individual responses to distress, the origin of depression. Somatic symptoms have been reported to be more prevalent in the Asian and Middle Eastern community, showing collective culture. 39 This suggests that cultural differences, along with a specific clinical background such as epilepsy, must be considered when creating or adapting instruments for use in local communities.

In addition to these cultural considerations, gender differences play a significant role in depression. Most participants in this study were females, who have a two‐fold risk of depression during puberty compared to males. 41 , 42 The data also showed that all adolescents who were clinically depressed, based on high NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) scores, were female. This rate of depression is consistent with results reported by Wagner et al. 16 and several other studies. 4 , 5 The higher incidence of depression in females is mainly attributed to endocrinological changes during puberty. Other contributing factors include brain maturation, increased self‐awareness, enhanced social understanding, and elevated stressors. 41 , 43 Given these high rates of depression, healthcare facilities must stress the importance of routine screening for depression and provide the appropriate preventive means, with particular attention to females.

In line with the need for effective screening tools, Fecske et al. 44 studied the use of NDDI‐E‐Y in their quality improvement projects, and it led to staff satisfaction and increased mental health referrals for adolescents with epilepsy. Some adolescents were even referred due to healthcare provider concerns despite not meeting the cutoff scores. Interestingly, most of the staff admitted that the NDDI‐E‐Y did not impact clinic flow and encouraged much‐needed conversations between patients and staff. 44 Moreover, previous study also showed that the NDDI‐E‐Y is superior to Neuro‐QOL SF (Depression Scale Short Form of the Pediatric Neuro‐QOL) as a depression screening tool for adolescents with epilepsy. The Neuro‐QOL SF is an instrument designed to assess quality of life, including depressive symptoms in children with neurological condition, including epilepsy. 36 Based on these encouraging results, the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) should be implemented for pediatric epilepsy care in various healthcare facilities in Indonesia.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

This is the first study to adapt the NDDI‐E‐Y for use among adolescents with epilepsy in Indonesia. Additionally, it is also the first report comparing NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) to CESD‐R as the gold standard. The Indonesian CESD‐R was chosen because it is a current depression screening instrument that has been studied (2020) in adolescents with chronic diseases in Indonesia. Another strength of this study included being conducted at CMH, one of the tertiary referral hospitals in Indonesia. Therefore, the recruited participants had epilepsy of various classifications with varying levels of severity. This implies that the instrument can be used in various clinical situations of epilepsy.

The process of data collection was conducted online using Google Forms, improving efficiency and reducing costs. However, this approach lacks direct control and supervision. To mitigate this, we have provided clear explanations prior to the participant's self‐completion of the instrument, and participants were permitted to ask questions. In real clinical practice, NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) will also be completed independently by the adolescents without strict supervision.

Other limitations of this study are related to the use of CESD‐R as gold standard, which is less than ideal. The CESD‐R serves as a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool for diagnosing depression. However, the included items in the CESD‐R measure symptoms of depression as defined in the DSM‐V. In addition, the Indonesian CESD‐R was validated against the diagnosis of depression established by psychiatrists. 21 We also obtained diagnostic performance values using a relatively small sample size (n = 30). This could affect the accuracy of conclusions related to the diagnostic performance of NDDI‐E‐Y(ID). However, measuring the diagnostic performance of the instrument was beyond the scope of this study, which only aimed to adapt and examine validity along with reliability. Despite this, data on the optimal cutoff point were presented to assist clinicians in using NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) while carrying out daily practices in Indonesia before further investigation is conducted.

FUTURE DIRECTION

This study showed that the NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) had excellent content validity and concurrent validity as well as reliability, showing its suitability for the detection and screening of depression among adolescents with epilepsy in Indonesia. By using a cutoff level of ≥27, clinicians can predict the occurrence of depression and appropriately refer susceptible youth to psychiatrists. Successful screening of depression would eventually reduce the negative impacts of depression, such as decreased treatment compliance, lower quality of life, and increased mortality. Moreover, routine implementation during each clinical visit was mandatory. Future studies using larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the optimal cutoff level of NDDI‐E‐Y(ID) and the diagnostic performance of the instrument with better accuracy.
