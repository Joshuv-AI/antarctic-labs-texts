Sociodemographic and structural correlates of past-year HIV testing in a statewide sample: Analysis of the 2024 California BRFSS
Authors: Roxana Rezai, Wilson H. Hammett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRoutine HIV testing is central to prevention, yet many remain unaware of their status. Although California has expanded screening initiatives, gaps in testing persist. Understanding current testing patterns and associated factors are essential to inform equitable screening strategies.MethodsData from the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System were used to examine prevalence and correlates of past-year HIV testing among California adults (N = 5799). Past-year testing was defined using date of most recent HIV test. Independent variables included sociodemographics, self-reported general health, and HIV risk behaviors. Survey weights using iterative proportional fitting were applied. Multivariable logistic regression models estimated adjusted associations with past-year testing.ResultsFifteen percent reported HIV testing in the past year. Adults aged 18–34 and 35–54 had higher odds of past-year testing than those ≥55. Black and Hispanic respondents had greater odds of recent testing compared to White respondents. Being single, insured, in fair/poor health, or engaging in HIV risk behaviors were also associated with recent HIV testing.ConclusionsRacial/ethnic testing patterns suggest positive improvements in testing among populations that historically tested at lower rates. However, findings highlight disparities and a need to expand routine testing beyond younger populations to identify and/or prevent new infections.

Introduction

Despite vast improvements in prevention and treatment in the four decades since its peak, the HIV epidemic in the U.S. has moved beyond its initial impact on specific populations and geographies to become a widespread health crisis impacting individuals of all ages, races, and sexes (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a). As of 2024, the CDC estimates that nearly 1.2 million Americans are living with HIV, with annual new infections still exceeding 38,000 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026a). Testing remains a vital component of HIV prevention efforts, as individuals living with HIV but unaware of their status generate almost 40% of new HIV transmissions (Li et al., 2019). Although nationwide initiatives to expand HIV testing have increased testing rates in certain demographic groups, testing percentages vary widely between states (Pitasi et al., 2019).

Access to healthcare and socioeconomic factors such as income and education level have been found to be associated with HIV testing behaviors, as have stigma and perceived risk of contracting HIV (Jamal et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025). Health system barriers and laws that criminalize HIV exposure or status non-disclosure are also widely viewed as a structural barriers to testing (Burris et al., 2008; Kavanagh and Gostin, 2019). In the last decade, several initiatives from the state of California have aimed to reduce these barriers, such as the 2016 removal of requirements for written informed consent and pre-test counseling when requesting an HIV test from a medical provider (Leibowitz and Tan, 2021; An act to amend Section 120990 of the Health and Safety Code, relating to public health., 2640, State Assembly, 2016). In 2018, California reformed its HIV criminalization law, making it a misdemeanor to transmit (rather than expose) an infectious disease to another individual and requiring evidence of intent to transmit. California also removed the law's explicit reference to HIV and reduced the penalty to a maximum of six months imprisonment (The Center for HIV Law and Policy, 2024). In the two years after this reform (2019–2020), the predicted marginal probability of past-year testing among adults at high risk for HIV increased by six percentage points, although it remained low (42%) (Keralis et al., 2024). No studies have examined patterns in past-year HIV testing in California after 2020, leaving our understanding of current, post-reform testing trends incomplete.

The present study seeks to provide an updated analysis on current patterns of past-year HIV testing in the state of California using the most recent data. This study expands upon prior analyses that are restricted to lifetime testing or past-year testing among adults at high risk for HIV (Leibowitz and Tan, 2021; Keralis et al., 2024). By analyzing patterns of past-year HIV testing among all California adults, this study aims to assess the state's HIV prevention efforts more broadly beyond those deemed high-risk.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross sectional study uses data from the 2024 California Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance Survey (BRFSS). BRFSS is a telephone survey that collects state-level data related to health, including risk behaviors and engagement in preventative services, from non-institutionalized adults aged 18 years or older. To be eligible for the study, survey respondents must be a California resident and have access to a working landline or cell phone.

Measures

The BRFSS survey collected information on the date of most recent HIV test, sociodemographic information, HIV risk behaviors, and self-reported general health status. Recent HIV testing was categorized as testing that took place within the last year. Sociodemographic information collected included race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian/American Indian/Alaskan Native/other), insurance coverage (yes or no), marital status (married/coupled or single/widowed/divorced), employment (employed, unemployed, or retired/student/homemaker), age (18–34 years, 35–54 years, or 55+ years), sex (male or female), education (high school diploma or less, some college, or college graduate), health insurance status (insured or uninsured). HIV risk behaviors were defined as engaging in any of the following activities in the past year: Injected any drug other than those prescribed for you, treated for a sexually transmitted infection, given or received money or drugs in exchange for sex in the past, had anal sex without a condom, had four or more sex partners. General health was measured using 5-point likert scale, which was dichotomized into ‘poor/fair health’ and ‘good/very good/excellent health.’

Statistical analysis

The dataset does not include identifiable information and therefore was except from institutional review board (IRB) oversight. Analysis accounted for the BRFSS sampling design. Survey weights, which are developed by the CDC using iterative proportional fitting (or ranking), along with the BRFSS strata and primary sampling units, were incorporated into all parts of the analysis to produce national representative estimates and appropriate standard errors (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024b). The distributions of variables of interest were summarized using frequencies (or percentages) and were compared by recent HIV testing status using Pearson's chi-square tests. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine factors associated with past-year HIV testing among California adults. All data, management, processing, descriptive analyses, and statistical modeling were done using STATA 16 (StataCorp, 2019).

Results

The study included a total of 6135 respondents to the 2024 California BRFSS. State-level response rate statistics are reported by the CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance Systems, 2024). The demographic breakdown of the full sample (Table 1) shows a diverse population: 59.10% identified as White, 31.8% as Hispanic, 7% as Black/African American, and 2.10% as Mixed/Asian/Other. Half of the participants (50%) were aged 55 years or older, while the remainder were distributed between the 18–34 (25.90%) and 35–54 (24.10%) age brackets. Fifty two percent of the sample reported being single and 44.8% had completed a 4-year college degree or higher. Nearly half of the sample were employed (48%), and the vast majority of respondents under age 65 (91.30%) reported having health insurance coverage. Overall, 836 respondents (weighted proportion = 14.80%) reported receiving an HIV test within the past year. Testing rates varied across demographic groups. Within the recently tested group, 43.50% were aged 18–34 years, compared to only 23.20% of the non-tested group. Racial and ethnic representation also varied between groups: while White participants made up 59.10% of the total sample, they accounted for only 38.20% of those recently tested. Conversely, Hispanic participants (36%) and Black/African American participants (11.40%) represented a larger proportion of the recently tested group than their overall sample prevalence (32% and 7%, respectively). When examining testing among those who reported engaging in high risk behaviors, the weighted prevalence of recent HIV testing was 36.60%.

After adjusting for covariates in the fully adjusted model, as seen in Table 2, several factors were associated with HIV testing within the past year. Age and race/ethnicity remained among the strongest predictors. Compared to the 55+ age group, participants aged 18–34 years had over three times the odds of being tested (aOR = 3.59; 95%CI: 2.56, 5.02), and those aged 35–54 years had over double the odds (aOR = 2.66; 95%CI: 1.88, 3.76). Black/African American participants were significantly more likely to have been tested than White participants (aOR = 2.89; 95%CI: 1.93, 4.34), as were Hispanic participants (aOR = 1.42; 95%CI: 1.10, 1.83). Single marital status was associated with higher odds of testing compared to those who reported being married or coupled (aOR = 1.63; 95%CI: 1.29, 2.05). While being employed did not significantly differ from being unemployed (aOR = 0.92; 95%CI: 0.69, 1.24), individuals in the student/retired/homemaker category were significantly less likely to have reported HIV testing in the past year compared to those who reported being unemployed. Access to care and behavioral risk were strongly associated with testing uptake. Having health insurance more than doubled the odds of recent testing (aOR = 2.20; 95%CI: 1.43, 3.40). Those who reported engaging in HIV risk behaviors were significantly more likely to be tested (aOR = 2.75; 95%CI: 2.05, 3.70) than those who did not. Finally, individuals who reported their general health status as being fair or poor had a 37% increase in the odds of testing compared to those in good to excellent health (aOR = 1.37; 95%CI: 1.04, 1.81).

Discussion

Consistent with prior research, our findings show that past-year testing rates among Black and Hispanic respondents are higher than other racial and ethnic groups (Office of Minority Health, 2026). However, current testing frequencies are still insufficient when measured against the disproportionate burden of the epidemic among these communities (Jamal et al., 2024; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026b). Although disproportionate burden among certain racial and ethnic groups is not indicative of biological predisposition to infection but instead points to underlying structural factors and systemic disparities (Muno et al., 2024), our findings suggest both the success of targeted testing interventions and a continued need for improved testing efforts among high-prevalence racial and ethnic groups.

Similarly, while the 18–34 age group showed the highest prevalence of past-year testing and made up a large proportion of all recently-tested respondents, less than half of this age group reported being tested for HIV in the past year. Considering the disproportionate burden of new HIV infection among young adults, this also suggests a continued need for improved testing efforts among younger adults (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. Fast Facts. Fund MHA, 2023).

Although the main analyses of this study examined past-year HIV testing among all California adults, our finding that those engaged in high-risk behaviors were significantly more likely to report recent HIV testing than those not reporting high-risk behaviors points to successful targeting in testing interventions and is consistent with prior literature (Keralis et al., 2024). While the previous study by [authors], using 2019–2020 BRFSS, found an initial increase in past-year testing among high-risk adults following the 2018 reform of California's HIV criminalization law, we found 2024 rates to be consistent with their reported rates. This could reflect a plateau of improved targeted testing interventions, however it is important to recognize that HIV testing rates dropped drastically across the U.S. during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have long-lasting impacts on testing access and behaviors (Moitra et al., 2022).

The findings presented here also highlight persisting disparities in HIV testing. While Hispanic and Black adults made up higher proportions of adults reporting a past-year HIV test than proportions of the total sample, white adults made up a smaller proportion of past-year HIV tested adults than of the overall sample. Older adults—particularly the 55+ group—reported past-year testing at much lower rates than younger adults. These results suggest that access to and normalization of testing as part of routine healthcare must be expanded outside of high-prevalence or high-risk groups.

Notably, past-year testing rates were also much higher among those who are insured than among those who are uninsured. These results highlight the role of structural access in shaping HIV prevention. Reliance on insurance-based healthcare models creates a ‘gatekeeper’ effect, where routine testing is largely accessible only to those with health insurance (Foley et al., 2024). Studies have demonstrated that competing life necessities, such as housing and food, will render preventative screenings a lower priority among those reporting no health insurance or lower annual income (Foley et al., 2024; Muhammad et al., 2025). This suggests that current HIV testing models may inadvertently favor those with the most stable access to healthcare. Adjacent to these results, we found that those with poorer self-rated general health status were more likely to report a past-year HIV test, potentially due to more frequent interactions with healthcare providers. Improved access to testing among uninsured populations and those not requiring consistent treatment for other health conditions in California may help mitigate these disparities and improve identification and/or prevention of new infections.

There are several limitations to this study. Response rates for BRFSS are relatively low (44% in California 2024), which may in some cases lead to selection bias in the data (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance Systems, 2024). For example, those most at risk of HIV or facing the most significant barriers to routine care may have been less likely to engage with a government-sponsored telephone survey such as the BRFSS, potentially leading to higher response rates among those who are insured, employed, and in self-reported poor health. This may also have led to higher reported rates of past-year HIV testing. Additionally, given the cross-sectional nature of the survey questions, no causal inferences can be made. Furthermore, our analysis would have been strengthen with the inclusion of poverty status, which was not available in the current dataset and could not be constructed from available income variables. Lastly, the BRFSS survey may not cover the wide range of covariates that are associated with HIV testing. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and recency of the 2024 California BRFSS lend strength to this study (see Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, the survey's robust, population-based sampling frame, which provides a representative snapshot of California's diverse demographic/sociodemographic landscape.

Conclusion

Testing is one of the most effective tools for HIV surveillance and preventing new infections. While decriminalization, stigma reduction, and targeted prevention efforts may be contributing to the declining HIV incidence, past-year testing rates remain low in California (Keralis et al., 2024; os Angeles County Department of Public Health, 2026). While improving access to HIV testing should be prioritized in those groups deemed at high risk of contracting HIV, these findings also highlight a need for improved testing efforts across sociodemographic characteristics.
