Differential laboratory monitoring in autoimmune disease: a matched case-control study from Qatar primary care
Authors: Aisha Al-Khinji, Dhafer Malouche, Abdullatif Al-Hor, Ahmad Haj Bakri, Mohamed Ghaith Al-Kuwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine whether patients with autoimmune diseases receive differential cardiometabolic and inflammatory laboratory monitoring compared to matched controls in primary care, and to identify predictors of monitoring patterns.MethodsWe conducted a matched case-control study using electronic health records from the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC), Qatar (January 2015 to December 2024). Adults with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), or systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) were matched 1:2 to controls without autoimmune disease on age and sex. Primary outcomes were receipt of cardiometabolic tests (haemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], complete lipid panel) and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]). We used conditional logistic regression adjusting for comorbidities, medications, and healthcare utilisation.ResultsAmong 27,911 participants (9,356 cases, 18,555 controls; mean age 46.7 years; 74.4% female) in 9,356 matched sets, we observed divergent monitoring patterns. Compared to controls, patients with RA had significantly lower odds of HbA1c monitoring (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.85) and complete lipid panels (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.82). Similar patterns were observed for SLE (HbA1c: OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.57–0.80; lipid: OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.51–0.70). Conversely, RA patients had 4.2-fold higher odds of CRP testing and 4.4-fold higher odds of ESR testing; SLE patients showed similar elevations (3.7-fold and 4.2-fold, respectively). Hashimoto’s patients showed modestly increased monitoring across all test types (HbA1c: OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.23–1.70).ConclusionsDespite elevated cardiovascular risk, patients with systemic autoimmune diseases (RA, SLE) receive less cardiometabolic laboratory monitoring than matched controls while receiving substantially more inflammatory marker testing. This differential monitoring pattern — which we describe as a “monitoring gap paradox” and which persisted across sensitivity analyses — is consistent with care coordination challenges at the primary care–specialty interface. The observational design precludes inferences about the underlying causes, but the findings identify a potential quality improvement opportunity warranting further investigation.Keywords: Autoimmune disease, Laboratory monitoring, Cardiovascular risk, Rheumatoid arthritis, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Primary care, Quality of care, HbA1c, Lipid panel

Introduction

Burden of autoimmune disease

Autoimmune diseases represent a heterogeneous group of chronic conditions characterised by immune system dysregulation and self-directed tissue damage, collectively affecting 3–5% of the global population—an estimated 400 million individuals worldwide [1, 2]. In Western populations, prevalence estimates reach 7.6% when accounting for the full spectrum of autoimmune conditions [3]. These diseases disproportionately affect women, who comprise approximately 78% of patients with autoimmune disorders, representing a striking sex disparity that has informed hypotheses regarding hormonal and genetic predisposition [4, 5].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), the most common systemic autoimmune arthropathy, affects 0.5–1% of the global population, with higher prevalence observed in Northern European and North American populations [6, 7]. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a prototypical multisystem autoimmune disease, affects 20–150 per 100,000 individuals, with a 9:1 female predominance that is particularly marked during reproductive years [8, 9]. Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, the leading cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions, affects up to 5% of the general population with a 7:1 female-to-male ratio [10, 11]. Notably, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis represents an organ-specific autoimmune condition predominantly managed within primary care and endocrinology, in contrast to systemic inflammatory conditions such as RA and SLE that typically require rheumatology involvement; its inclusion in the present study enables comparison of monitoring patterns between organ-specific and systemic autoimmune diseases within a unified primary care framework.

Epidemiological data on autoimmune diseases in the Middle East remain limited, with available estimates suggesting RA prevalence of approximately 0.3–1.0% and SLE prevalence of 15–50 per 100,000 [12, 13]. Qatar’s high prevalence of metabolic comorbidities—including one of the highest global rates of diabetes mellitus—and its comprehensive primary care system make it a particularly relevant setting for examining cardiometabolic monitoring practices in autoimmune populations [14].

Cardiovascular risk in autoimmune disease

A substantial body of evidence has established that patients with systemic autoimmune diseases face markedly elevated cardiovascular (CV) morbidity and mortality. The seminal meta-analysis by [15] demonstrated that patients with RA have a 1.39–1.61-fold increased risk of CV mortality compared to the general population (pooled relative risk 1.5). Subsequent meta-analyses have confirmed excess risks for myocardial infarction (RR 1.68), stroke (RR 1.41), and heart failure (RR 1.87) [16–18].

Cardiovascular risk in SLE is more pronounced, particularly in younger patients: a landmark study by [19] documented a 52-fold increased risk of myocardial infarction in women aged 35–44 years. Meta-analytic estimates indicate an overall 1.56–2.52-fold increased risk in SLE (pooled RR 1.98) [20, 21], and the excess burden translates to an estimated 10-year loss of life expectancy, predominantly from cardiovascular causes [22].

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and overt hypothyroidism have also been associated with increased CV risk, though the magnitude is smaller than that observed in systemic inflammatory conditions. Subclinical and overt hypothyroidism confer approximately 1.2-fold increased risk of coronary heart disease and up to 1.86-fold increased risk of heart failure [23–25]. The mechanisms include dyslipidaemia, diastolic hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and altered cardiac contractility—all of which are potentially modifiable with appropriate thyroid hormone replacement [26–33].

Guideline recommendations for cardiovascular screening

International guidelines from rheumatology, cardiology, and primary care organisations explicitly recommend CV risk assessment and management in patients with inflammatory rheumatic diseases. The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) issued evidence-based recommendations for CV disease risk management in 2016, subsequently updated in 2022 [34, 35]. Key recommendations include annual cardiovascular risk assessment, application of a 1.5 multiplication factor to traditional risk scores to account for inflammatory disease-attributable risk, lipid and blood pressure assessment at least annually, and disease activity control with glucocorticoid minimisation as integral components of cardiovascular risk reduction.

The 2022 EULAR update extended these recommendations beyond RA to encompass SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitis, systemic sclerosis, and other rheumatic conditions, establishing a blood pressure target of < 130/80 mmHg for SLE patients and endorsing hydroxychloroquine for its potential cardioprotective effects [35]. Complementing these guidelines [36], provided a comprehensive framework for lipid management in inflammatory joint diseases, emphasising that CV risk stratification and statin therapy should be integral components of rheumatological care.

The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 2015 guidelines for RA treatment similarly emphasise CV risk factor modification and recommend lipid screening at baseline and periodically thereafter [37]. The American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) 2019 guidelines on primary prevention of CV disease identify chronic inflammatory conditions as “risk-enhancing factors” that should inform shared decision-making regarding statin therapy [38].

Despite these concordant recommendations spanning over a decade, substantial gaps exist between guideline recommendations and clinical practice.

Evidence of monitoring gaps

Studies from North America and Europe have documented suboptimal CV preventive care in autoimmune disease populations. A systematic review found that only 30–50% of RA patients had documented lipid testing in the preceding year, despite guideline recommendations for annual screening [39]. Analysis of US Medicare claims by [40] reported that only 38% of RA patients with key cardiovascular comorbidities received recommended lipid testing.

A Canadian survey of rheumatologists by [41] revealed important care coordination challenges: while 91% of respondents agreed that SLE constitutes a major CV risk factor, only 58% felt comfortable performing CV risk assessment, 64% did not routinely inform family physicians about their patients’ elevated CV risk, and 15% did not check blood pressure at every visit. These findings suggest that despite awareness of elevated CV risk, systematic implementation of screening recommendations remains inadequate.

The “competing demands” model proposed by [42] offers a theoretical framework for understanding these monitoring gaps. When clinicians face limited consultation time and multiple clinical priorities, disease-focused care—including inflammatory marker monitoring for disease activity assessment—may inadvertently crowd out preventive services such as CV risk factor screening. This phenomenon may be particularly pronounced for patients with complex autoimmune diseases who require management of multiple concurrent clinical issues.

Knowledge gaps and study rationale

Several important knowledge gaps motivate the present study. First, most evidence on CV monitoring in autoimmune disease derives from specialty clinic populations in North America and Western Europe; data from primary care settings and Middle Eastern populations are sparse. The primary care context is particularly important because many patients with autoimmune diseases receive the majority of their longitudinal care from family physicians rather than specialists, and preventive CV services typically fall within the primary care domain.

Second, prior studies have generally examined screening rates without comparing autoimmune patients to matched controls, limiting inference about whether observed rates represent disease-specific deficits or reflect general healthcare system patterns. A matched case-control design enables direct comparison of monitoring patterns between autoimmune patients and demographically similar individuals without autoimmune disease.

Third, few studies have simultaneously examined cardiometabolic screening (HbA1c, lipid panels) and disease activity monitoring (inflammatory markers) within the same population. This dual focus enables assessment of whether elevated inflammatory marker testing in autoimmune populations coexists with—or potentially crowds out—CV preventive monitoring.

Fourth, comparative analysis across autoimmune disease categories may reveal condition-specific patterns that inform targeted quality improvement interventions. The inclusion of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis alongside RA and SLE is methodologically deliberate: as an organ-specific autoimmune condition predominantly managed within primary care without routine specialty co-management, Hashimoto’s functions as an internal comparator that tests whether any observed monitoring differential is specific to systemic inflammation-driven diseases requiring shared specialty–primary care arrangements, or whether it represents a broader pattern affecting all autoimmune diagnoses regardless of care setting. If the differential reflects autoimmunity per se, all three conditions should show similar monitoring patterns; if it reflects care fragmentation at the specialty–primary care interface, the systemic conditions (RA, SLE) should diverge from Hashimoto’s.

Study objectives

We conducted a matched case-control study using electronic health records from the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC), Qatar, to address these knowledge gaps. The study population comprised 27,911 participants: 9,356 patients with autoimmune disease (Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, RA, or SLE) matched to 18,555 controls without autoimmune disease based on age and sex.

Our primary objectives were to:

Compare patterns of cardiometabolic laboratory monitoring (HbA1c testing, complete lipid panel) between patients with autoimmune diseases and matched controls.

We hypothesised that patients with systemic autoimmune diseases would show differential—and potentially paradoxical—monitoring patterns, with elevated disease activity monitoring (inflammatory markers) potentially coexisting with gaps in cardiometabolic screening despite their elevated CV risk.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a matched case-control study using electronic health records (EHRs) from the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC), Qatar. The study period extended from January 2015 to December 2024. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for case-control studies.

Setting

PHCC is the principal public primary healthcare provider in Qatar, operating 31 health centres across the country. The corporation serves a diverse, multinational population and maintains a comprehensive EHR system (Cerner Millennium) that captures demographic information, clinical diagnoses (coded using ICD-10), laboratory results, prescribed medications, and healthcare utilisation data. All PHCC health centres are linked through a unified electronic system, enabling longitudinal tracking of patient encounters and laboratory investigations.

Controls were adults without any documented autoimmune disease diagnosis during the study period. Controls were randomly selected from the eligible pool without replacement using simple random sampling.

Participants were excluded if they were younger than 18 years at the time of data extraction (n = 429) or had incomplete matching (n = 34). After applying exclusion criteria, 27,911 participants remained in 9,356 matched sets (Fig. 1).

Variables

Exposure

The primary exposure was autoimmune disease status, categorised as: Control (no autoimmune disease), Hashimoto Only, RA Only, SLE Only, or Multiple Autoimmune. Autoimmune diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes recorded in the EHR at any point during the study period; no external validation of diagnoses against clinical criteria was performed.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were binary indicators of laboratory test receipt at any point during the cumulative study period (January 2015 to December 2024). Outcomes were assessed as ever-versus-never tested during the full observation window rather than per calendar year. This operationalisation captures any exposure to screening over the observation window and therefore represents a coarse, lower-bound measure of monitoring; it does not assess guideline-concordant longitudinal monitoring frequency (e.g., the EULAR recommendation of annual cardiovascular risk assessment in RA). Implications of this choice are discussed in Strengths and Limitations.

Cardiometabolic monitoring: Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) testing; complete lipid panel, defined as having recorded values for all four components (LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides) at any time during the study period. Components were not required to be collected on the same date; we assessed whether each component was ever measured.

Covariates

Models were adjusted for diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, cardiovascular disease (binary comorbidity indicators based on ICD-10 codes), corticosteroid exposure (any dispensing record during the study period), total medication count (number of distinct medication categories dispensed), and family medicine visits (count of primary care encounters).

Study size

The study included all eligible patients meeting inclusion criteria, resulting in 9,356 cases and 18,555 matched controls across 9,356 matched sets (9,199 complete 1:2 triplets; 157 incomplete 1:1 pairs). Post-hoc power analysis for the smallest pairwise comparison (SLE [n = 1,802] vs. controls [n = 18,555]; allocation ratio 10.3:1) indicated 99% power to detect the observed difference in HbA1c monitoring (SLE: 39.2% vs. controls: 61.3%; Cohen’s h = 0.45) at α = 0.05 (two-sided).

Statistical methods

Primary analysis

For binary outcomes (laboratory test receipt), we used conditional logistic regression stratified by matched sets to estimate adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). This approach accounts for the matched design by conditioning on matching strata, thereby controlling for age and sex by design. The Efron approximation was used for handling tied event times. Incomplete matched sets (157 pairs in which only one control was identified for a case) were retained in the analysis; conditional likelihood estimation handles variable stratum size by within-stratum conditioning and does not require complete 1:2 triplets. Given the limited number of pre-specified primary outcomes (two cardiometabolic, two inflammatory) and the consistency of effect estimates across outcomes and sensitivity analyses, we did not apply formal multiplicity adjustment; reported p-values should be interpreted in this context.

Secondary analysis

For count outcomes (total laboratory records), we used negative binomial regression with cluster-robust standard errors to account for overdispersion and the correlated matched design. These models included age and sex as explicit covariates.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness, we conducted: (1) analysis excluding patients with corticosteroid exposure; (2) restriction to patients with diabetes mellitus for HbA1c outcomes; and (3) models without healthcare utilisation adjustment.

Missing data

Laboratory test outcomes were defined by presence or absence of recorded values; absent values were interpreted as tests not performed rather than missing data. For covariates, missingness was minimal: diabetes mellitus (0%), hypertension (0%), dyslipidaemia (0%), cardiovascular disease (0%), corticosteroid exposure (0%), total medication count (0%), and family medicine visits (9.37%). Given this low missingness (maximum 9.4% for FM_Visits), complete-case analysis was used.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with packages including survival, MASS, sandwich, lmtest, and gtsummary. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the PHCC Department of Clinical Research (Protocol ID: BUHOOTH-D-25-00009) on April 24, 2025. The study used de-identified electronic health records with no direct participant interaction, and a waiver of informed consent was granted. All data were stored on password-protected and encrypted systems with access limited to authorised research team members. Results are reported in aggregate form without participant identifiers.

Results

Study population

A total of 27,911 participants were included in the final analysis, comprising 9,356 cases with autoimmune disease and 18,555 matched controls across 9,356 matched sets (Fig. 1). The majority of matched sets (9,199) were complete triplets (1 case matched to 2 controls), with 157 sets containing incomplete pairs (1 case matched to 1 control).

Among cases, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was the most prevalent condition (n = 2,042; 21.8%), followed by rheumatoid arthritis (n = 5,267; 56.3%), systemic lupus erythematosus (n = 1,802; 19.3%), and multiple autoimmune conditions (n = 245; 2.6%).

Baseline characteristics

The study population was predominantly female (74.4%) with a mean age of 46.7 ± 13.3 years (Table 1). Comorbid conditions were common: diabetes mellitus (27.8%), hypertension (28.6%), dyslipidaemia (27.3%), and cardiovascular disease (10.7%).

Primary analysis: conditional logistic regression

Cardiometabolic monitoring

Compared to matched controls, patients with RA had significantly lower adjusted odds of HbA1c monitoring (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.85; p < 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 4). Patients with SLE showed an even greater reduction in HbA1c monitoring (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.57–0.80; p < 0.001). In contrast, Hashimoto’s patients had modestly but significantly increased odds of HbA1c monitoring compared to controls (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.23–1.70; p < 0.001). Patients with multiple autoimmune conditions showed intermediate patterns (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.38–0.94).

For complete lipid panel monitoring, RA patients had significantly reduced odds (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.82; p < 0.001), as did SLE patients (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.51–0.70; p < 0.001). Hashimoto’s patients showed modestly increased lipid monitoring (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.08–1.45).

Inflammatory marker monitoring

The pattern for inflammatory markers was markedly reversed. RA patients had substantially higher adjusted odds of CRP testing (OR 4.21, 95% CI 3.80–4.66; p < 0.001) and ESR testing (OR 4.35, 95% CI 3.91–4.84; p < 0.001) compared to controls. SLE patients showed similar patterns: CRP (OR 3.71, 95% CI 3.14–4.37; p < 0.001) and ESR (OR 4.16, 95% CI 3.49–4.96; p < 0.001). Hashimoto’s patients also had elevated inflammatory marker testing, though to a lesser degree (CRP: OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.28–1.74; ESR: OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.19–1.69).

For any inflammatory marker testing (CRP or ESR), RA patients had 3.8-fold higher odds (95% CI 3.48–4.23) and SLE patients had 3.5-fold higher odds (95% CI 3.01–4.13) compared to controls.

Covariate effects

Among covariates, diabetes mellitus was the strongest predictor of HbA1c monitoring (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.26–1.62; p < 0.001), as expected (Fig. 5). Dyslipidaemia was strongly associated with lipid panel testing (OR 2.26, 95% CI 2.01–2.54). Corticosteroid exposure was associated with increased inflammatory marker testing (CRP: OR 1.30; ESR: OR 1.24). Healthcare utilisation (family medicine visits) was positively associated with all laboratory testing outcomes (Table 4).

Third, removing healthcare utilisation (family medicine visits) from the adjustment set did not attenuate the associations, suggesting that differential visit frequency does not fully explain the observed monitoring gaps. RA patients showed OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.50–0.59 and SLE patients showed OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.37–0.49 for HbA1c monitoring in models without visit adjustment.

Fourth, to gauge how much unmeasured external testing the differential-capture explanation would require, we computed the proportion of currently untested cases who would need a test recorded outside PHCC for case rates to equal matched-control rates. For HbA1c this was 30.7% of untested RA and 36.4% of untested SLE patients (17.1% and 22.1% of all RA and SLE patients, respectively); for the complete lipid panel, 27.8% and 34.8%. Within the diabetes subgroup, in which HbA1c is expected to be monitored in primary care, the required proportions rose to 48.2% (RA) and 58.8% (SLE) of untested patients.

Discussion

Principal findings

This large matched case-control study from primary care in Qatar documents a pattern of differential laboratory monitoring among patients with autoimmune diseases that mirrors findings from North American and European healthcare systems. Despite well-documented elevated cardiovascular risk in systemic autoimmune conditions, patients with rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus received significantly less cardiometabolic screening—including HbA1c testing (RA: OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.85; SLE: OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.57–0.8) and lipid panel assessment (RA: OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.82; SLE: OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.51–0.7)—compared to matched controls without autoimmune disease (Table 3). Conversely, inflammatory marker testing was substantially elevated in these groups, with CRP testing odds 4.21-fold higher in RA and 3.71-fold higher in SLE patients versus controls. This monitoring gap paradox persisted across sensitivity analyses, including restriction to patients with established diabetes (Table 6) and exclusion of corticosteroid-exposed patients, indicating that neither steroid-induced diabetes risk nor differential healthcare access fully accounts for the observed patterns. These findings are consistent with a differential monitoring pattern in administrative records that warrants further investigation; the observational design precludes inferences about underlying mechanisms or whether the pattern reflects practice variation, care coordination challenges, or differential data capture (see Limitations). Stated plainly, we observed an association consistent with, but not proof of, differential cardiometabolic monitoring; differential capture of tests performed outside PHCC remains an equally plausible explanation, and robustness across sensitivity analyses does not address it because such misclassification would be systematic.

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis patients exhibited a distinctly different profile, with modestly increased odds of cardiometabolic testing (HbA1c: OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.23–1.7) and minimal elevation in inflammatory markers (Fig. 2). Patients with multiple autoimmune conditions showed intermediate patterns, with reduced cardiometabolic monitoring (HbA1c: OR 0.6; lipid panel: OR 0.52) but elevated inflammatory testing, reflecting the composite influence of their underlying conditions.

Several non-mutually-exclusive explanations could account for the observed patterns and merit equal consideration. First, patients with RA and SLE may receive cardiometabolic tests through specialty clinics, hospital services, or private providers whose results are not captured in the PHCC electronic record, producing differential misclassification toward the null in these groups. Second, care fragmentation between rheumatology and primary care may leave cardiometabolic screening unattended when neither provider explicitly assumes responsibility — the so-called diffusion-of-responsibility phenomenon. Third, time-limited consultations in patients with active inflammatory disease may legitimately prioritise disease-activity assessment over preventive screening (the competing-demands phenomenon). Fourth, the patterns may reflect appropriate clinical judgement not captured in administrative data, such as recent normal results from external facilities or patient-specific decisions. Fifth, the differential could reflect true practice variation that is itself a quality improvement target. Distinguishing among these explanations requires linked specialty–primary care data and provider-attributed ordering records that were not available in this study. Comparison with existing literature

Before interpreting our findings, it is useful to situate them within the international literature. The monitoring patterns we observed in Qatar resemble gaps reported in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and continental Europe between guideline recommendations and clinical practice in cardiovascular screening for autoimmune populations. This apparent consistency is hypothesis-supporting context rather than evidence of direct transportability: differences in population, financing, and care organisation mean our estimates are robustly generalisable only to comparable Gulf Cooperation Council primary-care systems. The mechanisms commonly invoked—care fragmentation, competing clinical demands, and unclear role delineation between specialists and primary care—are plausible across systems but remain to be tested directly.

Cardiovascular risk in autoimmune disease

Our findings must be interpreted against the substantial body of evidence documenting elevated cardiovascular risk in autoimmune populations. A comprehensive meta-analysis by [43] demonstrated that RA patients face a relative risk of 1.55 (95% CI 1.39–1.73) for major cardiovascular events, while SLE patients experience even greater risk (RR 1.98, 95% CI 1.56–2.52). The UK Biobank study by [44], encompassing 22 million individuals across 19 autoimmune diseases, confirmed that patients with inflammatory rheumatic conditions carry substantially elevated cardiovascular burden extending beyond traditional risk factors.

The pathophysiology underlying this excess risk involves chronic systemic inflammation promoting accelerated atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and prothrombotic states [27, 45]. Inflammatory markers themselves—particularly CRP and ESR—have been associated with cardiovascular outcomes; [30] identified elevated ESR and CRP as independent risk factors for stroke in RA patients. This biological plausibility makes the reduced cardiometabolic screening we observed—with 6 statistically significant reductions in RA/SLE groups—particularly concerning from a clinical standpoint.

Cardiovascular risk management guidelines

International guidelines explicitly recommend cardiovascular risk assessment and management in inflammatory rheumatic diseases. The EULAR 2015/2016 recommendations called for annual cardiovascular risk assessment in all RA patients, with application of a 1.5 multiplication factor to traditional risk scores [34]. The original EULAR evidence-based recommendations by [46] established the foundation for systematic cardiovascular risk management in inflammatory arthritis.

Most recently, the 2022 EULAR recommendations extended cardiovascular risk management guidance beyond RA to encompass SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, gout, vasculitis, systemic sclerosis, and other rheumatic conditions [35]. These guidelines specifically recommend blood pressure targets below 130/80 mmHg in SLE patients, lipid management following general population guidelines, and disease activity control with glucocorticoid minimization. The recommendation for hydroxychloroquine in SLE acknowledges its potential dual benefit for both disease control and cardiovascular risk reduction.

Our findings suggest that despite these guideline recommendations spanning over a decade, implementation gaps may persist in primary care settings. It is important to note that disease-activity monitoring (e.g., inflammatory marker assessment for RA/SLE activity scoring) and cardiometabolic risk monitoring (e.g., HbA1c and lipid panels for cardiovascular risk stratification) are distinct, non-substitutable practices addressing different clinical questions; frequent ordering of the former does not satisfy guideline recommendations for the latter. The pattern observed here — RA and SLE patients showing 23% and 33% lower odds of HbA1c monitoring respectively — therefore points to a potential translational gap between specialty society guidance for comprehensive risk reduction and frontline clinical practice.

Cardiovascular screening practices

The reduced cardiometabolic screening we identified aligns with prior studies documenting suboptimal cardiovascular preventive care in autoimmune populations. A Canadian survey of rheumatologists by [41] found that while 91% believed SLE constitutes a major cardiovascular risk factor, 42% were not comfortable with cardiovascular risk assessment guidelines. Critically, 15% did not check blood pressure at every visit, 32% did not order cholesterol testing, and 34% did not screen for diabetes. Perhaps most concerning, 64% did not routinely inform family physicians about patients’ elevated cardiovascular risk—suggesting potential care coordination failures.

In the United States [40], analyzed Medicare claims and found that lipid testing rates among RA patients with key cardiovascular comorbidities remained below recommended levels [47]. compared cardiovascular risk factor management between RA patients and matched non-RA controls, documenting persistent gaps in screening and treatment [48] demonstrated that even among older RA patients with established cardiovascular risk factors, lipid-lowering therapy was underutilized.

The UK experience mirrors these findings [49] found that RA patients showed reduced adherence to secondary prevention pharmacotherapy even after myocardial infarction, suggesting that the monitoring gaps we identified may extend to treatment implementation as well.

The competing demands framework

The paradox of elevated inflammatory marker testing (OR 3.84–3.53 for any inflammatory marker in RA and SLE) alongside reduced cardiometabolic screening may reflect the “competing demands” phenomenon described in primary care literature [42]. When clinicians face limited consultation time and multiple clinical priorities, disease-focused monitoring may inadvertently crowd out preventive services. For RA and SLE patients, the immediacy of disease activity assessment—reflected in CRP and ESR ordering patterns (Fig. 6)—may take precedence over the more abstract goal of long-term cardiovascular risk reduction. One hypothesised contributor — which we tentatively term “inflammatory fatigue” in laboratory ordering — is a possible tendency to prioritise tests directly relevant to the known inflammatory diagnosis, with potential under-emphasis of metabolic and cardiovascular screening that is equally important for long-term outcomes. We present this as a generative hypothesis for qualitative research with primary care providers rather than as a mechanism established by the present data.

This interpretation is supported by [50], who identified key quality improvement challenges in rheumatology care, including delays in diagnosis, shortage of rheumatologists, and limited primary care awareness of disease-specific recommendations. The care fragmentation between specialty rheumatology care and primary care cardiovascular risk management may leave patients falling between jurisdictional boundaries.

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis: a different pattern

The distinct monitoring pattern observed in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis patients (n = 2,042) warrants separate consideration. Unlike RA and SLE, which are typically co-managed between rheumatology and primary care, thyroid disorders fall predominantly within the primary care domain. This organizational difference may explain the observed pattern of modestly increased cardiometabolic screening (HbA1c: OR 1.45) without the compensatory elevation in inflammatory markers seen in systemic autoimmune conditions.

The metabolic consequences of thyroid dysfunction—including dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance attributable to thyroid hormone effects on lipid and glucose metabolism—may prompt clinicians to order cardiometabolic tests as a routine component of thyroid disease management, independent of formal cardiovascular risk stratification. This contrasts with RA and SLE, where inflammatory disease monitoring may compete with preventive care delivery. From a clinical perspective, subclinical hypothyroidism—most commonly caused by Hashimoto’s thyroiditis—affects up to 10% of adults and has been associated with increased risk of heart failure, coronary artery disease events, and mortality from coronary heart disease [26].

Multimorbidity considerations

The UK Biobank analysis by [51] demonstrated that 75.7% of RA patients had multimorbidity, and those with RA plus four or more long-term conditions showed a three-fold increased risk of mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events. This multimorbidity burden should, in principle, prompt more intensive cardiometabolic monitoring rather than less. Our finding that even patients with multiple autoimmune conditions (n = 245) showed reduced cardiometabolic screening (Table 3) suggests that the monitoring gap persists regardless of overall disease complexity.

Clinical implications

For primary care providers

Our findings, consistent with international evidence, highlight the challenge that primary care clinicians face in providing comprehensive preventive care for patients with complex autoimmune diseases. When patients receive co-management from multiple specialists, assumptions about who bears responsibility for preventive services can lead to gaps—not through negligence, but through reasonable uncertainty about role boundaries.

For clinicians in shared-care arrangements, explicit communication with rheumatology colleagues about cardiovascular screening responsibility may help close potential gaps. The finding that cardiometabolic monitoring was appropriate in Hashimoto’s patients—who receive care predominantly within primary care—suggests that when role boundaries are clear, preventive care delivery is effective. The presence of frequent inflammatory marker testing should not be interpreted as adequate overall monitoring; disease activity assessment and cardiovascular risk evaluation serve distinct clinical purposes.

Second, the finding that cardiometabolic screening gaps persist even among the 7,756 diabetic patients (27.8% of the cohort) in our study (Table 5) suggests that existing diabetes management protocols may not adequately capture patients whose diabetes coexists with autoimmune disease. Enhanced attention to this intersection is warranted.

For healthcare systems

Quality improvement initiatives represent an opportunity to address a challenge that has proven difficult to solve across healthcare systems internationally. Automated reminders for HbA1c and lipid testing in patients with autoimmune disease diagnoses—similar to existing diabetes screening protocols—could serve as a safety net regardless of whether testing has occurred in external facilities. Such reminders would not duplicate testing inappropriately if systems include mechanisms to document external results.

Electronic health record-based clinical decision support could flag patients with RA, SLE, or other inflammatory conditions who have not received recommended cardiometabolic testing within guideline-specified intervals, while acknowledging that test results from external facilities may not be captured. Such a system-level approach frames the intervention as closing a documentation and coordination gap rather than correcting individual provider behaviour, and would be particularly valuable in shared-care settings where responsibility for preventive screening may be unclear.

Care coordination represents another opportunity. The finding by [41] that 64% of rheumatologists did not routinely communicate cardiovascular risk to primary care providers suggests that explicit role delineation—and systematic communication regarding which provider bears responsibility for cardiovascular risk factor monitoring—could improve care delivery.

For specialty-primary care interface

The monitoring gap paradox we identified reflects the broader challenge of integrating disease-specific specialty care with comprehensive primary care. While rheumatologists appropriately focus on disease activity and inflammatory burden, primary care providers must not assume that frequent specialty visits obviate the need for cardiovascular preventive services. Conversely, specialists should ensure that primary care colleagues are informed about patients’ elevated cardiovascular risk and the specific monitoring recommended by guidelines such as EULAR 2022 [35].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, the large sample size (N = 27,911) provided adequate statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences in monitoring patterns across autoimmune disease categories. The matched design, with 9,356 cases matched to 18,555 controls on age and sex across 9,356 matched sets, controlled for these fundamental confounders while preserving statistical efficiency.

Second, the comprehensive covariate adjustment—including comorbidities (diabetes in 27.8%, hypertension in 28.6%, dyslipidaemia in 27.3%), medication exposures (corticosteroids, immunosuppressants), and healthcare utilisation (total visits, specialist visits)—addressed potential confounding by disease severity and healthcare access. The persistence of findings across sensitivity analyses, including restriction to corticosteroid-naïve patients and diabetic subgroups, supports the robustness of the observed patterns.

Third, the primary care setting captures a broader population than specialty clinic-based studies, which may overrepresent patients with more severe disease or better access to care. The PHCC system provides comprehensive primary care coverage in Qatar, reducing selection bias inherent in studies relying on specialty referral populations.

Fourth, the examination of multiple autoimmune conditions within a unified analytic framework enables direct comparison across disease categories and identification of condition-specific monitoring patterns (Fig. 4).

Limitations

The most consequential limitation is potential differential misclassification of the outcomes. Laboratory tests performed outside the PHCC system — in private laboratories, hospital settings, and rheumatology specialty clinics — are not captured in our data and would be expected to occur disproportionately in patients with RA and SLE who receive co-management from specialists. If specialists or external providers order cardiometabolic tests that are not visible to us, our estimates would understate true monitoring rates in these groups specifically, and could in principle account for some or all of the observed gap. Several observations partially mitigate this concern: the monitoring gap persisted among patients with diabetes (RA: OR 0.50), a condition managed primarily within PHCC where HbA1c monitoring should occur regardless of rheumatology co-management; international studies with more comprehensive data capture — including linked hospital and primary care records — have documented similar patterns; and the distinctly different monitoring profile in Hashimoto’s patients, who receive care predominantly within primary care, suggests that the PHCC system functions effectively when not subject to shared-care arrangements. Nevertheless, future studies linking PHCC data with hospital, specialty clinic, and private-sector laboratory records are essential for unbiased estimates of total monitoring in autoimmune disease populations. A threshold analysis bounds, rather than excludes, this explanation: for unmeasured external testing to fully close the gap, 30.7% of untested RA and 36.4% of untested SLE patients would need cardiometabolic tests performed and recorded entirely outside PHCC, rising to 48.2% and 58.8%, respectively, within the diabetes subgroup. Although endocrinology services may indeed order HbA1c externally, magnitudes of this size are increasingly difficult to sustain as a complete explanation for a condition managed primarily within primary care.

Further limitations warrant acknowledgment. The cross-sectional design establishes association but cannot determine causality. We cannot distinguish whether reduced cardiometabolic testing reflects provider decision-making, patient preferences, system-level factors, or clinically appropriate decisions based on information not captured in the electronic health record.

Autoimmune disease diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes without external validation against established classification criteria (e.g., ACR/EULAR criteria for RA or SLE). This approach may introduce misclassification if diagnoses were entered incorrectly or if patients with suspected but unconfirmed disease were coded as having definite diagnoses. However, such misclassification would likely bias results toward the null by diluting true case-control differences.

We lacked measures of disease activity or severity, such as disease activity scores (DAS28 for RA, SLEDAI for SLE), inflammatory marker levels at baseline, or joint damage assessments. Disease severity could influence both the intensity of specialty care and the likelihood of receiving comprehensive preventive services, representing a potential unmeasured confounder.

We assessed whether testing occurred but not whether testing was clinically appropriate. Some patients may have had recent normal results obviating the need for repeat testing, while others may have had results from external laboratories not captured in the PHCC electronic health record system.

Generalisability of findings to other populations and healthcare contexts requires cautious interpretation. Qatar’s healthcare system, demographic composition (predominantly female at 74.4%), and disease prevalence patterns may differ from other settings. The predominantly expatriate population and universal healthcare coverage context may not translate directly to other Middle Eastern countries or to healthcare systems with different organisational structures. Our findings are therefore robustly generalisable only to comparable Gulf Cooperation Council primary-care settings with universal access and a high expatriate share. Apparent concordance with North American and European studies provides supportive context for the hypothesis but should not be read as evidence that the magnitude of the association transports to those systems; such extrapolation is speculative.

We could not assess the quality or follow-up of abnormal test results. A patient receiving HbA1c testing but without appropriate intervention for an elevated result would appear equivalent in our analysis to a patient receiving testing with appropriate follow-up.

Finally, despite comprehensive covariate adjustment, unmeasured confounding remains possible. Factors such as patient health literacy, provider training, or clinic-level resources could influence both autoimmune disease management patterns and laboratory ordering behaviour.

We were also unable to identify the type of provider who ordered each laboratory test. Whether tests were requested by family medicine physicians or specialist providers cannot be determined from the available data. The diffusion of responsibility phenomenon—wherein primary care providers assume that rheumatologists are handling cardiometabolic screening while rheumatologists assume that primary care providers address this need—is a clinically plausible mechanism for the observed monitoring gap, but cannot be directly tested without linked primary and specialty care records. Future studies with provider-attributed ordering data would enable direct assessment of this hypothesis.

Socioeconomic factors including educational attainment, health literacy, and occupational status are not recorded in the PHCC electronic health record and could not be included as covariates. These factors are recognised determinants of both healthcare utilisation and uptake of preventive services and represent a potentially important source of residual confounding. Their absence limits our ability to determine whether the observed monitoring differential reflects disease- or care-related factors, patient-level factors related to socioeconomic position, or some combination of these.

Our operationalisation of monitoring outcomes is a coarse measure. We defined laboratory monitoring as receipt of at least one test over the ten-year observation window (ever versus never tested), which captures any exposure to screening but does not reflect guideline-recommended frequency: a single lipid panel recorded once in ten years is counted identically to annual testing. Our measure therefore likely overestimates the proportion of patients receiving guideline-concordant monitoring and cannot distinguish adequate from inadequate longitudinal care as defined by guidelines such as the EULAR recommendation for annual cardiovascular risk assessment in RA. Reduced monitoring under this lenient ever-versus-never definition implies that gaps in guideline-concordant care would be at least as large. Future studies should operationalise monitoring outcomes as the proportion of patients meeting annual or biennial testing targets to enable more precise quality-of-care assessment. Frequency-based metrics (tests per year, the proportion of patients receiving repeat testing, or the proportion of observation-years with a test) would more directly capture sustained, guideline-concordant monitoring, but could not be computed here: the governance-approved extract records only the presence and most recent value of each test together with a single combined laboratory record count, and does not contain per-test dated results. The binary measure therefore functions as a conservative lower bound, and quantifying the shortfall in annual monitoring is a priority for future work using line-level laboratory data.

Future directions

Future research should address several questions raised by our findings. Longitudinal studies could examine whether monitoring gaps translate into adverse cardiovascular outcomes—testing the hypothesis that reduced cardiometabolic screening contributes to the excess cardiovascular morbidity observed in autoimmune populations. Implementation research could evaluate interventions designed to close monitoring gaps, including electronic health record-based reminders, care coordination protocols, and patient education initiatives.

Qualitative research exploring provider perspectives could illuminate the decision-making processes underlying the observed patterns—whether providers consciously prioritise disease activity monitoring over cardiovascular screening, perceive cardiometabolic assessment as another provider’s responsibility, or face time constraints that necessitate prioritisation.

Finally, extension to other autoimmune conditions not examined in our study—including psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and others—could determine whether the monitoring gap paradox generalises beyond the conditions we examined.

Conclusions

In this matched case-control study of 27,911 participants in primary care in Qatar, patients with rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus had lower odds of recorded cardiometabolic laboratory monitoring (HbA1c, lipid panel) than matched controls, alongside substantially higher inflammatory-marker testing. Hashimoto’s thyroiditis patients, managed predominantly within primary care, showed the opposite pattern. We observed associations that are consistent with, but do not prove, care-coordination gaps at the specialty–primary care interface; differential capture of tests performed outside PHCC is an equally plausible explanation. Consistency with studies from other healthcare systems provides supportive context but does not establish that the magnitude of these associations transports beyond comparable Gulf Cooperation Council primary-care settings. Linked specialty, hospital and private-laboratory data and provider-attributed ordering records are required to estimate true monitoring and before any quality-improvement intervention is warranted.
