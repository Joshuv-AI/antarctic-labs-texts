First record of Macrobilharzia (Digenea: Schistosomatidae) from Phalacrocorax lucidus in South Africa, with molecular evidence suggesting a distinct species
Authors: Ketumile H. Thobejane, Pieter H. King, Zamantungwa T.H. Mnisi, Nehemiah M. Rindoria, Willem J. Smit, Collins N. Mashilwane, Francois Roux, Wilmien J. Luus-Powell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schistosomatid flukes are mainly found in the vascular system of their definitive hosts. Avian schistosomatids, represented by 13 genera, are blood parasites affecting snails and aquatic birds in their larval and adult stages, respectively, with the genus Macrobilharzia Travassos, 1922 being a poorly documented clade, with limited records globally. During a parasitological investigation, adult schistosomatids (prevalence = 57%) were recovered from the intestinal mesenteric veins of white-breasted cormorants (Phalacrocorax lucidus; n = 14), sampled during July 2024 and March 2025 at Loskop Dam, Mpumalanga Province, South Africa. These flukes were identified as Macrobilharzia sp. based on morphological and molecular characterisation. Males are large and flat, measuring up to 28 mm in length, with a maximum width of 2.2 mm in the first quarter of the body. The gynaecophoric canal extends from the ventral sucker to the end of the body, and the ventral sucker is larger than the oral sucker. The oesophagus bifurcates into caeca in front of the ventral sucker and extends to the posterior end of the body, uniting to form the common caecum close to the posterior end of the worm. The number of testes varies in size, ranging from 104 to 184. The 28S rDNA gene sequence generated from this study (accession number: PX973526) showed a 99% homogeneity with Schistosomatidae sp. (KF672860) from Indoplanorbis exustus in Nepal, and 94% homogeneity with Macrobilharzia macrobilharzia (AY858885) from Anhinga anhinga in North America. The phylogenetic analyses in the current study positioned the specimen in a well-supported Macrobilharzia clade, forming a distinct lineage from M. macrobilharzia. This constitutes the first documented record of the genus from southern Africa and the first report from P. lucidus. The observed molecular divergence, together with its phylogenetic placement, suggests that the material may represent a distinct species within the genus. The record extends the known geographical distribution of the Macrobilharzia genus, highlighting significant gaps in knowledge regarding avian schistosomatid diversity in African aquatic ecosystems. These findings contribute to baseline parasite biodiversity data and emphasise the need for integrative surveys (morphological and molecular characterisation) to better understand host–parasite dynamics in these piscivorous birds and associated ecosystems.

Introduction

The family Schistosomatidae Stiles and Hassall, 1898, comprises 17 genera parasitising both mammalian and avian hosts (Loker et al., 2022). Avian schistosomatids dominate this family, with 13 genera (Allobilharzia, Anserobilharzia, Austrobilharzia, Bilharziella, Dendritobilharzia, Gigantobilharzia, Jilinobilharzia, Macrobilharzia, Marinabilharzia, Nasusbilharzia, Ornithobilharzia, Trichobilharzia, and Riverabilharzia) and 85 described species (Loker et al., 2022). Trichobilharzia is the most speciose genus with about 40 described species (Blair and Islam, 1983; Ashrafi and Brant, 2020), whereas Macrobilharzia is among the least represented genera with only two known species. These schistosomatids are characterised by their dioecious nature, distinct sexual dimorphism in their adult stage, and have a two-host life cycle involving a snail intermediate host and a vertebrate definitive host, where cercariae penetrate the definitive host's epithelial surface (Horak and Kolářová, 2011; Brant et al., 2013a).

The genus Macrobilharzia was first described by Travassos (1922) in Brazil based on one male specimen from the darter (Suliformes, Anhingidae: Anhinga anhinga), with the description of Macrobilharzia macrobilharzia (Travassos, 1922). Later, Kohn (1964) documented M. macrobilharzia in the same bird host, from Louisiana, North America, providing drawings and descriptions of both male and female worms. Subsequently, Fain (1955) described Macrobilharzia baeri in reed cormorants (Suliformes, Phalacrocoracidae: Microcarbo africanus), from Rwanda, and more recently, Abro et al. (2016) reported M. macrobilharzia from the intestinal portal vein of the little cormorant (Suliformes, Phalacrocoracidae: Phalacrocorax niger), in Pakistan. However, these descriptions rely only on morphological characterisation of adult worms.

Regarding molecular characterisation, only a single Macrobilharzia sequence is currently available in GenBank (AY858885), generated from the 28S rDNA gene by Brant et al. (2006). This latter study primarily aimed to establish reference molecular data linked to blood fluke life-cycle stages across multiple geographical regions. The molecular data for Macrobilharzia were derived from nine female specimens collected from two A. anhinga hosts; however, detailed morphological characterisation and morphometric measurements were not reported, limiting morphological-molecular integration.

Carmichael (1984) conducted a cladistic analysis, which, despite limitations in data, revealed that Macrobilharzia “is poorly understood, and it retains more primitive morphological characters than any other schistosome genus”. This characterisation remains relevant today, highlighting the considerable gap in our current understanding of Macrobilharzia. Its retention of primitive features highlights the evolutionary significance of the genus, making comprehensive morphology and molecular characterisation particularly important for resolving schistosomatid phylogeny.

Expanding upon Carmichael (1984) observation, the relatively understudied nature of Macrobilharzia can be attributed to several factors. Its host specificity for suliform birds, which are often overlooked in parasitological surveys, limits opportunities for collection (Ebbs et al., 2022). Additionally, associations with protected or rare vertebrate hosts restrict specimen availability (Lockyer et al., 2003). From a molecular perspective, the genus has limited representation in sequence databases, which has historically impeded its inclusion and resolution in broader schistosomatid phylogenies, as noted in early molecular analyses (Snyder and Loker, 2000). Together, these biological and logistical constraints would explain why Macrobilharzia remains poorly characterised despite its evolutionary importance.

As mentioned, only two species of Macrobilharzia have been documented to date with darters and cormorants identified as the definitive hosts (Devkota et al., 2014a), while there is a notable absence of records regarding their snail hosts. In contrast, avian schistosomatids have been found to broadly utilise at least 15 snail families from freshwater and marine habitats as intermediate hosts (Brant and Loker, 2013), highlighting the knowledge gap specific to the life cycle of Macrobilharzia (Brant and Loker, 2013; Devkota et al., 2014a).

Thus, this study aimed to expand our understanding of the Macrobilharzia genus by examining white-breasted cormorants (Suliformes, Phalacrocoracidae: Phalacrocorax lucidus) from Loskop Dam, Mpumalanga Province, South Africa, using an integrative approach to characterise these worms and infer their evolutionary relationships within Schistosomatidae family.

Methods and materials

Host collection

Fourteen white-breasted cormorants were collected during July 2024 and March 2025 (University of Limpopo Animal Research Ethics Committee ethics clearance number AREC/32/2024:PG; permits MPB5857 and MPB5947) from Loskop Dam (25°26′57.05″ S, 29°19′44.36″ E), within the Loskop Nature Reserve in Mpumalanga Province, South Africa (Fig. 1). Following collection, the specimens were transported to the field laboratory at Loskop Dam for parasitological examination.Fig. 1South Africa and the location of Loskop Nature Reserve, within which Loskop Dam is situated.Fig. 1

Dissection and parasite collection from the hosts

The white-breasted cormorant specimens were dissected to access the viscera following the protocol by Justine et al. (2012). These organs were removed and temporarily placed in separated petri dishes containing distilled water. Upon opening the organs, they were then transferred to saline and examined for parasites using a Leica EZ4 stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The recovered parasites were relaxed in saline, fixed, and preserved in 70% ethanol (hereafter as EtOH) for light microscopy and 96% EtOH for molecular characterisation.

Morphological identification of parasites

Light microscopy

Specimens which were fixed in 70% EtOH were stained in Van Cleave's haematoxylin, routinely prepared and mounted on slides using DPX mountant. Photomicrographs were taken using an Olympus BX53 compound microscope with Olympus Soft Imaging Solutions (Olympus, Münster, Germany). All measurements were made using the NIS-Elements AR imaging (Nikon, Japan) software programme. Morphological measurements are expressed in micrometres (μm), unless otherwise stated, and are presented as follows: minimum to maximum values, followed by the average values between parentheses.

Infection parameters

Prevalence was calculated according to Bush et al. (1997).

Molecular identification

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

DNA was extracted from two parasite samples that were previously characterised through a morphological approach to confirm they are representatives of this putative new Macrobilharzia sp. using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), adhering to the manufacturer's protocol, except that samples were eluted with 80 μl of buffer to increase DNA concentration. The extracted DNA was then transferred to a sterile microcentrifuge tube and stored at 4 °C until further analysis. Nested Polymerase chain reaction (nested PCR) was conducted to amplify the 28S rDNA gene, utilising outer primers: Dig12 (F); 5′-AAG CAT ATC ACT AAG CGG-3′ and 1500(R); 5′-GCT ATC CTG AGG GAA ACT TCG-3′ for first amplification round (Tkach et al., 2003), and for second amplification round the internal primers 300(F); 5′-CAA GTA CCG TGA GGG AAA GTT G-3′ and ECD2(R); 5′-CTT GGT CCG TGT TTC AAG ACG GG-3′(Littlewood et al., 2000; Tkach et al., 2003). The PCR was performed in a total volume of 20 μl containing of 10 μl NEB OneTaq 2X MasterMix with Standard Buffer, 1 μl Genomic DNA (10-30 ng/μl), 1 μl of 10 μM Forward primer, 1 μl of 10 μM Reverse primer, and 7 μl Nuclease free water. The thermocycling conditions were modified from Tkach et al. (2003) as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 68 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 68 °C for 10 min. The integrity of the PCR amplicons was visualized on a 1% agarose gel (CSL-AG500, Cleaver Scientific Ltd) stained with EZ-vision® Bluelight DNA Dye. The nested PCR amplicons were submitted to Inqaba Biotechnical Industries (Pty) Ltd in Pretoria, South Africa for Sanger sequencing.

Phylogenetic analysis

DNA sequence editing and consensus sequence generation were performed using BioEdit version 7.2 (Hall, 1999). The resulting consensus sequences were then subjected to the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (Altschul et al., 1990) for analysis to identify homologous sequences in the GenBank database, which were subsequently retrieved for comparative analysis and phylogenetic tree construction (Table 1). Sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW in MEGA 12.0 (Kumar et al., 2024), and the ends of the alignment were trimmed to the shortest sequence. The maximum-likelihood phylogram was constructed in MEGA12 using the Tamura-Nei Model, which was selected as the best-fit substitution model for the 28S rDNA region. Nodal support for maximum likelihood analysis was obtained through 1000 bootstrap replicates. The Bayesian Inference (BI) analysis was performed in Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis Sampling Trees (BEAST) v2.7.7 (Bouckaert et al., 2014) by implementing a data block criterion running two independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains for 10 million generations. The first 50% of the sample generations were discarded as burnin. Phylogenetic trees generated were visualized in FigTree v1.4.4. (Rambaut, 2018). The tree encompassed 15 nucleotide sequences with 1083 positions in the final dataset. Initially, two sequences generated in this study (PX973525 and PX973526) were included in the phylogenetic analysis. Sequence PX973525 comprised 843 bp, whereas PX973526 was longer 1072 bp. To maximise sequence coverage and improve phylogenetic resolution, only PX973526 was retained in the final tree.

Results

During this study, eight birds were infected with Macrobilharzia sp., representing a prevalence of 57%. A total of 41 parasites (comprising 32 males and 9 females) were recovered from the intestinal mesenteric veins. However, some recovered specimens were incomplete or damaged, and as a result, morphometric measurements were only obtained from 15 mature male and 4 mature female parasites.

Molecular identification

The 28S rDNA gene sequences, PX973525, 843 bp, and PX973526, 1072 bp, showed a 97% and 99% homogeneity, respectively, with the Schistosomatidae sp. (KF672860) sequence, which was identified from Indoplanorbis exustus (Gastropoda, Bulinidae) in Nepal by Devkota et al. (2014b). Additionally, the sequences showed a 93% and 95% homogeneity, respectively, with M. macrobilharzia (AY858885), identified from A. anhinga in North America by Brant et al. (2006).

Phylogenetic analyses using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) yielded highly similar tree topologies. The ML tree is presented (Fig. 3), with both ML bootstrap values and BI posterior probabilities indicated at the nodes to show branch support. Analyses based on the 15 nucleotide sequences (Table 1), including ingroup and outgroup taxa, resulted in a topology comprising three major clades: Clade 1-2 avian schistosomatids; and Clade 3, mammalian schistosomatids. The first avian schistosomatid clade comprised multiple genera and is resolved as a well-supported monophyletic group (ML = 100%; BI = 1.0). The mammalian schistosomatids formed a distinct and well-supported clade (ML = 100%; BI = 1.0), which is resolved as basal relative to the avian schistosomatid lineages in the present analysis. The Macrobilharzia sp. specimen obtained in this study (PX973526) grouped closely with the Schistosomatidae sp. sequence (KF672860). This sequence, however, was identified only to family level from a cercarial stage, and its taxonomic identity remains unsolved. In contrast, the previously published M. macrobilharzia sequence (AY858885) formed a comparatively long branch within this clade. Phylogenetic analyses resolved Macrobilharzia as a monophyletic lineage showing early divergence within the avian schistosomatids, sister to the other remaining avian genera, including Trichobilharzia, Anserobilharzia, Dendritobilharzia, Ornithobilharzia, and Gigantobilharzia. The overall topology remains consistent with previous molecular studies (e.g. Brant et al., 2006, 2013a; Devkota et al., 2014a; Loker et al., 2022).

Discussion

The present study confirms the occurrence of Macrobilharzia sp. in the white-breasted cormorant from Loskop Dam, Mpumalanga Province, South Africa.

Morphological examination of the parasites in the current study revealed characteristics consistent with previous descriptions of M. macrobilharzia by Travassos (1922), Kohn (1964), and Abro et al. (2016). Although the number of testes in our specimens (104-182) differed slightly from the original description (230-250) by Travassos (1922), similar discrepancy in testes number have been observed in other studies. Kohn (1964) documented 210-250, and Abro et al. (2016) recorded 120-122 testes for M. macrobilharzia. This difference may reflect biological factors such as worm size, host physiology, environmental suitability, or geographical variation as suggested by Carmichael (1984), and the issue that it could be a different species. While the precise cause of these differences remains unclear, the overall morphology of our specimens, including diagnostic characters, site of infection, and host association, aligns closely with the definition of the genus.

A high prevalence (57%) of Macrobilharzia sp. in the examined P. lucidus population and the presence of ovigerous female worms suggest that this parasite is not an accidental infection. This indicates the presence of a suitable intermediate snail host at Loskop Dam and surrounding areas, as the cormorants at Loskop Dam are local migrators. The study from Nepal (Schistosomatidae sp., KF672860) recorded a very low prevalence, with only 1 out of 5471 I. exustus (snails) collected, found to be shedding avian schistosomatid cercariae (Devkota et al., 2014b). According to these authors infections with larval avian schistosomatids are uncommon in Nepal, even though the country is known for its rich avian diversity. The authors suggested that the migratory behaviour of wetland birds facilitates the dispersal of these parasites, indicating that infections of avian schistosomatids are not necessarily restricted to Nepal. The high prevalence in the current study suggests that there is high potential for a widespread distribution of Macrobilharzia than previously documented. Although the intermediate hosts remain unknown, this may be facilitated by the distribution of potential intermediate hosts, including some snails of the Bulinidae family and possibly other related taxa. For example, I. exustus has been reported to host schistosomatid cercariae, which are responsible for cercarial dermatitis (Nithiuthai et al., 2004; Wooi et al., 2007; Horák et al., 2015). A Macrobilharzia-like, Schistosomatidae sp., which formed a clade with the Macrobilharzia sequence from the present study, has been recorded from this snail. Lastly some members of Bulinidae are already known to host schistosomatids (Horák et al., 2015, 2024). Given that this family is widely distributed across Africa (Morgan et al., 2002; Sil et al., 2022), this further supports the likelihood that suitable intermediate hosts are available within the region. These findings highlight the need for further studies to identify the intermediate host(s) and fully elucidate the life cycle of the genus, which would improve understanding of its ecology and distribution.

Molecular analysis of the 28S rDNA gene provided additional insights into the phylogenetic placement of the specimen. The sequence obtained in this study (PX973526) showed the highest similarity (99%) to the Schistosomatidae sp. sequence (KF672860) from Nepal, which was identified only to the family level by Devkota et al. (2014b). In that study they could not identify the genus of the cercarial type because cercarial morphology alone was insufficient for genus-level identification and molecular reference were limited. Molecular analysis revealed relatively high genetic divergence from the only GenBank sequence of M. macrobilharzia, with 28S rDNA showing 4.9% and COX1 showing 20.5% sequence divergence, highlighting the limitation of genus level identification for this cercarial type.

Furthermore, the sequence obtained from the present study (PX973526) shared a slightly lower identity (95%) with M. macrobilharzia (AY858885) from A. anhinga in North America (Brant et al., 2006) in comparison to Schistosomatidae sp. This divergence was also reported between Schistosomatidae sp. (KF672860) and M. macrobilharzia, suggesting the presence of genetically distinct lineages within the clade (Devkota et al., 2014b).

The close relationship between the sequence obtained in this study (PX973526) and Schistosomatidae sp. (KF672860), together with its phylogenetic placement within the Macrobilharzia clade, suggests that both sequences may represent an undescribed taxon of this genus.

Conclusion

The detection of Macrobilharzia sp. in P. lucidus at Loskop Dam highlights the need for continued integrative research on avian schistosomatids in southern Africa. This study provides a morphological and molecular baseline for future investigations into the life cycle, ecology, and evolutionary relationships of Macrobilharzia in the region.
