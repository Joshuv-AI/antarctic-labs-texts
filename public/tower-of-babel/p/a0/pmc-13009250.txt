Development and preclinical results of a transcatheter aortic valve implant with novel polymeric leaflets
Authors: J Ryan Stanfield, Garrett Johnson, Nelson Belais, Isaac George, Steven Yakubov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) systems use glutaraldehyde-fixed tissue leaflets, which are prone to structural valve degeneration. Polymeric valves may improve durability, hemocompatibility, and manufacturability. We designed, produced, and evaluated a self-expanding nitinol-framed TAVI device with siloxane poly(urethane-urea) (SiPUU) leaflets and an electrospun SiPUU skirt. Bench testing included frame fatigue to 200 million cycles, hydrodynamics per ISO 5840-3, and flow visualization using particle image velocimetry. Biocompatibility was assessed per ISO 10993. Preclinical evaluation involved nine ovine implants with serial imaging and histology to 90 days. In vitro tests showed EOAs of 1.3–1.5 cm² and 1.7–2.1 cm² (21 and 24 mm annulus, respectively, simulating under expansion), regurgitant fraction <6%, and preserved coaptation. Six animals completed follow-up with stable hemodynamics, no hemolysis, no PVL at implant, and no calcification at explant. The SiPUU-based TAVI demonstrated favorable durability, hydrodynamics, and biocompatibility, supporting further testing and delivery refinement toward first-in-human evaluation. Introduction

Aortic valve disease is a progressive and life-threatening illness traditionally managed with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). Mechanical valves offer excellent long-term durability and hemodynamics, while bioprosthetic valves provide improved quality of life and do not require lifelong anticoagulation; however, both require open-heart surgery, limiting treatment options for elderly and high-risk patients. The advent of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has transformed the management of aortic valve disease by enabling percutaneous valve deployment without the need for cardiopulmonary bypass1,2.

The origination and evolution of TAVI is well documented in the scientific literature3–6. Clinical trials have established the non-inferiority and, in some cases, superiority of TAVI to surgical replacement in high- and intermediate-risk patients, leading to regulatory approval for low-risk patient populations and expansion into younger cohorts7–9. Despite these successes, current TAVI systems rely on glutaraldehyde-fixed bovine or porcine pericardial tissue leaflets, which remain susceptible to structural valve degeneration (SVD), calcification, and limited durability, particularly in younger and more active patients10,11. These limitations have driven the development of polymeric leaflet TAVI valves, which aim to combine the durability and fatigue resistance of synthetic materials with the physiologic hemodynamics and low thrombogenicity of bioprosthetic valves12–18.

A variety of polymeric transcatheter valve technologies — using materials such as polyurethanes (PU), expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), and poly(styrene-isobutylene-styrene) (SIBS) — are currently under investigation, with multiple groups reporting promising in vitro and preclinical performance12–23. Comprehensive reviews of these polymeric TAVI development efforts, encompassing diverse material platforms, device architectures, and early feasibility data, are available in the recent scientific literature12–23. Collectively, the evolution from tissue-based TAVI systems to polymeric valve technologies represents the next frontier in structural heart intervention. If ongoing preclinical studies and forthcoming clinical trials confirm long-term durability and resistance to calcification, polymeric TAVI valves may extend device lifespans, reduce the need for reintervention, and enable expansion of transcatheter therapy into younger and lower-risk populations.

The design of a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) device—whether bioprosthetic or polymeric—must fulfill stringent mechanical, hydrodynamic, and biocompatibility criteria to ensure safety and long-term performance in the dynamic aortic environment. ISO 5840-3:2021 specifies the requirements for transcatheter prosthetic heart valves, including in vitro hydrodynamic performance (EOA, regurgitant fraction, and mean pressure gradient), accelerated wear testing (AWT) to a minimum of 200 million cycles, component fatigue resistance, and biocompatibility per ISO 1099324–30. In addition, device design must accommodate anatomical variability, allow for secure anchoring in the native annulus without excessive radial force, optimize for coronary ostia clearance, and minimize the risk of paravalvular leakage (PVL). Frame design is crucial to optimize for the variability in the aortic valve anatomy. Leaflet geometry and material processing can be tailored to reduce crimping damage, preserve leaflet integrity, and mitigate calcification. Delivery systems should enable atraumatic navigation through the vasculature, precise deployment, and repositionability or retrieval where applicable. These requirements are underpinned by robust preclinical testing—both in vitro and in appropriate animal models—prior to initiation of clinical evaluation7,31.

For polymeric TAVI devices, additional considerations challenge the design and development due to the unique properties and long-term behavior of the leaflet material. Polymeric leaflets should demonstrate mechanical properties closely matching those of native valve tissue to ensure physiological hemodynamics, while exhibiting exceptional fatigue resistance to withstand hundreds of millions (ultimately billions) of opening and closing cycles over the intended lifetime. The polymer should also be inherently resistant to calcification, oxidative degradation, and hydrolysis, with surface and bulk chemistries tailored to minimize thrombogenicity without reliance on chronic anticoagulation. Manufacturing processes that yield reproducible leaflet geometry and material consistency, with tight control over thickness, modulus, and anisotropy to avoid stress concentrations are highly desirable and a potential key advantage over tissue processing. Furthermore, polymer-to-frame integration—including methods for bonding, sewing, or overmolding—needs to maintain structural integrity under crimping, deployment, and long-term cyclic loading. These requirements, coupled with ISO 5840-3 performance benchmarks, guide the development of next-generation polymeric TAVI platforms with the potential for enhanced durability, low-profile delivery, and scalable manufacturing20,22,32–34.

AorTech was one of the pioneers in the development of polymeric heart valves using Elast‑Eon™, a biostable siloxane‑polyurethane copolymer designed to improve durability and resistance to calcification compared with early‑generation polyurethane valves35. Elast‑Eon™ has a lengthy and well‑established clinical history in implantable medical devices, including long‑term use in pacemaker leads, neurostimulation devices, and vascular access catheters, demonstrating excellent biostability and hemocompatibility36,37. The Elast-Eon™ platform served as a technological precursor to the Foldax TRIA valve, the latter of which employs next‑generation LifePolymer™ (SiPUU) leaflets to enhance mechanical performance, hemocompatibility, and long‑term durability38. Our initial approach for proof-of-concept favored a self-expanding valve configuration to address many of the unknowns in polymeric TAVI development. A portion of this early work was presented at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics (TCT) conference39,40. We now present details of the device design and development, including in vitro hydrodynamics, biocompatibility, and chronic animal studies following good laboratory practices (GLP).

Results

Nitinol frame fatigue testing

All tested nitinol frames successfully completed 200 million cycles without evidence of fracture, crack initiation, or gross deformation. Dimensional analysis at the end of testing showed no observable change in frame geometry, confirming shape-memory retention and structural stability. Surface inspection revealed no fretting, corrosion, or polymer delamination from the SiPUU-coated frame. These findings indicate that the nitinol frame design exhibits robust fatigue resistance under worst-case strain conditions for 200 million cycles; however, additional testing to 400 million cycles is required to fully demonstrate compliance with the ISO 5840 durability requirement.

In vitro hydrodynamic performance

Five (n = 5) 27 mm TAVI valves underwent pulsatile-flow hydrodynamic evaluation per ISO 5840-3:2021. Testing was performed in a Vivitro Labs pulse duplicator system at a cardiac output of 5 L/min and 70 bpm. EOA was 1.3–1.5 cm² in a 21 mm annulus, and 1.7-2.1 cm2 in 24 mm annulus. Mean Pressure Gradient (ΔP) was 15–20 mmHg in 21 mm annulus, and 9-12 mmHg in 24 mm annulus. Correspondingly, the regurgitant fraction was 3–6%, within ISO 5840-3 thresholds for clinically acceptable valve competence. Hydrodynamic results are summarized in Table 1. As an example, a regurgitation fraction of 5% in the 24 mm annulus corresponds with a closing volume of 1.5 mL, a leakage volume of 2.2 mL, and a forward flow volume of 74 mL. Deployment of the nominal 26 mm inflow ID valve in a 21 mm annulus results in an approximate 41% reduction in inflow cross-sectional area relative to free expansion, and approximately 25% reduction compared to the 24 mm configuration. The observed elevation in mean gradient is therefore consistent with geometric constraint rather than intrinsic leaflet dysfunction. The valve demonstrated stable hemodynamics, with acceptable transvalvular gradients and minimal regurgitation, consistent with the target design objectives of the SiPUU leaflet and skirt system.

Residence time analysis revealed that commissure regions consistently exhibited higher particle residence percentages than leaflet AOIs, indicating potential sites of thrombosis. The fixed 21 mm annulus demonstrated the highest particle residence, while the 24 mm annulus at 5 L/min cardiac output showed the lowest residence, reflecting the benefit of higher flow rates on washout. In the out-of-round annulus, asymmetric flow patterns emerged; notably, the “weak” leaflet (defined by a reduced commissure-to-commissure distance) and its opposite exhibited longer residence times and restricted central flow, producing a tighter systolic jet.

The velocity magnitude plots represent cycle-averaged data across systole and diastole for a single representative cardiac cycle, not a single time point; the averaging includes both forward and reverse flow phases, with systolic forward flow dominating the magnitude distribution. Additional analyses demonstrated smooth acceleration of flow through the central orifice, with gradual deceleration downstream. Elevated shear was defined as velocity gradients >0.5 m/s per mm within the PIV field of view, consistent with thresholds reported in prior TAVI hemodynamic studies42,43. Under these criteria, high-gradient regions were limited to the central jet and leaflet tips, while commissural regions exhibited low gradients, indicating minimal shear-related thrombogenic risk. In diastole, particle image velocimetry indicated complete coaptation of the leaflets with only minor peripheral recirculation zones, and no evidence of central leakage. These results suggest that the valve geometry effectively promotes physiological flow patterns, minimizes turbulent structures, and reduces regions of high shear that could otherwise contribute to thrombogenic risk.

Average velocity magnitude increased proportionally with cardiac output. Leaflet AOIs generally demonstrated higher velocities than commissure AOIs, except in asymmetric configurations where jet vectoring redirected flow towards specific lobes of the polymeric valve. Biocompatibility

All device components met ISO 10993 biological safety acceptance criteria (Table 3). The device extracts were noncytotoxic, nonsensitizing, and nonirritant, with no observable systemic toxicity in either acute or subacute systemic-toxicity evaluations. Hemolysis testing yielded a hemolytic index <2%, classifying the device as non-hemolytic. Complement activation remained within the acceptable range, with SC5b-9 concentrations comparable to negative controls. Genotoxicity assays showed no evidence of mutagenicity in either bacterial or mammalian cell systems. No material-mediated pyrogenic response was detected, and intracutaneous irritation scores remained within the negative-control range in all animals tested.

Preclinical study in ovine model

A total of nine animals underwent transcatheter implantation of the 27 mm polymer TAVI valve following completion of the model creation phase. Three of nine animals experienced subannular implantation with immediate valve migration after full release from the delivery system. These early procedural failures were attributed to the learning curve for precise valve deployment in the ovine model and were terminated immediately post-procedure.

The remaining six animals achieved successful valve deployment at the target implantation depth, confirmed via fluoroscopy by alignment of the midpoint of the sealing skirt to the native annulus (Fig. 5). The active flexion feature of the delivery system allowed fine control of deployment position. Coronary access remained unobstructed, confirmed by post-deployment angiography, and no PVL was observed on Day 0 in any animal.

Serial transthoracic echocardiography was performed pre-implant (i.e., after annuloplasty ring/root graft creation and before TAVI) and on post-implant days 0, 6, 14, 30, 60, and 90. The annuloplasty ring and aortic root elongation procedures produced an elevated baseline aortic gradient at the post‑annuloplasty, pre‑implant timepoint, after which gradients were lower and remained stable following TAVI (Fig. 6). Relative to the post‑annuloplasty, pre‑implant baseline, EOA decreased immediately after valve implantation (Day 0) and returned toward baseline by Day 6, remaining stable through Day 90 (Fig. 6). This pattern occurred with preserved leaflet motion and persistently low mean gradients on serial echocardiography. Mean transvalvular gradients decreased from the early post-implant period to 90 days, consistent with stabilization of leaflet function and adaptation to the surgically modified annulus. Aortic central regurgitation was observed as none to trace levels via echocardiography over 90 days. Angiography confirmed coronary artery patency at baseline, post-implant, and Day 90. No pop-off or obstruction events were observed; coronary flow remained normal throughout. These findings confirm that the polymer TAVI valve maintained competent forward flow and favorable hemodynamics over the 90-day follow-up.

Discussion

This study presents the bench and preclinical evaluation of a novel 27 mm self-expanding nitinol TAVI valve incorporating siloxane-based poly(urethane-urea) (SiPUU, LifePolymer) leaflets, LifePolymer-filled frame cells, and an external electrospun LifePolymer sealing skirt. The combination of synthetic polymeric leaflets and skirt is intended to enhance durability, reduce thrombogenicity, and promote tissue integration, while maintaining physiologic hemodynamics. Our results demonstrate that this polymeric TAVI platform achieves favorable hydrodynamic performance in vitro, stable valve function in an ovine model, and excellent biocompatibility with minimal tissue reaction over 90 days.

The self-expanding nitinol frame underwent high-frequency pulsatile fatigue testing to 200 million cycles, simulating approximately five years of physiologic loading. No fractures, crack initiation, or gross deformation were observed, and dimensional analysis confirmed stable frame geometry. The absence of fretting, corrosion, or polymer delamination indicates robust biostability of the frame–polymer interface. These fatigue results should be interpreted in the context of the boundary condition determination and FEA-based strain analysis that informed the experimental setup. The applied deflections were selected to challenge the fatigue life of the nitinol frame under worst-case strain conditions predicted by computational modeling.

The in vitro hydrodynamic evaluation confirmed that the polymer TAVI valve meets ISO 5840-3:2021 performance criteria for transcatheter heart valves, with an EOA > 1.05 cm² (21 mm annulus) and >1.35 cm² (24 mm annulus), and regurgitant fraction 20% across physiologic flow ranges. These parameters compare on par with current tissue-based TAVI valves, which typically demonstrate EOAs in the range of 1.6–2.3 cm² and mean gradients for comparable diameters44. With mild under-expansion (24 mm fixture), mean gradients were within an acceptable functional range. Under severe under-expansion (21 mm fixture), mean gradients increased to 15–20 mmHg, reflecting the approximately 41% reduction in inflow cross-sectional area relative to free expansion. As expected, increased radial constraint resulted in elevated mean pressure gradients. Despite this, leaflet mobility, coaptation, and regurgitant fraction remained preserved, indicating maintained functional competence under stress-testing conditions.

Particle image velocimetry (PIV) flow visualization provided additional insight into the local hemodynamics and thrombogenic risk of the polymeric TAVI valve. Analysis of leaflet and commissural regions revealed minimal recirculation and effective systolic washout, with commissures showing the longest particle residence times, as expected for sites of potential thrombosis. In out-of-round annulus configurations, PIV demonstrated asymmetric jet vectoring and localized increases in particle residence, but no large-scale flow stagnation. Importantly, pinwheeling was minimal under physiologic backpressure, likely attributable to the hourglass geometry of the frame, in which the leaflet coaptation zone (~23 mm ID) is less affected by inflow compression than the nominal 26 mm inflow diameter. Unlike cylindrical stent frames—where under-expansion can distort the free-edge geometry and induce rotational leaflet motion—the narrowed mid-frame region of the valve maintains stable commissural spacing and uniform leaflet strain, preserving symmetric closure dynamics even when the inflow is partially constrained. As a result, the valve’s structural layout inherently mitigates the mechanical conditions that typically precipitate pinwheeling in self-expanding TAVI devices.

Although VARC-3 (Valve Academic Research Consortium) criteria are designed for clinical post-implant echocardiographic assessment45, several PIV-derived hemodynamic indicators parallel its framework for evaluating prosthetic valve performance. The absence of central leakage in diastolic PIV frames is consistent with VARC-3 definitions of no intravalvular regurgitation, while the lack of stagnation zones or high-shear recirculation aligns with VARC-3 expectations for normal leaflet motion and unobstructed systolic flow. Additionally, the symmetric central jet observed in circular annuli and the controlled, predictable jet vectoring in out-of-round geometries correspond to VARC-3 descriptors for normal prosthetic function versus possible dysfunction when asymmetric flow or turbulence is present. While PIV is not a clinical diagnostic tool, these bench-top flow characteristics support the overall hemodynamic behavior expected of a normally functioning transcatheter valve per VARC-3 definitions.

The PIV results—demonstrating a stable central systolic jet, minimal recirculation, and complete diastolic coaptation—closely mirrored the hemodynamic findings in the preclinical ovine study. In vivo echocardiography similarly showed low transvalvular gradients, preserved leaflet mobility, and no central regurgitation, supporting the physiological flow patterns predicted by the PIV analysis. The absence of regions of flow stagnation in the PIV work is also consistent with the lack of thrombus or leaflet dysfunction at necropsy. Together, the bench-top PIV characterization and the ovine results provide convergent evidence that the polymeric TAVI design promotes efficient washout and stable leaflet mechanics under physiologic loading.

The TAVI valve met all biocompatibility acceptance criteria specified by ISO 10993 for long-term implantable cardiovascular devices. Across cytotoxicity, hemolysis, mutagenicity, and systemic toxicity assays, the device demonstrated no adverse biological responses. Importantly, the complement activation study confirmed that the polymeric and nitinol components did not provoke innate immune activation, while sensitization and intracutaneous irritation tests indicated minimal risk of allergic or irritant responses. Collectively, these results support the biocompatibility of the TAVI system and provide evidence that the LifePolymer and nitinol frame components, as configured in the finished device, are well-tolerated under simulated clinical conditions. This aligns with prior reports of favorable biocompatibility of LifePolymer-based materials for cardiovascular implants38.

The in vivo model creation phase presents considerable technical and physiological challenges, as surgical placement of an annuloplasty ring within the native aortic annulus imposes substantial procedural stress and alters normal hemodynamics. In our study, some animals did not survive this initial intervention, succumbing to perioperative complications before reaching the planned 30-day interval for TAVI implantation. Upon the 30-day interval, six of nine valves were successfully implanted, with procedural failures (missing the target landing zone) attributed to the early learning curve in the ovine model rather than device design. Specifically, in some cases, the landing zone was too inferior, where the valve sealing skirt expanded in the LVOT, resulting in valve migration into the LV after some cardiac cycles. There was limited understanding of in vivo valve foreshortening, and optimal positioning of the marker band relative to the native annulus was not fully established. That said, for the six successful animals, echocardiography showed an initial reduction in EOA after implantation, followed by stabilization through 90 days, with transvalvular gradients remaining low and leaflet motion preserved, indicating effective hemodynamic performance without progressive obstruction. The effective orifice area (EOA) decreased immediately after valve implantation compared to post-annuloplasty, pre-implant baseline, reflecting the combined effects of annuloplasty ring placement and device deployment, which reduce annular compliance and orifice size. This reduction was transient; EOA stabilized and remained similar between Day 0 post-implant and Day 90, indicating no progressive stenosis or device-related obstruction. Echocardiography confirmed normal leaflet motion and patent coronaries, supporting that the early decrease was procedural rather than pathological. No hemolysis or systemic toxicity was observed, and serial blood analyses remained within normal limits, confirming biocompatibility of SiPUU under physiologic conditions.

The histopathologic evaluation of the electrospun LifePolymer skirt demonstrated well-organized fibrinous matrix formation and early fibrocellular pannus, reflecting favorable tissue integration. Importantly, the inner polymer surface (internal flow path of the valve) remained resistant to fibrous overgrowth, preserving leaflet mobility and orifice area. While the ovine model provided valuable insights into device performance and tissue response, certain anatomical and physiological differences limit direct extrapolation to humans. Sheep have smaller, more compliant aortic roots and lack the calcification typical of severe human aortic stenosis, necessitating annuloplasty and graft augmentation to mimic clinical conditions. These modifications may exaggerate early reductions in EOA and influence pannus formation compared to human implants. However, key similarities—such as orthotopic positioning, coronary patency, and hemodynamic stability—support the relevance of these findings for predicting human outcomes, particularly regarding leaflet motion, thrombogenicity, and absence of calcification. Importantly, the absence of calcification, leaflet tears, and pannus formation across the 90-day period suggests that SiPUU polymeric leaflets may offer improved durability and resistance to structural valve degeneration (SVD) compared to glutaraldehyde-fixed tissue valves, which often demonstrate early calcific or fibrotic changes in ovine models.

Polymeric TAVI valves offer several potential advantages over traditional tissue-based valves. The synthetic SiPUU leaflets are engineered for high tear resistance, oxidative stability, and reduced thrombogenicity, which may extend valve durability and reduce the need for anticoagulation. The thin, light polymer leaflets of this TAVI device may contribute to improved hemodynamics and large EOA. The electrospun outer skirt provides a porous scaffold for tissue ingrowth, which can enhance sealing and minimize PVL, a known complication of TAVI procedures. The SiPUU-covered nitinol frame (struts and cells) may reduce flow turbulence and platelet activation, and its mechanical damping could mitigate fatigue-induced damage to the leaflets.

These design elements align with the next generation of polymeric TAVI technologies being developed by a number of groups, which aim to combine the durability of mechanical valves with the hemodynamics and low thrombogenicity of bioprosthetic valves12–19. Our data reinforce the potential for polymeric valves to address the limitations of tissue-based TAVI, particularly in younger patients who require greater valve longevity and are at risk for reintervention due to structural valve degeneration.

This study represents an early preclinical evaluation and is subject to several limitations. First, the ovine model, although widely used for valve testing, is a short-term, accelerated calcification model that may not fully predict long-term human valve durability. While histopathology methods were used to assess the polymer structural integrity, other traditional methods, such as Scanning Electron Microscopy, were not used. Second, three early procedural failures highlight the technical challenges of achieving precise subannular deployment in this model, underscoring the importance of delivery system refinement and operator experience. Third, the 90-day follow-up captures acute and chronic performance, but durability and biostability require longer-term studies and accelerated wear testing to support eventual first-in-human evaluation.

Future work will focus on long-term preclinical studies, accelerated durability testing, and optimizing the delivery system to reduce malposition risk and enhance procedural reproducibility and imaging-based commissural alignment for first-in-human trials.

The 27 mm TAVI valve features LifePolymer leaflets, a nitinol frame covered in LifePolymer (struts and cells), and an electrospun LifePolymer sealing skirt on the outer diameter near the base of the valve. It demonstrated excellent hydrodynamic performance in vitro and favorable preclinical outcomes in an ovine model. Nitinol frame fatigue testing to 200 million cycles confirmed structural durability, geometric stability, and interface biostability. Particle image velocimetry (PIV) flow visualization confirmed effective systolic washout with minimal regions of flow stagnation, supporting the low thrombogenic potential of the SiPUU-based TAVI design. Bench testing confirmed large effective orifice areas, low transvalvular gradients, and minimal regurgitation. 90-day in vivo evaluation showed secure implantation, normal leaflet motion, absence of calcification or pannus formation, and no evidence of hemolysis or systemic toxicity. These results support further investigation of polymeric TAVI valves to improve durability and reduce structural valve degeneration. Long-term preclinical studies, accelerated durability testing, and delivery system optimization are ongoing to validate chronic performance and enable progression to first-in-human trials.

Methods

Material and device design

The TRIA TAVI is a self-expanding nitinol-framed transcatheter aortic valve featuring several novel design elements intended to optimize hemodynamics, durability, and procedural outcomes. Our size 27 TRIA TAVI, used in this study, has an “hourglass” geometry with an inflow internal diameter (ID) of 26 mm, an outflow ID of 23 mm, and 48 mm overall height (Fig. 1), and the “halo” portion of the frame opens up to a diameter similar to the inflow. Leveraging our experience developing and testing a surgical prosthetic heart valve with polymeric leaflets23,38,46–48, the transcatheter aortic valve maintains siloxane-based poly(urethane-urea) (SiPUU, LifePolymer™) leaflets but uses a nitinol frame with an open architecture (no nitinol surrounding the leaflets, only between leaflets), enabling coronary access between commissures after deployment. The frame incorporates an hourglass profile, which increases clearance from the aortic root to promote unobstructed coronary blood flow, and an upper halo frame that stabilizes commissure position and supports leaflet coaptation across varying annular geometries. The nitinol frame and leaflets are dip-cast in LifePolymer™, meaning that the nitinol frame body is encapsulated in polymer, as well as the cells (spaces or intervals) between the nitinol struts. The three polymer leaflets are dip-cast onto the frame simultaneously, yielding a seamless contour of polymer from leaflet to frame to leaflet on external and internal surfaces, with leaflet thickness specifications similar to those described elsewhere38,46. The upper halo frame (superior to the commissures and leaflet free edge) is not coated with SiPUU.

Unique to this design, the cells and struts of the nitinol frame are coated with SiPUU, providing structural damping and potential thromboresistance38, while the external sealing skirt is fabricated from electrospun SiPUU (applied on top of the SiPUU-coated frame) with a controlled porosity engineered to encourage tissue ingrowth and minimize PVL (Fig. 9). The 20 Fr outer diameter delivery system is compatible with transfemoral or iliofemoral access and incorporates active flexion to facilitate crossing the aortic arch, along with commissural alignment features to ensure precise orientation of the valve within the native annulus (Fig. 10).

There is no nitinol frame between commissures (4) to allow for coronary access, and the top of the halo has controlled-release locking tabs (5).

Nitinol frame fatigue testing

Fatigue testing of the self-expanding nitinol frame for the polymeric TAVI valve was conducted to evaluate structural durability under cyclic physiological loading, following ISO 5840‑3:2021 principles, but limited to 200 million cycles (approximately 5-year equivalence) rather than the full 400 million cycles recommended for 10-year durability. The test protocol was informed by finite element analysis (FEA) of worst-case deployment scenarios across the anticipated clinical use range. FEA strain maps identified high-strain regions under extreme annular geometries, which guided the radial deflection targets applied during bench testing. Valve frames were mounted in a pulsatile radial fatigue system constrained to a 20 mm inner diameter and cycled between axial displacements corresponding to 0.6–2.2 mm radial post deflections, replicating or exceeding the highest strain conditions predicted in vivo. Testing was performed in a 37 °C saline bath to mimic physiologic conditions and promote any corrosion or fatigue-related degradation. Frames were inspected daily for instabilities or fractures, and checked every 50 M cycles using high-magnification visual inspection and optical microscopy.

Hydrodynamic testing

Hydrodynamic testing was performed according to ISO 5840-3:2021 requirements for transcatheter heart valves to evaluate EOA, pressure gradients, and regurgitant fractions under pulsatile flow conditions. Although the device is designated as a 27 mm TAVI valve, the frame has an hourglass geometry with a ~26 mm inflow internal diameter and a narrower 23 mm internal diameter at the leaflet outflow. Because this geometry results in differential deformation along the frame, compression of the inflow does not equivalently collapse the leaflet region where EOA and coaptation are measured.

Size 27 Aortic Valves were tested in a pulse duplicator system (Vivitro Labs Inc., Victoria, Canada) simulating physiological conditions of cardiac output with a 75 mL stroke and 35% systolic duration at 5 L/min flow rate, heart rate of 70 bpm, and mean arterial pressure of 100 mmHg. Regurgitation fraction is calculated by summing the closing and leakage volumes and then dividing by the forward flow volume. To represent various deployment conditions—including mild and severe undersizing scenarios for a 27 mm self-expanding valve—hydrodynamic performance was evaluated with the valve deployed into 21 mm and 24 mm inner-diameter test fixtures. Deployment of the nominal 27 mm valve in a 21 mm annulus results in an approximate 41% reduction in inflow cross-sectional area relative to free expansion, and approximately 25% reduction compared to the 24 mm configuration. The annular size refers strictly to the fixed IDs of the test fixtures, not to extrapolated measurements, which were idealized geometries, not anatomic. The fixtures were rigid plastic with the valve sealed at the fixture ID with silicone. All hydrodynamic parameters were directly measured with the size 27 device seated in each fixture and compared to ISO 5840-3 performance requirements.

Flow visualization

Flow visualization was conducted to evaluate the hemodynamic performance and thrombogenic potential of the polymeric TAVI valve under in vitro conditions. A pulsatile flow loop simulating left ventricular ejection and the ascending aorta was constructed featuring a glass aortic model with interchangeable annulus geometries (21 mm, 24 mm, and out-of-round configurations created using elliptical inserts of known major–minor axes). These out-of-round models represented intentional annular eccentricity, as recommended in ISO 5840-3 for non-circular geometry testing. The 21 mm and 24 mm annuli serve as under-expanded conditions for the 27 mm TAVI. The inner diameter of the valve was measured using digital calipers on the hard annulus inserts outside the flow loop. A 50:50 water-glycerol mixture heated to 37 °C was used to mimic the viscosity (0.0045 Ns/m²) and density (1133 kg/m³) of blood.

2D PIV was employed using 20 µm silver-coated hollow ceramic particles as tracers. Particles were selected for near-neutral buoyancy, with a density ( ~1.10 g/mL) closely matching the blood-analog mixture (1.13 g/mL), preventing settling or floatation. A 532 nm, 3 W laser sheet illuminated the imaging plane, and images were captured at 600 frames per second using a high-speed camera. Each condition was recorded for 10–12 full cardiac cycles, corresponding to 6000–7200 frames per dataset. Additional recordings at 3 and 7 L/min were performed to evaluate cardiac-output scaling; the 5 L/min datasets were used for the primary analyses. For image post-processing, raw images underwent background subtraction and intensity normalization. Vector fields were generated using a multi-pass cross-correlation algorithm (64 × 64 px → 32×32 px windows, 50% overlap), followed by universal outlier detection and local median filtering. Velocity maps were smoothed with a 3 × 3 Gaussian kernel (σ = 0.5).

Areas of interest (AOIs) were defined at fixed locations in the ascending aorta downstream of the valve leaflets. Specifically, leaflet AOIs were positioned 1–3 mm above the leaflet free edge on the aortic side, centered on the systolic jet region, and commissural AOIs were positioned 1–4 mm above each commissure, aligned with the commissural outflow trajectory. These locations are designed to capture washout, near-wall shear, and potential flow separation, as these regions are prone to flow stagnation and thrombus formation.

To quantify residence time, simulated particles were seeded in AOIs and tracked across a full cardiac cycle. Particle exit times, average residence duration, and percentage remaining were calculated using a custom Matlab routine with error-checking for valve border crossings, zero velocity artifacts, and recirculation events. Streamline and jet vectoring analyses were computed from phase-averaged velocity fields to characterize systolic jet formation, leaflet washout, and out-of-round induced asymmetry.

Biocompatibility

Biocompatibility testing of the polymeric TAVI valve was performed in accordance with ISO 10993 guidelines for long-term ( > 30 days) implantable, blood-contacting devices. The top-level packaged device was sterilized per validated ethylene oxide parameters and tested across a comprehensive series of assays designed to evaluate cytotoxicity, hemolysis, genotoxicity, pyrogenicity, systemic toxicity, complement activation, sensitization, and intracutaneous irritation. Testing was conducted by ISO 17025-accredited facilities in compliance with Good Laboratory Practice (GLP). Specific studies included: Cytotoxicity (MEM elution, ISO 10993-5), Hemolysis (ASTM F756-17), Genotoxicity (Mouse lymphoma assay, Bacterial reverse mutation test), Pyrogenicity (Material-mediated rabbit test), Systemic toxicity (ISO 10993-11), Complement activation (SC5b-9 ELISA), Sensitization (Guinea pig maximization, ISO 10993-10), and Intracutaneous irritation in rabbits (ISO 10993-10).

Extraction procedures strictly followed ISO 10993-12 using polar and non-polar media under both 24-hour and 72-hour conditions at specified surface-area-to-volume ratios. Positive and negative controls defined in each ISO standard were included in every test run to ensure validity. Acceptance criteria were those prespecified within each ISO method.

Because ISO 10993 assays are standardized pass–fail biological safety evaluations rather than hypothesis-driven analytical experiments, the outcomes do not generate figures or inferential statistical analyses. Instead, each assay yields a categorical determination of biological safety according to prespecified ISO acceptance criteria. Full GLP test reports containing raw data, control performance, and laboratory quality documentation were reviewed and archived.

Preclinical study in ovine model

Key endpoints included implantation success, hemodynamic performance, thrombogenicity, device integrity (assessing leaflet tears, stenosis, stent fracture, and early calcification), and local tissue response. The study was conducted in accordance with ISO 10993-2:2022 for animal studies and followed all institutional and national animal care guidelines, with protocol approval by the institutional animal care and use committee (IACUC).

Healthy adult sheep were selected for in vivo evaluation, as the ovine model is a widely accepted standard for assessing the performance and durability of surgical bioprosthetic and TAVI valves. Although sheep exhibit systemic hemodynamics and cardiac output similar to humans, their native aortic annulus is typically smaller and lacks calcification49,50, making the implantation of a self-expanding TAVI frame a challenge. Sheep also demonstrate accelerated calcification of valve leaflets51,52, which aids in the early assessment of bioprosthetic durability.

To overcome these anatomic limitations, a Model Creation Phase was conducted in 17 Ile de France male and female ovine 30 days prior to TAVI implantation. Animals were 71.5–90 kg and 12–39 months old. Animals were premedicated with intramuscular ketamine and maintained under general anesthesia with isoflurane. Continuous monitoring included ECG, arterial pressure, and oxygen saturation. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established via right atrial and femoral arterial cannulation. During an open-chest surgical procedure, first a sub-annular D-shaped annuloplasty ring (Duran AnCore™ 23 mm polyester mitral ring, Medtronic, USA) was implanted to mimic the reduced compliance of a calcified annulus, producing a post-ring annular diameter of 21–24 mm (perimeter 66–75 mm). Then an aortic root augmentation was performed using a 20–22 mm diameter and 30 mm long Hemashield Platinum™ polyester vascular graft (Maquet, Germany) superior to the sinotubular junction (STJ) to create sufficient root length for TAVI deployment. All surgical procedures were performed by veterinary surgeons with cardiothoracic surgical support, in compliance with GLP and institutional animal care guidelines. This preconditioning procedure (Model Creation Phase) allowed for stable anchoring and physiologic hemodynamic evaluation of the self-expanding polymeric valve in the ovine model.

Following the 30-day recovery after model creation, nine animals entered the Test Phase for TAVI implantation. Anticoagulation therapy consisted of enoxaparin 0.1 mL/10 kg (1 mg/kg) subcutaneously twice daily and acetylsalicylic acid 250 mg intramuscularly once daily from model creation until study termination. Day 0 of the Test Phase corresponded to TAVI implantation. Animals were premedicated with intramuscular morphine and midazolam, anesthetized with thiopental (Model Creation) or midazolam/propofol (Test Phase), and maintained under general anesthesia with isoflurane. Postoperative care (both Model Creation and Test Phases) included morphine and meloxicam for analgesia, and cefazolin was administered intravenously during surgery and intramuscularly for 8 days postoperatively for infection prophylaxis. Hemodynamic monitoring included ECG, invasive arterial pressure, and oxygen saturation. Target deployment was defined as alignment of the sealing skirt with the aortic annulus and the implanted annuloplasty ring (Fig. 11), such that the base of the valve is ~2-3 mm inferior to the native annulus, with valve commissures at the STJ, and the “halo” of the frame within the graft. 0.035-inch Lunderquist (Cordis) and Safari (Boston Scientific) guidewires were used to aid the delivery system with access via surgical cut-down to the external iliac artery. Implantation was performed under rapid ventricular pacing without CPB, with partial recapture and redeployment as necessary to achieve optimal positioning. Test phase implantation was performed by veterinary surgeons with assistance from device engineers for delivery system operation and commissural alignment, in accordance with GLP and institutional animal care guidelines. After delivery system removal, the vascular introducer was withdrawn, the iliac arteriotomy was closed surgically (repair or ligation, as appropriate), manual pressure was applied until hemostasis was achieved, and then the surgical wound was closed layer by layer (muscle, subcutaneous tissue, skin). No vascular closure devices or patch repair techniques were employed—standard surgical compression and layered closure were used.

Fluoroscopic placement of the self-expanding TAVI within a pre-implanted annuloplasty ring.

Deployment target is achieved by aligning the midpoint of the sealing skirt with the native annulus and previously implanted ring.

A total of nine animals were implanted with the 27 mm polymer TAVI valve and were followed for three months to evaluate hemodynamic function, valve competence, and biocompatibility through clinical monitoring, serial hematology and biochemistry, echocardiography and angiography, and macroscopic and histological assessments. At study termination, animals were euthanized under deep anesthesia using an intravenous overdose of pentobarbital sodium, consistent with AVMA guidelines for humane euthanasia. Hearts and major organs were then harvested, photographed, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The valve was not separated from the native aorta; the ascending aorta was trimmed only to the extent needed for fixation, and the entire aortic root–valve complex was submitted intact for macroscopic and histopathologic assessment. To assess both tissue histopathology and polymer structural stability, valve specimens underwent Faxitron X-ray imaging, sectioning, resin embedding, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining; systemic tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 4 µm, and stained. Histology followed ISO 10993 guidelines for pannus, inflammation, and thrombogenicity, with images captured using a standard light microscope (Model BX46, Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan) at multiple magnifications (4x-100x).
