IVT-free, chemically synthesized protein-encoding RNA oligonucleotides for rapid production of personalized cancer vaccines
Authors: Qian Pan, Chi Zhang, Xinyue Wang, Jie Yin, Shengnan She, Xi Chen, Ruitao Dong, Ying Wu, Xinyuan Liao, Yanyan Wang, Hui Yan, Haomeng Kou, Wanjia Wang, Yong Cao, Hui Yang, Liang Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificancePersonalized mRNA cancer vaccines hold great therapeutic promise, yet their production depends on multistep in vitro transcription (IVT) that generates immunogenic dsRNA impurities and prolongs manufacturing to nearly three months, often missing the optimal therapeutic window. Here, we introduced a cap-independent translation enhancer BBV and developed protein-encoding RNA oligonucleotides (PEOs), which were chemically synthesized RNAs bearing 5’-OH and 3’-P termini that drive rolling circle translation after endogenous circularization. PEOs contain undetectable dsRNA and exhibit minimal immunogenicity. In melanoma and orthotopic glioma models, PEO vaccines significantly inhibited tumor growth and synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy. In conclusion, this proof-of-concept study built an IVT-free platform for rapid, safe, and highly druggable vaccines.

Personalized mRNA-based cancer vaccines have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential against malignant tumors (1–5). However, industrial production of personalized mRNA vaccines requires up to three months (6, 7), often leading to miss the critical treatment window for cancer patients and impair patient survival (8, 9). This delay arises because current mRNA vaccine production depends on DNA templates and in vitro transcription (IVT), involving multiple time-consuming steps such as gene synthesis, plasmid construction and bacterial fermentation, IVT, and downstream purification (6, 10). As a clinical-grade product, each step requires rigorous quality control (11).

Furthermore, T7 RNA polymerase-mediated IVT produces double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) impurities that induce strong innate immune responses, leading to RNA degradation via activating RNase L, translation inhibition, and heightened safety concerns (12–15). Although RNA purification by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), usage of T7 polymerase mutants for IVT, or nucleoside modification can reduce the immunogenicity of IVT-produced RNAs to some extent, these approaches do not completely abolish IVT byproduct induced innate immune stimulation in vivo (16–20). Therefore, there is an urgent need for IVT-free RNA vaccine platforms that bypass DNA templates and biological fermentation to enable rapid production of personalized neoantigen vaccines.

RNA oligonucleotides like antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or siRNAs are produced by fully chemical solid-phase synthesis without requiring DNA templates or IVT hence enabling rapid manufacture and conferring potential advantages in safety and drug-likeness (21–23). Such oligonucleotides can be designed in silico and synthesized directly, which could substantially shorten the timelines of vaccine production and avoid enzymatically generated impurities, such as dsRNA (24). However, the limited length of chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotides (usually less than 150 nucleotides) constrains their capacity to encode proteins (25), as essential translation elements for linear mRNA include a 5’-Cap, the gene of interest (GOI), and a 3’-poly(A) tail, and circular RNAs (circRNAs) typically requires internal ribosome entry site (IRES) elements of approximately 500 to 700-nucleotides (nt) in addition to the GOI sequence. The poly(A) tail and long IRES result in exceeding the maximum length attainable by fully chemical synthesis (21, 26). Consequently, chemically synthesized RNA therapeutics have been predominantly ASOs, which function via transcriptional or posttranscriptional noncoding mechanisms and are unable to encode proteins (22).

Recently, several groups have been attempting to generate chemically solid-phase synthesized RNA oligonucleotides to encode proteins. For example, Hiroshi Abe and other groups chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotides with a cap structure (25, 27–31); however, the increased synthetic complexity and low translation efficiency might impede further applications. Linear mRNA translation requires both the 5’-Cap and 3’-poly(A) tail to physically close (noncovalent 5’ end-to-3’ end circularization) via eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4 gamma (eIF4G)-PABP interactions to initiate translation (32, 33). To overcome this limitation, two groups employed either enzymatic (T4 RNA ligase) or chemically catalyzed ligation to covalently circularize chemically synthesized linear RNAs in vitro, thereby increasing the translation efficiency to some extent (29, 34–36). However, additional enzymatic or chemical circularization and subsequent purification increase manufacturing time and complicate industrial scale-up due to stability and quality-control challenges (23). Moreover, chemical circularization strategies may introduce non-natural P–N chemical bonds in some cases, which could potentially affect the normal protein translation of chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotides (35, 37). Although cap analogs have been introduced to obviate long IRES sequences in circRNAs (38, 39), these approaches still depend on IVT and require enzymatic or chemical ligation between synthetic capped oligonucleotides and IVT-produced RNAs, further increasing complexity and manufacturing time.

A recent study showed that flanking RNA with Twister ribozyme sequences generates 5’-OH and 2’,3’-cyclic phosphate termini via self-splicing, and these RNAs are covalently circularized by endogenous RtcB RNA ligase to yield circRNAs when delivered into mammalian cells (40–44). Moreover, circRNAs achieve covalent phosphodiester bonds that supports efficient rolling circle translation (RCT) (45), which employs a single-initiation, continuous-elongation mechanism on circRNAs and produces markedly higher protein production than canonical linear translation (36, 46). Therefore, it is tempting to develop rapid, one-step chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotides with efficient protein-encoding capability that eliminates additional processing (Fig. 1A), thereby enabling rapid manufacture of personalized tumor neoantigen RNA vaccines while retaining the advantages of chemically synthesized oligonucleotides.

Short CITE Elements Enabled Translation Initiation of RNAs.

Current mature chemical solid-phase synthesis technologies for RNA oligonucleotides are generally limited to approximately 150 to 200 nt. Although recent preliminary studies have reported approaches that extend the length limits of chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotides, these RNA oligonucleotides still have limited capacity to accommodate the full set of canonical translational elements, such as the 5’-Cap and 3’-poly(A) tail, which are required for Cap-dependent translation (25, 31). They are also incompatible with conventional IRES elements, which commonly span over 500 nt (47). A viable alternative is to identify compact, IRES-like elements that can initiate translation while meeting the length constraints of chemical synthesis. Apart from IRES elements, some viruses use short cap-independent translational enhancer (CITE) elements as alternative initiation elements (48, 49) (Fig. 1B). Owing to their short length, CITE elements and other compact IRES-like elements are well suited to chemical synthesis limits and can be readily modularized within chemically synthesized RNA oligos. However, these compact IRES-like elements typically confer lower initiation efficiency than Cap-poly(A) or long IRES elements. Therefore, coupling these short elements to covalently closed circRNAs to leverage efficient RCT might make up for the shortcoming of low translation initiation efficiency of these CITE elements, thereby further improving its translation efficiency (36, 46). Importantly, RCT requires that the translation initiation elements should be free of stop codons. A recent study identified a ~ 50-nt short IRES-like element (34), but the sequence contains stop codons and is therefore incompatible with RCT.

Therefore, we collected 28 CITE elements from literature and database to further analyze the length, number of stop codons, and structure of these CITE elements (50). Notably, in comparison with IRES elements, which typically possess long sequences with multiple stop codons, some CITE elements are quite short and contain only a few stop codons, and one CITE element from Black Beetle Virus, termed BBV, comprised only 39-nt with only one single stop codon that can be excluded by changing reading frame (51) (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, we transfected HEK293T cells with IVT-derived linear RNAs, in which the CITE elements were fused with an Enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein (EGFP)-encoding RNA sequence, respectively, and detected the EGFP fluorescence signals via flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 1D). The results showed that the smallest one, BBV, exhibited significant protein translation capability (Fig. 1 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). To test whether BBV drives circRNA translation in a sequence-dependent manner, we generated a series of circRNA constructs encoding a 3×Flag-P2A [porcine teschovirus-1 2A (52)] fusion, transfected them into HEK293T cells, and assessed translation by western blot (Fig. 1 G and H). Results indicated that both the construct lacking the BBV sequence and the construct with inverted BBV orientation failed to produce detectable protein, demonstrating that translation requires the correct BBV element (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, truncation or mutagenesis of the BBV element significantly reduced translation, indicating that the intact BBV sequence is essential for robust circRNA translation (Fig. 1I). Next, we also predicted the secondary structure of BBV, and examined the interaction between BBV and eIF4G, a crucial initial factor in translation in mammalian cells (50, 53, 54). Computational docking suggested a potential interaction between BBV and eIF4G, further supporting BBV’s role in promoting translation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Collectively, given the constraint of chemical synthesis length, BBV emerged as one potential candidate among these CITE elements to drive efficient protein translation of chemically synthesized RNA oligos via RCT.

Chemically Synthesized RNA Oligos with 5’-OH and 3’-P Termini Could form CircRNAs in Mammalian Cells.

The suboptimal efficiency of translation via BBV shown by the MFI of EGFP expression (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) impelled us to further improve the translation efficiency. RCT presents an innovative mechanism for robust protein synthesis, even in conditions of low translation initiation efficiency (36, 46). The RtcB RNA ligase, known for its role in joining 2’,3’-cyclic phosphate or 3’-phosphate (3’-P) termini to 5’-hydroxyl (5’-OH) termini during tRNA maturation and XBP1 mRNA splicing, emerged as a candidate enzyme for RNA circularization strategy (Fig. 2A) (40, 41, 44).

We hypothesized that the chemically synthesized RNA oligos containing BBV element might be ligated into circRNAs and enable efficient protein production via RCT in mammalian cells. Therefore, we investigated whether the chemically synthesized RNA oligos with engineered characteristic 5’-OH and 3’-P termini could serve as the substrates for RtcB-mediated RNA circularization. The addition of 3’-P termini in the chemical solid synthesis of RNA oligos was previously reported (55). Then the RNA oligos with characteristic 5’-OH and 3’-P termini, or 5’-OH and 3’-OH termini as control, were chemically synthesized (Fig. 2B). Consistent with our hypothesis, in vitro incubation of 5’-OH/3’-P RNA oligos with RtcB enzymes exhibited a significant shift of RNA bands in Urea-PAGE RNA electrophoresis, indicating the occurrence of RNA oligo circularization (Fig. 2C). In contrast, no change in mobility was observed in control RNA oligos with 5’-OH and 3’-OH termini (Fig. 2C). To confirm that chemically synthesized RNA oligos adopts a circular conformation, we performed an RNase R assay and found that RNA oligos bearing 5’-OH/3’-P termini ligated by RtcB were resistant to RNase R digestion, consistent with efficient ligation of 5’-OH and 3’-P termini by RtcB and the formation of topologically closed circular RNA molecules (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, cellular transfection of chemically synthesized RNA oligos with 5’-OH and 3’-P termini could be precisely ligated to form circRNAs in different mammalian cells, further confirmed by the Sanger sequencing analysis (Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B).

Circularized Chemically Synthesized RNA Oligos Achieved RCT in Mammalian Cells.

Then we moved on to validate the occurrence of RCT with circularized chemically synthesized RNA oligos. After transfection with the same dose of RNA oligos, HEK293T cells were collected for assessment of protein expression. Western blot experiment showed that only the 5’-OH/3’-P RNA oligos without stop codon produced Flag proteins in HEK293T, whereas either the 5’-OH/3’-P RNA oligos with stop codon or 5’-OH/3’-OH RNA oligos without stop codon failed to generate detectable Flag proteins (Fig. 2G). The translation products exhibited apparent molecular weights exceeding 180 kDa, consistent with the high-molecular weight polypeptides characteristic of RCT (36) (Fig. 2G). Flow cytometry results further confirmed that Flag-encoding RNA oligos with 5’-OH/3’-P efficiently expressed Flag proteins in HEK293T (Fig. 2H). To definitively characterize the translation products, the cell lysate of RNA oligo-transfected cells was analyzed for measuring the protein products using mass spectrometry. A specific peptide sequence (HATMDYK) spanning from BBV-encoding sequence to the Flag-encoding sequence was identified by mass spectrometry, providing the direct evidence for RCT of circularized chemically synthesized RNA oligos (Fig. 2 I and J), as the translation would initiate from Flag-encoding sequence and the BBV-encoding sequence would not be translated if RCT did not occur. By inserting a P2A sequence downstream of the Flag-encoding region, we observed a ladder-like pattern of Flag peptide expression, consistently confirming occurrence of RCT (Fig. 2 K and L).

To verify translation of the chemically synthesized RNA oligos across cell types, RNA oligos were transfected into A549 and HeLa cells or electroporated into human primary T cells. Flow cytometry and western blot showed that RNA oligos bearing 5’-OH/3’-P termini and lacking a stop codon drove translation in all three cell types. (Fig. 2 M−Q and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C–F).

Collectively, these results demonstrated that the chemically synthesized RNA oligos with characteristic 5’-OH and 3’-P termini could form circRNAs by RtcB ligases, and further achieved protein products via RCT in mammalian cells. Therefore, this kind of chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotide with BBV elements and characteristic 5’-OH/3’-P termini was designated as protein-encoding RNA oligonucleotide (PEO).

PEO Demonstrated Minimal Immunogenicity and Low Cytotoxicity.

The immunogenicity of therapeutics RNAs profoundly affects both safety and efficacy (12, 56). A substantial proportion of immunogenicity derives from dsRNA impurities generated during IVT, which leads to exaggerated type I interferon responses and translation inhibition (57) (Fig. 3A). These effects may compromise efficacy and induce adverse reactions. Given the importance of safety in RNA-based therapeutics, we evaluated the potential immunogenicity of PEO. ELISA assessment revealed that there were undetectable dsRNA impurities in PEO group, while significant dsRNA contamination was observed in IVT-produced unmodified-mRNA group, m1ψ-modified mRNA group, and unmodified RNA with self-cleaving Twister ribozyme [termed icRNA by Tong et al. (44)] (Fig. 3 B and C). Furthermore, while unmodified-mRNA and icRNA induced remarkable phosphorylation of protein kinase R (PKR), PEO only elicited minimal level of PKR activation which was even lower than m1ψ-modified mRNA group (Fig. 3D). Consistent with this immunogenicity profile, PEO also exhibited minimal cytotoxicity in both A549 and HeLa cells (Fig. 3 E and F). Notably, the 50% cytotoxicity concentrations (CC50) of PEO in A549 cells significantly exceeded that of m1ψ-mRNA (Fig. 3E).

To further characterize the potential immunogenicity of PEO, we detected the levels of interferon-related cytokines and chemokines in RNA-transfected cells via reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The results showed that in both A549 cells and human primary T cells, PEO consistently induced significantly lower levels of proinflammatory cytokines or chemokines than IVT-produced unmodified-mRNA and icRNA (Fig. 3 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Moreover, IVT-produced m1ψ-modified mRNA potently induced RIG-I expression, whereas PEO exhibited minimal effects on RIG-I expression (Fig. 3 G and H). The transcriptome-wide RNA sequencing analysis further confirmed the minimal immunogenicity of PEO, in consistent with the above results (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, immune-related gene expression profiling in human primary T cells demonstrated that PEO-transfected cells maintained a signature of immunogenicity similar to untreated control, further confirming its immunoquiescent nature (Fig. 3J).

Collectively, these findings established a minimal immunogenicity and low cytotoxicity profile of PEO, positioning it as a highly promising platform for therapeutic applications that require precise and efficient protein expression without provoking detrimental immune stimulations.

PEO-Based Cancer Vaccine Significantly Inhibited Tumor Growth.

To validate the therapeutic potential of PEO platform, we developed a proof-of-concept neoantigen cancer vaccine targeting the model antigen ovalbumin (OVA) (58) (Fig. 4A). In vitro assessment demonstrated that transfection of DC2.4 dendritic cells with PEO encoding the OVA peptide (SIINFEKL) generated robust OVA peptide presentation via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 4B). Critically, the antigen presentation efficiency of PEO-OVApeptide treatment significantly exceeded that of either IVT-produced icRNAs (44) encoding OVA peptides or conventional linear mRNA encoding full-length OVA proteins (Fig. 4B). Building upon these in vitro results, the PEO constructs bearing 5’-OH and 3’-P termini were encapsulated with lipid nanoparticles (LNP), forming LNP-PEO-OVA-peptide (abbreviated as PEO-OVA) formulations for in vivo vaccination.

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of PEO-based cancer vaccines, the C57BL/6 J mice bearing subcutaneous OVA-expressing B16F10 (B16F10-OVA) tumors were randomly divided into groups and subcutaneously (s.c.) immunized twice with PBS or PEO-OVA at an interval of 6 d (Fig. 4C). Specifically, analysis of peripheral blood from vaccinated mice revealed a significant elevation in the proportion of OVA-specific CD8+ T lymphocytes, accompanied with increased activation as indicated by upregulated CD44+ lymphocytes (Fig. 4D). Importantly, vaccination by PEO-OVA achieved substantial tumor inhibition in B16F10-OVA mouse models (Fig. 4E).

Next, we also tested an alternative intramuscular (i.m.) route for the administration of PBS or PEO-OVA, and observed similar results to those achieved with s.c. administration (Fig. 4 F–H), demonstrating flexibility in administration methodology of PEO-based cancer vaccines. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed a higher frequency of SIINFEKL specific CD8+ T cells not only within the peripheral blood but also in the spleens of mice treated with PEO-OVA, accompanied by increased CD8+ T cell activation in these compartments (Fig. 4I). Besides, the tumor tissue specimens from treated mice were also harvested for RNA-seq analysis and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using RNA-seq data indicated that PEO-OVA boosted several adaptive immune pathways, including antigen receptor-mediated pathway, costimulation by the CD28 family pathway, and T cell costimulation signaling (Fig. 4J) in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Furthermore, the IHC staining of CD8+ T cells revealed a marked increase in the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into TME after the treatment of PEO-OVA, providing direct evidence of enhanced tumor-specific immune surveillance (Fig. 4K).

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of the PEO platform, we conducted a head-to-head comparison of antitumor efficacy among m1ψ-modified mRNA, PIE-derived circRNA, Tornado-generated icRNA, and PEO-based cancer vaccine under identical dosing conditions (Fig. 4L and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). In a subcutaneous B16F10-OVA tumor model, PEO-OVA inhibited tumor growth to a degree comparable to that achieved by m1ψ-modified mRNA, permuted intron-exon (PIE)-derived circRNA (59), or icRNA vaccines (Fig. 4M). Moreover, PEO-OVA treated tumor-bearing mice demonstrated survival benefits comparable to those of the mRNA vaccine group and significantly greater survival than those of untreated groups (Fig. 4N). These results demonstrate that PEO-OVA confers protective efficacy comparable to that of conventional mRNA vaccines while offering the distinct manufacturing advantages of an IVT-free platform.

PEO-Based Glioma Vaccine Showed a Promising Treatment Effect and Could Synergize With Immune Checkpoint Blockade.

Rapidly progressing malignant glioma is associated with significantly shortened overall survival, leaving patients with a narrow therapeutic window (60) (Fig. 5A). To further evaluate the potential of PEO in addressing this clinical urgency, we assessed the therapeutic efficacy of PEO-based vaccination in an IDH-R132H mutant orthotopic glioma mouse model. GL261 tumor cells stably expressing the IDH-R132H mutant protein were implanted into the striatum, and tumor-bearing mice were subsequently treated with PEO vaccine, anti-PD-1 or the combination of both (Fig. 5B). Similar to previous reports, PD-1 checkpoint blockade had limited efficacy against immunologically “cold” glioma (61) (Fig. 5 C and D). We also found that PEO treatment resulted in a slight inhibition of tumor growth, whereas coadministration of anti-PD-1 antibody markedly enhanced the therapeutic effect of PEO, suggesting that PEO remodels the TME and converts it toward an immunologically responsive state (Fig. 5 C and D). We also compared the protective efficacy in IVT-derived circRNA and PEO at a later stage of disease progression (Fig. 5E). IVT-derived circRNA failed to inhibit tumor growth, while PEO showed superior effect which could be amplified by combination with anti-PD-1 treatment (Fig. 5 F and G). Considering that IVT-derived circRNA triggered higher inflammatory response in vitro and that immune inflammatory response may cause damage to brain tissues (62), PEO hence represents a favorable strategy for glioma vaccine. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PEO effectively suppresses tumor growth in a rapidly progressing glioma model and synergizes with checkpoint blockade therapy to further enhance antitumor efficacy.

Finally, we explored the application of the PEO platform in protein-replacement therapy. Using T4 RNA ligase to perform in vitro ligation of RNA oligos bearing 5’-P/3’-P, and 5’-OH/3’-OH, respectively, we assembled two PEO sequences into a longer precursor (252-nt total), after which the remaining 5’-OH and 3’-P termini were circularized by RtcB (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C). Moreover, this construction could be translated in HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). These results extend the coding capacity of the PEO platform beyond the length limits of single-round chemical synthesis and demonstrate its potential for producing therapeutically relevant peptides.

Discussion

The clinical translation of personalized cancer vaccines is impeded by a central bottleneck: Rapid tumor progression demands prompt intervention, whereas conventional vaccine platforms often cannot meet these clinical windows due to prolonged production processes (4, 63, 64). Protein-based vaccines typically require complex protein expression and purification workflows (65), and conventional RNA-based vaccines depend on multi-step manufacturing, including DNA template preparation, biofermentation, IVT, and downstream purification (7). Here, we established a paradigm for rapid cancer vaccine production, enabling expedited transition from sequence design to finished products via direct chemical synthesis. Specifically, we introduced a 39-nt translation initiation BBV element. Although this BBV element alone has limited efficiency of translation initiation, efficient protein expression could be achieved through synergy with RCT of circRNAs. By leveraging endogenous RtcB-mediated ligation, we installed 5’-OH and 3’-phosphate termini on chemically synthesized RNA oligos, thereby enabling efficient intracellular circularization and protein production via RCT in vivo, and establishing a technical foundation for therapeutic applications. This approach bypasses reliance on canonical mRNA features such as 5’-Cap and 3’-poly(A) tail, as well as the long IRES elements often required in circRNA constructs. We designated this kind of chemically synthesized RNA oligonucleotides with translation initiation elements and characteristic 5’-OH/3’-P termini as PEOs.

While striving for efficacy, safety remains a central requirement for cancer vaccine development. The impurities such as dsRNA, which are unavoidable during IVT process, can significantly trigger excessive innate immune responses and pose clinical safety risks in vivo (12, 57). Existing technologies, such as HPLC purification, nucleoside modification, or the use of T7 RNA polymerase variants, can partially reduce immunogenicity but fail to completely eliminate the risks (17, 66). The chemical synthesis strategy employed in this study fundamentally eliminates such risks. Stringent quality control across the synthesis workflow yields products with minimal immunogenicity and a favorable safety profile (23). This advantage is not limited to the field of cancer vaccines but also provides broad avenues for the development of protein replacement therapies for inherited protein deficiency disorders, and tolerogenic vaccines offering a potential modality for intervention in autoimmune diseases (67–69).

A fundamental principle of drug development is that drug candidates must undergo systematic safety evaluation before clinical use. However, for personalized cancer vaccines, a tension exists between their narrow therapeutic windows and conventional, long-horizon safety-assessment paradigms. This clinical reality has created an urgent need for highly robust therapeutic platforms that can be deployed rapidly without compromising safety. Notably, PEO technology is highly aligned with previously established ASO therapeutics in its synthesis and purification workflows, inheriting the outstanding advantages such as high drug-likeness, robust manufacturing processes, and well-defined quality control standards (70, 71). Consequently, PEO technology can leverage the Contract Development and Manufacturing Organization (CDMO) platforms and regulatory review pathways of existing ASO drugs, significantly accelerating the transition from laboratory research to industrial production. Collectively, these features can substantially shorten the timeline from vaccine preparation to clinical application, providing a viable technical solution to meet the urgency requirements of personalized cancer treatment.

Nonetheless, there remains substantial scope for improvement. Rational design of CITE elements (e.g., BBV) and codon optimization of GOI are likely to further enhance translation efficiency. Chemical modification strategies, such as 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) incorporation, are expected to improve the stability and efficacy of PEO (25). The PEO platform now accommodates sequences of up to 252-nt by incorporating an additional T4-mediated ligation step to join two PEO sequences, while continued advance in RNA oligo synthesis technology that overcomes length constraints will expand the capacity to encode complex therapeutic proteins. Importantly, PEO technology also holds great promise for protein replacement therapy like GLP-1 as shown in our results, potentially providing treatment strategies for monogenic genetic diseases. Furthermore, owing to the minimal immunogenicity and low cytotoxicity, PEO technology has the potential to be applied to autoimmune diseases, inducing antigen-specific immune tolerance. These technical breakthroughs and conception innovations will accelerate the clinical translation of PEO platform, bringing potential therapeutic hope to patients.

Materials and Methods

Comprehensive details for computational modeling, chemical synthesis, cell viability detection, western blot, RT-qPCR, RNA-seq analysis, lipid nanoparticle encapsulation, in vitro RNA ligation, flow cytometry, mass spectrometry, antigen presentation detection, immune cells isolation, and animal experiments are provided in SI Appendix.

Cell Culture.

The HEK293T cell line was maintained in the laboratory (Fudan University). A549, HeLa, DC2.4, and B16F10-OVA cell lines were acquired from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. These cell lines were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The human primary T cells used in this study were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells obtained from one deidentified male donor prior to use in this study. The donor was randomly recruited and written informed consent was obtained. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University. Human primary T cells were isolated using a T cell isolation Kit (STEMCELL, #17951) and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, recombinant human IL-2 protein (MCE, #HY-P70758), and human T cell activator CD3/CD28 beads (Thermo, #11131D). All cells were grown at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO2.

IVT.

Template vectors for all RNA production were linearized using restriction enzymes (NEB) and purified employing the DNA Clean & Concentrator-25 Kit (Zymo Research, #D4033). RNAs were generated using the T7 High Yield RNA Transcription Kit (Vazyme, #TR101-02) or EasyCap T7 Co-transcription Kit with CAG Trimer (Vazyme, #DD4203). Following DNase I treatment (37°C, 15 min), circRNAs underwent RNase R treatment (Geneseed, #R0301). Then both mRNA and circRNA were purified via the RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research) combined with HPLC before being used for comparison experiments.

Statistical Analysis.

An unpaired two-sided Student’s t test was performed for comparison as indicated in the figure legends. Tumor growth curves were calculated by two-way ANOVA, and the log-rank t test was used to calculate the survival curves. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant.
