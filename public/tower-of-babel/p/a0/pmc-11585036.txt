Outpatient characteristics in a pediatric otolaryngology department during COVID-19 pandemic: A retrospective study
Authors: Masao Noda, Ryota Koshu, Hisashi Sugimoto, Tomokazu Yoshizaki, Akihiro Nomura, Makoto Ito
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the characteristics of outpatients in the pediatric otolaryngology department of a tertiary Japanese hospital before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsIn this retrospective study conducted at a tertiary center in Japan, pediatric patients aged 0–15 years who visited the pediatric otolaryngology department between 2017 and 2022 were included. The number of outpatients in the department was compared between before and during the COVID-19 pandemic (January 2017–December 2022) by year, age, sex, and the disease for which the patient was examined. Additionally, the diseases that most substantially contributed to the change in outpatient visits were evaluated in detail regarding their severity.ResultsAmong a total of 9219 outpatients, there were no significant differences between 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 (4650 vs. 4569). In contrast, the number of new outpatients decreased by 27.5% (from 1242 to 900) in each of the three years during the COVID-19 pandemic. In particular, the number of patients aged 1–6 years decreased as did that of male patients. Of eight disease categories, oral cavity- and pharyngeal- related diseases decreased the most significantly, by approximately 50%, and the decrease in the number of patients with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is thought to have had an impact on the decrease in the number of patients. Furthermore, in cases in which polysomnography testing was performed for OSA, the proportion of patients with moderate and severe categories showed a significant decrease.ConclusionsThe number of new pediatric ORL patients, particularly those with severe OSA, reduced during the COVID 19 pandemic. These results may provide indications about the characteristics of OSA incidence and exacerbations.Keywords: Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), outpatients, obstructive sleep apnea, epidemiology, pediatric otolaryngology, retrospective study

Introduction

On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization declared COVID-19 a global pandemic, leading to dramatic changes in healthcare systems worldwide. Although telemedicine and artificial intelligence have enabled remote healthcare access, most care is still delivered in person, albeit with notable shifts in healthcare delivery and patient behavior. 1 In 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic began, patient visits decreased by 20%–60%, affecting the healthcare system and disease management.2–5 This reduction led to concerns that patients, particularly those with serious or chronic conditions such as cancer, diabetes, hypertension, and rheumatism, were missing necessary medical consultations.6,7

Thus, while many of these conditions have less impact on patients by sudden changes in condition after a period of time,8–12 for pediatric diseases a delay in diagnosis and treatment could negatively impact relevant areas of development, such as the acquisition of language and social skills, growth due to malnutrition, and choking due to airway diseases. Therefore, it is necessary to identify and respond to changes in the patient population during and after COVID-19 pandemic.

To date, there have been limited studies in pediatrics examining changes in patient characteristics related to COVID-19, with most research published shortly after the first year of the pandemic. In 2021, some studies utilized questionnaires directed at pediatric otolaryngologists; however, none have analyzed patient data collected over an extended period. 13 Furthermore, although some reports analyzed databases based on insurance disease names and receipts, their accuracy has not been examined.13–18

Since pediatric otolaryngology includes several disease types, including infectious diseases, congenital malformations, and neoplastic lesions, it is necessary to accurately capture all of them and analyze changes due to pandemic. Conversely, the high risk of COVID-19 infection for healthcare providers also impacts the need to understand the impact on their characteristics and patient population in clinical practice. 16

In this study, the changes in pediatric otolaryngology outpatients before and after the COVID-19 pandemic were examined according to sex, age, and other individual characteristics, as well as disease classification. In addition, changes in factors such as severity and complications of the main diseases were examined before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Ethics statements

This study was approved by the Jichi Medical University Institutional Review Board of Clinical Trials (Approval No 23-087). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent revisions, most recently updated in 2013. Informed consent was obtained via an opt-out form provided on the website. Those who rejected were excluded. To protect patient confidentiality, all patient details were de-identified, ensuring that no individual was identified in any manner.

Study design and patient involvement

In this retrospective study, we conducted an analysis of pediatric outpatients aged 0–15 years who visited the Department of Pediatric Otolaryngology of Jichi Medical University Hospital and Children's Medical Center, a tertiary hospital in Japan, between January 2017 and December 2022. Patients were selected consecutively for this study, ensuring that every patient meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period was included.

To investigate the differences in the characteristics of outpatients before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, we collected data on the number of outpatients by year, age, sex, and the disease for which they were examined before and during the pandemic (January 2017–December 2022). Based on the year 2020, when COVID spread across Japan, we examined how the data for 2020–2022 changed compared with that for 2017–2019. Furthermore, we evaluated the diseases that significantly contributed to the change in outpatient visits and analyzed their severity in detail. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE guidelines. 19

Data collection and outcome measures

All data were independently collected by two pediatric otolaryngologists (M.N. and R.K.) by reviewing medical records and capturing data manually. Patient characteristics (age, sex, disease type, and number of visits) were collected. Each pediatric otolaryngologist (M.N. and R.K.) independently classified the pediatric otolaryngology conditions into eight categories (ear, nose, oral cavity and pharynx, head and neck surgery, laryngeal trachea-esophagus, logopedics and phoniatrics, dizziness and facial nerve, and swallowing and dysphagia). Based on the aggregated and organized data, we evaluated the trends regarding patient characteristics and disease types for each year.

Regarding the diseases that showed the most significant changes, to analyze the severity of OSA-related patients, the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) and complications related to apnea were collected. The AHI was scaled using polysomnographic data. The AHI data were classified as follows: normal, AHI < 1; mild. 1 ≤ AHI < 5; moderate, 5 ≤ AHI < 10; and severe, AHI ≥ 10.20,21 Considering that causes other than OSA, such as facial deformities, cerebral palsy, and 21 trisomy, might have been involved in complicated cases, AHI severity was examined after excluding such complicated cases.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software (IBM Corp.). For descriptive statistics, data from 2017 to 2019 were used as a baseline. Pearson's chi-squared analyses were conducted to evaluate the population of the patients and the association between OSA severity levels during the pandemic and previous terms. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

During the pandemic period (2020–2022), we observed a notable decrease in the number of new patients, particularly among those aged 1–6 years and male patients, these decreases were statistically significant, with χ²(1, N = 13769) = 14.17, p = 0.00017 for patients aged 1–6 years and χ²(1, N = 9220) = 6.90, p = 0.0086 for male patients (Figure 1). Overall, the number of new outpatients decreased by 27.5%, dropping from 1242 patients in the pre-pandemic period to 900 during the pandemic years. Among the age groups, the most significant reduction occurred in patients aged 1–6 years, with a decrease of 35.5% in 2020 and 2021, and a further decline to 55.7% in 2022 (Table 1, Figure 2A). This reduction was especially prominent among children aged 3–5 years (Table 2, Figure 2B).

Discussion

The present analysis of trends in pediatric otolaryngology outpatients before and during the COVID-19 pandemic using data from regional core hospitals in Japan revealed the following findings: First, the number of new pediatric otolaryngology outpatients decreased, particularly in the 1–6 years old range. Second, there was a decrease in the number of boys examined; the largest decrease in new patients according to the field was that for visits related to the oropharynx, particularly OSA. Finally, an analysis of patients with the main complaint of snoring based on the AHI, an indicator of OSA severity, showed a decrease in the proportion of patients with moderate or severe disease. These findings reveal trends in the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on pediatric otolaryngology, which implies that pediatric OSA is at least in some way associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.

Previous questionnaire survey studies of pediatric otolaryngologists in Italy reported decreases in the number of outpatient visits and surgeries compared with the pre-COVID levels. In the present analysis of actual data from Japan, no significant reduction in the total number of patients was observed although the number of new patients declined. The reduction in new pediatric otolaryngology patients, regardless of the country, suggests that common factors, such as behavioral restrictions and reduced social contact, likely influenced this trend. 17 Additionally, a study from Switzerland also reported a decrease in the number of ENT-related surgeries, with a particularly notable reduction in procedures related to the neck and ears. 22 Although Japan is experiencing a rapid decline in birth rates, the effect of which should be considered, the results from other countries suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic had a significant influence. These findings indicate that the observed changes in pediatric otolaryngology might be attributable to the declining birth rate and the broader effects of the pandemic.

The lower number of new patients was observed for children aged 1–6 years. The reasons for the present results may be due to patient-related factors, such as a reduced risk of infection, a lower number of consultations, and lifestyle changes. In line with these findings, single-center reports from other countries have shown a decrease in the number of otolaryngology emergency outpatients aged ≤18 years and ≥45 years one year after 2020. 23

In the Japanese education system, children attend nurseries and kindergartens until the age of six, when they attend primary school until they are 12 years old and move to junior high school. Childcare facilities provide opportunities for contact with young children and are considered a risk factor for otitis media 24 and viral and bacterial infections. Attendance at daycare or kindergarten is an important factor driving bacterial diversity. 25

We hypothesize that the risk of infection decreased because of the loss of personal contact, the reduction of medical visits to avoid COVID infection, and the lifestyle of parents of pediatric patients, such as teleworking, may have reduced the chances of going outside the home and being aware of minor abnormalities. Another hypothesis is that the decrease in clinic visits or the use of telemedicine might have hindered accurate oral examinations. Unlike general otolaryngology, a unique challenge in pediatric otolaryngology is that children often do not cooperate in clearly showing their oral or nasal cavities. Scott et al. noted that the inability to conduct in-person examinations during the COVID-19 pandemic made these observations more difficult, potentially leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment. 26

Our report also indicated a marked decrease in oropharyngeal diseases, especially those related to OSA. Pediatric otorhinolaryngology has a wide variety of diseases, including infectious, congenital, and neoplastic diseases. Previous reports have shown a significant decrease in infectious or inflammatory diseases such as otitis media and abscesses, whereas congenital anomalies and masses/malignancies remained particularly unchanged. 13 In that report, OSA/sleep-disordered breathing was classified as other, with respondents reporting a nearly equal increase, no change, and decrease at their institution. Interestingly, other reports from Germany observed a decrease in the number of patients with sore throat in primary care following the first lockdown. The present study also reported a significant decrease in all types of tonsil surgery, indicating that tonsil-related conditions were more significantly affected by the pandemic than other oral diseases. This trend appears to be consistent across various studies. 22

Pediatric OSA is particularly common at 2–8 years old, being caused by hypertrophy of the palatine tonsils, adenoid, or other immune tissues, which further narrow the airways, leading to inadequate oxygenation, stunting growth and resulting in daytime sleepiness and speech development disorders. 12 ,27–30 Between 5–8 years old airways get normally enlarged, even more so after infections and other events. We consider that the number of cases decreased due to reduced contact opportunities and infection risk lower preschool attendance, mask use, and hand washing. However, there was no clear decrease in repetitive or habitual tonsillitis, suggesting that the decrease in OSA may be due to causes other than reduced exposure.

Furthermore, we observed a significant decrease in the number of patients with moderate or severe OSA, particularly when patients with severe complications were excluded. This result could be due to overall reluctance to see a doctor or decreased severity of the disease; however, the significant decrease in moderate and severe cases suggests decreasing severity of the disease. One of the reasons for the decrease is that the COVID pandemic has reduced contact opportunities not only because of the voluntary refrain from attending nursery school due to the COVID pandemic but also because parents are now seeing their children at home due to the widespread use of telework, enabling detection at an earlier stage. The results suggest that reducing contact opportunities may help reduce the severity of OSA, even after COVID-19 transmission has settled down.

However, this study has some limitations. Specifically, the data were collected from a single medical institution, and the cohort was relatively small, which might have reduced the statistical power for detecting significant differences. Therefore, a larger sample size and more prospective studies are required to confirm the present results and generalize the findings to more diverse patient populations.

Conclusion

This study examined changes in the number of pediatric otolaryngology outpatients attributable to the spread of COVID-19, focusing on patient characteristics and major diseases. COVID-19 decreased the number of new pediatric otolaryngology patients, particularly among those aged 1–6 years and male patients, as well as those with conditions related to the oral cavity and pharynx. Additionally, the proportion of patients with moderate and severe OSA decreased during the pandemic, providing further insights into how the pandemic influenced the presentation and severity of conditions typically seen in pediatric otolaryngology. These findings offer valuable information for understanding the broader impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on patient care in this field.
