Digital Health Connectivity as Infrastructure for Rural Cardiovascular Equity
Authors: Amber E. Johnson, Jared W. Magnani, Melvin R. Echols, Spyros Kitsiou, Ami B. Bhatt
Published: 2026

Digital health has advanced faster than the infrastructure required to make it equitable. Telehealth and internet-based devices such as remote monitoring systems, wearable sensors, and app-based care models are often presented as solutions for geographic barriers. Yet many of these approaches to patient care assume household broadband access, device ownership, digital and health literacy, and reliable connectivity. Such resources are least available in communities with the greatest cardiovascular need.1 In rural cardiovascular care, the risk is not only that digital health will fail to close disparities. The risk is that technological resource dependency will reproduce health access disparities in a new form: continuous care for those who are digitally connected and episodic, travel-dependent care for everyone else.

Digital connectivity is clinically important. Internet infrastructure has been linked to cardiovascular outcomes. Using 2018 U.S. Census and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data, one study found that state-level household broadband internet access was inversely associated with cardiovascular mortality.2 Even after adjustment for population-level demographic characteristics, education, income, and health insurance, the association between broadband access and cardiac mortality remained. Given the difficulty in measuring confounding social and structural determinants, broadband internet access may serve as a surrogate marker for socioeconomic advantage. Further obfuscating the link between broadband and cardiac outcomes is the fact that household internet access can vary with structural determinants like geographic location, rurality, or race. As opposed to household access, smartphones can be used as an alternative source of internet. Survey data reveal differences in smartphone-based internet access by user demographics.3 When the infrastructure for connection is uneven, the infrastructure for cardiovascular prevention and management is uneven as well.

Cardiovascular disease management depends on timely communication for medication adjustment, risk factor control, and access to updated information. Further complicating the digital divide is the fact that digital devices commonly require Bluetooth pairing to a smartphone via an internet connection for data uploading. Therefore, the end-user (the patient) must navigate a complex network infrastructure to obtain clinical care. Subscriber Identity Module (SIM)-based cellular connectivity offers a solution. SIM or embeded SIM-enabled devices can run applications, store data, and can be password protected. SIM-enabled devices overcome this data uploading challenge since they can operate as a standalone technology capable of transmitting data to a central server for further analysis. Thus, they can be used by virtually anyone irrespective of having a smartphone, home broadband connection, or not.

Rural geographies often face disparities in clinical outcomes and access to care. In comparison with individuals living in more metropolitan settings, people living in rural areas may face difficulties with social isolation, long travel distance to care, limited transportation, clinician workforce shortages, lower availability of specialty services, and reduced access to preventive or emergency resources.4,5 Recent analyses sharpen the point. Rural adults have persistently higher cardiovascular mortality than urban adults, with widening disparities among younger adults.4,6 Nearly half of U.S. counties lack a practicing cardiologist, and these counties are disproportionately rural, socioeconomically disadvantaged, and burdened by cardiovascular risk factors.6 For a patient with hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or coronary heart disease, delays in detecting worsening health due to inaccessible digital infrastructure can translate to a gap in clinical outcomes.

Digital infrastructure is currently limited, and policy efforts to expand household broadband remain essential. The Emergency Broadband Benefit and its successor, the Affordable Connectivity Program, showed that subsidized connectivity could rapidly become a public health intervention. However, the end of Affordable Connectivity Program funding in 2024 demonstrated the vulnerability of access strategies that depend on temporary political consensus.7 Advances in rural digital health depend on reliable broadband connectivity that currently does not reach every household, individual’s ability to maintain an internet subscription, router configurations, and technical savviness for application downloads or device troubleshooting.

SIM-based connectivity uses basic, ubiquitous technology to directly connect to cellular networks without relying on broadband, Wi-Fi, routers, or internet service providers. For health systems, SIM could shift the implementation question from “Does this patient have broadband?” to “Can this device obtain a reliable signal where the patient lives or receives care?” Admittedly, cellular dead zones remain a potential barrier because signal quality varies by geography, terrain, environment, and carrier. However, SIM/cellular availability is often more tractable than fixed broadband deployment across regions. Current cellular infrastructure can support high-quality audiovisual communications and data transmission, and upcoming sixth generation is estimated to enable improvements in streaming, deep learning possibilities, and advanced security features.8

We envision immediate clinical imperatives for SIM connectivity. In hypertension, a digital blood pressure cuff can automatically transmit readings via a SIM-enabled smartphone without requiring the patient to record values manually. In heart failure, a connected scale or biometric monitor can support diuretic adjustment, clinician outreach, and close surveillance. In diabetes and cardiometabolic disease, cellular glucometers or continuous glucose monitoring hubs can help align risk factor management with cardiovascular prevention. In emergency medical services, SIM-based transmission of vital signs, electrocardiograms, and other prehospital data can narrow the diagnostic gap created by long transport times. Cellular tablets can also support community health workers during home visits and mobile clinic encounters.

Thus, enhanced connectivity can be seen as an investment in equity infrastructure. SIM connectivity could be conceptualized as an essential component for digital or technological tools rather than a consumer-led add-on feature. Health systems, federally qualified health centers, payers, device manufacturers, and carriers could create health-specific connectivity programs in which devices arrive preconfigured, data transmission is automated, and patients are not responsible for monthly connectivity costs. Medicare remote physiologic monitoring policy already recognizes the clinical logic of connected devices that collect and transmit physiologic data for chronic and acute conditions.9,10

Current experience mandates several essential safeguards for program design as exemplified in Table 1. Connectivity alone is not care. Data streams must be paired with clinical workflows, escalation protocols, staffing models, and accountability for response. Deploying a SIM-enabled rural cardiac program introduces potential risks, most of which are technology-related, including software reliability and the need for digital and health literacy training for all employees and users. The real-time detection of an acute event relies heavily on reliable data transfer; however, this process can fail due to cellular dead zones, high-volume networks, or slow system response times. While the use of SIM-based systems is important, they will never be sufficient to address acute events on their own, and therefore, there is potential for increased liability due to delayed responses when using these systems. Programs must address digital literacy, community life-saving skill training, language access, disability access, and trust. A device mailed without training may generate no data, unreliable data, or user uncertainty about the data. Privacy and data security are essential components for the safe and ethical use of mobile health technologies, especially if devices are shared, loaned, used in multigenerational households, or supported by community workers. Equity measurement should be built into deployment: enrollment, activation, data completeness, response times, medication changes, and outcomes should be stratified by rurality, race, ethnicity, language, insurance status, and neighborhood deprivation. Finally, SIM connectivity is not a substitute for broadband investment. The core insight guiding this strategy is clear: in rural cardiovascular care, geography should not determine whether a clinician can see a blood pressure trend, respond to early decompensation, or support a patient during transitions of care. SIM connectivity can decouple digital health from household broadband and make the patient’s location less determinative of access to timely care. Delivering technology that can actualize real-time interventions is essential. Vendors must address consumer-level barriers to enhance user interface and prevent connectivity issues. In digital health equity, the end justifies the means. The end goal is to achieve improved health care delivery in our increasingly technological environment, which will require redesigning care around the realities of patients' lives.
