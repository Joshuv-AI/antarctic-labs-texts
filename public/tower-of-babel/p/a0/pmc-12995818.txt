TLR8 gain-of-function: on the highway to the danger zone
Authors: Alice Y Chan, Christopher C Dvorak
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Arnold et al1 expand the inflammation, neutropenia, bone marrow failure, and lymphoproliferation caused by toll-like receptor 8 (INFLTR8) cohort to 10 patients and focus on therapeutic outcomes, particularly medical management and allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT). Extending the initial 2021 report of 6 male patients with germ line or somatic variants,2 they report the first female patient with full disease due to a germ line heterozygous variant and expresses both alleles, establishing X-linked dominant inheritance. All harbor gain-of-function (GOF) TLR8 mutations that drive disease refractory to immunomodulation and fatal in nearly all cases without HCT.

The TLRs are a family of innate immune receptors that recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from microbes or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from stressed, injured, or dying host cells. TLR8, an endosomal receptor, specifically recognizes single-stranded RNA from viruses, bacteria, and host cells. It is primarily expressed in monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and myeloid dendritic cells, but it is also expressed in nonhematopoietic cell types.3 When TLR8 is activated, myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) protein is recruited, leading to activation of the NF-κB pathway. This drives transcription of proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and type I interferons (-alpha and -beta), which in turn activate T and natural killer cells and also further activate myeloid and dendritic cells. Consequently, in patients with TLR8-GOF variants, their innate immune system continuously senses danger, resulting in persistent inflammation.

Patients with INFLTR8 have inborn errors of immunity (IEI), which are considered as primary immune regulatory disorders,4 and are characterized by severe immune-mediated manifestations. They develop refractory cytopenias (especially neutropenia), hepatosplenomegaly, oral ulcers, and recurrent infections. Pneumonia is a particularly common infection possibly due to expression of TLR8 in bronchial epithelial cells.5 The combination of susceptibility to infection plus refractory immune-mediated manifestations makes these patients challenging to treat. Immunosuppressive therapies, including corticosteroids, are often insufficient to control disease manifestations and HCT was often pursued. Although the number of reported cases of this rare disease is low, several key diagnostic and treatment features can be gleaned from this report:

Given the established role of TLR8 hyperactivation in driving disease, therapies that directly inhibit TLR8 signaling would be ideal and could serve as a bridge to safer, more effective HCT, analogous to JAK inhibitors in STAT1 GOF disorder.6 In vitro studies have demonstrated that chloroquine can suppress cytokine production induced by TLR8 agonists, and clinically available agents that target endosomal TLR signaling (eg, hydroxychloroquine) exist. However, 1 patient treated with hydroxychloroquine showed no clinical benefit. Several TLR8 antagonists are currently under evaluation in clinical trials and may offer future therapeutic promise.

The optimal approach to HCT for these patients remains somewhat unclear given both disease severity at time of HCT plus the small numbers (n = 6) of patients who underwent transplantation. A general principle of HCT for treatment of IEI due to GOF variants is that, unlike patients with loss-of-function variants who can have complete phenotypic disease correction with mixed donor chimerism, these patients likely require 100% donor engraftment in the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) and T-cell lineage. In fact, somatic variants as low as 7% were sufficient to mediate fatal disease. Only 3 patients received conditioning that included a classical HSC-clearing agent, such busulfan or treosulfan, whereas the others received regimens primarily of immunoablative agents, including fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, serotherapy, and low-dose total body irradiation. Interestingly, all survivors were 100% donor chimeric in all measured cell fractions, although one required donor lymphocyte infusions for treatment of falling donor chimerism.

Given that PAMPs and DAMPs function as potent stimulators for host and donor-derived antigen presenting cells (APCs), which in turn activate and amplify the responses of alloreactive donor T cells, they are linked to the initiation of graft-versus-host-disease (GVHD).7 Standard conditioning prior to HCT is typically insufficient to eliminate all residual host APCs, which in this disease are hyper activatable. Not surprisingly, GVHD in this cohort was very high, with 4 of 6 patients showing signs of classic acute or chronic GVHD. Furthermore, 3 of 6 patients developed an unusual post-HCT pancytopenia, which might be a form of marrow-directed GVHD,8 such that 100% of patients required additional immunosuppressive treatment after HCT. However, this graft-versus-marrow effect may also explain why all patients achieved 100% donor chimerism.

From this limited experience, to hopefully avoid severe toxicities and GVHD, some general principles of HCT for this rare disease might be developed:

•As with most IEIs, the closest HLA-matched donor should be utilized.

•There is likely minimal need for traditional HSC-clearing agents, such as busulfan, treosulfan, or melphalan, which may produce superfluous toxicity in these patients.

In summary, Arnold et al have substantially added to our knowledge of this rare disorder and underscored the importance of personalized therapies tailored to specific immune-pathway defects. Now that clinicians are warned to be on the lookout for patients with TLR8 GOF variants and better understand their optimal management, we might be able to start assisting these patients with exiting their personal highway to the danger zone.
