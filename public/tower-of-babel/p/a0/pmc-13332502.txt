Do home modifications reduce caregiver burden? A cross-sectional analysis of factors associated with caregiver burden and resilience in Hong Kong
Authors: Tarani Chandola, Patrick Rouxel, Vivian W Q Lou, Simon Ng, Vera Mun Yu Tang, Tom Chun Wai Tsoi, Wenyi Lin, Jianchao Quan, Wai Sze Chan, Peiyi Lu, Xingnan Yi, Julie Blaskewicz Boron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe impact of modifiable housing factors on the mental health of caregivers of older adults and people with disabilities is often underestimated. This study examines the role of housing modifications in influencing caregiver burden and resilience in Hong Kong.Research Design and MethodsData from a population-based survey of 2,960 caregivers of older adults (aged 60 and older) and younger individuals with disabilities (under 60) were analyzed. Modifications to the care recipients’ homes—classified as any, wheelchair-accessible, stair/chairlift, or other modifications—and the proportion of homes modified within each building or estate were assessed. Quantile regression models of the Caregiver Burden Inventory and the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale were used to estimate the association between home modifications and caregiver burden and resilience.ResultsCaregivers living with their care recipients in buildings or estates where all homes had been modified reported approximately 0.3 standard deviations lower burden (at the 90th quantile) and 0.23 standard deviations higher resilience (at the 80th quantile) than those in non-modified buildings or estates. Similar associations were observed between modifications of individual care recipients’ homes and caregiver burden, although not for resilience.Discussion and ImplicationsThere is some evidence that home modifications are associated with reduced caregiver burden and increased caregiver resilience, particularly when measured at the building or estate level. Enhancing the housing quality of older adults and people with disabilities may improve not only their well-being, but also that of their caregivers.Keywords: Housing interventions, Caregiving, Resilience, Building modifications, Disability

Our findings highlight the important role of home modifications in reducing caregiver burden and enhancing resilience, especially when implemented across entire buildings or estates. By demonstrating that caregivers experience significantly lower burden and higher resilience in modified environments, this research underscores the value of housing interventions. Improving housing quality for older adults can have far-reaching benefits, not just for people with disabilities but also for their caregivers.

Caregiver burden, a multidimensional construct encompassing functional limitation, disturbance behavior, and caregiving self-efficacy (Chou et al., 2002), is a challenge for many informal, unpaid family members supporting older adults (aged 60+) and people with disabilities. This burden arises from prolonged caregiving demands, often leading to chronic stress, depression, and social isolation among caregivers. In Hong Kong, where 43% of older adults with disabilities rely on informal family care (Census and Statistics Department, Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, 2021), the sustainability of this model is threatened by poor housing conditions that may exacerbate the burdens of caregivers. Despite extensive research on psychosocial interventions (e.g., support groups, psychoeducation) (Sun et al., 2022) and pharmacological treatments for care recipients (Adelman et al., 2014), the role of environmental factors, particularly housing, remains underexplored as a determinant of caregivers’ mental health.

Housing, a foundational social determinant of health (Bentley et al., 2025), provides more than shelter. It shapes safety, autonomy, and stress levels (Clapham et al., 2018; Rolfe et al., 2020). Yet, mental health research has historically prioritized medicalized interventions over modifiable environmental factors (Engel, 1977). This gap is striking given evidence linking poor housing quality (e.g., inadequate accessibility, overcrowding) to poor health (Bentley et al., 2025). Home modifications, such as installing ramps, handrails, or stairlifts, help people with disabilities, such as mobility impairments, to safely move inside and outside the home, thereby enhancing their independence (Chandola & Rouxel, 2022). However, the potential for home modifications to alleviate their caregivers’ physical and emotional burden—by reducing the need for manual transfers, minimizing fall risks, or enabling uninterrupted sleep—remains debatable. Despite increasing research on intelligent assistive technologies to reduce the burden of caregiving for older adults (Ezulike et al., 2025), there is a concern that (technologically) simpler changes to the home environment may be overlooked.

Existing studies have resulted in conflicting insights. A U.S. randomized trial found no significant improvement in caregivers’ self-efficacy or emotional distress after home modifications for dementia patients, despite marginal reductions in objective burden measures (Gitlin et al., 2001, 2003). Conversely, Australian and Taiwanese studies reported fewer caregiving hours and lower stress levels post-modification (Carnemolla & Bridge, 2019; Yang et al., 2021). These discrepancies may reflect methodological biases: observational studies struggle to disentangle the benefits of home modifications from the higher care needs they often accompany. For instance, caregivers in Thailand reported reduced physical burden in homes with one handrail but increased strain in homes with multiple rails, a paradox likely tied to the severity of care recipients’ disabilities (Phetsitong & Vapattanawong, 2022). It is difficult for observational studies to control for selection into home modifications. This selection bias may be reduced by analyzing building-level measures of home modifications, which are less directly determined by any single care recipient’s care needs than modifications within individual homes.

In the Stress Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1990), stressors such as caregiving burden are primary stressors, whereas social and psychosocial resources can limit the impact of these stressors. Resilience models also emphasize the role of social and environmental factors that support and enhance workers in high-risk occupations to adapt to the stressors they face (De Terte & Stephens, 2014). For example, home modifications enabling care recipients to use bathrooms independently may reduce caregivers’ nighttime interruptions, improving sleep quality and recovery. If housing interventions can differentially affect negative and positive dimensions of caregiving, this requires an analysis of dual outcomes of burden reduction and resilience.

Hong Kong’s hyper-dense, high-rise housing stock presents distinct challenges. Over 50% of residents occupy public housing estates, many constructed before 1990 without universal design principles. Older buildings may lack elevators, and private tenants, often living in small units measuring under 400 square feet, face financial and logistical barriers to retrofitting (Chan et al., 2009). Yet, initiatives like the Hong Kong Government’s Housing Society’s Age-Friendly Home Program suggest that home modification solutions are possible: occupational therapists assess fall risks, recommend modifications, and subsidize assistive devices. NGOs in Hong Kong also provide modification services to older people living in public rental housing, including grab bars and assistive technology for the residents. Although other high-income cities may differ significantly from Hong Kong in terms of their building and housing stock, they all share common challenges related to aging in place. Insights from the effects of home modifications in Hong Kong on aging-related processes may be relevant to other high-income urban contexts.

Guided by the Stress Process Model and resilience theory, this study tests two hypotheses:

H1. Direct Stress Reduction: Caregivers in modified homes report lower burden due to reduced physical caregiving tasks.

H2. Resilience Enhancement: Home modifications improve caregivers’ restorative capacity.

Caregiver burden is influenced by many factors potentially correlated with the home environment. There is potential for reverse causality: homes with modifications may house care recipients with greater needs, increasing the burden of caregiving. To address this, we also analyze building-level modifications (e.g., ramps in housing estates), which are less subject to individual selection bias. Additionally, we control for a wide range of potential confounding factors in the statistical models, which include the socioeconomic and demographic backgrounds of the care recipient and caregiver, specific caregiving tasks, and support/services received.

Method

Data

A survey in Hong Kong (2024) was conducted of caregivers providing care (for at least 6 hr per week) for adults aged 60 and older or for someone with disabilities aged below 60 years. Caregivers looking after adults with clinical diagnoses of dementia (0.7% of the total population) or acute health conditions were excluded due to their different care needs. A two-stage stratified random sample design (addresses within neighborhood blocks) was used to draw a sample. Invitation letters were mailed to 37,841 addresses prior to household visits to check eligibility and consent. This resulted in a sample of 2,960 caregivers, with 2,284 interviews conducted with carers of older adults and 676 in-person interviews with carers of people with disabilities. A weighting variable for carers of older adults was calculated by age, gender, and economic status using the 2021 Hong Kong population census data to make the survey data representative of the Hong Kong population.

Explanatory variables

Home modifications: We adopted questions from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (Zhao et al., 2014) and added specific items from the Health & Retirement Study (Sonnega et al., 2014). Caregivers were asked if the care recipients’ home had features to help older persons or someone with a disability get around. Features included wheelchair-friendly building or entrance facilities (e.g., ramps), stairlifts/devices to assist with stairs, and other features such as accessible toilet/shower/kitchen, handrails, grab bars, and bed levers/rails. Four variables were derived: any modifications, wheelchair, stair/chairlift, and other.

Around 74% of caregivers lived with their care recipient. From this subsample of caregivers who lived with their care recipient, we derived characteristics of buildings or estates where the latter lived. There was substantial clustering of the sample at the building or estate level—around 75% lived in buildings or estates with five or more sampled caregiver respondents. We derived a measure of modifications at the building/estate level (where the care recipient lived with the caregiver), indicating the proportion of homes with any, wheelchair, stair/chairlift, or other modifications. This building/estate-level measure was dichotomized so that “0” indicated buildings or estates where less than 50% of homes had been modified, and “1” indicated buildings or estates where 50% or more of the homes had been modified. Additionally, caregivers were asked if the care recipients’ building had an elevator, from which a binary “lift” indicator was created.

Dependent variables

The Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI) is a 24-item questionnaire with five sub-dimensions measuring time-dependent, developmental, physical, social, and emotional burden. Each of the 24 questions has five response options from “never” to “nearly always,” yielding a score from 0 (no burden) to 96 (extreme burden). The Chinese version’s reliability and validity have been established (Chou et al., 2002).

The Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) is a 25-item measure of positive adaptation to stress or trauma (Connor & Davidson, 2003). It evaluates a person’s positive adaptation through five main factors: personal competence and tenacity, trust in one’s instincts and acceptance of stress, positive acceptance of change and secure relationships, control, and spiritual influence. CD-RISC-10 is a 10-item version validated for use in Hong Kong (Ni et al., 2016). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from “never” to “nearly always,” with scores ranging from 0 (very low resilience) to 40 (very high resilience).

Control variables

As this is a cross-sectional study with limited causal inference, we controlled for a prespecified set of potential confounders identified from prior literature and substantive knowledge. Caregiver characteristics included age, sex, education, caregiving hours (past 6 months), years of caregiving, financial condition, employment status, number of family members, whether they had relatives/friends or domestic helpers to share caregiving, whether they received carer support services (e.g., psychological counseling, training, education), and the caregiving tasks provided (supervision, financial/emotional support, activities of daily living (ADLs), instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), communication, decision-making). Care recipient characteristics included their disability status (limitations in ADLs, disability types, health conditions), housing tenure, and district of residence. With around 60–70 parameters and over 2,000 observations in each model, there are many observations per parameter, which limits the risk of overfitting.

Analysis plan

We used quantile regression models (Rios-Avila, 2020) to estimate the association between home modifications and the two dependent variables (CBI and CD-RISC-10). Quantile regression is useful in stress and burnout models, as stronger effects of risk/protective factors are expected at the extremes of the distribution for stress, such as at the 90th percentile or quantile (Atkins et al., 2020). Standard models estimating effects at the mean may underestimate effects at higher stress levels. We used the recentered influence function (RIF) method, applying the RIF transformation at different quantiles of caregiver burden and resilience. A double robust approach was used with a treatment model specified (predicting home modifications) from which inverse probability weights were derived and used to adjust the quantile regression models for selection into the treatment (details in Supplementary Text 1).

Results

Table 1 shows the distribution of all caregiver and care recipient variables by any home modifications. Around 24.5% of care recipients lived in homes with modifications. Among those, “other” modifications were most common (78%), followed by wheelchair (56%) and stairlift or chairlift modifications (42%). Caregivers for care recipients in modified homes had significantly greater caregiving burden (mean CBI 25.5 vs. 23.2 in non-modified homes) but also greater resilience (mean CD-RISC 24.6 vs. 23.1). Care recipients in modified homes had higher levels of disability and health conditions. Their caregivers provided more caregiving hours and helped with more tasks. Care recipients in public rental or subsidized homes were more likely to live in modified homes. Supplementary Table 2 shows similar distributions by home modifications at the building or estate level. Around 17% of caregivers living with their care recipients resided in buildings or estates where 50% or more of the homes had some modification.

Supplementary Figures 3 and 4 display the corresponding figures for resilience by any home modifications and home modifications in the building/estate, respectively. Additionally, the distribution of resilience is not as skewed as the CBI, although the peak of resilience for those living in modified homes or buildings/estates occurs at the 80th quantile of resilience. It is also clear that the distribution of resilience among caregivers living in modified homes or estates/buildings (the green bars in Panel A in Supplementary Figures 3 and 4) has a rightward shift compared to those living in non-modified homes or estates/buildings (the red bars). Among caregivers living in buildings or estates with fewer modifications, resilience peaks around the 20th percentile, whereas among those in buildings or estates with more modifications, resilience peaks around the 30th percentile.

Caregivers of care recipients living in modified homes did not report significantly higher resilience at quantiles 10 to 90 (see Supplementary Figure 3 Panel A). In contrast, at quantiles 20, 60, 70, and 80 in Supplementary Figure 4 Panel B, resilience was significantly higher among caregivers who lived with their care recipients in buildings/estates where half or more of the homes had been modified, compared to those living in buildings/estates where fewer than half of the homes had been modified. Because the 20th, 60th, 70th, and 80th quantiles correspond approximately to the lowest-resilience 20% and an additional 20% of caregivers with moderate-to-high resilience, these estimates imply that building/estate modifications could positively affect resilience for around 40% of caregivers.

Because the strongest associations were concentrated at the upper ends of the CBI and resilience distributions, the remaining figures and tables derived from quantile regression models focus on the 90th quantile for CBI and the 80th quantile for resilience. Associations between specific home modification types and outcomes are shown in Figures 1 (caregiver burden) and 2 (resilience). Caregiver burden was significantly lower among those who cared for people living in modified homes (−4.4, 95% CI: −7.7, −1.1), and this pattern was observed for homes with wheelchair modifications, stair/chair/lift modifications, or other modifications (Figure 1). For home modifications at the building/estate level (Figure 2), much of the significantly lower caregiving burden associated with building/estates where the majority of the homes had been modified (an effect size of approximately 0.3 SD of caregiver burden) appeared to be driven by the large negative effect of living in a building with a lift (−11.5, 95% CI: −19.4, −3.6). The full model coefficients for Figure 1 are in Supplementary Table 3, and the corresponding coefficients for Figure 2 are in Supplementary Table 4. In the propensity-score–reweighted models (Supplementary Tables 3–6), some districts have no observations for specific home modification types (e.g., wheelchair or stair/chair/lift), leading to perfect prediction of treatment status and the exclusion of all observations from these districts. For resilience, individual-level home modifications do not appear to increase the resilience of caregivers (Figure 2), but at the building or estate level, there is a consistent pattern of an increase in resilience at the 80th quantile, associated with any wheelchair, stair/chair/lift, and other modifications. Caregivers of care recipients living in buildings/estates where the majority of homes were modified reported 1.5 units higher resilience (95% CI: 0.4, 2.6) than those living in buildings/estates where less than 50% of the homes were modified (Supplementary Table 6). This corresponds to an effect size of 0.23 SD of the resilience scale.

We carried out some sensitivity analyses, restricting the analysis to older (aged 60+) care recipients alone. This did not change the results much, as the majority of the sample (77%) were caregivers of older adults. We also examined whether there was an interaction between the age group of the caregivers (younger or older than age 60) and the home modifications variables, but there was little evidence of an age patterning in the results by the age of the caregivers. Results were very similar in more parsimonious models that included only core sociodemographic and disability measures (not shown), suggesting that our findings are not driven by model overfitting.

Discussion

This study found some evidence supporting both our hypothesis that home modifications are associated with lower caregiver burden and higher caregiver resilience. This association appeared stronger for caregivers of care recipients living in buildings or estates where the majority of surveyed homes had modifications. Caregivers living with care recipients in buildings or estates with a greater proportion of modified homes tended to report significantly lower burden (at the 90th quantile of caregiver burden distribution) and much higher resilience (at the 80th quantile of resilience distribution). In other words, modifications may particularly reduce caregiving burden among the worst-off 10% of caregivers and enhance resilience for a substantial minority, roughly one in five caregivers.

The increase in caregiver burden associated with living in a building without a lift was large. Although only 6% of caregivers lived in such buildings, the strong association highlights potential pathways from home modifications to caregiver well-being. Some modifications likely reduce physical demands—such as lifting or carrying adults with mobility impairments—thereby reducing muscular strain and pain among caregivers. The positive association between wheelchairs and stair/chairlift modifications in the building or estate and resilience also suggests that improved care recipient independence could enable caregivers to recover better from caregiving stressors.

Some previous research has shown that home modifications were associated with greater depressive symptoms (Thomése & Broese Van Groenou, 2006), or that non-frail older adults with modifications were more likely to be institutionalized than those without (Hollinghurst et al., 2020). These contradictory findings likely reflect the nonrandom distribution of home modifications; modifications are installed when care recipients develop disability (Wu & Grundy, 2025). Hence, spillover effects on other members of the household are important to examine due to the lower risk of such selection into home modifications. For example, a decline in one spouse’s functioning may lead to home modifications that later benefit the other spouse. Eriksen et al. (2015) found that such modifications reduced serious fall risk for the second spouse by 20%. Similarly, our results show how home modifications may benefit the caregivers of care recipients, even if they may have been installed primarily due to the care recipients’ condition.

Around a quarter of caregivers reported their care recipients lived in a home that had some modifications. Although it is difficult to obtain comparable estimates of home modifications from other contexts, in England this proportion ranged from 22% in 2006 to 60% in 2019 (Wu & Grundy, 2025). The Our Hong Kong Foundation has recommended expanding access to home modification services in both public and private housing to promote aging in place (Ip et al., 2025). Modifications may be especially important in Hong Kong given the low rate of homeownership and aging public housing stock, as they can support older adults’ mobility, autonomy, and ability to remain safely in their homes. Although Hong Kong’s unique housing stock limits generalizability to rural or suburban contexts, many cities around the world face similar aging-in-place challenges with aging populations and aging housing stocks. With 50% of Hong Kong’s population in public housing, retrofitting older estates and buildings with mandatory accessibility features could yield population-level reductions in caregiver burden and improvements in older adults’ functioning, fall risk, and likelihood of avoiding or delaying institutionalization. At the community level, more accessible housing can facilitate older adults’ social participation within high-rise estates and reduce isolation. At the societal level, investments in home modifications may help contain long-term care and healthcare costs and promote more equitable aging trajectories in dense urban settings. The government’s existing Age-Friendly Home Program could be expanded, whereas private-sector participation could be incentivized through tax rebates or grants for landlords installing modifications. Moreover, housing assessments could be integrated into caregiver support, geriatric, and primary care services so that both caregiver needs and older adults’ home environments are explicitly addressed in plans to support aging in place.

This study provides strong support for our hypotheses while addressing mixed findings in earlier research. Although causal claims can’t be firmly made using cross-sectional data, the consistency and strength of the observed protective associations increase confidence in these results. Extensive controls for care recipient disability and health help mitigate selection bias.

Despite rigorous controls for care recipient disability and socioeconomic confounders, the observed associations from cross-sectional data limit causal inference. Unmeasured confounders, such as caregiver coping styles or cultural norms around familial caregiving, were not assessed but may mediate modification effects. Moreover, estate/building-level modification measures were derived from the survey data, which did not include all the homes within the estate or building. Although the sample was from a stratified random sampling process, there may not have been enough addresses within an estate or building to accurately describe the level of modifications.

Although psychoeducational and psychosocial interventions remain essential for caregiver well-being, housing conditions also deserve attention. Home modifications may reduce burden, particularly for caregivers of recipients with physical or mobility limitations. Notably, modifications at the estate level were stronger than individual home-level modifications. Improving housing quality for older adults and people with disabilities may improve not just their outcomes but also lower the burden and increase the resilience of caregivers.
