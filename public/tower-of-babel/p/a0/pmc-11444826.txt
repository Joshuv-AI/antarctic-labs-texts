Ultrasonographic Study of the Corkscrew Arterial Image in Buerger Disease Patients
Authors: Kaori Homma, Tomoko Kagayama, Takehisa Iwai, Hiroko Kume, Shinya Koizumi, Kenichi Sakurazawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective: Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease) is known as an intractable vascular disease that has been reported as thrombosis in distal arteries and occasional venous occlusion, as well as inflammatory changes in the thrombus and vascular wall. Patients often require limb amputation due to limb necrosis. Corkscrew (CS), a small arterial coiling, is an important diagnostic finding that was mainly found with angiography. Recently, however, it can also be identified using a modern ultrasonographic technique.Methods: In these 22 cases, in 48 areas of study, we used the ultrasonographic technique to identify the CS, which allowed us to observe its relationship with the surrounding nerves and arteries.Results: In all cases, it was possible to identify the CS easily and it was confirmed that the CS and the nerve were carried down in their sheath. The sites of the CS existed in areas other than the area around the occluded main arteries and some CS that ran inside the nerve (16 areas) and some CS that accompanied the outside of the nerve (10 areas) were confirmed, suggesting the CS work as collateral blood supply vessels, with well-developed normal vessel-like anatomy.Conclusion: When we observe the CS, it is important to observe not only around the main trunk artery but also areas where nerves mainly run, even if they do not accompany the main trunk artery. (This is a translation of Jpn J Vasc Surg 2023; 32: 345–350.)

Introduction

Buerger’s disease (thromboangiitis obliterans [TAO]) was reported as an illness characterized by thrombi and inflammatory changes in the arterial and venous vessel wall in the thrombotic occlusion site based on a pathological study by Winiwarter in Austria in 1878.1) Then, Buerger reported detailed pathological studies on the illness for a long period.2) Clinical symptoms start with numbness and coldness and progress to intermittent claudication, pain at rest, and ulcer. It is an intractable disease often requiring limb amputation due to necrosis. In Japan, the number of cases meeting the criteria and being diagnosed as TAO has recently been decreasing.3,4) Several diagnostic criteria for TAO have been reported, and all of them are stringent. In Japan, the criteria of Shionoya5) tend to be used. TAO is diagnosed when the following criteria are met: (1) onset before the age of 50 years, (2) history of smoking (many are heavy cigarette smokers), (3) presence of infrapopliteal artery occlusive lesions, (4) history of upper limb arterial occlusive lesions or phlebitis migrans, and (5) absence of arteriosclerosis-promoting factors (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus).4–7) If the patient has even one of the arteriosclerosis-promoting factors, TAO is not diagnosed, and angiography is not performed usually. One of the angiography findings, the presence or absence of corkscrew (CS) collateral, is considered an important criterion for the diagnosis and pathophysiological characterization of TAO.8,9) Furthermore, the remarkable technological advancement of ultrasonographs has made it possible to identify CS through noninvasive ultrasonography.10,11) In particular, a new technique, superb microvascular imaging (SMI), can visualize fine, low-velocity blood flow, which is difficult to visualize with the color Doppler method, for more detailed observation of the CS morphology. Here, we report the successful observation of the morphology of CSs and surrounding nerves and arteries by performing additional ultrasonographic assessments based on the diagnosis of CSs through angiography.

Subjects and Methods

Twenty-two patients who were diagnosed with TAO (September 2020–November 2022) according to the diagnostic criteria of Shionoya and underwent ultrasonography after the presence of CSs was confirmed through angiography were included in this study. The findings on the relationships of CSs with the surrounding tissues were also examined. The diagnosis of TAO was made using the aforementioned Shionoya criteria. Furthermore, other items evaluated included angiograms as well as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia as comorbidities since onset. As the differential diagnoses that are often confused, the following conditions were carefully ruled out: collagenosis, thoracic outlet syndrome with peripheral artery occlusion of hands and fingers, popliteal artery entrapment syndrome, and cystic adventitial disease of the popliteal artery. The Aplio a550 CUS-AA550 diagnostic ultrasound system (Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) was used to observe the CSs, and 7.5 MHz and 12.0 MHz linear probes were used as probes. All patients underwent an angiographic examination (angiography or contrast-enhanced computed tomography), and detailed observation was performed for the sites of CSs. The investigation involved broad observation around the CSs confirmed through angiography; once the CS was confirmed, the morphological observation using SMI in addition to B-mode observation and observation/evaluation of the vascular diameters of the CSs, surrounding nerves (nerves with a diameter of about 1.0 mm or more that could be differentiated), and accompanying arteries in the same manner.

Results

The mean age at the investigation was 63 ± 13 (29–88) years. The patients included 21 males and 1 female. All patients were considered to be in the chronic phase because their clinical symptoms at the time of the investigation included neither elevated C-reactive protein, pain, ulcer, nor necrosis. For all sites where the CSs were confirmed through angiography, ultrasonography provided similar images, with which CSs could be determined and their morphology could be observed (Fig. 1). Three CSs were found in the upper limbs, and 45 CSs were found in the lower limbs. Observation from the forearm to the palms and from the inguinal area to the ankle joint area showed that 40 of the 48 CSs identified were accompanied by nerves. All of these CSs ran within or accompanied the nerve sheath (Fig. 2). For eight sites, ultrasonography was unable to determine whether the CSs were accompanied by nerves. The nerves accompanying CSs were the tibial nerve in 16 sites, the saphenous nerve in five sites, the sciatic nerve in four sites, the deep peroneal nerve in four sites, the superficial peroneal nerve in three sites, the common peroneal nerve in two sites, the medial sural cutaneous nerve in one site in the lower extremity, and the median nerve in two sites in the upper extremity. The presence of nerves accompanying two CSs running under the medial fascia of the knee and a branch of the tibial nerve accompanying a CS was confirmed, but their names were unclear. The measured CS diameters ranged from 0.7 to 2.5 mm, and detailed observation of the CS vessels was possible using SMI, even for those with a diameter of 1.0 mm or smaller. Moreover, detailed observation of the CS vessels made it possible to classify the mode of pathways for all 40 CS sites where the nerves could be identified. The CSs were divided into three categories: (1) CSs running inside the nerve sheath (16 sites) (Fig. 3A), (2) CSs running outside the nerve sheath while accompanying the nerve (10 sites) (Fig. 3B), and (3) CSs that were confirmed to accompany the nerve but could not be determined whether they were running inside or outside the nerve based on the investigation images (14 sites) (Fig. 4). There was no CS-like recanalization within the occluded arteries. The CSs developed proximally to the arterial lesion (site of obstruction or site suspected of being obstructed previously) and maintained distal inflow and blood flow to distal arteries through bifurcation, suggesting that the CSs are collateral pathways of the arteries. The thrombi in the obstructed arteries were organized, and all the thrombi were considered to represent old, chronic arterial occlusions. Twenty CSs (tibial and deep peroneal nerves) were associated with the obstructed arteries, and 20 CSs (sciatic, saphenous, medial sural cutaneous, superficial peroneal, common peroneal, and median nerves) were associated with the nerves distant from the obstructed arteries. Regarding the development of other CSs in the limb arteries, no CSs were observed around the subclavian, axillary, brachial, radial, and ulnar arteries in the upper limbs and the iliac, common femoral, superficial femoral, and deep femoral arteries in the lower limbs. By age at the time of investigation, one patient was in their 20s, one patient was in their 30s, six patients were in their 50s, seven patients were in their 60s, five patients were in their 70s, and two patients were in their 80s (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, all CSs determined through angiography were also confirmed through ultrasonography, demonstrating that CSs can be evaluated equally effectively through ultrasonography. Therefore, the presence of CS was considered possible to confirm through noninvasive ultrasonography, without using invasive angiography, in patients with a positive result of the Allen test, an investigation of the upper limb circulation, or clinical symptoms or other signs suggesting TAO. Moreover, ultrasonography enabled us to observe the CSs and their positional relationships with the surrounding tissues simultaneously and confirm the presence of the nerves. In all cases where the presence of nerves was confirmed, the CSs accompanied the nerves, and recanalization of the accompanying artery was mostly not observed. The CSs occurred in the arterial regions with obstruction or previous obstructive changes, such as severe wall thickening. CSs were suggested to play a bypassing role based on blood inflow to the distal part of the artery or to maintain blood flow to the distal side, as the CSs subsequently branched into smaller vessels and were considered to be vasa nervorum based on their pathways alongside nerves. Baş et al.12) investigated CSs and vasa nervorum using magnetic resonance imaging and ultrasonography and reported that all CSs identified in the lower limbs with popliteal artery occlusion in TAO were vasa nervorum associated with the tibial nerve. Our ultrasonographic examinations of wide areas around the CSs showed that the greatest number of the CS vessels accompanied the tibial nerve, and other vasa nervorum also developed as CSs. Anatomically, each nerve is fed by a different vasa nervorum, which is fed by branches from arteries to maintain blood flow.13–15) Observing the CSs and the nerves revealed CSs running within and outside the nerve sheath. The CSs running within the nerve sheath were characteristically associated with relatively large nerves, such as the tibial and sciatic nerves, which are common examples. The CSs running outside the nerve sheath were associated with thinner nerves, such as the superficial peroneal nerve and the saphenous nerve, and these CSs were as thick as or thicker than the accompanying nerves. The highest number of CSs were observed around the tibial nerve. The feeding arteries of the posterior tibial artery supply blood flow mainly to the soleus muscle, and those of the anterior tibial artery feed mainly to the tibialis anterior muscle. Sato et al.16) have reported that the upper 1/5 of the soleus muscle is fed by branches of the popliteal and anterior tibial arteries, and the upper-lower, middle, and lower portions are fed by branches of the posterior tibial and peroneal arteries. It has been reported that many CSs are observed as collaterals around the posterior tibial artery because the posterior tibial artery provides the soleus muscle with more feeding arteries than the anterior tibial artery, in addition to the fact that the soleus muscle fed by the posterior tibial artery has large amounts of muscle mass and motion.5,8) The eight CSs around which no nerves were identified included some CSs of the fingers, which were unclassifiable because no nerves could be identified. Recently, high-frequency probes with a frequency of 20.0 MHz or higher and probes that can easily be attached to thin body parts such as fingers (e.g., hockey stick probes) have become available. Thus, the use of devices with a higher resolution power may allow for the depiction of nerves that were otherwise undetectable and confirmation of the presence of arteries accompanying thin nerves. Based on the age at the time of investigation, CSs could be confirmed even in the youngest patients in their 20s, and there was no difference in the degree of development of CSs between the younger patients and those with the disease long after the onset. This result suggests that the CSs are not injured by arteriosclerosis, develop as collateral pathways at a young age, and continue functioning as collateral pathways even in old age. In TAO, clinical symptoms often do not coincide with ankle–brachial index decline, and our findings suggest that the degree of development of the CSs and their role as bypass routes may be a contributing factor. It remains to be examined whether CSs around nerves directly act on these nerves and cause sensory disturbance and movement disorder or they act on autonomic nerves or other nerves that cause disorders.

Conclusion

The CS collaterals in Buerger’s disease (TAO) could be evaluated through ultrasonography. The ultrasonographic evaluation indicated that the CSs were vasa nervorum developed as collateral pathways. Furthermore, many CSs ran within or outside the nerve sheath. When we search for CSs, it is important to observe the area around the obstructed artery and the areas where no nerves accompany the arteries, such as areas around the saphenous nerve and superficial peroneal nerve.
