Estimation of onion crop evapotranspiration and crop coefficients using weighing lysimeters and machine learning models in semi-arid region
Authors: Saba Hashempour Motlagh Shirazi, Fatemeh Razzaghi, Ali Reza Sepaskhah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water scarcity is a significant challenge in Iran’s agricultural sector, particularly for onion (Allium cepa L.) cultivation, which is a vital crop for the country’s economy and diet. However, there is a lack of standardized data on onion evapotranspiration (ETc) in semi-arid conditions, making precise irrigation management difficult. To address this gap, a two-year field experiment was conducted at the Kooshkak Agricultural Research Station, Shiraz University, Iran to measure ETc using digital weighing lysimeters based on the water balance method and to develop predictive models using machine learning algorithms. The ETc of onion in the first and second years were 447.1 mm and 432.2 mm, respectively. Soil evaporation accounted for 36.6% and 32.8% of the total ETc in the first and second years, respectively. The average of single crop coefficient values for the initial, mid, and late growth stages across both years were 0.41, 0.68, and 0.51, respectively. Additionally, the basal crop coefficient values for the initial, mid, and late growth stages were 0.10, 0.51, and 0.37, respectively. These models utilized meteorological variables (temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, net radiation) and crop parameters (leaf area index and plant height) as input features and measured \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} was utilized as target outputs for calibrating and validating the model. Among the algorithms, the RF and DT achieved the highest predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.98, NRMSE = 0.04), followed by ANN (R2 = 0.97, NRMSE = 0.07), SVR (R2 = 0.97, NRMSE = 0.08), and LASSO (R2 = 0.85, NRMSE = 0.18). Using lysimeter measurements as reliable reference data to evaluate machine-learning models provides a dependable framework for optimizing irrigation scheduling and enhancing water-use efficiency in onion cultivation under semi-arid conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43887-w.

Introduction

Iran is predominantly arid and semi-arid1, with an annual mean precipitation of only about 235.2 mm, which is well below the global average2,3. Agriculture is the largest consumer of the country’s water resources4, emphasizing the critical need for efficient water management to ensure water security. Proper management and efficient use of agricultural water are therefore essential to sustainable production under water-scarce conditions5,6. Accurate measurement of crop evapotranspiration (ETc) is a critical factor in optimizing irrigation strategies and increasing water use efficiency7. The ETc represents the total water loss via soil evaporation and crop transpiration under non-stressed field conditions, and is influenced by climatic (i.e., grass reference evapotranspiration, ETo), crop, soil, and management factors8,9. The ETc is determined using direct and indirect methods8. Weighing lysimeters (the most accurate method) have been widely used to measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} using the water balance principle10,11. However, these systems are expensive to install and maintain, and their applicability is restricted in terms of spatial coverage12. In this regard, studies have been conducted on many crops such as onion using weighing lysimeters13, maize with four weighing lysimeters14, wheat and maize with two weighing lysimeters15, and barley with two weighing lysimeters16. To indirectly estimate the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}, it is necessary to calculate the grass reference crop evapotranspiration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}$$\end{document}) and crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}) that vary widely across crops and environments. These methodological challenges are particularly pronounced in semi-arid regions like Iran, where environmental heterogeneity and data scarcity hinder the development of region-specific standard ETc. The Penman-Monteith method is a widely recommended indirect and common method to calculate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}$$\end{document}, although, it requires a significant amount of climatic data, including temperature, humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation8 in comparison with other methods such as Hargreaves-Samani17, Blaney-Criddle18, and etc. The crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}) is a variable parameter that changes throughout the crop growth cycle and is influenced by factors such as crop type and management, growth stage, planting date, weather conditions, and soil properties8. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} is defined as the ratio of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}$$\end{document} and can be applied using either a single or dual crop coefficient approach. The single crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}) combines crop transpiration and soil evaporation. In contrast, the dual crop coefficient method separately considers transpiration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{b}}$$\end{document}) and soil evaporation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{e}}$$\end{document}). The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{b}}$$\end{document} represents plant transpiration under optimal conditions without additional soil wetting, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{e}}$$\end{document} reflects soil surface evaporation15.

In a study19, found that the water requirements for onions in a semi-arid region (Spain) for optimum yield (75 t \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{h}\mathrm{a}}^{-1}$$\end{document}) were 662.3 mm when using drip irrigation and plant density of 35 plant m−2. In a study conducted by20 in Albacete, Central Spain, the seasonal evapotranspiration of onions measured in the lysimeter was 893.4 mm with plant density of 40 plant m−2 in a continental climate with moderate humidity and sprinkler irrigation system. It was reported by Abyaneh et al.21 that the garlic water requirements in a semi-arid area of Iran, where cooler temperatures and partial rainfall contributed to lower water use, were 546.5 mm and 519.2 mm in 2008 and 2009, respectively. In a semi-arid region in Punjab, India22, reported onion evapotranspiration as 346.5 mm in 2004 and 323.1 mm in 2005 under full irrigation irrigated with micro-sprinkler irrigation system and limited seasonal rainfall (27–36 mm). The wide range of ETc values (323–893 mm) reported in the literature highlights the sensitivity of crop water requirements to environmental and management factors. This diversity underscores the current lack of standardized ETc data for onion cultivation under semi-arid Iranian conditions, which this study aims to address.

Onion (Allium cepa) belongs to the Alliaceae family, which is grown as an annual crop for bulb production and as a biennial crop for seed production23. Onion originated in Iran and its neighboring countries24. In the 2022–2023, the cultivated area of onion in Iran was 51,499 ha, with a total onion production of 2,422,637 tons. Onion is rich in vitamins such as ascorbic acid, thiamine, niacin and A, as well as antioxidants, proteins, and minerals like riboflavin, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, and iodine25. Despite being recognized as a water-sensitive crop and a staple in Iranian diets23–25, no comprehensive, standardized ETc and Kc dataset currently exists for onion under Iran’s semi-arid conditions. This lack of reliable data limits the calibration of irrigation scheduling and model-based water management systems.

Evapotranspiration is a highly nonlinear process influenced by meteorological, soil, and crop-related factors26, and its estimation accuracy strongly depends on both the quality and the diversity of available input data27. A cross-study comparison reveals several consistent patterns in ML model performance. Ensemble tree-based methods—such as Random Forest, Bagging, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost—tend to achieve strong results when high-dimensional meteorological or multisource datasets are available, as demonstrated in studies on sugar beet28, sugarcane29, and ETo estimation under drought conditions30. Support vector-based models (SVM, LSSVR) often perform best in data-limited contexts due to their strong generalization ability, with notable success reported for sugar beet28 and ETo estimation31. Deep architectures such as DNN outperform traditional ML models when soil water content or crop parameters (LAI, SWC) are included, as shown for maize transpiration in semi-arid regions of China32. In contrast, regression-based methods such as MNLR or M5P offer simpler and faster computation and may exceed neural networks when input-output relationships are more linear, as reported for potato ET33 and ETo estimation30,31. ANN-based models have shown strong performance in semi-arid barley systems due to lower data requirements, although they remain sensitive to parameterization and lack physical interpretability16.

Despite these advances, most prior studies exhibit recurring limitations: reliance on coarse resolution meteorological data rather than high-precision reference ET (e.g., lysimeter-based) measurements; evaluation across limited stations or single crops; and a lack of systematic comparison of multiple ML models under unified conditions. These shortcomings underscore the need for studies that integrate high-resolution ground-truth ET measurements and assess multiple ML approaches across diverse input scenarios in semi-arid environments—gaps that the present study directly addressed. Due to the dietary importance of onion and the limited availability of standardized ETc data for this crop under semi-arid conditions, this study was conducted. This study aims (1) to determine onion evapotranspiration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}), the single crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}), and the basal crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{b}}$$\end{document}) in a semi-arid region of Iran; (2) to analyze and compare five top used machine learning algorithms [(ANN, SVM, RF, DT (Decision Tree), and LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) Regression)] covering both linear and nonlinear modeling approaches for onion ETc simulation; and (3) to evaluate the relative contributions of meteorological vs. crop growth parameters in ETc estimation. The novelty of this research lies in using high-resolution lysimeter measurements as reliable reference data for evaluating multiple machine-learning approaches for onion production under semi-arid conditions, providing the first standardized ETc and Kc values for the region. This work not only identifies the most reliable predictive model but also enhances data-driven irrigation management for data-scarce environments.

Materials and methods

Experimental site description

A two-year experiment was conducted at the Kooshkak Agricultural Research Station, School of Agriculture, Shiraz University, situated in the Fars province, southwest; Iran with latitude of 30°4′45′′ N, longitude of 52°35′14′′ E, and 1620 m above mean sea level (Fig. 1). Kooshkak Agricultural Research Station is administratively affiliated with the School of Agriculture, Shiraz University, under the same institutional authority. The local climate of the study region is classified as semi-arid34. Climate data, including maximum and minimum temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, sunshine hours, and rainfall depth, were recorded daily in the weather station close to the experimental field during 2021–2022 and 2022–2023. Crop evapotranspiration measurement

Onion seeds (cv. Armaghan G.) were sown on 10 and 9 November 2021 and 2020 (following a typical practice of farmers that cultivate onion seeds in late autumn), respectively, in two large-scale weighing circular lysimeters (Fig. 1) and surrounding area. The onion seeds were obtained from local farmers. Each lysimeter had a surface area of 7.07 m2 and they were located centrally in a 1600 m2 experimental field (40 × 40 m) to minimize the side effects. Two lysimeters (with a resolution of measured daily \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}$$\end{document} of 0.28 mm (15-minute measuring intervals), 15-minute measuring intervals, a diameter of 3 m, and depth of 1.7 m), contained 11 rows of onion plants, with a row spacing of 30 cm and a plant spacing of 5 cm within each row. Prior to the experiment, both lysimeters were calibrated using step-loading methods to validate measurement precision under dynamic loading conditions35. Similar planting practices were also followed in the surrounding field. Prior to sowing, 150 kg ha−1 phosphorus fertilizer (in the form of triple superphosphate) was applied to soil in both years. Also, 150 kg ha−1 nitrogen fertilizer (in the form of urea) was applied to soil in two stages in both years.

Irrigation water was applied after sowing to ensure uniform germination. A surface (flood) irrigation method was used on a flat lysimeter surface to uniformly distributed the irrigation water over the lysimeter surface and measured the volume of irrigation water with volumetric flow meter to ensure precision. Differences between lysimeter weights after each irrigation event and before the next irrigation were used to determine the amount of irrigation water. The soil water measurements indicated that soil water content before irrigation events were higher than the critical soil water content for many field crops (i.e., 0.3 cm3 cm−38, ensuring the non-stress ETc condition. The irrigation interval was 7 days. However, the lysimeter weight was monitored daily to ensure that the soil water content is higher than the readily available water. The lysimeters and the surrounding field were maintained in non-stressed conditions throughout the entire experiment.

Drainage water was calculated by weighing the amount of drained water from the bottom of the lysimeters (there is a tank beneath the lysimeters to collect the free drained water, Fig. 1).

Crop coefficient

The single crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}-\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}$$\end{document}) was calculated by dividing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}, measured using lysimeters, by the daily grass reference evapotranspiration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}$$\end{document}) as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}-\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}=\frac{{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}}{{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}}$$\end{document}

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{o}}$$\end{document} was calculated based on modified Penman-Montieth equation36 and FAO-56 manual8.

The dual crop coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document}) was determined to separate the crop transpiration and soil evaporation. The soil surface evaporation (E) was measured using PVC micro-lysimeters with an inner diameter of 10.5 cm and a depth of 20 cm. The micro-lysimeters were installed inside the weighing lysimeters between two crop rows and filled with field soil at its natural bulk density to ensure the soil surface inside was level with the surrounding ground. Each micro-lysimeter had a perforated plate at the bottom to allow excess water to drain freely. They were irrigated simultaneously with the main lysimeters during each irrigation event. The micro-lysimeters were weighed daily, using a precision balance. The difference in weight between the two measurements represented the daily soil evaporation, and transpiration (T) was calculated as the difference between soil evaporation and the crop’s daily evapotranspiration. Crop height and leaf area index

Three plants were selected in each lysimeter to measure the height of the onion at the main crop growth stages throughout the growing seasons (10 times). The leaf area of selected plants was measured by the WinDias image analysis system (Delta-T Devices, Cambridge, UK) 12 times during the main crop growth stages. Thereafter, the leaf area index of each plant was calculated by dividing the leaf area of a plant by the area occupied by that plant (product of the row spacing and the plant spacing on each row). As the leaf of onion is similar to cone, the leaf length and leaf diameter in the middle of each leaf was measured during the first and second year to calculate the lateral surface area of onion leaf37.

Machine learning methods

In this research, five machine learning algorithms were used to represent a range of linear and non-linear modeling approaches with different levels of complexity and interpretability. This diversity allowed us to identify which modeling structure best captures the relationship between input variables (Table 3) and the daily evapotranspiration of onion (A total of 400 data in two growing seasons).

Artificial neural network

An artificial neural network (ANN) is a massively parallel computational system designed to mimic the functioning of the human brain. It consists of many simple processing units where the connections are described by assigned weights38. A multilayer network is classified as a type of feedforward artificial neural network that consists of at least three layers: input, hidden, and output. In this artificial neural network, the outputs of the first layer are used as the inputs of the next layer. This process continues in the same manner, and the outputs of the last hidden layer are used as the inputs of the output layer. For the ANN model, a feed-forward multilayer perceptron structure was used with one or two hidden layers. The optimal number of neurons in each hidden layer was determined through a trial-and-error approach to minimize the testing error. For more detailed explanations, please refer to39.

Support vector regression

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is one of the supervised machine learning algorithms that is widely used for classification problems. In regression problems, the model allows small errors within a certain range to be ignored, defined by ε, and only focus on larger deviations. This makes SVM effective for regression tasks, specifically referred to as Support Vector Regression (SVR)40. The goal of SVR is to find a function that estimates the relationship between the input variables and the output variable while minimizing the prediction error. The SVR models were tested with different kernel functions, including the radial basis function (RBF) and polynomial kernels of degrees 2 and 3. The RBF kernel yielded the best performance and was selected for the final modeling. After selecting the kernel, the main hyperparameters—cost (C), kernel scale (γ), and the insensitive loss function width (ε)—were optimized automatically using the OptimizeHyperparameters function in MATLAB. Find more detailed explanations in41.

Decision tree

A decision tree (DT) is a flowchart-based predictive model that links observations of an item to conclusions about its target variable. The DT is commonly referred as the Classification and Regression Tree (CART)42. Various algorithms like CART, C4.5, CHAID, and QUEST are used to build decision trees but CART analysis has been popularized as an umbrella term for any decision tree learning method43. The DT is made up of different nodes. The root node is the starting point of the decision tree, which usually contains all the data for the problem. The leaf nodes are the end points of each branch of the tree, or the final output of a set of decisions42. The objective of constructing a regression tree is to minimize the sum of squared errors through a recursive process that generates a binary tree, identifying optimal feature selections and split points44. The tree structure can be adjusted to align with the characteristics of the data being modeled. However, when the tree grows excessively, it may lead to issues such as overfitting. The main parameters, MaxNumSplits, MinLeafSize, and NumVariablesToSample, were tuned using the OptimizeHyperparameters function to minimize the validation error. For further details, please refer to45.

Random forest

Random forests (RF), an ensemble method for classification and regression, are multiple decision trees in agreement with a small variation of trees instead of a single tree. The RF uses majority voting for classification, while the average of the tree outputs forms the basis for regression tasks and makes the RF one of the most reliable algorithms for predictive analysis46. RF constructs an ensemble of unpruned decision trees using bootstrap sampling which means that instead of training on all the observations, each tree of RF is trained on a subset of the observations. The chosen subset is called the bag, and the remaining are called Out of Bag (OOB) samples47. At each split, the best feature is selected from a random subset of features, promoting diversity among trees. This approach enhances model robustness and enables efficient OOB-based estimation of generalization error, strength, and correlation without needing a validation set. The combination of bootstrap sampling and random feature selection reduces overfitting and improves predictive performance48. The Random Forest model was built as an ensemble of decision trees using bootstrap aggregation (bagging). The optimal MinLeafSize value was determined using the OOBPrediction (Out-of-Bag) option, which estimates the prediction error on unseen data. After fixing the optimal leaf size, the number of trees (NumTrees) was adjusted through trial-and-error to balance model accuracy and computational cost. For more detailed explanation, please refer to47.

LASSO regression

Lasso, also known as Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator, is a regression technique widely used in statistics and machine learning49. It combines variable selection and regularization to improve the predictive performance and interpretability of statistical models. This method operates under the assumption that the coefficients of the linear model are sparse, meaning only a small number of them are non-zero50. LASSO regression was used to provide a regularized linear baseline and to perform embedded feature selection among the candidate predictors. Prior to fitting the LASSO model, all input variables were normalized to the zero and one range to ensure comparable scaling across predictors and to match the preprocessing applied to the other machine learning methods. We fitted LASSO with 10-fold cross-validation to select the regularization parameter λ that minimized the cross-validated mean squared error (MSE). The final model was chosen at LambdaMinMSE, and the corresponding coefficient vector (with many coefficients shrunken exactly to zero) was used for test predictions and model interpretation.

Considering the effective parameters, several scenarios with different structures and input parameters were tested to estimate onion evapotranspiration (Table 3). The reason for considering sunshine hour (n) and extraterrestrial radiation (Ra) separately in scenarios was due to the fact that they represent different concepts with distinct measurement characteristics and availability. Sunshine hours measure actual sunlight duration received at the surface, reflecting local atmospheric conditions, cloud cover, and immediate solar energy availability influencing evapotranspiration. Extraterrestrial radiation, on the other hand, represents the maximum potential solar radiation received at the top of the atmosphere based on location, date, and time, independent of local weather conditions. Testing them separately allows analyzing their relative importance and predictive value in the ML models because Ra is a theoretical, constant upper boundary of solar input, while n is an observed variable that captures local variability. Statistical indicators were used to evaluate the effect of scenarios on ETc prediction. The impact of meteorological and crop variables on model performance was assessed by comparing seven different input scenarios using statistical index.

Comparative result of different machine learning

Across all scenarios, ANN, SVR, RF, and DT demonstrated higher predictive accuracy than LASSO regression. Scenarios six and seven, which incorporated both meteorological and crop parameters, produced the best performance for most algorithms. In contrast, scenarios relying solely on meteorological variables generally resulted in lower accuracy.

Among scenarios one to four, the SVR model in scenario two yielded the best performance across the tested algorithms. The optimal cost (C) parameter for the SVR model varied among scenarios, reflecting differences in input dimensionality and data characteristics. The RF model achieved the highest overall accuracy, with RF7 identified as the best-performing model–scenario combination (Fig. 6c). The DT model showed improved performance when crop parameters were included, although it consistently ranked below the RF model. For the ANN models, configurations with two hidden layers outperformed those with a single hidden layer under the sixth scenario (Fig. 6a and d). However, the difference in performance between one and two hidden layers was relatively small. The ANN with two hidden layers and the SVR model produced similar accuracy levels under comparable input scenarios.

The relative importance of predictor variables was assessed for the RF7 model using the PredictorImportance function in MATLAB, based on reductions in mean squared error (MSE) across all trees. The importance values were as follows: Tmin (0.34), Tmax (0.62), Tavg (0.49), RHmin (0.08), RHmax (0.67), RHavg (0.38), wind speed (0.95), net radiation (0.92), plant height (1.03), leaf area index (1.29), and the interaction term (plant height × leaf area index) (0.85). Scenarios including net received radiation, particularly scenario seven, resulted in improved performance for RF, SVR, and LASSO regression compared to scenarios excluding this variable.

Discussion

Plant height and Leaf area index

Leaf area index (LAI) and crop height (H) are key indicators of vegetation structure54,55. Efficient estimating these parameters is crucial for enhancing crop growth monitoring, assessing plant health, and predicting yield56. The maximum LAI achieved in our study was 0.32 and 0.27 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}}^{2}$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}}^{-2}$$\end{document}, with corresponding maximum H of 55.4 and 51.6 cm in the first and second year, respectively. Onion exhibited a lower early relative growth rate, which was attributed to the crop canopy’s limited light interception ability and the low initial radiation use efficiency37. Also, onion due to its upright and vertical leaves, typically exhibits low LAI and even lower fractional ground cover compared to other row crops57. In a study58, showed that the maximum LAI of transplanting Behbahan onion was 2.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}}^{2}{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}}^{-2}$$\end{document}. The higher LAI observed in the latter study compared to the current study is attributed to the plants spending the first 85 days of growth under controlled conditions before being transplanted into the field, where the onions experienced warmer temperature during rapid growth stage. Also23, demonstrated that transplanting onion (cv. kharif) in Punjab, India, with varying rainfall ranging from 900 to 1100 mm, using a spacing of 15 × 10 cm, led to significantly increased plant height (30.87 cm) and number of leaves per plant (12.20) compared to 10 × 7.5 cm spacing. The differences between our LAI and H with other studies may be attributed to variations in planting density, onion cultivars, precipitation levels and environmental conditions.

Evapotranspiration of onion

In the arid and semi-arid climates, accurate estimation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} can help calculate crop water requirements and provide a scientific basis for developing irrigation schedules that can increase crop production59. In our study, the amount of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}, transpiration and surface evaporation slightly decreased in the second year by 3.3%, 7.9% and 13.4% compared to the first year. This decrease was attributed to lower temperature and higher relative humidity (Fig. 2). The seasonal onion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} for non-deficit irrigation treatment in Turky’s arid region was the highest as 680.7 mm and the lowest as 190.0 mm in the non-irrigation treatment60. Also20, reported the seasonal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} for onion measured in the lysimeter as 893.3 mm under semi-arid conditions in Spain. Additionally61, reported that the accumulated onion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} in the Winter Garden region of Texas, with rainfall of 80 mm and 166 mm and irrigation water of 238 mm and 215 mm in 2002 and 2004, respectively, was 362 and 438 mm in first and second growing years, respectively. Our results fall within the range of water requirements (350 to 550 mm) for optimal onion yield (35,000 to 45,000 kg ha−1) as described by62. Single and dual crop coefficient

The maximum Kc and Kcb was 0.68 and 0.51 obtained at mid stage, respectively. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{e}}$$\end{document} starts at a high value (0.31) in the early growing season and gradually decreases as the season progresses. This trend is expected as the evaporation rate from the soil is initially high when the onion is first planted and has limited vegetation cover. As the plant grows and the vegetation cover on the soil increases, the evaporation rate decreases until the end of the growing season (Fig. 5b). The existing literature shows various \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} values depending on the onion cultivar, planting density, cropping techniques, local climatic conditions, and irrigation system9. The lower values of single and double crop coefficient observed in the current study compared to that presented in FAO-56 manual8,63, and9 were due to application of different cultivars, plant density (as farmers use in the study) and irrigation method (sprinkler and sub-surface drip irrigation). It was explained that canopy development of onion (Armaghan G. cultivar) was lower than other cultivars as having maximum LAI of only 0.32 cm2 m−2 and height of 55 cm. Although the planting density was within the normal range for the region, the sparse canopy limited radiation interception and transpiration. Cold winter conditions also delayed growth and shortened the effective growing season. Therefore, the lower Kc and Kcb values observed here primarily reflect the canopy architecture and environmental conditions. The results of our study indicate that all the models used (ANN, SVR, RF, and DT) except LASSO regression, can effectively capture nonlinear relationships between weather and crop parameters to simulate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}. The selection of input variables significantly influences the accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document} estimation using machine learning models27. Comparing machine learning algorithm results revealed that scenarios six and seven, incorporating both meteorological and crop parameters, outperformed other scenarios. In situations where measuring all meteorological data is not practical, machine learning algorithms offer a viable alternative for estimating directly crop evapotranspiration because it can provide reliable, accurate estimates by learning from available data and capturing complex variable relationships65. Moreover, the machine learning models trained on local canopy and environmental data can adapt to site-specific conditions, giving more precise ETc estimates than generalized FAO-56 coefficients66. The strong performance of the Random Forest model can be attributed to its ensemble learning framework, which reduces overfitting and improves generalization by aggregating multiple decision trees. This structure allows RF to remain robust in the presence of noisy inputs and collinearity among meteorological variables, making it particularly effective for agricultural applications where environmental data often exhibit high variability67,68. Following the RF, the DT algorithm also showed improved results. The RF outperformed the decision tree due to its use of multiple decision trees for simulation, preventing overfitting issues68. Artificial Neural Networks and Support Vector Regression exhibited comparable predictive behavior, suggesting that both methods can effectively learn nonlinear relationships between input variables and ETc69. Increasing ANN model complexity through additional hidden layers resulted in only marginal performance gains, indicating that evapotranspiration dynamics can be captured without excessive network depth when relevant input variables are provided68. Similarly, variations in SVR performance across input scenarios reflect its sensitivity to kernel configuration and regularization, underscoring the importance of appropriate parameter tuning69. The LASSO regression performs poorer than other algorithms due to the nonlinear relationship between input and output parameters. LASSO applies linear shrinkage, the removal of these RH and T parameters (which often act nonlinearly and interactively with other drivers of evapotranspiration) likely led to loss of important explanatory information and contributed to its lower predictive accuracy compared with nonlinear algorithms70,71.

The seventh scenario in RF, SVR, and LASSO regression algorithms show better performance possibly due to the net received radiation parameter being a significant input, indicating a stronger influence on onion evapotranspiration compared to other parameters72. Canopy-related parameters such as LAI and plant height, along with net radiation and wind speed, were the most influential predictors of onion evapotranspiration73,74. This finding highlights the crucial role of both plant structural and energy-related variables in accurately estimating ETc68. Variables such as leaf area index and plant height influence aerodynamic resistance, radiation interception, and transpiration efficiency, thereby strengthening the physical linkage between crop growth dynamics and water fluxes72. This finding confirms that models trained with combined meteorological and crop information are better able to represent site-specific evapotranspiration behavior than those relying on atmospheric data alone. In contrast, humidity parameters, especially RHmin, exhibited relatively lower importance, suggesting that aerodynamic and air moisture-related factors had a smaller influence under the semi-arid conditions of the study area71. Advantages and limitations of the study

This study offers significant advantages in experimental and modeling approaches. It provides precise and continuous lysimetric measurements of onion evapotranspiration (ETc) and crop coefficients (Kc and Kcb) under semi-arid field conditions, which are scarce in the literature, especially for the Arghavan onion cultivar in Iran. The high-precision digital weighing lysimeters used in this study allowed for daily monitoring ETc with minimal measurement uncertainty, capturing fine-scale temporal dynamics and supporting accurate estimation of crop coefficients. This capability is valuable for calibrating and validating crop water requirements and irrigation scheduling in water-limited environments. Moreover, this study integrated crop growth parameters and meteorological variables into five machine learning frameworks with distinct structures to estimate ETc.

Limitations include the restriction of the experiment to a single research station and soil type, limiting the generalizability of the results. Variability in soil texture, fertility, and microclimatic conditions in other regions may affect ETc and model accuracy. Lysimeter-based measurements, while obtaining precise results, are costly, labor-intensive, and spatially limited, reducing large-scale replication feasibility. The machine learning models were trained on a relatively small dataset, potentially limiting generalization to unseen conditions. Key variables influencing ETc, such as soil water content and canopy temperature, were not included in the input features, potentially affecting model performance under extreme stress conditions. Future research should focus on validating the models across multiple locations and seasons, incorporating additional physiological indicators to improve ETc prediction and develop scalable irrigation tools for semi- arid and arid environments.
