Behavioral Patterns Behind Tobacco Smoking among Patients Admitted to a Tertiary Care Hospital of a Developing Nation
Authors: Aayushi Goyal, Juhi Kalra, Aswani Kumar Seth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Globally, more than one-fifth of smokers end up being hospitalized due to consequences. Hence, the study was designed to explore the behavioral pattern of smoking among patients admitted to a tertiary care hospital in North India.Methods:A qualitative study was conducted among 50 patients admitted to a tertiary care hospital in North India. The participants were categorized into groups using the Heaviness of Smoking Index (HSI). In-depth interviews using an interview guide were conducted with participants from these groups. Inductive thematic analysis was performed to identify themes and subthemes. The natural history of tobacco addiction was presented using concept maps.Results:All participants were male with a mean (SD) age of 51.0 (16.5) years, and the median (IQR) duration of smoking was 20.5 (5.9, 21.2) years. The majority (60%) of participants had moderate to severe addiction. Five major domains emerged drivers of smoking initiation (peer pressure, community norms, and individual curiosity), continuation of smoking (perceived benefits, normalization, concealment, and lack of familial regulation), consequences of smoking (progression to serious illnesses such as COPD, TB, and cancer, and social/familial conflict), thoughts of quitting (triggered by illness, medical advice, social disapproval, and family pressure), and failure to quit (nicotine dependence, withdrawal symptoms, and persistent peer influence).ConclusionSmoking behavior among hospitalized male patients is shaped by complex interplay of their own willingness, peer and community influences, perceived benefits, and limited early awareness of harm. Medical illness and healthcare advice often initiate quitting intentions, but dependence and social pressures hinder cessation.

INTRODUCTION

Around the world, there are about 1.1 billion tobacco smokers, and among them, 7.69 million deaths are due to tobacco smoking.[1] India ranks second in the consumption of tobacco, with 267 million tobacco smokers.[2] Among them, 1.3 million deaths occur due to tobacco consumption every year.[3]

Nicotine is addictive due to its pharmacokinetics. When a person smokes a cigarette, nicotine is released, which is absorbed and dissipates quickly in the blood and brain.[4] Nicotine present in tobacco stimulates the brain to release dopamine, which transiently makes a person happy.[5] So, to maintain the pleasurable effects of smoking, patients smoke tobacco again and again. This led to difficulty in quitting tobacco.[4] Nearly 70% of tobacco smokers wanted to quit tobacco smoking but were unable to quit.[6] Around 1.8% of the adults have stopped smoking.[7] Nearly two in five (38.5%) of smokers attempted to quit smoking.[7] According to the Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS), India 2019, 10.6% of people have stopped smoking.

The major reasons for quitting tobacco are medical complications and social pressure. Among them, medical complications lead to the emergence of diseases whose management is often difficult as these are chronic diseases and mostly lead to death. The long-term complications include various types of cancers, lung diseases, coronary heart disease, and stroke. Around 30% of deaths among all cancers occur due to smoking.[8] In India, 169,000 was the estimated burden of cancer due to tobacco in 2015, and it was projected to be around 236,000 by 2025.[9] Around 90% of lung cancer deaths occur due to smoking.[10]

Most smokers initially stop smoking, but as time passes by, relapse occurs,[11] and 75% of smokers relapse within 6 months.[12] In USA, there are about 7.8% adults who have started consuming tobacco again.[13] Factors such as nicotine dependence and the absence of support for quitting smoking are responsible for the relapse to smoking.[14]

There is an increasing burden of smokers in India, supplemented by an increasing burden of individuals addicted to smoking. The smoking journey of an individual is influenced by their lifestyle and behavioral patterns. Understanding these patterns would enable healthcare workers at the grassroots level to decrease the burden of smoking. Not many studies are conducted in this part of the country to understand these patterns. Hence, the study was conducted to explore the behavioral pattern of tobacco smoking among patients admitted to the tertiary care hospital in North India.

METHODS

Study design and study setting

A qualitative study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Dehradun, India. It is a 1200-bed private hospital with almost all specialties available. The study was conducted after seeking ethical clearance from the Institute Ethics Committee (IEC No.: SRHU/HIMS/PHARMA/E-1/2018 dated 24.01.2018) and was part of the Indian Council of Medical Research—Short-term Studentship (ICMR-STS). Participant consent was taken, and the confidentiality of the data was maintained.

Study participants and sampling strategy

The study participants were patients admitted to the general medicine, general surgery, psychiatry, oncology, and otorhinolaryngology wards of the hospital. Each of these wards consists of 100 beds. The participants, more than 18 years of age, admitted to these wards, having a history of smoking at least one tobacco stick per day for one year, and consenting to participate in the study, were included. The patients who consumed smokeless tobacco and patients who had quit smoking tobacco more than one month ago were excluded from the study. Purposive sampling was used to recruit study participants. Around 50 study participants were recruited to achieve thematic data saturation.

Study tool and data collection strategy

The eligible study participants were categorized into groups based on the heaviness of smoking index (HSI). This was conducted in the patients’ local language. The heaviness of the smoking index included two questions: a) After waking up, how soon do the participants have their first cigarette? and b) How many cigarettes do they typically smoke per day? Each parameter was scored on a scale of 0 to 3, and the participants were classified as having low addiction (scores 0 to 2), moderate addiction (scores 3 to 4), and high addiction (scores 5 to 6) based on HSI scoring.[15] In 2021, the HSI was validated in Indian settings against the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) with a kappa of 0.7, a sensitivity of 78%, and a specificity of 91%.[16]

In-depth interviews (IDIs) using an interview guide [Supplementary File 1] were used as a data-collection tool. The interview guide was prepared in the local language and included questions and probes that tried to explore the participants’ entire journey from initiation of tobacco smoking to date. The interview guide was pretested in patients attending the dental outpatient department. The IDIs were conducted face-to-face in the local language after obtaining the written informed consent. The IDIs were conducted in the procedure rooms of the wards where participants were admitted. During the IDIs, the researcher, study participants, and a nurse were present in the procedure room. No repeat interviews were conducted. The IDIs were audio-recorded. On the same day, transcripts were generated from IDIs, and field notes were added as needed.

Researcher reflexivity

The research was conducted as part of the ICMR-STS project under the guidance of a pharmacology professor. The principal investigator was a female undergraduate student studying in the second year of the MBBS course. The principal investigator underwent training for conducting IDIs in the department of pharmacology before initiating data collection. The researcher informed the study participants of the study’s objectives. The participants were also informed that the data obtained from them would be submitted to the ICMR as a report. This information might have introduced social desirability bias among the study participants.

Statistical analysis

The audio recordings of the IDIs were transcribed by the principal investigator in the same language as the interviews. The transcript was checked for appropriateness by the other researcher. The cross-check transcript was finally approved by the senior researcher and then translated into English for data analysis. The transcripts were not returned to the study participants for comments. Inductive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006) was used to analyze the data. The analysis was done manually after each IDI. Two researchers coded the data individually, and any discrepancies found were resolved by the principal investigator. The line-to-line coding was done, followed by the generation of the subthemes. The subthemes were then reviewed thoroughly to generate themes. The Lucid Chart [17] Online software was used to create concept maps for each participant, followed by a collaborative concept map summarizing all the individual maps. The data were separately analyzed for low addiction participants and moderate to severe addiction participants. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to guide the reporting [Table S1].

RESULTS

A total of 50 study participants were recruited for the study. Of these 50 participants, 4 dropped out midway through the interview. The mean (SD) duration of the IDIs was 21.6 (2.5) minutes. All of the study participants were male. No female patient gave consent to participate in the study. The mean (SD) age of participants was 51.0 (16.5) years, and the median (IQR) age of initiation of smoking was 22 (17,36) years. Among these participants, the median (IQR) total duration of smoking was 20.5 (5.9, 21.2) years, with a median (IQR) frequency of 17 (2,36) cigarettes per day. The mean (SD) HSI score was 2.8 (1.96). Around 24 (48%) study participants started smoking within 5 minutes of waking up in the morning, and 12 (24%) smoked more than 30 tobacco sticks daily [Table 1].

Drivers of tobacco smoking initiation

Peer-mediated social pressure within the college environment

The study participants with low addiction initiated smoking tobacco in college. They initiated smoking under peer influence and to maintain their status among friends. However, some of these study participants wanted to explore the process of tobacco smoking as they felt that it would act as a stress buster.

“Seeing the seniors make rings out of smoke influenced us to try to explore the same thing.”

- ---Study participants (P6) with low addiction

Individual predispositions and enabling environmental factors

A study participant reported that many elderly people in his village gather to smoke hookahs and bidis during discussions. Participating with them resulted in the initiation of smoking. Another participant mentioned that smoking cigarettes with co-workers helps them increase their bond with co-workers, leading him to start smoking. The findings were corroborated by the study participants’ verbatim accounts.

“In the village, everyone used to sit together and smoke hookahs and bidis. Just sitting with them, I also started smoking bidis. I used to collect remains of the bidi to start of.”

- ---Study participant (P12) with moderate to severe addiction

“The people I used to work with smoked cigarettes. Seeing them, I also started thinking, “What’s the harm in trying?”

- ---Study participant (P1) with moderate to severe addiction

Continuation of tobacco smoking

Normalization and perceived benefits of tobacco use

The study participants in both the low addiction and moderate to severe addiction groups expressed that they felt satisfied and good after smoking tobacco. They were even unaware that smoking is injurious to health and therefore never considered it to be bad. They also reported that smoking early in the morning helps them to pass bowel movements smoothly.

“I was suffering from constipation, my neighbour told me that 2-3 puffs of bidi smoking during defecation would help. I tried, and it was beneficial initially.” ----Study participant (P5) with low addiction

The study participants with moderate to severe addictions reported that the cigarettes were easily available, and they could easily hide from their parents. Some of these study participants were truck drivers. They reported that during the night shift, smoking bidis kept them awake and enabled them to reach the destination on time. The extra earnings generated from it motivated them to continue smoking.

“My friends never informed me that tobacco was bad. My father also smoked cigarettes and never suffered from any illness.”

- ---Study participant (P27) with moderate to severe addiction

“I was a driver, sir, and had to drive at night, so smoking cigarettes helped me stay awake.”

- ---Study participants (P36) with moderate to severe addiction

Concealment and autonomy from parental oversight

The study participants with low addiction reported that they could continue smoking because they used to buy cigarettes from the shop and gather in a closed space behind the shop to smoke. This helped them to remain unrecognized for many years and also hide the habit of smoking from their parents. One of these participants reported that he used to conceal the smell of tobacco coming from his mouth after smoking.

“Every day, on my way home, I smoked a cigarette in a shop which was about 15-20 mins from my house. After smoking, I used to clean my mouth with mouthwash and eat a chewing gum.”

- ---Study participant (P50) with low addiction

Limited familial and social regulations

One of the study participants with moderate to severe addiction revealed that his parents, upon discovering his smoking habit, never encouraged him to quit or imposed any punishment, thereby contributing to his habit of smoking. Some of these study participants reported that whenever a guest visited them, it was their cultural practice to serve them food and hookah. If not served, it was considered poor hospitality. It was one of the reasons that allowed them to continue smoking.

“I am the eldest son and sole breadwinner of the family. They never interfered in my things and never asked me to change my habits”

- ---Study participant (P42) with moderate to severe addiction

Consequences of tobacco use and resulting behavioral and social responses

Progression to serious health consequences

Almost all the study participants reported serious health consequences. The study participants with moderate to severe addiction reported the development of life-threatening conditions like lung cancer, whereas those with low addiction reported the development of chronic diseases like COPD, TB, and bronchiectasis (continuous production of sputum). One of the study participants reported that they started smoking to improve bowel movement, but over the years, they felt constipated after smoking.

“My neighbor asked me smoke bidi to solve constipation but after 5-6 years of regular smoking, I started suffering from severe constipation”

----Study participant (P11) with moderate to severe addiction.

Conflict and breakdown of the relationship

The study participants with low addiction reported that when their parents got to know that they were smoking cigarettes secretly, they beat them in public. One of the participants also reported that after his marriage, when his wife came to know that he was smoking secretly every day, they got separated. Even after reducing the number of sticks, the marriage did not last.

“When my wife got to know my smoking habit, she was furious. She abused my parents and moved to her parent’s house. To save my marriage, I wanted to stop smoking but I could not. I reduced the number of cigarettes but that did not convince her to return back”

----Study participants (P16) with low addiction.

As told by one of the study participants, due to the continuation of smoking by the participant, he started having difficulty breathing, and later on, he developed a respiratory disease. He also has a responsibility to care for his family, so he opted to quit smoking for this reason, while another participant said that a lot of fights used to occur in his family due to his smoking habit. The children of this participant stopped talking to their father, leading to the development of guilt in the father and, hence, prompting him to quit smoking.

“Sir, there used to be so many fights at home, even the children had stopped talking. Eventually, I had to quit somehow, but I couldn’t.”

----Study participant (P28) with moderate to severe addiction

Thoughts of quitting smoking

Social enforcement of non-smoking norms

The study participants with moderate to severe addiction reported that they were so addicted to smoking that they could not resist themselves even when they were in public places. He reported that he felt relaxed when he used to smoke in a moving auto. He was once humiliated by a copassenger in the auto, which made him think of quitting smoking.

“One day I was travelling to my workplace in an auto. In the same auto, a lady with her 8-9 years son was also there. I pulled out a bidi and started smoking. The lady create a scene and auto driver stopped the auto. I was humiliated and cussed by the lady. The auto driver asked me to step down from his auto. That day I felt disgusted”

----Study participant (P24) with moderate to severe addiction.

Failure to stop smoking

Nicotine dependence and withdrawal as a barrier

The majority of the study participants reported that they could not control the urge to smoke. They even reported having withdrawal symptoms if they stopped smoking for even a day. The participants with low addiction reported having headache, anxiety, and insomnia, whereas those with moderate to severe addiction reported being lazy, irritable, restless, and even feeling lonely.

One of the study participants attributed his smoking habit to frequent conflicts within their family, motivating them to consider quitting smoking. However, due to peer pressure from coworkers, they started smoking again and have been unable to quit since then.

“There was so much turmoil at home that once, I quit for a month, but because of the people I worked with, I started smoking again and haven’t been able to quit till today, sir.”

----Study participant (P36) with moderate to severe addiction.

DISCUSSION

The study identified the behavior patterns behind smoking tobacco among male patients admitted to the tertiary care hospital in India, with a mean age of 51 years and a median duration of smoking tobacco of 21 years. The majority of the participants had moderate to high addiction to smoked tobacco and had quit smoking tobacco.

The present study reported that the willingness of the participants and their peers and the community influences were major factors in the initiation of smoking among them. A hospital-based cross-sectional study among 100 current smokers in Tamil Nadu, India, reported the surrounding influence as a major factor for the initiation of tobacco smoking.[18] This highlights the role of the community in establishing a tobacco-smoking culture in India. The study participants belonged to the hilly regions of Uttarakhand, which serve as the major catchment area for the tertiary care hospital in the present study. Smoking tobacco in this region is considered a means of socializing.[19] Early initiation of tobacco smoking among study participants also depicts a significant societal role. A study among 18 to 22-year-old students studying at the University of Kolkata, India, reported peer dynamics as one of the factors for the initiation of smoking tobacco.[20] Among youth, independence from family, the challenging environment at the university/workplace, and lack of coping mechanisms with difficult situations affect their decisions.[21]

Our study reported that factors responsible for the continuation of smoking were subjective wellbeing and unawareness of the adverse effects of smoking. Studies conducted in different parts of the world reported that continuation of smoking leads to a worsening of subjective wellbeing.[222324] These are contrary to most findings in the present studies as enjoyment and feeling good were considered aspects of subjective wellbeing among study participants. Global Adult Tobacco Survey – two (GATS-2) India survey reported that around 40% of smokers were unaware of the adverse effects of tobacco.[25] The present study is qualitative; hence, quantitative comparison between the studies is out of the question. But the GATS-2 India survey highlighted a lack of awareness of the adverse effects of tobacco among a large section of the population. However, studies in the past have reported that most smokers were aware of cancer as the only adverse effect of smoking tobacco. The present study also reported that the participants were never asked to quit smoking tobacco. A study conducted among British smokers also reported similar findings.[26] In the present study, participants reported an increase in the number of sticks smoked per day within a short span of 6 months, leading to their addiction and, hence, continuation of smoking tobacco. A supportive environment among family and friends who are smokers, where they collectively perceive neither the benefit of quitting nor harm in continuing to smoke, can be a strong reinforcer as well as an initiator of the habit of smoking.[2728] The present study also found that almost half of the participants smoked their first cigarette of the day within 5 minutes of waking up, which is a strong predictor of dependence on nicotine and points toward a possible link between bowel habits and smoking that contributes to the continuation of smoking tobacco.

Our findings show that the effects of smoking resulted in illness, including cancer, respiratory complications, systemic diseases, addiction, and other social issues. All the participants acknowledged that there was a gradual increase in the number of cigarettes smoked, as happens in a typical case of addiction. The increase in the number can be explained by the involvement of the dopaminergic pathways. The dopaminergic reward pathways have been implicated in reinforcing the pleasurable effects of substances of abuse. Repeated exposure to nicotine leads to compulsive and repetitive use through very powerful conditioning and upregulation of the nicotine receptors. The increase in the dopamine levels of the reward circuits of the brain is also responsible for withdrawal symptoms during abstinence.

The present study reported that the thoughts of quitting smoking arose among participants upon a doctor’s advice. Self-motivation emerged as an important parameter for quitting tobacco smoking. Similar findings were reported in a study conducted among 84 participants in the US.[29] A study also reported that doctors can help increase the quit rate from 5% to 15%, which would contribute to reducing the number of smokers worldwide by 2 million.[30] Guidance from any healthcare professional, whether a doctor, respiratory therapist, nurse, or other clinician, seems equally effective. Thus, multiple clinicians should encourage smoking cessation.[31]

Factors contributing to the failure to quit smoking included strong peer pressure and withdrawal symptoms. A study conducted in Poland highlighted additional reasons for unsuccessful smoking cessation, such as insufficient willpower, lack of improvement in mental and physical wellbeing, stressful situations, lack of family support, and contact with other smokers.[22] Another study also pointed out factors like peer pressure, work-related anxiety, weight gain, family-related anxiety, and mood changes as reasons for failing to quit smoking.[25] In our study, some participants experienced withdrawal symptoms, and it is these individuals who are most likely to become chronic smokers. Such chronic smokers may show resistance to quitting.[30]

The qualitative approach allowed for an in-depth understanding of the natural history of tobacco addiction, revealing important themes such as initiation of smoking, continuation of smoking, consequences of smoking, thoughts of quitting, and outcomes of quitting. The study very well addresses credibility, dependability, and confirmability. The study also ensures transferability by clearly stating its scope and defining the study participants and setting. One of the major limitations of the study was that the female patients refused to participate. Hence, the results solely represent the behavioral patterns of male smokers. There may be a recall in the participants who had stopped tobacco smoking. To address this, we exclude participants who had quit smoking more than a month ago. There is a potential for social desirability bias in responding to the questions related to their smoking addiction. This may be one reason for recruiting 50 participants to achieve data saturation. One limitation was that no member checking was conducted. Considering the Indian context, one of our limitations of the study is the noninclusion of dual tobacco users and smokeless tobacco users.

CONCLUSION

The participant’s willingness to experiment was the biggest factor for the initiation of tobacco smoking, followed by peer and community influence. The lack of awareness about the harms of tobacco smoking emerged as a major factor in continuing with the habit. Self-motivation and the role of the doctor emerged as very useful factors influencing them to stop smoking tobacco. The family plays a crucial role in the behavioral pattern of a smoker. These findings necessitate the need for opportunistic screening for tobacco addiction in patients and their family members attending the hospital. The family members should be encouraged to help patients stop smoking. Awareness programs focus on timely intervention among early initiators and knowledge of the adverse effects of tobacco smoking to decrease the burden of tobacco consumption. Orientation of doctors in addressing tobacco addiction at routine clinics and setting up of tobacco cessation clinics will be strong steps toward helping tobacco smokers quit smoking. Finally, the peer-led support interventions would help tackle this challenge.
