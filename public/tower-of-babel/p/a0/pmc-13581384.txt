Comprehensive review of current management and precision medicine in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Hung-Yuan Yu, Le-Gin Lin, Yee Chao, Chung-Pin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies, with an increasing incidence and limited improvement in survival despite therapeutic advances. Surgical resection remains the only curative option; however, recurrence is frequent, and long-term outcomes remain dismal. Major risk factors include smoking, obesity, diabetes, chronic pancreatitis, and pathogenic germline variants such as BRCA1/2, ATM, and mismatch repair genes. Routine population screening is not recommended; instead, surveillance using endoscopic ultrasonography and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography is recommended for high-risk individuals. Diagnosis relies on multiphasic pancreatic protocol computed tomography and histological confirmation. Treatment strategies are determined based on resectability: upfront surgery with adjuvant chemotherapy for resectable disease, neoadjuvant chemotherapy for borderline resectable or high-risk patients, and palliative chemotherapy with or without chemoradiation for locally advanced cases. Systemic therapies, including FOLFIRINOX, gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, and NALIRIFOX, remain the cornerstone of therapies for metastatic disease, with precision medicine offering targeted options such as PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutated tumors. Modern radiotherapy techniques including stereotactic body radiation therapy and particle therapy enhance local control and reduce toxicity. The integration of next-generation sequencing and multidisciplinary management is essential for improving pancreatic cancer outcomes.Keywords: Chemoradiotherapy, Pancreatic neoplasm, Precision medicine, Surgery, Systemic therapy

1. INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic cancer is a highly lethal malignancy, accounting for approximately 3% of all cancer diagnoses and 7% of cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 The global age-standardized incidence rate is approximately 5.9 per 100 000 population, with approximately 508 533 new cases and 505 752 deaths annually as of 2021.2 In Taiwan, pancreatic cancer also ranks seventh in causes of cancer-related death, and both its incidence and mortality have been steadily increasing over the past decades.3 Despite improvements in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic strategies, the overall prognosis remains dismal, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of only 13% in the general population and approximately 3% among those with metastatic disease.4 Due to its insidious onset and lack of specific symptoms, most patients present with either locally advanced (30%-35%) or metastatic disease (50%) at diagnosis.5 Although the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force does not recommend population-based screening in asymptomatic adults,6 targeted surveillance in high-risk individuals has been proposed, leading to earlier detection and a higher proportion of localized disease compared with symptomatic cases.7–9

Over the past few decades, significant progress has been made in the treatment of pancreatic cancer. Surgical resection remains the only curative approach5,10; however, recurrence rates remain high, with most relapses occurring within 12 months postoperatively.11,12 Chemotherapy continues to be the cornerstone of systemic treatment,13 and advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have enabled the development of tumor-agnostic therapies for patients harboring actionable genomic alterations.13,14 Radiotherapy also plays a role in achieving local control of primary tumors and metastatic lesions, with technological innovations allowing for more precise and effective radiation delivery.15

Despite these advances, pancreatic cancer remains a devastating disease, with limited long-term survival. The heterogeneity of tumor biology, lack of effective screening strategies, and challenges in integrating novel therapies into current treatment paradigms highlight the need for continuous evaluation and innovation.

This review aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of recent advances in the diagnosis and management of pancreatic cancer, summarize current therapeutic strategies, and discuss future directions for improving patient outcomes.

2. RISK FACTORS

Pancreatic cancer risk factors can be divided into two categories: modifiable and nonmodifiable. Modifiable factors include cigarette smoking, central obesity, new-onset diabetes mellitus, chronic pancreatitis, and heavy alcohol intake (>26-30 g/d).5,16 Genetic predisposition is the most important non-modifiable factor, with pathogenic germline variants in BRCA1/2 (relative risk 2.4 and 3.3, respectively), ATM (relative risk 6.5), MLH1 (relative risk 6.7), MSH2 (relative risk 1.6), and MSH6 (relative risk 2.0) markedly increasing risk.16 These germline variants are associated with several hereditary diseases, such as hereditary breast/ovarian cancer, Lynch syndrome, hereditary pancreatitis, and Peutz–Jeghers syndrome. A family history of pancreatic cancer in one or two first-degree relatives further elevates susceptibility, with a relative risk of 4 to 9.3.5,16

3. SCREENING AND SURVEILLANCE

Despite the poor prognosis of pancreatic cancer, routine population screening is not recommended due to its low prevalence and high cost.6 Instead, surveillance is advised for high-risk individuals, including carriers of pathogenic variants (eg, STK11/LKB1, PRSS1, CDKN2A, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, TP53, ATM, BRCA1/2, and PALB2) with affected first- or second-degree relatives.8 Annual endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/MRCP enable early detection of pancreatic cancer and precursor lesions in high-risk group. Notably, EUS appears to be more sensitive for detecting solid lesions, whereas MRI/MRCP is superior for detecting cystic lesions, supporting the use of both modalities in surveillance protocols.17,18 Surveillance-detected pancreatic cancer has shown improved outcomes, with some cohorts reporting 5-year survival rates approaching 50%.9

4. DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

4.1. Diagnosis

Because symptoms of pancreatic cancer are usually nonspecific and the pancreas is anatomically difficult to access, diagnosis is often delayed.19 Imaging plays a central role in the initial evaluation of patients with suspected pancreatic cancer, while confirmation requires pathological examination.

4.2. Image

Abdominal ultrasonography is widely available and useful for the initial evaluation; however, its sensitivity to pancreatic lesions is limited. Multiphasic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with a pancreatic protocol, including pancreatic parenchymal, portal venous, and arterial phases (particularly for surgical assessment), provides high-resolution cross-sectional images and a more accurate evaluation of the pancreas and surrounding structures.20 MRI is a valuable alternative, especially for indeterminate liver lesions or when CT findings are inconclusive. EUS is another important modality that offers both high-resolution anatomical evaluation and tissue sampling using fine-needle biopsy. However, EUS is not recommended as a routine staging tool.

4.3. Pathology

Histological confirmation is essential not only for diagnosis but also for guiding precision medicine. Preoperative biopsy is not necessary for patients with highly suspected pancreatic cancer who undergo upfront resection. In contrast, tissue sampling is indicated for unresectable diseases or when neoadjuvant therapy is planned. EUS-guided biopsy is preferred over CT-guided biopsy in non-metastatic disease because it minimizes the traversal of normal tissues and reduces the risk of tumor seeding.21 In patients with metastatic disease, biopsy of metastatic sites (eg, sonography-guided liver biopsy) may be an appropriate alternative.

4.4. Staging

Comprehensive staging is critical for treatment planning after diagnosis. Standard staging includes pancreatic protocol CT of the abdomen extending to the chest and pelvis. MRI should be considered when liver lesions remain indeterminate, whereas positron emission tomography (PET)/CT or PET/MRI may be used to detect extrapancreatic disease in patients at a high risk for distant metastasis.

The American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM system is the most widely adopted system for prognosis based on tumor size, vascular involvement, nodal status, and distant metastasis. In clinical practice, patients are commonly categorized as having resectable, borderline resectable, locally advanced, or metastatic disease. More than half of the patients present with metastatic disease, most commonly involving the liver, peritoneum, lungs, and extraregional lymph nodes. For non-metastatic cases, resectability is determined by tumor–vessel relationships, particularly those involving the celiac axis, superior mesenteric artery, hepatic artery branches, and portomesenteric venous axis. In addition to anatomical staging, patient prognosis is influenced by biochemical and clinical factors. The “ABC” model—Anatomical (TNM/resectability), Biochemical (eg, CA19-9), and Conditional (eg, performance status)—has been proposed as an independent predictor of OS.23

5. TREATMENT STRATEGIES

Although surgery is the only curative treatment for pancreatic cancer, more than half (57%) of patients are diagnosed with metastatic disease, and only 15% of patients are diagnosed with localized disease.13 Moreover, recurrence and metastasis rates remain high, with 48% recurring within 12 months and 86% at 5 years postoperatively.24 Consequently, systemic therapy, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy in selected patients, constitutes the backbone of treatment. Radiotherapy offers an alternative for the improved local control of both primary and metastatic lesions.

5.1. Resectable disease

5.1.1. Neoadjuvant therapies

In patients with resectable pancreatic cancer, upfront surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy remains the current standard of care.25,26 The role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy is still debated; however, growing evidence suggests that it may improve R0 resection rates and survival, particularly in patients with high-risk features such as elevated CA19-9, large tumors, or poor performance status.25–28 Further studies are needed to define the criteria for selecting patients who may benefit from neoadjuvant therapy. The optimal regimens, treatment durations, and patient selection require confirmation in large prospective trials.

5.1.2. Curative-intent surgery

Surgical resection is the only curative treatment available for pancreatic cancer. The surgical approach depends on the tumor location and its relationship with the surrounding vasculature. Procedures include pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure), distal pancreatectomy, total pancreatectomy, and vascular resection with reconstruction.29,30 Minimally invasive surgery, including laparoscopic and robotic approaches, provides noninferior surgical outcomes and a shorter recovery time,31 and may be considered in selected patients. One systematic review revealed that laparoscopic and robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy had similar resection margins, major complication rates, and mortality rates compared with open pancreaticoduodenectomy.32 One comparative effectiveness study reported that minimally invasive surgery was associated with better OS and disease-free survival (DFS) than open pancreatectomy.33 The long-term follow-up of the DIPLOMA trial showed comparable radical resection rates, long-term OS, and DFS.34

Pancreaticoduodenectomy is the standard surgery for tumors of the pancreatic head or uncinate process and involves resection of the pancreatic head, duodenum, proximal jejunum, gallbladder, and distal stomach, followed by reconstruction. A pylorus-preserving variant should be considered in selected patients. Owing to the complexity of reconstruction, postoperative complications are common, including delayed gastric emptying, bile leakage, and pancreatic fistula, with morbidity rates of 40% to 55% and mortality rates of 1.6% to 5%.35 Distal pancreatectomy is performed for tumors of the pancreatic body or tail, typically with splenectomy. Adrenalectomy may also be performed during posterior radical antegrade modular pancreatosplenectomy. Total pancreatectomy is reserved for diffuse disease, but results in profound endocrine and exocrine insufficiency. En bloc resection may require vascular resection. Venous resections such as those involving the portal or superior mesenteric veins can be safely performed. In contrast, arterial resection carries high perioperative morbidity and mortality and is generally avoided. Splenic artery resection may be safely performed in selected cases of extended distal pancreatectomy. For locally advanced tumors involving the celiac trunk, distal pancreatectomy with celiac artery resection can be considered in selected patients who respond to neoadjuvant therapy to achieve R0 resection.36

5.1.3. Adjuvant chemotherapy

Given the high risk of local and distant recurrence, the 5-year survival rate of patients with resectable disease remains below 20%, underscoring the importance of adjuvant therapy. The CONKO-001 trial established adjuvant gemcitabine as a standard, showing improved median OS and DFS compared with observation.5,37 The ESPAC-1 established the role of 5-fluorouracil in adjuvant chemotherapy,38 and the ESPAC-3 revealed comparable survival benefits of 5-fluorouracil and gemcitabine in adjuvant chemotherapy.39 The ESPAC-4 trial demonstrated that gemcitabine plus capecitabine yielded a modest but significant survival benefit over gemcitabine monotherapy (median OS 28.0 vs 25.5 months).5,40 The JASPAC-1 trial reported noninferior OS with S-1 compared with gemcitabine (5-year OS 44.1% vs 24.4%), although its efficacy outside Asian populations remains uncertain.41 The PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA.6 trial established mFOLFIRINOX as the preferred adjuvant regimen, with a median OS of 54.4 vs 35 months for gemcitabine alone.42 However, higher rates of grade 3/4 neutropenia and febrile neutropenia have been reported in Asian cohorts, even with dose adjustments,43 highlighting the need for careful regimen selection based on patient tolerance.44

5.2. Borderline resectable disease

5.2.1. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is the standard treatment for borderline resectable disease, leading to improved clinical outcomes. According to phase II trials and systematic reviews, (m)FOLFIRINOX is associated with high rates of R0 resection (up to 67%-75%) and favorable survival outcomes, with a median OS ranging from 24 to 42 months in borderline resectable cohorts.45–47 Gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, combined with S-1 in a phase II trial, is also a safe and effective choice for neoadjuvant chemotherapy, demonstrating improved OS and a higher R0 resection rate.48,49

Recently, the randomized phase III CASSANDRA trial evaluated the cisplatin, nab-paclitaxel, capecitabine, and gemcitabine (PAXG) regimen and suggested a potential shift in the neoadjuvant treatment paradigm. Compared with mFOLFIRINOX, the PAXG regimen was associated with superior event-free survival, improved disease control, enhanced CA19-9 response, higher rates of pathological complete response and node-negative resection, and a lower incidence of early postoperative metastatic disease. Notably, these benefits were achieved without a significant increase in grade 3 or higher toxicities, positioning PAXG as a promising neoadjuvant option for both resectable and borderline resectable disease.50

The role of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy remains debatable. Although selected patients may derive benefits, the evidence to date has yielded mixed results.51 The PREOPANC-2 trial reported comparable OS between neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX and neoadjuvant gemcitabine combined with hypofractionated radiotherapy. Accordingly, gemcitabine-based chemoradiotherapy may be considered a reasonable alternative in patients who are unable to tolerate multi-agent systemic chemotherapy such as FOLFIRINOX.52

Following neoadjuvant chemotherapy, re-evaluation is necessary to determine the next treatment step. PET/CT or PET/MRI should be considered both before and after neoadjuvant therapy for a more accurate assessment of the treatment response. If the tumor remains stable or demonstrates regression, surgical intervention should be performed when appropriate. If the tumor is unresectable or has progressed, but does not preclude surgery, alternative neoadjuvant therapy should be considered. If the disease deteriorates, further treatment should be administered according to locally advanced or metastatic disease protocols.

5.2.2. Adjuvant chemotherapy

Adjuvant chemotherapy is strongly recommended for patients with resectable disease after upfront surgery. However, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with borderline resectable tumors remains unclear. Randomized trials have demonstrated that adjuvant chemotherapy after upfront surgery significantly improves OS, with modified FOLFIRINOX showing the greatest benefit in fit patients.

For patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy is less clearly defined. In recent observational and retrospective studies, adjuvant chemotherapy with 5-fluorouracil–based regimens has been associated with improved OS and recurrence-free survival, especially in patients with adverse pathological features such as lymph node involvement, larger tumor size, perineural invasion, or positive resection margins.53,54 Gemcitabine-based regimens can also serve as alternatives for patients intolerant to 5-fluorouracil–based regimens.5 The optimal duration of adjuvant chemotherapy remains inconclusive; however, the neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen can generally be continued for up to a total of 12 cycles if well tolerated.54 The benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy may be less pronounced in patients who have already received a high number (≥8) of neoadjuvant (m)FOLFIRINOX cycles.54 Therefore, decisions regarding the regimens and duration of both neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy should be individualized based on the patient’s condition, pathological findings, and tolerance to chemotherapy.

5.3. Locally advanced pancreatic cancer

5.3.1. Palliative chemotherapy

For patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer (LAPC), treatment selection should be based on performance status. Palliative chemotherapy with mFOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel for 4 to 6 months is the current standard, although no phase III trial has defined an optimal regimen. Conversion surgery should be considered if downstaging after chemotherapy can markedly improve clinical outcomes.55 If the disease progresses despite chemotherapy, subsequent systemic treatment should be initiated. Concurrent chemoradiation (CCRT) should be considered if the disease is controlled by chemotherapy, which can improve local control and pathological responses.56

5.3.2. Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy has played an evolving role in the management of LAPC, particularly with the advent of modern radiation techniques. CCRT improves local control and progression-free survival.57,58 However, its effect on OS remains uncertain, and limited benefits have been observed in patients with early metastatic disease.59 Modern radiotherapy techniques, including stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) and particle therapy, provide precise dose delivery, better sparing of adjacent organs, and shorter treatment courses. Proton therapy exploits the Bragg peak to reduce toxicity and allow dose escalation,60 whereas carbon-ion radiotherapy (CIRT) offers additional biological advantages such as higher linear energy transfer and effectiveness in hypoxic tumors.61–63 Retrospective and early phase studies have shown promising local control and survival outcomes,64–66 but large randomized trials are still lacking.67 Recent retrospective study revealed an excellent local control rate, with 1-year local control rate of approximately 95% and minimal toxicity when patients were treated with risk-adaptive carbon-ion therapy. The median PFS was nearly 14 months after CIRT in patients without metastases.68

5.4. Metastatic disease

5.4.1. Palliative chemotherapy

Palliative chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of the treatment of metastatic pancreatic cancer. Genetic testing for germline mutations and molecular profiling of tumor tissues provide valuable information to guide personalized therapeutic strategies. Based on phase III trials, FOLFIRINOX and nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine are the preferred first-line regimens.69 Due to the substantial toxicity of standard FOLFIRINOX, particularly in Asian populations, mFOLFIRINOX has been developed as a more tolerable option.70 The NAPOLI-3 trial demonstrated superior OS with nanoliposomal irinotecan, oxaliplatin, and 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin (NALIRIFOX) compared with nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine.71 However, no randomized trials have directly compared mFOLFIRINOX with either NALIRIFOX or nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine. A systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant difference in OS between mFOLFIRINOX and NALIRIFOX, although their toxicity profiles differed.72 In a phase I/II trial conducted in Taiwan,73 the oxaliplatin, gemcitabine, S-1, and folinic acid regimen significantly prolonged OS compared with gemcitabine alone,70 making it another viable first-line option, particularly in Asian populations. A phase III clinical trial demonstrated the potential therapeutic role of erlotinib in the treatment of advanced pancreatic cancer.74

For patients with controlled disease after 6 months of therapy, maintenance treatment should be considered. In patients with germline BRCA mutations, olaparib is the recommended maintenance option following platinum-based chemotherapy, with improved progression-free survival, but no significant difference in OS.75

Second-line chemotherapy is recommended following disease progression. Nanoliposomal irinotecan with 5-fluorouracil and leucovorin is the standard option after progression to gemcitabine-based therapy.76 For patients previously treated with fluoropyrimidine-based regimens, gemcitabine-based chemotherapy can be considered a second-line option, although supporting evidence is limited.

5.4.2. Oligometastatic disease

Although all patients with distant metastases are classified as having metastatic disease, a subset of oligometastatic diseases has been recognized. It is characterized by a limited number of metastatic lesions, most often defined as fewer than four lesions confined to one or two organs (commonly the liver, lungs, and peritoneum). In these patients, systemic therapy remains the backbone of treatment, but more aggressive local therapies may improve the prognosis. Potential approaches include surgical resection, ablation, SBRT, proton or CIRT, transarterial therapies (transarterial chemoembolization and transarterial radioembolization), and intraperitoneal chemotherapy (intraperitoneal hyperthermic chemoperfusion, normothermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, and pressurized intraperitoneal aerosol chemotherapy).

5.5. Precision medicine

Pancreatic cancer develops through multiple tumorigenic pathways, some of which involve potentially actionable somatic and germline alterations.77 Identification of these molecular changes enables more individualized and potentially effective treatment strategies. Accordingly, comprehensive NGS is increasingly recommended for detecting such alterations. Large-scale initiatives, such as the Know Your Tumor program, have demonstrated that the most common mutations include KRAS (approximately 88%), TP53 (61%-74%), CDKN2A (16%-44%), and SMAD4 (20%-22%), and that approximately 25% to 50% of patients may harbor potentially actionable molecular findings.77 The most established precision-based therapeutic strategies target DNA damage repair deficiencies (eg, BRCA1/2 mutations), mismatch repair deficiency or microsatellite instability-high (dMMR/MSI-H) status, and rare oncogenic fusions, such as NTRK, NRG1, ALK, RET, and BRAF fusions. Although numerous oncogenic fusion events have been identified and linked to targeted therapies, the overall incidence of pancreatic cancer remains low.

Despite the high prevalence of KRAS mutations in pancreatic cancer, only therapies targeting KRASG12C, such as sotorasib, have been approved. Therefore, recent studies have focused on the development of inhibitors that target the more prevalent KRAS mutations. MRTX1133, a selective KRASG12D inhibitor, has demonstrated potent antitumor activity in preclinical models and is currently being evaluated in early-phase clinical trials.78 In addition, several pan-KRAS inhibitors, including BI-2493 and RMC-6236, have shown promising efficacy in preclinical studies.79 However, clinical experience with KRAS-targeted monotherapy has revealed rapid development of resistance, often driven by secondary mutations or reactivation of downstream signaling pathways. Future perspectives include the development of next-generation KRAS inhibitors and the exploration of optimal combinations or sequential treatment strategies.78,80–82

Apart from NGS-based precision medicine, several emerging approaches may further refine patient stratification and facilitate more individualized treatment strategies, including transcriptomic subtyping (eg, classical vs basal-like subtypes)83 and organoid-based drug sensitivity testing.84

6. FOLLOW-UP STRATEGIES

The follow-up schedule and choice of modality are crucial for accurately evaluating the treatment response and monitoring disease status. Contrast-enhanced CT is the preferred imaging tool, offering high spatial resolution, rapid acquisition, and reliable assessment of both the primary tumor and common metastatic sites (liver, peritoneum, lung, and lymph nodes).85,86 CT angiography provides additional value by evaluating tumor–vessel relationships. MRI serves as an alternative in cases of indeterminate CT findings and is particularly effective for detecting small liver metastases when gadoxetate disodium is used.87 PET/CT or PET/MRI enables whole-body metabolic assessment, supporting the detection of viable tumors and occult metastases in selected patients.

According to the NCCN and American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines, regular follow-up every 3 to 6 months is recommended, and cross-sectional images are appropriate. However, regular surveillance CT scans are still controversial because previous studies have revealed no correlation between regular surveillance CT scans and survival benefits. Treatment response should be assessed using RECIST v1.1. Monitoring serum CA19-9 levels is recommended as a complementary tool to detect recurrence and monitor disease progression. No randomized controlled trials have established a recommended follow-up interval during treatment. According to most guidelines and consensuses, the recommended follow-up interval is 2 to 3 months for a 2-year duration, including physical examination, laboratory testing, and CA19-9 and radiographic assessment. The interval can increase to 6 months if stable disease is confirmed.88 If recurrence is identified, further management should be determined through multidisciplinary team discussions. This multimodal approach ensures accurate disease monitoring and facilitates timely therapeutic decisions.89,90

7. CONCLUSIONS

Pancreatic cancer continues to pose major challenges owing to its aggressive nature and poor prognosis. Improving outcomes relies on early detection of high-risk patients, precise staging, and the integration of systemic therapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, when appropriate. Advances in molecular profiling and precision medicine have opened up new opportunities for personalized care, and future research should focus on translating these findings into effective patient-centered treatment strategies.
