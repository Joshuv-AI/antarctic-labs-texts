Acute heat stress induces short- and long-term epigenetic changes in the buff-tailed bumblebee (Bombus terrestris)
Authors: Thibaut Renard, Morgane Boseret, Serge Aron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal warming and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events threaten organisms worldwide, with cold-adapted species like bumblebees being especially vulnerable. Although heat stress has been shown to affect bumblebee physiology and behaviour, whether it also impacts their epigenome remains unknown. In this study, we addressed this gap by investigating the short- and long-term effects of acute heat stress experienced early in adult life on DNA methylation profiles in the buff-tailed bumblebee (Bombus terrestris). The DNA methylomes of males exposed either to control conditions (25°C) or to acute heat stress (42 °C for 60 min) were sequenced 1 day and 15 days after exposure to disentangle the short- and long-term effects of thermal stress.ResultsHeat stress induced differential methylation at hundreds to thousands of sites in both the short- and long-term, yet these signatures were largely distinct across time points. Background

Global warming and extreme weather events, such as heatwaves, are impacting animal physiology and behaviour, leading to population declines and species extinction [1]. Insects are particularly vulnerable to heat stress because they have limited capacity to physiologically regulate body temperature. In addition, their relatively small body size and high surface-to-volume ratio reduce their thermal buffering capacities against temperature fluctuations [2–4]. Cold-adapted species, such as bumblebees, are especially susceptible to heat stress [5, 6].

Bumblebees are key contributors to both global ecosystems and agriculture as they pollinate most wild and cultivated flowering plants across temperate and boreal ecosystems in the Northern hemisphere [7, 8]. Bumblebee colonies are typically annual, starting when fertilized reproductive females (queens) emerge from overwintering diapause and search for nesting sites. Once established, the queens lay eggs that develop into workers, which perform all logistical tasks required for colony growth and maintenance, such as foraging for pollen and nectar and brood rearing. Later in the season, typically during summer, the colony produces sexuals, i.e., males and new (virgin) queens. The lifecycle concludes in autumn when newly fertilized queens enter diapause underground for winter, while the rest of the colony (workers, males, and the old queen) dies. Therefore, temperature is a central factor shaping the bumblebee lifecycle, as workers and males must remain active during the warmest season, while queen must tolerate cold temperatures during overwintering diapause.

Bumblebee mainly evolved under temperate and boreal climates, making them adapted to overall low temperature rather than prolonged elevated heat [8]. As a result, bumblebees are expected to be significantly impacted by ongoing and future global warming. Observational and modelling studies confirm this prediction, reporting that bumblebee populations are declining at a faster rate than other bees and insects, with climate warming identified as a leading cause [6, 9–12]. Moreover, observational data spanning more than 100 years indicate that bumblebees have not shifted their distribution northward and are experiencing range contraction in the Southern ends of their range, further increasing their vulnerability to rapid climate change [10].

The detrimental impacts of elevated environmental temperatures on bumblebee workers [13–17], queens [18, 19], and colonies [18–23] have been well documented (but see [19, 20, 24] for potential positive effects). They range from impaired individual behaviour (e.g., foraging activity [16, 21]) and physiology (worker sensory responses [25] and queen reproductive outputs [18, 19]) to colony-level alterations leading to decreased productivity and fitness [21]. By contrast, males are comparatively understudied, despite their crucial reproductive function. Notably, males may be particularly vulnerable to heat stress, as they are typically produced during the warm season and do not benefit from colony-level thermoregulation as they spend most of their adult life outside the nest searching for mating partners [7, 26]. While heat stress has been shown to impair male morphology [27] and physiology (including altered visual abilities [28] and reduced fertility [29, 30]), its molecular effects remain unexplored.

The epigenome is a prime candidate to assess how environmental stressors, like heat stress, affect bumblebees at the molecular level, as it plays a central role in regulating gene expression in response to environmental cues [31]. DNA methylation, the covalent addition of methyl groups (CH3) to the DNA, is of particular interest because it is evolutionarily conserved from prokaryotes to plants and mammals [32–35]. Its patterns are also strongly correlated with lifespan, health span and environmental stress exposure across a range of species [36–40]. Studies of DNA methylation in bumblebees remain scarce and have largely been conducted independently of environmental stress exposure [41–44] (but see [45]). We previously showed that DNA methylation is involved in lifespan regulation in bumblebee workers [46], and can be used to develop epigenetic clocks responsive to lifespan-extending interventions in males [44]. In addition, a recent study reported different DNA methylation profiles between workers from populations differing in their thermal niche, suggesting a potential role for epigenetic variation in local adaptation [47]. Together, these findings indicate that the DNA methylome of bumblebees integrates external signals. However, whether acute heat stress directly reshapes DNA methylation profiles has not been investigated.

In this study, we addressed this gap by directly testing whether acute heat stress (42 °C for 60 min) experienced early in adult life alters DNA methylation profiles in male buff-tailed bumblebee (Bombus terrestris). We investigated both the short- and long-term effects of heat stress by sequencing the DNA methylome of males 1 day and 15 days after exposure, and compared them with those of age-matched control males exposed to control conditions (25°C). Differential methylation analysis was used to investigate the effects of heat stress on genome-wide, base-resolution DNA methylation profiles, and to assess the overlap between short- and long-term changes. Using random forest classification, we tested whether short-term changes in DNA methylation profiles associated with heat stress could be leveraged to predict heat stress exposure in the long-term. Furthermore, we developed an epigenetic clock to test whether heat stress affects epigenetic age prediction (a robust proxy of biological aging).

Results

To investigate the short- and long-term impacts of heat stress on DNA methylation patterns, we exposed 6-day-old male of B. terrestris to either heat stress (42 °C for 60 min) or control conditions (25 °C for 60 min). We then sequenced their DNA methylomes 1 day (i.e., 7-day-old males) or 15 days (21-day-old males) after exposure and compared stressed males with age-matched controls. Principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 1A) and unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1B) showed that samples grouped based on the combination of treatment (control vs heat stress) and age (7d vs 21d). Specifically, 7d and 21d control samples clustered together across both PC1 and PC2. In contrast, 7d heat stress samples showed greater dispersion, particularly along PC1, reflecting inter-individual variability. We then performed pairwise differential methylation analyses to identify sites whose DNA methylation levels were altered by heat stress. We detected 452 differentially methylated sites (DMS) between heat-stressed and control males sampled at 7 days of age and 1,836 DMS between 21d males (Fig. 2A, ESM Tables S1 and S2). Several DMS were mapped to genes involved in lifespan regulation and stress response pathways, including MTOR associated protein LST8 and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK). DMS were enriched for 71 biological process (BP) terms in 7d males, and for 192 BP terms in 21d males (Fig. 2B, ESM Tables S3 and S4). We investigated the temporal dynamics of changes in DNA methylation profiles by categorizing DMS as transient (only significant between 7d control vs. 7d heat stress) or delayed (only significant between 21d control vs. 21d heat stress). DMS common to both comparisons were considered as lasting or reversed if they followed the same or the opposite direction, respectively. We identified 405 transient DMS, 692 delayed DMS, 10 lasting DMS and 5 reversed DMS (Fig. 2C, ESM Table S5). Thus, most DMS were specific to one timepoint, but not both. GO enrichment analysis identified 76 and 180 BP for transient and delayed DMS, respectively (Fig. 2D, ESM Table S6). Both transient and delayed BP terms were related to chromatin and epigenetic regulation, cytoskeleton processes, proteostasis, signalling, and stress response. Despite being associated with similar broad functional categories, only 20 BP terms were shared between transient and delayed DMS. Additional delayed-enriched terms were related to the cell cycle and DNA metabolism, intracellular transport and neuronal processes (Fig. 2E, ESM Table S6). The low number of lasting and reversed DMS precluded biologically meaningful GO enrichment. We next applied random forest classification to determine whether subtle DNA methylation changes not detected as significant by differential methylation analyses could still predict prior exposure to heat stress. To evaluate the persistence of heat stress-associated methylation signatures over time, we trained four models using 7d control and heat-stressed males and subsequently tested their predictive performance on 21d males. The consensus model, trained on a fixed set of the ten sites most consistently ranked as informative across all cross-validation folds, achieved the best training performance (mean classification error ± SD: 0.02 ± 0.03), outperforming models trained on all sites (full model, 0.18 ± 0), DMS (0.18 ± 0), or on a subset of informative sites (subset model, 0.26 ± 0.06) (Fig. 3A). During independent testing on 21d males, overall accuracy was 71.4% for the full, consensus, and subset models; it was lower for the fourth model, which was trained exclusively on transient DMS (DMS model, 57.1%). The three best-trained models showed high specificity, as they correctly categorized 21d control males (consensus = 90%, full = 100%, subset = 90%). However, sensitivity for 21d heat-stressed males was low for all models: the full model failed to correctly classify any of the four 21d heat-stressed males (sensitivity = 0%), while the consensus and subset models correctly identified only one out of four (sensitivity = 25%). Thus, random forest classifiers failed to identify 21d males as heat-stressed based, indicating that the DNA methylome of 7d and 21d heat-stressed males is largely distinct. Finally, we evaluated the effects of heat stress on epigenetic aging by training an epigenetic age prediction model (epigenetic clock) on 7d and 21d control males. The model achieved high predictive accuracy both during training (correlation = 0.98, mean relative error (MRE) = 8.6%, mean absolute error (MAE) = 1.1 days) and when applied to independent control samples excluded from training (correlation = 0.95, MRE = 10.8%, MAE = 2.0, Fig. 3B). Application of the model to heat-stressed samples showed that epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), defined as the difference between predicted epigenetic age and actual chronological age [48], were significantly higher in 7d males exposed to heat stress relative to age-matched controls (mean EAA ± SD: controls = 2.7 ± 1.7 days, heat stress = 11.4 ± 3.8 days, Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney U test: p = 0.012, Fig. 3B). This suggests that an acute heat stress induces a rapid increase in epigenetic age. In contrast, EAA did not differ significantly between 21d control and heat-stressed males (controls = 2.3 ± 3.6 days, heat stress = −3.9 ± 3.9 days, Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney U test: p = 0.11, Fig. 3B).

Discussion

Our results show that acute heat stress induces dynamic remodelling of the DNA methylome in males of the buff-tailed bumblebee (Bombus terrestris). The absence of a conserved DNA methylation signature across time points, combined with the transient increase in epigenetic age immediately after stress exposure, suggests that heat stress triggers temporally distinct changes in DNA methylation rather than stable, persistent responses.

At the site-specific level, heat stress induces hundreds to thousands of DMS, including some located within genes encoding key components of evolutionarily conserved longevity-regulating pathways, like the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling [49], and in BP terms related to hallmarks of aging [42]. Interestingly, DNA methylation changes at loci within these same pathways and biological functions occur during aging and are induced upon pharmacological lifespan extension in B. terrestris males [44] and workers [46], and upon viral infection in the western honeybee (Apis mellifera) [50]. Moreover, in line with our functional enrichment analyses, transcriptomic studies showed that heat stress upregulates metabolic, energy-production and heat-shock chaperone genes in workers of B. vosnesenskii [51] and B. terrestris [13]. The convergence between transcriptomic and DNA methylation responses to environmental stress suggests that, in bumblebees, lifespan and stress response may be regulated by pathways that are evolutionarily conserved across yeasts [52], insects [53], and vertebrates [54], such as mTOR signalling. Because they regulate the life-history trade-off between somatic maintenance and reproductive investment, a shift in the DNA methylation status (or gene expression) of these pathways due to environmental stress could reallocate organismal resources toward somatic maintenance rather than to reproduction, potentially affecting reproductive fitness [55]. Although speculative, this hypothesis is supported by evidence that heat stress reduces sperm quality in bumblebee males [29], even when sperm cells are not directly damaged [30].

Our temporal classification reveals that most DMS were detected at only one of the two timepoints. The low number of lasting DMS indicates that the short- and long-term DNA methylation responses to heat stress are largely distinct. These findings suggest that the DNA methylome of B. terrestris is actively regulated, rather than passively experiencing and maintaining alterations induced by heat stress. Consistent with this, random forest classification consistently failed to correctly classify 21-day-old heat-stressed males whose DNA methylation profiles more closely resembled those of 7-day-old controls than those of recently stressed individuals. Transcriptomic predictors of environmental stress have similarly failed to capture the molecular signature of acute stress once the stressing conditions have ceased [56]. This contrasts with our findings indicating lasting impact of heat stress on the DNA methylome, which may therefore be more suited to capture environmental stress exposure than the transcriptome.

Epigenetic clock analyses identified a significant short-term acceleration of epigenetic aging in 7d heat-stressed males, indicating that epigenetic age is sensitive to acute environmental stress in B. terrestris. This acceleration was detected despite the inherent survivor bias of our experimental design, indicating that even the most resilient individuals experience measurable epigenetic aging responses to heat stress. By contrast, epigenetic age did not differ between 21d control and heat-stressed males, suggesting a recovery of the epigenetic aging trajectory over time after acute stress exposure. This findings aligns with the few existing insect epigenetic clocks studies, which point to a high degree of plasticity in epigenetic aging (B. terrestris [44]; Nasonia vitripennis [57]). It also reflects the dynamic regulation of epigenetic aging in mammals, whereby epigenetic age is elevated following acute stress and restored to baseline levels upon recovery [40]. This suggests that some features of epigenetic aging may be evolutionarily conserved across vertebrates and invertebrates.

Our study has several limitations that should be taken into account. First, our experimental design is subject to the ‘survivor bias’ effect: because heat stress decreased mean lifespan by 58%, corresponding to a 6-fold increase in mortality risk, only individuals that survived heat stress were available for 21d sampling, thereby biasing the dataset toward more resilient males. This may attenuate the apparent long-term impact of heat stress on the methylome and obscure persistent alterations occurring in more vulnerable individuals. Nonetheless, the presence of significant DMS between 21d control and heat-stressed males demonstrates that heat stress also leaves detectable epigenetic signatures in more resilient individuals. Distinguishing between epigenetic recovery and selective survival of less-affected individuals would require longitudinal sampling of the same subjects, which is not currently feasible in B. terrestris. Second, our analyses were conducted on DNA extracts composed of multiple tissues and cell types, precluding the detection of tissue- or cell type-specific patterns. However, our results from bulk analyses suggest that significant changes are either shared across tissues, or strong enough in a few tissues to be detected. Third, as previously mentioned, our study focused on males because (i) they are more ecologically prone to heat stress than females since they spend most of their adult life outside a nest [7, 26], and (ii) we already showed that epigenetic clocks can be developed in B. terrestris males and capture lifespan extension [44], indicating that the DNA methylome is sensitive to external influences. However, whether environmental changes affect DNA methylation in females remains equivocal. For instance, B. terrestris workers exposed to pesticides show no difference in DNA methylation despite changes in gene expression [45]. Notably, epigenetic clocks derive their predictive accuracy from the combined contribution of individual sites that may not be detected as differentially methylated via traditional differential methylation analyses [39]. Therefore, epigenetic clocks may provide a sensitive tool to detect environmental stressors in bumblebee females. Future epigenetic clock studies should investigate whether environmental stressors like heat stress similarly impact epigenetic aging across sexes and female castes (i.e., queens and workers). Finally, because DNA methylation is weakly correlated with gene expression in bumblebees [41–43, 45, 46], whether the DMS identified in stress response and longevity-related pathways translate into functional changes in gene expression or pathway activity remains to be examined. In addition, whether the observed changes in DNA methylation are directly caused by heat stress or reflect an adaptive response to mitigate it is unknown. Nonetheless, our study demonstrates that DNA methylation can be used as a biomarker of environmental stress regardless of its functional consequences, opening new avenues for the use of epigenetic-based monitoring of insect physiology, which is essential in the actual context of global warming.

Conclusions

By showing that the bumblebee DNA methylome is sensitive to heat stress and may be actively regulated rather than passively altered by environmental exposure, this study provides new insights into how environmental stressors may shape the insect epigenome. As climate change is predicted to increase the frequency and intensity of heatwaves [58], insect populations will likely experience more frequent episodes of acute thermal stress. Such events can induce long-term epigenetic changes and transiently accelerate epigenetic aging, with potential consequences on organismal physiology and reproductive fitness. Future studies combining repeated or chronic stress exposures, longitudinal epigenetic sampling, and experimental manipulation of DNA methylation will help determining the cumulative functional consequences of environmental stress in insects.

Methods

Rearing, heat stress and survival

Buff-tailed bumblebees (Bombus terrestris) were obtained from Biobest (Westerlo, Belgium). A total of 250 males were randomly assigned to 10 experimental microcolonies (16 × 11 × 9 cm) so that each contained 25 males. Microcolonies were maintained under standard laboratory conditions (12:12 light:dark; temperature = 25 ± 1 °C; relative humidity = 50%) and were provided with ad libitum access to sugar syrup (Biogluc, Biobest, Westerlo, Belgium).

Six-day-old males (n = 209) were individually placed in glass tubes (20 × 2 cm) sealed with a dry cotton plug. The tubes were then fully immersed in a digitally controlled water bath (SW22, Julabo GmbH) maintained at either 25 °C (control, n = 66) or 42 °C (heat stress, n = 143), for 60 minutes. The stressing temperature (42°C) was selected based on pilot experiments showing that it induces sub-lethal physiological stress within a relatively short time frame (mean time to heat stupor ± SD = 71.4 ± 23.3 min), thereby minimizing confounding effects such as starvation. The temperature within the tubes was monitored using 0.075-mm-diameter thermocouples (type K, Chromel/Alumel, Glasgow, UK) connected to a digital thermometer (RS Pro RS52 Digital Thermometer, RS Components Ltd, Corby, UK). After heat stress, males were returned to standard rearing conditions and kept in microcolonies containing only males from the same experimental group.

Survival was monitored daily and compared between control and heat stress males using Kaplan-Meier survival curves (‘survfit’ function; survival R package) and Cox proportional Hazard models with microcolony as a random effect (‘coxme’ function; coxme R package). As expected, heat stress significantly reduced male survival, decreasing mean lifespan by 58% (mean lifespan ± SD: control = 28.7 ± 7.6 days, heat stress = 12.2 ± 6.3 days, Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney U test: p < 2.2e-16) and increasing mortality risk 6-fold (n = 209, Cox proportional hazards model: Hazard Ratio = 6.075, Wald test: p < 2.2e-16, Supplementary Figure S1).

DNA methylation sequencing

Because global DNA methylation levels are very low (~1%) in bumblebees [41–44, 46, 47], we used Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long-read sequencing to comprehensively profile C5-methylcytosines (5mC), which is the main type of DNA methylation. ONT sequencing enables the accurate and robust detection of DNA methylation across all genomic contexts, including non-CpG contexts, which are mostly inaccessible to traditional bisulfite sequencing because it detects base modifications (i.e. epigenetic marks) on directly on native DNA, without requiring than bisulfite conversion [59–61]. To disentangle between short- and long-term effects, we compared males 1 day (7-days-old; 7d) or 15 days (21-days-old; 21d) after exposure, respectively. Three to 5 males were sampled per temperature (control vs heat stress) and age (7d or 21d) (total n = 19, 7d control = 4, 7d heat stress = 7, 21d control = 4, 21d heat stress = 4). All individuals were sampled between 10-11 AM to avoid circadian influences on DNA methylation profiles [62].

Males were killed by flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen. High molecular weight genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the thorax and legs using an in-house SDS/proteinase K protocol. Frozen tissues were dry ground, suspended in an SDS/proteinase K solution, and left in suspension overnight. gDNA was washed with phenol-chloroform solution and chloroform, then precipitated with ethanol and sodium acetate. gDNA integrity was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis. gDNA quantity and absorbance ratios were measured using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a NanoDrop ONE spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. gDNA libraries were prepared with the Ligation Sequencing gDNA - Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 (ONT, SQK-NBD114.96) and sequenced with a PromethION platform (ONT, PRO-SEQ002). Raw reads were basecalled using Dorado (ONT, v. 0.7.2) with the super high accuracy model (‘sup’ command) to capture 5mC in any genomic context. Basecalled reads were aligned to the B. terrestris GCF_910591885.1 v. 1.2 reference genome using Dorado (‘aligner’ function). SAMtools was used for indexing and sorting. Genome-wide, base-resolution 5mC levels were generated with modkit (ONT, v. 0.3.2) ‘pileup’ command. Only sites with a minimum coverage of 10X were kept for the subsequent analyses.

DNA methylation analyses

Principal component analysis (PCA) (‘prcomp’ function in R) and hierarchical clustering were used to capture broad genome-wide signatures of heat stress. Pairwise differential methylation analyses (‘dmr pair’ function, modkit, ONT, v0.3.2) were conducted within timepoint between conditions (7d control vs. 7d heat stress, 21d control vs. 21d heat stress), and within conditions between timepoints (7d control vs. 21d control, 7d heat stress vs. 21d heat stress). False discovery rate was accounted for using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing, and the significance threshold was set at an adjusted p-value < 0.05. To characterise the temporal dynamics of the DNA methylation response to heat stress, we classified differentially methylated sites (DMS) into four categories based on their pattern across timepoints. DMS detected only between 7d control vs. 7d heat stress were classified as transient, DMS detected only between 21d control vs. 21d heat stress as delayed, and DMS identified at both timepoints as lasting or reversed, depending on their direction of effect between timepoints (i.e., lasting = same direction, reversed = opposing direction). Because previous work revealed that the DNA methylome of B. terrestris is influenced by age [44, 46, 63], DMS whose methylation levels changed between timepoints in control males (7d control vs. 21d control) were excluded from the 21d control vs. 21d heat stress comparison and from the within-heat stress temporal comparison (7d heat stress vs. 21d heat stress) prior to classification, as such changes could be attributable to aging rather than heat stress. Age-correction correction was not applied to the 7d comparison due to the short time gap between heat stress and analysis. Delayed DMS were cross-validated against the age-corrected within-heat stress temporal comparison: sites also detected as differentially methylated between 7d and 21d heat stress males were considered as supported by an independent line of evidence for a true delayed response. Functional enrichment analyses using gene ontology (GO) terms were conducted by linking DMS to biological process (BP) terms from the Hymenoptera Genome Database [64].

Predictive modelling

DNA methylation profiles may experience subtle, non-statistically significant changes that together correlate robustly with specific traits, such as age and stress exposure. Therefore, while highlighting strong differences, differential methylation analysis may not capture the combined predictive power of small changes on site-specific DNA methylation. To account for this limitation, we adopted a random forest classification approach. To determine whether heat stress leaves long-term DNA methylation signature, we trained models on 7d males (control and heat stress) using the site-specific DNA methylation levels as predictors. Model training was performed with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). The trained model was then applied to 21d control and heat stress males as an independent test set. If the short-term DNA methylation signature of heat stress persists to 21d, heat stress males should retain a DNA methylation profile that the model classifies as heat stress. Conversely, failure to correctly classify 21d heat stress males would indicate that the short-term signature is not maintained. To assess the robustness of this approach, we evaluated four model variants. The full model used all sites retained after near-zero variance filtering as input features. The subset model used the ten most informative sites, re-selected independently within each cross-validation fold. The consensus model used a fixed set of the ten sites most consistently ranked as informative across all cross-validation folds. Finally, the DMS model used only the transient DMS. Model performances were assessed by comparing accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. Accuracy reflects the overall proportion of correctly classified samples; however, it can be biased with imbalanced classes. In our case, the test set contains more control than heat stress samples, so a classifier that predicts all samples as control would be considered as highly accurate. To account for this, we measured model sensitivity, defined as the proportion of true heat stress samples correctly identified. It is therefore the most biologically relevant metric for detecting whether the 7d heat stress signature persists at 21d. Specificity measures the proportion of true control samples correctly classified as control, reflecting the model ability to avoid false positives.

We developed an epigenetic age prediction model (epigenetic clock) to quantitatively measure the impact of heat stress on epigenetic age, a robust proxy of biological aging [36, 39, 65]. We previously extended this relationship to B. terrestris males by showing that epigenetic clocks trained on different types of DNA methylation (i.e.,C5-methylcytosines vs N6-methyladenines) track both chronological and biological age [44]. The model was trained using the B. terrestris male dataset generated with ONT sequencing (n = 22) [44], which has been successfully used to construct epigenetic clocks [44, 66–68]. Penalized regressions (elastic net, mixing parameter alpha = 0.5) was used to identify cytosines whose methylation levels best predict chronological age [39]. Model training was performed within a nested LOOCV framework to optimize model performances (outer loop) and lambda hyperparameter selection (inner loop) while avoiding data leakage [69, 70]. The epigenetic clock was based on 47 sites (cytosines) selected during model training. Predicted epigenetic age was calculated based on the combined methylation values of the selected sites, which were weighted by their respective coefficients. Model accuracy during training was assessed based on the correlation between predicted epigenetic age and chronological age, the mean absolute error (MAE) and mean relative error (MRE). Independent model testing was performed by applying the clock to the control samples from the heat stress experiment (which were not used during model training, n = 8). Then, the epigenetic age of heat stressed males was predicted to measure the difference between estimated epigenetic age and actual chronological age, defined as epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) [48].
