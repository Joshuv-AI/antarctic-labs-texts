Combining Reactive Quantum-Mechanical Molecular-Dynamics Simulations with Mutagenesis, Crystallography, and Enzyme Kinetics to Reveal Plausible Steps of Isocyanide Hydratase Catalysis
Authors: Rae A. Corrigan Grove, Michael A. Moxley, Christian F. A. Negre, Marc J. Cawkwell, Anders M. N. Niklasson, Susan M. Mniszewski, Nathan Smith, Kevin Prososki, Mark A. Wilson, Michael E. Wall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A complete understanding of enzyme mechanisms requires atomistic details of chemical reactions. Quantum-based molecular dynamics simulations (QMD) are a potential source of this information, but trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost have limited their use. We previously developed extended Lagrangian Born–Oppenheimer molecular dynamics (XL-BOMD) methods that leverage a negligible compromise in accuracy to substantially decrease the cost of QMD simulations. Here, we develop a reactive QMD approach using the latest XL-BOMD formulation, which enables efficient simulations of highly reactive systems, and use it to investigate mechanisms of intermediate formation in isocyanide hydratase (ICH) catalysis. In QMD simulations, molecular analogs of ICH active site residues reacted with para-nitrophenyl isocyanide, forming a thioimidate. Analysis of simulated atomic configurational and charge dynamics revealed a pathway where protonation of the isocyanide carbon occurs prior to thioimidate formation and suggested a possible role of Asp17 as a proton donor in the early phase of ICH catalysis. To test whether the pathway seen using the reactive QMD approach might be relevant to ICH catalysis, we performed X-ray crystallography and pre-steady-state enzyme kinetics studies of wild-type and D17N mutant ICH. Both the structure and kinetics are sensitive to the D17N mutation in a manner that is consistent with the order of the reaction steps seen in the simulations. Mobile protons play essential roles in many enzymes, yet they are difficult to observe experimentally, making the ordering of proton-dependent steps ambiguous in many enzyme mechanisms. The ability to directly simulate model reactions for the design of experiments that provide information about enzyme mechanisms involving mobile protons demonstrates the significance of our reactive QMD approach and motivates further biological applications.

Introduction

Understanding enzyme catalysis in atomic detail is crucial to solving various problems in biology − and biochemistry. − A complete mechanistic understanding of enzymes requires knowing atomistic details of each step in the catalyzed chemical reaction. A comprehensive inventory of all such details is beyond the reach of current experimental methods; however, the information available from X-ray crystallography has increased dramatically with the advent of time-resolved structural studies of enzyme catalysis using X-ray free-electron lasers (XFELs) and synchrotrons. − These methods can, in favorable cases, provide atomic-level details for enzyme intermediates that are prerequisites for understanding enzyme mechanisms. ,,−

Classical molecular dynamics simulations (MD) can help to elucidate enzyme structure and dynamics at a level of detail that is otherwise difficult to achieve experimentally. Adding quantum mechanical (QM) electronic structure calculations enables the simulation and characterization of chemical reactions, revealing details of reaction mechanisms beyond what can be seen using classical MD alone. For example, hybrid quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) methods , have long been used to assess the likelihood of alternative enzymatic mechanistic steps. − Such studies are typically confined to small quantum regions or short time durations due to their high computational cost. Using QM/MM, reaction mechanisms typically are investigated by sampling along a particular reaction coordinate (or collective variable including several internal coordinates) that culminates in the desired product. Certain QM/MM methods enable specifying the product as a target and allow for the investigation of multiple pathways given a starting point enabling previously unknown reaction mechanisms to be revealed (see, e.g., ref ).

In addition to QM/MM, all-atom quantum mechanical molecular dynamics simulation (QMD) is emerging as a key computational tool for materials science, chemistry, and biology. − General applications of QMD still face challenges, however. Methods such as density functional theory (DFT), − Hartree–Fock (HF) , or other high-level theories are still too expensive for many applications. Conversely, faster semiempirical methods can lack the accuracy required for many applications. −

Density functional tight binding (DFTB) theory approximates first-principles DFT, providing quantum-mechanical fidelity and reasonable accuracy at a significantly lower computational cost than DFT. − Practical applications of DFTB to QMD simulations of large systems are now possible, for example, using the generalized extended Lagrangian Born–Oppenheimer Molecular Dynamics (XL-BOMD) method. − The XL-BOMD method uses an exact calculation of interatomic forces but for an approximate shadow Born–Oppenheimer potential that is given by a constrained optimization of an approximate shadow energy functional. This backward-error approach can reduce the computational cost and improve the stability of QMD simulations. , The original XL-BOMD method works most efficiently in the absence of reactions, when the energy difference between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (referred to as the HOMO–LUMO energy gap, or simply the energy gap) is sufficiently large. Studies of reactive systems using the original method, however, require switching to slow, iteratively converged self-consistent charge calculations when the energy gap becomes small. In contrast, the most recent formulation of XL-BOMD requires no iterative charge optimization even when the energy gap is small, enabling QMD simulations of highly reactive extended chemical systems. When combined with graph-based electronic structure theory, this formulation enables stable QMD simulations of reactive systems with tens-of-thousands of atoms, where all atoms are treated quantum mechanically.

Here we use the most recent formulation of XL-BOMD to develop a “reactive QMD” procedure, and apply it to investigate molecular mechanisms of enzyme catalysis in atomic detail. The enzyme we have chosen as a model system is isocyanide hydratase (ICH), which catalyzes the formation of N-formamides from isocyanides through an enzyme-linked thioimidate intermediate. The ICH reaction plays a key role in the defense of pseudomonad bacteria against isocyanide products from other microbes and is implicated in antibiotic resistance. ,, ICH is an appealing model system for developing QMD approaches to studying enzyme mechanisms because it uses both covalent and acid–base catalytic strategies and has been extensively characterized structurally. ,,, Prior studies have indicated that ICH conformational dynamics are closely coupled to active site electrostatics, including both the nucleophile cysteine and the general acid/general base aspartic acid residue. , These studies have been unable to reveal the order of protonation of the substrate/intermediate and nucleophilic attack in ICH, however. QMD has the potential to help resolve this mechanistic uncertainty and increase understanding of the role of proton mobility in the ICH mechanism and subsequent electrostatically gated conformational dynamics.

As a first step toward studying the detailed mechanism of the full ICH reaction, we focus our investigation here on the initial step: the formation of the thioimidate intermediate from para-nitrophenyl isocyanide (p-NPIC), an isocyanide that has previously been used to study ICH catalytic function. Intermediate formation has been the subject of several previous structural biology studies. Our long-term goal is to perform simulations of catalysis in the context of the full protein, treating all atoms quantum mechanically. To enable rapid prototyping and exploration of conditions, here we begin by simulating reactions relevant to catalysis in smaller systems containing molecular analogs of residues in the ICH active site. Simulations of p-NPIC in the presence of these analogs were carefully analyzed for configurational and charge dynamics. Reactions were detected by analyzing interatomic distances in combination with the atomic partial charges, revealing a progression of events that lead to thioimidate formation.

The QMD simulations suggested a possible mechanism of thioimidate formation in which protonation of the isocyanide carbon occurs prior to nucleophilic attack by the thiolate sulfur. We found that such a mechanism could be rationalized by comparing electron densities of either protonated or nonprotonated p-NPIC calculated using high-level DFT calculations. X-ray crystallographic and enzyme kinetic analyses of an engineered ICH mutant were consistent with the mechanism seen in the simulations. These findings are described below, followed by a discussion of the implications of our findings for ICH catalysis and the scope for applying our approach to further studies of enzyme catalysis in ICH and other systems.

Results

Computational Results

To begin our investigation, we assembled a small system of molecules that would demonstrate thioimidate formation. Our strategy in implementing the reactive QMD approach was to simulate test systems containing p-NPIC with molecular analogs of key ICH active site residues Cys101 and Asp17. Simulations that exhibit thioimidate formation are analyzed to reveal individual steps that might suggest experimentally testable mechanisms of enzyme catalysis. In one system, ethanethiolate (EtS-) served as an analog for Cys101 and propanoic acid as an analog for Asp17 (Figure ). In another system, methanethiolate (MeS-) served as an analog for Cys101 and acetic acid as an analog for Asp17 (SI Figure S5). Based on prior experimental characterization of ICH , and of the homologous DJ-1 protein, Cys101 is expected to be unprotonated and Asp17 is expected to be protonated prior to thioimidate formation; protonation states of the analogs therefore mirrored the states of these residues (Materials and Methods). These reactants were placed with p-NPIC in a box of water molecules along with a hydronium ion (H3O+). QMD simulations were performed for a duration of at least 50 ps, using steering to keep key reactants in close proximity (Materials and Methods).

For the present study, we extended our QMD simulation parameters beyond CNOH elements to include S (Supporting Information), which is essential for simulating catalysis in all cysteine-dependent enzymes, including ICH. An evaluation of the accuracy and transferability of these new parameters was made using comparisons to the results of a large suite of high-level DFT calculations of atomization energies and interatomic forces and experimental data (heats of formation). These tests indicate that the new S parameters are suitable for reactive chemistry simulations including thiolates (Materials and Methods; Supporting Information).

Simulations of both the MeS- and EtS- system exhibited thioimidate formation. Thioimidate formation occurred in a simulation of the MeS- system after about 15 ps (SI Figure S6). The reaction was initiated by the transfer of a proton from the acetic acid to the isocyanide carbon. The methanethiolate then participated in nucleophilic attack at the p-NPIC isocyanide carbon, forming the full thioimidate intermediate. The acetic acid remained neutral due to the transfer of a proton from the H3O+ ion through a network of two waters to the carboxyl oxygen of the acetic acid (SI Figure S7B).

To avoid reactions in the EtS- system that would not lead to thioimidate formation, guided by the behavior of the MeS- system, we steered the H3O+ oxygen toward the propanoic acid carbonyl oxygen (Materials and Methods). Thioimidate formation in the EtS- system was initiated within 50 fs by the transfer of a proton from the propanoic acid carbonyl hydrogen to the p-NPIC isocyanide carbon (Figure ), followed by formation of the thioimidate intermediate. The propanoic acid remained neutral due to the acceptance of a proton directly from the steered H3O+ ion. Simulation of the dynamics of electronic partial charges is a unique feature of QMD methods and provides a means of gaining insight into the chemical changes underlying the reactions inferred by visualization of atom configurations. Whenever atom positions are updated a corresponding electronic structure solution is calculated. The typical electron rearrangements observed in organic reactions (electron hopping) are gauged using the Mulliken charges, Δn i = −(n i – n i 0), at each atomic site i (Materials and Methods), where n i is the total number of electrons obtained from a Mulliken population analysis for atom i at the self-consistent electronic ground state, n i 0 is the electronic population of the corresponding neutral atom, and the negative sign denotes the electronic charge such that an atom with one excess electron will have a Mulliken charge of Δn = −1. SI Figure S9B shows the Mulliken charge dynamics for selected atoms during 50 ps of simulation time while Figure shows an analysis during the period of thioimidate formation for the same atoms, highlighting the reaction events. This form of qualitative charge dynamics analysis, combined with visual inspection of trajectories, helped us follow the reaction steps in sequence across the simulation.

Mulliken charge dynamics for the oxygen atom of H3O+ and the carboxyl oxygen of propanoic acid (Asp17 Oδ1 analog) are qualitatively consistent with the proton transfer observed in the simulation trajectory (Figure A,B, Figure A,B). In rapid succession, the charges on the carbonyl oxygen of propanoic acid (Asp17 Oδ2 analog) and the isocyanide carbon changed as the carbonyl hydrogen (Hδ2 analog) was transferred to the isocyanide carbon; the maximum point in the charge of the isocyanide carbon is consistent with the formation of a carbocation at that site (Figure C, Figure C). Finally, the electronic population of the thiolate sulfur changed as it bonded with the isocyanide carbon to form the full thioimidate compound (Figure D, Figure D): before the reaction, the value of Δn was near −1 on the sulfur, denoting an excess electronic population, and after the reaction it increased to a value near zero, indicating a neutralization of the charge. Biomolecular products and electron populations were stable across the remainder of the simulation.

The midpoints in charge transitions, indicating intermediates, also approximately correspond to midpoints in configurational transitions. For example, SI Figure S10 shows overlays of the Mulliken charges, Δn, for sets of two atoms with the distance from each of these atoms to the proton being exchanged between them. Once the proton is equidistant between the two atoms, the charge of each correspondingly changes. The reaction sequence shown in Figure differs from a previous proposal that the thiolate nucleophile would attack the isocyanide carbon prior to protonation of the resulting carbanion. We hypothesized that the order of events seen in QMD simulations could be due to a redistribution of charge following protonation that would make the cationic isocyanide carbon more susceptible to nucleophilic attack by the thiolate sulfur. To test this hypothesis, we performed a charge density analysis for both protonated and nonprotonated p-NPIC using high-level DFT calculations (Materials and Methods).

The DFT-calculated partial electron density shows a distinct change upon the protonation of the isocyanide carbon (Figure ). Before protonation (Figure A), the positively charged (red) isosurface surrounding the isocyanide carbon is a small, relatively flat disk and is closely flanked by negatively charged (green) isosurfaces from above and below. After protonation (Figure B), the positive (red) isosurface surrounding the isocyanide carbon is larger, indicating an increased positive charge which should facilitate nucleophilic attack by the thiolate anion.

Experimental Results

In our simulations, thioimidate formation is initiated by the transfer of a proton to the isocyanide carbon of p-NPIC from the molecular analog of Asp17 (propanoic acid in the EtS- system or acetic acid in the MeS- system). These observations suggested that Asp17 might be the general acid that protonates substrate to initiate ICH catalysis. To test this hypothesis, we made the D17N ICH mutation in Pseudomonas fluorescens ICH (PfICH). PfICH has been extensively characterized structurally and kinetically, ,, facilitating the interpretation of the effect of this mutation. An Asp-to-Asn mutation was chosen because these residues are nearly isosteric, but Asn cannot readily donate a proton, which in our simulations initiates thioimidate formation. A new crystal structure of wild-type PfICH was obtained at pH 6.5 (0.89 Å resolution), as well as crystal structures of D17N PfICH at pH values of 6.5 (1.15 Å resolution) and 8.8 (1.20 Å resolution). A prior crystal structure of wild-type PfICH at pH = 8.8 (PDB 3NON) completes the pH mutant set, permitting comparison of the effect of the D17N mutation on PfICH structure at two pH values where the enzyme has markedly different k cat values and which straddle pH = 7.0 where kinetic measurements were made (see below). Comparison of D17N and wild-type ICH active site structures show maximum displacements of ∼0.5 Å in the position of the Asn17 side chain at pH = 6.5 and ∼1.4 Å at pH = 8.8 (Figure ). These differences in residue 17 side chain position are modest, despite these structures being in different space groups (SI Table S6). The atomic resolution of the diffraction data allows us to distinguish between oxygen and nitrogen atoms based on electron density, permitting an unambiguous experimental assignment of Asn side chain orientation. Surprisingly, the N17 side chain flips ∼180° between the pH 6.5 and pH 8.8 (SI Figure S11).

Despite the overall conservative character of the Asp to Asn substitution at residue 17, there are larger changes that occur due to this substitution at pH = 6.5 that may be important for interpreting the kinetic effects of this mutation. The helix near the active site (helix H) has previously been shown to sample multiple conformations in response to Cys101 charge state that are coupled through hydrogen bonding to Ile152, which is a severe Ramachandran outlier. , Helix H is in the strained position in both wild-type and D17N PfICH crystal structures at pH = 8.8, indicating a deprotonated (ionized) Cys101 thiolate as expected at this higher pH value. In the wild-type enzyme at pH = 6.5, the helix is also predominantly in the strained conformation, consistent with prior pH-dependent structural analysis. However, some peaks in the mFo-DFc electron density map are consistent with a minor shifted helix conformation (SI Figure S12) that was not modeled owing to its low occupancy. By contrast, this helix shifts to its relaxed position in D17N PfICH at pH = 6.5, resulting in Cys101 sampling two conformations owing to the more open active site pocket (Figure ). The helix shift in D17N ICH suggests that the Asp to Asn substitution increases the pK a of Cys101, resulting in more protonated Cys101 at pH = 6.5 and thus a greater propensity to sample the relaxed helix conformation. Although we did not directly assay Cys101 pK a values here, an increase in Cys101 pK a would likely slow ICH catalysis at pH values near 6.5, due to decreased concentration of the more nucleophilic thiolate anion. This may be relevant for the interpretation of the early phase of presteady state D17N ICH kinetics at pH = 7.0 reported below. To investigate the potential role of Asp17 in intermediate formation and hydrolysis, we measured the presteady state kinetics of wild-type and D17N ICH acting on para-nitrophenyl isocyanide (p-NPIC) substrate using time-resolved UV–visible spectroscopy. The existence of the thioimidate intermediate in ICH catalysis was previously established by time-resolved serial X-ray crystallography. However, it was not possible to deconvolute the kinetics of thioimidate and product formation in the single-wavelength presteady state kinetic data in that prior study because both the thioimidate intermediate and N-formamide product absorb at 320 nm where the reaction was monitored. We addressed this by measuring presteady state kinetics of wild-type using a multiwavelength charge-coupled device (CCD) detector that records full absorption spectra at each measurement (Materials and Methods). Both single-turnover (where substrate is limiting) and multiple-turnover (where enzyme is limiting) experiments were conducted to permit analysis of the time-dependent spectra and kinetics of the reaction for each spectrally resolvable component (Figure ). Single-turnover spectra of wild-type ICH show a decrease at wavelengths ranging from 240 to 280 nm (where the p-NPIC substrate absorbs) and increase from 280 to 400 nm (where para-nitrophenylformamide (p-NPF) product absorbs), as expected during ICH catalysis. Consistent with prior results, we observe a kinetic burst in the 320 nm absorbance during the first few hundred msec of the multiple-turnover experiment before steady-state conditions are established (Figure B).

In this scheme, S is the p-NPIC substrate, EI is the enzyme-linked thioimidate intermediate, and P is the p-NPF product. During the fitting process the rate constants k –1 and k –3 consistently went to zero and thus were fixed to this value for the final fitting. The software used for the global fitting, KinTek Global Explorer, reconstructs the estimated spectral components (Figure C). The candidate thioimidate species corresponds to the third SVD component (0.36, 1.05), and its deconvoluted spectrum features an absorption maximum at 335 nm (Figure C, yellow), which closely resembles a species observed in a prior single-turnover measurement of a catalytically impaired G150A ICH mutant that we proposed was the thioimidate intermediate. The predicted concentrations of substrate, intermediate, and product with time under single-turnover conditions (20 μM enzyme and 15 μM substrate) show the intermediate decreasing as product accumulates, as expected (Figure D). These data permit fitting of rate constants for each step of the proposed model (SI Table S8). The fitted rate constant for formation of the intermediate (k 2 = 7.74 s–1) is greater than the product release step (k 3 = 0.926 s–1), providing a rationale for the observed burst and supporting a prior suggestion that product release is rate-limiting in the wild-type ICH kinetic mechanism.

Similar presteady state kinetics and SVD analysis of D17N ICH yields three spectral species with singular values of 8.56, 0.929, and 0.0378 and autocorrelation values of 1.00, 1.04, and 1.01, respectively. The deconvoluted spectra are similar to those of wild-type ICH (Figure ), and the small third SVD amplitude indicates that D17N ICH has a reduced thioimidate spectral component compared to wild-type ICH (see above). To facilitate kinetic comparison between wild-type and D17N ICH, k –1 and k –3 were set to zero when globally fitting the D17N kinetic data. Single wavelength data at 320 nm (Figure B) show a much slower reaction for D17N ICH relative to the wild-type enzyme, as reflected in its 490-fold lower calculated steady-state k cat value (SI Table S8). The rates of enzyme–substrate complex (k 1) and thioimidate formation (k 2) in D17N ICH are 19- and 7-fold slower than wild-type ICH, supporting the QMD result that the general acid at residue 17 is important for early steps in the ICH mechanism. The rate of intermediate hydrolysis/product release (k 3) is over 400-fold slower in D17N ICH, consistent with an important later role of this residue in activating water to attack the scissile C–S bond of the thioimidate to liberate p-NPF product (Figure ). That portion of the reaction was not simulated. The larger kinetic impairment in intermediate hydrolysis/product release resulting from the D17N mutation suggests that Asp17 is most important for this step, likely as a general base.

Discussion

We developed a reactive QMD method and applied it to solvated systems of p-NPIC with molecular analogs of ICH active site residues. The method revealed plausible steps of thioimidate formation, leading to experiments that yielded new insights into the ICH enzyme mechanism. The ICH mechanism features a catalytically essential cysteine nucleophile that attacks isocyanide substrates to form a thioimidate covalent intermediate, which is subsequently hydrolyzed to an N-formamide product. , The initially hypothesized ICH mechanism was that the cysteine nucleophile attacks the isocyanide substate in its electrophilic carbenoid form first, followed by a protonation from the carboxylic acid to stabilize the thioimidate intermediate, though the ordering of the protonation step was acknowledged to be uncertain. These mechanistic questions hinge on the time evolution of the charge states of active site residues, which is challenging to observe experimentally. The present QMD simulations using molecular analogs revealed a “protonation-first” mechanism that precedes nucleophilic attack to form the thioimidate intermediate differing from the “attack-first” mechanism previously proposed for ICH. Comparisons of electron densities calculated using high-level theory yielded additional insights into how initial protonation may enhance nucleophilic attack at the isocyanide carbon atom (Figure ).

The simulations suggested that Asp17 might donate a proton during intermediate formation in ICH catalysis, leading to the design of new experiments involving a D17N mutant that we performed as a part of this study. New crystal structures and presteady state kinetics measurements support an important role for Asp17 during intermediate formation and hydrolysis. Analysis of time-resolved UV–visible spectra and presteady state kinetics establish that the ionizability of Asp17 plays an important role in initial substrate binding and thioimidate formation, although it is not strictly necessary, as the D17N mutant enzyme retains low activity. We speculate that this residual activity is due to less efficient proton donation from solvent when a properly positioned general acid at position 17 is absent, as it is in D17N ICH. We note that an ordered water was observed near Cys101 in the first crystal structures of ICH and was speculated to be a potential proton source. However, our subsequent time-resolved crystallographic studies of ICH show that this water is displaced by the intermediate, making direct protonation by D17 more structurally likely. In addition, D17N ICH hydrolyzes the thioimidate intermediate 400 times slower than the wild-type enzyme (Figures and , SI Table S8), demonstrating the clear importance of Asp17 in later reaction steps, where we propose it acts as a general base to activate water to hydrolyze the thioimidate intermediate. In total, these kinetic results support an important role for early proton donation by Asp17 and a kinetically dominant role later as a general base that facilitates hydrolysis of the thioimidate.

The ability to provide information for designing informative experiments demonstrates the utility of the reactive QMD method for investigating chemical reactions relevant to ICH catalysis. Future work will address some of the limitations of the current application. For example, our investigation here was limited to intermediate formation; to provide a mechanistic picture of the full reaction, a reactive QMD investigation of product formation is underway. Another key limitation of this study is that the simulations did not include the full protein environment. To address this limitation, efforts to extend the reactive QMD approach to all-atom simulations of enzyme catalysis are underway. Although the computational cost of quantum electronic structure calculations has typically limited QMD simulations to small systems or short time scales, by using updated computing hardware and a novel graph-based electronic structure scheme, , longer simulations of solvated protein systems are now feasible. Such simulations can potentially aid in determining whether the mechanisms seen using molecular analogs occur during enzyme catalysis. They also might reveal additional plausible mechanisms of catalysis and suggest further experiments.

Although the present experimental results are consistent with the protonation of substrate seen in the QMD simulations, they do not definitively address whether proton donation precedes or follows nucleophilic attack by Cys101. Previous experimental studies of hydrolysis of methyl isocyanide, however, do support an initial hydrogen bonding between a general acid and the isocyanide carbon preceding nucleophilic attack. Because deprotonation of Asp17 is coupled to a displacement of the Ile152 loop that permits water to enter into the ICH active site, ,, determining the order of Cys101 attack and Asp17 deprotonation is important for understanding how electrostatic changes in the ICH active site gate enzyme motion. Therefore, interpreting the QMD-supported “protonation-first” mechanism in the context of prior time-resolved crystallography and crystalline MD simulation results, we propose a revised model for ICH catalysis where early Asp17 deprotonation is required for Ile152 loop motion that occurs concomitantly with or after helix H displacement that results from later thioimidate formation. This revision illustrates the potential for reactive QMD simulations of small molecule analogs to yield insights about mechanisms of full proteins.

In the simulations presented here, H3O+ donates a proton, leaving the Asp17 analog in a neutral state after thioimidate formation. Intermediate formation in ICH, however, might occur by direct donation of a proton by the Asp17, leaving it in a charged state that is later neutralized upon accepting a proton during product formation. To determine whether donation of a proton by H3O+ is necessary for thioimidate formation, we performed simulations of the EtS- system where the propanoic acid becomes charged after donating a proton, without accepting a proton from the environment (unpublished). In one simulation, the H3O+ was replaced by a NH4 + (we do not yet have parameters for common elemental ions and therefore chose a positive molecular ion to maintain charge neutrality) and the propanoic acid proton was steered toward the isocyanide carbon; in this simulation, the proton was transferred directly and left the propanoic acid in a charged state. In another simulation, we kept the H3O+ and added a water molecule that bridged a gap between the propanoic acid and the isocyanide carbon, and used steering to shuttle the propanoic acid proton indirectly through the water toward the isocyanide carbon. In this simulation, the bridging water donated a proton to the isocyanide carbon and accepted a proton from the propanoic acid, which was left in a charged state. Together these additional simulations indicate that protonation can occur prior to thioimidate formation even when H3O+ is not a donor. Nevertheless, several independent factors suggest a possible role for H3O+ as an indirect proton donor through the Asp17 carboxyl group in ICH catalysis: (1) wild-type ICH activity shows a strong pH dependence with a maximum at an acidic value of 4.8; (2) the stoichiometry of the full reaction demands that the proton must ultimately be returned to the solvent, giving hydronium the role of a catalyst rather than a substrate; and (3) an ordered water visible near Cys101 in early ICH crystal structures (see above) is a potential location for a H3O+ ion.

Whereas typical chemical reaction diagrams show the movement of whole electrons (e.g., Figure ), our QMD simulations provide information about the electronic structure through the analysis of fractional electronic populations. Analysis of the Mulliken charges, Δn i , for each atom i can reveal electronic rearrangements enabling identification of charged intermediates, such as the carbocation formed during thioimidate formation (Figure ). In addition, although the Mulliken charge dynamics do not directly provide information about electron movement, charge transfer can be inferred when pairs of atoms change charge concurrently, coinciding with changes in molecular bonding inferred by configuration analysis.

As another potential way to track chemical reactions in simulation, the HOMO–LUMO energy gap can be computed across simulation time. Indeed, our simulations show a sharp increase in the energy gap from ∼1.0 eV to just over 2.5 eV corresponding to changes observed in the dynamic bonding and Mulliken charge analyses that suggested the formation of thioimidate. This increase in HOMO–LUMO energy gap signals the transition of a species with high chemical reactivity to a species with lower chemical reactivity, as is expected with the formation of the stable thioimidate intermediate. Plots of the energy gap over simulation time are shown in SI Figure S13.

ICH is a member of the large DJ-1 protein superfamily, nearly all of whose members feature a conserved, reactive cysteine residue. Prior study of other DJ-1 proteins has shown that the active site Cys residues have pK a values of ∼5, ,, indicating that they are ionized to the reactive thiolate anion at physiological pH. However, despite sharing the key low pK a Cys nucleophile, these proteins do not all possess ICH activity. Therefore, other key structural determinants of ICH activity beyond the catalytic active site cysteine residue must exist. ICHs are distinguished from nearly all other DJ-1 superfamily proteins by having an Asp rather than a Glu proximal to the Cys nucleophile, providing a possible explanation for their distinct enzymatic activity. Prior atomic resolution bond length analysis of 1.05 Å resolution crystal structure of wild-type PfICH shows that this Asp is protonated at pH values above 8, which is similar to the protonation observed for the Glu in human DJ-1. Given the broad similarities among different DJ-1 superfamily enzyme active sites, observing the dynamics of active site ionization during catalysis using computational methods might help to elucidate the mechanisms of other members of this large group of proteins.

Mobile protons play essential roles in many enzymes, yet they are difficult to observe experimentally, making the ordering of proton-dependent steps ambiguous in many enzyme mechanisms. As was the case for ICH, reactive QMD can potentially suggest specific orderings for other enzymes that can be tested experimentally. In some cases, even the route of proton transport between the enzyme active site and the environment can be uncertain; for example, room-temperature X-ray crystallography studies of Photosystem II (PS II) provide clues about which water channel connecting the oxygen-evolving complex to the bulk is favored for proton release but do not provide direct evidence of the pathway. Classical MD simulations of PS II already have yielded a picture of possible water networks, providing additional clues; reactive QMD simulations might provide an atomistic picture of whether the proposed water networks can plausibly support proton transport.

Our findings indicate that reactive QMD simulations can be used to discover plausible reaction mechanisms in systems of interacting biomolecules, motivating new experiments and yielding insight into enzyme function. The simulation and analysis techniques developed here can, in principle, be applied to a broad range of enzymes to better understand the underlying mechanisms. To set up a test system, one need only know the ″key players″ in a catalytic reaction, e.g., substrates and side chain functional groups. From these components, the reactive QMD approach developed here can be used to study many kinds of reactions, leading to the design of new experimental studies and a deeper understanding of catalytic mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Computational Design

Quantum Mechanical Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a powerful computational tool for investigating atomic-scale processes. Classical MD simulations have a long history of providing insights that complement and expand upon laboratory experiments but are limited in their treatment of charge dynamics and are often unable to capture phenomena beyond simple conformational changes. Quantum mechanical molecular dynamics (QMD) methods extend the capabilities of classical MD, including the ability of simulating chemical reactions and charge equilibration dynamics and analyzing elements of the electronic structure. Unfortunately, QMD methods are costly and have previously been prohibitively expensive for large systems or long simulation times. Using a reformulated version of direct Born–Oppenheimer MD developed by Niklasson and co-workers, , it is possible to preserve the accuracy of tightly converged QMD simulations while substantially reducing the computational cost. The reformulated method, termed extended-Lagrangian Born–Oppenheimer MD (XL-BOMD) was described in detail previously. XL-BOMD starts from the Born–Oppenheimer approximation , with the assumption that fast and light electrons evolve on a much faster time scale than the heavier nuclear degrees of freedom. The nuclear motion is then driven by interatomic forces that are determined by the instantaneously relaxed electronic ground state in each nuclear time step. The self-consistent calculation of the electronic ground state densities, however, is expensive and requires iterative nonlinear solvers that slow down the calculations for each time step, and in practice the convergence is always approximate. Instead, a shadow energy functional can be constructed by linearizing the original energy functional around an approximate electronic ground state density, n(t). Then, instead of iteratively solving for the ground state density of a nonlinear functional, we can find the exact ground state density in a single step for the approximate linearized energy functional. In this way we replace (A) the iterative ground state optimization for the calculation of approximate forces for an exact potential with (B) the fast, direct calculation of exact forces for an approximate shadow potential. This method introduces an approximate ground-state density, n(t), that is propagated dynamically through a harmonic restraint to the exact Born–Oppenheimer ground state. The deviation of n(t) from the exact density (known as the residual) determines the accuracy of the method (difference between the exact and approximate potential energy). The equations of motion for n(t) include a kernel function to help keep the value of n(t) close to the exact ground state density. Full calculation of the kernel can be expensive, so a preconditioned low-rank approximation can be used to improve computational efficiency. The selection of the kernel function and the full calculation of low-rank approximation have been described in detail previously. In this study, a third-rank approximation was used. This shadow QMD scheme has been shown to be highly accurate and efficient for a broad range of problems. ,,,

QMD simulations of biomolecular systems, which may require models with tens-of-thousands of atoms or more, typically run too slowly to observe reactions. This limitation can potentially be overcome using parallel computing; however, quantum-based molecular dynamics simulations are often difficult to parallelize in a memory distributed mode. A recently developed graph-partitioning scheme, however, enables distribution of electronic structure calculations during QMD simulations across large computing clusters. , This scheme has been used successfully to partition and simulate large protein systems using quantum-based electronic structure methods. , The current implementation also includes first-level shadow potential formulations to address extremely reactive chemical systems at a low additional cost.

The GPMDK code used for the present QMD simulations includes all of these XL-BOMD advances (https://github.com/lanl/qmd-progress/tree/master/examples/gpmdk). GPMDK implements the latest graph-based electronic structure theory including the first-level shadow potential formulation in which a low-rank preconditioned Krylov subspace approximation is used to integrate the extended electronic degrees of freedom. , The LATTE Hamiltonian DFTB method was used with the lanl31 parametrization for CNOH elements, extended to include sulfur (below). The implementation was extended to accelerate matrix math calculations on GPUs, using the Basic Matrix Library (BML) for quantum chemistry.

lanl31+S DFTB Parameters

To enable simulation of organosulfur compounds (such as thiolates), the lanl31 DFTB parameters for CNOH were extended to include S, as described in Supporting Information. The resulting optimized lanl31+S parameters were evaluated using the 7,173 molecules from the QM-7 data set − that contain only C, H, N, O, and/or S (SI Figure S3). The coefficient of determination and RMSE of the lanl31+S per-atom atomization for the full QM-7 data set are 0.992 and 0.0195 eV per atom, respectively. The corresponding values for the subset of molecules containing sulfur are 0.968 and 0.0498 eV per atom, respectively. In addition, measured heats of formation were obtained for a set of 217 organosulfur compounds and were compared with lanl31+S computed values (SI Figure S4), yielding a coefficient of determination and RMSE of R 2 = 0.932 and 58.6 kJ mol–1, respectively. The lanl31+S parameters therefore are suitable for modeling the electronic structure of organosulfur compounds such as the ethanethiolate used in our simulations.

Simulation Setup

To set up the EtS- test system, ethanethiolate, propanoic acid, and p-NPIC were arranged in a 15.0 × 15.0 × 15.0 Å3 periodic box and output to a PDB file using the Avogadro software package. , Protonation states of ethanethiolate and propanoic acid were chosen to reflect the expected protonation states of the Cys101 and Asp17 side chains in the ICH active site. The pK a of cysteine is known to vary widely based on protein microenvironment, ranging from 3.5 to 12. − Previous experimental studies of this ICH enzyme suggest a pK a for Cys101 of ∼6 (Figure 2 in ref ) which is within 1 pH unit of the pK a values for catalytic cystines in other members of the DJ-1 superfamily; for example, we previously experimentally determined that the equivalent Cys106 in the homologous DJ-1 protein has a value of pK a = 5.4. The deprotonation of the thiol in catalytically active ICH is also supported by previous studies which find that ICH has a maximum catalytic activity around pH 4.8 and that catalytic activity is lost at lower pHs where Cys101 is most likely protonated. , Therefore, we simulated ethanethiolate (Cys101 analog) without a hydrogen on the sulfur atom. Although the ICH Asp17 pK a has not been measured experimentally, several crystal structures of WT ICH in the pH ∼7–9 range that show a protonated Asp17 carboxylic acid by atomic resolution bond length analysis, suggesting that in the pH range of maximum ICH activity, Asp17 is expected to be protonated. Therefore, the propanoic acid (Asp17 analog) was protonated in the simulation. Equivalent choices were made for the MeS- simulation (Supporting Information).

The EtS- system was solvated using the GROMACS gmx solvate method in a 15.0 × 15.0 × 15.0 Å3 simulation box containing 94 water molecules. An extra proton was manually added to the oxygen of a water molecule to form a hydronium ion (H3O+) in the vicinity of the carboxylic acid functional group, to neutralize the system. No additional counterions were added. The final system contained 317 atoms. For comparison, the MeS- system had 95 water molecules and 314 atoms (Supporting Information).

Simulations were performed using a Langevin thermostat at a temperature of 300 K with a time constant of 0.01 fs and an electronic temperature of 0.025 eV. An integration time step of 0.25 fs was used, and hydrogen atom masses were converted to deuterium mass. A rank 3 update was used for the XL-BOMD kernel method. This combination of choices yielded sufficiently small atom position updates to maintain the stability of the simulations. No atomic positions were restrained or fixed. Simulations were run on the Chicoma high performance computing platform at LANL, using a Nvidia A100 GPU for acceleration. For the EtS- system containing 317 atoms and 856 electrons, each time step took ∼0.97 s wall-clock time.

Snapshots of the atom positions were recorded every 10 time steps, yielding simulation trajectories that were used for analysis. To identify reaction events, the trajectories were visualized in VMD. , Atoms were displayed using van der Waals spheres. The “dynamic bonds” representation was used to infer covalent linkages. In this representation, each trajectory snapshot is analyzed to display a connection between selected atom pairs that are within a specified cutoff distance, enabling visual tracking of bond formation and breaking. The cutoff distance used here for dynamic bonds was 1.8 Å to show connectivity for typical bond lengths, which are generally between 1.09 Å (for a carbon–hydrogen bond) to 1.54 Å (for a carbon–carbon bond). This cutoff was used for all bonds except the carbon–sulfur bond in the thiolate - this dynamic bond cutoff distance was set to 2.0 Å since the average C–S single bond length is ∼1.83 Å. Bond lengths for Cys101 C–S bonds in Protein Data Bank (PDB) entry 6UND, a crystal structure of ICH bound to p-NPIC obtained using mix-and-inject serial crystallography, are consistent with the C–S bond lengths seen in the simulations.

Steered Molecular Dynamics Method

In QMD simulations, observation of reactions can be hindered by the inability to reach simulation durations sufficient for reactants to find each other via diffusion. Steering techniques are popular in classical MD simulations to study a variety of important biological processes including protein–ligand binding, − protein folding, , and membrane transport. − We developed a simple steered molecular dynamics (SMD) method for our simulations, to enable reactions to occur during the simulation duration (see the Supporting Information for full implementation details). The steering is applied to a set of selected atom pairs. A constant force of 0.15 eV Å–1 was applied between the atoms at long distance, decreasing to zero at short distance using a logistic function with a switching distance of 2.0 Å. These parameters were tuned with the aim of bringing molecules into close proximity without substantially influencing subsequent chemical transformations. Our use of SMD is therefore distinct from approaches that bias simulations using the active site geometry, a reaction coordinate, or the reaction product as a target. In the MeS- system, the following pairs of atoms were selected for SMD: (methanethiolate sulfur, p-NPIC isocyanide carbon) and (acetic acid neutralizing hydrogen, p-NPIC isocyanide carbon). In the EtS- system, the following pairs of atoms were selected: (ethanethiolate sulfur, p-NPIC isocyanide carbon); (propanoic acid neutralizing hydrogen, p-NPIC isocyanide carbon); and (H3O+ oxygen, propanoic acid carbonyl oxygen). Using SMD, simulations could proceed for 15 ps or longer without initiating thioimidate formation (SI Figures S6 and S9A), indicating that SMD worked as intended in these simulations.

Charge Dynamics Analysis

Mulliken population analysis is a technique that can be used to better understand the distribution of electrons among orbitals within a molecular system. Mulliken population is a key element of the electronic structure and can be understood as the probability of finding an electron integrated over the vicinity of a certain atom. Within the context of the DFTB method, the Mulliken charge, Δn i , for atom i is derived from the corresponding Mulliken populations via Δn i = −(n i – n i 0) where n i = ∑ν∈i (Sρ) νν is the number of valence electrons in atom i; S is the overlap matrix, in which the elements S μν are precisely defined as the inner product S μν = ∫ψ μ (r)­ψν *(r) dr, where μ and ν are elements of the basis set (localized atomic orbitals); ρ is the density matrix; and n i 0 is the number of valence electrons in the neutral species of atom i; and ρ is the density matrix. The density matrix is the key determinant of the electronic structure dynamics as every element ρ μν contains information about the degree of electronic coupling between orbitals μ and ν at each time point of the simulation. Using tight binding theory, the density matrix for a system is calculated based on amplitudes corresponding to electron occupancies of molecular orbitals. The Δn values are calculated for each snapshot and are recorded along with atomic coordinates in custom XYZ structure files, enabling them to be analyzed and plotted across simulation time. Tracking the Mulliken charges for atoms across simulation time provides a means of combining information about charge dynamics with changes in atom configurations, to gain additional insights into potential reaction mechanisms. Although Mulliken charges can vary depending on the basis set and have limitations for some quantitative applications, they are readily available from our simulations and are well-suited for such a qualitative analysis.

DFT Electronic Structure Calculations

DFT calculations for p-NPIC either with or without a hydrogen added to the isocyanide carbon were performed using the SIESTA code. The GGA exchange correlation functional together with a double-ζ basis set was used. Atomic configurations were optimized up to a tolerance of 0.04 eV Å–1. Calculation of the electronic structure was performed at the Gamma point for a 10.0 × 10.0 × 10.0 Å3 simulation box. Input files used for the SIESTA calculations are available at https://chemrxiv.org/engage/chemrxiv/article-details/67d1defbfa469535b900b19a.

Experimental Design

Purification of ICH

Wild-type Pseudomonas fluorescens ICH (PfICH) and the D17N mutant protein were expressed and purified based on a previously published protocol. Briefly, 100 mL of Luria–Bertani, Miller (LB) medium was inoculated with E. coli BL21­(DE3) containing either wild-type or D17N PfICH genes cloned into the pET15b expression vector and grown overnight to stationary phase. These starter cultures were used to inoculate 1.5 L of LB medium at OD600 = 0.1. Cells were grown at 37 °C with shaking to an OD600 = 0.4 then induced with 200 μM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 4 h at 37 °C. Cells were collected via centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES pH = 7.5, 350 mM NaCl). Resuspended cells were lysed by addition of 1 mg of egg white lysozyme per gram of cell pellet and incubated with gentle stirring for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then sonicated (Misonix 3000) on ice and centrifuged to remove cell debris. Clarified supernatants were then incubated with 5 mL of His-select Ni2+ resin (Sigma) for 30 min at 4 °C. The column was then washed with lysis buffer and bound protein was eluted using 250 mM imidazole in lysis buffer. The 6xHis-tag was cleaved via 1 unit thrombin/mg purified protein overnight at 4 °C during dialysis into storage buffer (25 mM HEPES pH = 7.5, 150 mM KCl). The protein was then passed over the His-select Ni2+ resin once more to remove the 6xHis tag followed by benzamidine-sepharose (Cytiva) resin to remove thrombin. Purified PfICH was concentrated via centrifugation with a 10 kDa MWCO filter (Millipore) to ∼20 mg/mL and then flash cooled in 50–200 mL aliquots in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Crystallization, X-ray Crystallographic Data Collection, and Refinement

Previous PfICH crystallization conditions were used with slight modification. Crystals were grown via hanging drop with a 1:1 ratio of protein (20 mg/mL) to reservoir solution (23% PEG 3350, 200 mM MgCl2, 100 mM 2-(N-morpholino)­ethanesulfonic acid (MES) pH = 6.5 or 23% PEG 3350, 200 mM MgCl2, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH = 8.8). Crystals were cryoprotected by serial transfer through reservoir solution supplemented with ethylene glycol in concentrations that increased by 5% increments to a final concentration of 20–25% v/v ethylene glycol. Crystals were mounted in nylon loops and plunge-cooled in liquid nitrogen.

X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamline 12–2 at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource (SSRL) at 100 K using shutterless data collection and a Dectris Eiger X 16M pixel array detector. Data were indexed and integrated using XDS and scaled using Aimless in the CCP4 suite. Final data statistics are reported in SI Table S6. Initial models were determined using molecular replacement phasing using Phaser, then refined in PHENIX using riding hydrogen atoms, anisotropic atomic displacement parameters (ADPs), and optimized stereochemical and ADP restraint weights. Models were improved in Coot using 2mFo-DFc and mFo-DFc electron density maps followed by refinement in PHENIX. Model validation was performed using the validation tools in Coot and MolProbity and final model statistics are reported in SI Table S6.

ICH Stopped-Flow Kinetics

Kinetic experiments were performed on a KinTek SF-300X stopped-flow mixing device utilizing a deuterium lamp and monitored using a multiwavelength CCD array detector. Data were collected in 100 mM potassium phosphate (pH = 7.0), 50 mM KCl reaction buffer and the cell was thermostated at 25 °C using a circulating water bath. The final concentration of enzyme after mixing was 20 μM in all experiments with various concentrations of p-NPIC, ranging from 5 to 240 μM after mixing. A stock solution of p-NPIC was prepared fresh in DMSO at 25 mM, with less than 1% DMSO carried over into the final enzyme mixture.

Spectral data were averaged from at least three kinetic runs. Kinetic progress curves at 270 and 320 nm were extracted from the original multiwavelength data collection. Spectra collected under single-turnover conditions using 20 μM enzyme (after mixing) and 15 μM p-NPIC substrate (after mixing) were subjected to a singular value decomposition using KinTek Global Kinetic Explorer software (Austin, TX). Significant amplitudes, or time-dependent eigenvectors, were chosen based on the magnitude of singular values. SVD amplitudes and single wavelength data (at 270 and 320 nm), were globally fitted using KinTek software to the following mechanism, E + S ↔ ES ↔ EI → E + P, using nonlinear least-squares and numerical integration. The absorbance signal of in the single wavelength data at 320 nm were estimated with a molar extinction coefficient of 1.33 × 104 M–1 cm–1 for the p-NPF product and 1.05 × 104 M–1 cm–1 at 270 nm for p-NPIC substrate. After fitting the model, model-derived state variables ([S]­(t), [EI]­(t), and [P]­(t)) corresponding to significant spectral eigenvectors were used to solve for basis spectra using SVD-based matrix least-squares. All parameter sets that produced an error ratio (errorbest‑fit/error i ) threshold value within 0.833 of the best-fit value were used to determine upper and lower parameter bounds. Steady-state kinetic parameters were calculated from the fitted rate constants using their definitions provided by the fitted mechanism. All kinetic figures were generated using MATLAB R2024a software.
