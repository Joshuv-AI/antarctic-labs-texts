Development and evaluation of a recombinant bivalent HVT-vectored vaccine candidate against H9N2 avian influenza virus and fowl adenovirus serotype 4
Authors: Xun Jing, Hongyu Zhao, Wenbin Chen, Shishi Zhang, Chenghui Li, Ao Wang, Zhimin Jiang, Honglei Sun, Yipeng Sun, Juan Pu, Jinhua Liu, Litao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractH9N2 subtype avian influenza virus (AIV) is one of the most prevalent AIV subtypes in poultry worldwide. Meanwhile, fowl adenovirus serotype 4 (FAdV-4), associated with hepatitis-hydropericardium syndrome (HHS), has been widely distributed in poultry worldwide and has circulated in China since 2015. Notably, co-infections with H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 are frequently reported in the field, leading to increased mortality and substantial economic losses in the poultry industry. Herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT) has been extensively used as a viral vector for poultry vaccines. In this study, a recombinant bivalent HVT-vectored vaccine candidate (HVT-H9-FAd) co-expressing the hemagglutinin (HA) gene of H9N2 AIV and the Fiber-2 gene of FAdV-4 was constructed and evaluated. The recombinant virus exhibited plaque morphology and growth kinetics comparable to the parental HVT strain and stably expressed HA and Fiber-2 proteins for 20 serial passages. Immunization of specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens with HVT-H9-FAd induced robust antibody responses against H9N2 AIV and Fiber-2, with antibody kinetics comparable to those elicited by the corresponding monovalent HVT-H9 and HVT-FAd. Following challenge with the homologous H9N2 AIV strain, HVT-H9-FAd immunized chickens were completely protected, with no detectable viral shedding. Following challenge with the homologous FAdV-4 strain, complete protection was observed, as evidenced by the absence of mortality, viral replication, and pathological lesions in immunized chickens. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the recombinant HVT-H9-FAd is a promising bivalent vaccine candidate for controlling H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 in poultry and provides an effective strategy for preventing co-circulating pathogens in chicken flocks.Keywords: FAdV-4, H9N2 AIV, HVT-H9-FAd, HVT, Vaccine

Introduction

H9N2 subtype avian influenza virus (AIV) is widely prevalent in poultry worldwide, particularly in Asia (Yang et al., 2025a) and the Middle East (Mosaad et al., 2025). Infection with H9N2 AIV typically causes respiratory disease and reduced production performance (Bin Aslam et al., 2024). More importantly, co-infections with other pathogens can exacerbate clinical symptoms and increase mortality in flocks, resulting in substantial economic losses (Kong et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024). Since 2016, H9N2 AIV has become the dominant AIV subtype circulating in China (Bi et al., 2020). Recent studies have indicated that circulating H9N2 strains have acquired molecular features associated with human receptor binding, and serological surveillance in occupationally exposed populations has revealed evidence of H9N2 AIV exposure in humans, highlighting the risk of cross-species transmission (Yang et al., 2025a; Bo et al., 2026). Furthermore, H9N2 AIV can donate a complete or partial set of internal genes to novel human-infecting AIVs, such as H7N9 (Pu et al., 2015), H10N8 (Qi et al., 2014), H5N6 (Yang et al., 2015), and H3N8 (Yang et al., 2022), posing a severe threat to public health.

In 1987, hepatitis-hydropericardium syndrome (HHS) caused by fowl adenovirus type 4 (FAdV-4) was first reported in Pakistan (Anjum et al., 1989), and was characterized by pericardial effusion, hepatic necrosis, and renal swelling (Abe et al., 1998). Subsequently, the disease has spread to multiple regions with intensive poultry production, including Asia (Choi et al., 2012; Mase et al., 2012), South America (Toro et al., 1999), and Europe (Schachner et al., 2018), where it continues to circulate in poultry populations and poses a persistent challenge to the poultry industry. Since 2015, outbreaks of highly pathogenic FAdV-4 have been reported in several provinces in China (Zhao et al., 2015). Due to its high transmissibility and pathogenicity, FAdV-4 has rapidly spread in intensive poultry farms, causing substantial economic losses. Notably, co-infections with H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 are frequently detected in commercial broilers, which further increase mortality and worsen production performance in the poultry industry (Yu et al., 2019).

In China, multivalent inactivated whole-virus vaccines (IWVs) are currently used to control H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 infections. However, accumulating evidence indicates that IWVs against H9N2 AIV fail to completely prevent upper respiratory tract infection and viral shedding, thereby allowing viral replication under immune pressure and promoting antigenic drift and immune escape (Hu et al., 2025). Although IWVs can significantly reduce mortality caused by FAdV-4 infection, highly pathogenic FAdV-4 remains prevalent in the field (Niu et al., 2022). Herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT) is regarded as one of the most effective viral vectors among live multivalent vaccines and has been widely applied for the control of various avian pathogens (Wang et al., 2024). Recombinant HVT-vectored vaccines expressing antigens from Newcastle disease virus (NDV), AIV, infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), and infectious laryngotracheitis virus (ILTV) have demonstrated robust protective efficacy in previous studies (Gergen et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; van Hulten et al., 2021; Criado et al., 2023). Compared with other vaccines, recombinant HVT-vectored vaccines are characterized by early-life applicability following in ovo or one-day-old chick administration, and the ability to induce long-lasting humoral and cellular immune responses. (Calnek, 2001; Yang et al., 2025b). Moreover, simultaneous immunization with multiple monovalent HVT-vectored vaccines has been reported to reduce the protective efficacy of each vaccine (Dunn et al., 2019). The multiple insertion sites within the HVT genome enable the development of multivalent vaccines expressing multiple antigens (Gao et al., 2011; Zai et al., 2022). A multivalent HVT-vectored vaccine capable of delivering multiple antigens within a single vector represents a promising approach to control infections caused by multiple pathogens. In this study, a recombinant HVT-H9-FAd virus co-expressing the HA gene of H9N2 AIV and the Fiber-2 gene of FAdV-4 was constructed. The expression of the inserted genes, genetic stability, and replication of HVT-H9-FAd were evaluated in vitro, and its protective efficacy against H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 was further assessed in SPF chickens following challenge.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal research was approved by the Beijing Association for Science and Technology (approval ID SYXK, Beijing, 2007-0023) and was performed in compliance with the Beijing Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics guidelines, as issued by the Beijing Administration Committee of Laboratory Animals, and in accordance with the China Agricultural University (CAU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines (ID: AW32013202-2-1) approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of CAU.

Animals, viruses and cells

One-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) White Leghorn chickens (sex-linked) were obtained from Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Vital Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The HVT FC126 strain (NCBI accession number, AF291866.1) was used as the parental virus in this study. HVT and recombinant HVT viruses were propagated in primary chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEF) cells. CEF cells and leghorn male hepatoma (LMH) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 1% antibiotics and 5% or 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), respectively. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The H9N2 AIV strain A/chicken/Chongqing/1201/2022 (CQ/2022) was previously isolated from Chongqing, China, and preserved in our laboratory. The viral titer was determined as 50% chicken infectious doses (CID50). The FAdV-4 strain SD1504/2015 (SD1504) was isolated from Shandong Province, China, as previously described (Jiang et al., 2019), and its 50% lethal doses (LD50) was determined.

Generation of recombinant HVT viruses

The recombinant HVT viruses were generated using the bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) combined with galactokinase (galK) selection and the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing system, as previously described (Liu et al., 2019). Briefly, the HA gene derived from the CQ/2022 strain was amplified by the H9-F/R primers and cloned into pcDNA3.1+ vector (Invitrogen, USA) to generate the HA expression cassette, which was driven by the CMV promoter and terminated by the bGH polyadenylation. The Fiber-2 gene of the SD1504 strain was amplified by the Fiber-2-F/R primers and cloned into pDC315 vector (Microbix Biosystems, Inc. Canada) to generate the Fiber-2 expression cassette, driven by the mCMV promoter and terminated by the SV40 polyadenylation. Subsequently, the galK cassette was first amplified using primers (homo-UL45-galK-F/R, Table 1) containing 50-bp homologous arms corresponding to the UL45/46 intergenic region and inserted into the HVT-BAC by homologous recombination, generating HVT-BAC-UL45/galK. The galK cassette in HVT-BAC-UL45/galK was replaced with the HA expression cassette by homologous recombination in SW102 cells following electroporation, generating recombinant HVT-BAC-H9 clones. Similarly, the Fiber-2 expression cassette was inserted into the SORF3/US2 intergenic region by replacing the US2-galK cassette (homo-US2-galK-F/R, Table 1) using the same homologous recombination strategy, generating the recombinant HVT-BAC-H9-FAd clone. Finally, BAC sequences were excised from the recombinant HVT-BAC-H9-FAd using the CRISPR/Cas9 system as previously described (Liu et al., 2019). The pX458-sgRNA, donor DNA and HVT-BAC-H9-FAd DNA were co-transfected into CEF cells using Lipofectamine®3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were kept at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 4 days. Plaques lacking GFP expression were identified using the inverted fluorescence microscope and further purified. Using the same strategy, two monovalent recombinant viruses were also generated as controls. The HA expression cassette and the Fiber-2 expression cassette were individually inserted to generate HVT-H9 and HVT-FAd, respectively.

Characterization of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd

Recombinant HVT viruses were inoculated into CEF cells, and plaques were observed using the inverted microscope at 120 hours post inoculation (hpi). For indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), CEF cells were fixed with ethanol-acetone (3:2, v/v) and then incubated with CQ/2022 mouse antiserum (H9-MS, 1:1000 dilution), followed by the FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, 1:400 dilution) to detect the HA protein. To detect Fiber-2 protein, cells were incubated with anti-SD1504 chicken serum (FAdV-CS, 1:1000 dilution), followed by RBITC-conjugated rabbit anti-chicken IgG (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, 1:400 dilution). Finally, plaques were visualized using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

To evaluate the growth kinetics, CEF cells cultured in 6-well plates were infected with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9, HVT-FAd, or parental HVT at 100 plaque-forming units (PFU) per well. Infected cells were harvested at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hpi, subjected to 10-fold serial dilutions, and inoculated onto fresh CEF monolayers in 6-well plates. Viral titers were determined by IFA at 72 hpi.

To further confirm HA and Fiber-2 expression, CEF cells cultured in 6-well plates were infected with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9, HVT-FAd, or parental HVT at 100 PFU per well. CEF cells infected with the CQ/2022 virus and LMH cells infected with the SD1504 virus were used as positive controls. At 72 hpi, infected cells were harvested and lysed, and proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked and incubated with H9-MS or FAdV-CS, followed by incubation with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

To assess the genetic stability of the inserted genes, HVT-H9-FAd was serially passaged in CEF cells for 20 passages. Cell lysates were collected every five passages to evaluate the expression of HA and Fiber-2 proteins by Western blot analysis. In addition, the expression of HA protein and Fiber-2 proteins at 20th passage was further confirmed by IFA.

Serological antibody responses induced by recombinant HVT viruses in SPF chickens

To evaluate serum antibody responses induced by recombinant HVT vaccination, 40 one-day-old SPF chickens were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10). Chickens were subcutaneously immunized with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-FAd, HVT-H9, or parental HVT at a dose of 2,000 PFU per chick at 1 day of age, respectively. Blood samples were collected weekly from all chickens in each group until 5 weeks post vaccination (WPV), and sera were separated for antibody detection. Serum antibody titers specific for H9N2 AIV were determined using hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay. The specific antibodies against Fiber-2 protein were measured using the fowl adenovirus serotype 4 indirect ELISA antibody test kit (Product code: FQE006, Beijing Yisen Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), which is based on recombinant Fiber-2 protein-coated antigen, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Protective efficacy of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd against H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 challenge

To evaluate the protective efficacy of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd against H9N2 AIV, 40 one-day-old SPF chickens were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10). Chickens were subcutaneously immunized with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9, or parental HVT at a dose of 2,000 PFU per chick at 1 day of age, while the control group was inoculated with an equal volume of PBS. At 4 WPV, chickens were challenged intranasally with 10 CID50 of the homologous CQ/2022 strain in a volume of 100 μL, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS via the same route. The challenge dose was selected based on previously established H9N2 AIV infection models in chickens (Chen et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025). Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were collected at 3 and 5 days post challenge (DPC) to assess viral shedding, and viral titers were determined as 50% egg infectious doses (EID50). At 5 DPC, all chickens were humanely euthanized, and trachea and lung tissues were collected for viral detection and histopathological examination. Portions of tracheas and lungs were homogenized in PBS containing antibiotics to a final concentration of 10% (w/v) and titrated by EID50. Samples were serially diluted 10-fold prior to EID50 determination as previously described (Fan et al., 2025). No blind passage amplification was performed prior to virus titration. The detection limit of the EID50 assay was defined as the lowest virus dilution tested (10−1). Viral titers were calculated using the Reed-Muench method. The remaining tissues were fixed in 10% phosphate buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathological changes were examined under a light microscope.

To evaluate the protective efficacy of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd against FAdV-4, 40 one-day-old SPF chickens were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10). Chickens in each group were subcutaneously immunized with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-FAd, or HVT at a dose of 2,000 PFU per chick at 1 day of age, while the control group was inoculated with an equal volume of PBS. At 4 WPV, chickens were challenged intramuscularly with 30 LD50 of the homologous SD1504 strain in a volume of 100 μL, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS via the same route. Mortality was recorded daily. Cloacal swabs were collected from surviving chickens at 3, 5 and 7 DPC, and viral titers were determined as 50% tissue culture infectious doses (TCID50) assay. Samples were serially diluted 10-fold prior to TCID50 determination in LMH cells as previously described (Xie et al., 2021). No blind passage amplification was performed prior to virus titration. The detection limit of the TCID50 assay was defined as the lowest virus dilution tested (10−1). Viral titers were calculated using the Reed-Muench method. Chickens that died during the experiment were immediately necropsied, and gross lesions were recorded, with tissues (heart, liver, and kidney) collected for further analysis. At 7 DPC, surviving chickens were humanely euthanized and necropsied. Gross lesions were recorded, and cloacal swabs and tissues (heart, liver, and kidney) were collected. A portion of the tissue samples were homogenized in PBS containing antibiotics to a final concentration of 10% (w/v) and titrated by TCID50 assay, while the remaining portion was fixed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E for histopathological examination under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc, USA). Antibody response data were analyzed using repeated-measures two-way ANOVA. Viral shedding and viral load data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. And significance levels were defined as follows: * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001; ns, not significant.

Results

Generation and characterization of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd

The HA and Fiber-2 expression cassettes were successfully inserted into the HVT-BAC backbone to generate recombinant HVT-BAC-H9-FAd, as schematically illustrated (Fig. 1A). Recombinant HVT was subsequently rescued following removal of BAC sequences using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Non-GFP-expressing plaques were further purified, indicating successful excision of the BAC sequence. The recombinant viruses, including HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9, and HVT-FAd, formed plaques with morphology and size comparable to those of the parental HVT strain, suggesting that insertion of foreign genes did not affect plaque formation (Fig. 1B). Growth kinetics analysis showed that the replication of HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9, and HVT-FAd were similar to that of the parental HVT at all examined time points, indicating that insertion of HA and Fiber-2 genes did not significantly impair viral replication in vitro (Fig. 1C).

The HA expression cassette was inserted into the UL45/46 region, and the Fiber-2 expression cassette was inserted into the US2/SORF3 region of the HVT-BAC genome using a galK selection system. The BAC sequences were subsequently excised by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing system to generate recombinant HVT-H9-FAd. (B) Plaque morphology of recombinant and parental HVT viruses in CEF cells at 120 hpi (magnification, 100 ×). (C) Growth kinetics of recombinant and parental HVT viruses. Cells were infected with recombinant HVT or parental HVT and collected at 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 hpi, and viral titers were determined by plaque assay.

To verify the expression of the inserted genes, IFA were performed using H9-MS and FAdV-CS antisera. The results showed that HVT-H9-FAd reacted with both H9-MS and FAdV-CS, while HVT-H9 and HVT-FAd reacted only with H9-MS or FAdV-CS, respectively, confirming the co-expression of HA and Fiber-2 proteins in HVT-H9-FAd (Fig. 2A). Consistently, Western blot analysis further confirmed the expression of both HA and Fiber-2 proteins in CEF cells infected with HVT-H9-FAd. Only HA or Fiber-2 protein was detected in CEF cells infected with HVT-H9 or HVT-FAd, respectively, while no target proteins were detected in HVT-infected cells (Fig. 2B). Genetic stability of HVT-H9-FAd was further evaluated by serial passaging in CEF cells for 20 passages. Western blot analysis demonstrated that both HA and Fiber-2 proteins were stably expressed at all examined passages (Fig. 2C). In addition, IFA results showed that all plaques at the 20th passage remained positive for both H9-MS and FAdV-CS (Fig. 2D), indicating stable maintenance and expression of the inserted genes.

Antibody responses in SPF chickens after immunization with HVT-H9-FAd

To evaluate the antibody responses induced by recombinant HVT vaccines, one-day-old SPF chickens were immunized with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9, HVT-FAd or HVT. Sera were collected weekly, and antibodies against H9N2 AIV were measured by HI assay using the CQ/2022 strain, while specific antibodies against Fiber-2 protein were measured by ELISA. The results showed that HI titers induced by HVT-H9-FAd were comparable to those induced by HVT-H9, with no significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). The mean HI titers induced by HVT-H9-FAd reached 4.1log2, 5.4log2, 6.6log2, 7.9log2 and 8.1log2 at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 WPV, respectively, which those induced by HVT-H9 were 4.4log2, 5.5log2, 6.7log2, 8.2log2 and 8.4log2 (Fig. 3A). Similarly, ELISA results showed that the Fiber-2 antibody levels induced by HVT-H9-FAd were comparable to those induced by HVT-FAd, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3B). Collectively, these results demonstrated that HVT-H9-FAd simultaneously induced HI antibody responses against H9N2 AIV comparable to those elicited by HVT-H9, as well as antibody responses against Fiber-2 protein comparable to those induced by HVT-FAd. The antibody levels induced by HVT-H9-FAd increased progressively after vaccination and reached a plateau at approximately 4 WPV.

Protective efficacy of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd against H9N2 AIV

To evaluate the protective efficacy of HVT-H9-FAd against H9N2 AIV, one-day-old SPF chickens were immunized with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-H9 or HVT. At 4 WPV, chickens were intranasally challenged with CQ/2022 strain at a dose of 10 CID50. All chickens immunized with HVT exhibited viral shedding in oropharyngeal swabs at both 3 and 5 DPC, and a proportion of cloacal swabs were also positive. Viral loads were detected in the lungs and tracheas. In contrast, no virus was detected in oropharyngeal swabs, cloacal swabs, tracheas, or lungs from chickens immunized with either HVT-H9 or HVT-H9-FAd, with viral titers below the detection limit (Fig. 4A, 4B, and 4C). Histopathological examination further revealed that non-immunized chickens developed severe respiratory lesions following H9N2 AIV challenge, including necrosis and edema of the tracheal mucosal epithelium, hyperplasia of goblet cells and mucosal glands, and marked infiltration of inflammatory and mononuclear cells. In the lungs, diffuse consolidation, congestion, edema, and extensive interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration were observed. In contrast, no apparent histopathological lesions were observed in the tracheas or lungs of chickens immunized with HVT-H9 or HVT-H9-FAd (Fig. 4D). Collectively, HVT-H9-FAd provided complete protection against H9N2 AIV, comparable to that conferred by HVT-H9.

Protective efficacy of recombinant HVT-H9-FAd against FAdV-4

To evaluate the protective efficacy of HVT-H9-FAd against FAdV-4, one-day-old SPF chickens were immunized with HVT-H9-FAd, HVT-FAd or HVT. At 4 WPV, chickens were intramuscularly challenged with SD1504 strain at a dose of 30 LD50. All chickens immunized with HVT died within 4 DPC (Fig. 5A). Necropsy revealed typical HHS-associated lesions, including hydropericardium, severe hepatitis and renal swelling. In contrast, chickens immunized with HVT-H9-FAd or HVT-FAd showed no clinical signs throughout the observation period and exhibited no obvious gross lesions (Fig. 5D). Histopathological examination further confirmed these findings. Severe pathological changes were observed in the HVT immunized group, including lymphocytic infiltration in the cardiac tissues, hepatocellular degeneration and necrosis accompanied by lymphocytic infiltration within the hepatic sinusoids, and degeneration and necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells and glomeruli. In contrast, no apparent histopathological lesions were observed in chickens immunized with HVT-H9-FAd or HVT-FAd (Fig. 5E). Viral loads in cloacal swabs (3, 5, and 7 DPC), and tissues (heart, liver, and kidney) were further determined. High viral titers were detected in cloacal swabs of surviving chickens in the HVT group at 3 DPC. Moreover, chickens in the HVT group that died during the experiment exhibited high viral loads in the hearts, livers, and kidneys. In contrast, no virus was detected in cloacal swabs or tissues from chickens immunized with HVT-H9-FAd or HVT-FAd at any examined time point, indicating that HVT-H9-FAd and HVT-FAd effectively blocked viral replication and shedding of the SD1504 strain (Fig. 5B and 5C). Collectively, HVT-H9-FAd provided complete protection against lethal FAdV-4 challenge, comparable to the protection conferred by HVT-FAd.

Discussion

The intensification of poultry production has increased the risk of pathogen transmission and co-infection under high-density farming conditions, posing substantial challenges to disease control in poultry production systems. H9N2 AIV has been widely distributed in poultry populations across Asia, the Middle East, and Africa (Fusaro et al., 2024). FAdV-4 are also widely distributed worldwide and have been reported in Asia, Europe, and South Africa (Schachner et al., 2018; Rashid et al., 2024). In China, both H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 are widely prevalent and co-circulate in poultry populations (Chen et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2026), highlighting the need for effective multivalent vaccination strategies. The continuous antigenic evolution of H9N2 AIV necessitates the selection of antigen genes that better match currently circulating strains. Therefore, the HA gene derived from the CQ/2022 strain, belonging to the predominant clade and antigenic group currently circulating in China, was selected in the present study to improve antigenic matching (Dong et al., 2022; Fusaro et al., 2024). For FAdV-4, Fiber-2 has been identified as a major virulence determinant and protective antigen capable of inducing strong immune responses (Schachner et al., 2014; Ruan et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018). A recent study has further demonstrated that Fiber-2-based subunit vaccine can provide complete protection against FAdV-4 challenge, supporting its use as a key antigen (Liu et al., 2024). Collectively, these antigen selections provide a rational basis for the development of the bivalent HVT-vectored vaccine candidate evaluated in this study.

In commercial poultry production, vaccination against H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 is still largely dependent on multivalent inactivated vaccines (Pan et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2025). Although these vaccines are widely used and effective, they require repeated administration and booster immunizations, increasing labor costs and management complexity (Bodman-Harris et al., 2024). In addition, inactivated vaccines mainly induce humoral immune responses, and antibody levels typically decline over time, which may limit the duration of protection. In contrast, HVT-vectored vaccines can be administered via in ovo inoculation or as a day-old chick vaccination, enabling early-life immunization with minimal handling. More importantly, HVT establishes persistent infection in lymphoid tissues, leading to sustained antigen expression and induction of both humoral and cellular immune responses, which may contribute to more durable immune protection following a single vaccination (Wang et al., 2024). In the present study, the recombinant HVT-H9-FAd induced a gradually increasing antibody response, with higher titers observed at 4 WPV. This delayed antibody kinetics is likely associated with the in vivo replication and persistent antigen expression characteristics of HVT-vectored vaccines. Similar antibody kinetics have also been reported in previous studies, which typically show steadily increasing antibody responses (Liu et al., 2019; Litao et al., 2024).

Simultaneous vaccination with multiple monovalent HVT-vectored vaccines may lead to reduced protective efficacy (Dunn et al., 2019). The development of multivalent HVT-vectored vaccines capable of expressing multiple antigens within the single vector is of considerable significance. Several bivalent HVT vaccines have been reported, such as HVT-ND-IBD (van Hulten et al., 2021), HVT-NDV-ILT (Gergen et al., 2019), and HVT-ND-AI (Criado et al., 2023; Ingrao et al., 2024). However, most studies have primarily focused on the construction and protective efficacy, while comparative evaluations between monovalent and bivalent HVT-vectored vaccines remain limited. In the present study, the bivalent HVT-H9-FAd vaccine candidate showed no significant differences compared with the corresponding monovalent HVT-H9 and HVT-FAd in terms of viral replication, genetic stability, antibody response, or protective efficacy. Moreover, HVT-H9-FAd provided complete protection against homologous H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4 challenges. The protective efficacy may be associated with the ability of HVT-vectored vaccines to induce both humoral and cellular immune responses. Previous studies have shown that HVT-vectored vaccines can induce cellular and mucosal immune responses, including T cell responses and mucosal antibodies in respiratory tissues, which may contribute to broader protective immunity (Yang et al., 2025b). These immune responses may have contributed to the control of viral replication and shedding, which is consistent with the absence of detectable infectious virus in swabs and tissues observed in this study. However, cellular and mucosal immune responses were not directly evaluated and warrant further investigation.

However, several limitations of the present study should be considered when evaluating the field application potential of HVT-H9-FAd. First, animal experiments were performed in SPF chickens over a 5-week observation period, which mainly reflects vaccine performance within a short production cycle and does not fully address its efficacy in long-lived poultry such as layers or breeders. In addition, the use of SPF chickens does not account for the potential influence of maternally derived antibodies. Although previous studies have demonstrated the field effectiveness of HVT-vectored vaccines (Chen et al., 2023; Litao et al., 2024), the application of HVT-H9-FAd still requires further validation under commercial production conditions. Collectively, these limitations indicate that the present findings primarily support the application of HVT-H9-FAd in controlled broiler production systems, while further evaluation under field conditions is required to fully define its broader applicability.

In summary, the recombinant bivalent HVT-H9-FAd vaccine candidate exhibited stable genetic characteristics and did not compromise viral replication or immunogenicity compared with monovalent HVT-vectored vaccine candidates. HVT-H9-FAd provided effective protection against homologous H9N2 AIV and FAdV-4. These findings support the feasibility of multivalent HVT-vectored vaccine strategies and provide a basis for simplified vaccination programs targeting co-circulating poultry pathogens.
