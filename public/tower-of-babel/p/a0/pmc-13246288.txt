Cardiovascular Risk of Alcohol Consumption due to Renal Impairment: A 10-Year Prospective Study in a Community-Dwelling Japanese Population
Authors: Aya Hirata, Sei Harada, Miho Iida, Minako Matsumoto, Naoko Miyagawa, Ryota Toki, Shun Edagawa, Takuma Shibuki, Kazuyo Kuwabara, Shun Kohsaka, Yoshikane Izawa, Mitsuaki Sawano, Tsubasa Takizawa, Satoshi Shoji, Masahiro Katsumata, Daisuke Sugiyama, Tomonori Okamura, Toru Takebayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims: The association between alcohol consumption and the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) varies according to the presence of underlying cardiovascular risk factors. Incorporating such risk factors may be important to effectively reduce harmful alcohol use through health guidance. However, whether the risk of alcohol consumption is affected due to renal impairment remains unclear. Methods: A total of 10,583 community-dwelling Japanese adults (mean age 59.4 (SD 10.1) years; 46% men) were followed for 10 years. Alcohol consumption was categorized into five groups for men: never drinkers, former drinkers, light drinkers (＜20 g/day), moderate drinkers (20-39 g/day), and heavy drinkers (≥ 40 g/day), and four groups for women with moderate and heavy combined. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios for incident CVD stratified by the presence or absence of reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (＜60 mL/min/1.73m2) and proteinuria, with adjustment for confounders. Results: Among men with proteinuria, alcohol consumption was associated with a higher risk of atherosclerotic CVD, whereas an inverse association was observed in men without proteinuria [hazard ratio (95% confidence interval): moderate drinkers without proteinuria, 0.58 (0.34-0.97); moderate drinkers with proteinuria, 3.49 (1.15-10.56)]. Moderate to heavy drinking increased the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage irrespective of the renal status. In women, moderate to heavy drinking was associated with an increased CVD risk only when proteinuria was present. In contrast, a reduced eGFR did not clearly affect the association in either sex. Conclusions: The CVD risk associated with alcohol consumption may differ according to the renal status, particularly depending on the presence or absence of proteinuria. Keywords: Alcohol consumption, Cardiovascular disease, Proteinuria, Reduced eGFR, Cohort study

Introduction

Alcohol consumption is often a part of social and cultural norms and is therefore prevalent globally. According to recent reports, nearly half of the world’s population aged 15 years and older consumes alcohol, while alcohol use simultaneously contributes to a major global burden of disease 1 , 2) . Alcohol consumption is associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer, infectious diseases, and other health conditions; therefore, it has been proposed to reduce the harmful use of alcohol worldwide 3) .

The association between the alcohol consumption volume and the risk of subsequent CVD events has been investigated over the last several decades. Moderate to heavy alcohol consumption is associated with an elevated risk of stroke, particularly intracerebral hemorrhage; some studies have reported a negative association between alcohol consumption and the risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) 4 - 7) . Moreover, the impact of alcohol on the risk of CVD could differ depending on the presence or absence of risk factors such as hypertension and dyslipidemia 8 - 10) . For instance, among individuals without dyslipidemia, alcohol consumption was negatively associated with the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), whereas this association was not evident in those with dyslipidemia 10) . However, few studies have evaluated whether the association between alcohol consumption and CVD differs according to the renal impairment status 11) , even though renal impairment, particularly proteinuria, can be caused by alcohol consumption 12 , 13) , and it is also a strong risk factor for CVD 14 , 15) .

Accordingly, to develop more effective strategies to reduce alcohol use, health guidance must reflect individual risk profiles. Current national recommendations, including those in Japan, specify uniform upper limits for alcohol intake, but do not adjust these limits for coexisting risk factors. Therefore, we investigated the association between alcohol consumption and incident CVD in a community-dwelling Japanese population by stratifying our analyses according to the renal impairment status.

Methods

Study Participants

The Tsuruoka Metabolomics Cohort Study (TMCS) is an observational, population-based study conducted in Tsuruoka City, Yamagata Prefecture, Japan. Participants were recruited from individuals who underwent health checkups either through programs provided by Tsuruoka City or by their employers. Further details of the TMCS have been previously reported 16 - 19) . In the present study, 11,002 participants aged 35-74 years, who participated in the baseline survey between fiscal years 2012 and 2014 were followed up until June 30, 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Keio University School of Medicine in Tokyo, Japan (approval number: 20110264), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Of all participants (n = 11,002), individuals with a history of cardiovascular disease (n = 365), those with an eGFR ＜15 mL/min/1.73 m2 (n = 5), who were considered to have renal failure and were excluded to avoid the influence of alcohol consumption habits and CVD risk associated with renal failure 20) , and those with missing data (n = 49) were excluded. The remaining 10,583 participants (4,856 men and 5,727 women) were included in this study.

Baseline Measurement

Detailed measurement methods have been described previously 16 - 19) . The body mass index (BMI) was determined by dividing each participant’s body weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters. Blood pressure was assessed twice while the participant was seated using an automated device (Omron HBP-T105S-N). Blood samples were collected after a 10-hour overnight fast. Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting plasma glucose, and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels were measured using standardized methods. Non-HDL-C levels were derived by subtracting the HDL-C from TC levels. Information on smoking habits, alcohol consumption, medication use, and medical history was obtained using standardized self-administered questionnaires and subsequently confirmed through interviews conducted by trained interviewers. Serum creatinine was measured using an enzymatic method and urine protein was assessed using semi-quantitative screening tests. A semiquantitative urine protein test was performed.

Hypertension was defined as having a systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher, a diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher, or the use of antihypertensive medication 21) . Diabetes was identified by an HbA1c level (NGSP) of at least 6.5%, a fasting plasma glucose concentration of ≥ 126 mg/dL, or the use of glucose-lowering medication 22) . Dyslipidemia was determined based on any of the following criteria: triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, non-HDL cholesterol ≥ 170 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol ＜40 mg/dL, or the use of lipid-lowering medication 23) .

The questionnaire ascertained the participants’ drinking status, classifying them as never drinkers, former drinkers, or current drinkers. For current drinkers, information on the frequency of alcohol consumption during a typical week and the average amount of each alcoholic beverage consumed was obtained. The types and amounts of alcoholic beverages were recorded based on daily consumption as the number of the following: large bottles (633 mL), medium bottles (500 mL), or small bottles (350 mL) of beer; gou (traditional Japanese alcohol units) of sake (180 ml), servings of shochu (180 mL), glasses of wine (110 mL), and a single (35 mL) or double (70 mL) shot of whisky. We calculated the ethanol content in these beverages using standardized alcohol concentrations as follows: 5% for beer, 16% for sake, 20-25% for shochu according to the value reported by each participant, 12% for wine, and 40% for whisky. The ethanol content (g) was obtained by multiplying the beverage volume (mL) by the alcohol concentration (%) and by a conversion factor of 0.8 to account for the specific gravity of ethanol. To estimate the average daily alcohol intake, the number of drinking occasions per week was multiplied by the amount of alcohol typically consumed on each occasion, and the resulting weekly total was divided by seven. The alcohol consumption status was categorized into five groups: never drinkers, former drinkers, light drinkers (＜20 g/day), moderate drinkers (20-39 g/day), and heavy drinkers (≥ 40 g/day). Women were categorized into the following four groups: never drinkers, former drinkers, light drinkers (＜20 g/day), and moderate to heavy drinkers (≥ 20 g/day) because the number of heavy drinkers was very small.

The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was estimated using the following equation for Japanese individuals proposed by a working group of the Japanese Chronic Kidney Disease initiative 24) : eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 194 × serum creatinine−1.094 [Enzymatic method] × age−0.287 × 0.739 (if female). Reduced eGFR was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of ＜60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and proteinuria was defined as a positive result (trace or greater) on a semi-quantitative urine dipstick test 20 , 25) .

Identification of Incident Cardiovascular Events

In the present study, the outcomes were defined as the incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD), including CAD, stroke, cerebral infarction, and intracerebral hemorrhage, and ASCVD, including CAD and atherothrombotic cerebral infarction. Most patients with CVD were transported to four hospitals according to the public emergency transport records in Tsuruoka City. During the follow-up period, researchers visited these hospitals annually to review the medical records of the cohort study participants. Furthermore, with permission from the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare, researchers reviewed cause-of-death records from vital statistics. Thus, information on suspected CVD cases was collected. The final diagnosis of the first incidence of each case was confirmed by a panel of expert physicians, including at least two cardiologists or stroke specialists, based on clinical symptoms, electrocardiograms, coronary angiography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging findings.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as the mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables were reported as numbers and proportions according to groups. The crude mortality rates and hazard ratios (HRs) for the incidence of CVD, CAD, stroke, cerebral infarction, intracerebral hemorrhage, and ASCVD were calculated for men. For women, HRs were calculated only for CVD and stroke, as the number of cause-specific CVD events was too small for a reliable analysis. We estimated the HR and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for former drinkers and current drinkers with never drinkers as the reference after adjusting for potential confounders using Cox proportional hazards models. Furthermore, we performed stratified analyses of the association between alcohol consumption status and CVD outcomes according to the presence or absence of reduced eGFR and proteinuria. Model 1 was adjusted for age. Model 2 was further adjusted for body mass index, smoking status, diabetes, dyslipidemia, proteinuria, and reduced eGFR. Model 3 includes hypertension. Hypertension was included in a separate model because alcohol consumption can increase blood pressure 26) and hypertension may act as a mediator in the pathway between alcohol consumption and CVD. When stratified by proteinuria or reduced eGFR, the corresponding variables were excluded from model covariates. Statistical analyses were conducted using the STATA/SE 18.5 data analysis and statistical software program (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

The baseline characteristics of the men and women according to alcohol consumption status are shown in Tables 1 and 2 , respectively. The characteristics stratified by renal impairment status are presented in Supplemental Tables 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 . Among men, all drinker groups (light, moderate, and heavy) had a slightly higher average age than the never drinkers. The prevalence of dyslipidemia and reduced eGFR was lower among all drinker groups than among never drinkers, whereas the prevalence of hypertension and proteinuria tended to be higher with increasing alcohol consumption. Former drinkers had the highest mean age and highest prevalence of adverse health conditions across all groups. Among women, light drinkers were younger than never drinkers and had a lower prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, reduced eGFR, and proteinuria. Moderate to heavy drinkers were also younger and had a lower prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and reduced eGFR than never drinkers, but the prevalence of proteinuria was similar to that of never drinkers. Former drinkers were slightly younger than never drinkers and had a slightly lower prevalence of adverse health conditions, except for diabetes, which was similarly prevalent. Among former drinkers, 66% of men and 46% of women reported quitting alcohol owing to adverse health conditions.

Among men, current drinkers had a slightly lower HR for CVD than did never drinkers. Clear inverse associations were observed between ASCVD and CAD, with current drinkers showing lower HRs than never drinkers. In contrast, the HRs for incident stroke and cerebral infarction were slightly higher among the current drinkers. Moderate-to-heavy alcohol consumption was associated with an increased HR for intracerebral hemorrhage. In stratified analyses by renal impairment status, current drinkers without proteinuria had lower or similar HRs for CVD, ASCVD, CAD, and cerebral infarction than never drinkers. However, among men with proteinuria, current drinkers consistently had higher HRs for these outcomes. For example, the HR for CVD was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.42-0.93) among heavy drinkers without proteinuria, while it was 2.88 (0.98-8.47) among those with proteinuria. Although some HRs, particularly for intracerebral hemorrhage, could not be estimated due to the absence of events in certain groups, the observed event rates and estimable HRs suggested that moderate to heavy alcohol consumption was generally associated with higher cardiovascular risk than never drinking, regardless of proteinuria status or eGFR levels. Among men with proteinuria, former drinkers tended to have higher HRs for ASCVD. Among women, HRs for CVD and stroke were slightly higher in current drinkers, including both light and moderate to heavy drinkers, than in never drinkers. In stratified analyses by renal impairment status, women with proteinuria who consumed alcohol at moderate-to-heavy levels had higher HRs for both outcomes, while this association was not observed among those without proteinuria. Reduced eGFR did not clearly affect this association in either sex.

Discussion

In the present study, compared to never drinkers, alcohol consumption was associated with an increased risk of incident CVD, CAD, and cerebral infarction among men with proteinuria, whereas an inverse association was observed in men without proteinuria. Additionally, moderate-to-heavy alcohol consumption appeared to be associated with a higher occurrence of intracerebral hemorrhage among men, irrespective of renal impairment status. Among women, alcohol consumption was associated with an increased risk of incident CVD only in the presence of proteinuria. In cases where alcohol consumption was associated with a higher risk, such as among individuals with proteinuria or in cases of intracerebral hemorrhage irrespective of renal function, moderate to heavy drinkers showed relatively higher risks than light drinkers in both sexes, whereas reduced eGFR had no evident impact on the association. In the overall population, without stratification by renal function, the associations between alcohol consumption and CVD outcomes observed in the present study were largely consistent with the findings of previous studies 4 - 7) .

A Mendelian randomization study reported that favorable lifestyle-related factors attenuated the observational benefits of light-to-moderate alcohol consumption 27) . This finding suggests that the apparent protective effects of alcohol observed in conventional studies may be attributable to healthier conditions among light-to-moderate drinkers, indicating that the association between alcohol consumption and ASCVD risk may be modified by lifestyle-related factors. The differential association between alcohol consumption and ASCVD risk according to the proteinuria status, as observed in the present study, supports the findings of this Mendelian randomization study. A meta-analysis of individual-level data suggested that both lower eGFR and elevated urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) are associated with an increased risk of CVD 28) . Notably, cardiovascular risk begins to rise even at very low levels of albuminuria, with increases observed from approximately 10 mg/g of UACR. This risk becomes more pronounced in individuals with microalbuminuria and overt albuminuria. In contrast, the risk associated with eGFR remains relatively stable until the kidney function declines below approximately 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 28) . In the present study, a positive association between alcohol consumption and the risk of ASCVD was more evident among individuals with proteinuria than among those with reduced eGFR, possibly reflecting these background factors.

Proteinuria is considered to reflect increased glomerular permeability, which is thought to result from systemic endothelial dysfunction 29 , 30) and is strongly associated with an increased risk of CVD and ASCVD 14 , 15) . In individuals with proteinuria, it is possible that subclinical atherosclerosis is already progressing, and in such cases, alcohol-related increases in blood pressure and decreased heart rate variability may further elevate the risk of CVD and ASCVD events 31 - 33) . In contrast, among individuals without proteinuria, the anti-atherosclerotic effects of alcohol may play a beneficial role, particularly for ASCVD events. Previous studies have reported that alcohol consumption, particularly light to moderate drinking, is associated with a lower risk of both coronary and cerebral infarction events 4 - 7) . The proposed mechanisms underlying this association include increased HDL-C levels, which exert anti-atherosclerotic effects, improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced inflammatory responses, which may help regulate platelet aggregation and maintain arterial wall integrity 34 - 36) . That is, individuals with proteinuria may have greater vascular vulnerability, making them more susceptible to the adverse hemodynamic and autonomic effects of alcohol. Proteinuria may also reflect a more advanced stage of vascular and endothelial injury than a reduced eGFR, which could partially explain the differential associations observed across renal impairment categories. However, it should be noted that alcohol consumption itself is a risk factor for the future development of proteinuria 12 , 13) , which in turn may increase the further long-term risk of CVD and ASCVD, even among those without proteinuria at baseline. Therefore, even in the absence of renal impairment at the time of assessment, the continuous monitoring of indicators of renal impairment may be warranted among alcohol drinkers.

As mentioned earlier, the presence or absence of a reduced eGFR did not clearly modify the association between alcohol consumption and CVD incidence in the present study. Similarly, a previous analysis of three US cohorts reported no substantial modification of the inverse association between alcohol consumption and coronary events across eGFR levels, although a slightly greater adverse effect on cerebral infarction risk was observed among individuals with lower eGFR 11) . This discrepancy may be partly explained by differences in the distribution of stroke subtypes between the United States and Japan, where lacunar infarction is more prevalent and atherothrombotic stroke is less common 37 , 38) . Fundamentally, the relationship between alcohol consumption and eGFR remains controversial. Some studies have reported a higher risk of eGFR decline among drinkers compared to non-drinkers, while others have found no association or even a reduced risk 12 , 13 , 39) . These inconsistencies may reflect the limitations of eGFR as a renal function marker, such as the dependence on muscle mass and the overestimation of true renal function inherent in serum creatinine-based estimation 40 , 41) , as well as the potential influence of glomerular hyperfiltration in the early stages of renal impairment 42 , 43) . Furthermore, as previously discussed, eGFR has been shown to be associated with an increased risk of CVD only at lower levels 28) ; thus, in populations with a relatively preserved kidney function, such as the general population, this risk may not be readily apparent. Taken together, these factors make it difficult to determine the precise impact of eGFR based on serum creatinine levels. In contrast, proteinuria may reflect a more harmful systemic condition, such as widespread endothelial dysfunction, which is more susceptible to the adverse effects of alcohol consumption than a reduced eGFR.

This study is associated with several limitations. First, alcohol consumption was assessed only at baseline and changes in drinking habits during the follow-up period were not considered. Second, as this study was conducted in Japan, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. However, the high participation and follow-up rates suggest that the study population was representative of the community 16) . Third, the number of drinkers among women and their alcohol consumption levels were relatively low, limiting their ability to conduct detailed analyses in this subgroup. Fourth, cardiovascular mortality and incident heart failure could not be evaluated in this study, although alcohol consumption may affect these outcomes differently than the associations observed in the main findings of the present study. Cardiovascular mortality could not be examined because the number of identified events was limited, and it was difficult to reliably ascertain incident heart failure in community-based cohort studies. Future research with a more complete event identification is required. Lastly, the number of participants with proteinuria was limited, which reduced the statistical power of this group. Consequently, risk estimates for alcohol consumption among individuals with proteinuria may be less stable. Although our stratified results suggest a possible modification of the relationship between alcohol consumption and incident CVD according to the proteinuria status, these estimates should be interpreted with caution. Larger studies with more proteinuria cases are required to confirm these associations.

In conclusion, a Japanese community-based observational cohort study indicated that the impact of alcohol consumption on CVD events may differ according to the disease subtype and renal impairment status. Among men with proteinuria, alcohol consumption was associated with a higher risk of ASCVD, suggesting the opposite trend in those without proteinuria. Moderate to heavy alcohol consumption appeared to be associated with an increased occurrence of intracerebral hemorrhage regardless of renal impairment status. Among women, moderate-to-heavy alcohol intake was associated with an increased risk of CVD in those with proteinuria, whereas no clear association was observed in those without proteinuria. Additionally, among individuals with proteinuria or in cases of intracerebral hemorrhage, regardless of renal impairment, our findings suggest that higher levels of alcohol consumption, particularly moderate to heavy drinking, may be an important determinant of cardiovascular risk. Given previous findings that alcohol consumption may be a risk factor for incident proteinuria, recommending moderation or abstinence from alcohol may be appropriate in the long run even in the absence of any apparent renal impairment.
