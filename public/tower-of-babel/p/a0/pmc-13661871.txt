Health impacts of the Aral Sea disaster: Current state, research gaps, and mitigation perspectives in the North Aral Sea region
Authors: Piotr Rzymski, Włodzimierz Marszelewski, Michał Rybak, Piotr Klimaszyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe North Aral Sea (Kazakhstan) has been the focus of restoration efforts to counter the desiccation of one of the world’s largest lakes. As restoration ultimately aims to improve human well-being, monitoring population health is essential. This review synthesizes health issues reported in the region in 2015–2025. Findings indicate multifactorial adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular, endocrine, reproductive, and nutritional disturbances linked to historical contamination, dietary deficiencies, psychosocial stress, and limited healthcare access. Although partial restoration has reduced some environmental hazards, residual risks persist, particularly in southern desiccated zones. Integrated preventive actions are recommended, including environmental management, strengthened healthcare, improved nutrition, education, and infrastructure. Any future industrial expansion, including resource extraction, must be paired with sustainable community engagement. The North Aral case provides broader lessons for environmentally stressed dryland basins, highlighting how coupled ecological and social disruptions can compound health risks across generations and underscoring the need for equity-focused recovery strategies.Graphical abstract Keywords: Ecological disaster, Environmental medicine, Kazakhstan, Public health, Syr-Darya

Introduction

The Aral Sea, once the fourth-largest inland water body on Earth, has experienced one of the most severe anthropogenic ecological disasters of the twentieth century. Although technically a lake, its immense size and ecological role earned it the designation of a “sea”. Until the 1960s, it covered an area of over 67 thousand km2, supporting diverse aquatic ecosystems and sustaining the livelihoods of hundreds of thousands of people in the Central Asian republics of the former Soviet Union, now Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, through fisheries, agriculture, and related industries (Aladin and Potts 1992; Krivonogov et al. 2014). The collapse of this system began in the mid-twentieth century, when large-scale irrigation schemes, primarily for highly water-demanding crops—cotton and rice cultivation, diverted the inflow of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers, feeding the Aral from the south and north, respectively (Krivonogov et al. 2014; Singh et al. 2018). These interventions, implemented under Soviet central planning, dramatically reduced river discharge into the Aral Sea basin. Within decades, evaporation greatly exceeded inflow, leading to the rapid desiccation of the lake, fragmentation into separate basins, and a cascade of ecological and socioeconomic consequences (Glantz 2009; Micklin 2014a; Aladin et al. 2019; Kurbanova et al. 2024).

By the 1980s, the surface area of the Aral Sea had already shrunk by almost half, and to one-third of its original volume. Salinity levels increased several-fold, driving the extinction of most freshwater fish species and the collapse of local fisheries that once yielded tens of thousands of tons of fish annually (Ermakhanov et al. 2012; Izhitskiy et al. 2016; Aladin et al. 2019). Beyond the ecological consequences, the disaster deeply undermined economic stability, public health, and the region’s cultural fabric, turning once-thriving fishing towns into marginal settlements facing unemployment, poverty, and depopulation (Spoor 1998; Glantz 2009; Crighton et al. 2011; Kurbanova et al. 2024). The degradation of aquatic ecosystems was paralleled by the transformation of vast former lakebed into the Aralkum Desert, a new source of saline and toxic dust transported across Central Asia by prevailing winds (Bosch et al. 2012; Šikoparija 2020; Xi et al. 2025). Emissions from Aralkum nearly doubled from 14.3 Tg/yr in the 1980s to 27.1 Tg/yr in the 2010s, increasing Central Asia’s total dust by about 7%. More than half of the transport was mainly driven by westerlies, affecting regions to the east. However, the strongest springtime emissions occur under heavy cloud cover (90% in spring; two-thirds overall), which hampers satellite detection and risk assessment (Banks et al. 2022). Nevertheless, this dust, enriched with agrochemical residues from decades of intensive farming, became a chronic health hazard, impacting local communities (Bennion et al. 2007; Törnqvist et al. 2011).

In Kazakhstan, the northern basin of the Aral Sea, known as the North Aral (or Small Aral Sea), has become hydrologically isolated from the southern ones (Berdimbetov et al. 2024; Kirillin et al. 2025). While the South Aral has deteriorated mainly due to the near absence of inflow from the Amu Darya (Izhitskiy et al. 2016; Aladin et al. 2019), the North Aral has been the focus of targeted restoration efforts. The most notable intervention was the construction of the Kokaral Dam, completed in 2005 with the economic support of the World Bank. It was designed to prevent water outflow from the North Aral Sea into the southern basins (Micklin 2014a, b). This engineering effort allowed the regulation of water levels, reduction of salinity, and partial recovery of aquatic ecosystems in the northern basin. Subsequent monitoring documented improved fish stocks, re-emergence of certain species, and modest revival of local fisheries, providing new economic opportunities for communities that had previously experienced decades of decline (Aladin et al. 2019; Klimaszyk et al. 2022; Plotnikov et al. 2023). Thus, the dam represents a rare example of ecological recovery in the context of a global environmental disaster, and it has rekindled hope for restoring at least part of the Aral Sea ecosystem.

Despite these successes, major uncertainties remain regarding the broader implications of such restoration efforts for human health and well-being. Communities living along the shores of the North Aral Sea may continue to face a unique combination of challenges, including exposure to residual contaminated dust, limited economic resilience, and the health impacts of long-term social and environmental disruption. At the same time, ongoing ecological improvements may be associated with health benefits, such as improved access to locally caught fish, reduced environmental stressors, and partial mitigation of dust exposure. However, reviews on current health outcomes and challenges in this area are absent.

Therefore, this review aimed to synthesize available evidence on the current health issues affecting populations living in the region of the North Aral Sea in Kazakhstan. To this end, the studies published within the last decade (2015–2025) were summarized, discussed, and critically appraised. By highlighting what is known and where critical knowledge gaps persist, we seek to provide a picture of the ongoing health burden and identify research priorities that could support more effective public health interventions. Given the global relevance of the Aral Sea disaster as a cautionary tale of large-scale environmental mismanagement, such insights are crucial not only for improving the quality of life of affected communities but also for informing broader discussions on the long-term health impacts of ecological crises and the potential benefits of targeted restoration efforts.

Characteristics of the North Aral Sea region

The North Aral Sea is located in the Aral District (encompassing 68.4 thousand km2), a part of the Kyzylorda Province in Kazakhstan (Fig. 1). According to 2025’s statistical data, its total population amounted to 80 287 people, including 18 310 young people aged 14–29 (23%) (AAD 2025). The reported unemployment rate was 5%, slightly above the country’s average of 4.6% (AAD 2025). Aral town, with a current population of over 30 000, serves as the district’s administrative and economic center. The region experiences a sharply continental climate, with hot, arid summers (mean July temperatures around 26–28 °C) and cold winters often below − 15 °C. Annual precipitation rarely exceeds 150 mm, which, together with intense evaporation, limits vegetation recovery. These climatic conditions strongly influence air quality, soil salinization, and agricultural productivity (ESMF 2020). Between 1960 and 2016, climate warming led to an increase in average temperatures and a slight increase in annual precipitation. However, the increase in precipitation is not sufficient to compensate for water losses due to evaporation (Massakbayeva et al. 2019).

The current state of the North Aral Sea reflects the interplay among freshwater inflow from the Syr Darya, evaporative water loss, and controlled outflow through the dam—three counteracting processes that together determine its hydrological and ecological equilibrium. Despite substantial seasonal and interannual fluctuations in water level, primarily driven by variability in freshwater inflow and evaporation, the North Aral Sea currently maintains a relatively stable surface area of approximately 3400 km2 and a total water volume of about 27.5 km3 (Kirillin et al. 2025). The mean water level is ~ 42 m a.s.l. The average depth of the North Aral Sea reaches 8.5 m, while the deepest depressions, exceeding 15 m, occur in Shevchenko Bay and the main basin. In contrast, Bolshoy Sarysheganak Bay remains very shallow, with an average depth of around 1 m (Micklin 2016). After the Kokaral Dam’s construction, salinity declined from about 30 g/L to 6–8 g/L. At present, a distinct horizontal salinity gradient is observed, ranging from approximately 3.2 g/L near the mouth of the Syr Darya River to about 6 g/L in the main basin and exceeding 10 g/L in the Butakov and Shevchenko bays, which are located farther from river inflows. During periods of low freshwater discharge from the Syr Darya, salinity in the North Aral Sea can increase above 12 g/L (Kulikov et al. 2024). Sulfates, chlorides, and sodium ions dominate the ionic composition of the North Aral Sea water (Klimaszyk et al. 2022; Andrulionis et al. 2025).

Although the Syr Darya River constitutes a continuous source of freshwater, it simultaneously transports biogenic elements and diverse anthropogenic pollutants, including nutrients and trace contaminants of agricultural and industrial origin (Amirgaliyev et al. 2023; Satybaldiyev et al. 2023). The highest concentrations of pollutants are recorded in the direct Syr Darya inflow zone (Rzymski et al. 2019; Klimaszyk et al. 2022). Spatial heterogeneity in chemical conditions has led to distinct planktonic assemblages within the reservoir. In the most saline bays, halophilic plankton species prevail (Aladin et al. 2019; Klimaszyk et al. 2022).

In the past, the increase in salinity resulted in the collapse of freshwater biocenoses, causing the extinction of most native aquatic flora and fauna. Consequently, once central to the regional economy, fishery activities declined from over 34 000 t/yr in 1961 to less than 3000 t/yr by 1980 and almost ceased entirely (Ermakhanov et al. 2012). The euryhaline flounder (Platichthys flesus L.) was introduced to revive the local fishery, quickly becoming the foundation of a renewed fishing industry along the North Aral Sea coast (Aladin et al. 2019). In the record year 2004, more than 1200 t of flounder were caught (Ermakhanov et al. 2012). The substantial reduction in salinity observed since the twenty-first century, coupled with the formation of a freshened zone near the Syr Darya delta, has facilitated the natural recolonization of numerous freshwater species associated with the river.

Currently, the ichthyofauna of the North Aral Sea comprises 17 species. The most commercially important taxa include common carp (Cyprinus carpio L.), zander (Sander lucioperca L.), silver crucian carp (Carassius auratus Bloch, 1782), bream (Abramis brama L.), and asp (Aspius aspius L.). The total annual catch varies between 6000 and 20 000 t, representing approximately 17% of Kazakhstan’s overall capture fisheries production (Berdiakhmetkyzy et al. 2021; Kulikov et al. 2024).

Health status of population in the North Aral Sea region: An updated review

Classically, the major health threat that has been associated with the ecological disaster of the Aral Sea was exposure to dust and salt of the former lakebed, with particles laced with organochlorine pesticides and toxic elements such as mercury and arsenic (Jensen et al. 1997; O’Hara et al. 2000; Opp et al. 2017, 2024). Such exposures are known to elevate the risk of respiratory and cardiovascular events, cancer, and reproductive toxicity. Indeed, an increased frequency of these health issues has been reported in the past for populations living near the Aral Sea (Zetterström 1999; Ataniyazova et al. 2001; Van Der Meer et al. 2002; Wiggs et al. 2003; Crighton et al. 2011; Mamyrbayev et al. 2016). However, recent analyses indicate that the Aral District situation has partially stabilized. The comprehensive assessment by Khaibullina et al. (2022), covering the 2005–2019 period, demonstrated declining mortality rates (from 7.20 in 2009 to 5.68 in 2019 per 1000 persons), a stable birth rate (~ 26 per 1000), and overall population growth in the Aral District (Khaibullina et al. 2022). Importantly, while the district remains officially recognized as an “environmental disaster zone” under Kazakhstan’s law, the authors reported a gradual improvement in public health indicators, supported by infrastructure development and expanded access to artesian drinking water (Khaibullina et al. 2022).

The results of recent studies also challenge the assumption that sand and dust storms remain a permanent health hazard in the North Aral Sea region. Although the research for the 1971–2010 period has shown that in the Aral District of the Kyzylorda Province, there were 55–60 days with dust storms annually (Issanova et al. 2015), this district covers a large area south of the Kokaral Dam, with the desiccated Aral lakebed. Therefore, it would be reasonable to assume that these dust storms did not occur as frequently in the area of the North Aral Sea. In line with this, the spatial analysis of dust emissions and dispersion modeling has shown that the North Aral Sea, along with the delta of the Syr Darya river, is in fact the least affected by sand/dust emissions in the Aral Sea area (Chen et al. 2022). This can be attributed to the presence of wetlands in the area of the Syr Darya inflow and a systematic increase in vegetation surrounding the North Aral Sea following the construction of Kokaral Dam (Adilov et al. 2025). Afforestation programs, particularly plantations of the drought-resistant saxaul tree (Haloxylon spp.) native to the deserts of Central Asia, are also likely to play an important role in stabilizing dust on the former lakebed and reducing aerosolized salt exposure. As declared, these plantations should cover 11 000 km2 by 2025, approximately 40% of Kazakhstan’s portion of the desiccated lakebed (Ualikhanova 2024).

Moreover, the biomonitoring study conducted at the beginning of the 2000s has already demonstrated that children living in Aral town do not exhibit elevated levels of mercury, arsenic, organohalogen compounds, dichlorodiphenyldichloroethylene (DDE), and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) in urine and blood (Erdinger et al. 2004), further suggesting that the direct exposure to contaminated dust and salt aerosols has substantially decreased over the past decades. This improvement likely reflects the combined effect of several environmental and infrastructural changes: the partial restoration of the North Aral Sea following the construction of the Kokaral Dam (Fig. 1A), which has reduced the extent of exposed lakebed; the stabilization of soils by the resurgence of vegetation and wetlands in the Syr Darya delta; and the decline in the use and environmental circulation of legacy pesticides (Amirgaliyev et al. 2023). Over the years, much of the easily windborne salt has likely been removed from the former lakebed along with a portion of surface-bound contaminants (Liu et al. 2025), while some persistent pesticide residues have undergone gradual degradation under solar radiation and microbial activity. The monitoring of air quality through Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service indicated that median seasonal levels of particulate matter (PM10) in the Aral District in the 2018–2022 period varied from 11.1–14.0 μg/m3 in winter to 20.1–23.0 μg/m3 in summer (Bočkova et al. 2023). Winter PM10 levels are below, or close to, the WHO annual guideline value of 15 µg/m3, whereas summer levels exceed this threshold but remain well below the WHO 24-h guideline of 45 µg/m3 (WHO 2021). This pattern suggests moderate seasonal increases in particulate matter during summer, without evidence of sustained or frequent acute PM10 pollution events. Moreover, these levels are substantially below winter PM10 levels noted in various European cities (Volná et al. 2021). The main reason for the lower PM10 levels during the winter season is the sparse and scattered human population, and also the region’s transition to natural gas heating, which produces significantly less particulate matter compared to traditional solid fuels. Furthermore, spatial analysis clearly shows that during summer, the PM10 levels are higher in the southern part of the district compared to the northern part (20.1–23.0 vs 23.1–26.0 μg/m3), where the North Aral Sea is situated (Bočkova et al. 2023). This pattern again implies that the proximity of the North Aral Sea and the revitalized wetlands of the Syr Darya delta continues to mitigate dust generation and improve local air quality compared to the more arid southern areas of the Aral District.

Consequently, while the northern Aral region continues to face socioeconomic and health challenges related to its arid climate and limited health infrastructure, the once-dominant pathway of chronic exposure to toxic dust appears to have been significantly mitigated. Nevertheless, episodic dust storms originating from the southern, desiccated part of the former Aral basin may still sporadically affect the area, particularly under strong south-westerly winds (Chen et al. 2022), highlighting the need for ongoing environmental and health surveillance to ensure that these improvements are sustained. Such dust storms are known to have multifaceted impacts, including health and economic consequences (Opp et al. 2021). Nevertheless, it remains important to distinguish current health impacts near the North Aral Sea from those in other parts of the former Aral Sea to avoid overestimation.

Despite the apparent reduction in environmental exposure to contaminated dust and aerosols, recent biomedical data indicate that specific health abnormalities persist among the inhabitants of the North Aral Sea region. This discrepancy suggests that attributing current health effects solely to dust-related pollution is no longer justified. Instead, these outcomes may arise from a complex interplay of other factors, including limited access to healthcare, psychosocial stress, or dietary insufficiencies. Indeed, a survey conducted among inhabitants of Aral town revealed that while the majority (75%) frequently consume non-fish meat and meat products, fewer than 40% reported regular consumption of fruits and vegetables (Atshabarova 2016). This low intake of plant-based foods may constitute a nutritional concern, which may contribute to health-related issues in the region. A reassessment based on contemporary data is required to better understand the present determinants of health in this population.

Current health status of the North Aral population: Evidence from 2015 to 2025 studies

To characterize the current health status of populations in the North Aral Sea region, we have reviewed peer-reviewed original research papers addressing the Aral District of the Kyzylorda Province. A semi-quantitative literature review approach was employed by combining Boolean database searches (Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus) with subsequent qualitative screening and analysis of the evidence. The Boolean searches were conducted using a combination of keywords related to the Aral Sea and human health. To provide an updated overview reflecting the present state of human health, we included studies published within the last decade, i.e., between 2015 and 2025. The studies concerning other districts of the Kyzylorda Province or neighboring areas were excluded because, although they often refer to the “Aral Sea region,” they are geographically distant from the North Aral Sea and represent distinct ecological and exposure contexts. Including them would confound the interpretation of health outcomes specific to the population living near the North Aral Sea and its immediate ecological and socio-environmental setting. Overall, 37 references published in English and Russian were identified, among which ten focused on direct health outcomes and included specific population studies or risk factors with a clear link to human well-being in the region. The remaining 27 references included reviews and studies on a wide range of topics, including ecology, geography, climate, dust transport, water chemistry, fisheries, and policy, that are interrelated with the health situation in the region. These were also incorporated into our synthesis. Additionally, official data from governmental sources, including information from the Akimat of the Aral District in Kazakhstan, were used to complement the analysis.

The analysis revealed that several health alterations persist among residents of the North Aral Sea region. According to one study, 22% of residents of the Aral District reported chronic illnesses, predominantly gastrointestinal, blood, or upper respiratory tract diseases, confirming the continuing, albeit reduced, burden of environmentally influenced morbidity (Khaibullina et al. 2022). Importantly, recent research also indicates a pronounced clustering of cardiovascular risk among adults living in Aral town, reflecting the long-term impact of ecological degradation in the area. The study examining the risk of cardiovascular diseases found that higher odds of disease were observed in parallel with worsening environmental conditions, peaking in the ecological disaster zone, particularly in Aral town, where the odds ratio (OR) was 3.8 (95% confidence interval (CI) 3.0–7.2) compared to the control population in Atasu, located 775 km away (Otarbaeva et al. 2020). Specifically, the odds of developing atherosclerosis (OR = 2.9, 95%CI 1.8–5.0), coronary heart disease associated with atherosclerosis (OR = 23.2, 95%CI 7.5–63.5), arterial hypertension associated with atherosclerosis (AH; OR = 9.6, 95%CI 3.4–23.5) and cardiomyopathy associated with atherosclerosis (OR = 4.1, 95%CI 1.4–10.4) were markedly elevated among residents of Aral town when compared with control inhabitants of Atasu (Otarbaeva et al. 2020). Overall, these findings indicate a pronounced spatial clustering of cardiovascular disease odds in Aral town that is consistent with long-term residence in an environmentally degraded setting. However, given the observational nature of the study, these associations cannot be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship between environmental conditions and cardiovascular disease outcomes.

Examining biochemical parameters in plasma collected from the population living near the North Aral Sea also revealed selected alterations. Among male inhabitants of the Aral town (aged 18–69 years), 61% of studied individuals had increased levels of total protein, gamma-glutamyl transferase (41%), total cholesterol (38%), triglycerides (34%), and alanine aminotransferase (27%), whereas glucose concentration was reduced (28%) (Sabirov et al. 2020). In women, the 8-oxo-2’-deoxyguanosine level, a marker of oxidative DNA damage, was elevated, particularly in those aged 30–39 years (Jangildinova et al. 2019).

Studies on reproductive health in the North Aral Sea ecological disaster zone of Kazakhstan reveal disruptions in both men and women (Kultanov et al. 2015; Turdybekova et al. 2015). Among men from Aral town and surrounding villages, persistent abnormalities in sperm quality were observed, including reduced sperm count, high prevalence of morphologically deformed sperm (notably head and body defects), and altered molecular markers such as degradation of nucleic acids and imbalances in histone fractions, all indicating impaired spermatogenesis (Kultanov et al. 2015). In turn, women (n = 1406, aged 18–49) from the same region showed a high prevalence of endocrine-gynecological disorders: late menarche (> 16 years in 39% of cases), a trend toward earlier menopause (including premature cases before 40 years), widespread inflammatory genital diseases (34%), increased rates of infertility (particularly secondary infertility), and significant perinatal losses, with nearly one in four reporting repeated spontaneous pregnancy terminations (Turdybekova et al. 2015).

Further supporting evidence of endocrine disturbances in women comes from a large-scale cross-sectional study conducted between 2014 and 2016 and involving 2205 women of reproductive age (18–49 years) living for ≥ 5 years in the North Aral Sea region (Serikbayeva et al. 2020). A considerable prevalence of thyroid dysfunction was revealed, particularly among women residing in the ecological catastrophe zone, which includes Aral town. In this group, diffuse goiter was diagnosed in about 12% of participants, and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies were elevated in 32%, indicating a high frequency of autoimmune thyroid involvement. Median concentrations of thyroid-stimulating hormone often exceeded the upper reference limit (3.4 μIU/mL), while free T4 values remained within or slightly below the lower end of the normal range (10–23.2 pmol/L), suggesting a tendency toward reduced thyroid activity. Manifest hypothyroidism (overt hypothyroidism) was detected in about 2–3% of women, and subclinical forms were several times more frequent (Serikbayeva et al. 2020). These findings point to thyroid underactivity rather than hyperfunction, consistent with chronic autoimmune thyroiditis or environmentally mediated thyroid suppression. Possible contributing factors include residual or localized iodine deficiency, chronic exposure to goitrogenic pollutants, and nutritional imbalances such as selenium or iron deficiency (Zimmermann and Boelaert 2015; Khatiwada et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2023). These findings imply that endocrine dysregulation among women in the Aral region extends beyond reproductive hormone imbalance and includes thyroid function disturbances, which may contribute to the observed reproductive and metabolic outcomes.

Despite inhabiting the ecological disaster zone, residents claim to have largely adapted to regional conditions. In a survey of 15 settlements in the Aral District, over 80% of respondents reported satisfaction with their health and drinking water quality, which is currently mainly supplied from deep artesian wells such as Sarybulak (Khaibullina et al. 2022).

In summary, the data published between 2015 and 2025 indicate that health impairments in the North Aral Sea region, while less likely to be driven by ongoing exposure to toxic dust, remain multifactorial. They may reflect the cumulative biological effects of past contamination, persistent nutritional deficiencies, psychosocial stress associated with economic hardship, and limited access to preventive healthcare. Persistent endocrine alterations, such as hypothyroidism and reproductive disorders, point to the need for integrated public health interventions focusing on screening, nutritional support, and environmental health monitoring.

Knowledge gaps and future research prospects

Despite considerable progress in environmental restoration and public health infrastructure, the contemporary health profile of populations inhabiting the North Aral Sea region remains incompletely characterized. Several knowledge gaps continue to limit our understanding of the determinants and magnitude of health risks in the Aral District.

First, contemporary epidemiological data are both scarce and fragmented. Most available studies rely on relatively small and/or convenience samples, often without control populations from unaffected areas. The absence of longitudinal designs makes it difficult to distinguish between lingering effects of historical exposures and emerging influences such as nutrition, psychosocial stress, or economic hardship. Similarly, publicly accessible regionally disaggregated health registries are lacking, limiting the ability to track temporal trends in chronic disease prevalence. For example, national data indicate that the Kyzylorda Province experienced an exceptionally high increase in cancer mortality from 2014 to 2022 compared to other regions of Kazakhstan (Akhmedullin et al. 2024). However, whether this trend extends specifically in the Aral District is unknown. Further research is needed to disentangle the potential contribution of the ecological disaster zone from other regional or lifestyle-related factors. Comparative studies with unaffected neighboring districts would help clarify local versus regional drivers of disease and strengthen causal identification.

Second, exposure pathways have not been comprehensively reassessed since the early 2000s. Although earlier biomonitoring in children demonstrated reduced levels of toxic metals and organochlorines (Erdinger et al. 2004), up-to-date analyses are largely unavailable. The persistence, redistribution, or secondary release of contaminants from soils, sediments, and groundwater remains uncertain, as do the potential cumulative effects of mixed low-dose exposures. However, one should note that toxicological assays conducted in 2002–2003 found no increased cyto- or genotoxic potential of the water samples collected in the Aral town area – indicating that the pollution problem may be lower than previously assumed (Bosch et al. 2007). Future research should integrate environmental sampling with biomarker-based health assessments using harmonized protocols for temporal comparisons.

Third, the nutritional and endocrine dimensions of health remain poorly characterized. While evidence indicates widespread thyroid dysfunction, reproductive disorders, and low consumption of fruits and vegetables (Turdybekova et al. 2015; Atshabarova 2016; Serikbayeva et al. 2020), underlying micronutrient deficiencies, including iodine, selenium, and iron, have not been quantified at a representative scale. Dietary surveys are limited in scope, leaving open questions about current nutrition patterns, food security, and their interaction with endocrine and metabolic health. Comprehensive nutritional assessments are required to fully define intake gaps and to develop targeted educational campaigns.

Fourth, cardiovascular and other chronic disease risks require further investigation. Although existing studies document pronounced clustering of cardiovascular risk among adults in Aral town, including elevated odds of atherosclerosis, coronary heart disease, and arterial hypertension (Otarbaeva et al. 2020), the relative contributions of historical environmental exposures, current lifestyle factors, and healthcare access remain unclear. Similar uncertainties exist for oxidative stress markers, metabolic disturbances, and other non-communicable conditions, highlighting the need for integrative assessments that consider both biological and social determinants. Moreover, while chronic non-communicable diseases remain the dominant burden in the region, the prevalence of infectious diseases such as hepatitis A, tuberculosis, and others remains underexplored.

Fifth, psychosocial factors and adaptive capacity remain largely unexplored. While self-reported satisfaction levels suggest resilience among local residents (Khaibullina et al. 2022), the long-term impacts of chronic stress, economic hardship, and environmental stigma on mental and physical health are poorly documented. Evidence from other environmental disasters demonstrates that such events can produce enduring psychosocial consequences. These include anxiety, depression, and loss of identity to social fragmentation and mistrust of institutions, which may persist long after the physical environment begins to recover (Neria et al. 2008; Edelstein 2018). Similar dynamics have been observed in other Kazakhstani disaster zones, such as the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site. There, communities living amidst radioactive contamination have developed a distinctive “mutant” subjectivity—an understanding of themselves as biologically adapted, but not necessarily healthy (Stawkowski 2016). This belief in bodily adaptation functions as both a coping mechanism and a means of reclaiming dignity in the face of uncertainty. However, evidence simultaneously indicates persistent physical ailments and elevated disease burdens. In the case of the Aral Sea region, these psychosocial stressors may be exacerbated by ongoing economic marginalization, intergenerational trauma, and the perception of living in a “sacrifice zone”. Therefore, although residents of the Aral area may self-report satisfaction with the conditions of their lives, such declarations may reflect resilience and cultural adaptation rather than the absence of psychosocial strain or health impacts. The coexistence of perceived well-being with chronic exposure-related stress or illness underscores the complexity of human responses to long-term environmental degradation. Accordingly, multi-disciplinary studies combining psychological, sociological, and biomedical perspectives are essential to capture the full scope of well-being in the Aral District.

Finally, integrative, population-based surveillance is urgently needed. Linking environmental monitoring, clinical indicators, dietary and psychosocial data, and social determinants of health would enable evidence-based public health planning and targeted interventions, particularly in rural areas with limited healthcare infrastructure. Establishing local biobanks and longitudinal health databases could serve as a cornerstone for this effort, ensuring that both ongoing recovery processes and residual risks are captured over time.

In summary, although environmental stabilization and partial normalization of living conditions have been achieved in the North Aral Sea region, contemporary health outcomes are still shaped by a complex interplay of historical exposures, nutritional insufficiencies, psychosocial stress, and healthcare limitations. Addressing these knowledge gaps will require coordinated, interdisciplinary, and locally embedded research that integrates environmental, biological, and social perspectives to inform effective public health interventions.

Required mitigation measures

Caring about health and well-being implies acknowledging that environmental degradation and human suffering are inseparably linked. Without addressing the health dimension, solutions to the Aral Sea problem will remain incomplete, because the ultimate purpose of ecological restoration and sustainable management should be to safeguard human lives, dignity, and resilience.

Further dust mitigation

Although environmental stabilization measures, including the partial restoration of the North Aral Sea and the establishment of saxaul plantations, have significantly reduced dust exposure, episodic dust storms originating from the southern desiccated lakebed may still affect communities, particularly under strong south-westerly winds (Chen et al. 2022). Continued dust mitigation is therefore essential. This involves expanding vegetation cover to stabilize exposed soils and prevent aerosolized salt from reaching settlements (Fig. 2D). Wetlands in the Syr Darya River delta act as natural buffers and should be prioritized for further restoration and maintenance. Saxaul plantations remain the cornerstone of phyto-reclamation efforts in the Northern Aral region. However, their long-term effectiveness varies widely depending on soil type, salinity, hydrology, and human activity. Recent monitoring in Kazakhstan revealed that saxaul survival rates across 24 sites ranged from less than 1% to as high as 78%, with the best outcomes achieved on sandy loam or saline light loamy soils covered with wind-drifted sand. In contrast, plantations established on crusted salt marshes or dry-type playa (takyr-like) soils suffered massive losses, sometimes complete die-off. Excessive groundwater mineralization, particularly near the Syr Darya riverbed and the Kokaral Dam, was also found to inhibit root development and survival (Salmukhanbetova et al. 2020). Additional challenges include degradation of older (10-year-old) plantations due to high initial planting density, gall formation on stems, and damage from rodents and grazing camels. Experimental measures such as mechanical sand-fixing with reed fences have shown promise, increasing saxaul survival to 32–57% in shifting dune zones (Salmukhanbetova et al. 2020). To sustain the effectiveness of these plantations, future efforts should prioritize matching species to soil conditions, reducing grazing pressure, and maintaining plant density that allows natural regeneration. Monitoring during both the first and fifth years after planting is critical, as saxaul typically reaches its generative phase within this period, allowing self-seeding and natural cluster formation. Combining phyto-reclamation with community-based protection and awareness programs will be vital to prevent degradation of existing plantations and to ensure continuity of ecosystem services in the North Aral basin. Community-based afforestation programs could combine ecological benefits with livelihoods by engaging local residents in planting and maintaining vegetation, while simultaneously increasing awareness of dust-related health risks and promoting protective behaviors during dust events. Moreover, native vegetation is deeply embedded in local folk medicine, e.g., Alhagi maurorum (camelthorn), which is widely used in traditional remedies and exhibits documented pharmacological activities (Ahmad et al. 2015). Building on this ethnobotanical approach within afforestation programs can create actual benefits that motivate local society to participate in planting (Ahmad et al. 2015). Monitoring air quality and disseminating timely alerts during high-risk periods are also important to minimize acute respiratory exposures.

Strengthening the regional healthcare system

The regional healthcare infrastructure remains heavily concentrated in Aral town, with other hospitals located hundreds of kilometers away, limiting access for remote villages. Mobile health teams, equipped with telemedicine technologies, could bridge this gap by providing primary care and screening services in smaller settlements. Training local nurses, midwives, and community health workers to detect respiratory, cardiovascular, and reproductive disorders early would enhance preventive care and enable timely interventions. Rotational placements of doctors from medical universities in Almaty and Astana could strengthen local capacity while fostering knowledge transfer. Incorporating routine screening for anemia, thyroid dysfunction, reproductive disorders, and cardiovascular risk factors into standard primary care packages would address the region’s prevalent and persistent health concerns. Targeted public health programs should also emphasize health education to improve community awareness of nutrition, hygiene, and the management of chronic diseases. Developing shared digital platforms that combine environmental, clinical, and epidemiological indicators would improve transparency and facilitate collaboration between research institutions and local authorities.

Improving the nutrition

Persistent nutritional deficiencies, particularly low fruit and vegetable intake (Atshabarova 2016), remain a major concern for the North Aral Sea population, contributing to cardiovascular, metabolic, and endocrine disorders. Enhancing local food security requires a multi-pronged approach. Encouraging home gardens, community farms, and small-scale greenhouse cultivation can increase access to fresh produce. Implementing safe livestock management and small-scale aquaculture initiatives would diversify protein sources while minimizing contamination risks. Micronutrient supplementation programs targeting children, women of reproductive age, and pregnant women should be integrated into existing health services. Nutrition education campaigns addressing balanced diets, food safety, and proper storage practices would reinforce behavioral changes. Facilitating market access to ensure the affordability and availability of nutrient-rich foods would further strengthen dietary quality and support long-term population health.

Development of wastewater and sanitation infrastructure

Sanitation deficiencies remain a pressing public health concern in the North Aral Sea region. In most rural settlements, pit latrines remain the predominant form of sanitation, often constructed without proper lining or adequate separation from groundwater, thereby increasing the risk of fecal contamination of drinking water (Gwenzi et al. 2023). Such conditions contribute to the persistence of enteric infections, intestinal parasitic diseases, and outbreaks of diarrheal illness, particularly among children (Prüss-Ustün et al. 2019; Wolf et al. 2019). The gradual introduction of improved sanitation technologies, including ventilated improved pit latrines or composting toilets, offers a practical, low-cost solution that can be locally managed without extensive sewage infrastructure (Obeng et al. 2023). In larger settlements, such as Aral town, modernization of sewage treatment facilities should be prioritized, as untreated wastewater contributes both to environmental degradation and human exposure. Sustainable improvement of sanitation systems requires not only infrastructural investment but also community engagement, health and hygiene education, as well as behavioral change programs to promote hygienic practices and public ownership of facilities (Bartram and Cairncross 2010).

Development of waste management systems

Safe collection, recycling, and disposal of solid waste are urgently needed in the North Aral region to prevent uncontrolled dumping, open burning, and toxic exposures. Effective waste management depends on developing a reliable road network linking villages to collection and processing points in Aral town. At present, waste is frequently dumped in ditches, left exposed on the former lakebed, or incinerated, increasing environmental contamination and health risks (Fig. 2F). Accumulation of waste near fishing areas and along the shores of the North Aral Sea is also of concern, with discarded plastics, nets, and other debris threatening aquatic life, degrading habitats, and compromising the safety of local fishery resources (Fig. 2G). Addressing this issue requires targeted cleanup actions, effective enforcement of anti-dumping regulations, and long-term prevention measures integrated into regional waste management planning. Establishing controlled community collection points with scheduled transport to Aral town and low-tech recycling initiatives for plastics, scrap metal, and other reusable materials could provide environmental and economic benefits. Equally important are education and awareness programs to foster responsible waste disposal practices among residents, fishermen, and local enterprises. Such initiatives, implemented through schools, community workshops, and media campaigns, can build a sense of shared responsibility and ensure lasting behavioral change. Pilot projects supported by non-governmental organizations could test decentralized waste sorting in larger villages, with volunteers playing key roles in education, monitoring, and community engagement to ensure sustainable adoption.

Strengthening education in the area

Education is a cornerstone of human health and resilience. Improved access to quality education is associated with longer life expectancy and better health decision-making (Raghupathi and Raghupathi 2020; Balaj et al. 2024), which may likely play a particular role in regions affected by environmental degradation. As of 2025, the Aral District hosts 50 schools with over 17 500 students in grades 1–11 and approximately 2500 teaching staff, 59% of whom meet advanced qualification standards (moderator, expert, research, or master teacher) (AAD 2025), with further investment ongoing in villages such as Zhalanash (Fig. 2I). Priority measures should include introducing school-based programs on nutrition, hygiene, and reproductive health, delivered by locally trained teachers. Complementary adult education campaigns should address practical health behaviors, including safe food storage, water treatment, and safe pesticide use. Incentives to retain teachers in remote settlements, such as housing subsidies or rotation schemes, are critical for sustaining educational quality and ensuring continuity of health-related knowledge dissemination.

Development of roads and improvement of connectivity

Improving transportation networks is crucial for ensuring timely access to healthcare, education, markets, and essential services. At present, many routes remain unpaved (Fig. 2E), making travel slow, physically demanding, and hazardous for vulnerable populations, while simultaneously increasing exposure to airborne dust. Inhabitants of villages such as Zhalanash or Tastubek regularly travel to Aral town across the former lakebed, similar to fishermen reaching the lake’s shore. Enhancing connectivity through asphalting key access roads and seasonal maintenance of rural tracks prone to flooding or erosion would substantially improve mobility and safety. Targeted investment in small-scale public transport, such as subsidized minibus routes, would further reduce the health risks associated with off-road travel and facilitate equitable access to healthcare, educational facilities, and local markets. Improved road infrastructure would also strengthen local food and fish distribution chains, enhance income opportunities, and indirectly support nutrition and well-being.

Conclusions

This paper provides an updated synthesis of evidence on the health status of populations living in Kazakhstan’s North Aral Sea region, highlighting that the impacts of the Aral Sea disaster must be understood in a spatially differentiated manner. Restoration of the North Aral has, to some extent, mitigated many environmental hazards, particularly dust exposure, in contrast to the southern, desiccated basins where risks remain far higher. The observed health effects in the region appear to be multifactorial and should no longer be attributed solely to dust storms. Cardiovascular, endocrine, reproductive, and nutritional disturbances likely reflect a combination of historical contamination, persistent nutritional deficiencies, psychosocial stress, and limited healthcare access. These findings emphasize the need for continued research to disentangle these complex determinants and to guide evidence-based public health interventions. Preventive measures should integrate environmental management, healthcare strengthening, improved nutrition, education, and infrastructure development. In the context of potential industrial exploitation of the North Aral Sea region (e.g., related to fossil fuel extraction), any investment must be coupled with a firm commitment to sustainable local development. This includes constructing and maintaining roads and transport networks, enhancing educational facilities, and strengthening healthcare services. From an ethical perspective, such commitments align with the principle of distributive justice, ensuring that communities benefit directly from economic activities rather than bearing disproportionate environmental or social costs. Industry engagement in community development not only supports resilience and equitable growth but also fosters long-term social license to operate in a region already burdened by ecological and public health challenges.
