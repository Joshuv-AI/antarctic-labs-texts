Characteristics of CLA⁺Tregs in rheumatoid arthritis and their potential as biomarkers of treatment response
Authors: Danxue Zhu, Feng Sun, Xi Zheng, Wenxin Cai, Ping Wang, Sitian Zang, Yingni Li, Xu Jin, Yuke Hou, Peishi Rao, Yang Xie, Shuyan Liu, Jiachen Li, Yan Wang, Chaonan Wei, Xiaolin Sun, Fanlei Hu, Zhanguo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe newly discovered Treg subset (CLA+ Tregs) expressing cutaneous lymphocyte-associated antigen is associated with inflammatory conditions. This study elucidated the role of CLA+ Tregs in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) development and its responses to treatment.MethodsA total of 12 T cell subsets, together with total T cells, B cells, and NK cells were analyzed in 169 patients with RA and 114 healthy controls using flow cytometry. Autoantibodies were detected by ELISA. The logistic regression models were employed to assess the association between cell subsets and rheumatoid arthritis. Relationships between CLA+ Tregs and clinical variables were assessed.ResultsAmong the immune cell subsets analyzed, CLA⁺ Tregs were the most significantly reduced ones in patients with RA compared with healthy controls (OR 0.825 [0.764–0.891], p < 0.001). Decreased CLA⁺ Tregs were strongly correlated to active disease status (adjusted OR 0.814 [0.733–0.902], p = 0.004; AUC 0.916 [0.869–0.963], p < 0.001). Decreased CLA+ Tregs were associated with higher swollen joint count, tender joint count and DAS28 scores, as well as systemic involvements, including interstitial lung disease (ILD), anemia, fever, and skin vasculitis. Additionally, rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-mutated citrullinated vimentin antibody (anti-MCV) were correlated to lower CLA+ Tregs. Compared to baseline prior treatment, CLA+ Tregs increased significantly in RA patients, accompanied by improvements in disease activity (median, 9.60% vs. 18.80%, p = 0.044; 3.41 cells/µL vs. 7.86 cells/µL, p = 0.011, respectively).ConclusionsThe results demonstrated a significant reduction of CLA+ Tregs in active RA, especially in patients with systemic damages. Recovery of CLA+ Tregs may predict therapeutic responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03791-0.Keywords: CLA+ Tregs, Rheumatoid arthritis, Disease activity, Therapeutic responses

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, T cell-mediated systemic inflammatory disorder characterized by cartilage and bone destruction [1–6], which affects approximately 0.28-1% of the global population [7, 8]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are a naturally occurring subpopulation of CD4+ T cells, maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing inflammatory damage [9]. Dysregulation of Tregs is peculiarly prone to activating autoreactive T cells, impairing immune tolerance and boosting systemic inflammation [10–14]. Recent studies have revealed a novel Treg subset expressing cutaneous lymphocyte antigen (CLA), a cell surface glycoprotein that is highly expressed on Tregs than on effector T cells, permits cells migrating to sites of immune response and cutaneous inflammation [15–17].

CLA+ Tregs are capable to inhibit the proliferation of CD4+CD25− T cells [18]. Additionally, CLA gene-deficient Tregs have been shown to lose their ability to suppress T cell proliferation in lymph nodes and to prevent contact between dendritic cells and T cells. Consequently, autoreactive T cells become activated, rendering murine models highly susceptible to experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [19].

CLA+ Tregs consistently showed an overall decrease in psoriasis and GVHD, and associated with disease progression and recurrence [20, 21]. Recently, Paul et al. [22] showed that the percentage of CLA+ Tregs in systemic sclerosis could increase dramatically with effective therapy, suggesting that CLA+ Tregs may be crucial in the pathogenesis of the autoimmune disease.

In this study, we evaluated CLA⁺ Tregs in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and determined their relevance to disease activity, systemic involvements and response to treatment.

Methods

Patients

We analyzed 169 patients (31 males and 138 females, age 59.08 ± 12.79 years) and 114 healthy people (50 males and 64 females, age 44.89 ± 13.96 years). All patients met both the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria and European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria for RA.

Data collection

Clinical medical records, radiological examinations and laboratory data were recorded during their initial clinical visit. Specifically, those medical records included demographic data, medical history, articular manifestation, comorbidities and treatment. Additionally, radiological and laboratory findings were composed of chest computed tomographic scans data, routine laboratory tests, cytokines and antibodies.

Detections of cell subsets

Absolute counts and percentages of lymphocyte subsets were determined using a single-platform flow cytometry technique. Different immune cell subsets were analyzed, including total T cells, B cells, and NK cells, as well as 12 T cell subsets: CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, CD127lowTregs, Foxp3⁺ Tregs, CLA⁺ Tregs, CD161⁺ Tregs, pTfh, naïve Th, IL-2⁺ Th, IL-4⁺ Th, IFN-γ⁺ Th, and TNF-α⁺ Th cells. 50 µL of EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood was added to BD Trucount™ tubes (BD, #340334) and stained with 20 µL of BD Multitest™ IMK kit antibodies (BD, #662965) for 15 min. After lysing with 450 µL of 1X BD FACS Lysing Solution (BD, #349202), samples were analyzed on a BD FACSCanto™ flow cytometer within 24 h. For T-cell subset characterization, 100 µL of whole blood was stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies (BD Pharmingen), including anti-CD3-APC-H7 (BD, #641397), anti-CD4-FITC (BD, #340133), anti-CD25-PE (BD, #341009), anti-CD127-BV605 (BD, #562662), anti-CD45RA-BV510 (BD, #563031), anti-CCR7-BV421 (BD, #562555), anti-PD-1-PE-Cy7 (BD, #561272), anti-CXCR5-AF647 (BD, #558113), anti-CD161-BV421 (BD, #562615), anti-CLA-AF647 (BD, #562758), and anti-CD3-PerCP (BD, #652831). Erythrocytes were lysed (1X BD FACS Lysing Solution), followed by washing and centrifugation. For FOXP3 analysis, intracellular staining was performed using the Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set (eBioscience, Cat. No. 88-8824-00). Anti-FOXP3-AF647 antibody from BD Biosciences (Cat. No. 560045) and anti-FOXP3-BV421 antibody from BioLegend (Cat. No. 320123) were used for the staining. Data were acquired on an Aria II cytometer (BD) with FACSDiva software. For intracellular cytokine analysis, heparinized blood was stimulated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail and BD GolgiPlug™ (4–6 h, 37 °C). Cells were surface-stained with CD4-PE-Cy7 (BD 663493), fixed and permeabilized with the BD IntraSure™ kit (BD 641776), Stained for FITC-IFN-γ/PE-IL-4 (BD 340456), and BV421-IL-17 (BD 562933). Samples were analyzed on a FACSAria™ II (FlowJo). The flow cytometry gating strategies were shown in the Fig.S1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Commercially available anti-CCP elisa kits (QUANTA Lite), GPI elisa kits (Genstar, Beijing, China) and anti-MCV elisa kits (ORGENTEC) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results were measured on a EUROIMMUN Sprinter 1(Beijing, China).

Follow up

We longitudinally traced the proportion and absolute number of CLA+Tregs of 19 RA patients. The follow-up time of these RA patients was 3 months. We detected cell subsets, autoantibodies and recorded clinical variables.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables unsatisfying gaussian distribution. Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test applied to matched pairs studies. Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyze the association of cell subsets with clinical features, lab variables and antibodies, with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate (BH-FDR) correction applied for multiple testing.

Binary logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) to explore the associations between cell subsets and RA. The binary outcomes were defined as (1) rheumatoid arthritis versus healthy controls, (2) active RA (DAS28 > 2.6) versus remission, and (3) RA-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) versus RA without ILD. Age, gender, and cells showing significant associations in univariable analysis models (p < 0.1) qualified for further analysis in multiple logistic regression models. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed separately for each multivariate binary logistic regression model using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and all covariates showed VIF values < 5, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity (Tables S1–S3).

Additional analyses focused on the RA-ILD patients were performed in R (version 4.4.2). Partial correlations were calculated for all cell subsets within RA-ILD using the R package ppcor with FDR correction. The network graphs were visualised by using the igraph package. Data visualizations, including bar plots, forest plots, heatmaps, and volcano plots, were generated using the ggplot2 package and related libraries. The p value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of RA patients

A total of 169 RA patients were recruited for this study, with a mean age of 59, and 85.5% female (138/169.). Median disease duration of RA was 13 years, ranging from 2 months to 60 years and the mean 28-joint count disease activity score (DAS28) was 4.16, ranging from 0.68 to 8.42. There were 26 patients in remission (DAS28 < 2.6) and 143 active RA patients (DAS28 > 2.6). Interstitial lung disease (ILD) occurred in 47 (27.81%) patients, and 28 (16.57%) RA patients were with secondary Sjogren Syndrome (sSS). Other systemic manifestations or complication included keratitis (n = 7; 4.14%), rheumatoid nodules (n = 16; 9.47%), cutaneous vasculitis (n = 19; 11.24%), fever (n = 7; 4.14%), anemia (n = 52; 30.77%). Additionally, 125 (74%) patients were positive for rheumatoid factor (RF) and 148 (88%) patients were positive for anti-CCP. 47 (28%) of 169 RA patients were using glucocorticoids. 115 (68%) patients were using csDMARDs and 27 (16%) patients were receiving b/tsDMARDs. CLA+Tregs decreased in active RA

Total T cells, B cells, NK cells, together with 12 T cell subtypes were analyzed in this study (Table S4). In univariable logistic regression analysis, CLA+ Tregs were inversely associated with rheumatoid arthritis (OR 0.859 [95% CI 0.827–0.893], p < 0.001). After adjustment for age, sex, and cell subsets showing potential associations in univariable analyses (p < 0.1), the multivariable logistic regression model confirmed that CLA+ Tregs remained independently associated with RA (OR 0.825 [95% CI 0.764–0.891], p < 0.001) (Fig. 1a). There were lower percentage of CLA+ Tregs among Tregs in RA patients than that in healthy donors (median 9.40% [IQR 5.65-14.0] vs. 20.55% [IQR 14.60-28.43], p < 0.001) (Fig. 1b), Specifically, the percentage of CLA+ Tregs among CD4+ T cells (median, 0.79% [IQR 0.40–1.15] vs. 1.61% [IQR 1.08–2.15]) or among lymphocytes (median, 0.36% [IQR 0.19–0.54] vs. 0.55% [IQR 0.42–0.75]) still showed significant reduction in RA patients compared with healthy donors (p < 0.001). Additionally, the absolute numbers of CLA+ Tregs decreased significantly in RA patients compared with healthy controls (median 5.09 cells/µl [IQR 2.40–7.95] vs. 12.57 cells/µl [IQR 8.39–18.15], p < 0.001) (Fig. 1b). Moreover, the predominant FOXP3 expression within CLA⁺ Tregs in both RA patients and healthy controls further confirmed the regulatory T cell identity of these cells (Fig. S2). To address potential confounding, additional analyses demonstrated that CLA⁺ Tregs were not associated with age or sex, and this cell subset showed no significant differences after stratification by glucocorticoid or DMARD use (Figs. In the T cell subsets (Table 2), there was clear association between CLA+ Tregs and active RA (unadjusted OR 0.775 [95% CI 0.706–0.850], p < 0.001), which remained significant when adjusting for CRP, IgA, IgG, and IgM (adjusted OR 0.814 [95% CI 0.733–0.902], p = 0.004) (Fig. 1c). Close correlation of CLA+Tregs with disease severity and systemic involvements

Decreased CLA+ Tregs were associated with clinical severity in patients with RA (Fig. 3a, Table S7), showing obvious correlation with DAS28 (r = − 0.53, p < 0.001), SJC (r= -0.34, p < 0.001) and TJC (r= -0.39, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3b, c and d).

RA patients with systemic involvements showed CLA+ Tregs deficiency (Fig. 2a, Table S8). Patients with ILD had a significantly lower proportion of CLA+ Tregs (median 7.20% [IQR 3.15–11.15] vs. 10.0% [5.75,14.0], p = 0.045). A close association of CLA+ Tregs have also been found with cutaneous vasculitis, fever and anemia. The number of CLA+ Tregs was significantly lower in patients with cutaneous vasculitis (median, 3.02 cells/µL [IQR 1.96–4.19] vs. 5.55 cells/µL [IQR 2.51–8.37], p = 0.005), fever (median, 1.17 cells/µL [IQR 0.37–2.06] vs. 5.24 cells/µL [IQR 2.68–8.10], p < 0.001) and anemia (median, 3.97 cells/µL [IQR 1.98–6.20] vs. 5.64 cells/µL [IQR 3.03–9.05], p = 0.012). Among multiple cell subtypes, CLA⁺ Tregs were the most significantly reduced in RA-ILD patients compared to patients without ILD (Fig. 2b). CLA⁺ Tregs remained significant when adjusting for age, gender, disease duration, T cells, CD8+ T cells and TNF-α+ Th (adjusted OR 0.22 [95% CI 0.05–0.97], p = 0.045) (Fig. 2c).

Associations of CLA+Tregs with immunological variables

Negative correlations were observed between CLA+ Tregs and RF, anti-MCV, IgG, IgA, ESR and CRP (Fig. 3e, f and g, Table S7). Additionally, compared with the percentage of CLA+ Tregs, the number of this Treg subset was reported slightly weaker correlations with RA disease activity variables such as ESR, CRP and IgG (p < 0.05).

Low CLA+ Treg frequency was associated with T cell (OR 2.814 [95%CI 1.025–7.723], p < 0.05) (Table S9) that remained significant when adjusting for DAS28 and RF in RA patients (OR 3.014 [95%CI 1.051–8.645]). In patients with ILD, CLA⁺ Tregs were embedded within the broader network of immune cell subsets (Fig. 2d). They displayed significant partial correlations with TNF-α⁺ Th (r = -0.34, p = 0.05, FDR = 0.33) and naïve Th cells (r = -0.37, p = 0.04, FDR = 0.33) (Table S10). Associations between increased cytokines and CLA+Tregs were examined (Table S11). CLA+ Tregs level was higher in the IL-4 high group (median 13.80% [IQR 7.45–19.40]) vs. median 7.2% [IQR 4.63–13.38], p < 0.05; median 9.36cells/µl [IQR 4.33–14.17] vs. median 3.84cells/µl [IQR 2.18–6.89], p < 0.05).

Recovery of CLA+Tregs with treatment

After 3 months of treatment, RA patients showed a significant increase in CLA⁺ Tregs concomitant with improvements in disease activity (Fig. 4a; Tables S12–S13). Specifically, both the proportion of CLA⁺ Tregs (median, 9.60% [IQR 4.20–17.40] vs. 18.80% [IQR 8.90–25.40], p = 0.044) and their absolute counts (median, 3.41 cells/µL [IQR 1.70–8.01] vs. 7.86 cells/µL [IQR 4.88–16.50], p = 0.011) increased significantly compared with baseline. These changes were accompanied by significant reductions in DAS28, SJC28 and TJC28 (Fig. 4b and c). Further studies revealed that the decrease in the DAS28 value (∆DAS28) was closely related to the increase in CLA+ Tregs (∆CLA+ Tregs) after 3-month treatment (r= -0.46, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4d). These findings suggest that increased CLA+ Tregs were associated with improvement of RA disease activity.

Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the roles of CLA+ Tregs on RA patients. We have revealed that the frequency and number of CLA+ Tregs decreased dramatically in RA patients. This Treg subset was negatively associated with the disease activity, and could be recovered after effective therapy in RA. CLA+ Tregs might serve as a promising indicator of RA development.

Our data showed significant differences of CLA+Tregs between RA patients and HC, with a close association with DAS28. Notably, among multiple immune populations, including T cells, B cells, NK cells and 12 T cell subtypes, CLA⁺ Tregs represented the most significantly reduced subset in active RA. The multivariate logistic regression model included age, gender, and cell subsets identified in univariable analyses further confirmed that CLA⁺ Tregs remained independently associated with RA. Consistent with our findings, Lee et al. [21]. reported a marked reduction of CLA⁺ Tregs in psoriasis that correlated with disease activity, suggesting that dysregulation of CLA⁺ Tregs may represent a shared feature of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases. To further substantiate the regulatory identity of this population, we confirmed that the majority of CLA⁺ Tregs expressed FOXP3 in both RA patients and healthy controls, supporting that the CLA⁺ population analyzed represents bona fide regulatory T cells rather than activated conventional T cells.

This study explored a comprehensive list of clinical parameters associated with CLA+ Tregs. CLA+ Tregs were closely correlated with SJC28, TJC28 and DAS28. Additionally, patients with ILD exhibited a reduced proportion of CLA⁺ Tregs, and this association remained significant after adjustment in the logistic regression model, indicating a potential link between CLA⁺ Tregs and ILD. Similarly, mice lacking CLA gene tended to develop ILD, showing pulmonary interstitium thickening, lung vessels remodeling, more leukocyte infiltrate [23] and a decrease of Treg [24]. Besides, CLA+ T cells were significantly associated with the high body temperature [25] and skin inflammation [15]. Those might support our results that the significant reduction of CLA+ Tregs was observed in RA patients with fever or cutaneous vasculitis. Correlation analysis supports significant associations of CLA+ Tregs with IgA, IgG, anti-MCV and RF in RA patients. So, CLA+ Tregs may be involved in mucosal and systemic immune, which needs further study.

A reduction in CLA⁺ Tregs was associated with lower IL-4 levels. IL-4, predominantly secreted by Th2 cells, is widely recognized for its potent anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory functions. Beyond its classical role in suppressing Th1-driven immune responses, IL-4 has been shown to inhibit TNF-α release, limit matrix metalloproteinase production, and downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8 [26, 27]. Taken together, these observations suggest that the cytokine milieu, particularly IL-4, may play a crucial role in preserving CLA⁺ Tregs. In 47 patients with ILD, CLA⁺ Tregs were positioned within the broader network of cell subsets associated with ILD. They showed significant partial correlations with naïve Th and TNF-α⁺ Th cells, which were closely related to ILD [28–32]. Although these associations did not withstand FDR correction and thus do not constitute strong or direct evidence for links between CLA⁺ Tregs and these Th subsets in RA-ILD, they may nonetheless merit further validation in larger cohorts. These findings raise the possibility that CLA⁺ Tregs participate in the complex immune networks underlying RA-ILD and may influence disease progression through their interactions with Th cell subsets.

Our study investigated the potential of CLA⁺ Treg cells as an immunological biomarker associated with disease remission in RA. We demonstrated that CLA⁺ Treg cell levels were significantly associated with remission status, with an AUC of 0.916 [95% CI 0.869–0.963], indicating high discriminatory performance. Moreover, longitudinal analyses in a subset of RA patients revealed a marked increase in CLA⁺ Treg levels following treatment, which was accompanied by significant improvements in disease activity indices, including DAS28, SJC28, and TJC28. These findings suggest that restoration of CLA⁺ Tregs is closely linked to effective control of systemic inflammation and may reflect rebalancing of regulatory immune networks during disease improvement. Consistent with our observations, elevated CLA⁺ Treg levels have also been reported to associate with disease improvement and reduced recurrence in systemic sclerosis [22]. Given the established role of CLA+ Tregs in immune regulation, the restoration of CLA⁺ Tregs may reflect rebalancing of regulatory immune networks during disease improvement. Collectively, these results indicate that CLA⁺ Tregs may serve as a dynamic immune marker reflecting changes in disease activity and therapeutic response in RA.

This study’s limitations include all results from a single-center study, potentially causing detection bias. Further multi-center studies may be needed to confirm the alteration of CLA+ Tregs in RA. We only traced 19 patients and larger studies are needed. Additionally, the results only focused on the frequency and count of CLA+ Tregs. In-depth functional analyses of this Treg subset will further show its effects in the pathogenesis of RA.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings indicate that CLA⁺ Tregs are significantly reduced in RA and are closely linked to disease activity and systemic complications, particularly interstitial lung disease. Importantly, the recovery of CLA⁺ Tregs may serve as a potential indicator of therapeutic response.
