Assessment of parasacral ischial plane block success using sonographically measured regional hemodynamic variables and perfusion index: an observational diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Ruchi Raina, Nidhi Bhatia, Kajal Jain, Revathi Nair, Varun Mahajan, Suman Arora, Sameer Aggarwal, Amarjyoti Hazarika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional methods of assessing block success pose challenges in patients with traumatic brain injuries, who are often uncooperative and non-compliant to subjective assessment. We thus planned this study with the aim of evaluating effectiveness of objective parameters, including perfusion index(PI) and ultrasound-guided regional hemodynamic parameters, for indicating success of ultrasound-guided parasacral ischial plane(PIP) block, a lower extremity fascial plane block.MethodsWe enrolled 89 consecutive adult trauma patients scheduled for lower-limb orthopaedic surgery under ultrasound-guided PIP block (plus a femoral block), and insonated the popliteal artery for the following parameters blood‑flow velocities: peak systolic velocity (PSV), end diastolic velocity (EDV), Time-averaged maximum velocity (Tmax), Time averaged mean velocity (Tmean) with Doppler ultrasound and perfusion index (PI) at the big toe at baseline and 10, 20, 30, 40 min after the block. We assessed block success with conventional sensory and motor tests (pinprick and strength) — this was the “clinical reference standard” and looked at how well the percentage changes in PI and Doppler measurements predicted the clinical (subjective) determination of block success. Our secondary objectives compared objective parameters with subjective parameters in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value(PPV) and negative predictive value(NPV).ResultsOf the 89 patients, 83 (93%) had a successful block, tracked by significant increases in regional hemodynamic variables and PI at 20 min. Successful blocks showed significant increases in regional doppler measures and perfusion index by 20 min. At 20 min, the optimal thresholds were a 38.2% rise in popliteal PSV and a 46.5% rise in PI. The AUROCs were 0.92 (95% CI 0.84–0.99) for %ΔPI and 0.86 (95% CI 0.74–0.99) for %ΔPSV. In this sample the specificity and positive predictive value at these thresholds were 100%, but these estimates are imprecise because of the small number of failed blocks.ConclusionRegional hemodynamic parameters, more so percentage increase in PSV and PI, can be used as early and reliable adjunctive indicators of a successful ultrasound-guided parasacral ischial plane block.Clinical trial registrationThe trial was prospectively registered with Clinical Trial Registry (https//ctri.nic.in/Clinicaltrials/rmaindet.php?trialid=84902&EncHid=63217.30513&modid=1&compid=19; Registration number CTRI/2023/06/053375 Date of Registration01062023 Date of first patient enrolment15062023).Keywords: Perfusion index, Regional hemodynamic variables, Nerve block, Lower extremity, Surgery, Ultrasonography, Doppler

Introduction

Success of ultrasound (US)-guided peripheral nerve blocks (PNB) is traditionally evaluated by sensory and motor testing [1]. These methods are subjective, require patient’s cooperation, and can be particularly unreliable in unresponsive or uncooperative patients, for example, those with concomitant head injuries. Objective measures such as, perfusion index (PI) measured by a pulse oximeter, and ultrasound regional hemodynamic parameters like peak systolic velocity (PSV), end diastolic velocity (EDV), time averaged maximum velocity (Tmax) and time averaged mean velocity (Tmean), measured using pulsed wave doppler (PWD) ultrasound, offer an alternative, because nerve blocks produce a sympathetic blockade, vasodilatation and measurable increases in blood flow [2–7].

At our level-I trauma center, many polytrauma patients with concomitant traumatic brain injury (TBI) require urgent lower limb surgery and receive sacral plexus block (parasacral ischial plane [PIP] approach) for the same, as presence of TBI with/without spine trauma often precludes administration of central neuraxial blocks. PIP approach is a fascial-plane technique in which local anaesthetic is deposited superficial to the ischium at the greater sciatic foramen [8]. Previous studies have evaluated objective metrics following brachial plexus block, with one previous report evaluating its utility following popliteal sciatic nerve block [2–7]. PIP block, however, being a fascial plane block, may show variable spread, inconsistent success and delayed onset – complicating clinical assessment [9]. Further, clinical assessment is challenging in patients with associated TBI, as they are often uncooperative and non-compliant to subjective assessment. Hence, in such cases, objective parameters of assessing block success can prove extremely useful. There being no previous reports in literature – we evaluated the objective metrics for proximal lower extremity surgery. We designed this study to determine whether these parameters are reliable indicators of block success.

The primary objective of our study was to assess the diagnostic performance of changes in PSV, EDV, Tmax, Tmean, PI for predicting US-guided PIP block success; secondary objectives included describing the temporal changes in these measures and comparing their sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) with motor and sensory assessment.

Methods

This prospective observational diagnostic accuracy study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Reference Number: IEC-INT/2023/MD-962) and registered in the Clinical Trial Registry of India CTRI/2023/06/053375 on 01/06/2023; with the first patient enrolled on 15/06/2023.

Written informed consent was obtained from all of the participants in the study. We consecutively enrolled 89 American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I-II patients, aged 18–85 years, scheduled to undergo post-trauma lower limb orthopaedic surgery under US-guided PIP block. To provide complete lower limb anaesthesia, an US-guided femoral nerve block was also administered. Exclusion criteria were peripheral vascular disease, allergy to local anesthetic drugs, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, injuries that prevent ultrasound probe application, polytrauma, pre-existing motor or sensory deficits in the operative limb, thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy, morbid obesity (BMI > 35) and infection at the intervention site.

Pre-procedural evaluation

Fitness of patients was assessed after complete clinical history, general physical and systemic examination for the proposed surgical procedure. Following routine investigations and after confirmation of fasting based on standard NPO guidelines, patients were shifted to the operating room and monitored for heart rate (HR), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), electrocardiogram (ECG), oxygen saturation (SpO2), and temperature using multichannel monitors (Datex-Ohmeda S/5 Avance). An intravenous access was secured before starting the procedure.

Baseline measurements

Baseline popliteal-artery haemodynamic parameters were obtained with a Sonosite (Sonosite Inc., Bothell, WA, USA) ultrasound, with a 5–12 MHz linear array transducer placed in the popliteal fossa (Fig. 1). After optimizing the longitudinal B-mode image, pulse-waved doppler (PWD) mode was activated and the sample volume was centered within the arterial lumen. Doppler angle ≤ 600 between ultrasound beam and blood flow was maintained for accurate velocity measurement. When a stable, high-quality optimal spectral waveform was achieved, peak systolic velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), maximum time-averaged velocity (Tmax), and mean time-averaged velocity (Tmean) were recorded. Baseline perfusion index (PI) was measured using a Masimo pulse oximeter placed on the great toe of the blocked limb.

Sonoanatomy of parasacral ischial plane (PIP) block and hemodynamic parameters measured at variable time intervals

Administration of ultrasound-guided parasacral ischial plane block

With the patient in the lateral decubitus position (Sim’s position), operative side up, hip rolled forward, with the knee flexed at 90°angle, the sacral region was prepared and draped under aseptic conditions. A 2–5 MHz curvilinear ultrasound probe, in a sterile cover, was placed between posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) and the mid-point of the line from the PSIS to greater trochanter. The ilium was seen as a bright hyperechoic line beneath the gluteus maximus and medius muscles. The probe was swept inferomedially, with a parasacral tilt to identify the iliac bone and the sciatic notch, posterior border of the ischium and the sacral plexus. The sciatic notch was identified as a break in the continuity of the iliac bone. At the level of the greater sciatic foramen, the posterior border of the ischium was identified with the piriformis overlying it. At this level, the sacral plexus was observed as a dense hyperechoic cord-like structure (Fig. 1). After infiltration with 2–3 ml of 1% lidocaine, a 100 mm, 22-gauge needle was advanced in a lateral to medial in-plane approach, aiming towards the postero-medial border of the ischium. Following bony contact, 20 ml of 0.5% ropivacaine was injected while observing for a characteristic fascial plane spread tracking under the piriformis towards the sacral plexus. The total dose of ropivacaine did not exceed 3 mg/kg in any patient.

Following PIP block, patients were made to lie supine with the hip abducted. Under aseptic precautions, high-frequency (5–12 MHz) ultrasound probe was placed at the mid-point of the inguinal ligament, oriented parallel to it. The femoral artery was identified. The probe was then moved laterally to identify the femoral nerve as an oval or triangular hyperechoic structure lying lateral to the artery. After localizing the femoral nerve, 10 ml of 0.5% ropivacaine was injected while observing for the spread of local anesthetic drug around the nerve.

All blocks were performed by an operator experienced in the PIP technique, with more than 20 previous US-guided PIP blocks. An independent observer, who was not present in the operating room during block placement, evaluated the block using subjective sensory and motor parameters. A separate investigator, blinded to the sensory/motor assessments, performed all pre- and post-block Doppler and perfusion index measurements.

Post-block measurements

Objective parameters

Popliteal‑artery hemodynamics (PSV, EDV, Tmax, Tmean) were recorded by pulsed‑wave Doppler and perfusion index (PI) was measured at the great toe using a Masimo pulse oximeter at baseline and at 10, 20, 30 and 40 min after block administration (Fig. 1). All ultrasound Doppler measurements were obtained by the investigator blinded to clinical sensory/motor assessments (Fig. 1).

Subjective parameters

Sensory function was assessed at 10, 20, 30 and 40 min using a blunt 24‑gauge needle applied to the dorsal and plantar surfaces of the foot (sciatic distribution) and the medial leg (femoral distribution), and graded by the Hollmen scale: 0 = normal pinprick perception, 1 = decreased compared with the contralateral limb, 2 = blunt sensation, 3 = loss of tactile sense [3].

Motor assessment was done in the distribution of the (1) sciatic nerve (plantar flexion / dorsiflexion of the foot, knee flexion) and (2) femoral nerve (knee extension). Motor block was graded using a three-point scale (0 = normal function; 1 = decreased strength compared to the contralateral limb; 2 = complete motor block) [10].

Surgical anesthesia was defined as the absence of movement and as a loss of pinprick sensation in the sciatic nerve distribution of the lower limb [3]. Blocks not meeting these criteria by 40 min were classified as failures and managed with general anesthesia as per standard protocol.

Sample size calculation

The cut-offs for each objective parameter were determined by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. On one axis, the readings of objective parameter and on other axis, the outcome of interest, that is PIP block success or failure, as determined from subjective criteria was plotted. The optimal cut-off point was determined using ROC curves with the maximum Youden index (sensitivity +specificity − 1). Based on Lu et al. [3], we anticipated an area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of more than 0.85 for the primary index tests and specified a null hypothesis of an AUROC curve of 0.5. Assuming two-sided alpha = 0.05 and 80% power to detect an AUROC of 0.85 versus 0.50, and expecting a 10% block failure rate, we calculated at least 85 patients were required. We therefore enrolled 89 patients to allow a small margin for exclusion or missing data. Optimal cut-offs were determined from the ROC curves using the Youden index; diagnostic performance estimates are reported with exact 95% confidence intervals and were internally validated by bootstrap resampling.

Statistical analysis

The sensory and motor response following block administration was considered as the clinical reference standard for nerve block success. Data were cleaned and managed in Microsoft excel and analyzed using SPSS V23 and R (packages: lme4, pROC, boot, epiR). Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) or median (IQR) as appropriate; categorical variables as counts and percentages. Distribution checks used histograms and the Kolmogorov Smirnov test. Non-normal variables were transformed or analyzed with nonparametric methods as indicated. Repeated measures of hemodynamic variables (baseline, 10, 20, 30, 40 min) were analyzed in a long format using linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for subject and fixed effects for time (categorical), block outcome (success/failure) and their interaction; Between-group comparisons of normally distributed data were analysed using the independent sample t-test. Within-group comparisons were done using paired t-test. Categorical variables were compared as counts and percentages using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Diagnostic performance of candidate index tests (percentage change from baseline in PI, PSV, EDV, Tmax, Tmean) was evaluated by ROC analysis. Percentage change in perfusion index at 20 min was prespecified as the primary timepoint, other timepoints were considered as exploratory. Optimal thresholds were identified using the Youden index (sensitivity +specifificity − 1). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Of 93 patients screened, 4 were excluded, and 89 were enrolled and analysed. PIP block was successful in 83 patients, but failure was reported in 6 (6.74%) patients (Fig. 2). Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were similar between successful and unsuccessful groups (Table 1).

Mean values of PSV, EDV, Tmax, Tmean and PI differed significantly between successful and unsuccessful block (Table 2). Area under the curve estimates for percentage change in hemodynamic parameters and perfusion index

Mean PSV, Tmax, Tmean and PI showed a significant rise at 20 min in the successful block group; EDV rose significantly at 40 min. ROC analysis using percentage change from baseline at these timepoints yielded apparent AUROCs of 0.92 (95% CI[0.84, 0.99]) for PI, 0.86 (95% CI[0.74, 0.99]) for PSV percent change, and 0.80 (95%[0.66, 0.94)] for Tmax (Fig. 3). As AUROC values of more than/equal to 0.8 and 0.9 are considered to represent good and excellent outcomes respectively [7], hence our data suggested that percentage increase of PI, PSV and Tmax at 20 min after PIP block depicted excellent ability to differentiate successful and unsuccessful blocks.

Subjective assessments reached maximal effect later: median Hollman sensory score in the successful-block group reached 3.0 (IQR 2.0–3.0) at 30 min of successful block versus median score of 2 (IQR 2.0–2.0) in patients with failures. The motor response reached a median score of 2 (IQR 1.8-2.0) at 30 min following a successful block, whereas it did not increase beyond a median score of 1 (IQR 0.0–1.0) in unsuccessful block group.

Sensitivity, specificity and predictive values of hemodynamic changes and PI for block success

At the reported thresholds, the highest sensitivity observed was 79.5% for mean EDV at 40 min. Specificity and PPV reached 100% for mean PSV, Tmean and PI at 20 min; NPV ranged from 15% for percentage change in EDV at 40 min to 25% for percentage change in PI at 20 min (Table 3). These point estimates should be interpreted with caution given the small number of failed blocks (n = 6).

Discussion

It is important to have a reliable and efficient method of evaluating block success. In this study, we evaluated block success by using certain objective parameters, including popliteal artery regional hemodynamic variables (PSV, EDV, Tmax, Tmean) and perfusion index. ROC analysis of percentage change from baseline showed good discrimination at 20 min (apparent AUROCs: %ΔPI 0.92, %ΔPSV 0.86, %ΔTmax 0.80), and the derivation thresholds (~ 46.5% for PI, ~ 38.2% for PSV) yielded high apparent specificity and PPV in this cohort. These findings are consistent with prior reports that sympathetic blockade produces early increases in limb perfusion detectable by PI and Doppler, though earlier studies of distal or brachial plexus blocks often observed changes within 10 min. The later onset in our series plausibly reflects PIP being a proximal fascial‑plane block with variable spread and a thicker proximal nerve target, which may delay sympathetic and sensory effects.

However, cautious interpretation is warranted. First, six block failures occurred, so specificity, NPV and threshold performance are imprecise; point estimates (including 100% specificity/PPV) must be interpreted in light of wide exact confidence intervals. Second, thresholds were derived and tested on the same dataset without external validation; although internal validation (bootstrap) is recommended, external replication is required before clinical adoption. Third, all patients also received a femoral block and systemic factors (blood pressure, temperature, sedation) can influence PI and Doppler measures; these confounders may limit attribution of changes solely to PIP. Fourth, Doppler and PI measurements are operator‑dependent and we did not report intra/inter‑observer reliability. Finally, the clinical reference standard was subjective sensory/motor testing, and the ROC timepoint was chosen after inspecting when differences emerged, introducing potential bias.

In summary, percentage increases in popliteal PSV and PI at ~ 20 min after PIP block are promising objective indicators of block success, but these thresholds are exploratory. Confirmation in larger, blinded, multicenter studies—with pre‑specified primary indices, assessment of measurement reliability, control for systemic confounders and external validation of cutoffs—is needed before recommending routine clinical use.

This study evaluated objective, bedside measures (Doppler indices and perfusion index) as predictors of PIP block success. Significant increases in PSV, Tmax, Tmean and PI as early as 20 min foretold a successful block; EDV significantly increased at 40 min. Of all the evaluated objective parameters, percentage increase in PI and PSV had the greatest ability to discriminate between successful and unsuccessful blocks, with a specificity and positive predictive value reaching 100%. The optimal cut-off for percentage increase in PSV and PI, at 20 min, was 38.2% and 46.5% respectively for block success. Buono RD et al. [11], highlighted the utility of change in PI as a reliable predictor of popliteal sciatic nerve block success. Bereket et al. [2] investigated the use of PI and ultrasonographic regional hemodynamic changes for indicating infraclavicular block success. The authors reported significant differences in PI and regional hemodynamic variables at 10 min post-block. Lu B et al. [3] studied lower extremity perfusion indices by looking at anterior tibial artery(ATA) and posterior tibial artery(PTA) indices to predict success of popliteal sciatic nerve block. Significant changes in PI and PSV came at 10 min, contrary to our study, where thresholds were reached at 20 min. This difference likely reflects the PIP technique: as a fascial plane block, local anesthetic must diffuse medially to reach the sacral plexus, producing a slower and more variable onset. In addition, the PIP block targets the sciatic nerve proximally at the sciatic foramen, where the nerve trunk is larger than its distal branches in the popliteal fossa or nerves targeted at the infraclavicular level, which may further delay functional blockade. For example, Li J et al. [7], reported a 3.7 times increase in EDV within 5 min of an axillary block. By contrast, our most prominent early changes were of PSV and PI at 20 min, while EDV did not increase significantly until 40 min.

The changes in PSV and PI had high positive predictive value, suggesting that increase in these parameters following PIP block was associated with a successful block. However, their low negative predictive value indicates that the absence of any measurable increase in the hemodynamic variables, following the block, does not reliably predict block failure. Low negative predictive value may be due to technical variability in doppler measurements or physiological differences in sympathetic response. Thus, while changes in regional hemodynamic variables may serve as a useful adjunct to confirming block success, they should not replace the clinical sensory and motor assessment.

We compared objective parameters against the commonly used clinical reference standard of subjective sensory and motor testing, and found that the percentage increase of PI and PSV at 20 min discriminated successful from unsuccessful PIP blocks, with a high accuracy in this cohort (positive predictive value of 100%). However, because thresholds were derived and tested in the same small dataset, the performance estimates are only exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

Objective measures increased earlier (at 20 min) than median sensory motor changes (30 min), consistent with a faster onset of sympathetic block than a somatic block effect. Early detection of block success/failure using objective metrics could allow for more timely clinical interventions in uncooperative trauma patients and decrease intraoperative time, in case of block failure. We used percentage change (from baseline) in hemodynamic parameters instead of the actual value because relative change better accounts for inter-individual baseline variability.

The popliteal artery was chosen for Doppler because of its superficial, easily accessible location although the femoral block and systemic factors can influence downstream blood flow (the popliteal artery being a continuation of the femoral artery). However, the popliteal artery receives its sympathetic innervation mainly via the sciatic nerve, so observed changes likely reflect PIP-mediated sympathetic blockade. PI was measured at the big toe based on prior literature reports of early distal temperature and perfusion changes after sciatic nerve block [3].

Our study had a few limitations. Firstly, our results exclude preexisting peripheral vascular disease, so the findings may not generalize to that population. Secondly, being single-center, limits external applicability owing to differences in patient mix, operator skill and equipment. Thirdly, measurement (operator dependence of Doppler/PI and unreported inter/intra-observer reliability) and potential differences in block technique or equipment sensitivity could influence results. Fourthly, we assessed only short-term outcomes (up to 40-minutes) and did capture delayed complications, analgesia duration or patient satisfaction, Finally, the small number of failed blocks (n = 6/89) makes sensitivity, NPV and threshold estimates imprecise.

Conclusion

In this cohort, a 38.2% rise in popliteal PSV and 46.5% rise in PI, were the greatest discriminators of clinically assessed PIP block success. Thet can serve as a useful adjunctive tool for confirming block success alongwith the subjective sensory and motor assessment. However, these thresholds were derived and tested in the same, small dataset and should be considered exploratory. Internal validation and external replication in larger, blinded multi-center studies – incorporating reliability assessment and control for confounders – are required before routine clinical adoption.
