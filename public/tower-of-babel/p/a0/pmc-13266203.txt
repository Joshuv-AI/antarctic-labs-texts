Neural focalization of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the inferior parietal lobule is associated with inhibitory control performance in adolescence
Authors: Wesley J. Meredith, Joāo F. Guassi Moreira, Adriana Méndez Leal, Natalie Saragosa-Harris, Emilia Ninova, Yael Waizman, Elizabeth Gaines, Katherine H. Karlsgodt, Jennifer A. Silvers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adolescence is a period of marked cognitive development, especially for inhibitory control—the ability to inhibit prepotent responses in accordance with one’s goals. Neural specialization is speculated to undergird adolescent cognition, where neural activity within a region becomes spatially focalized and less variable while handling multiple cognitive functions. However, quantifying this kind of focalization is difficult with univariate neuroimaging methods. The present study quantifies neural focalization underlying inhibitory control using Gini coefficients, an adapted multivariate method that measures neural focalization. We replicated non-linear, age-related behavioral stabilization of inhibitory control around mid-adolescence in a sample of 49 adolescents aged 9–22 years (mean =15.3 years, SD = 3.7 years; 49% female). Neural specialization was estimated from task-related neural activity within and between pre-specified regions of dorsal anterior cingulate (dACC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), insula, and inferior parietal lobule (IPL). After accounting for age, individuals with greater specialization (more focalization) in subregions of DLPFC demonstrated better behavioral performance. Gini coefficients within dACC and IPL were consistently and positively correlated with task performance, but not age. These results suggest neural focalization is an important neurodevelopmental feature that facilitates successful inhibitory control during adolescence over and above chronological age.

Introduction

Cognitive control skills improve throughout adolescence and support an individual’s ability to plan, update, and prioritize actions in day-to-day life (Diamond, 2013, Gyurkovics et al., 2020, Kim-Spoon et al., 2021, Luna et al., 2010, Roalf et al., 2014). While univariate neuroimaging methods have revealed age-related differences in activation of prefrontal regions involved in cognitive control during adolescence (Bunge et al., 2002, Rubia et al., 2007), some studies have reported positive age-activation associations while others have reported negative associations (Crone and Dahl, 2012, Crone and Steinbeis, 2017). These inconsistencies may reflect inter-individual variability in how cognitive control skills are tuned via experience-dependent experimentation and feedback during adolescence (Marek et al., 2015, Murty et al., 2016, Sherman et al., 2014), such that neural specialization begets cognitive control gains in unique ways across individuals (Baum et al., 2020, Luna et al., 2015; L. B. Smith and Thelen, 2003). Leveraging a novel neuroimaging analytic technique, the present research sought to formally test whether adolescent neural specialization is positively associated with cognitive control abilities.

Inhibitory control—the ability to suppress goal-irrelevant responses—is a cognitive control skill that develops dramatically during adolescence and predicts emotional health (Rodrigues et al., 2021, Simonds et al., 2007) and well-being across the lifespan (Lee and Chao, 2012). When adolescents practice inhibitory control, their efforts promote improvements in inhibitory control skills (Braver, 2012, Crone and Steinbeis, 2017, Dwyer et al., 2014, Insel et al., 2019, Lannoy et al., 2022, Murty et al., 2016). With age, intra-individual variability in inhibitory control performance decreases (Kim-Spoon et al., 2021), and adolescents generally perform at adult-like levels by mid-adolescence (Diamond, 2013). However, inhibitory control maturation trajectories vary depending on measurement domain (e.g., motor tasks vs. caregiver questionnaires; Atherton et al., 2020; Theodoraki et al., 2020), motivational contexts (e.g., monetary or social incentives; Bowers et al., 2021; Sharp et al., 2022), and task demands (e.g., rule sets, proactive vs. reactive control; Andrews-Hanna et al., 2011; Velanova et al., 2008, Velanova et al., 2009). Normative declines in intra-individual variability in inhibitory control performance may reflect the developing brain selecting and strengthening neural pathways to optimize behavioral performance (i.e., specializing). This comports with broader evidence that better task performance in adolescence across a variety of domains, including emotion regulation and memory tasks, is subserved by more specialized neural activity (Calabro et al., 2025, Callaghan et al., 2021, Finn et al., 2010, Guassi Moreira et al., 2019, Thorp et al., 2022). Based on existing findings in the neuroscience literature, we propose specialization could be operationalized as the experience-driven refinement of neural activity subserving a particular cognitive function. This specification of specialization is supported by evidence demonstrating normative increases in the precision and flexibility of engaging task-relevant activation within brain regions and across functional networks (M. H. Johnson, 2011; Luna et al., 2015; Ravindranath et al., 2022). In adolescence, specialization may be supported across multiple levels of organization—from well-established molecular and cellular changes in dopaminergic signaling and Hebbian learning processes, to newer but promising findings on potential network-level remapping and refinement of within-network communication (Calabro et al., 2025; C. Johnson and Wilbrecht, 2011; Juraska and Willing, 2017). For example, in seminal work in the human cognitive control literature, Durston et al., (2006) found that across development, shifts from diffuse to focalized prefrontal recruitment supported mature behavioral performance in young adults relative to adolescents. These seminal findings also suggest that indirect measures of neural activity like the blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) signal in human neuroimaging studies are adequately equipped to characterize neurodevelopmental specialization that aligns with more results published from direct neural recordings in the non-human animal literature.

Refinement of local and network-level deployment of prefrontal cortex and other task-relevant regions appears to supports developmentally normative decreases in the effort required to engage cognitive control skills (Velanova et al., 2008, Velanova et al., 2009). While this suggests that adolescent inhibitory control development is likely supported by neural specialization vis-á-vis neural focalization, specialization need not be synonymous with rigid modularity. Here, we focus on within-region specialization and seek to quantitatively test whether focalized neural activity supports successful inhibitory control performance. Within this scope, we posit that specialization is achieved by subregions of the brain most essential for performing a given task becoming increasingly activated within smaller clusters of voxels, while subregions less essential for performing a given task becoming decreasingly activated in tandem.

In the present study, we used behavioral and fMRI measures to characterize inhibitory control in 51 adolescents who completed the Flanker inhibitory control task (Kopp et al., 1996). First, we replicated prior behavioral findings demonstrating that performance nonlinearly stabilizes around middle adolescence. Second, we leveraged Gini coefficients – a tool from macroeconomics (Pyatt, 1976) – to summarize activation focalization and test the hypothesis that neural specialization in key prefrontal and parietal brain regions is associated with adolescent inhibitory control performance. Previous implementations of the Gini coefficient approach have demonstrated its capacity to characterize local spatial focalization of task-elicited neural activity in task-positive regions (Guassi Moreira et al., 2019), as well as distributional variability in Euclidean distances between task-positive and task-negative voxels across functional networks (Leech et al., 2014). Implicit in the within-region approach presented here and by Guassi Moreira et al. (2019) is the assumption that increasingly focal patterns of activation within task-positive brain regions reflect specialization and correspond to better task performance. The Gini coefficient presents itself as a useful means of quantifying focalization within a predefined ROI that allows for inter-individual variability in which specific voxels within the ROI support task performance. In this way, the Gini coefficient offers a scale-free measure of focalization that is not dependent on any particular voxel within an ROI being more or less active across participants.

Materials and methods

Overview

For the current study, we hypothesized that neural specialization—operationalized as neural focalization—would track with better task performance in adolescents, independent of chronological age effects. To examine this, we analyzed adolescents’ behavioral and neuroimaging data from a Flanker task in a series of linear models. We first aimed to replicate the finding that behavioral performance on inhibitory control tasks stabilizes around middle adolescence. Using the task fMRI data, we next derived Gini coefficients to characterize the degree of neural specialization within our regions of interest (ROIs). This was done at two levels of granularity: coefficients derived from whole ROI activation and separately from multiple, non-overlapping spherical masked “packed” within each ROI (referred to as our sub-ROI analysis). Because Gini coefficients have only been implemented in this way once before (Guassi Moreira et al., 2019), we sought to examine focalization at multiple levels to test the specificity of brain-behavior relationships at different scales.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

Sample

Data from 51 typically developing adolescents (9–23 years; Mean = 15.3 years) were used to examine behavioral inhibitory control performance and neural focalization. Participants were invited to come in for the baseline neuroimaging data collection period for a longitudinal study investigating the impact of early life adversity in the form of institutional orphanage care on socioemotional development (Guassi Moreira et al., 2021, Rosenberg et al., 2025). Data used in the current set of analyses came from typically developing comparison (never-adopted) youth. For participants younger than 18 years, caregivers provided their written, informed consent for their children to participate, and those youth provided their written, informed assent. Participants 18 years or older provided their own written, informed consent to participate. Prior to the MRI session, participants were screened for any contraindications, such as metallic implants (including braces), likely or confirmed pregnancy, medications known to impact cerebral blood flow, and claustrophobia or fear of confined spaces. Participants were compensated $50 for completing the scanning session. The study protocol was approved by the University of California, Los Angeles Institutional Review Board and operated under the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Flanker Task

Participants completed the Flanker task (Kopp et al., 1996) as a behavioral index of inhibitory control ability while undergoing MRI. During the task, participants indicate the direction an arrow in the center of the screen points (left, right) on a series of trials. On congruent trials, the center arrow is flanked by arrows pointing in the same direction. On incongruent trials, the center arrow is flanked by arrows pointing in the opposite direction. Participants utilize inhibitory control to suppress the goal irrelevant information from the flanking arrows to respond accurately on incongruent trials. Participants completed one run of the Flanker task, consisting of 40 congruent and 40 incongruent trials presented in a randomized order. Each trial began with a jittered presentation of a fixation cross in the center of the screen, drawn from an exponential distribution ranging from 1000 and 9871 ms duration (Mean = 2487.04 ms; SD = 1983.46 ms), followed by a congruent or incongruent array of arrows, presented for 1000 ms. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly as possible once the arrows appeared, and responses timed out after the 1000 ms presentation of the trial. MRI acquisition

Data were acquired using a 3.0 Tesla Siemens Prisma MRI scanner with a 32-channel head coil at the UCLA Ahmanson-Lovelace Brain Mapping Center. During the scan, participants completed a series of tasks using a button press box affixed to their right hand. Structural T1-weighted, magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) images were acquired first for all participants (repetition time (TR) = 2400 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.22 ms, flip angle = 8°, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm2, 208 slices, 0.8 mm3 isotropic voxel size). For the Flanker task, functional T2*-weighted images were acquired (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 75°, FOV = 192 mm2, 33 axial slices, voxel size = 3x3x4 mm, 215 volumes).

Behavioral analyses

Quantifying behavioral performance on Flanker task

Behavioral performance on the Flanker task was quantified by combining both accuracy and reaction time in accordance with the NIH Toolbox Flanker task scoring (Weintraub et al., 2013). Each participant’s accuracy score was calculated using the total number of correct trials (40 congruent trials, 40 incongruent trials) and normalized such that all values were bounded between 0 and 5. Next, a reaction time score was derived from the median reaction time for correct incongruent trials that are greater than or equal to 100 ms and no larger than three standard deviations from the individual’s mean reaction time. Median reaction times were logarithmically scaled to normalize the distribution between 0 and 5. Methods related to Hypothesis 1: Behavioral performance on inhibitory control tasks stabilizes around middle adolescence.

Our first aim in the current study was to replicate prior work showing behavioral stabilization of task performance around middle adolescence. To estimate the nonlinear relationship between task performance and chronological age, we defined a continuous piecewise linear model in R using the base stats package (R Core Team, 2020). This piecewise approach was selected in lieu of other more optimal nonlinear modeling approaches like generalized additive models due to power constraints within our sample. The inflection point, or “knot,” for the piecewise age effect was set to 15.27 years for the chronological age variable using the segmented package in R (Muggeo, 2008), a data-driven, iterative process that minimizes model error when inserting a provided number of breaking points for the estimated slope(s). Here, the segmented function iteratively estimated task behavior from participants’ chronological age by inserting an inflection point to define two slopes, one for associations between age and task performance before the knot and associations between age and task performance after the knot value. The age value for the knot is selected by comparisons of the model error estimates for each iterated pair of piecewise linear associations. In subsequent analyses utilizing chronological age, we instead express a quadratic age term in our models to capture this non-linear association while limiting the effects of the multicollinearity of our age terms. fMRI analyses

fMRI Pre-Processing

Prior to analysis, MRI data were visually screened for anatomical abnormalities and artifacts. All participants met inclusion criteria based on head motion, having fewer than 20% of volumes with more than.9 mm displacement during the run. For all participants, approximately 2.68 volumes exceeded the framewise displacement threshold on average (SD = 6.31). Functional MRI data were preprocessed using the fMRI Expert Analysis Tool (FEAT; version 6.0) of the FMRIB Software Library package (FSL, version 5.0.9, fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk). First, brain tissue extraction was conducted using the brain extraction tool (BET; S. M. Smith, 2002), followed by spatial realignment to account for head motion using MCFLIRT (Jenkinson et al., 2002). Finally, data were high pass filtered (100 s cutoff). Functional data were not spatially smoothed because doing so would render Gini coefficients uninterpretable. Following preprocessing, we created general linear models in FSL comprised of task regressors for congruent and incongruent trials, as well as the incongruent-congruent contrast. Motion parameters, their derivatives, and the squares of the parameters and derivatives were included as nuisance regressors (24 total). For the analyses described below, we used the incongruent-congruent contrast to index inhibitory control.

Defining regions of interest

Brain areas associated with inhibitory control were sourced from the Neurosynth (Yarkoni et al., 2011) association map for keyword “inhibition,” generated from 601 contributing studies. Notably, the studies contributing to the “inhibition” association map necessarily includes multiple task paradigms and target populations of all ages within the studies comprising the meta-analysis. We opted for meta-analytically defined ROIs from Neurosynth because our limited sample could not accommodate independently derived ROIs from a subset of task-related neural activation within our own sample. Neurosynth maps also allow us to test our hypotheses about neural focalization against a priori regions defined across MRI scanner, task designs, and other study-specific idiosyncrasies.

Neurosynth association maps provide Z-score estimates of cortical activation using a two-way ANOVA to test against non-zero voxel-wise activation for a term of interest. The association map was thresholded at Z = 3.1 using FSL’s cluster function, and the remaining clusters composed of 75 voxels or more were used in subsequent analyses. The cluster size threshold was implemented to ensure comparable units of comparison across regions of interest. Four right-lateralized clusters spanning regions of the dorsal anterior cingulate (dACC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), insula, and inferior parietal lobule (IPL) emerged (Fig. 2, left). The IPL region included portions of the supramarginal and angular gyri. Methods related to Hypothesis 2: Neural focalization measured by Gini coefficients tracks with Flanker performance over and above age.

We were primarily interested in testing whether neural specialization—operationalized as focalization—tracked with adolescent task performance. In order to quantify the degree of focalization within our ROIs, we utilized the Gini coefficient, a measure originally developed for macroeconomics research (Pyatt, 1976). The Gini coefficient measures the concentration of some quantified unit (here, neural activation) across a given region. Coefficient scores are bound between zero and one, where scores closer to zero reflect greater dispersion of neural activity across the region, and scores closer to one reflect greater concentration of activity within a smaller subset of voxels. Larger Gini coefficients reflect more neural focalization, which we hypothesize is associated with better task performance. Importantly, prior work (Guassi Moreira et al., 2019) demonstrated that higher Gini coefficient values reflected a smaller number of voxels contributing to the overall activation and that these voxels were concentrated together rather than distributed throughout the ROI.

Due to the relative novelty of the Gini coefficient method with fMRI data, there remain open questions regarding the degree of granularity required to observe consistent brain-behavior relationships. As such, we computed Gini coefficients using parameter estimates in our ROIs at two levels of granularity: at the whole ROI level and also in equivalently sized sub-ROI masks nested within each parent ROI. This was done to explore whether whole ROI Gini coefficients wash out meaningful local foci of neural activity driving focalization for inhibitory control within our ROIs. For the sub-ROI analysis, each ROI was decomposed into a series of non-overlapping, equivalently sized spherical masks (4 mm diameter) that were “packed” inside the region, starting at the center of gravity. Quantifying Gini Coefficients

Parameter estimates from the GLM were used to generate a vector of Z-statistic images for the incongruent-congruent contrast. Gini coefficients were derived for each whole ROI or sub-ROI. Voxelwise statistics for each ROI or sub-ROI were minimum centered to restrict the range of Gini coefficients between 0 and 1. From this vector of minimum-centered estimates, voxels were rank-ordered based upon the magnitude of activation and then used to generate Gini coefficients. In line with previously published methods translating the Gini coefficient derivation for functional MRI data (Guassi Moreira et al., 2019), a Gini coefficient was generated for each ROI or sub-ROI by summing the ranked activation parameters and scaling the activation by the number of voxels within the ROI or sub-ROI. Here, the Gini coefficient indexes the extent to which higher ranked (i.e., more active) voxels account for the total activation within an ROI or sub-ROI, while normalization procedures utilizing total activation information helps ensure coefficient derivation is not disproportionately driven by spuriously active voxels. Hypothesis 2A: Global neural focalization in ROIs tracks with task performance over and above age

In order to test whether neural focalization related to better inhibitory control task performance, we first looked at associations between whole ROI Gini coefficients and Flanker scores. Flanker composite scores were estimated from participants’ age and task-related neural activity in each ROI. Whole ROI Gini coefficients were submitted to a general linear model in R (R Core Team, 2020), alongside linear and nonlinear chronological age variables, to estimate brain-behavior relationships over and above the influence of age. Model selection was conducted using an additive approach, whereby model performance scores were compared across stepwise additions in complexity (i.e., inclusions of interaction terms) using AIC values and F statistics derived from analyses of variance (ANOVA). Hypothesis 2B: Granular neural focalization additionally tracks with task performance over and above age

In addition to the whole-ROI analyses performed to test Hypothesis 2A, we also examined whether more granular patterns of neural focalization within ROIs would track with inhibitory control task performance. To estimate the brain-behavior relationships between nested sub-ROI Gini coefficients within each parent ROI and Flanker composite scores, we utilized a hierarchical measurement model, as has been done in prior work (Guassi Moreira et al., 2019), using the lme4 and lmerTest packages in R (Bates et al., 2015, Kuznetsova et al., 2017). This specification was selected because it (1) allows for a within-participant idealized mean Gini coefficient value—removed of measurement error—to be derived for each Neurosynth ROI by including overparameterized dummy codes (i.e., the number of dummy codes matches the number of parent ROIs) that map each sub-ROI to its parent ROI; (2) allows for information about mean Gini coefficient values to be encoded in slopes for estimated relationships due to the lack of intercept (i.e., β0); and (3) can use interaction effects to estimate ROI-specific relationships between latent Gini coefficient values and predictor variables (e.g., age, Flanker performance).

Flanker performance and linear chronological age variables were submitted to a hierarchical measurement model as predictors with Gini coefficients as the dependent variable. Four hyperparameterized dummy variables for each Neurosynth ROI (dACC, DLPFC, insula, IPL) mapped their respective sub-ROIs to their parent ROIs and provided an idealized mean Gini coefficient estimate for each ROI. Sensitivity Analyses: Assessing specificity of effects and robustness to motion.

Given the relative novelty of quantifying neural focalization using Gini coefficients, we conducted a sub-ROI analysis investigating the relationship between Gini coefficients and Flanker performance in two regions ostensibly unrelated to inhibitory control performance: the fusiform gyrus (FFA) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). We additionally examined whether Gini coefficients were disproportionately susceptible to head motion by submitting framewise displacement (FD) measures to partial correlations. The motivations for each sensitivity analysis and greater details on their methods are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Supplemental univariate analyses

Additional univariate whole-brain and ROI analyses were conducted to compare brain-behavior relationships across traditional neuroimaging methods and the Gini coefficient approach. Descriptions of the univariate methods and results are described in the Supplemental Materials.

Hypothesis 1: Behavioral performance on inhibitory control tasks stabilizes around middle adolescence

In line with prior findings, adolescent performance on the Flanker task showed a non-linear relationship with chronological age. Specifically, age was associated with improved performance until around middle adolescence, such that increases in age before 15.27 years were associated with a greater increase in behavioral performance compared to increases in age for individuals older than 15.27 years (ßage ≤ 15.27 = 4.81, p < 0.001; ßage ≥ 15.27 = 0.32, p = 0.03). Fig. 4B shows the plotted piecewise function estimating Flanker scores from chronological age. The attenuated relationship between age and performance after 15.27 years-old suggests that behavioral performance on inhibitory control tasks begins to stabilize by mid-adolescence. The piecewise model explained approximately 32% of the variance in behavioral performance (R2adj = 0.32) and was a better fit compared to a traditional linear model estimating behavioral performance from age, F(2,46) = 12.26, p < 0.001). Full model estimates are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Correlational associations between Gini coefficients, age, and task performance

While primary hypotheses revolved around our models that simultaneously controlled for age and performance, in the interest of transparency, here we report uncorrected correlations (i.e., correlations between age and Gini coefficients that do not account for task effects, and correlations between task performance and Gini coefficients that do not account for age effects) between Gini coefficients and age as well as Gini coefficients and task performance. Age and task performance were both negatively correlated with the whole ROI Gini coefficients in dACC (rage= −0.28, rtask= −0.14; ps < .05) but positively correlated in one sub-ROI of dACC (rage= 0.33, rtask= 0.38; ps < .05). Outside of the dACC, whole-ROI IPL Gini values were positively correlated with both age and performance (rage= 0.35, rtask= 0.23; ps < .05), and both whole-ROI and sub-ROI DLPFC Gini values were positively correlated with task performance (rtask = [0.16, 0.38]; ps < .05), but negatively correlated with age (rage= −0.21; p < .05). All other relationships for other whole- and sub-ROIs were non-significant. Descriptive statistics for whole ROI and sub-ROI Gini coefficient values—including their correlational relationships with age and performance—are described further in the Supplementary Materials.

Hypothesis 2: Neural focalization tracks with Flanker performance over and above age

We examined relationships between neural focalization (Gini coefficients), chronological age, and Flanker performance at two levels of granularity: the whole ROI and nested sub-ROIs within each parent ROI. For both levels of analysis, we tested piecewise associations between Gini coefficients and inhibitory control performance, after controlling for nonlinear age effects. Model fit and comparison statistics for whole ROI and nested sub-ROI models are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

Hypothesis 2A: Whole ROI neural focalization in IPL tracks with task performance over and above age

Our first brain-behavior analyses examined whether whole ROI Gini coefficients were related to task performance and revealed region-specific relationships. A significant, positive relationship was initially observed between Gini coefficients in the IPL ROI and Flanker performance, (βIPL = 4.37, p = 0.02), such that participants with a one-point higher standardized Gini score are expected to show an approximate 4-point higher Flanker composite score compared to an individual with a one-point lower standardized Gini score. However, this association did not survive multiple comparisons correction (padj = 0.08). Importantly, this result was obtained while controlling for chronological age and the standardized Gini values from other ROIs. To better characterize the relationship between the IPL Gini coefficients and Flanker performance, we subsequently examined the data without controlling for chronological age. When age was removed from the equation, the relationship between IPL Gini coefficients and behavioral performance was non-significant (βIPL = 2.33, p = 0.29), suggesting a suppressor effect such that the inclusion of the nonlinear age variables explained more residual variance and thus helped strengthen the statistical relationship between IPL Gini coefficients and Flanker performance. IPL Gini coefficients were positively associated with Flanker composite scores (r = 0.16, p = 0.28, 95% CI [−0.13, 0.42]) but negatively associated with chronological age (r = -.21, p = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.47, 0.07]). Since the overall age-related change in IPL coefficient values trended negative, this may point toward the overall nonlinear age-related effect in behavior and performance, whereby the relationship between Gini coefficient values and performance is reduced as youth transition into middle adolescence. Moreover, the reported relationship between IPL focalization and task performance did not survive multiple comparisons correction. Together, the suppressor effect and the non-significant association between IPL and performance could reflect either a small and unreliable effect or the absence of an effect altogether, though further interrogation in a larger sample is needed to determine which is more likely true.

Hypothesis 2B: Granular neural focalization in DLPFC relates to higher Flanker composite scores

To account for the possibility that neural focalization for inhibitory control might instantiate at more granular spatial patterns of activation, we next focused on nested sub-ROIs within each parent ROI using a hierarchical measurement model (c.f., Guassi Moreira et al., 2019). This specification required us to estimate Gini coefficient values using age and Flanker composite scores as predictors in the model, instead of specifying Flanker scores as the outcome like the whole ROI model. By including interaction terms with the dummy variables for each ROI in our measurement model, we could estimate relationships between age, Flanker performance, and region-specific latent Gini coefficients for a given ROI.

A significant, positive association was observed between Flanker performance and Gini coefficient values in the DLPFC sub-ROIs (βperf x DLPFC = 0.02, p = 0.003). Specifically, while controlling for chronological age, individuals with higher Flanker composite scores one-point higher displayed higher Gini coefficient values in DLPFC sub-ROIs relative to participants with lower Flanker scores. This suggests that greater local spatial focalization within areas of the DLPFC tracked with better performance on the Flanker task. Gini coefficients outperform traditional univariate statistics for estimating inhibitory control behavior

Because the univariate and whole cluster Gini coefficients were extracted from the same cortical regions, direct comparison of univariate methods and Gini coefficients were deemed most appropriate by using the whole cluster Gini coefficients. Model performance comparisons between traditional univariate methods and the Gini analysis revealed that Gini coefficients explained more variance in Flanker performance scores compared to univariate whole ROI Z-statistics. Additional univariate data and full results from comparisons between methods are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Sensitivity analyses for Hypothesis 2: Associations between Gini coefficients and performance are regionally specific and are robust against head motion

Across two sets of sensitivity analyses provide supporting evidence that the reported Gini associations with behavioral performance are region-specific, meaning that coefficients derived in task-unrelated regions like the FFA and mPFCdo not track with performance. Discussion

The current study used a novel Gini coefficient method as a potential means to quantify a scale-free measure of neural focalization as a candidate neural feature likely supporting adolescent inhibitory control performance. Under this framework, we provide preliminary support for the utilization of the Gini coefficient as a multivariate summary score of task-related focalization. Across two levels of analysis (within and between ROIs), we observed that greater focalization – thought to reflect specialization – of prefrontal and parietal regions conferred better inhibitory control performance during adolescence. The whole ROI analysis revealed that greater focalization in the IPL related to superior inhibitory control performance, suggesting that clustered activation within the IPL supports inhibitory control performance. We additionally found that local patterns of focalization in DLPFC sub-ROIs related to better behavioral performance on the Flanker task. Importantly, both effects were observed while controlling for age-effects. Together, these results demonstrate that neural focalization may be an important feature of inhibitory control specialization, whereby spatial topographies of neural activity across different scales mature toward fewer, more concentrated regions of interest support maturation of inhibitory. The results of the current study not only highlight and extend prior work identifying adolescence as an important developmental period for inhibitory control development, but also provides a unique multivariate framework that could potentially be leveraged to better understand neural specialization in other cognitive domains.

Middle adolescence is a key developmental period for inhibitory control

Our results provide compelling evidence that adolescence is a particularly important period for inhibitory control neurodevelopment. We observed steep age-related improvements with inhibitory control performance prior to 15.27 years, but not after, replicating prior findings showing behavioral stabilization during middle adolescence (Fukushima et al., 2000, Klein and Foerster, 2001, Luna, 2009, Ordaz et al., 2013). Behavioral stabilization may reflect maturation of task-related systems as well as other cognitive resources required to maintain, update, and coordinate task states. Stronger relationships between chronological age and behavioral performance for younger adolescents may reflect improvements in the reliable deployment of task-relevant inhibitory control skills (Luna, 2009), or track broader developmental improvements in coordinating cognitive and motor skills to achieve goals (Davidow et al., 2018, Harris and Thiele, 2011). Notably, we report a large effect size (F2=0.57) for linear, age-related associations in the whole ROI model and smaller effect sizes for nonlinear age associations across whole and sub-ROI models (F2s = 0.12, 0.01). The effect sizes we report here suggest that age effects on behavior are more robust and consistent across individuals than are the precise neural mechanisms supporting these effects (i.e., Gini coefficients). One potential interpretation for this could be that while most individuals follow a consistent age-related pattern of improved inhibitory control behavior, the exact ROIs (or sub-ROIs) that support task performance vary slightly between individuals and our methods did not capture this variability. These effect sizes warrant further investigation of nonlinear age associations within larger samples, as discussed in further detail in our future direction recommendations.

Neural focalization tracks with better inhibitory control performance

As hypothesized, we found that greater neural focalization, indexed by Gini coefficients, may help explain developmental shifts in inhibitory control task performance. It is noteworthy that this was observed specifically in brain regions previously identified as drivers of age-related differences in inhibitory control performance from univariate analyses in children (Bunge et al., 2002, Durston et al., 2002). In our whole ROI analysis, greater focalization in IPL, but not other ROIs, related to higher Flanker composite scores over and above chronological age. This relationship did not survive multiple comparisons corrections, however. Within our sub-ROI analysis, better behavioral performance related to greater focalization within nested DLPFC spheres, while also accounting for chronological age effects. These results further extend prior work demonstrating the role of neural specialization in emotion regulation ability during adolescence (Guassi Moreira et al., 2019). Moreover, sensitivity analyses suggest that neural focalization in task-irrelevant regions like the fusiform gyrus do not relate to Flanker performance. Taken together, these results provide evidence that the Gini coefficient could serve as a promising measure to characterize local spatial properties of neural activity related to task performance—a potentially important neurodevelopmental milestone for cognitive maturation. However, these preliminary findings necessitate examining the reliability of focalization effects in larger, more robust samples. While the preliminary findings demonstrate that neural focalization positively relates to task performance, reported whole and sub-ROI results carry small effect sizes (F2s = 0.01–0.13), suggesting that replication will help determine the meaningfulness of these associations in more robust and representative samples.

Despite the developmental relevance for regions where we found positive associations between neural focalization and task performance (IPL, DLPFC), interaction terms for Gini coefficients and chronological age (both linear and non-linear age regressors) did not reach statistical significance in either model. This potentially bolsters support for neural focalization as an experience-dependent process as opposed to an age-related maturational process, however current limitations preclude making strong interpretations regarding these null results. Larger samples, especially from longitudinal designs, will be better suited to examine nonlinear trends in neural focalization. Future work utilizing more robust samples may also elucidate heterogeneous correlational results we report in the Supplementary Materials, whereby positive and negative pairwise associations between Gini coefficient values and chronological age emerge across whole or sub-ROIs. Notably, this heterogeneity also bolsters support for nonlinear age-related associations better captured in our piecewise approach. Given evidence that functional specialization within the prefrontal cortex supporting adolescent inhibitory control is uniquely related to pubertal stage (Ravindranath et al., 2022), future work might formally examine whether neural focalization in task-relevant regions map more closely onto pubertal processes rather than chronological age. These associations would further suggest that neural focalization as described here might serve as a useful indirect measure of local functional specialization features within a given region.

We additionally provide preliminary evidence that Gini coefficients may characterize spatial features of neural activity that potentially support specialization at different scales across brain regions, such that whole ROI findings were observed in IPL and more localized patterns of specialization were observed in DLPFC. One interpretation of these findings is that DLPFC and IPL support different task demands and thus require different levels of specialization. This is consistent with prior work that demonstrates dissociable contributions of brain regions, like the DLPFC and ACC, to components of task features for inhibitory control paradigms (MacDonald et al., 2000, Tamm et al., 2002). Another possibility is that brain regions uniformly develop toward some optimal spatial pattern across regions. For example, focalization within-region might first instantiate at a macro (i.e., whole ROI) level and subsequently develop toward more granular focalization (i.e., sub-ROIs within a region). This possibility echoes work showing regional specialization for executive functions borne through increased structural and functional coupling, which enables greater network efficiency and information flow (Baum et al., 2017, Baum et al., 2020). Given the cross-sectional design of our current study, we are unable to test these competing developmental trajectories of neural focalization but encourage future longitudinal studies to test both possibilities. Notably, the IPL was the largest ROI used in our neuroimaging analyses and the only region that demonstrated a positive association with performance in our whole ROI analysis before correcting for multiple comparisons. Because the IPL whole ROI coefficient was quantified from a larger area (i.e., more voxels), it more likely washes out meaningful variation across all the voxels within the defined perimeter. These IPL ROI features could help explain both the observed suppressor effect and the non-significance of the association following multiple comparisons corrections. Focalization in the IPL ostensibly supports inhibitory control performance, however the limitations of our sample and design discussed in the following section highlight the need for a more rigorous examination of these effects in larger, better equipped samples, and likely at smaller “whole” ROI scales.

Limitations and future directions

Given prior evidence suggesting that different inhibitory tasks require varied attentional and motor resources (MacDonald et al., 2000, Tamm et al., 2002), it will be critical to conceptually replicate the present findings across different inhibitory control tasks, and test the specificity of these findings using cognitive tasks that do not assess inhibitory control (particularly given that ROIs in the present analysis are relevant for other aspects of cognitive functioning). We currently demonstrate that Gini coefficients from regions related to higher-order cognitions outside the umbrella of cognitive control (FFA, mPFC) do not track with Flanker performance. However, additional sensitivity analyses in future work should also parse the specificity of focalization relationships within a broader suite of comparison regions. Doing so will strengthen our understanding of general versus task-specific effects and will also allow for further interrogation of levels of analysis relevant for neural specialization (e.g., whole ROIs, nested subregions). Future work will also benefit from examining neural focalization in larger, more representative samples (e.g., the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study; Luciana et al., 2018), that would not only increase confidence in brain-behavior relationships (Gratton et al., 2022, Marek et al., 2022), but also would facilitate testing contextual influences on inhibitory control neurodevelopment (Gunnar et al., 2007, Hostinar et al., 2012; D. Johnson et al., 2021; Micalizzi et al., 2019; Sheridan et al., 2017). Moreover, the longitudinal design of datasets like the ABCD sample are equipped to examine the reliability of the Gini coefficient and its relationships to task performance within-participants across multiple runs (the present data only have one run per participant) and timepoints alongside generalizability in comparable discovery and replication subsamples of the dataset. Indices of reliability and generalizability stand to inform suggestions for best practices in defining units of measurement (e.g., ROI size) for quantifying Gini coefficients. Robust, longitudinal samples like the ABCD dataset increase the statistical power and capability of gold-standard approaches to modeling nonlinear maturational effects (e.g., generalized additive models, as in Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023).

For the current analysis, we focus on a within-region operationalization of neural specialization, measuring spatial focalization of voxelwise activity in task-positive regions. Our a-priori ROI approach necessarily limits the scope of our analysis to a set of predefined regions, which precludes making strong claims about adolescent neural specialization in excluded brain regions ostensibly unrelated to Flanker task performance (though our two pre-selected “control” analyses in the mPFC and FFA were null), as well as network-level specialization principles. Contemporary theoretical frameworks nest the concept of specialization within a more dynamic developmental process, whereby experience promotes flexible deployment of network-level activity across multiple contexts (e.g., M. H. Johnson, 2011). Future work in larger samples (e.g., ABCD Study) could take a more data-driven approach to relate local spatial properties to common multivariate measures of functional network properties (Farahani et al., 2019; e.g., clustering, efficiency; Sporns, 2018).

Conclusion

The present study provides preliminary evidence that neural focalization, as indexed by Gini coefficients, is a critical neurodevelopmental ingredient for inhibitory control performance. Brain-behavior relationships were observed at both micro- and macro-level estimates of neural focalization and may motivate future lines of inquiry examining how neural specialization may unfold in part as a function of experience-related neural focalization during adolescence.
