Comparative assessment of quality, consistency, and reference accuracy of MIH-related clinical information generated by ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek R1
Authors: Zübeyde Uçar Gündoğar, Derya Sarıoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMolar incisor hypomineralization (MIH) is a clinically challenging developmental enamel defect that requires accurate diagnosis and nuanced management decisions. Although large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as sources of clinical information in dentistry, the quality, consistency, and reference accuracy of their MIH-related explanations remain largely unexplored. To date, no study has systematically compared the clinical quality or citation accuracy of LLM-generated MIH information. This study comparatively evaluated how two widely used LLMs generate clinical information across repeated sessions.MethodsTwenty open-ended MIH questions were developed in accordance with current clinical guidelines and organized into four categories: diagnosis, etiology, treatment, and differential diagnosis. ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek R1 were each prompted with all questions during three independent sessions (morning, afternoon, evening) on the same day, generating a total of 120 responses. All responses were anonymized and evaluated by 24 calibrated pediatric dentists using the five-point Global Quality Scale (GQS). References provided by the models were independently verified by two reviewers and categorized as real or fabricated. Statistical analyses included Shapiro–Wilk tests, paired t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, repeated-measures ANOVA, Friedman tests, Holm-corrected post-hoc comparisons, and ICC(2,1), with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAcross all time points and all four MIH-related categories, DeepSeek R1 consistently achieved significantly higher GQS scores than ChatGPT-4o (all adjusted p < 0.005). Mean score differences ranged from + 0.36 to + 0.71, with the largest gap observed for etiology questions in the evening session. When overall scores were examined, DeepSeek R1 (4.44 ± 0.54) again outperformed ChatGPT-4o (3.99 ± 0.59) (t(23) = 11.83, p < 0.0001). Both models showed statistically significant but clinically small session-related variations, with acceptable reliability indicated by ICC values (0.72 for ChatGPT-4o; 0.77 for DeepSeek R1). Reference verification revealed notable fabrication rates in both models: ChatGPT-4o provided 46.5% real and 53.5% fake references, while DeepSeek R1 provided 34.2% real and 65.8% fake references.ConclusionsDeepSeek R1 and ChatGPT-4o each demonstrated distinct strengths in generating MIH-related clinical explanations, with DeepSeek providing more detailed and context-focused responses and ChatGPT-4o producing clearer, more structured overviews. Although the score differences were modest, they reflect meaningful variations when applied to a condition as diagnostically complex as MIH. Both models showed acceptable temporal stability; however, their substantial rates of fabricated references underscore the need for careful expert oversight. Overall, while LLMs may support early learning, patient communication, or preliminary clinical orientation, neither model currently meets the accuracy or citation standards required for autonomous clinical use in pediatric dentistry.

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems designed to mimic human cognitive functions such as reasoning, learning, and decision-making. Recent advances in machine learning and natural language processing have enabled the development of more sophisticated AI models capable of analyzing complex datasets, producing contextually relevant outputs, and aiding decision-making across various professional fields [1, 2]. In dentistry, these tools are increasingly integrated into clinical workflows, supporting diagnostic interpretation, treatment planning, patient communication, and educational activities for both students and practitioners [2, 3]. Among these advances, large language models (LLMs) have gained particular attention for their ability to process natural language queries and produce coherent, context-aware explanations in real time. These models utilize deep learning transformer architectures and are trained on large publicly available text datasets, enabling them to understand complex questions and produce coherent, context-aware responses [4, 5].

LLMs are increasingly integrated into the daily work of clinicians, educators, and students in dentistry. Across various specialties, LLMs are utilized to address discipline-specific questions, support clinical reasoning, develop educational resources, and enhance patient communication by simplifying complex dental information [6–9]. ChatGPT, developed by OpenAI (2022), and DeepSeek R1, developed by a China-based consortium (2024), represent two widely adopted large language models built on transformer-based architectures. Although the exact architectural details of GPT-4o remain proprietary, it is broadly characterized as a dense model refined through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). In contrast, DeepSeek R1 utilizes a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework that selectively activates task-relevant expert modules, improving computational efficiency and enabling targeted performance in specific tasks [10, 11]. Despite their widespread use and promising applications, LLM-based chatbots are not free from limitations. Concerns persist about the accuracy and consistency of their output, the reliability of references cited, and their potential to produce fabricated or misleading information. These challenges have raised concerns about their reliability in healthcare contexts and led to studies evaluating their performance in various dental specialties. Nevertheless, their capacity to address the challenges specific to pediatric dentistry has yet to be thoroughly investigated [5, 12–15].

One of the most prevalent and diagnostically challenging conditions in pediatric dentistry is molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH), first defined by Weerheijm et al. in 2001 [16]. MIH typically affects at least one first permanent molar and often includes permanent incisors [17]. The global prevalence of MIH ranges from 0.6% to 46.6%, with a mean of approximately 15.5% [18]. The condition may arise from systemic disturbances during enamel maturation, influenced by factors such as early childhood illnesses, antibiotic use, perinatal complications, and environmental or genetic factors. However, no single etiology has been conclusively identified [19, 20]. A disruption in enamel mineralization during the maturation phase results in decreased mineral content and increased protein and water levels, leading to porous, discolored enamel [21]. According to the European Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (EAPD), diagnosis requires at least one affected first permanent molar exhibiting demarcated opacities, post-eruptive enamel breakdown, hypersensitivity, atypical restorations, or prior extraction due to MIH [19]. The condition presents both functional and aesthetic concerns, significantly affecting children’s oral health-related quality of life [22]. Although the EAPD has established standardized diagnostic criteria, diagnostic variability persists due to the heterogeneous clinical presentation of MIH and its clinical resemblance to other enamel defects, such as dental fluorosis and amelogenesis imperfecta.

Although previous studies have explored AI-based systems for image-driven MIH detection, no research has examined how LLM-based chatbots respond to clinician-focused, textual questions about MIH [23–25]. Some studies have demonstrated the utility of AI for image-based MIH detection; however, the clinical reliability of chatbot-generated textual content—particularly in diagnostic reasoning, etiology explanation, treatment planning, and citation integrity—remains unknown [26–28]. This gap is significant because dental students and clinicians increasingly rely on LLMs for quick access to explanations about diagnosis, etiology, differential diagnosis, treatment planning, and scientific references. Understanding whether these tools provide accurate and consistent information is essential before they can be considered reliable aids in pediatric dental education and clinical decision-making. Therefore, this study aims to comparatively evaluate the clinical reliability, consistency, and reference accuracy of two widely used LLM-based chatbot models, ChatGPT (GPT-4o) and DeepSeek R1 (Research Version 1), in their responses to standardized MIH-related questions. It is hypothesized that ChatGPT (GPT-4o) will provide more fluent and contextually appropriate narrative outputs, while DeepSeek R1 will deliver greater technical precision and citation accuracy. Additionally, no significant difference is expected between the models in terms of overall clinical reliability.

Materials and methods

Study design

This methodological comparative study aimed to assess the reliability, consistency, and reference accuracy of two AI chatbot models, ChatGPT (GPT-4o, OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) and DeepSeek R1 (DeepSeek Inc., Hangzhou, China), in generating responses to clinical questions concerning the diagnosis, etiology, treatment, and differential diagnosis of molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH). All chatbot interactions were conducted on a single day at the Faculty of Dentistry, Gaziantep University, Gaziantep, Turkey, using a stable, high-speed internet connection. Ethical approval was obtained from the Gaziantep University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (2025/229). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating evaluators. This study did not require clinical trial registration; therefore, a clinical trial number is not applicable.

Question development

A total of 20 open-ended questions were developed by two pediatric dentists with more than 10 years of clinical experience in MIH diagnosis and management. The questions were developed based on the most current clinical literature and the latest evidence-based guidelines published by the European Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (EAPD) [19] and the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD)[29]. Four clinical content categories, diagnosis, etiology, treatment, and differential diagnosis, were identified, and five questions were written for each category. To assess reference accuracy, each question concluded with the identical request: “Please list the references from which you obtained this information in APA format.” The full set of questions is presented in Table 1.

Chatbot response generation

Both chatbots were tested under controlled and standardized conditions. Each of the 20 questions was administered to each model across three independent sessions: morning (09:00–10:00), afternoon (14:00–15:00), and evening (20:00–21:00). This within-day repeated-measures design allowed assessment of short-term consistency. Each question was entered into a fully new chat window to prevent memory carryover, and all prompts were typed manually to ensure independence of responses. A total of 120 responses were generated (2 models × 20 questions × 3 sessions). All outputs—including references—were copied verbatim into Microsoft Word immediately following generation and subsequently imported into Microsoft Excel for coding and analysis. Each response was assigned a unique identifier, and metadata (model, timepoint, content category) were recorded.

Evaluator selection and calibration

Twenty-seven pediatric dentists from various universities and private clinics across Türkiye were invited to evaluate the chatbot-generated answers. Inclusion criteria required at least five years of professional experience in pediatric dentistry. A calibration procedure was performed before scoring: four pre-selected sample responses (one from each category) with pre-determined scoring values were embedded within the evaluation set. Evaluators whose scores did not reach an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of ≥ 0.80 in comparison with the reference scores were excluded. Following calibration, 24 evaluators met the threshold and were included in the study.

Scoring method and blinding

All chatbot responses were anonymized and labeled as “Answer 1” or “Answer 2” to keep raters blind to chatbot identity. Experts scored each answer using a five-point Likert scale adapted from the Global Quality Scale (GQS):º 1 = Very poor (inaccurate or misleading)º 2 = Poor (mostly incorrect, some correct elements)º 3 = Fair (partially correct, but lacks clarity or depth)º 4 = Good (correct but missing details)º 5 = Excellent (comprehensive and evidence-based).

Data management and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed on participant-level mean Global Quality Scale (GQS) scores for each model across the three daily sessions and four content category. For each evaluator, mean scores were first computed within each category and each session. These participant-level category means served as the units of analysis for all subsequent comparisons. The normality of score distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, supported by visual inspection of Q–Q plots and histograms. To compare the two chatbot models, paired t-tests were conducted for overall scores and within each session. Because Likert-type data may violate parametric assumptions, complementary non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were also performed. For comparisons across the three sessions within each model, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA and Friedman’s test were used as parametric and non-parametric approaches, respectively. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm correction to control the family-wise error rate. Intraclass correlation coefficients [ICC(2,1)] were calculated to assess the reliability and internal consistency of evaluator ratings across sessions. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 24 calibrated pediatric dentists from various universities and private clinics across Türkiye participated in the study. For each evaluator, mean Global Quality Scale (GQS) scores were computed within each clinical content category (Diagnosis, Etiology, Treatment, Differential Diagnosis) and each session (morning, afternoon, evening). These participant-level mean values were used as the units of analysis, yielding 24 paired observations per comparison. The participants’ mean age was 36.4 ± 4.2 years old (range: 30–45), of whom 14 (58.3%) were female, and 10 (41.7%) were male. The average clinical experience in pediatric dentistry was 9.8 ± 2.7 years. All included evaluators met the predefined calibration threshold prior to scoring.

The study included 20 standardized and clinically oriented questions distributed evenly across four main categories related to MIH: Diagnosis, Etiology, Treatment, and Differential Diagnosis (Table 2). Each category contained five questions (25%), providing equal representation across content categories for both chatbot models.

When examined across different times of day, this difference remained significant: in the morning (4.54 ± 0.49 vs. 4.12 ± 0.55; t(23) = 6.35, p < 0.0001), in the afternoon (4.36 ± 0.56 vs. 3.88 ± 0.63; t(23) = 8.11, p < 0.0001), and in the evening (4.48 ± 0.54 vs. 3.99 ± 0.57; t(23) = 7.42, p < 0.0001). Non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed the same results (all p < 0.0001).

As shown in Table 5, both models demonstrated small but statistically significant fluctuations in response quality across the three daily sessions. For ChatGPT-4o, scores were highest in the morning (4.12 ± 0.39), decreased in the afternoon (3.88 ± 0.45), and rose slightly again in the evening (3.99 ± 0.37). This variation was confirmed by repeated-measures ANOVA (F(2,46) = 9.10, p = 0.00047) and the Friedman test (χ²(2) = 21.78, p < 0.0001). DeepSeek R1 showed a similar pattern with smaller changes (morning 4.54 ± 0.46; afternoon 4.37 ± 0.48; evening 4.48 ± 0.37), also supported by significant ANOVA (F(2,46) = 4.58, p = 0.015) and Friedman results (χ²(2) = 11.86, p = 0.0027). ICC values indicated acceptable reliability for both models [ICC(2,1) = 0.72 for ChatGPT-4o; 0.77 for DeepSeek R1], with DeepSeek R1 showing slightly higher stability in evaluator scoring.

Discussion

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being incorporated into dental education and clinical decision-support systems; however, their performance in generating clinically reliable information for MIH remains insufficiently explored [30–32]. In this context, the present study examined the responses of two widely used LLMs, ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek R1, to MIH-related clinical questions, focusing on accuracy, internal consistency, and reference reliability as evaluated by expert reviewers. This study provides a comparative perspective on how large language models generate MIH-related clinical information. The results contribute to a broader understanding of both LLMs’ capabilities and current constraints in supporting dental education and clinical information needs. To our knowledge, this study is the first comparative evaluation of ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek R1 in generating MIH-related clinical information. Several studies have investigated deep learning models trained on standardized intraoral photographs to detect MIH and reported high accuracy in detecting MIH and differentiating it from other enamel defects [26–28]. However, these approaches focus exclusively on image-based recognition rather than on generating clinical knowledge.

Because MIH encompasses a broad range of clinical presentations and requires diverse knowledge for accurate assessment and management, evaluating chatbot performance meaningfully demands more than a single general question format. MIH covers a broad clinical range—from well-demarcated opacities to widespread post-eruptive breakdown—and its multifactorial cause is only partly understood. Diagnostic judgments often rely on subtle differences in color, texture, and lesion depth, while management decisions must balance variables such as age, hypersensitivity, cooperation, and long-term restorability [19, 33]. As current clinical guidelines offer broad principles rather than algorithmic decision pathways, MIH questions typically require contextual interpretation rather than rule-based responses. For this reason, we included four distinct content categories—diagnosis, etiology, treatment, and differential diagnosis—to capture the different cognitive demands clinicians face in practice. By structuring the evaluation around multiple content categories, we aimed to capture a more realistic and comprehensive picture of each model’s capabilities. The consistently higher scores achieved by DeepSeek R1 across all categories suggest that its advantages were not limited to isolated question types but extended across both conceptual and clinically oriented content. At the same time, the greater variability observed in etiological questions highlights the aspects of MIH that remain challenging not only for clinicians but also for large language models.

Although LLMs have been examined across various dental specialties, none of these investigations have addressed MIH directly, making cross-disciplinary evidence essential for interpreting our results. Nevertheless, research from other areas of dentistry provides a meaningful framework for interpreting the present results. Recent investigations across various dental disciplines have shown that LLMs differ substantially in factual accuracy, clinical coherence, and susceptibility to hallucinations. A recent study evaluating ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek-v3 in text-based oral pathology scenarios reported that DeepSeek achieved significantly higher diagnostic accuracy and more clinically coherent responses, whereas ChatGPT exhibited a markedly higher rate of fabricated references (80.6% vs. 16.7%) [25]. Similarly, a comparative analysis in oral and maxillofacial surgery found that although overall response quality was comparable between DeepSeek R1 and ChatGPT-4o, model-specific strengths emerged depending on the type of question, with DeepSeek performing better on patient-focused items and ChatGPT performing slightly better on the more technical questions [34]. In a recent prosthodontic evaluation of patient inquiries regarding dental and maxillofacial prostheses, Özcivelek and Özcan reported significant model-dependent variation in chatbot accuracy and readability, with DeepSeek-R1 achieving the highest readability scores while ChatGPT-4 showed the lowest diagnostic accuracy [35]. These observations closely parallel our findings, where DeepSeek R1 demonstrated higher clinical accuracy and more consistent MIH-related responses than ChatGPT-4. DeepSeek’s responses were not only more frequently aligned with established MIH diagnostic and management principles, but they also displayed greater internal consistency across repeated sessions. ChatGPT-4, by contrast, tended to provide more variable, and at times less clinically grounded, explanations, mirroring the inconsistencies and the generation of non-verifiable or inaccurate information reported in other areas of dentistry. Taken together, these studies and the results of the present study show that LLMs do not perform at the same level across models or clinical topics. Instead, their accuracy and consistency vary depending on the model and the type of question. This pattern helps explain the performance differences observed between DeepSeek R1 and ChatGPT-4 in MIH-related responses.

Temporal consistency has become an increasingly important dimension in evaluating LLMs, as fluctuations in output quality across repeated queries may undermine their reliability in clinical or educational settings. Prior research has shown that LLM performance can drift over time, either within the same day or across longer intervals, depending on model updates, contextual sensitivity, or inherent stochastic variation [36, 37]. Accordingly, studies in dentistry have adopted heterogeneous temporal assessment strategies to examine short- and long-term stability [6, 38, 39]. However, there is currently no methodological consensus regarding the optimal timeframe or frequency for such assessments. In the present study, we focused specifically on within-day temporal stability by administering identical MIH-related questions in morning, afternoon, and evening sessions, allowing us to evaluate short-term variability under tightly controlled conditions. Within this framework, both ChatGPT and DeepSeek showed relatively stable performance across the morning, afternoon, and evening sessions. Although their scores changed slightly over the course of the day—and these differences reached statistical significance—the size of the change was small, indicating that the variation is unlikely to have a meaningful impact on practical use. DeepSeek demonstrated slightly better temporal consistency than ChatGPT, reflected in smaller score fluctuations and a higher ICC value (0.77 vs. 0.72), suggesting that its responses were rated more uniformly throughout the day. These findings are consistent with previous LLM research showing that minor fluctuations can occur across repeated queries, but that such changes generally remain limited. Overall, both models maintained acceptable within-day stability for text-based MIH questions. From a clinical perspective, this is reassuring, as it implies that dentists, students, or parents are unlikely to receive noticeably different information depending on the time the question is asked.

A further finding in the present study is the low reliability of the references generated by both models. For example, in response to MIH-related questions, some LLM outputs cited references that appeared plausible in format but could not be retrieved from major scientific databases. In one instance, a citation addressing enamel microhardness and chemical composition in MIH-affected teeth included complete author, year, and journal information, yet the referenced article could not be verified in indexed sources. Similarly, for treatment-related questions, a long-term clinical study on composite restorations in MIH-affected molars was cited with specific bibliographic details, although the cited article could not be identified in the stated journal or database records. These examples illustrate how fabricated or non-verifiable references may create a false impression of evidence-based support, even when they appear scientifically credible. Previous LLM evaluations in dentistry have shown that citation accuracy can vary widely across topics and models. Kaygisiz and Teke reported that ChatGPT-4o produced a very high proportion of fabricated citations (80.6%), while DeepSeek-v3 generated far fewer false references (16.7%), suggesting a clear advantage for DeepSeek in oral pathology [25]. Özcivelek and Özcan noted that some responses contained unverifiable citations; however, they did not provide any numerical data regarding the frequency or distribution of these errors [35]. In contrast, the results of this study did not follow this pattern. ChatGPT produced a higher proportion of real references than DeepSeek (46.5% vs. 34.2%), while DeepSeek produced a substantially larger number of fabricated citations (65.8%). One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the limited and uneven MIH literature base, which may lead models that generate longer reference lists—such as DeepSeek—to fill gaps by producing unverifiable or fabricated sources. The presence of duplicate citations in both models further supports the notion that LLM-generated reference lists cannot be taken at face value. Together, these findings emphasize that citation integrity is highly model- and topic-dependent, and that references generated for MIH-related content require careful manual verification before use in academic or clinical settings.

Given these overall performance patterns, an important question is how to interpret the differences between the two models from a clinical and educational perspective. Although the differences in scores were modest, they provide a useful perspective when viewed in the context of MIH’s clinical complexity. This condition often requires nuanced interpretation—such as distinguishing between mild demarcated opacities, deep post-eruptive breakdown, hypersensitivity-driven treatment choices, or situations where early extraction must be evaluated against long-term orthodontic consequences. In such scenarios, even small variations in the clarity, accuracy, or clinical coherence of an explanation can influence how a practitioner frames a case or how a parent understands its urgency. Within this context, DeepSeek’s higher ratings, particularly for etiology and differential diagnosis questions, suggest that it tended to provide more detailed and context-focused explanations. ChatGPT-4o, by contrast, produced answers that were somewhat broader in scope; however, these responses remained clinically acceptable and may be more accessible for users seeking clear, structured overviews rather than highly detailed narratives. Overall, these differences do not imply superiority of one model over the other; rather, they highlight complementary strengths that may suit different levels of user need. Rather than indicating a definitive advantage for one system, these patterns highlight complementary strengths that may align with different informational needs—ranging from student learning to parent communication or initial clinical orientation—when engaging with MIH-related content.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although the evaluation included a relatively large number of expert raters, only two large language models were assessed. This choice was deliberate rather than restrictive: the study required the generation and expert scoring of 120 detailed narrative responses across three time points, making the inclusion of additional models impractical and potentially reducing rating reliability. As a result, the findings cannot be generalized to all LLMs, particularly those developed for multimodal applications or specialized professional tasks. Second, the analysis was based solely on text-based MIH scenarios without incorporating clinical photographs or mixed-modality inputs, which may limit the ecological validity of the results for real-world MIH assessment. Additionally, the English-language format, while suitable for standardization, does not capture possible variations in performance across different languages commonly used in clinical practice. Finally, although the expert panel demonstrated strong interrater consistency, subjective judgment is inherent to qualitative scoring systems, and future studies would benefit from multi-center validation using larger model sets, image-based prompts, and multilingual evaluations. Despite these limitations, the study provides a meaningful early comparison of two widely used LLMs in one of the most diagnostically challenging areas of pediatric dentistry.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic comparison of ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek R1 in generating clinical information related to molar incisor hypomineralization. DeepSeek R1 demonstrated higher clinical accuracy, greater consistency, and more detailed MIH-focused explanations, whereas ChatGPT-4o produced clearer and more structured narrative outputs. Although both models showed acceptable within-day stability, their high rates of fabricated references underscore the need for expert verification before integrating LLM-generated information into clinical or educational decision-making. Overall, current LLMs may assist with preliminary learning and communication but are not yet reliable for autonomous clinical use in pediatric dentistry.
