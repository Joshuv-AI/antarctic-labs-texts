Posterior cortical atrophy with initial psychiatric presentation: a case report
Authors: Vivian Swane, Bo Biering-Sørensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPosterior cortical atrophy (PCA) is a rare neurodegenerative syndrome most commonly associated with atypical Alzheimer’s disease. It is characterized by progressive visuospatial and visuoperceptual deficits with relative sparing of memory and language in early stages. Diagnosis is frequently delayed or missed due to clinical heterogeneity, subtle early symptoms, and limited sensitivity of routine cognitive screening tools. Psychiatric symptoms may further obscure the underlying neurological disorder. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by PCA presenting in the context of prominent psychiatric symptoms, combined with a rapid clinical decline.Case presentationA 68-year-old white Danish male presented with suicidal ideation and was initially admitted to a psychiatric ward. He had a remote history of a suicide attempt and a family history of suicide but no known neurodegenerative disease. Within a short period, severe cognitive and functional impairment became evident, dominated by visuospatial deficits, apraxia, simultanagnosia, optic ataxia, and features of Gerstmann and Balint syndromes, with relative preservation of memory and language. Neuroimaging revealed marked posterior cortical atrophy and parieto-occipitotemporal hypometabolism with sparing of the posterior cingulate cortex. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed mildly reduced amyloid-β1–42 and markedly elevated neurofilament light chain levels. Electroencephalography demonstrated focal posterior slowing without epileptiform activity. Extensive evaluation excluded autoimmune, epileptic, metabolic, and prion-related causes. Based on the clinical syndrome, characteristic neuroimaging findings, and supportive CSF biomarkers, a diagnosis of posterior cortical atrophy likely related to underlying Alzheimer’s disease pathology was made. Disease-specific treatment was discussed but not initiated due to patient preference.ConclusionsThis case illustrates how posterior cortical atrophy may be overlooked, particularly when psychiatric symptoms dominate the initial presentation. It underscores the importance of considering neurodegenerative disorders in older patients with atypical psychiatric presentations or unexplained functional decline. Early recognition of PCA is crucial for accurate diagnosis, appropriate counseling, and tailored supportive care.Keywords: Posterior cortical atrophy, Alzheimer’s disease, Visuospatial dysfunction, Neurodegenerative disorders, Case report

Background

Posterior cortical atrophy (PCA) was initially characterized in 1988 as a neurodegenerative syndrome marked by gradual impairment of visual processing and other posterior occipito-parietal-temporal functions, with relative preservation of memory and language in the early stages [1]. It most frequently shares pathology with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), although it reflects an atypical, posteriorly predominant form of AD, with an earlier age at onset (50–65 years) [2, 3]. Earlier reports suggest that up to 20% of patients presenting with a PCA phenotype had underlying non-AD pathology, including dementia with Lewy bodies, corticobasal degeneration, and prion disease [3–5]. However, many of these reports predated current diagnostic criteria, and some cases may be more appropriately classified as PCA-like posterior cortical syndromes rather than PCA as currently defined [6, 7].

In the early stages, patients with PCA often complain of visuoperceptual symptoms. As the disease progresses, symptoms characteristic of Gerstmann and Balint syndrome may emerge, including dysgraphia, dyscalculia, finger agnosia, left–right disorientation, simultanagnosia, oculomotor apraxia, and optic ataxia [5, 8]. Ultimately, the disease leads to a more diffuse pattern of cognitive dysfunction [6]. Neuroimaging usually demonstrates asymmetric atrophy and hypometabolism in the occipital, parietal and posterior temporal regions, predominantly affecting the right hemisphere. This contrasts with the pattern in typical AD, in which medial temporal lobe atrophy is more pronounced [9]. In PCA with AD pathology, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers frequently show reduced amyloid beta 1–42 (Aβ1–42) with or without tau abnormalities [10–13].

Despite established diagnostic criteria encompassing clinical, cognitive, and radiological features (Table 1), PCA is frequently diagnosed late or misdiagnosed [6]. Many patients initially undergo ophthalmologic evaluation, and some receive psychiatric diagnoses before the correct neurological diagnosis is established [14, 15]. Factors contributing to diagnostic delay include early age at onset, clinical heterogeneity, limited awareness of PCA, and the low sensitivity of global cognitive screening tools. The present case illustrates these diagnostic challenges in the context of suicidal ideation requiring psychiatric admission, prominent posterior cortical deficits, and an unusually rapid clinical decline.

A 68-year-old white Danish male presented unannounced to his general practitioner expressing suicidal ideation. He had worked as a postman until his retirement 1 year earlier. Approximately 4 months before presentation, he had complained of memory difficulties, but his general practitioner found no signs of dementia (MMSE = 30), and no further evaluation was pursued.

Given his acute suicidal ideation, the patient was admitted to a psychiatric ward. On admission, he appeared unkempt and distressed, with sparse eye contact and reduced facial expressivity. His mood was depressed, with a congruent affect. Speech was coherent but characterized by mild response latency. Thought processes were generally logical and goal-directed, with mild distractibility. He endorsed suicidal ideation without specific plans or intent. No psychotic symptoms were evident, and psychomotor activity was mildly slowed. Based on a clinical suspicion of moderate-to-severe depression, nortriptyline (25 mg twice daily) was initiated. During hospitalization, however, he was noted to exhibit severe cognitive dysfunction, conspicuous behavior, and rapidly declining gait function related to marked apraxia. He struggled with basic instrumental activities of daily living, such as using cutlery or operating a coffee machine, frequently collided with surrounding objects, and reported difficulty reaching for objects during meals.

The patient had a history of a suicide attempt more than a decade earlier but no documented recurrent affective episodes or long-term psychiatric treatment. No psychotropic medications were used prior to admission. Several family members had died by suicide at a relatively young age, suggesting familial vulnerability to affective disorders. There was no known family history of dementia.

Twelve days after admission, neurological examination revealed severe global cognitive dysfunction (MMSE = 15, approximately 4 months after the previous score of 30), with relatively less affected memory function compared with the severity of visuospatial deficits. Eye movement examination demonstrated impaired voluntary saccades with compensatory head movements, consistent with oculomotor apraxia. He showed right-sided visual and tactile neglect. He consistently preferred using his left side, even when instructed to use the other side (referred to as “the other side” as left-to-right confusion was also present). Apraxia was evident, for example when dressing, using cutlery, and drinking from a straw (blowing bubbles instead). Furthermore, visuospatial deficits were prominent. When describing the Cookie Theft Picture from the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination, it was fragmented, without grasp of the overall scene. Simultanagnosia was demonstrated by the patient’s inability to recognize individual objects (for example, a remote control or a pen) when presented among other items yet recognized them immediately when shown in isolation. Optic ataxia was evident when the patient was asked to reach for items.

Reading ability was preserved, but writing attempts resulted in barely illegible scribbles. Simple calculations were consistently incorrect. Mild finger agnosia was present when the patient was asked to name and differentiate his fingers. No motor weakness, cerebellar signs, or parkinsonism were observed. Emotional expression was appropriate, with no prominent affective disturbance observed.

Formal neuropsychological assessment was limited by severe visuospatial deficits and inability to use writing utensils due to apraxia. Attention span was relatively preserved (digit span forward 5 digits), whereas working memory was severely impaired. Executive dysfunction was prominent, with marked impairment on verbal fluency tasks. Verbal learning and delayed recall were impaired (RBANS list learning 15/40; delayed recall 2/10), although recognition performance was relatively better (18/20). Visual object recognition remained intact when single items were presented in isolation, whereas right-sided neglect, anosognosia, optic ataxia, and other higher-order visuospatial deficits were evident throughout testing. Overall, the findings indicated severe visuospatial and executive dysfunction, while memory and language functions were relatively less severely affected.

Brain MRI demonstrated non-specific white matter lesions and marked central and cortical atrophy, most prominent in the occipital regions, with slightly greater involvement of the left hemisphere (see Fig. 1). A supplementary contrast-enhanced MR showed no enhancing lesions.

FDG-PET demonstrated bilateral parieto-occipitotemporal hypometabolism with mild left-sided predominance (Fig. 2). Metabolic reduction was most pronounced in the lateral parietal and occipitotemporal cortices. In contrast, metabolism in the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus was relatively preserved bilaterally.

CSF showed no pleocytosis. Analysis for AD biomarkers showed mildly reduced Aβ1–42 (794 pg/mL; laboratory reference range > 1030 pg/mL) with normal tau levels (total tau 167 pg/mL; reference range < 250 pg/mL; phosphorylated tau 13 pg/mL; reference range < 25 pg/mL). Neurofilament light chain (NfL) was markedly elevated (5700 pg/mL; reference range < 1200 pg/mL for age). RT-QuIC testing for prion disease was negative. A broad panel of autoimmune and paraneoplastic antibodies was negative, including antibodies against GABA-B receptor and IgLON5.

Electroencephalography (EEG) showed a posterior dominant rhythm of 7.6–8 Hz that was well organized, symmetric, and suppressed by eye opening. Intermittent polymorphic slow-wave activity (3.5–6 Hz) was observed over the left posterior temporo-parietal region, interpreted as non-specific focal cerebral dysfunction. No epileptiform discharges, electrographic seizures, or EEG evidence of non-convulsive status epilepticus were detected.

Laboratory findings revealed mildly reduced vitamin B12 (180 pmol/L; reference range > 250 pmol/L), folate (4.0 nmol/L; reference range > 8.6 nmol/L), and vitamin D (13 nmol/L; reference range > 50 nmol/L), considered insufficient to account for the severity of deficits; supplementation was initiated.

Based on the clinical syndrome and neuroimaging findings, posterior cortical atrophy was diagnosed. The CSF biomarker profile was considered suggestive of underlying Alzheimer’s disease pathology, although not definitive. Disease-specific treatment was discussed but declined by the patient. Given his progressive functional impairment, home care services were arranged.

Discussion and conclusions

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of posterior cortical atrophy, particularly when psychiatric symptoms obscured the recognition of an underlying neurodegenerative disorder.

Psychiatric symptoms, including depression and anxiety, are not uncommon in PCA and AD, but they are not core diagnostic features and rarely constitute the presenting manifestation [6, 14, 16]. In this patient, suicidal ideation prompted psychiatric admission, delaying neurological assessment despite emerging visuospatial and functional deficits. The etiology of the suicidal ideation remains uncertain. While his history of a previous suicide attempt and family history of suicide suggest an underlying vulnerability to affective disorders, the emerging neurodegenerative disease and associated functional decline may also have contributed. Regardless of the underlying cause, the psychiatric presentation initially diverted attention from the evolving neurological syndrome and contributed to diagnostic complexity. This underscores the importance of considering neurodegenerative etiologies in older adults presenting with new or atypical psychiatric symptoms, particularly when accompanied by subtle perceptual, coordination, or functional impairments.

A recent large retrospective study by Shir et al. included 558 participants over a 28-year period. Twenty-three percent had received a psychiatric diagnosis prior to the diagnosis of PCA [14]. Their data represent one of the most extensive PCA cohorts reported to date and highlight the potential for psychiatric misdiagnosis. However, several limitations must be noted, including insufficiently described diagnostic criteria, neuroimaging information is largely absent, and many reported symptoms are not specific to PCA. Consequently, it is uncertain whether all cases met contemporary PCA criteria. It is also unclear precisely when diagnoses were established. These considerations emphasize the need for careful clinical and imaging-based evaluation to distinguish PCA from other neurodegenerative or psychiatric conditions.

This patient demonstrated the characteristic neuropsychological profile of PCA, including profound visuospatial dysfunction, neglect, optic ataxia, apraxia, and features of Balint and Gerstmann syndromes. Although anterograde memory was impaired, it was relatively less affected than the severe visuospatial and executive deficits, while language functions remained largely preserved. The case also highlights the limitations of global cognitive screening tools such as the MMSE, which may fail to detect posterior cortical deficits in early stages. Targeted visuospatial and parietal lobe testing, in combination with neuroimaging, is crucial for accurate diagnosis.

Neuroimaging findings of posterior parietal and occipitotemporal hypometabolism are characteristic of PCA [6]. In addition, metabolism in the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus was relatively preserved in this patient. Left-hemispheric involvement was slightly more pronounced than right-hemispheric involvement. Although this deviates from the more commonly reported pattern in PCA, hemispheric asymmetry is not a defining feature of current diagnostic criteria [6].

PCA is a clinico-radiological syndrome that may arise from different underlying pathologies, most commonly Alzheimer’s disease, but also other neurodegenerative disorders presenting with PCA-like phenotypes [3–7]. The CSF biomarker profile provided limited support for underlying Alzheimer’s disease pathology. Aβ1–42 was only mildly reduced, whereas total tau and phosphorylated tau levels were within the normal range. Furthermore, amyloid PET and tau PET were not performed. Consequently, the biomarker findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as supportive rather than diagnostic evidence of Alzheimer’s disease pathology. Normal total tau and phosphorylated tau levels do not exclude Alzheimer's disease pathology, particularly when interpreted in the context of a characteristic clinical syndrome and supportive neuroimaging findings [10–13]. Nevertheless, Alzheimer’s disease remains the most common underlying pathology in PCA, and the overall clinical and neuroimaging phenotype was considered most compatible with this etiology.

Elevated CSF NfL reflects active neurodegeneration but lacks disease specificity. High NfL levels may occur in neurodegenerative disorders such as corticobasal degeneration and prion disease, both of which can present with posterior cortical dysfunction. Under contemporary diagnostic frameworks, such cases are often considered PCA-like phenotypes rather than typical PCA associated with Alzheimer's disease pathology [6, 7]. While high NfL levels have been associated with faster clinical progression in AD [17–19], data specifically addressing NfL levels in PCA are limited. It is therefore possible that the markedly elevated NfL level reflects the rapid progression observed in this patient, although this interpretation remains speculative.

The rate of clinical progression in this case appeared unusually rapid. The patient reportedly remained cognitively and functionally independent approximately 4 months before hospitalization and had an MMSE score of 30/30 recorded by his general practitioner. At neurological assessment, his MMSE had declined to 15/30, accompanied by profound visuospatial and functional impairment. However, the interpretation of this decline should be made cautiously. The reliability of the initial MMSE score cannot be independently verified, and MMSE performance may be relatively insensitive to early posterior cortical deficits. Consequently, it is possible that cognitive impairment was already present but underrecognized. Nevertheless, the history obtained from the patient suggested a substantial deterioration over a relatively short period.

Given the apparent rapid clinical decline, delirium was considered in the differential diagnosis. However, stable consciousness, absence of fluctuating attention, and lack of identifiable metabolic, infectious, or toxic precipitants made delirium unlikely. Autoimmune encephalitis and non-convulsive status epilepticus were also considered because of the patient's unusual behavior and seemingly rapid clinical decline. However, EEG showed no epileptiform activity or evidence of non-convulsive status epilepticus, and CSF analysis revealed no pleocytosis. Furthermore, comprehensive testing for autoimmune encephalitis and paraneoplastic antibodies, including GABA-B receptor and IgLON5 antibodies, was negative. Although seronegative autoimmune encephalitis cannot be completely excluded, the clinical presentation, neuroimaging findings, and biomarker profile were considered more consistent with a neurodegenerative disorder. Other neurodegenerative diseases, including the Heidenhain variant of CJD, were also considered. RT-QuIC testing was negative, substantially reducing the likelihood of CJD, although the sensitivity of the assay is not absolute. In addition, the neuroimaging findings and absence of characteristic EEG abnormalities were considered atypical for CJD.

Management of PCA remains symptomatic and etiology-directed. In AD-related PCA, cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine may be considered. Evidence is limited due to the absence of randomized controlled trials in PCA, but some patients with PCA show stabilization or mild improvement in global cognition and daily functioning with medical treatment, although visuospatial deficits usually continue to progress [5, 20]. In this case, treatment was not initiated due to patient preference. The patient also declined further clinical follow-up.

In conclusion, this case emphasizes how PCA may be easily overlooked, particularly in its early stages when visuospatial deficits and memory impairment may be subtle. The case also suggests a potentially rapid clinical decline, although the true rate of progression remains uncertain because of limited baseline cognitive data. It underscores the importance of thorough neurological assessment in older patients presenting with atypical psychiatric symptoms or unexplained functional decline. Furthermore, it highlights the limitations of routine cognitive screening tools such as the MMSE, which may underestimate posterior cortical dysfunction, and the importance of targeted visuospatial assessment and appropriate neuroimaging when PCA is suspected. Early recognition of PCA is essential for accurate diagnosis, appropriate counseling, and tailored supportive care.
