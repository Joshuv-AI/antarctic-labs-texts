Determinants of fall prevention implementation among older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment: a systematic review of recent evidence from 2021 to March 2026
Authors: Fengjiao Pu, Hangcheng Liu, Miao Li, Huan Wang, Xixi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults with dementia or cognitive impairment have a two- to three-fold higher fall risk than cognitively intact peers. Multifactorial fall-prevention interventions often require understanding, memory, and sustained adherence, which may be difficult in this population. This review synthesized recent evidence on barriers and facilitators affecting implementation and mapped determinants using CFIR 2.0, with ERIC-informed recommendations for future implementation design.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM/SinoMed for empirical studies published from January 2021 to March 2026. Eligible studies reported implementation outcomes, barriers, or facilitators related to fall prevention, fall-risk management, safe mobility, assistive-device use, or fall-related monitoring for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment. Methodological quality was assessed using MMAT. Determinants were synthesized narratively and thematically, then mapped to CFIR 2.0 domains.ResultsTwenty-seven studies were included. The synthesis identified 49 barriers and 40 facilitators. Key barriers were intervention complexity, cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms, resistance to care or devices, limited dementia-specific fall-prevention knowledge among caregivers or staff, staffing constraints, workflow barriers, digital-access problems, caregiver burden, and limited follow-up after recommendations. Key facilitators included low-burden or passive technologies, simplified and familiar delivery formats, caregiver involvement, staff training, multidisciplinary collaboration, environmental adaptation, standardized communication, and dementia-sensitive instruction. Determinants varied by setting, with home/community studies emphasizing caregiver, transport, and digital-access barriers, and institutional studies emphasizing staffing, handover, workflow, and organizational culture.ConclusionsFall-prevention implementation for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment is shaped by patient, caregiver, organizational, environmental, and process-level factors. Current evidence suggests that interventions requiring active memory and sustained independent adherence may be difficult to implement without cognitive adaptation, caregiver support, environmental modification, and workflow alignment. The ERIC-informed recommendations generated in this review are author-derived options for future implementation design and evaluation, not strategies proven by this review to improve implementation or clinical outcomes.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD420261296956.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08004-6.

Background

With the acceleration of global population aging, the incidence of dementia is showing a marked upward trend, placing a heavy care burden on public health systems. Previous studies have shown that cognitive impairment is associated with an increased risk of falls; compared with older adults with normal cognitive function, this population has a higher risk of falls and fall-related injuries [1, 2]. Their risk of falling is not solely explained by decreased muscle strength or impaired balance but may also be related to declines in executive function, abnormal gait control, limited attentional resources, and behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) [3, 4].

Multifactorial fall prevention interventions typically include risk assessment, exercise training, medication management, environmental modifications, assistive device use, and health education [5, 6]. However, the implementation of such interventions in individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment may be limited [7]. Some measures require patients to understand, remember, and consistently follow recommendations—such as the correct use of assistive devices, adherence to activity reminders, or participation in exercise programs—while manifestations such as cognitive decline, communication difficulties, agitation, wandering, and resistance to care may reduce intervention acceptance and adherence. Therefore, even if certain fall prevention measures demonstrate effectiveness in the general elderly population, their practical application in individuals with dementia may face varying degrees of challenges.

Existing fall prevention guidelines and systematic reviews provide an evidence base for fall risk management in older adults [5, 6]. However, for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment, simply answering whether “a particular intervention is effective” is insufficient to explain its outcomes in clinical and care settings. Fall prevention for this population often requires the joint participation of family caregivers, nursing staff, rehabilitation therapists, physicians, and facility administrators, and implementation outcomes may be influenced by factors such as intervention complexity, staff training, workflows, environmental conditions, and organizational culture. Therefore, this study focuses on implementation determinants to identify which factors hinder or facilitate the actual implementation of fall prevention interventions for older adults with dementia. Compared to efficacy-centered evaluations, this perspective is better suited to explaining why some interventions are feasible in research settings but difficult to implement in real-world care settings.

To better understand these complex determinants, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) provides a structured approach for systematically identifying and categorizing factors that influence implementation. The updated CFIR 2.0 organizes implementation determinants into five domains: innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals, and implementation process [8, 9]. By integrating patient-, caregiver-, organizational-, and environmental-level factors within a unified analytical structure, CFIR 2.0 facilitates a comprehensive examination of the challenges involved in implementing fall-prevention interventions. Previous reviews of implementation barriers have primarily focused on general older adult populations and may not have sufficiently distinguished the specific implementation needs of people with dementia or cognitive impairment. Although a recent systematic review summarized implementation strategies for fall prevention among community-dwelling older adults with cognitive impairment since 2000 [10], there remains limited theory-informed synthesis of barriers and facilitators across different care settings.

Accordingly, this review aimed to identify and synthesize the barriers and facilitators affecting the implementation of fall-prevention interventions for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment. Its added contribution is the explicit use of CFIR 2.0 to classify implementation determinants across care settings and the subsequent, clearly labelled use of the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) framework to translate key determinants into actionable implementation recommendations. These ERIC-informed recommendations are intended to support future implementation design and evaluation but have not yet been empirically verified.

Methods

Formulation of research questions

This review was designed as a focused synthesis of recent evidence published from January 2021 to March 2026. The specific research questions were: (1) What recent barriers and facilitators affect the implementation of fall-prevention interventions for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment? (2) How can these determinants be mapped to the domains of CFIR 2.0? (3) Where data permit, how can key determinants be aligned with ERIC implementation strategies to inform future practice and research.

Study design and reporting standards

This study is a systematic review and thematic synthesis, reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [11]. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO International Registry of Systematic Reviews on April 15, 2026, under registration number CRD420261296956. After an initial scoping search, the protocol was amended on April 30, 2026, before formal title/abstract screening, full-text screening, and data extraction. The amendment narrowed the target population from general older adults at risk of falling to older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment, because the scoping search indicated that dementia-specific implementation barriers and facilitators were insufficiently distinguished under the broader population definition. Accordingly, the eligibility criteria and search terms were refined to reflect this population focus. The core objective of examining implementation determinants of fall-prevention interventions remained unchanged, as did the planned thematic synthesis, CFIR 2.0 mapping, and ERIC-informed strategy alignment. The PROSPERO record was updated on June 29, 2026, to reflect this amendment.

The study process includes literature search, manuscript screening, data extraction, methodological quality assessment, thematic synthesis, and CFIR 2.0 theoretical mapping. Since the included studies are expected to encompass qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research, and the study outcomes primarily focus on implementation barriers, facilitators, and implementation-related outcomes, a meta-analysis is not planned for this study.

Eligibility criteria

This review established inclusion and exclusion criteria by combining the PCC framework with PICOS elements.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Population and informants (P): Eligible studies had to focus on older adults aged ≥ 60 years with diagnosed dementia, any subtype of dementia, mild cognitive impairment, or other chronic cognitive impairment, or on stakeholders directly involved in the implementation of fall-prevention interventions for this population. Eligible stakeholders included family or informal caregivers, physicians, nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, nursing assistants, care-home staff, and other professional caregivers. Studies that collected data solely from caregivers or professional staff were eligible only when the target population was explicitly older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment and when the reported views, experiences, implementation outcomes, or barriers/facilitators were directly related to fall prevention or fall-risk management for this population. The age threshold of ≥ 60 years was used because definitions of older adults vary across countries, health systems, and care settings; studies using a threshold of ≥ 65 years were also retained if they otherwise met the eligibility criteria. If a study included both cognitively normal older adults and those with dementia or cognitive impairment, it was included only when data related to dementia or cognitive impairment could be separately extracted. (2) Intervention and setting (C): Eligible interventions included any active or passive strategy intended to prevent falls, reduce fall risk, improve safe mobility, or support the implementation of fall-prevention practice among older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment. These included, but were not limited to, exercise or rehabilitation programs, environmental modification, assistive-device use, fall-risk assessment, staff or caregiver education, care-process optimization, medication or fracture-risk management when explicitly linked to fall or injury prevention, digital monitoring, alarms, sensor-based systems, and multicomponent fall-prevention programs. No restriction was placed on the implementation setting, including home, community, adult day care, acute care, emergency departments, rehabilitation units, and long-term care facilities. (3) Core concepts and outcomes (C/O): Eligible studies had to report at least one implementation-related outcome or determinant. Implementation outcomes included acceptability, feasibility, adoption, adherence, fidelity, appropriateness, sustainability, uptake, implementation cost or burden, and related process outcomes. Studies were also eligible if they reported barriers or facilitators affecting the implementation of fall-prevention interventions or fall-risk management for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment. Determinants were extracted only when they were explicitly linked to fall prevention, fall-risk management, safe mobility, assistive-device use, fall-related monitoring, or implementation of fall-prevention care for the target population. (4) Study design (S): Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods empirical studies were eligible.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Pure clinical efficacy studies that do not report implementation processes, implementation outcomes, or barriers and facilitators; (2) Ineligible study participants were those with intact cognitive function, or those whose cognitive impairment was caused by non-degenerative or non-chronic conditions such as acute stroke, delirium, acute poisoning, or severe mental disorders. These conditions were excluded because their clinical course and fall-prevention implementation needs may differ substantially from those of people with dementia or chronic cognitive impairment; (3) Ineligible interventions: Interventions aimed at treating complications of falls rather than preventing falls; (4) Literature types, publication status, and full-text availability: Reviews, editorials, commentaries, research protocols, conference abstracts without a full empirical report, animal studies, unpublished studies, and preprints without a corresponding peer-reviewed or complete empirical full-text report were excluded. Theses or dissertations indexed in the searched databases were considered eligible when the full text was available and the study met all other inclusion criteria. Records for which the full text could not be obtained through institutional access, database links, open-access sources, or reference-list tracing were excluded and recorded during full-text screening.

Information sources and search strategy

Two researchers independently conducted the literature search. The following databases were searched: PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, VIP Database, and the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM/SinoMed). The final searches were conducted on April 30, 2026, and the publication-date limit was set from January 1, 2021, to March 31, 2026. This window was selected because the review was designed as a focused synthesis of recent implementation evidence rather than an exhaustive historical review. The period reflects the increasing relevance of implementation-science frameworks, digital and remote monitoring technologies, telehealth, and hybrid care delivery models in fall-prevention implementation for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms, where available, with free-text terms related to three concept blocks: dementia or cognitive impairment; fall prevention or fall-risk management; and implementation determinants or implementation outcomes. English search terms included dementia, Alzheimer disease, cognitive impairment, cognitive dysfunction, cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment, accidental falls, fall prevention, fall risk, fall management, fall reduction, barrier, facilitator, determinant, challenge, enabler, acceptability, feasibility, adherence, fidelity, adoption, implementation, and process evaluation. Chinese-language databases were searched using Chinese equivalents of these terms, and the complete original Chinese search strings are provided in Supplementary Table S1 to ensure reproducibility.

Publication date was the only database-level limit. Restrictions related to study design, publication type, population, intervention relevance, implementation-related outcomes, and full-text availability were applied during eligibility screening rather than through database filters. To reduce the risk of overlooking important foundational evidence, we manually traced the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews. Earlier evidence was used to contextualize the findings where appropriate, but was not included in the primary synthesis. Literature screening and methodological quality assessment

Literature screening was conducted independently by two researchers using the reference management software Zotero. The search results were deduplicated, and both initial and secondary screenings were performed. Disagreements during the screening process were resolved through discussion between the two researchers; if consensus could not be reached, a third researcher made the final decision. The study selection process was reported using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram. The flow diagram presents the number of records identified from each database and additional sources, records removed before screening, duplicate records removed, records screened by title and abstract, records excluded at the title/abstract stage, reports sought for retrieval, reports not retrieved, full-text reports assessed for eligibility, full-text reports excluded with specific reasons, and the final number of studies included in the synthesis. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the 2018 version of the Mixed Methods Assessment Tool (MMAT) [12, 13]. Two researchers independently completed the assessment based on the study design type, using the corresponding items; results were recorded as “Yes”, “No”, or “Unclear”. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher serving as an arbitrator when necessary. The MMAT is primarily used to identify key methodological limitations across different study designs; however, direct numerical comparisons between different study types are not appropriate. Therefore, no overall quality rating or study-level categorization (e.g., “high quality”) was applied. Data extraction and thematic synthesis strategy

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form. The form was piloted on three included studies and refined before formal extraction. Extracted variables included author, year, country, study design, setting, sample size, participant or informant type, cognitive status or dementia diagnosis, intervention type, intervention components, implementers, data-collection methods or tools, implementation outcomes, barriers, facilitators, and supporting text. Implementation outcomes were extracted as reported in the original studies, including acceptability, feasibility, adherence, fidelity, adoption or uptake, appropriateness, sustainability, and implementation burden or cost. Because these outcomes were inconsistently defined and measured across studies, they were synthesized narratively and used to inform the coding of related barriers and facilitators rather than pooled quantitatively. The two reviewers compared the extracted data, and disagreements were resolved through discussion; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer.

Thematic synthesis was conducted by two reviewers using a hybrid inductive–deductive approach. Microsoft Excel was used to manage extracted text, codes, codebook revisions, and CFIR mapping. The final determinant table was structured as an evidence-linked matrix by retaining the contributing study citations for each facilitator and barrier and by classifying the supporting evidence according to study design, stakeholder or data source, and type of evidence.

In the first stage, two reviewers independently performed inductive, line-by-line coding of text related to implementation outcomes, barriers, facilitators, and contextual factors. Similar codes were merged into descriptive themes, and a codebook was developed iteratively with code names, definitions, and examples. In the second stage, the agreed descriptive themes were deductively mapped to CFIR 2.0 domains and constructs. The mapping was guided by the CFIR 2.0 construct definitions and by the contextual meaning of each determinant in the original study. For each determinant, the reviewers considered: (1) the main implementation mechanism described by the extracted text; (2) the actor or level primarily involved, such as patient, caregiver, staff, organization, external service environment, or implementation process; and (3) the setting in which the determinant influenced fall-prevention implementation. Two reviewers independently assigned each determinant to a CFIR 2.0 domain and construct, and then compared their assignments with the supporting extracted text and study context. Disagreements in coding, theme naming, or CFIR mapping were resolved through discussion; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated the final assignment.

For determinants that could potentially be associated with more than one CFIR 2.0 construct, the review team assigned the determinant to the most proximal construct according to its primary mechanism and implementation context. For example, a determinant related mainly to device complexity or cognitive burden was mapped to the Innovation domain, whereas a determinant related mainly to staffing, workflow, communication, or organizational culture was mapped to the Inner Setting domain. A determinant was not double-counted across multiple constructs unless the extracted evidence indicated clearly distinct mechanisms operating at different implementation levels. Mapping decisions were recorded during codebook refinement to maintain consistency across reviewers. Using this approach, the final agreed themes comprised 40 facilitators and 49 barriers, grouped under the five CFIR 2.0 domains and 31 constructs, as shown in Table 4. After CFIR 2.0 mapping, author-generated implementation recommendations were developed using the ERIC strategy compilation and CFIR–ERIC matching approach. The initial matching was conducted independently by two reviewers, who first identified major barrier themes from the final CFIR-mapped determinant matrix and then matched each barrier theme to potentially relevant ERIC strategies. The matching process considered three criteria: conceptual fit between the barrier and ERIC strategy, relevance to fall prevention for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment, and feasibility across the care settings represented in the included studies. The two reviewers compared their proposed matches and resolved differences through consensus discussion; unresolved disagreements were discussed with a third reviewer. Strategy priority was determined by consensus according to the recurrence and importance of the underlying barrier across included studies, the number and diversity of supporting studies or stakeholder perspectives, and the practical relevance of the strategy to dementia-specific fall-prevention implementation. The resulting recommendations were therefore generated through a structured CFIR–ERIC-informed consensus process. They should be interpreted as author-generated implementation-design recommendations rather than strategies empirically tested across the included studies.

To ensure that the extracted determinants were specifically relevant to fall prevention among older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment, the reviewers applied a two-step relevance check during data extraction and coding. First, each extracted item was required to meet three criteria: it had to be linked to a population with dementia or cognitive impairment; it had to relate to fall prevention, fall-risk management, safe mobility, fall-related monitoring, assistive-device use, or the implementation of fall-prevention care; and it had to describe an implementation outcome, barrier, facilitator, process issue, or contextual factor affecting implementation. Second, when studies included mixed populations, mixed interventions, or broad care processes, only data that could be clearly attributed to dementia or cognitive impairment and fall prevention were extracted. Determinants reflecting general geriatric care, general dementia care, post-fall treatment alone, or broad organizational issues without a clear connection to fall prevention were excluded from the synthesis or retained only as contextual information. For studies based solely on caregiver, staff, or clinical-record data, the extracted determinants were coded according to the stakeholder source of evidence. These data were treated as proxy or implementation-provider perspectives rather than direct patient-reported experiences. During synthesis, the review team distinguished patient-reported determinants from caregiver- or staff-reported determinants where possible, and disagreements about relevance or CFIR mapping were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. During synthesis, determinants were interpreted in relation to the contributing study context, including care setting, stakeholder source, intervention type, study design, and dementia severity where reported, rather than being treated as uniformly applicable across all settings.

Results

Basic characteristics of included studies

A total of 1,807 records were identified through database searching. After removal of 739 duplicate records, 1,068 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 745 were excluded because they clearly did not meet the eligibility criteria. A total of 323 reports were sought for retrieval, and 33 reports could not be retrieved because the full text was unavailable. The remaining 290 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 267 reports were excluded after full-text assessment: 69 did not meet the eligibility criteria for population, intervention, or implementation relevance, and 198 were pure clinical efficacy studies without implementation outcomes, barriers, or facilitators. Finally, 23 studies were included from database searching, and 4 additional studies were identified through reference-list tracing, resulting in 27 empirical studies included in the review.

The 27 included studies comprised 10 qualitative studies, 13 quantitative studies, including 3 randomized controlled trials, 5 non-randomized intervention studies, and 5 descriptive quantitative studies, and 4 mixed-methods studies. Implementation settings spanned a variety of care environments, including home and community care, adult day care centers, emergency departments, acute care wards, rehabilitation departments, and long-term care facilities. Determinants varied across these settings. In home and community-based settings, barriers were mainly related to caregiver burden, transportation, home environmental constraints, and limited access to digital or professional support. In acute and emergency care settings, implementation was more strongly affected by competing clinical priorities, short length of stay, fragmented handover, and limited opportunities for sustained patient engagement. Rehabilitation settings highlighted the need for tailored progression and cognitive adaptation, whereas long-term care settings were more affected by staffing levels, staff turnover, routine-based care processes, legal-representative consent, and organizational support. These differences suggest that fall-prevention implementation strategies should be adapted to the care setting rather than transferred unchanged across settings. Some included studies did not collect data directly from older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment but instead used caregiver reports, professional staff perspectives, or clinical records. These studies were retained because caregivers and professional staff are frequently the direct implementers of fall-prevention measures for this population, particularly when cognitive impairment limits patients’ ability to independently report, remember, or execute fall-prevention recommendations. However, these studies were included only when the target care recipients were explicitly older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment and when the reported determinants were directly related to fall prevention, fall-risk management, safe mobility, assistive-device use, fall-related monitoring, or implementation of fall-prevention care.

Across the included studies, the implementation outcomes most commonly extracted were feasibility, acceptability, adherence or participation, adoption or uptake, and implementation burden. Fidelity, appropriateness, sustainability, and implementation cost were reported less consistently. These outcomes were not sufficiently homogeneous for quantitative synthesis because they were measured using different designs, indicators, and stakeholder perspectives. Therefore, they were summarized narratively and integrated into the thematic synthesis as evidence explaining why implementation was facilitated or hindered. For example, low acceptability or poor adherence was coded in relation to patient resistance, cognitive burden, caregiver workload, or intervention complexity, whereas good feasibility or uptake was linked to simplified delivery, caregiver support, passive monitoring, or workflow adaptation.

Assessment of the methodological quality of included studies

The results of the methodological quality assessment of the included studies are presented in Table 2. Overall, most studies met the basic methodological standards for their designs; however, certain limitations persisted across study types. In qualitative studies, the fit between the research question and the qualitative methods used was most clearly articulated, and the data collection and interpretation processes were relatively comprehensive, resulting in excellent overall methodological quality. However, the results remain influenced by the sample context, institutional background, and participant composition; therefore, external generalizability must be assessed within specific settings [31–40]. In randomized controlled trials, some studies had limitations regarding the implementation of blinding [19–21]; In non-randomized intervention studies, some studies did not adequately control for confounding factors [15–18] ; while in some descriptive quantitative studies, there were issues regarding sample representativeness or insufficient reporting [22–25]. In mixed-methods studies, although the included studies effectively integrated qualitative and quantitative results to answer research questions, they failed to simultaneously meet the quality requirements of the individual study types involved during implementation [27–30] .

Innovation layer

Included studies indicate that the complexity of the intervention, the delivery method, and its alignment with cognitive abilities are key factors influencing the implementation of fall prevention for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment. Some studies have found that highly structured, multi-component interventions—such as programs combining digital applications, printed materials, and complex exercise instructions—face challenges in real-world implementation. This is particularly true when the intervention requires patients to continuously understand, remember, and actively perform multi-step tasks, which can lead to unstable or interrupted implementation [20, 32]. Similarly, digital devices that rely on active interaction also face certain limitations in real-world applications. In the one hand, patients and some family caregivers may struggle with using digital devices; on the other hand, device setup, maintenance, and data interpretation often require family involvement, increasing the burden on family caregivers [20, 21]. Conversely, some studies indicate that low-tech interventions are more readily accepted and sustained, such as visual demonstration materials, large-print educational resources, common communication platforms, and passive monitoring devices with low interaction requirements [15, 30, 31, 40]. Furthermore, some research suggests that monitoring technology alone does not guarantee effective implementation. If risk data is not linked to clear, timely response protocols, its role in promoting actual behavioral change may be limited [28]. This suggests that, for fall prevention in older adults with dementia, the feasibility of an intervention depends not only on the sophistication of the technology but also on its cognitive burden, operational requirements, and the integrity of the follow-up response mechanisms.

Outer setting

The accessibility of resources and the availability of social support in community and home settings significantly influence the sustained implementation of fall prevention interventions. Some studies indicate that people with dementia in remote or relatively resource-poor areas face difficulties accessing continuous rehabilitation guidance, participating in day care programs, or receiving remote support; these challenges are related to transportation conditions, service accessibility, and digital infrastructure [15, 17]. An even more prominent barrier stems from the capacity of the informal care system. Multiple studies have noted that fall prevention for older adults with dementia in home settings relies heavily on family caregivers; however, when external support is insufficient, caregivers often must shoulder multiple long-term responsibilities, including supervision, reminders, companionship, and risk management. This situation may undermine their ability to sustain participation in interventions and affect the continuity of interventions after patients are discharged or return home [14, 24]. These findings suggest that the conditions for implementing fall prevention do not depend entirely on individual or institutional factors. For older adults with dementia, external service resources, transportation conditions, digital accessibility, and care support networks may all influence the transition and continuity of interventions from healthcare facilities to the community and home.

Inner setting

Barriers at the inner setting level primarily involve organizational resources, workflows, and team culture. Some studies indicate that healthcare and rehabilitation facilities often face pressures related to efficiency, staffing, and service workload in their daily operations, whereas fall prevention for older adults with dementia typically requires more time for communication, demonstration, accompaniment, and repeated training. This time requirement may create tension with the facility’s work culture, which emphasizes efficiency and task completion, thereby affecting the depth and sustainability of intervention implementation [34, 35, 37]. Other studies have noted a risk-averse care culture in certain care settings. Under high workloads and pressure to meet accountability standards, frontline caregivers may be more inclined to adopt management approaches that are easy to implement and quickly reduce apparent risks, rather than individualized non-restraint strategies that require more communication, judgment, and continuous observation [26, 29], thereby limiting the implementation of non-restraint fall prevention measures. In summary, implementation issues at the organizational level manifest not only as a lack of resources but also involve whether organizational processes support individualized care, whether teams possess the necessary dementia care competencies, and whether institutional culture permits a more nuanced balance between safety and autonomy.

Individual level

At the individual level, patient-related and caregiver-related factors jointly influence the implementation of fall prevention measures. Where severity information was available, barriers appeared to differ by the degree of cognitive impairment. For mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia, barriers often involved reduced insight, risk-perception mismatch, privacy concerns about monitoring, fear of falling, stigma, apathy toward physical activity, and difficulty learning unfamiliar technologies. For moderate to severe dementia, barriers more often involved behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), wandering, agitation, resistance to alarms or assistive devices, inability to remember multi-step instructions, and greater dependence on caregivers or staff for implementation. However, dementia or cognitive-impairment severity was not consistently or sufficiently reported across all included studies; therefore, this severity-stratified interpretation should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. Overall, these findings suggest that cognitive decline itself is not the sole barrier; emotional, behavioral, motivational, and caregiver-resource factors may jointly affect patients’ acceptance of and participation in fall-prevention interventions [16, 22, 25, 38]. At the same time, some patients may overestimate their functional abilities, leading them to attempt independent mobility even when at high risk; others may avoid activities due to excessive anxiety about falling. These varying behaviors may all undermine the effectiveness of standard education or training programs [22, 25]. For caregivers, implementation barriers manifest not only in physical exertion but also in resource depletion resulting from constant vigilance, task switching, and the long-term pressure of responsibility. Some studies indicate that when family caregivers are burdened with multiple long-term tasks—such as fall monitoring, behavioral reminders, and daily living care—their ability to sustain home-based fall prevention interventions may be limited [14, 22, 39, 40]. Therefore, implementation barriers at the individual level cannot be reduced to “patient non-compliance” or “caregiver burden”, but should be understood as the combined effects of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral symptoms, as well as caregiving resources. It should also be noted that only a portion of the data in the included studies originated directly from patients themselves; Patient-reported data mainly reflected lived experience, motivation, fear, acceptability, privacy, and trust; proxy-reported data from caregivers or staff more often emphasized safety risks, supervision burden, adherence problems, and workflow constraints. Because patient self-report was less common, conclusions about patient motivation and resistance should be interpreted as partly mediated by caregiver and professional perspectives. Consequently, the topics addressed in this review regarding patient motivation, risk perception, acceptance, and resistance behaviors reflect, to some extent, the care system’s observations and interpretations of patient behavior, rather than fully corresponding to the subjective experiences directly expressed by the patients themselves.

Implementation process

Key challenges in the implementation process primarily arise during intervention delivery, ongoing support, and decision-making coordination. Some studies indicate that even when healthcare providers have prescribed assistive devices or offered fall prevention recommendations, patients may fail to benefit from the intervention—or even face new risks—due to misuse, forgetfulness, or misunderstanding if follow-up support, such as instruction tailored to cognitive abilities, repeated demonstrations, and follow-up support, is lacking [22]. Furthermore, in long-term care facilities or settings for end-stage dementia care, decisions involving medication adjustments or the initiation of new interventions often require the consent of a legal representative. Relevant studies indicate that when patients have lost their capacity for independent decision-making, delays in information transmission, inadequate communication, or coordination difficulties during the family authorization process may affect the timing of intervention initiation and the continuity of implementation [18]. Therefore, implementation barriers do not arise only during the intervention design phase but may also occur in the chain of events following the proposal, specifically in how recommendations are translated into sustained action. For fall prevention among older adults with dementia, post-delivery support, follow-up, and coordination mechanisms are critical components influencing implementation outcomes.

From identifying barriers to author-generated ERIC-informed recommendations

Building on the CFIR framework, this review also draws on the ERIC tool to match strategies to key barriers. Overall, consistent strategic directions include: training and ongoing guidance for caregivers and frontline staff; reducing the complexity of interventions; optimizing instruction and follow-up on assistive device use; improving interprofessional collaboration; enhancing family involvement; and facilitating implementation through environmental and process adjustments. This provides a reference framework for future implementation research and clinical management [41, 42]. It should be noted that the specific action recommendations in Table 5 were generated by the authors through ERIC-informed interpretation of the synthesized barriers and facilitators. They should not be interpreted as strategies directly evaluated, empirically confirmed, or proven to improve outcomes in the included studies. Rather, they represent theoretically informed implementation-design options that require further feasibility testing and implementation-effectiveness evaluation.

Discussion

This review integrates barriers and facilitators in the implementation of fall prevention for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment based on CFIR 2.0. The comprehensive results indicate that implementation difficulties do not stem solely from cognitive impairment at the patient level, but are simultaneously influenced by multifaceted factors such as intervention complexity, caregiver competence, organizational conditions, external support, and implementation processes. Therefore, directly applying fall prevention programs designed for the general elderly population to individuals with dementia may not yield the same implementation outcomes; in some cases, interventions that lack adaptation may even increase the burden of implementation or lead to unintended consequences [7, 22].

Shifting from a focus on patient cooperation to an equal emphasis on cognitive adaptation and environmental support

This study indicates that most implementation barriers are associated with interventions’ high demands on explicit memory, sustained comprehension, and active execution. For patients with progressive cognitive decline, such demands may limit the implementation of interventions. Consequently, intervention methods should be appropriately adjusted to reduce reliance on complex verbal instructions and long-term self-management skills, and instead adopt simpler, more repetitive, and cognitively appropriate implementation approaches [20, 32]. Furthermore, short-term, repetitive, and highly demonstrative training, or interventions that combine nonverbal cues with environmental supports, may be better suited to the implementation conditions of older adults with dementia [25, 35]. More specifically, intervention content should be matched to cognitive status. For mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia, interventions may retain more self-management elements but should use simplified written materials, large-print cues, teach-back, familiar technologies, privacy-sensitive monitoring, and caregiver-supported goal setting. For moderate dementia, interventions should rely less on independent recall and more on repeated demonstration, environmental cues, caregiver or staff prompting, and errorless or implicit learning. For severe dementia or late-stage disease, implementation should prioritize environmental modification, staff-led routines, comfort, avoidance of distressing alarms, non-restraint approaches, and alignment with goals of care rather than expecting independent adherence. At the same time, for patients who lack disease awareness or have inaccurate risk assessments, relying solely on self-regulation may yield limited results. In contrast, supportive environmental adjustments—such as low-stimulus, low-burden facility designs that reduce operational steps, and passive safety supports (e.g., ultra-low beds, anti-slip mats, and unobtrusive radar monitoring)—may, to some extent, compensate for patients’ limited ability to actively perform tasks [22, 36, 37]. For individuals with dementia, the degree of alignment between the intervention format and cognitive abilities may be more critical than the intervention content itself.

Clarifying the role of family caregivers across care settings

Family caregiver involvement was most directly applicable to home and community-based settings. In these settings, family caregivers often shoulder a significant burden in preventing falls among older adults with dementia, including supervision, reminders, assistance with assistive devices, coordination of medical resources, and ensuring safety during daily activities. This suggests that many home- and community-based fall prevention interventions are implemented with joint participation from both patients and caregivers [14]. However, caregiver involvement should not be assumed to operate in the same way across all care settings. In adult day care, acute care, rehabilitation, and long-term care settings, families may contribute to goal setting, consent, history taking, communication of patient preferences, and care transitions, but daily implementation often depends more on staff capability, staffing patterns, handover quality, and organizational routines. This setting-specific interpretation is consistent with the distribution of data sources across the included studies. In addition to patient-only perspectives, studies involving patient-caregiver dyads, family caregivers alone, and multiple stakeholders accounted for a substantial proportion of the data. This conclusion is consistent with the distribution of data sources across the included studies. An analysis of data sources reveals that, in addition to the patient’s own perspective, studies involving patient-caregiver dyads, those focusing solely on family members, and multi-stakeholder studies accounted for a significant proportion of the data. This suggests that in the daily care of older adults with dementia, family caregivers are not only sources of information but also key implementers responsible for the implementation, adjustment, and maintenance of many fall prevention interventions. Therefore, when designing future dementia-friendly fall prevention programs, it may be advisable to consider patients and family caregivers as a joint implementation unit. Some studies have confirmed that approaches such as co-designed education, home-based exercise support, and caregiver training can help improve the acceptability and sustainability of interventions [30, 40]. However, simply shifting implementation responsibilities to family caregivers without adequate training, emotional support, and respite resources may further increase the burden of care and undermine the sustainability of interventions [21, 39]. Therefore, involving caregivers in the implementation design should not simply mean increasing their responsibilities; rather, it should simultaneously address their support needs by embedding psychosocial support modules and providing substantive respite services as well as dementia-specific fall prevention knowledge, to effectively mitigate their caregiving burden in a long-term, high-pressure environment [34, 38].

Organizational and institutional environments may determine whether interventions are truly implemented

Implementation challenges in fall prevention among people with dementia do not occur solely at the patient or caregiver level but are also influenced by organizational and institutional environments, and these influences differed across care settings. Staffing shortages, workload pressure, fragmented handover, competing acute priorities, and risk-averse organizational cultures were most relevant to acute care, rehabilitation, and long-term care settings, where daily implementation depends heavily on staff capability, workflow integration, and institutional routines. In contrast, home and community-based studies more often emphasized caregiver burden, digital access, transportation barriers, and home environmental constraints. These setting-specific differences suggest that organizational implementation strategies should not be applied uniformly across care contexts but should be adapted to the dominant constraints of each setting. Some studies suggest that staffing shortages, tight workflows, high workloads, and a risk-averse organizational culture within institutions may all make it difficult to implement personalized, low-restraint fall prevention strategies that require continuous observation [26, 34, 35]. This finding suggests that improving the implementation of fall prevention for older adults with dementia may not rely solely on training frontline staff. If organizational processes, performance metrics, and care culture are not adjusted accordingly, the scope for implementing individual interventions may remain limited. Furthermore, some studies have indicated that certain measures perceived as safe—such as high-intensity alarms or defensive restraint measures—may, in specific contexts, provoke agitation, resistance, or other adverse outcomes in patients [26, 36]. When analyzed using the CFIR mapping table, it becomes evident that the determinants identified within the internal environment domain are particularly dense, encompassing multiple components, including structural characteristics, communication, culture, compatibility, relative priority, and access to resources and knowledge. This suggests that, in addition to patient-level factors, inadequate alignment between organizational systems and workflows is a significant barrier to the implementation of fall prevention interventions for older adults with dementia. Future research should not only focus on “which interventions to add”, but also assess which existing practices, although intended to ensure safety, may, in practice, impose additional burdens, reduce autonomy, or even conflict with overall patient care goals. Therefore, de-implementation—that is, identifying and gradually phasing out practices that may be harmful, burdensome, ineffective, or inconsistent with current care goals—is considered an integral part of implementation optimization, alongside the introduction of new interventions [18, 22].

For patients with dementia in the late stages or with a limited life expectancy, evaluating whether an intervention is effective should not be limited to “whether fractures were prevented” or “whether physical function improved”, but should incorporate broader care outcomes. Based on existing research, these outcomes are often reflected in more nuanced aspects: such as the timely discontinuation of high-risk medications, the alleviation of caregivers’ anxiety, the preservation of patients’ dignity, and the provision of psychological respite and support for family members. For people with advanced dementia, limited life expectancy, or those living in long-term care or palliative-oriented care settings, evaluation should not focus solely on fall counts, fracture prevention, or physical-function outcomes. Interventions should also be assessed in terms of their alignment with comfort-focused goals, avoidance of distress, preservation of mobility and dignity, caregiver or staff burden, and ethical acceptability [18, 40].

Confidence in the synthesized findings

Using this CERQual-informed narrative approach, we judged the confidence in several core synthesized findings as moderate to high. The finding that cognitively demanding, complex, or memory-dependent interventions hinder implementation was supported by multiple studies across different designs and settings, showed good coherence, and was highly relevant to the target population; therefore, confidence in this finding was judged as high. The findings that caregiver involvement, caregiver training, and environmental adaptation facilitate implementation were also coherent and directly relevant, but confidence was judged as moderate because supporting evidence was stronger in home and community settings than in acute-care or long-term care settings. Confidence in organizational-level findings, including the influence of staffing, workflow, interprofessional communication, and risk-averse culture, was judged as moderate because these determinants were reported across several studies but were often based on staff or proxy perspectives. Confidence in findings related to digital monitoring and passive sensor-based technologies was judged as low to moderate because the evidence was mainly derived from feasibility or small-scale studies and varied by technology type, user capability, and response protocol. Finally, confidence in findings related to proxy decision-making, legal-representative consent, and late-stage dementia care goals was judged as low because these issues were reported in fewer studies and were strongly setting-specific. Overall, the most robust synthesis findings concern the need to reduce cognitive burden, adapt interventions to dementia-related symptoms, involve caregivers appropriately, and align fall-prevention practices with organizational workflows.

Relationship to existing reviews and added value

Existing reviews and guidelines have summarized fall-prevention interventions, caregiver involvement, digital technologies, and implementation strategies in older adults or cognitively impaired populations [1, 3, 5, 7, 10]. The present review extends this literature by classifying recent implementation determinants using CFIR 2.0, distinguishing setting-specific barriers and facilitators, and translating these determinants into clearly labelled ERIC-informed recommendations. This review is generally consistent with those conclusions but differs in its research focus: while previous reviews emphasized “which implementation strategies were adopted,” this review further examines “which factors influence the implementation of these strategies.”Additionally, this review includes a variety of settings—such as hospitals, acute care wards, rehabilitation departments, and long-term care facilities—and employs CFIR 2.0 and ERIC for structured analysis. Consequently, it supplements the evidence regarding implementation related to organizational processes, institutional culture, cross-setting transitions, and proxy decision-making. Based on this, this review is better suited as a supplement to existing reviews; when combined, they contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the key issues in fall prevention for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment, ranging from strategy design to implementation.

Limitations and directions for future research

First, the included studies exhibited significant heterogeneity in terms of country, care setting, dementia or cognitive-impairment severity, stakeholder type, intervention content, study design, and outcome measures, limiting direct comparisons of results and making quantitative pooling unsuitable. Therefore, the synthesized determinants should be interpreted as context-sensitive rather than universally transferable. For example, caregiver burden and transport barriers may be more relevant to home or community settings, whereas staffing, workflow, handover, and risk-culture barriers may be more relevant to acute care, rehabilitation, or long-term care settings; Second, some original studies employed retrospective interviews, cross-sectional surveys, or process evaluation designs, and some data were derived from reports by family caregivers or professionals acting as proxies, which may be subject to recall bias, selection bias, and differences in perspective. Therefore, determinants related to patient motivation, resistance, acceptability, and perceived need should be interpreted cautiously when they were derived from caregiver or staff perspectives rather than direct patient reports. Third, this review included only studies published between January 2021 and March 2026. Therefore, it should be interpreted as a structured synthesis of recent implementation evidence rather than a comprehensive historical review of all evidence in the field. Important implementation studies published before 2021 may have been excluded from the primary synthesis. To mitigate this limitation, we manually traced the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews and used earlier literature to contextualize the findings where appropriate. Nevertheless, future reviews with a broader historical search window may be useful for examining how implementation evidence in dementia-related fall prevention has evolved over time. Fourth, although no database-level language restriction was applied, the search strategy was primarily developed in English and Chinese. Therefore, relevant studies published only in other languages may have been missed. In addition, gray literature was not systematically searched through dedicated gray-literature repositories, trial registries, conference proceedings, organizational reports, or preprint servers. Unpublished studies, conference abstracts without a full empirical report, and preprints without a corresponding complete empirical report were excluded. Records for which the full text could not be obtained were also excluded. These decisions were made to ensure sufficient methodological detail for quality appraisal and thematic synthesis, but they may have introduced publication bias, language-related bias, and availability bias. Future reviews could reduce these risks by incorporating broader multilingual search strategies, dedicated gray-literature searches, author contact for unavailable full texts, and sensitivity analyses comparing peer-reviewed and non-peer-reviewed evidence. Fifth, although we provided a narrative assessment of confidence in the synthesized findings, we did not conduct a formal GRADE-CERQual or equivalent confidence assessment. Therefore, the confidence judgments should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as formal certainty ratings. In addition, CFIR 2.0 mapping and ERIC-informed strategy alignment inevitably involved reviewer interpretation, although disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion. Finally, because most included studies reported determinants, feasibility, acceptability, or process outcomes rather than directly testing implementation strategies, this review cannot determine whether the ERIC-informed recommendations improve implementation performance, fall outcomes, adherence, caregiver burden, or other clinical outcomes.

Future research could advance in three areas. First, more mixed-methods implementation-effectiveness studies should be conducted to simultaneously evaluate clinical outcomes and implementation indicators. For older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment, reporting fall incidence alone may not sufficiently explain why an intervention is sustained or discontinued; future studies could incorporate indicators such as acceptability, feasibility, adherence, fidelity, uptake, caregiver burden, and resource consumption; Second, the suitability of existing fall prevention measures for individuals with cognitive impairment should be assessed, particularly regarding resistance, agitation, or increased caregiver burden that may result from highly stimulating alarms, memory-dependent directive interventions, or restrictive measures. In addition to introducing new interventions, identifying, reducing, or replacing practices with low suitability, high burden, or potential harm warrants attention, and outcomes such as patient safety, activity autonomy, caregiver burden, and ethical acceptability should be reported concurrently; Third, future studies should improve transparency in reporting implementation processes by clearly specifying intervention components, implementers, intervention frequency, training methods, caregiver involvement, environmental modifications, and follow-up support mechanisms. This would facilitate more precise strategy mapping and enable cross-setting comparability of implementation outcomes.

Conclusion

This systematic review included 27 studies and identified 49 barriers and 40 facilitators affecting the implementation of fall prevention for older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment. The determinants synthesized from the included literature indicate that implementation is influenced by multifaceted factors, including intervention complexity, patients’ cognitive and behavioral symptoms, caregivers’ knowledge and burden, organizational resources, external support conditions, and follow-up and coordination mechanisms during implementation. Based on the CFIR 2.0 mapping, current evidence suggests that interventions relying primarily on patients’ active memory and sustained adherence may be difficult to implement in real-world dementia care contexts. In contrast, cognitive adaptation, environmental support, caregiver involvement, and organizational process adjustment emerged as important implementation conditions in the literature.

The ERIC-informed recommendations presented in this review should be interpreted separately from the determinants identified in the included studies. These recommendations were generated by the authors through CFIR–ERIC-informed interpretation of the synthesized barriers and facilitators. They are intended to inform future implementation design, hypothesis generation, and empirical evaluation, but this review does not demonstrate that these ERIC-aligned strategies improve implementation outcomes, fall rates, adherence, caregiver burden, or other clinical outcomes. Further studies are needed to examine the feasibility, acceptability, and potential effects of these proposed strategies in different care settings. The transferability of these determinants and author-generated recommendations should therefore be judged according to the similarity between the contributing study contexts and the intended implementation setting.
