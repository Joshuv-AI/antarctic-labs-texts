Performance of Circulating Inflammatory Biomarkers and Krebs von den Lungen 6 for Predicting Progressive Pulmonary Fibrosis in Patients With Systemic Sclerosis–Associated Interstitial Lung Disease
Authors: Keina Yomono, Suiyuan Huang, Dinesh Khanna, Masataka Kuwana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the predictive performance of inflammatory biomarkers, including C‐reactive protein (CRP) and Krebs von den Lungen 6 (KL‐6), for the development of progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) in patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc)–associated interstitial lung disease (ILD).MethodsA total of 135 patients with SSc‐ILD were retrospectively selected from a single‐center prospective registry. The predictive performance of individual biomarkers and their combinations for PPF development was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC) values from receiver operating characteristic analysis. A time‐varying Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the prognostic significance of near‐term dynamic changes in KL‐6 levels.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 43 months, 26 patients (19%) developed PPF. Baseline CRP and KL‐6 levels did not correlate with each other. Patients with high baseline KL‐6 (≥1,000 U/mL) had more extensive ILD and reduced forced vital capacity compared to those with low levels. Baseline KL‐6 demonstrated superior predictive performance for ILD progression (with the highest AUC) compared to CRP. The PPF‐free survival rate was significantly lower in the high KL‐6 group than in the low KL‐6 group (P = 0.02). A doubling of KL‐6 levels at any time from 12 months to 6 months before PPF assessment or censoring was associated with a significantly higher risk of subsequent PPF development (hazard ratio 1.66 [95% confidence interval 1.13–2.45]).ConclusionBaseline KL‐6 and CRP values identify distinct subsets of SSc‐ILD patients. Elevated baseline KL‐6 levels and a significant rise over the subsequent six months are associated with an increased risk of PPF, supporting their potential as prognostic biomarkers.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease characterized by progressive fibrosis of the skin and internal organs, along with widespread vasculopathy. 1 Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a common manifestation of SSc and represents the leading cause of mortality in these patients. 2 A subset of SSc‐ILD patients develop progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), which is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. 3 , 4 Given the highly variable clinical course of SSc‐ILD, early identification of patients at risk for progression is critical to enable timely therapeutic interventions that may preserve lung function and improve outcomes. 4

Several clinical and serologic factors have been identified as predictors of accelerated forced vital capacity (FVC) decline and increased mortality, including diffuse cutaneous SSc (dcSSc), male sex, short SSc duration, rapid skin thickening, and anti–topoisomerase I (anti–topo I) antibodies. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In addition, circulating inflammatory biomarkers such as serum C‐reactive protein (CRP) and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) have been shown to correlate with ILD progression and reduced survival. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 A phase 3 randomized controlled focuSSced trial of tocilizumab used elevated markers of inflammation as part of its inclusion criteria, effectively enriching for patients at risk of FVC decline in the placebo group. 5 Post hoc analysis confirmed CRP as a prognostic marker of pulmonary function deterioration. 13

Beyond markers of inflammation, the alveolar epithelial injury biomarker Krebs von den Lungen 6 (KL‐6) has emerged as a valuable tool for screening, assessing severity, and predicting outcomes in SSc‐ILD. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 KL‐6 is an antigenic epitope on the sialylated glycan chain of the high‐molecular‐weight glycoprotein MUC1 and is recognized by a monoclonal antibody generated by immunizing mice with a lung adenocarcinoma cell line. 19 MUC1 is primarily produced by type II pneumocytes during regeneration, and its translocation into the bloodstream requires basement membrane breakdown and increased vascular permeability, which could be induced by lung parenchymal destruction. Recent data from the Scleroderma Lung Study II (SLS II) further demonstrated that longitudinal increases in KL‐6 following treatment with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or cyclophosphamide (CYC) were associated with subsequent development of PPF. 20 These circulating biomarkers have utility not only in clinical practice to guide early treatment decisions, but also in clinical trials to identify and enrich appropriate “at risk” patient populations at higher risk of progression. However, the predictive performance of these risk factors has varied across studies and cohorts, 21 and disease progression remains difficult to forecast on an individual level. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed data from a single‐center prospective SSc registry to evaluate the predictive utility of CRP and KL‐6, individually and in combination, for progression of ILD in patients with SSc, with the aim of improving risk stratification in this heterogeneous disease.

METHODS

Study participants

This was a retrospective analysis using a single‐center prospective SSc cohort database that consecutively enrolled patients with SSc since August 2014 at the Scleroderma/Myositis Centre of Excellence, Nippon Medical School Hospital, Tokyo, Japan. 22 The eligibility criteria for this study included fulfillment of the 2013 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification criteria for SSc 23 and the presence of ILD, as confirmed by high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest at the time of cohort enrollment. Patients with missing data regarding ILD progression outcomes were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Nippon Medical School Hospital (B‐2019‐007), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Clinical data

Essential clinical data collected at the time of enrollment and at follow‐up visits were prospectively registered in the database until mortality, loss to follow‐up, or the last visit before August 2022. The assessment interval was not predefined. Additional clinical, laboratory, and imaging data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. Disease duration was defined as months since the onset of non‐Raynaud symptoms attributable to SSc. dcSSc was classified according to the criteria proposed by LeRoy et al. 24 Pulmonary functional test parameters included FVC and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, which were used as actual measures and demonstrated predicted values. The modified Rodnan total skin thickness score (mRSS) was used as an index for the extent and degree of skin thickness. 25 In terms of the assessment of chest HRCT images, the morphologic pattern of ILD was classified based on the official American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) statement, 26 and the extent of ILD was semiquantitatively assessed according to the procedure proposed by Goh et al. 27 ILD progression was defined by PPF, which is defined according to the official ATS/ERS/Japan Respiratory Society/Latin American Thoracic Society clinical practice guidelines. 28 The worsening of respiratory symptoms was evaluated using validated patient‐reported outcome measures, including the modified British Medical Research Council dyspnea scale 29 and visual analog scales for worsening dyspnea and cough. Under Japanese health insurance, all patients are required to undergo a follow‐up visit within three months. Symptoms were collected at each visit, and pulmonary function test and chest HRCT were performed every 3 to 12 months depending on the clinical practice. Any patients who fulfilled the PPF definition during the follow‐up were regarded as development of PPF.

SSc‐related autoantibodies were identified via a commercially available chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay or an in‐house immunoprecipitation assay. 30 Serum KL‐6 levels were measured by an assay system using a latex‐fixed anti‐KL‐6 monoclonal antibody with an automated analyzer (Nanopia KL‐6; Sekisui Medical Co Ltd). A CRP level ≥6 mg/L or ESR ≥28 mm/h was used as an index of elevated markers of inflammation according to the inclusion criteria of the focuSSced trial. 5 The cutoff value of KL‐6 was provisionally set at 1,000 U/mL, 31 although various cutoff values that have been reported as predictors of ILD progression in patients with SSc 15 , 18 , 32 , 33 were tested in the sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean and SD or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Two‐sided Student's t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare parametric and nonparametric variables, respectively. Fisher's exact test or the chi‐square test was used for categorical binary variables, as appropriate. For three or more groups, analysis of variance was employed for continuous variables that satisfied the assumption of normality, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for variables that did not satisfy this assumption. Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons, and pairwise comparisons were performed when the overall P value was <0.05. Single regression analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between two continuous variables, and results are shown as correlation coefficient (r) and P value. The optimal cutoff value was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis combined with the Youden index and was shown with the 95% confidence interval (CI). Cumulative rates free from ILD progression events were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis. To investigate whether other risk factors for rapid progression exert an additive effect on the predictive utility of KL‐6 for PPF, subgroup analyses were conducted across clinically relevant covariates. Fisher's exact test and DeLong's test were employed to compare sensitivity/specificity and AUC, respectively. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. To assess the prognostic significance of dynamic KL‐6 changes, a time‐varying Cox model was applied using KL‐6 measurements taken 12 and 6 months before either the first PPF event or the last follow‐up visit. KL‐6 values were log2‐transformed to facilitate interpretation per doubling. These models were adjusted for clinically relevant covariates with Firth's penalized likelihood approach. All the statistical analysis was not prespecified. The number of patients for each analysis varies based on the availability of complete data.

Data availability

The raw data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Among the 460 patients who were enrolled in our prospective SSc cohort as of August 2022, 135 patients who had HRCT‐confirmed ILD at baseline were eligible for this study (Supplementary Figure S1). Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age at study entry was 56.4 years, and the median duration of SSc was 23.0 (IQR 6.0–60.0) months. Eighty‐seven patients (64.4%) were classified as having dcSSc, and the predominant autoantibody was an anti–topo I antibody, which was detected in 54.8% of patients. Most patients demonstrated limited disease on HRCT, resulting in a median extent of ILD on HRCT of 10% and a mean percent predicted FVC (ppFVC) of 88.0% in the whole cohort, whereas 34.1% of the patients demonstrated extensive disease on HRCT. Only a few patients were receiving SSc therapies (5%) before enrollment (Supplementary Table S1). There was no significant difference observed in baseline characteristics between the 135 enrolled patients and the 40 patients who were excluded from the analysis due to missing data (data not shown).

Development of PPF during follow‐up

Of the 135 patients with SSc‐ILD, 26 (19.3%) developed PPF during a median follow‐up of 43.0 (IQR 16.0–68.5) months despite treatment with CYC, MMF, methotrexate, tocilizumab, rituximab, nintedanib, or a combination of these therapies in 63.7% of patients (Supplementary Table S1). Patients who developed PPF were treated more frequently than those who did not (96.2% vs 56.0%). Of the 26 patients who developed PPF, the most common PPF‐defining components were radiologic and symptomatic criteria (n = 16; 61.5%), followed by physiologic and symptomatic criteria (n = 5; 19.2%), physiologic and radiologic criteria (n = 3; 11.5%), and all three criteria (n = 2; 7.7%). As shown in Table 1, compared with the non‐PPF group, the PPF group had a higher prevalence of anti–topo I antibody (P = 0.04) and a greater ILD extent on HRCT (P = 0.01).

Correlations between markers of inflammation and KL‐6 at baseline

Baseline CRP, ESR, and KL‐6 levels were distributed across a wide range among the patients. When correlations between the biomarkers were examined, CRP and ESR levels were correlated with each other (r = 0.51, P < 0.001); however, no correlation was observed between a marker of inflammation (CRP or ESR) and KL‐6 (Figure 1), indicating that the levels of markers of inflammation and KL‐6 were independent. When patients with SSc‐ILD were classified into four subgroups based on above‐ or below‐predefined cutoff values for CRP (6 mg/L) and KL‐6 (1,000 U/mL), only five patients (4%) exhibited high levels of both CRP and KL‐6. Twelve patients (9%) demonstrated isolated increases in CRP, whereas 29 (22%) exhibited isolated increases in KL‐6. The remaining patients (64%) exhibited low levels of both CRP and KL‐6. As shown in Table 2, patients with high KL‐6 levels demonstrated a greater extent of ILD on HRCT and lower ppFVC at baseline, resulting in a greater proportion of extensive disease (irrespective of CRP levels). Similar results were obtained when ESR was used as a marker of inflammation instead of CRP (Supplementary Table S3).

Performance of circulating biomarkers for predicting PPF development

We first compared baseline CRP, ESR, and KL‐6 levels between patients who developed PPF and those who did not (Table 1). There was no difference in CRP or ESR levels between the patients with PPF and those without PPF, but baseline serum KL‐6 levels were significantly higher in the PPF group than in the non‐PPF group (median 963 [IQR 640–1,365] vs 609 [IQR 385–934], P = 0.009).

We next examined the performance of baseline CRP, ESR, and KL‐6, as well as their combination, for predicting PPF development (Table 3). Specificity of baseline CRP and KL‐6 levels was favorable (>70%); however, the sensitivity was much lower in CRP compared with KL‐6 (11.5% vs 44.0%, respectively), resulting in a lower area under the curve (AUC) value for CRP than for KL‐6 (0.49 vs 0.61, respectively). When various combinations of biomarkers were tested, the combination of CRP and KL‐6 exhibited the highest AUC of 0.58, which was comparable to that of KL‐6 alone (AUC of 0.61). The addition of CRP in combination with KL‐6 resulted in a slight increase in sensitivity but in a decrease in specificity and positive predictive value. Performance of KL‐6 for predicting risk for PPF development was consistently observed in patients who received treatment with immunosuppressive or antifibrotic therapies, as well as in patient subgroups stratified by the presence or absence of risk factors for rapid ILD progression, 33 including dcSSc, anti–topo I antibody positivity, and disease duration <18 months, whereas the AUC value was lower in patients with mRSS ≥18, compared with those without (P = 0.04), primarily due to low sensitivity (Supplementary Figure S2).

The cumulative survival rates of patients free of PPF were compared between the two groups, as stratified based on the above‐ or below‐predefined cutoff levels of CRP, KL‐6, or their combination (Figure 2). The baseline CRP failed to discriminate patients who developed PPF from those who did not develop PPF. There was no better separation on survival curves when CRP was combined with KL‐6. When the patients were stratified and divided into four groups based on high or low levels of CRP and KL‐6, the cumulative survival rates free of PPF tended to be lower in the group with high KL‐6 regardless of the CRP, thereby again demonstrating the lack of additional benefits of measuring CRP in addition to KL‐6 (Supplementary Figure S3).

Optimal cutoff value of KL‐6 for predicting PPF development

This study used 1,000 U/mL as a provisional cutoff value for the serum KL‐6 level. ROC analysis of our cohort of SSc‐ILD patients revealed that 745 U/mL was the optimal cutoff value for predicting PPF development (Supplementary Figure S4). We compared the predictive performance for predicting the PPF development of various KL‐6 cutoff levels previously reported to be useful for predicting ILD progression in the literature, including 1,273 U/mL, 15 1,000 U/mL, 31 923 U/mL, 33 and 500 U/mL18 (Supplementary Table S4). The cutoff value of 745 U/mL exhibited the best AUC due to the favorable balance of sensitivity and specificity. When 745 U/mL was used as a cutoff of KL‐6 level instead of 1,000 U/mL, AUC values of KL‐6 alone and the combination of CRP and KL‐6 for predicting PPF development were slightly improved; however, there was again no superiority of the combination of KL‐6 and CRP compared with KL‐6 alone (Supplementary Table S5 and Figure S5).

Prognostic significance of near‐term dynamic changes in KL‐6 levels

We further evaluated whether rising KL‐6 levels were predictive of subsequent PPF in patients with SSc‐ILD (Table 4). In the time‐varying Cox model adjusted for clinically relevant covariates (ILD extent on HRCT, usual interstitial pneumonia pattern on HRCT, and FVC), a doubling of KL‐6 levels at any time during the six months was associated with 66% increased risk of subsequent PPF (model 1; hazard ratio [HR] 1.66 [95% CI 1.13–2.45]; P < 0.01). This association remained significant after adjusting for clinically relevant covariates, including anti–topo I antibody status (model 2; HR 1.84 [95% CI 1.24–2.74]; P < 0.01), and anti–topo I antibody was an independent predictor for PPF development in this model. Patients with positive anti–topo I antibody showed 257% higher risk of subsequent PPF compared to the negative (HR 3.57 [95% CI 1.61–7.92]; P < 0.01). Sensitivity analysis using 72 patients who received treatment found the same trend for a doubling of KL‐6 levels at any time during the six months as a risk of subsequent PPF development; however, the results were not statistically significant (Supplementary Tables S6).

DISCUSSION

Circulating biomarkers have gained considerable attention as convenient, noninvasive tools for diagnosis and prognosis in patients with SSc and those with SSc‐ILD. 35 In this study, we found that serum levels of CRP and KL‐6 were not correlated, suggesting that these biomarkers capture distinct biologic processes. Stratification based on CRP and KL‐6 levels identified different patient subgroups, with KL‐6 outperforming CRP in predicting the development of PPF. Notably, combining CRP with KL‐6 did not improve predictive accuracy. Furthermore, a six‐month increase in KL‐6 levels was significantly associated with near‐term PPF occurrence, highlighting its potential as a dynamic prognostic marker.

The divergent roles of CRP and KL‐6 in SSc‐ILD pathogenesis likely explain their lack of correlation and differing predictive value. KL‐6, a high‐molecular‐weight glycoprotein expressed on regenerating type II pneumocytes, reflects lung parenchymal destruction. Elevated KL‐6 levels have been associated with severity and progression risk in various chronic fibrotic ILDs and are thought to result from epithelial damage, basement membrane disruption, and increased vascular permeability. 19 , 36 In contrast, CRP is an acute‐phase reactant synthesized in the liver in response to interleukin‐6, a cytokine known to be up‐regulated in the skin and lungs of patients with SSc and linked to adverse outcomes. 37 The pathogenesis of PPF in SSc‐ILD is believed to involve an initial immune‐mediated or inflammatory insult, followed by lung parenchymal destruction and the progression of fibrotic remodeling. 38 In this context, elevated CRP may reflect upstream inflammation, whereas increased KL‐6 levels likely indicates downstream lung‐specific injury and ongoing fibrosis. This distinction may explain why KL‐6 correlates more strongly with ILD severity and progression. Indeed, in our cohort, patients with high KL‐6 levels exhibited a greater extent of ILD on HRCT, lower ppFVC, and a higher prevalence of extensive disease.

Our findings suggest that baseline serum KL‐6 levels are a reliable predictor of ILD progression, as defined by the development of PPF, in patients with SSc‐ILD. Importantly, the predictive value of KL‐6 was consistent regardless of the presence or absence of known risk factors for rapid ILD progression. In contrast, CRP appears to have more limited utility as a predictive biomarker, potentially applicable only within specific patient subgroups, such as those with early dcSSc and active skin disease, like the patient population enrolled in the focuSSced trial. 5 These findings suggest that the prognostic performance of CRP may be highly dependent on the characteristics of the population under investigation. Notably, in our study, 9 of 17 patients with elevated baseline CRP levels were treated with tocilizumab, which may have attenuated ILD progression and thereby underestimated CRP's prognostic value. Given this context, future clinical trials aimed at evaluating therapies for fibrotic ILD progression may benefit from incorporating both KL‐6 and CRP into inclusion criteria. Although KL‐6 provides robust and consistent predictive utility, the addition of CRP could enhance sensitivity and capture a broader range of patients at risk, particularly when interpreted alongside relevant clinical features such as early dcSSc or active cutaneous disease. 5

We also demonstrated predictive significance for serially obtained KL‐6 levels for six months before PPF occurrence. Although previous studies have yielded mixed results regarding the association of change in KL‐6 levels for progression of SSc‐ILD, 20 , 39 , 40 these inconsistencies may be due to differences in the timing of KL‐6 assessments relative to progression events. In our study, we specifically evaluated KL‐6 dynamics immediately preceding PPF development, rather than evaluating the association of change in KL‐6 levels for PPF development because of lack of systematic longitudinal data. We found that rising KL‐6 levels were predictive of imminent ILD progression. These findings support the use of serial KL‐6 measurements, not just baseline values, as a noninvasive biomarker to monitor disease activity, assess progression risk, and guide clinical decision‐making in SSc‐ILD.

Previous studies in patients with SSc‐ILD have consistently demonstrated the utility of KL‐6 as a biomarker for predicting subsequent FVC decline and the development of end‐stage lung disease. However, the optimal cutoff value for KL‐6 varies across studies. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 37 , 38 In our cohort, the optimal threshold for predicting the PPF development was 745 U/mL, which is lower than those reported in earlier studies. For instance, in the Genetics versus Environment in Scleroderma Outcome Study (GENISOS) cohort, a cutoff of 1,273 U/mL yielded high predictive performance for end‐stage lung disease, with a sensitivity of 87.5% and specificity of 100%. 17 Similarly, a post hoc analysis of the SLS II found that a KL‐6 threshold of 1,549 U/mL predicted FVC decline in patients treated with MMF, with 100% sensitivity and 74% specificity. 16 These discrepancies in cutoff values may be partly explained by differences in baseline ILD severity. In the GENISOS and SLS II cohorts, the mean ppFVC values were 75.2% and 66.5%, respectively, substantially lower than the mean ppFVC of 89.0% in our cohort. Given the established inverse correlation between KL‐6 and FVC in SSc‐ILD, 41 and the greater extent of ILD among patients with elevated KL‐6 in our study, it is likely that the optimal KL‐6 threshold for predicting disease progression depends on the underlying severity of ILD in the study population.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, this study was the single‐center, retrospective design involving relatively small number of Japanese patients. This may limit the generalizability of our findings and restricts extrapolation to other ethnic groups with different genetic or clinical backgrounds. Second, treatment effects during follow‐up likely influenced ILD progression, as therapeutic decisions were made at the discretion of treating physicians. As a result, patients perceived to be at higher risk may have received earlier or more aggressive treatment, potentially confounding the relationship between baseline biomarkers and outcomes. Finally, since analyses were not prespecified and assessment intervals were not predefined, lack of timely HRCT or pulmonary function test evaluation could lead to the fact that some PPF cases were misclassified as non‐PPF. Therefore, our results need to be validated in larger, independent dedicated prospective cohorts or clinical trials.

In summary, both baseline and longitudinal measurements of KL‐6 serve as valuable noninvasive biomarkers for monitoring progression of lung fibrosis and informing clinical decision‐making in patients with SSc‐ILD.
