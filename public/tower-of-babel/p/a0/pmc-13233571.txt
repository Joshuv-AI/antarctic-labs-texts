Effect of Buddhist music on intraoperative and postoperative opioid requirements during general anesthesia: A randomized controlled pilot study
Authors: Wei-Ti Hsu, Mei-Ling Shen, Ai-Chia-Lin Liang, Yan-Cen Lin, Wei-Yong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMusic therapy has been associated with reduce perioperative anxiety and pain, but evidence on intraoperative Buddhist music therapy during general anesthesia is limited. We conducted a double-blind, randomized controlled pilot study in 32 adults undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia, assigned to Buddhist music, mute (ambient noise insulation), or control group. Earbuds were applied after anesthetic induction, and the music intervention was continued throughout surgery. Outcomes included intraoperative and postoperative opioid (fentanyl) consumption, administration frequency, and postoperative pain intensity. Compared with the mute group, the music group had lower cumulative postoperative opioid use (p = 0.009) and fewer opioid administrations (p = 0.011). The music group also used less intraoperative opioid than controls (p = 0.003) and had fewer intraoperative opioid administrations than both mute (p = 0.013) and control (p = 0.003) groups. These preliminary findings suggest that Buddhist music may be a feasible and convenient adjunct to perioperative analgesia; larger, preferably stratified trials are required to confirm efficacy and mechanisms.

Introduction

In modern medicine, pain management during and after surgery is critically important. Intraoperative pain stimulation activates the sympathetic nervous system, prompting the body to release considerable amounts of stress hormones. This increases heart rate and blood pressure, which, in turn, elevate myocardial oxygen consumption and potentially cause myocardial hypoxia or cardiovascular-related complications.1 However, postoperative pain should also not be underestimated. Studies have shown that the incidence of postoperative pain is as high as 80%, with approximately 10%–50% of these cases potentially progressing to chronic pain.2,3 Inadequately controlled postoperative pain can lead to hypertension, arrhythmia, and impaired ventilation, and it may cause sleep disorders, decreased immune function, and emotional problems in patients.3,4 These issues substantially impact the speed of overall recovery and quality of life.

In clinical practice, opioids are widely used as the primary means of anesthesia and analgesia to manage pain during surgery and maintain cardiovascular stability. However, excessive opioid use can lead to a range of adverse effects, including hypotension, respiratory depression, nausea, constipation, and cognitive disturbances.5 Elderly patients and patients with obesity are particularly susceptible to respiratory depression owing to altered opioid metabolism.6,7 Therefore, the development of non-pharmacological interventions that can complement or partially replace opioids has become a key focus in efforts to enhance surgical safety and improve patient comfort.

In recent years, music therapy has gained considerable attention as a non-pharmacological intervention. Numerous studies have shown that music can significantly reduce stress, anxiety, and pain perception.8,9 It is thought that its analgesic mechanism involves the activation of brain regions related to emotional regulation, such as the limbic and reward systems. Music stimulates the release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and endorphins, which help suppress pain and enhance psychological comfort.10,11 Previous studies have used Western classical music, soft instrumental music, folk songs, and popular music.12 However, research exploring the effects of Eastern religious music, such as Buddhist chants, on pain management is lacking. As an ancient religious practice, religious hymns (Buddhist chants) not only play a pivotal role in religious ceremonies but are also believed to calm the mind, purify emotions, and promote mental well-being.13,14,15 Nevertheless, empirical evidence is still needed to determine whether such Buddhist chants offer comparable analgesic benefits during the perioperative period.

Therefore, this randomized controlled pilot study aimed to evaluate the effect of Buddhist music on intraoperative and postoperative pain management in patients who have undergone surgery under general anesthesia.

Similarly, the opioid administration frequency in the PACU significantly differed among the three groups (p = 0.035). Secondary outcomes

Significant differences were observed in the cumulative intraoperative opioid consumption among the three groups (p = 0.012). The music group exhibited significantly lower total opioid consumption than the control group (50.0 [43.8–50.0] μg vs. 100.0 [100.0–125.0] μg; p = 0.003). However, no significant difference was observed between the music and mute groups (p = 0.208) or between the mute and control groups (p = 0.079) (Figure 2 and Table 2).

Similarly, the frequency of intraoperative opioid administration differed significantly among the three groups (p = 0.007). The music group received opioids significantly less frequently than the mute (1.0 [1.0–1.3] vs. 2.5 [2.0–3.0]; p = 0.013) and control (1.0 [1.0–1.3] vs. 2.5 [2.0–4.0]; p = 0.003) groups. However, no significant difference was observed between the mute and control groups (p = 0.599) (Figure 2 and Table 2).

The median numeric rating scale (NRS) score upon arrival at the PACU was comparable among the three groups: 3.0 (1.8–4.5) in the music group, 5.5 (3.0–7.0) in the mute group, and 3.0 (2.0–6.0) in the control group (p = 0.464). Furthermore, the NRS scores at PACU discharge were similar across the groups (p = 0.228), as were the changes in the NRS scores from PACU arrival to discharge (p = 0.631). No significant differences were observed in preoperative mean blood pressure (MBP) among the music, mute, and control groups (p = 0.580). Similarly, no significant differences in MBP were observed upon arrival in the PACU (p = 0.474). The changes in MBP from before surgery to PACU arrival were also not statistically significant among the three groups (p = 0.199) (Table 3).

Moreover, significant differences in heart rate were found among the three groups before surgery and upon arrival at the PACU (p = 0.021 and p = 0.044, respectively). The Tukey-Kramer post-hoc test revealed that the music group exhibited a significantly lower heart rate than the control group. However, the changes in heart rate from before surgery to PACU arrival were not significantly different among the three groups (p = 0.844) (Table 3).

Adverse events

Adverse events were sparse across all three groups. One patient in each group developed intraoperative hypertension that required nicardipine administration. Intraoperative hypotension necessitating ephedrine administration occurred once in each group. In the PACU, one episode of vomiting occurred in the mute group, and one episode of bradycardia (heart rate <50 beats/min) requiring atropine administration occurred in the music group. These adverse events were deemed unrelated to the music intervention, and no serious side effects were observed.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to focus on the use of Buddhist music, representing a music type distinct from Western classical or instrumental music, during general anesthesia for pain management. Several published articles have explored the effect of religious music, such as its ability to reduce anxiety during dental extraction and surgery under regional anesthesia,16,17 as well as the role of Buddhist-based mindfulness in relieving pain in patients with cancer.18 The results of our pilot study suggest that listening to Buddhist music during surgery under general anesthesia may be associated with lower intraoperative and postoperative opioid requirements and administration frequency. These preliminary findings suggest that Eastern Buddhist music may have potential as an analgesic adjunct.

Muzzi et al. investigated the effect of intraoperative music stimulation in pediatric adenotonsillectomy. They found that patients who received ambient noise insulation or no intervention during surgery reported higher pain perception during the postoperative recovery period than those who received intraoperative music stimulation.19 Furthermore, Nilsson et al. investigated the effect of intraoperative and postoperative music exposure on patients undergoing ambulatory surgery under general anesthesia. Their results indicated that patients with intraoperative exposure to music reported significantly lower pain intensity at 1–2 h postoperatively than the controls, although no difference in opioid requirement was observed compared with the control group.20 In our pilot study, no significant differences in postoperative pain scores and blood pressure were found among the three groups. In addition, heart rate was imbalanced among the groups at baseline and upon arrival in the PACU, whereas the change in heart rate over time was not significantly different. Therefore, these exploratory hemodynamic findings should not be interpreted as evidence of cardiovascular modulation by Buddhist music. However, intraoperative exposure to Buddhist music was associated with lower postoperative opioid requirements than ambient noise insulation (mute group) and lower intraoperative opioid requirements than the control condition. These findings suggest a potential opioid-sparing association of Buddhist music. This association should be interpreted independently of postoperative pain scores and hemodynamic measures, which did not differ significantly among the groups.

Perioperative music interventions vary widely in genre (e.g., classical, instrumental, folk, rock, popular, religious, or patient-selected), timing (before, during, or after surgery), and anesthetic approach (regional anesthesia, sedation, or general anesthesia). Two meta-analyses of perioperative music interventions have summarized studies showing that intraoperative music exposure may affect postoperative pain, stress, and opioid consumption during general anesthesia; however, effects appear to vary by the type of surgery and music intervention design.21,22 Together, these findings suggest that the impact of music under general anesthesia may depend on both the music genre and the surgical context. Our pilot study suggests that Buddhist music may represent a feasible adjunct to analgesia during general anesthesia for minor surgical procedures.

It is generally believed that the most crucial aspect of music therapy is its influence on patients’ subjective consciousness. In most studies reporting that music can effectively reduce pain perception, music intervention was typically given before or during surgery under regional anesthesia or light sedation. In such scenarios, the subjects were conscious, and music reduced their attention to pain by diverting it, thereby lessening their subjective pain perception.23 Alternatively, it could be a placebo effect, where the mind’s influence leads to physiological changes based on improvement expectations, resulting in better pain relief for subjects listening to music,24 particularly if they preferred the music style. Nevertheless, Schwender et al. reported that intraoperative auditory processing may be, at least partially, preserved during general anesthesia.25 Consistently, a recent meta-analysis suggested that intraoperative auditory stimulation can affect postoperative outcomes.26 In our pilot study, the observed association between listening to Buddhist music under general anesthesia and lower perioperative opioid requirements suggests that some mechanisms beyond conscious distraction may be involved. However, these findings should be considered hypothesis generating. Placebo and expectancy effects cannot be excluded, as preoperative expectations and other contextual factors may indirectly influence perioperative outcomes despite reduced conscious perception under general anesthesia.

To date, the mechanism underlying the possible analgesic adjuvant effects of Buddhist music, such as Buddhist chanting, remains unclear. Several studies have confirmed the effects of Buddhist chanting on human physiology and neuroscience. Taniyama et al. confirmed that listening to Buddhist chanting can reduce the levels of physiological stress markers, such as salivary alpha-amylase.14 Gao et al. employed multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to observe the brain responses of participants who performed Buddhist chanting while viewing fearful images; they observed enhanced brain activity in the amygdala, thalamus, and midbrain.27 Moreover, Sik et al. used high-density electroencephalogram (EEG) and functional MRI to study the brain’s response and found that repetitive Buddhist chanting can induce lower centrality and higher delta-wave power.28 In addition to effects on the central nervous system, perioperative music interventions may mitigate the surgical stress response through modulation of the autonomic nervous system. Music may increase parasympathetic activity and heart rate variability, thereby influencing nociception and opioid requirement.29,30,31 Based on the existing literature, prior findings raise the possibility that Buddhist music may influence nociceptive or stress-related processes through mechanisms involving emotional regulation, relaxation-associated brain-wave activity, or autonomic nervous system modulation. However, these mechanistic inferences remain hypothesis generating in the context of the present study, as EEG, stress hormone concentrations, and heart rate variability were not evaluated in this pilot study. Future studies should standardize intervention parameters (e.g., music genre, volume, and duration) and include objective physiological metrics (e.g., EEG, stress hormones, and heart rate variability) to elucidate the possible pathways through which Buddhist music may influence perioperative opioid requirements.

In this study, one episode of bradycardia occurred in the music group, which was attributed to the patient’s pre-existing history of bradycardia. No other side effects related to music intervention or serious side effects were reported, consistent with previous review articles.32 Our findings suggest that Buddhist music may offer practical advantages as an adjunct to multimodal analgesia: it is safe, convenient, low cost, and non-invasive, and it can be delivered passively via earphones, even during general anesthesia. Potential limitations include variability in exposure time due to differences in operative duration, interindividual differences in musical preference or cultural familiarity, and heterogeneity in the music (e.g., melody and rhythm).

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center pilot study, relatively few participants were enrolled in each group, thereby limiting the precision of the results. In the future, larger randomized controlled trials should be conducted. Second, the eligible participants were not restricted to patients who had undergone any specific type of surgery or anesthesia. Varying surgical and anesthetic methods across groups may introduce bias due to different degrees of stress and pain intensity. Because of the exploratory design and heterogeneity of procedures and anesthetic protocols, these preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution. Future research should assess Buddhist music by using more homogeneous surgical and anesthetic protocols or larger trials with stratified randomization to optimize study design. Third, although the participants were unconscious under general anesthesia, we cannot rule out the notion that differences in personal religious beliefs might have biased the effects of Buddhist music. Therefore, future subgroup analyses based on religious beliefs are recommended to determine the effects of Buddhist music on opioid requirements. Fourth, due to the inherent limitation of subjective assessment using the NRS score, we evaluated only the postoperative pain intensity. Future studies should consider monitoring intraoperative pain intensity to observe the analgesic effects of Buddhist music more clearly. Fifth, the influence or association of sex and/or gender with study outcomes was not examined because this pilot study was not powered for subgroup analyses, and data on gender, race, ethnicity, and ancestry were not collected. Therefore, the generalizability of these findings across demographic groups remains uncertain. Sixth, these discussions of the potential advantages of Buddhist music should be regarded as conceptual rather than comparative, as this study did not directly evaluate Buddhist music against other non-pharmacological interventions. Finally, the observed opioid-sparing association should be interpreted independently of postoperative pain scores and hemodynamic measures, which did not differ significantly among the groups.

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence that listening to Buddhist music during surgery under general anesthesia may be associated with lower demand for intraoperative and postoperative opioid requirements compared with routine care and an ambient noise-insulation condition, without increasing anesthesia-related complications; however, no significant effects of Buddhist music were observed on postoperative pain intensity or hemodynamic parameters. Given the small sample size and procedural heterogeneity, these preliminary findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger, adequately powered and, preferably, stratified trials are needed to confirm whether Buddhist music has clinically meaningful efficacy and to optimize intervention parameters and elucidate underlying mechanisms. Overall, Buddhist music may represent a feasible and safe non-pharmacologic adjunct for further investigation into perioperative pain management.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

All enrolled participants were recruited from Taichung Tzu-Chi Hospital, Buddhist Tzu-Chi Medical Foundation, Taiwan. Adults aged 20 to 80 years were eligible for inclusion, regardless of sex. Signed written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Ethical approval for this study (REC No.: REC113-11) was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Taichung Tzu-Chi Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan. Among the 29 participants included in the final analysis, 15 were female and 14 were male. Method details

Trial design

This single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled pilot study was designed to evaluate the efficacy of Buddhist music in perioperative pain management in patients undergoing surgery under general anesthesia. Ethical approval for this study (REC No.: REC113-11) was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Taichung Tzu-Chi Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06467747).

Participants

The study included patients who were scheduled for surgery at Taichung Tzu-Chi Hospital, Buddhist Tzu-Chi Medical Foundation, Taiwan. All enrolled patients were screened and informed of the study protocol prior to surgery. The inclusion criteria were age 20–80 years, undergoing elective or ambulatory surgery under general anesthesia (including thoracic, otolaryngologic, breast, varicose vein ligation, and spinal procedures; with or without endotracheal intubation; anticipated operative duration ≥30 min), and American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS) class I to III. The exclusion criteria included hearing impairment, severe pulmonary or cardiovascular disease, neuropsychiatric disorder, chronic pain syndrome (defined as long-term systemic steroid or opioid use), pregnancy, and refusal to participate. Prior to randomization, all participants provided signed, written informed consent. Based on a previous meta-analysis of the effects of music on postoperative opioid requirements21 and established guidelines for sample size selection in pilot studies,33 a sample size of 10 participants per group was recommended for a continuous outcome measure. To account for potential dropout, a total sample size of 40 participants (approximately 13 per group) was targeted. Randomization was not stratified by surgical type, operative duration, or intubation/anesthetic technique because of the exploratory pilot design.

Study protocol

All patients were randomly assigned to one of the following groups: (1) music, (2) mute, and (3) control. A computer-generated random number table was used for participant allocation following simple randomization procedures. In the music group, patients used earbuds (Apple AirPods Pro) connected to a smartphone (Apple iPhone) to listen to Buddhist chants (Taiwanese music). The Buddhist chants, “Infinite Dharma,” are excerpts from the Sutra of Infinite Meanings, and the “Infinite Dharma MV” (updated June 2010, Buddhist Tzu-Chi Foundation) was used. A single prerecorded Buddhist music track (“Infinite Dharma”) with a total duration of 5 min was played in full and looped continuously from after induction until the end of surgery; therefore, the total exposure duration corresponded to the individual surgical time. The smartphone volume was preset preoperatively to a patient-confirmed comfortable level and was not adjusted during surgery. Patients in the mute group wore earbuds to block ambient noise without being connected to a smartphone. In contrast, patients in the control group did not wear earbuds and received routine care corresponding to the current standard of general anesthesia. The research nurse prepared the auditory stimulation device according to the assigned research groups. The patients were blinded to the group allocation.

Figure 1A shows the study flowchart. Upon arrival at the operating room, patients were administered oxygen at 6 L/min via an oxygen mask, and standard monitors were placed, followed by venous access. Standardized general anesthesia induction was performed. The earplugs were placed by a research nurse after anesthetic induction, before the start of surgery. In both the music and mute groups, earbuds were used to reduce ambient noise and maintain blinding. Then, the music or mute intervention was maintained throughout the duration of general anesthesia. All patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia with or without orotracheal intubation. During surgery, maintenance anesthesia was achieved with O2 and sevoflurane (1.5%–3% vol) inhalation or target-controlled infusion of propofol (target plasma concentration: 3–5 μg/mL). Intraoperative rescue analgesia was permitted at the discretion of the anesthesiologist in charge, who was blinded to the group allocation. Intraoperative analgesic IV therapy included fentanyl (1–2 μg/kg) or morphine (0.05–0.1 mg/kg). The earplugs were removed by the research nurse at the end of the surgery, immediately before awakening. Upon arrival at the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), pain was evaluated at rest using an 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS), where 0 denotes no pain and 10 represents the worst pain imaginable. Higher values indicated greater pain. Patients with an NRS score >3/10 received fentanyl (1–2 μg/kg) via intravenous boluses. NRS scores were reassessed 1 h later. The nursing staff in the PACU were blinded to the group allocations. The patients were discharged from the PACU once they met the following discharge criteria: NRS score ≤3/10 and absence of adverse events. A phone call was scheduled 1 day postoperatively to assess the degree of postoperative pain and any adverse events.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were cumulative opioid consumption, calculated as the fentanyl dose, and opioid administration frequency in the PACU after surgery. Morphine 1 mg IV was considered equivalent to 15 μg of fentanyl.34

The secondary outcomes included cumulative opioid consumption (calculated as fentanyl doses) and opioid administration frequency during surgery; patients’ NRS scores upon arrival at the PACU, at discharge from the PACU, and 24 h after surgery; and hemodynamic data during the perioperative period. Reported adverse events included nausea, vomiting, drowsiness, pruritus, and dizziness. Adverse hemodynamic events such as hypotension or bradycardia were also recorded. Hypotension was defined as a systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or the requirement for vasopressor use. Bradycardia was defined as a heart rate <50 beats/min or requiring atropine use.

A research assistant—blinded to group allocations and not involved in perioperative care—collected relevant data at the end of surgery, upon PACU discharge, and 24 h postoperatively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the baseline characteristics of the three groups. Data are expressed as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed data, median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data, or number (percentage). The normality of continuous variables distributions was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Quantitative variables across three independent groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, according to the normality of their distribution. The paired t test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare within-group values according to the normality of their distribution. Categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-squared test or Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, as appropriate. Statistical p-value < 0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Inc., USA) and MedCalc Statistical Software (version 22.009; MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2023).
