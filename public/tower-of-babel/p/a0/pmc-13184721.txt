Striatal Prediction Error Tracking Moderates the Influence of Social Exposure on Adolescent Substance Use Curiosity
Authors: Jessica S Flannery, Elizabeth S Escalante, Ruofan Ma, Kristen A Lindquist, Eva H Telzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdolescence is a period of social development marked by increased sensitivity to social feedback and substance use experimentation. Although reinforcement learning (RL) models provide a powerful framework for examining how individuals learn from experience, they have rarely been applied to understand how adolescents learn from social experiences or how these processes relate to real‐world behavioral outcomes. In a sample of 261 youth (11.0 ± 1.6 years old), we applied computational modeling to a novel social RL fMRI paradigm. Q‐learning models estimated individual differences in learning and trial‐by‐trial prediction errors, which were used as a parametric modulator to assess brain activity that tracked the degree to which social feedback was better or worse than expected. Among older participants (n = 73; 12.9 ± 0.9 years old), we assessed associations between RL metrics and measures of substance use propensity. Greater substance use curiosity and household exposure to substance use were both linked to weaker striatal prediction error tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes. However, among youth with substance‐using peers, curiosity was associated with elevated striatal prediction error signals and better positive RL performance. Findings suggest that both hypo‐ and hyper‐sensitivity to positive reinforcement learning signals may confer an increased propensity for substance use, possibly through distinct pathways.Keywords: adolescence, insula, prediction error, reinforcement learning, social, striatum, substance use

Introduction

Adolescence is marked by heightened sensitivity to social feedback (Blakemore and Mills 2014; Somerville 2013; Smith et al. 2015) and increased experimentation with substances such as alcohol, nicotine, and cannabis (Miech et al. 1975–2023; Sussman et al. 2008; Wagner and Anthony 2002; DeWit et al. 2000; Kandel and Logan 1984). Heightened social sensitivity can facilitate adaptive social learning and relationship building during a time of increasing autonomy (Blakemore and Mills 2014; Telzer et al. 2023; Crone and Dahl 2012). However, it may also increase susceptibility to peer influences toward health risk behaviors, such as substance use, especially when social norms or peer contexts promote such behavior (Steinberg and Monahan 2007; Albert et al. 2013). Although experimentation with substances during adolescence is considered normative (Gray and Squeglia 2018; Siebenbruner et al. 2006), understanding the mechanisms involved in how these behaviors begin and progress could help identify factors that elevate risks for prolonged substance use and its associated harms. Social influence research suggests that peer and familial environments play a critical role in early substance use (Duncan et al. 1995; Pomery et al. 2005; Ashby Wills and Yaeger 2003; Boyle et al. 2001; Dishion and Tipsord 2011; Chassin et al. 1990). Accordingly, understanding how youth learn from social information may help clarify how social exposure translates into substance use curiosity and ultimately early initiation. Prior research has documented the dual effects of social selection (i.e., gravitating toward peers with shared interests or predispositions) (Simons‐Morton 2007), and socialization (i.e., being influenced by exposure, access, peer pressure, and explicit feedback) (Henneberger et al. 2021). Social feedback can positively and negatively reinforce adolescents' behaviors and attitudes. As such, examining sensitivity to social forms of reinforcement may further clarify phenotypes linked to early substance use. Social rewards, such as peer approval, smiles, or inclusion, and social punishments, such as disapproval or exclusion, are particularly salient during adolescence (Kwon et al. 2019; Braams et al. 2015; Chein et al. 2011; Foulkes and Blakemore 2016). Further, individual differences in adolescents' sensitivity to, and learning from, positive and negative social feedback may underlie differential susceptibility to peer and familial influences toward early substance use (Telzer et al. 2021; Turpyn et al. 2021).

Computational reinforcement learning models offer a powerful framework for characterizing how individuals learn from positive and negative feedback over time, providing both behavioral and neural indices of learning mechanisms (Gläscher and O'Doherty 2010). However, relatively few studies have applied reinforcement learning models to understand the social feedback learning mechanisms involved in early substance use. These models can estimate how expectations are updated based on prediction errors, the degree to which outcomes are better or worse than expected (Lerner et al. 2021; Diederen and Fletcher 2021), and can disentangle learning from rewards versus learning from punishments (Frank et al. 2004, 2007; Dombrovski et al. 2019). Dopaminergic signaling within frontostriatal circuits is thought to encode prediction errors (Lerner et al. 2021; Diederen and Fletcher 2021) with increased neuronal firing rates when outcomes are better than expected and decreased firing when outcomes are worse than expected (Schultz 1998). By signaling outcomes based on their predictability, prediction errors support reinforcement learning and thus play a key role in guiding future behavior (Cohen and Frank 2009). Empirical support for this framework comes from both pharmacological (Frank et al. 2004) and genetic studies (Frank et al. 2007) suggesting that sensitivity to reward and punishment, which impacts how people learn from their environments, may be partly rooted in neurobiological variability in frontostriatal functioning.

This framework is particularly relevant for understanding risk for substance use (Keiflin and Janak 2015) as variation in reward‐related brain function has been linked to use propensity (Schneider et al. 2012; Squeglia et al. 2015; Urošević et al. 2015; Flannery et al. 2023; Bart et al. 2021). Supporting a neural risk factor hypothesis, evidence has shown adolescents (Büchel et al. 2017) and young adults (Bart et al. 2021) with relative striatum hypoactivity during non‐drug reward anticipation are more likely to engage in later substance use, potentially as a means of compensating for decreased activation of reward circuits. However, other studies have linked heightened striatal reward responsivity with propensity for early substance use (Radoman et al. 2021; Tervo‐Clemmens et al. 2020; Bjork 2020), suggesting the possibility of multiple risk phenotypes. For instance, blunted reward responses may contribute to elevated substance use curiosity as a compensatory mechanism for reduced reward sensitivity, whereas heightened reward responses may be associated with greater susceptibility to social influences by increasing sensitivity to the perceived social rewards of engaging in risky behaviors with peers (Griffin et al. 2001; Wang et al. 2025). Reinforcement learning models may help reconcile these findings by modeling individual differences in learning signals, rather than relying on broader measures of reward responsiveness (Keiflin and Janak 2015; Tolomeo et al. 2021).

In the present study, participants (N = 261; 11.0 ± 1.6; 54.7% female) completed a probabilistic social reinforcement learning task during a functional magnetic resonance (fMRI) scan, in which feedback took the form of adolescent facial expressions. In the learning phase of the task, participants learned through trial and error which adolescent faces were the “friendliest” (i.e., more likely to deliver positive [smiles] vs. negative [scowls] feedback). Participant responses in the learning phase of the task were fit to reinforcement learning computational models using variational Bayesian methods to obtain individual‐level posterior parameter estimates and trial‐by‐trial prediction errors. Based on prior meta‐analytic work, we focused on prediction error‐related activity in the striatum and anterior insula, regions implicated in prediction error processing in social contexts (Corlett et al. 2022). Among a subsample of older participants (n = 73; 12.9 ± 0.9 years old), we examined whether behavioral and neural indices of feedback learning were associated with electronic‐cigarette (e‐cigarette), cannabis, or alcohol use curiosity and whether this learning moderated the relationship between social exposure to substance use (e.g., household and friend group use) and substance use curiosity.

Consistent with a hypo‐reward responsivity risk hypothesis (Trifilieff and Martinez 2014; Casey et al. 2014; Volkow et al. 2007), we predicted substance use curiosity would be associated with diminished sensitivity to positive social reinforcement and blunted striatal prediction error responses. We further hypothesized that household and friend group substance use would be associated with greater substance use curiosity and this association would be strongest among those with heightened sensitivity to social reinforcement and greater neural responsivity to positive prediction errors.

Methods

Participants and Procedures

Participants (N = 261; 54.7% female at birth; 7.4–14.6 years old) were recruited in 2nd through 8th grade in the southeast United States. Exclusion criteria for fMRI scanning included braces, permanent retainers, claustrophobia, metal in the body, learning disabilities, seizures, and head trauma. The sample was racially and ethnically diverse and varied in socioeconomic status indicators (Table S1). All study procedures were approved by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill's Institutional Review Board. Participants provided consent/assent, self‐reported demographic information (verified against parental reports for accuracy), and underwent a 1.5‐h fMRI scan. During the scan, participants completed a series of tasks, including a social reinforcement learning task called the Finding Friendliness Task. A subsample of older participants additionally self‐reported their use of, curiosity about, and household exposure to addictive substances. Older participants also reported frequency of substance use among their five closest friends.

Social Reinforcement Learning Task (Finding Friendliness Task)

Participants completed two runs of an adapted probabilistic selection task (Frank et al. 2004, 2007), the “Finding Friendliness Task”, used to assess the behavioral and neural processes involved in learning from social feedback. This task includes a ~6‐min learning phase (Figure 1A) and a ~6‐min testing phase (Figure 1B). In the learning phase, participants are presented with pairs of neutral faced adolescents from the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Child Emotional Faces Picture Set (Egger et al. 2011). On each trial, they chose one of the adolescents and then received social feedback from the adolescent they chose: a picture of that adolescent either smiling or scowling. Feedback was presented based on predetermined probabilities (see Figure 1A). Participants learn, through trial and error, which adolescents are the “friendliest”, meaning which adolescents are more likely to smile and less likely to scowl. In the learning phase, participants are instructed to always try to pick the friendliest adolescent. In the testing phase, participants again choose the adolescents whom they learned to be the friendliest, but in this phase, they do not receive feedback.

Social reinforcement learning task (Finding Friendliness Task). (A) During the learning phase, three different adolescent pairs (AB, CD, EF) are presented 20 times each for a total of 60 learning phase trials. Through ‘trial and error’ participants learn to choose which adolescent is the “friendliest” based on probabilistic positive and negative social feedback. Positive feedback is a smiling picture of the adolescent they chose while negative feedback is a scowling picture of the adolescent they choose. In AB trials, adolescent A smiles 80% of the time whereas adolescent B smiles 20% of the time. CD and EF pairs are less reliable, such that adolescent C smiles on 70% of CD trials and adolescent D smiles on 30% of CD trials and adolescent E smiles on 60% of EF trials and adolescent F smiles on 40% of EF trials. Participants learn to choose adolescent A, adolescent C, and adolescent E more often than B, D, or F. Adolescent pairs are neutral faced pictures from The NIMH Child Emotional Faces Picture Set (Egger et al. 2011). Each time the task is started, six adolescent stimuli (3 girls and 3 boys) are randomly assigned as A–F, with their respective probabilistic outcomes, to minimize stimulus‐specific effects. Each trial in the learning phase consists of a choice screen in which an adolescent pair is presented, and participants use their left and right index fingers to pick one of the neutral adolescent faces (FRIENDLY?: 2500 msec). Each adolescent is presented on the left and right side an equal number of times to account for any screen side biases. Following a fixation screen (jittered duration: 1000–3200 msec), participants receive social feedback from the adolescent they chose based on the trial's probabilistic contingencies (FEEDBACK: 1000 msec). If participants did not choose quickly enough, they receive a “too slow” message. (B) During the testing block, novel combinations of adolescent pairs that include either adolescent A (Approach A: AC, AD, AE, AF) or adolescent B (Avoid B: BC, BD, BE, BF) are presented and no feedback is provided. In total, there are 24 Approach A trials, 24 Avoid B trials and 24 Control trials in which C, D, E and F adolescents are paired with each other and A and B adolescents are not choice options (Control: CE, CF, DE, DF). Each trial in the testing phase consists of presenting a pair of neutral faces (FRIENDLY?: 2500 msec) and the participants pick one of the faces. However, in the testing phase, no feedback is provided such that the next trial begins following a fixation screen (jittered duration: 1000–3200 msec).

Performance in the testing phase was used to separately assess the ability to learn from positive social feedback (smiles) and negative social feedback (scowls). Positive reinforcement learning performance was operationalized as the ability to approach adolescent A, the adolescent with the highest probability of providing a smiling outcome (80% of the trials) during the learning phase. Negative reinforcement learning performance was operationalized as the ability to avoid choosing adolescent B, the adolescent with the highest probability of providing a scowling outcome (80% of trials) during learning.

To assess behavior in the learning phase, we calculated the proportion of trials in which the participant made the “optimal” choice (chose the adolescent with the higher probability of a positive outcome) for each trial type (i.e., AB, CD, EF), and across trial types. To estimate each participant's positive and negative social feedback learning rates, as well as choice stochasticity, behavior during the learning phase was fit to an asymmetrical Q‐learning model (Frank et al. 2004, 2007; Frydecka et al. 2016). This computational modeling approach estimated each participant's trial‐specific expected value (i.e., expected probability of receiving a smile), as well as the corresponding prediction error (i.e., difference between expected and actual feedback), which was used as a parametric modulator in fMRI analyses.

Nine participants were excluded from behavioral analyses due to learning phase responses not meeting quality control thresholds (> 60% no responses or > 80% screen side bias). In addition to these nine, 14 more participants were excluded from fMRI analyses due to fMRI data loss or high motion. Further, 16 participants' testing phase behavioral data were excluded from behavioral analyses having not met the same quality control cutoffs during this run.

Q‐Learning Models

We characterized underlying components of participants' decision‐making in the task using a model selected from a family of established Q‐learning algorithms fit to participants' trial‐by‐trial responses (variational Bayesian analysis (VBA) toolbox, MATLAB (Daunizeau et al. 2014)). This approach allowed us to (1) estimate model parameters that represent distinct aspects of reinforcement learning, (2) examine individual differences in such parameters, and (3) investigate how trial‐by‐trial variation in prediction errors relates to brain responsivity during the task. Ultimately, an asymmetric two‐learning rate Q‐learning model, used in prior implementations of probabilistic selection tasks (Frank et al. 2004, 2007), was selected and estimated model parameters were extracted for further analyses. The Q value or expected friendliness of each adolescent and the corresponding prediction error (difference between the expected value of the chosen stimulus and feedback) was tracked across trials. Further details on model features, fitting, and selection can be found in the supplement (Figure S1). Forward simulations for each participant, based on their best fit model parameters, validated model selection, showing that parameters from the selected model generate simulated data that reproduces key aspects of actual behavioral performance (see Figure S2 for details).

MRI Data Processing and Analysis

Acquisition and preprocessing of all imaging data is reported in the supplement. Following pre‐processing, participant‐level general linear models (GLM; AFNI's 3dDeconvolve and 3dREMLfit) were conducted for the learning run of the Finding Friendliness task and included the following 16 nuisance regressors: cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) and white matter (WM) signal and their temporal derivatives, as well as six head‐motion estimates and their temporal derivatives. Timepoints with a framewise displacement exceeding 0.9mm3 were censored. Participants with greater than 25% of timepoints censored were excluded from fMRI analyses. On average, 2.8% ± 4.5% of TRs were censored among participants that were included.

Three task‐related regressors were included: choice, feedback, and no response. We used a parametric modulator to examine brain activity that tracked increasing prediction error for each feedback presentation. Trial‐by‐trial prediction error was calculated for each participant by subtracting the current expected value of their choice (a number between −1 and 1) from the actual feedback they received (1 for positive feedback and −1 for negative feedback) such that prediction error was always greater than 0 for positive feedback and less than 0 for negative feedback. Increasingly positive prediction error values represent the degree to which the outcome was better than expected, whereas decreasingly negative prediction error values represent the degree to which the outcome was worse than expected.

Feedback and no response regressors were modeled as impulse response functions while choice was modeled as a duration modulated stimulus based on participant response times. The main effect of prediction error was assessed (AFNI's 3dMEMA). We also considered whole brain activity that significantly tracked prediction error for positive and negative feedback separately. Whole‐brain statistical maps are available on NeuroVault. Average prediction error‐related brain activity during feedback presentation was extracted from a priori ROIs: bilateral striatum and anterior insula, defined via the Brainetome Atlas (Fan et al. 2016).

Substance Use Measures

A subset of older participants (n = 73; ages:10.9–14.6, 52.1% female at birth) reported the degree to which they have ever been curious about using an e‐cigarette, using cannabis, or drinking alcohol on a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “very curious”. We considered curiosity ratings for each substance as well as the participants' maximum curiosity rating across all three substances. Participants additionally reported whether they had ever tried the substances and how many of the adults (e.g., parents, grandparents, stepparents, aunts, uncles, other caregivers) and children/teenagers (e.g., siblings, stepsiblings, cousins, foster siblings) currently living in their home use the substances on four‐point scales from “none” to “all”. We averaged across household use ratings to get an approximate measure of how many people living in the participant's household use substances. Participants also reported how frequently their five closest friends use each substance on a four‐point scale ranging from “never” to “always”.

Analyses

We began by examining social reinforcement learning behavior in the Finding Friendliness task. We characterized task performance based on learning phase behavior: proportion of trials in which the participant made the optimal choice (i.e., chose the adolescent with the higher probability of a positive outcome), and testing phase behavior: proportion of correct approach and avoid trials. Next, to identify learning parameters (positive learning rate [α₊], negative learning rate [α₋], and inverse temperature [β]) that were linked to better overall task performance, we built a natural‐neighbor surface from observed data and defined the optimum as the surface maximum of task performance: a composite of learning phase and testing phase accuracy.

Then we characterized whole‐brain activity that tracked trial‐by‐trial prediction error values during feedback delivery and examined associations between behavioral task performance and average prediction error tracking in a priori ROIs. We then conducted Pearson correlations to assess relationships between substance use curiosity and social exposure to substance use (i.e., household and friend group use). Spearman's rho was applied for skewed variables. Subsequently, we examined links between substance use propensity measures and social reinforcement learning behavior, as well as prediction error‐related brain activity (Pearson correlations). One striatal prediction error activity outlier was observed among the older sub‐sample. However, this value was not an outlier among the full sample, suggesting that it did not necessarily reflect measurement error. As such, we ran all sub‐sample analyses that included this variable with both raw values and with the outlier winsorized.

Finally, to examine whether individual differences in reinforcement learning processes moderated the relationship between substance use exposure and substance use curiosity, we conducted two multiple moderation analyses using PROCESS (Version 5.0; Model 2; (Hayes 2017)). In analyses examining learning from positive social feedback, the predictor variable was exposure to substance use (peer or household exposure), and the outcome variable was substance use curiosity. To examine whether neural and behavioral reinforcement‐learning processes jointly influenced the association between exposure and curiosity, two moderators were simultaneously included in the model: striatal prediction error activity during the learning phase and behavioral approach performance during the test phase. Including both moderators allowed the unique moderating effect of each variable to be evaluated while accounting for the other.

Another multiple moderation model, examining learning from negative social feedback, tested whether insular tracking of worse‐than‐expected outcomes and avoidance learning moderated the association between substance use exposure and curiosity. For both moderation analyses, friend group substance use was treated as a binary variable (friend use reported vs. not reported) to address its skewed distribution. Additional follow‐up moderation analyses were conducted in which each moderator was examined independently to confirm that the observed moderation effects were not dependent on the simultaneous inclusion of both moderators in the model.

Results

Prediction Error‐Related Brain Activity and Relationships With Social Learning Behavior

A parametric modulator was used to examine sample average brain activity that tracked prediction error during feedback (degree to which feedback was better than expected vs. worse than expected). Trial‐by‐trial prediction error was calculated for each participant by subtracting the current expected value of their choice (a number between −1 and 1) from the actual feedback they received (1 for positive feedback and −1 for negative feedback) such that prediction error was always greater than 0 for positive feedback and less than 0 for negative feedback. Brain activity that significantly increased as prediction error increased was observed in the bilateral putamen, nucleus accumbens, ventral medial prefrontal cortex, and paracentral gyrus, as well as bilateral clusters in the fusiform face area and occipital lobe (Figure 2A). Brain activity in the bilateral supplementary motor area, bilateral anterior insula, bilateral temporal pole, and right superior temporal gyrus, as well as an epithalamic cluster encompassing the habenula, was negatively correlated with prediction error values, such that activity increased as outcomes were increasingly worse‐than‐expected. Observed prediction error tracking during feedback was largely consistent with prior work (Corlett et al. 2022; Colas et al. 2017; Cohen 2007). To further characterize the valence‐related specificity of these effects, we examined whole‐brain prediction error tracking separately for positive and negative feedback (Figure S3). During positive feedback, activity in the bilateral anterior insula, amygdala, thalamus, midbrain, and occipital lobe increased as feedback was increasingly better‐than‐expected. During negative feedback, activity in the bilateral putamen, supplementary motor area, and occipital lobe decreased as feedback was increasingly worse‐than‐expected (cluster coordinates reported in Table S2). These analyses may suggest that the anterior insula encodes deviations from expectations regardless of valence, but with a bias toward worse‐than‐expected outcomes.

Next, we examined relationships between task behavior and prediction error‐related brain activity from the primary analysis within atlas‐defined a priori regions of interest (ROIs): the bilateral striatum and anterior insula. Sample average social learning behavioral performance is reported in the supplement (Figure S4). Stronger anterior insula tracking of worse‐than‐expected outcomes was associated with selecting the friendliest adolescent (over the less friendly adolescent) on a greater proportion of AB trials (r[236] = −0.2, p = 0.010). However, insula prediction error tracking was not significantly associated with learning phase choices across all trial types (r[236] = −0.1, p = 0.068) or testing phase performance (approach: r[234] = −0.1, p = 0.123; avoid: r[234] = −0.04, p = 0.574; Figure 2 B1). Contrary to expectations, prediction error‐related striatal activity was not significantly associated with any task performance (p's > 0.5; Figure 2 B2). These results did not change when controlling for age.

Substance Use Curiosity, Household Use, and Friend Group Use

Overall, 27.4% of the sample reported being at least a little curious about using e‐cigarettes, cannabis, or alcohol, and 16.4% reported that they had tried one of the substances. Substance use curiosity was significantly higher among participants that had tried at least one of the substances (t [71] = −3.0, p = 0.012). 71.2% of the sample reported that at least some of the individuals living in their household use e‐cigarettes, cannabis, or alcohol, and 12.3% reported substance use among their five closest friends. We examined relationships between social exposure to each substance and curiosity about using that substance. Household e‐cigarette use (r [71] = 0.6, p < 0.001) was positively associated with participants' e‐cigarette curiosity, but friend group e‐cigarette use was not (ρ [70] = 0.2, p = 0.1; Table S4). Household cannabis use (r [71] = 0.3, p = 0.025) and friend group cannabis use (ρ [70] = 0.3, p = 0.017) were both positively associated with participants' cannabis curiosity. However, both household alcohol use and friend group alcohol use were not significantly associated with alcohol curiosity. We did not observe any significant age or sex effects on substance use curiosity, household use, or friend group use in this sample.

Associations Between Prediction Error‐Related Brain Activity and Substance Use Propensity Measures

More substance using people living in the participant's household (r [70] = −0.4, p = 0.001) and higher curiosity for using substances (r [70] = −0.3, p = 0.010) were significantly associated with weaker tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes in the atlas‐defined bilateral striatum ROI. However, friend substance use was not (ρ [70] = 0.5, p = 0.687). To confirm associations were not driven by an outlier, analyses were re‐run with the outlier winsorized and associations with household substance use (r [70] = −0.4, p = 0.001) and curiosity (r [70] = −0.3, p = 0.016) remained significant (Figure 3A). Stronger prediction error tracking of worse‐than‐expected outcomes in the anterior insula was also significantly associated with a higher amount of household substance users (r [70] = −0.3, p = 0.033), but not with friend group substance use, or substance use curiosity (Figure 3B). Further, given that voxel‐wise analyses also identified prediction error tracking in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), a region implicated in corticostriatal circuits (Haber 2016) and thought to play a key role in value representation (Roy et al. 2012), we additionally conducted post hoc exploratory analyses of prediction error tracking within vmPFC subregions. These analyses revealed a negative correlation between anterior vmPFC tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes and substance use curiosity (r [70] = −0.4, p = 0.001; details in the supplement Figure S5).

Social Reinforcement Learning and Brain Activity Moderate the Effect of Friend Group Substance Use on Curiosity

The multiple moderation model examining whether learning from positive social feedback moderated the association between friend group substance use exposure and substance use curiosity was significant and explained approximately 30% of the variance in substance use curiosity (R 2 = 0.30, F [5, 63] = 5.4, p = 0.0003; Figure 4A). When holding approach learning performance at the mean, striatal prediction error activity significantly moderated the association between friend group substance use and curiosity (t = 3.0, p = 0.004). Among individuals with stronger striatal tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes, those with substance‐using friend groups reported significantly greater curiosity (+1 SD; t = 3.2, p = 0.002), but this same effect of friend use was not seen among those with weaker prediction error striatum tracking (Figure 4B). A similar moderation effect was observed for behavioral approach learning performance (t = 2.2, p = 0.031) when holding striatal prediction error activity at the mean, friend use predicted greater curiosity only among adolescents with higher approach learning performance (+1 SD; t = 2.6, p = 0.012) but not among those with average or low approach learning (Figure 4C). The effect of friend group substance use on curiosity was further amplified among adolescents showing both elevated striatal prediction error tracking and higher approach learning performance. Specifically, among adolescents high in both moderators (+1 SD), friend use was even more strongly associated with increased substance use curiosity (t = 4.1, p < 0.001). Follow‐up analyses, testing each moderator independently, produced similar results. Further, in exploratory analyses prediction‐error tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes in the vmPFC also moderated the effect of friend group use on curiosity (details in the supplement).

The multiple moderation analysis examining whether learning from negative social feedback moderated the association between friend group substance use and curiosity was not significant (R 2 = 0.07, F [3, 67] = 1.7, p = 0.19). Neither anterior insula prediction error tracking nor avoidance learning moderated the effect of friend group substance use on curiosity. Further, none of the learning metrics significantly moderated the effect of household substance use on curiosity (p's > 0.05). Moderation results were robust to heteroskedasticity, with all effects remaining significant when re‐estimated with heteroskedasticity‐consistent standard errors. Further, results did not significantly change when controlling for age.

Discussion

We utilized a novel social reinforcement learning task to investigate how adolescents learn from positive and negative social feedback. We explored how these learning processes relate to real‐world outcomes, including substance use curiosity and exposure to household and peer group use. By integrating task behavior, prediction error‐related brain activity, and self‐reported substance use propensity measures, this study shows individual differences in reinforcement learning mechanisms that may underlie susceptibility to social influences and substance use curiosity.

Prediction Error‐Related Brain Activity and Relationships With Social Learning Behavior

Theory suggests that learning occurs when an outcome differs from expectations. The magnitude and valence of this difference can be quantified as a prediction error (Fouragnan et al. 2018). Consistent with prior work (Schultz 1998; O'Doherty et al. 2003; Daw et al. 2011; Preuschoff et al. 2008; Palminteri et al. 2012), we observed that ventral striatal activity was positively correlated with prediction error values, such that activity increased as outcomes were increasingly better than expected, whereas anterior insula activity was negatively correlated with prediction error values, such that activity increased as outcomes were increasingly worse than expected.

Like prior work (Frömer et al. 2021; Lohse et al. 2020; Hester et al. 2009), individual differences in prediction error–related neural responsivity during learning were not associated with test‐phase performance. However, attenuated insula tracking of worse‐than‐expected outcomes was associated with poorer learning‐phase performance. This dissociation suggests that variability in prediction error tracking may influence ongoing adjustment of choice behavior during acquisition without necessarily producing enduring differences in value representations that persist into the test phase.

Notably, this relationship with learning phase behavior was specific to the anterior insula. Striatal prediction error tracking was not associated with learning‐phase or test‐phase behavior. One possible interpretation of these findings may be that the anterior insula and striatum contribute differently to reinforcement learning. Prior work suggests that anterior insula activity is particularly sensitive to the salience of negative outcomes (e.g., (Jensen et al. 2007; Ullsperger et al. 2010)), whereas striatal prediction error signals are thought to be more involved in value‐updating processes (Jensen et al. 2007; Lejarraga and Hertwig 2017). Signals reflecting the salience of negative outcomes may therefore exert a more immediate influence on trial‐by‐trial behavioral adjustments during learning, whereas variability in striatal value‐updating processes may not always translate into observable differences in task performance.

Developmental findings suggest that this distinction may be especially relevant during adolescence. For example, adolescents have displayed stronger anterior insula tracking of social prediction‐errors (Jones et al. 2014) but reduced coupling between striatal prediction‐error signals and behavioral performance (Christakou et al. 2013) when compared to adults. Thus, the present findings in a relatively young sample, together with prior work, raise the possibility that insula‐mediated prediction‐error signals may play a particularly important role in guiding behavioral adjustment during social learning in adolescence, whereas the coupling between striatal value signals and task behavior may still be strengthening during this developmental period. However, as the present study was not designed to directly test age‐related differences, additional work will be needed to characterize prediction‐error tracking changes across development.

Substance Use Curiosity, Household Use, and Peer Exposure

Our findings support longstanding social influence models of adolescent substance use (Henneberger et al. 2021; Telzer et al. 2017; Graham et al. 1991), demonstrating that youth curiosity about substance use is positively associated with both household (Pomery et al. 2005; Ashby Wills and Yaeger 2003; Merikangas et al. 1998) and friend group use (Reed and Rountree 1997). While this cross‐sectional study design cannot establish causality, these observed associations align with prior work and suggest that, when peers engage in substance use, mechanisms of social influence, including social selection and socialization (Simons‐Morton 2007; Henneberger et al. 2021), may lead to higher substance use curiosity. Further, as environmental exposure and heritable vulnerability have been shown to independently contribute to substance use (Henneberger et al. 2021), the observed link between household use and substance use curiosity may also be due to multiple interacting mechanisms.

Associations Between Prediction Error‐Related Brain Activity and Substance Use Propensity Measures

Our results support prior work linking reduced striatal responsivity during reinforcement processing to substance use risk (Flannery et al. 2023, 2019; Bart et al. 2021; Büchel et al. 2017; Peters et al. 2011). We found that adolescents with weaker positive prediction error‐related striatal and vmPFC tracking reported higher curiosity about using substances. One interpretation of this finding is that diminished reward responsivity increases vulnerability to substance use by making especially potent reinforcers, like addictive substances, more appealing. While blunted reward responses have been associated with early substance use in adolescence (Flannery et al. 2023; Büchel et al. 2017; Peters et al. 2011), prediction error tracking has not previously been examined as an early marker of use propensity. Extending prior evidence from substance‐dependent adults showing weaker striatal prediction error tracking (Tolomeo et al. 2021; Tanabe et al. 2013), our findings link this neural signature to substance use propensity among youth. Addictive substances are thought to appropriate the brain's natural learning mechanisms (Redish 2004) and repeated drug exposure has been linked to disrupted learning‐related neural signals that are hypothesized to perpetuate drug‐taking (Monterosso et al. 2012; Takahashi et al. 2007). Our findings suggest that similar disruptions, previously observed among substance dependent individuals (Tanabe et al. 2013), may already be present among youth with elevated use propensity, even before heavy or chronic use begins.

Further, as more substance‐using people living in the participant's household were also significantly associated with weaker striatal tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes, but peer group substance use was not, blunted striatal responsivity to unexpectedly positive outcomes may represent a preexisting phenotype, potentially shaped by inherited factors (Stokes et al. 2013) and/or early rearing environment (Kamkar et al. 2017; Hanson et al. 2021; Robinson et al. 2013). However, more research is needed to parse genetic and environmental contributions to striatal tracking of prediction errors and implications for substance use propensity. Overall, measures of substance use propensity were related to individual differences in neural signals of social learning in the absence of differential learning behavior.

Social Reinforcement Learning Mechanisms Moderate the Effect of Friend Substance Use on Use Curiosity

Weaker striatal and vmPFC tracking of better‐than‐expected social reinforcement was generally associated with higher curiosity for using substances. However, among adolescents who reported substance use among their five closest friends, the opposite pattern was observed. These findings suggest that variability in striatal and vmPFC reinforcement learning signals may be associated with substance use risk through multiple, mechanisms. Specifically, blunted neural tracking of better‐than‐expected outcomes may contribute to elevated substance use curiosity because reduced reward sensitivity may increase the tendency to seek out more potent alternative rewards. Whereas heightened tracking of better‐than‐expected social outcomes may indicate an increased susceptibility to social influences due to greater sensitivity to social rewards. Supporting this interpretation, participants who demonstrated task behavior indicative of better learning from positive social feedback (i.e., greater approach learning performance) also showed a greater positive effect of friend group use on substance use curiosity. Our findings may offer preliminary evidence for a subgroup of adolescents that is particularly sensitive to positive social reinforcement and, consequently, is more vulnerable to peer influences toward substance use. Alternatively, this heightened susceptibility could also reflect a general sensitivity to reinforcement which, given the heightened motivational salience of social contexts during this developmental period, could also amplify the influence of peers during adolescence. Interestingly, individual differences in social reinforcement learning did not moderate the influence of household substance use, suggesting that mechanisms probed by our social reinforcement learning task may be more relevant to peer‐based, rather than familial, social learning. Overall, these findings suggest the complex ways social reinforcement processing may influence early substance use risk.

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, our measures of household substance use cannot disentangle effects of social learning from inherited predispositions, nor can our measure of peer substance use fully distinguish effects of socialization from peer selection. Second, while our focus on prediction error‐related activity in the striatum and anterior insula was theory‐driven, activity in other brain regions likely interacts with these regions and contributes to reinforcement learning. Further, while the social reinforcement delivered in the Finding Friendliness Task is not as quantifiable as monetary reinforcement, such ambiguity may better replicate how social feedback is processed in real‐world environments. Next, although participants were instructed to always try to select the higher‐probability option during the learning phase, the present task design cannot distinguish whether suboptimal responding during this phase reflects differences in learning, purposeful exploratory sampling, or choice stochasticity. Future studies using paradigms designed to dissociate exploration from exploitation might help clarify the mechanisms underlying individual differences in learning‐phase choice behavior. Furthermore, substance use data were available only for a smaller subset of the sample, and lastly, the distribution of ages represented in the sample was not sufficient for age‐based comparisons.

Conclusions

This study suggests that adolescents with weaker striatal reinforcement learning signals may be interested in seeking out more potent rewards like substances, whereas adolescents with relatively higher neural responsivity to positive social feedback may be more susceptible to peer influences. This dual‐risk framework may help shed light on prior research that has linked both hyper‐ and hypo‐responsivity in reward‐related brain systems to substance use vulnerability (Büchel et al. 2017; Tervo‐Clemmens et al. 2020; Hammond et al. 2015). Further, these results may suggest the value of tailoring prevention strategies to specific neurocognitive profiles. For instance, interventions that emphasize peer resistance skills may be especially useful for youth who are highly sensitive to social rewards. Whereas those with reduced reward responsivity may instead need more help finding other sources of reinforcement.
