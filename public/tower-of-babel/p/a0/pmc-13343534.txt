Upcycled battery-derived MnO2 for ultrafast lead removal from wastewater
Authors: Othai Saha, Md Humayun Kabir, Muhammad Shahriar Bashar, Mehedi Hasan, Tajnin Jahan, Md. Al-Amin, Md. Sanwar Hossain, Monira Akter Somapti, Sabina Yasmin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Waste generation from discarded batteries poses a significant environmental challenge, while lead (Pb2+) contamination in water threatens public health and aquatic ecosystems due to its high toxicity, persistence, and non-biodegradable nature, even at trace concentrations. To address these dual issues, this study presents manganese oxide (MnO2) synthesized from discarded battery materials as a single, highly efficient adsorbent for Pb2+ removal from aqueous solutions, demonstrating a sustainable waste-to-waste remediation strategy. The synthesized MnO2 was comprehensively characterized to confirm its crystalline structure, surface properties. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of contact time, solution pH, adsorbent dosage, initial Pb2+ concentration, and temperature on adsorption performance. Notably, the synthesized MnO2 demonstrated exceptionally rapid adsorption kinetics, removing more than 94% of Pb2+ within 1 minute at neutral pH without external pH adjustment, using an adsorbent dosage of 0.125 g L−1. Kinetic analysis showed that the adsorption follows a pseudo-second-order model, indicating a chemisorption mechanism. Equilibrium data were best fitted by the Langmuir isotherm, suggesting monolayer adsorption with a maximum adsorption capacity of approximately 167.78, 201, and 222 mg g−1 at 25, 40, and 50 °C, respectively. Furthermore, the MnO2 demonstrated excellent reusability and performance stability over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles. In addition, the adsorbent maintained high Pb2+ removal efficiency in the presence of competing inorganic ions, demonstrating good selectivity and potential applicability in complex water matrices. These findings highlight the potential of battery-derived MnO2 as a sustainable, low-cost, and highly effective adsorbent for rapid Pb2+ remediation in contaminated water.

Introduction

The dual crises of growing electronic waste accumulation and water resource contamination represent major environmental challenges of the age of rapid industrialization and technological advancement. The rapid growth of electronic devices worldwide has resulted in a steady increase in the consumption of dry cell batteries, which are widely used in everyday household applications such as remote controls, toys, clocks, and flashlights due to their low cost and convenience. As a result, nearly 45 million metric tons of spent dry cell batteries are discarded annually.1–3

Improper disposal transforms these batteries from potentially recoverable secondary resources into long-term environmental hazards, releasing toxic metals and electrolytes that contaminate soil and water while causing the loss of valuable materials.4,5 On the other hand, diverse anthropogenic activities such as industrial effluents, mining runoff, electroplating, battery manufacturing, agricultural practices, and e-waste disposal continue to release toxic heavy metals, particularly lead (Pb2+), into aquatic systems.6–8 Lead (Pb2+) is highly hazardous due to its persistence and strong affinity for biological tissues, accumulating in bones, blood, and organs, where it disrupts enzymes, induces oxidative stress, and interferes with metal homeostasis, causing neurotoxicity, anemia, and organ damage even at trace levels. Therefore, removing lead from water is a critical environmental and health priority, necessitating the development of reliable and sustainable treatment strategies.6,7,9,10

Conventional techniques, including chemical precipitation, membrane filtration, and biological treatment, often suffer from high energy consumption, secondary pollution, or slow kinetics.11,12 In contrast, adsorption is widely recognized as one of the most practical and efficient approaches due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ability to remove metal ions even at trace concentrations.13,14 Among various adsorbent materials, MnO2 has attracted considerable attention owing to its high surface area, negatively charged surface, tunable oxidation states, and abundant surface hydroxyl groups. These features promote ion exchange, surface complexation, and redox activity, endowing MnO2 with a strong affinity for Pb2+ through mechanisms such as electrostatic attraction, inner-sphere complexation, and oxidation-precipitation.13,15

Several MnO2-based adsorbents have demonstrated notable Pb2+ adsorption capacities, such as MnO2-modified diatomite (81.42 mg g−1),16 amino/MnO2 co-modified biochar (109.5 mg g−1),17 and MnO2–Coal composites (61.98 mg g−1).18 Despite these promising results, most MnO2-based materials are synthesized from high-purity chemical precursors through multi-step processes, resulting in high production costs and increased environmental impact. Notably, MnO2 is also the primary active component of many discarded battery cathodes.19 Recovering manganese from discarded batteries as MnSO4 and its subsequent conversion to MnO2 offers a sustainable route to address electronic waste while producing value-added functional materials. In this context, the present study bridges the dual environmental challenges of battery accumulation and heavy-metal-contaminated water by adopting a circular economy-oriented strategy, where MnO2 synthesized from battery-derived MnSO4 is used as a high-performance nano-adsorbent for ultrafast Pb2+ removal without additional functionalization or composite formation. By utilizing Mn-containing secondary resources from e-waste, this approach not only provides an efficient and cost-effective solution for Pb2+ remediation but also promotes resource recovery and an environmentally sustainable pathway for water purification, emphasizing both environmental protection and sustainable material reuse.

Materials and method

Chemicals

The lead standard solution (Pb, 1000 mg L−1) for atomic absorption spectrometer (AAS) (lead nitrate in nitric acid), was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Switzerland. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, analytical grade) was sourced from a certified supplier. Concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%, CAS 7647-01-0) was purchased from AppliChem, Germany. Oxalic acid (C2H2O4, CAS 144-62-7) was used as an analytical-grade reagent. Potassium permanganate (KMnO4, ≥99.0%, CAS 7722-64-7) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95–97%, CAS 7664-93-9) were obtained from Scharlau, Spain. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure deionized water with a resistivity of 18 MΩ cm.

Instrumentation and characterization

The chemical functionalities of MnO2 were investigated using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) over the range of 4000–400 cm−1. Crystalline structures and phase composition were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an ARL EQUINOX 1000 X-ray diffractometer (Thermo Scientific™, USA) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) at 40 kV and 60 mA. Surface morphology and elemental composition were examined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM, EVO18, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) at various magnifications, coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, TEAMEDS, EDAX, USA). Finally, lead concentrations in the samples were quantified using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS, Agilent-AAS 240FS, Australia), providing precise measurements for evaluating heavy metal removal efficiency.

Collection of Mn-based compound from discarded battery

Manganese dioxide was recovered from discarded dry cell batteries by mechanically separating the cathode material, which appeared as a dark powder, as illustrated in Scheme 1. The collected material was repeatedly washed with deionized water and filtered to eliminate residual soluble impurities, including zinc and ammonium salts. After filtration, the wet MnO2 precipitate was collected and slowly added under constant stirring to an acidic reaction medium composed of concentrated sulfuric acid and an aqueous oxalic acid solution. The reaction was allowed to proceed until gas evolution completely ceased, indicating the full conversion of MnO2 to manganese sulfate. Upon completion of the reaction, the suspension was filtered to remove unreacted or insoluble components. The resulting clear solution was gently heated at 50 °C to concentrate the filtrate and induce crystallization of MnSO4. The precipitated manganese sulfate was collected by filtration, thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove residual acids, and dried overnight at 50 °C.20

Synthesis of MnO2

To synthesize MnO2, a 0.05 M KMnO4 solution (50 mL) was slowly added to 50 mL of 0.075 M MnSO4 solution under continuous stirring, as illustrated in Scheme 1. The synthesis proceeds via a redox reaction between permanganate (MnO4−) and Mn2+ ions, according to the following reaction212KMnO4 + 3MnSO4 + 2H2O → 5MnO2 + K2SO4 + 2H2SO4

The reaction mixture was stirred for 5 h at ambient conditions to ensure complete conversion. During the process, a dark-brown MnO2 precipitate formed at the bottom of the reaction flask. The resulting solid was separated and thoroughly washed several times with deionized water to remove residual ions and by-products. Finally, the washed product was dried overnight at 50 °C to obtain the final MnO2 powder.

Adsorption studies

Batch adsorption experiments were carried out to evaluate the Pb2+ removal efficiency of the synthesized adsorbent. A primary Pb2+ standard solution (1000 mg L−1) was diluted with deionized water to prepare working solutions of desired concentrations. For each run, 40 mL of Pb2+ solution was placed in 50 mL conical flasks. Before the adsorbent was introduced, the solution pH was adjusted to the required level using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. The suspensions were agitated at 250 rpm to ensure adequate solid–liquid interaction. After the selected contact time, the mixtures were centrifuged at 4000 rpm to separate the adsorbent. The supernatants were further filtered using a syringe filters to remove any remaining particulates. The concentrations of Pb2+ before and after adsorption were determined using AAS.

To establish the optimal adsorption conditions, the influence of solution pH (2–12), adsorbent dosage (0.025–0.3 g L−1), contact time (0.5–60 min), initial Pb2+ concentration (2–100 ppm), and temperature (25, 40, and 50 °C) was examined by varying one parameter at a time while keeping all others constant. Kinetic experiments were conducted by altering only the contact time, while isotherm experiments were performed by varying the initial Pb2+ concentration under controlled conditions. Results and discussion

Characterization of the adsorbent

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction analysis was employed to examine the crystal structure and phase purity of the synthesized MnO2. Fig. 1a shows the XRD pattern of the MnO2 sample, which exhibits characteristic reflections at 12.66°, 37.31°, and 66.53°, indexed to the (001), (111), and (005) crystallographic planes, respectively.23–25 The low-angle reflection at 12.66° corresponds to an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.70 nm, calculated using Bragg's equation. All observed diffraction peaks are in good agreement with standard MnO2 reference data, and no additional peaks related to impurity phases are detected, confirming the formation of phase-pure MnO2. The diffraction peaks exhibit noticeable broadening, as indicated by their relatively large full width at half maximum (FWHM), suggesting a nanocrystalline structure with lattice strain and partial structural disorder. The average crystallite size of the synthesized MnO2 was calculated to be approximately 3.74 nm using the Scherrer equation, further confirming the formation of nanoscale crystallites. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Fig. 1b shows the FT-IR spectrum of the synthesized MnO2 sample recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 to identify surface functional groups and metal–oxygen bonding. A broad absorption band appearing in the region of 3412 cm−1 is attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of adsorbed water molecules and surface hydroxyl groups on MnO2.27 Additionally, a band observed around 1633 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of H–O–H, further confirming the existence of residual moisture on the MnO2 surface.28 The prominent absorption bands in the low-wavenumber region below 750 cm−1 are characteristic of Mn–O vibrations in MnO2. Strong peaks located around 738, 580, 510, and 432 cm−1 are assigned to Mn–O stretching modes confirming the formation of MnO2 with an octahedral framework.27,29 The relatively simple FT-IR spectral pattern indicates good structural uniformity of the synthesized MnO2. Overall, the FT-IR results confirm the successful synthesis of MnO2 with characteristic Mn–O bonding features, and the observed bands are in good agreement with previously reported literature.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Fig. 2a and b presents the SEM micrograph of the synthesized adsorbent. The image reveals a morphology composed of irregular, quasi-spherical particles with a rough and heterogeneous surface, with an average particle size of approximately 224 nm (Fig. 2c). The particles are closely packed, forming cauliflower-like clusters with noticeable interparticle voids and pores, resulting in a porous structure that provides abundant active sites and facilitates the diffusion of adsorbate molecules.26,30 This well-formed MnO2 framework enhances adsorption performance by offering a high surface area and promoting strong interactions with Pb2+ ions. The corresponding EDX analysis in Fig. 2d confirms the presence of Mn (51.77%) and O (48.23%), consistent with the formation of MnO2 and supporting the successful synthesis of the adsorbent.

Determination of the point of zero charge (pHpzc)

The point of zero charge (pHpzc) of MnO2 was determined using the solid addition method to assess the surface charge behavior of the adsorbent.31 A series of flasks were prepared, each containing 50 mL of 0.01 M KCl solution, and the initial pH (pHi) was adjusted in the range of 2–12 using HCl (0.1 M) or NaOH (0.1 M). Subsequently, a fixed amount of MnO2 was added to each flask, and the suspensions were agitated to ensure proper solid–liquid interaction until equilibrium was reached. After equilibration, the final pH (pHe) of each solution was measured, and the pH difference (ΔpH = pHe − pHi) was calculated. The pHpzc of MnO2 was determined from ΔpH measurements and identified at the pH where ΔpH equals zero. The pHpzc value was 2.85, indicating a surface charge transition. The obtained pHpzc value has important implications for Pb2+ adsorption. At pH values above 2.85, deprotonation of surface hydroxyl (–OH) groups produces negatively charged Mn–O− sites, which favor the electrostatic attraction and binding of Pb2+ ions. Conversely, at pH values below the pHpzc, surface protonation leads to a positively charged surface, resulting in electrostatic repulsion and reduced Pb2+ uptake.6 Since most wastewater systems operate near neutral pH, the negatively charged MnO2 surface above its pHpzc promotes efficient Pb2+ removal without the need for external pH adjustment.

Adsorption studies

Effect of adsorbent dose

The adsorbent dose plays an important role in achieving efficient Pb2+ removal. Therefore, batch experiments were carried out using adsorbent doses ranging from 0.025 to 0.3 gL−1 to determine the optimal adsorbent dose. As shown in Fig. 3a, the removal efficiency increased rapidly at lower doses due to the increased availability of active adsorption sites. However, beyond approximately 0.125 gL−1, the improvement in removal slowed and approached a plateau, due to particle aggregation and an excess of adsorption sites relative to Pb2+,which limited effective site utilization.2,32 On the other hand, the adsorption capacity showed a decreasing trend with increasing adsorbent dose. This decline can be explained by the excessive availability of adsorption sites relative to Pb2+ ions, resulting in site unsaturation and lower adsorption capacity, even though the overall removal percentage remained nearly constant.33,34 Considering both adsorption performance and material economy, 0.125 gL−1 was selected as the optimal adsorbent dose for subsequent experiments.

Effect of contact time

Contact time is a crucial factor in adsorption, as it governs the duration of interaction between Pb2+ ions and MnO2 nanoparticles, affecting both efficiency and practical feasibility. The impact of contact time was examined over 0.5 to 60 minutes (Fig. 3b). The removal efficiency increased very rapidly, reaching more than 94% within the first 1 min, due to the readily accessible active sites on MnO2 nanoparticles. Thereafter, adsorption gradually approached a steady state and remained nearly constant up to 45 min, indicating that equilibrium was achieved due to the dynamic balance between adsorption and desorption.35 The high adsorption and fast equilibrium suggest a strong affinity of Pb2+ ions toward the MnO2 nanostructures, primarily through electrostatic attraction and surface complexation. At longer contact times, a slight decrease in removal was observed, likely due to site saturation and minor desorption of Pb2+ ions as equilibrium was reached.6,12 These results demonstrate that the adsorbent achieves near-complete Pb2+ removal almost immediately, and extended contact time does not significantly improve adsorption efficiency. Based on these observations, a contact time of 1 min was considered optimal for further experiments.

Effect of pH

The pH of the solution is a crucial factor in Pb2+ adsorption because it governs both the surface charge of MnO2 and the chemical form of Pb ions in solution.15,36 The MnO2 adsorbent has a point of zero charge (pHpzc) of 2.85, meaning that at pH values below 2.85 the surface is positively charged, while above 2.85 it becomes negatively charged. As shown in Fig. 3c, Pb2+ removal is limited at low pH due to protonation of Mn–OH groups, which generates strong electrostatic repulsion toward Pb2+ ions and competition with excess H+/H3O+ for adsorption sites.36 With increasing pH, removal efficiency increases and remains nearly constant between pH 4 and 9, as deprotonation of Mn–OH groups produces Mn–O− sites capable of binding cationic Pb species, while Pb2+ gradually hydrolyzes to form Pb(OH)+ and Pb(OH)2.37,38

Under alkaline conditions (pH 9–10), slight formation of a white precipitate was observed, indicating that a minor contribution from Pb(OH)2 precipitation may occur in addition to adsorption. At strongly alkaline conditions (pH > 10), Pb predominantly exists as anionic hydroxo-complexes (Pb(OH)3−, Pb(OH)42−), which are electrostatically repelled by the negatively charged MnO2 surface, causing a slight decrease in removal efficiency.39,40 In contrast, no visible precipitation was observed at pH 7, indicating that Pb2+ removal under the optimized conditions is primarily governed by adsorption. Therefore, pH 7 was selected as the optimum operating condition, providing high adsorption efficiency while eliminating the need for external pH adjustment.

Effect of initial concentration

The adsorption behavior of the MnO2 adsorbent was evaluated over an initial metal ion concentration range of 2–100 ppm (Fig. 3d). At lower concentrations (around 2 ppm), the adsorbent showed high removal efficiency (94.92%) due to the abundance of vacant active sites relative to the few metal ions present.41,42 As the initial concentration increased, the quantity of metal ions in solution became higher than the number of available adsorption sites, reducing site accessibility and resulting in a gradual decline in removal efficiency.33 With further increases in concentration the adsorbent surface approached saturation, limiting further adsorption. Lowering the overall removal efficiency. Based on the high removal efficiency and practical applicability, an initial concentration of 10 ppm was selected as the optimum concentration for subsequent experiments.

The positive value of ΔH confirms that the adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto the MnO2 nanoparticle is an endothermic process, indicating that heat is absorbed during the interaction between the metal ions and the adsorbent surface. This endothermic nature implies that increasing temperature supplies additional energy to overcome mass-transfer resistance and activates more adsorption sites, resulting in an enhancement of adsorption capacity. The positive ΔS value indicates an increase in randomness at the solid–liquid interface, which can be attributed to the displacement of water molecules and structural rearrangements occurring during Pb2+adsorption.53

The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) values were negative at all studied temperatures, confirming the spontaneous nature of the adsorption process.54 Moreover, ΔG became more negative with increasing temperature, decreasing from −28.97 to −34.54 kJ mol−1 as the temperature increased from 25 to 50 °C. This trend indicates that higher temperatures favor the spontaneity of adsorption, which is consistent with the endothermic character of the process and the observed increase in adsorption capacity at elevated temperatures.

Stability and reusability

The reusability of MnO2 is a critical factor in assessing its practical applicability for wastewater treatment. In this study, Pb2+-loaded MnO2 was regenerated using HCl (1.0 mol L−1), followed by rinsing with deionized water until the supernatant reached near-neutral pH.17,37 This regeneration process effectively desorbed Pb2+, allowing the adsorbent to be reused over multiple cycles. As shown in Fig. 6b, after 10 consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles, the removal efficiency decreased slightly from 96.26% to 92%, indicating good retention of adsorption performance. The modest decline in efficiency can be attributed to partial material loss, gradual degradation of active MnO2 surface sites, and surface modifications induced during repeated acid treatment.

Effect of coexisting ion

In natural and wastewater systems, multiple inorganic ions are present simultaneously, which can collectively influence the adsorption behavior of target contaminants. To simulate such realistic conditions, Pb2+ adsorption was investigated in a mixed-ion solution containing Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, NO3−, and Cl−, where all coexisting ions were present at three different equal concentrations (10, 20, and 30 ppm), while the Pb2+ concentration was maintained at 10 ppm throughout the experiments. The results are presented in Fig. 6c.

The adsorption capacity of MnO2 for Pb2+ exhibited only slight variation across all conditions, indicating that the presence of multiple competing ions has a limited effect on overall performance. Even at higher concentration levels, Pb2+ removal remained relatively effective, demonstrating the robustness of the adsorbent in complex aqueous environments. This behavior can be attributed to simultaneous competition among coexisting ions for available surface sites, along with electrostatic screening effects that arise in multicomponent solutions.55–57 Nevertheless, the limited decrease in adsorption performance suggests that Pb2+ has a stronger affinity toward MnO2 active sites compared to background ions, enabling effective uptake even in realistic mixed-ion systems.

At low pH, excess protons in solution protonate these surface hydroxyl sites (Mn–OH2+), rendering the MnO2 surface positively charged. This positive surface charge suppresses Pb2+ adsorption because of electrostatic repulsion between Pb2+ ions and the protonated surface, as well as competition between Pb2+ ions and H+ for available adsorption sites.

Proposed adsorption mechanism of Pb2+ ions on the MnO2 nanoparticle.

As the pH increases, deprotonation of surface hydroxyl groups (Mn–OH → Mn–O−) occurs, leading to the development of a negatively charged MnO2 surface. This negatively charged surface enhances electrostatic attraction toward Pb2+ ions, promoting their adsorption predominantly as outer–sphere complexes through coulombic interactions. In addition to electrostatic attraction, Pb2+ ions can interact with surface oxygen-containing functional groups of MnO2 to form inner-sphere complexes via direct coordination with surface oxygen atoms. This strong metal-surface interaction indicates a chemisorption-controlled process, which is consistent with the pseudo-second-order kinetic model.6,15,65 The good agreement of the experimental data with the Langmuir isotherm further suggests that Pb2+ adsorption mainly occurs on energetically uniform and specific active sites of the MnO2 surface, forming a monolayer coverage. The proposed adsorption mechanism is further illustrated by the post-adsorption FTIR spectrum and SEM images (Fig. S1 and S2, SI). Overall, the adsorption behavior of Pb2+ on MnO2 is governed by the combined effects of surface electrostatic interactions and chemical complexation, leading to efficient and selective removal under optimized conditions.

Conclusion

The results of this study provide clear evidence that MnO2 derived from discarded dry cell batteries possesses a strong intrinsic capability for Pb2+ removal without the need for additional modification or composite formation. The high removal efficiency achieved within a short contact time, together with a maximum adsorption capacity of 222.71 mg g−1, indicates that Pb2+ ions readily interact with the surface of MnO2. This observation highlights the important role of surface hydroxyl groups in governing adsorption behavior through electrostatic attraction and surface complexation mechanisms. Kinetic and equilibrium analyses suggest that the adsorption process is predominantly governed by chemisorption and follows monolayer coverage of Pb2+ ions on energetically uniform active sites. The adsorbent also exhibited excellent selectivity in the presence of coexisting ions. The stable performance observed over repeated adsorption–desorption cycles further confirms the robustness and practical reliability of the material. Importantly, the effective performance of this single-component adsorbent demonstrates that sustainable, waste-derived materials can compete with more complex engineered systems for heavy-metal remediation. From a broader perspective, this work underscores the dual benefit of mitigating lead pollution while valorizing discarded battery waste, offering a promising pathway toward circular and environmentally responsible water treatment solutions.
