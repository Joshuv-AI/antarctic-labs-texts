Generation of anti-bovine CD20 monoclonal antibody with cytotoxic activity for the depletion of bovine B-cell lymphoma cells
Authors: Shuya Mitoma, Tomofumi Uto, Tomohiro Fukaya, Nao Yamanokuchi, Yumiko Sato, Yotaro Nishikawa, Shoichi Wakitani, Naoyuki Fuke, Go Kitahara, Satoshi Sekiguchi, Masahiro Yasuda, Katsuaki Sato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBovine leukemia virus (BLV) is the causative agent of enzootic bovine leukosis (EBL), which manifests as B-cell lymphomas in cattle. However, the effective therapy for EBL has not yet been developed. While the usage of anti-human CD20 monoclonal antibody (mAb) for B-cell depletion therapy (BCDT) can provide remarkable curative effects on several types of B-cell tumors in humans, this approach remains problematic in cattle due to the lack of anti-bovine CD20 (boCD20) mAb for BCDT. Here, we show that newly developed mAb targeting boCD20 utilizes effector function to deplete EBL-driven B-cell lymphoma cells and bovine B cells. Anti-boCD20 mAb not only specifically recognized boCD20 but also exhibited the cytotoxic function against EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells and bovine B cells mediated through Ab-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement-mediated cytotoxicity (CDC) as well as direct cell death. Furthermore, the treatment of immunodeficient mice transplanted with EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells with anti-boCD20 mAb led to inhibition of their growth. Thus, our findings highlight boCD20 as a valid marker for identifying B cells and EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells in cattle and provide support for boCD20 as a potential target for mAb-mediated depletion for the therapy of EBL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54691-x.Keywords: Cattle, Enzootic bovine leukosis, CD20, Monoclonal antibody, CytotoxicitySubject terms: Cancer, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Enzootic bovine leukosis (EBL) is a B-cell malignant lymphoma caused by bovine leukemia virus (BLV), a member of the Retroviridae Deltaretrovirus family, which also contains Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1)1. BLV mainly infects bovine B cells and integrates into the host genome as a provirus, resulting in persistent infection for the host’s lifetime2. Approximately 30% of BLV-infected cattle exhibited persistent lymphocytosis (PL), and fewer than 5% progressed to EBL3. On the other hand, higher BLV-proviral load is associated with a higher risk of either horizontal4 and vertical5 transmission, leading to weight loss and decrease in milk production in cattle6,7. Despite the remarkable efforts have been conducted to develop various means that include vaccines8, antiviral drugs9 and immune checkpoint inhibitors10,11 for the therapies of BLV infection, PL, and EBL, there is still no commercially available effective treatments.

CD20 is a glycosylated transmembrane phosphoprotein with two extracellular domains12. In humans and mice, CD20 is expressed on a broad range of B cells, spanning from pre-B to mature B cells, but is not expressed on mature plasma cells or B-cell progenitors13. While the precise function of CD20 in B cells and its physiological ligand remain unclear, several studies suggest that the function of CD20 links to B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling, which influences B-cell survival and class switching14,15.

Due to specific expression of CD20 on B cells in humans, it is an ideal target of B cell-depleting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). These anti-human CD20 mAbs, including rituximab, are used for B-cell depletion therapy (BCDT) in the clinic for the treatment of B-cell tumors such as Burkitt lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) or B cell-associated autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis (RA)13,16,17. The function of anti-human CD20 mAbs to deplete B cells and their malignant cells are thought to be mediated through the most common mechanisms that include complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and direct cell death16,18.

Whereas immunohistochemical analysis revealed the expression of bovine CD20 (boCD20) in B cells in healthy cattle and B-cell tumors in EBL-affected cattle19,20, their cell surface expression level of boCD20 remains unclear owing to the absence of anti-boCD20 mAb for flow cytometry. Although boCD20 could be a good candidate for BCDT of EBL-affected cattle as well as BLV-infected cattle with high proviral load, this strategy has been hampered by the lack of anti-boCD20 mAb for the depletion of bovine B cells and their malignant cells.

In this study, we show the usage of newly developed mAbs against boCD20 for flow cytometry to detect cell-surface expression of boCD20 on bovine B cells and EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells. We further demonstrate that anti-boCD20 mAb exerts the effector function for their depletion in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, anti-boCD20 mAb has the potential to be used for BCDT of EBL.

Results

Specific binding of boCD20 expressed on bovine B cells and EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells with anti-boCD20 mAbs

As boCD20 could be a therapeutic target for BCDT of EBL-affected cattle, we generated mAbs against boCD20. To examine the specific binding of anti-boCD20 mAbs to boCD20, RBL-2H321 expressing mock-GFP or boCD20 (boCD20/RBL-2H3) were stained with various clones of anti-boCD20 mAb. Flow cytometry analysis showed that all generated clones (7B12, 15A2, A144-1, and A213) of anti-boCD20 mAbs bound to RBL-2H3 expressing boCD20 (boCD20/RBL-2H3), but not parental RBL-2H3 (Fig. 1A, left and middle). Furthermore, these clones of anti-boCD20 mAbs reacted with boCD20 expressed on BLSC-KU1, known as EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells (Fig. 1A, right). While the anti-human CD20 mAb (clone MEM-97) has reportedly cross-reacted with boCD2022, we observed its little or no specific binding to boCD20/RBL-2H3 and BLSC-KU1 (Fig. 1A, middle and right).

To compare the binding affinity of the generated clones of anti-boCD20 mAbs, boCD20/RBL-2H3 and BLSC-KU1 were stained with serially diluted each clone of anti-boCD20 mAbs, and their expression levels were analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 1B). Among all clones of anti-boCD20 mAbs, clone A213 exhibited a higher affinity to the boCD20 expressed on boCD20/RBL-2H3 or BLSC-KU1 than other clones with the estimated dissociation constant (Kd) value of 2.72 nM or 11.05 nM, respectively (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the competitive binding assay of clone A213 among anti-boCD20 mAbs showed that clone A213 exhibited a higher inhibition on the binding of clone A213 to BLSC-KU1 than A144-1, although it had little or no effect on the binding of clone 7B12 or 15A2 to BLSC-KU1 (Supplementary Fig. S1), implying that both clones A213 and clone A144-1 bind to the adjacent epitopes, whereas the epitope recognized by clone A213 are different from those by clone 7B12 and 15A2 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

We also examined the expression of boCD20 on bovine leukocytes from healthy cattle by flow cytometry with various clones of anti-boCD20 mAb. While clone MEM-97 did not react with CD20 on boIgM+ B cells, all generated clones of anti-boCD20 mAbs detected boCD20 expressed on boIgM+ B cells, but not boCD3+ T cells (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S2). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis with anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) revealed that boCD20 was expressed on boCD21+ B cells and boCD5+ B cells as well as boLA-DR+boCD11c+CD79α+ B cells, but not other leukocytes (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S2).

We next examined the expression of boCD20 by biochemical analysis with the generated clones of anti-boCD20 mAbs. Western blotting analysis showed that only clone 7B12 reacted with boCD20 with molecular weight of 32–35 kDa in whole cell lysates in boCD20/RBL-2H3 and BLSC-KU1 (Fig. 1D, left, Supplementary Fig. S3A), whereas silver staining detected boCD20 in the immunoprecipitates with clone 7B12, 15A2 or A213 from the soluble fraction of cell lysates in boCD20/RBL-2H3 (Fig. 1D, middle). We also showed the expression of boCD20 in the immunoprecipitates with clones 7B12, 15A2 and A213 from the soluble fraction of cell lysates in BLSC-KU1 by western blotting analysis (Fig. 1D, right, Supplementary Fig. S3B, Supplementary Fig. S4). These results indicate that clone 7B12 of anti-boCD20 mAb recognizes linear epitopes of boCD20, and other mAbs recognize conformational epitopes.

We further addressed the expression of boCD20 in mesenteric lymph nodes (MesLNs) from healthy cattle and EBL-affected cattle. Immunohistochemical analysis with anti-boCD3 mAb and anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) showed both boCD3+ T-cell regions and boCD20+ B-cell regions in MesLNs from healthy cattle (Fig. 1E, left, Supplementary Fig. S5), whereas the massive expansion and infiltration of boCD20+ B cells and few boCD3+ T cells were observed in MesLNs from EBL-affected cattle (Fig. 1E, right, Supplementary Fig. S5).

Collectively, these results indicate that the generated mAbs to boCD20 specifically recognize boCD20 expressed on bovine B cells and EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells.

Anti-boCD20 mAb exhibited a cytotoxic function depending on CDC and ADCC against EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells

To evaluate the function of anti-boCD20 mAb, we addressed the ability of anti-boCD20 mAb to utilize CDC and ADCC for the destruction of BLSC-KU1. The clone A213 exhibited a higher CDC against BLSC-KU1 than clone A144-1 (Fig. 2A). On the other hand, clone A213 and clone A144-1 had a similar ability to elicit the interleukin (IL)-2-activated bovine natural killer (NK) cell-mediated ADCC against bovine B cell lines (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S6). We also observed little or no ability of clone 15A2 to induce CDC and ADCC against bovine B cell lines (Fig. 2A-B). Similar results were observed when BLSC-KU17 and EBL-derived primary B cells (EBL1) were used to determine their ability to exert CDC and ADCC (Supplementary Fig. S7A-B).

To determine the influence of anti-boCD20 mAb on the survival of bovine B cells, bovine whole blood was cultured with anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213), and we evaluated the frequency of boIgM+ B cells in bovine whole blood by flow cytometry (Fig. 2C). Treatment of bovine whole blood with anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) specifically reduced the frequency of boIgM+ B cells in a dose-dependent manner, and a peak of boIgM+ B-cell depletion was observed at a 1 μg/mL concentration of mAb.

Taken together, these results indicate that anti-boCD20 mAb had a capacity to exert CDC and ADCC against EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells.

Anti-boCD20 mAb induced the direct cell death of EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells

Having demonstrated that anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) showed CDC- and ADCC-independent cell death of B cells (Fig. 2A,B), we examined the frequency of cell death of BLSC-KU1 by using Annexin V/ PI following the treatment with anti-boCD20 mAb (clones 15A2, A144-1 and A213). Flow cytometric analysis showed that clones A144-1 and A213 induced the early apoptosis (PI-Annexin V+) and the direct cell death (PI+) of BLSC-KU1 in a dose-dependent manner, although clone 15A2 did not induce their direct cell death (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, clone A213 had a greater ability to induce the direct cell death in BLSC-KU1 than clone A144-1 at their lower concentrations (Fig. 3A). We also observed that clone A213 induced the direct cell death in BLSC-KU17 and EBL1 (Supplementary Fig. S7C).

To clarify how anti-boCD20 mAb induced direct cell death of EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells, we examined the influence of the enzyme inhibitors on their direct cell death. Treatment with actin-polymerization inhibitor (Latrunculin B) not only significantly inhibited the direct cell death and early apoptosis of BLSC-KU1 elicited by anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) (Fig. 3B) but also abrogated their homotypic adhesion (Fig. 3C). As caspases had been reportedly crucial regulators of apoptosis23, pan-caspase inhibitor (Q-VD-OPh) almost completely inhibited caspase-dependent apoptosis of BLSC-KU1 induced by Staurosporin24 (Fig. 3D). However, the treatment with pan-caspase inhibitor (Q-VD-OPh) did not affect the cell death of BLSC-KU1 caused by anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) (Fig. 3D).

Triggering lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) in response to apoptotic stimuli has reportedly caused the release of cathepsins in the cytosol, which activated the pathways of death signaling25. It has been reported that humanized anti-human CD20 mAb (Obinutuzumab, GA101) induced the direct cell death of human B-cell tumors via actin-reorganization followed by LMP26. Therefore, we investigated the ability of anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) to trigger LMP and release of lysosomal contents into the cytosol, indicating the lysosomal cell death, in BLSC-KU1. By quantifying the leakage of acridine orange from lysosome into cytosol as a decreasing in red and increase in green fluorescence27, the treatment with anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) resulted in LMP of BLSC-KU1 and their release of acridine orange into the cytosol (Fig. 3E). We also observed that treatment of BLSC-KU1 with cathepsin partially inhibited on the direct cell death of BLSC-KU1, but not their apoptosis (Fig. 3F).

Taken together, these results indicate that anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) induced the direct cell death in EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells possibly mediated through the homotypic adhesion and actin reorganization that trigger LMP leading to the release of cathepsins into the cytosol.

Anti-boCD20 mAb inhibited the progression of the growth of EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells in a xenotransplant bovine lymphoma model

Above study indicate that clone A213 of anti-boCD20 mAb could be a good candidate for in vivo depletion mAb targeting boCD20-expressing malignant cells due to the superior capacity to exert the cytotoxicity than other clones. To address the capacity of anti-boCD20 mAb to deplete EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells in vivo, we generated a xenotransplant lymphoma model by intravenous (i.v.) injection of BLSC-KU1 into the tail vein of severe immunodeficient NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Sug/ShiJic (NOG) mice, and their frequency in peripheral blood (PB) and spleen (Spl) was evaluated by flow cytometry after the administration of clone A213 and clone 15A2 as a control of anti-boCD20 mAb. Treatment of tumor-bearing NOG mice with clone A213, but not clones 15A2, showed a marked depletion of BLSC-KU1 in PB on day 3 after treatment, while their population had fully recovered on day 7 (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, this treatment also exhibited a significant clearance of BLSC-KU1 in Spl of tumor-bearing NOG mice (Fig. 4C).

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of anti-boCD20 mAb on EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma, NOG mice were subcutaneously (s.c.) injected with BLSC-KU1 in the right flank, and we monitored tumor growth following the administration of anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213). Treatment of tumor-bearing NOG mice with clone A213 significantly inhibited the progression of the established tumor in a dose-dependent manner, and the value of tumor-growth inhibition (TGI) of 23.9%, 45.3%, and 70.8% were observed at doses of 30 μg/mouse, 100 μg/mouse, and 300 μg/mouse, respectively (Fig. 5A–C, Supplementary Fig. S8). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis showed the detection of the injected anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) and the reduced expression of Ki-67 in tumor tissues in tumor-bearing NOG mice receiving its administration compared to those in tumor-bearing NOG mice receiving the administration of cont. IgG (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Discussion

While BCDT with anti-human CD20 mAbs has been employed to treat various B-cell malignancies in humans, this approach targeting boCD20 has not been developed for therapy of EBL-affected cattle. In this study, our generated mAbs to boCD20 exerted the cytotoxicity against EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells mediated through CDC and ADCC as well as direct cell death, resulting in the inhibition of their development in xenotransplant models.

Having demonstrated the specific binding of our generated anti-boCD20 mAbs to boCD20 by flow cytometric analysis as well as biochemical and immunohistochemical analysis, they could provide multiple opportunities for the characterization of bovine B cells and their malignant cells. Although anti-human CD20 mAb (clone MEM-97) has been reportedly cross-reacted with boCD20, it requires a step-by-step procedure for staining bovine B cells22. Indeed, flow cytometric analysis with this clone failed to show its clear cross-reaction to boCD20. On the other hand, our generated anti-boCD20 mAbs had high specific bindings to boCD20 on bovine B cells and EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells under the conventional staining procedure in flow cytometry. Although anti-human CD20 polyclonal Ab with cross-reacting with boCD20 has been used for the pathological diagnosis of EBL and B-cell malignancy in cattle19,20, the use of our generated anti-boCD20 mAbs might have the potential advantageous means to their pathological diagnosis due to the specific recognition of boCD20.

We showed that clone A213 of anti-boCD20 mAb showed the superior capacity to exert the cytotoxicity based on CDC, ADCC, and direct cell death than other clones. On the other hand, clone A213 of anti-boCD20 mAb showed higher affinity to the boCD20 expressed on boCD20/RBL-2H3 or BLSC-KU1 than other clones. Therefore, the excellent efficacy of clone A213 in the cytotoxicity might be due to its higher affinity to boCD20 than other clones.

Analysis of the cytotoxic function of anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) suggest that ADCC and direct cell death mainly mediate the destruction of EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells rather than CDC. Anti-human CD20 mAbs have reportedly been classified into type I mAbs (such as rituximab) or type II mAbs (such as obinutuzumab) based on their ability to redistribute human CD20 in the plasma membrane and activate various effector functions28. The type I mAbs has a higher capacity to induce CDC than type II mAbs possibly due to the shift of type I mAbs/CD20 complexes to lipid rafts followed by binding to C1q for initiating the classical complement activation pathway29,30. On the other hand, type II mAbs not only exhibit a higher capacity to induce ADCC than type I mAbs but also induce the direct cell death without cross-linking of their Fc portion30,31. These phenomena imply that the function of anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) is similar to those of type II anti-human CD20 mAbs.

Several anti-human CD20 mAbs have been shown to induce the direct cell death28. Cross-linking of rituximab has reportedly inhibited the signaling pathways involving p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), nuclear factor-κ B (NF-κB), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) 1/2 and Akt, leading to the inhibition of the expressions of B-cell/ chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) or B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-xL) and the induction of apoptosis32–35. It has been reported that type II anti-human CD20 mAb (GA101) has a potent capacity to induce the direct cell death without its cross-linking26,30. Furthermore, the ligation of human CD20 by anti-human CD20 mAb (GA101) have been shown to results in actin-reorganization followed by lysosomal membrane permeabilization, release of cathepsins, and generation of reactive oxygen species via nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) in nonapoptotic cell death26,36. On the other hand, mAbs targeting various human cell surface molecules, including HLA-DR37, CD4738, CD7439, and CD9940, have shown to trigger homotypic adhesion and actin reorganization, resulting in the direct cell death. Given that the ligation of boCD20 by anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) induced homotypic adhesion and actin-reorganization to trigger the LMP for the release of cathepsins into the cytosol, leading to the induction of direct cell death, the function of this mAb could be similar to that of anti-human CD20 mAb (GA101).

NOG mice have been shown to display the immunodeficiency with lacking T, B, and NK cells, complement activity41, as well as dysfunctional macrophage and dendritic cells42. Although NOG mice bearing EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells apparently deficient for the major mediators of CDC and ADCC, treatment of NOG mice bearing EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells with anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) not only reduced their circulating proportion in blood and Spl but also their cutaneous growth. Therefore, direct cell death could mainly mediate the function of anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) to deplete EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells in a xenotransplant lymphoma model. While BLV-infected cattle have reportedly exhibited the T-cell exhaustion43 and the decreased cytotoxic function of NK cells44, these functional impairments might reduce the capacity of BCDT to destroy the target cells via ADCC in EBL-affected cattle. As the direct cell death could be a main pathway for the function of anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) to destroy EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells, the usage of this mAb served a promising candidate for BCDT of EBL-affected cattle. Because the model of NOG mice transplanted with EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells cannot recapitulate the full potential effector mechanisms likely operative in EBL-affected cattle, future studies will be needed to determine the potential availability of anti-boCD20 mAb for the depletion of EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells in murine xenotransplant bovine lymphoma model endowed with a functional bovine immune system by using severely immunodeficient mice transplanted with bovine hematopoietic stem cells.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the newly developed mAb targeting boCD20 could be useful for the dissection of bovine B cells and their malignant cells by flow cytometric analysis as well as biochemical and immunohistochemical analysis. In future studies, the application of the generated anti-boCD20 mAb might contribute to the better understanding of biology of bovine B cells and their malignant cells in cattle. Furthermore, our generated anti-boCD20 mAb could deplete EBL-derived B-cell lymphoma cells to inhibit their progression in vivo. Thus, targeting boCD20 with mAbs may open new avenues for the incremental reliability of the diagnosis of BLV infection and EBL and for providing their new therapies.

Materials and methods

Animals

The following 6- to 10-week-old male and female mice were used in this study: C57BL/6 mice (Japan Clea), BALB/c mice (Japan Clea), and NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Sug/ShiJic mice41 with severe immunodeficient (NOG mice; In-Vivo Science). All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions in the animal facility at the University of Miyazaki. All experiment was performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Animal Experiment Committee (No. 2019-501, No. 2019-507, No. 2022-501, and No. 2025-013) and Gene Recombination Experiment Committee (No. 753 and No. 754) at the University of Miyazaki. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under the anesthesia with Isoflurane using Small Animal Anesthetizer (Muromachi Kikai). Cattle were euthanized by electric shock following intravenous injection with a combination of xylazine and pentobarbital45.

Cell lines

Bovine B cell lymphoma cell lines, BLSC-KU146,47 and BLSC-KU1747,48 (purchased from RIKEN cell bank), and bovine kidney-derived epithelial-like cell line, MDBK49, and rat mast cell line, RBL-2H321, were cultured in RPMI1640 medium (Wako) containing an antibiotic–antimycotic (Wako) and 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and air. Primary cell culture (EBL1; > 99% IgM+ cells, Supplementary Fig. S10A) derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) in EBL-affected cattle was maintained as described above with change the concentration of heat-inactivated FBS at 20%.

Retrovirus-mediated gene transfer

The full-length DNA sequence of boCD20 (NCBI: NM_001077854.2), which was tagged with BamHI and XhoI sites in 5’- and 3’-terminals, was custom-made using Geneart (Invitrogen). After restriction enzyme digestion, the DNA sequence was cloned into the sites of the pMX-IRES-green fluorescent protein (GFP) vector21, and then transfected into a retroviral packaging cell line, Phoenix21, using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen). The culture supernatant of Phenix, after 24 h of culture, was collected and concentrated using the Retro-X concentrator (Clontech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The retroviral vector was transfected into RBL-2H3 on the RetroNectin (Takara Bio)-coated 48-well plate by centrifugation at 1000g for an hour at room temperature, and transfectants were then collected in order to examine the expression of GFP and boCD20 (boCD20-GFP). Subsequently, boCD20/RBL-2H3 were sorted from transfectants by CytoFlex SRT cell sorter (Beckman Coulter).

Generation of murine IgG Fc fusion protein

The DNA sequence containing the long loop extracellular domain lacking the transmembrane and intracellular domain of boCD20 (NP_001071322.1, aa 140-189), which was tagged with BamHI and EcoRV sites in 5’- and 3’-terminals, was custom-made using GeneArt. After restriction enzyme digestion, the synthesized DNA sequence was cloned into sites of pFUSEN-mG2A-Fc (Invivogen), and transfected into Expi293F cells (Gibco), using ExpiFectamine 293 Transfection kit (Gibco) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the purification of recombinant boCD20-murine Fc fusion protein (boCD20-mIgFc fusion protein), the collected supernatant was purified using MonoSpin ProG (GL Science).

Mouse cell preparation

To prepare single-cell suspensions from Spl and popliteal LNs of mice, tissue samples were digested with collagenase type III (Worthington Biochemical) at 37 ℃ for 20 min and were ground between glass slides. Single-cell suspensions were obtained by forcing tissue samples through a 100-μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences). Splenocytes were further treated with RBC lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich). Mouse blood cells were collected using a heparinized hematocrit tube (Drummond) from the tail vein, and mouse PBMCs were isolated using Lympholyte-M cell suspension Media (Cederlane).

Bovine blood cells preparation

Prior to the collection of blood in cattle, we obtained the permission from the Animal Experiment Committee at the University of Miyazaki (No. 2025-013). In brief, fresh blood was collected with 2 mM EDTA from 18- to 21-year-old healthy female Japanese Black cattle kept at the experimental farm in University of Miyazaki as described previously50, and bovine PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva). Fresh blood was used for a whole blood assay to evaluate bovine B-cell depletion by anti-boCD20 mAb, and bovine PBMCs were used for NK-cell isolation or cryopreserved with the CELLBANKER 1 (Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo) in liquid nitrogen for the subsequent experiments.

Generation of anti-boCD20 mAbs

C57BL/6 mice and BALB/c mice were immunized with boCD20-mIgFc fusion protein (25 μg) plus TiterMax Gold (Funakoshi) three times on day 0, day 14 and day 21. Three days after final immunization, lymph node cells and spleen cells were fused with P3U1 mouse myeloma cells as described previously21,51. After hypoxanthine-aminopterin-thymidine selection, Abs that could react with transfectants were screened by flow cytometry using PE-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) polyclonal Ab (Abcam). Several hybridomas producing mAbs were identified by their strong binding to RBL-2H3 expressing boCD20-GFP and were cloned by limiting dilution. Subsequently, mAbs to boCD20 were purified from the supernatant after culturing hybridomas in serum-free HY200 medium (GMEP Cell Technologies) using MonoSpin ProG (GL Sciences). Purified mAbs were dialyzed with PBS using a 100 K Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter (Merck Millipore). The subclasses of purified mAbs were determined using a Rapid ELISA Mouse mAb Isotyping Kit (Thermo Scientific). For additional screening and verifying the specificity of anti-boCD20 mAbs by flow cytometry, bovine PBMCs were stained with individual anti-boCD20 mAb followed by PE-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) polyclonal Ab, and then stained with fluorescein-conjugated mAbs to boCD3 (clone MM1A, Kingfisher Biotech) and boIgM (clone IL-A29, Bio-Rad). For competitive binding assay by flow cytometry, BLSC-KU1 cells were stained with mixture of 100 ng/mL of biotinylated anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) and serial dilutions of other clones of non-labeled anti-boCD20 mAbs. Subsequently, the washed cells were stained with PE-conjugated streptavidin (eBioscience) to detect cell-bound biotinylated anti-boCD20 mAb.

Genomic polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

The existence of BLV-provirus in bovine B cell lines was confirmed by genomic PCR. In brief, genomic DNA from bovine B cell lines was extracted with a Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA concentration was determined using the NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and diluted to 25 ng/μL for PCR. PCR was performed using Quick Taq HS Dye Mix (Toyobo) on a Veriti Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems). BLV provirus was detected with specific primer pairs for LTR1-F (5’-tgtatgaaagatcatgccga-3’) and LTR531-R (5’-tgtttgccggtctctcctgg-3’) targeted on BLV long-terminal repeat (LTR) region, or env503252 (5’-tctgtgccaagtctcccagata-3’) and env5608r52 (5’-aacaacaacctctgggaagggt-3’) targeted on BLV envelope region. The primer set of HL03053 (5’-atcctctctctgcagcacatttcc-3’) and HL03253 (5’-tcgccgctgcacagtgaaactctc-3’) was used as an endogenous control to detect BoLA-DRB3 exon 2. The PCR products were electrophoresed on 1.5% Agarose gel plate and visualized using ultraviolet light (Supplementary Fig. S10B).

Flow cytometry

Bovine and murine single cell suspensions were stained with fluorescein-conjugated mAbs after Fc blocking with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 mAb (clone 2.4G2, BD Biosciences) and 2% FBS or with 2% FBS for staining of bovine CD16. The mAbs and fluorescent conjugation kit used in this study are listed in Table 1. To exclude dead cells, the cells were further stained with PI (Sigma-Aldrich) or Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience). For staining CD79α in bovine PBMCs, the cells were resuspended in Fixation and Permeabilization solution (eBioscience) after other staining, and then intracellular staining was carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence staining was analyzed with a CytoFlex Flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and FlowJo v10.8.1 Software (BD Biosciences; https://www.bdbiosciences.com/ja-jp/products/software/flowjo-software?tab=flowjo-v10-software).

Western blotting and immunoprecipitation of boCD20

The cell lysates of BLSC-KU1, MDBK, RBL-2H3, and boCD20/RBL-2H3 cells (2 × 107/mL) were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and blots were probed with anti-boCD20 mAb (1 μg/mL; clone 7B12, in house) and anti-beta actin mAb (10 ng/mL; clone 2D4H5, Proteintech) followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (10,000-fold dilution, Abcam). For immunoprecipitation, the cell lysates of boCD20/RBL-2H3 and BLSC-KU1 were reacted with the prepared anti-boCD20 mAb (clone 7B12, 15A2, A144-1 or A213)-bound protein G Mag Sepharose (Cytiva) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To detect boCD20, the elution of anti-boCD20 mAb-bound protein was analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and they were stained with Silver Stain kit (Bio-Rad). Alternatively, the blot was probed with a biotinylated anti-boCD20 mAb (1 μg/mL, clone 7B12) followed by HRP-conjugated streptavidin (1,000-fold dilution, Thermo Scientific). Immunohistochemical analysis

Bovine MesLNs from healthy cattle or EBL-affected cattle were embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetechnical) and frozen in liquid N2. Embedded tissues were cut into 7-μm-thick sections and placed onto silane-coated slide glasses. Frozen sections were fixed in cold acetone and blocked in 1% BSA/PBS containing normal mouse IgG (100 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich). Anti-boCD3 mAb (clone MM1A, Kingfisher Biotech), anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213), and control IgG (clone MOPC-21, Selleck) were conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 555 by Zenon mouse IgG1 labeling kits (Invitrogen). After blocking, the sections were stained with fluorescent-conjugated antibodies (10 μg/mL) for 1 h at room temperature. At the end of staining, the slides were washed, counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen) and mounted with SlowFade Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen). Alternatively, bovine MesLNs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and then embedded in OCT compound followed by frozen in liquid N2. Embedded tissues were cut into 4-μm-thick sections and placed onto silane-coated slide glasses. Frozen sections were fixed in cold acetone and then incubated with 0.3% H2O2 in methanol to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. After blocking with 1% BSA/PBS containing normal goat IgG (100 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich), the sections were stained with primary Abs; anti-boCD3 mAb (clone MM1A, Kingfisher Biotech), anti-boCD20 mAbs (clone 7B12, 15A2 or A213) or anti-human CD79α mAb (HM57, Invitrogen). The sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG polyclonal Ab (Abcam) followed by Histofine-simple stain 3-Amino-9-Ethylcarbazole (AEC) solution (Nichirei). Tissues were counterstained with Mayer’s Hematoxylin solution (Muto Pure Chemicals Co.). The stained slides were examined with a BZ-X710 fluorescence microscope (Keyence).

CDC assay

Bovine B cell lines were stained with calcein-AM/PBS (1 μM; AAT Bioquest) for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by two washes with PBS. Calcein-AM-stained cells were incubated at a density of 1.25 × 105 per well in a 96-well U-bottom plate. Diluted mAb was added to the culture for 15 min before the addition of rabbit complement (final concentration of 25%, Cedarlane). After incubation for 1 h at 37 ℃, cells were stained with PI to confirm dead cells before flow cytometric analysis, and CDC was estimated by the percentage of PI-negative and calcein-AM-retained cells, which were considered as live cells. Spontaneous lysis (corresponding to the dead target cells with complement in the absence of mAb) was defined as 0% of specific cell-death. To evaluate the effect of complement, the percentage of dead target cells was calculated using the following equation: Target cell death (%) = 100 × [1 − (calcein AM-positive live cells in mAb-treatment/Average of calcein AM-positive live cells in untreated control)]. Data were obtained from more than 10,000 cells per well.

ADCC assay

Bovine NK cells were prepared from fresh boPBMC by sorting with anti-boCD335 mAb (clone AKS1, Bio-Rad), which was conjugated with Readilink 647/674 (Bio-Rad). They were subsequently cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with recombinant bovine IL-2 (50 ng/mL)50 used for ADCC assay. Bovine B cell lines were stained and plated in the same manner as described in the CDC assay, and mAb was added in the culture. Subsequently, they were incubated for 15 min before the addition with bovine NK cells as effector cells. Effector and target cells were cultured at a 1:1 ratio for 4 h at 37 °C. Incubated cells were washed with PBS and stained with PI. The live cells were identified by the calcein-AM and PI-negative region by flow cytometry. The percentage of the dead target cells was calculated in the same way as described in CDC assay. Data were obtained from more than 5,000 calcein AM-positive live cells/well.

Bovine whole blood B-cell depletion assay

Whole blood containing 2 mM EDTA from healthy cattle was placed as 100 μL aliquots in 1.5 mL tubes. Subsequently, 10 μL of serially diluted mAb was added, and the tubes were mixed gently and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. After lysis of erythrocytes using RBC lysis buffer for 5 min at room temperature, cells were stained with fluorescent-conjugated mAb and Fixable viability Dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience). B-cell depletion was evaluated by the percentages of the populations of boCD3+ T cells and boIgM+ B cells, displaying more than 50,000 cells in the gate of the live-lymphocyte region in flow cytometric analysis. Evaluation of the B-cell depletion was performed using the average bovine B-/T-cell ratio, with untreated sample set as 100% bovine B cells (equivalent to 0% of B-cell depletion)30. Bovine B-/T-cell ratio = percentage of boIgM+ B cells in lymphocyte region/ percentage of boCD3+ T cells in lymphocyte region; B-cell depletion = 100 × [1 − (bovine B-/T-cell ratio in sample with mAb)/(average of bovine B-/T-cell ratio in sample without mAb)].

Assessment of the direct cell death

Bovine B cell lines (1.25 × 105/well) were seeded into a 96-well plate in the presence of mAb, and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were stained with FITC Annexin V (BioLegend) and PI (Sigma-Aldrich) in Annexin V binding buffer (BD Biosciences) for 15 min at room temperature. The cell population stained with PI was defined as dead cells, and PI-negative/Annexin V-positive cells were defined as early apoptosis.

Direct cell death inhibition assay

BLSC-KU1 (1.25 × 105/well) were treated with a pan-caspase inhibitor (20 μM, QVD-Oph, MedchemExpress), an actin-polymerisation inhibitor (10 μM, Latrunculin B, Abcam), or a cathepsin inhibitor III (50 μM, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. Then, cells were incubated in the presence of mAb for 4 h and stained as described above for Annexin V and PI. Staurosporine (2 μM, Cayman Chemical) was used as a positive control for caspase-dependent apoptosis.

Lysosomal staining

BLSC-KU1 were stained with acridine orange (5 μM, Dojindo) for 10 min at 37 °C. After washing, the stained cells were incubated with mAb (10 μg/mL) for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Data collection was focused on the FITC channel and the PerCP-Cy5.5 channel to evaluate lysosomal permeabilization (increasing Green Fluorescence) and lysosomal shrinking (decreasing Red Fluorescence).

Xenotransplant bovine lymphoma model

NOG mice were i.v. transplanted with the BLSC-KU1 (5 × 106/mouse) through the tail vein. After 28 days of the transplantation, mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with anti-boCD20 mAb (clones A213 and 15A2, 100 µg/mouse) or isotype control Ab (100 µg/mouse, clone MOPC-21, Selleck). Subsequently, PB (100 µl/mouse) was collected in 2 mM EDTA from the tail vein on day 0, day 3 and day 7 after the injection. Spl were also obtained from tumor-bearing mice on day 7 after the injection to evaluate the proportion of BLSC-KU1 by flow cytometry. In brief, PBMCs and splenocytes were stained with fluorescein-conjugated mAbs to boCD3 (clone MM1A), boIgM (clone IL-A29, Bio-Rad), boCD11b (clone CC126, Bio-Rad), boCD335 (clone AKS1, Bio-Rad), boCD45 (clone CC1, Bio-Rad), and murine CD45 (clone 30-F11, BD Horizon), and the proportion of BLSC-KU1 in leukocytes analyzed by flow cytometry. Alternatively, NOG mice were subcutaneously (s.c.) inoculated with the BLSC-KU1 (5 × 106 /mice) into the right flank. The tumor size was measured every four days from day 28 to day 50 after tumor inoculation using a digital caliper (CP-15CP; Mitsutoyo), and tumor volumes (TV) were approximated using the ellipsoidal formula: TV = (length; longer diameter) × (width; shorter diameter)2 × 0.5. For the treatment, the anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213, 100 µg/mouse) or isotype control Ab (clone MOPC-21) was administered i.p. into tumor-bearing mice with an average tumor size of 651.8 mm3 every four days for a total of five times starting from on day 28 after tumor inoculation. Alternatively, anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213; 300 µg/mouse, 100 µg/mouse or 30 µg/mouse) was administered i.p. into tumor-bearing mice with an average tumor size of 718.2 mm3 weekly for a total of three times. Antitumor activity was assessed by calculating TGI based on the average of tumor size by using the following formula as described29: TGI = 100 × (1-Average [TV_treatment (day 50) − TV_treatment (day 28)]/ Average [TV_reference (day 50) − TV_reference (day 28)]). For the detection of the injected anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213) and the proliferation of tumor cells in tumor tissues, tumor-bearing NOG mice were treated with anti-boCD20 mAb (clone A213, 100 µg/mouse) or isotype control Ab (clone MOPC-21, 100 μg/mouse) on day 21 after BLSC-KU1 inoculation, and then tumor tissue were obtained on day 24 after BLSC-KU1 inoculation. Subsequently, tumor tissues were embedded, cut and placed onto slide glasses as described above. After fixing and blocking, the sections were stained with rabbit anti-human Ki67 polyclonal Ab (Proteintech) followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG polyclonal Ab (Invitrogen) to detect the proliferation of transplanted tumor cells or APC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG polyclonal Ab (BioLegend) to confirm the engagement of boCD20 expressed on tumor cells by the injected anti-boCD20 mAb.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad Software; https://www.graphpad.com/). Data are expressed as the mean ± s.d. from three to five individual samples in a single experiment of at least three independent experiments. The statistical significance of the differences between the values obtained was evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparison post-hoc test. The data from the animal study were expressed as the median ± 95% confidence interval, based on four to nine individual samples from all pooled experiments, with at least two independent experiments. The statistical significance of the differences between the values obtained was evaluated using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. The Kd value was estimated using the Nonlinear Regression One Site-Total binding curve fit.
