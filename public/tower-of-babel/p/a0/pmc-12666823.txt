Ionotropic receptors mediate arachidic acid perception and food avoidance behavior in Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Roshani Nhuchhen Pradhan, Muhammad Atif, Youngseok Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chemosensory functions are essential for animals to distinguish between nutritious and harmful compounds. Fatty acids are vital dietary components, but their taste perception mechanisms remain poorly understood. In Drosophila melanogaster, we identified a cluster of ionotropic receptors (IR7g, IR25a, IR51b, IR67a, and IR76b) that mediate the detection of the long-chain saturated fatty acid, arachidic acid (ARCH), through bitter-sensing gustatory receptor neurons. Our findings demonstrate that high doses of ARCH elicit strong aversive responses and exhibit toxic effects, decreasing survival rates in a dose-dependent manner. ARCH detection was found to require the activation of bitter-sensing gustatory receptor neurons, and the inhibition of sugar-sensing neurons contributed to diminished feeding responses. This study provides critical insights into the molecular and neural mechanisms underlying fatty acid perception in Drosophila, opening new avenues for understanding how animals manage dietary lipid intake and its implications for health.

INTRODUCTION

Taste is one of the most fundamental senses vital for survival across species. It allows organisms to evaluate the nutritional value and potential toxicity of food, guiding feeding behavior. Understanding how taste influences behavior is crucial not only for insights into feeding behavior but also for its broader implications in nutrition, health, and disease prevention. The exploration of taste perception dates back to ancient philosophers like Aristotle. Significant advancements in the 19th and 20th centuries led to the discovery of taste receptors and the mapping of taste pathways in the brain (Brandt et al., 2024, Hendriksen and Wragge-Morley, 2022).

The study of taste perception has been central to understanding how flies respond to basic taste modalities, such as sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami (Aryal et al., 2022a, Atif and Lee, 2025, Dahanukar et al., 2007, Dweck et al., 2022, Ganguly et al., 2017, Jaeger et al., 2018, Lee et al., 2009, Moon et al., 2006, McDowell et al., 2022, page number Sang et al., 2024; Shrestha and Lee, 2021a, Shrestha and Lee, 2021b Shrestha et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2013a). These tastes are detected by specialized receptors located on the tongue and other parts of the digestive tract, which send signals to the brain, triggering appropriate responses (Depoortere, 2014, Xi et al., 2022). In Drosophila melanogaster, the fruit fly, taste perception has become an invaluable model for studying the molecular and neural mechanisms of taste detection. The fly’s taste system is well-characterized, with receptors located not only in the mouthparts but also on the legs, wings, and ovipositor (Montell, 2021, Shrestha and Lee, 2023). These receptors are housed in sensory structures called sensilla, which contain gustatory receptor neurons (GRNs) that respond to different taste modalities. The labellum, the major taste organ of Drosophila, contains 31 taste sensilla, which are categorized as short (S), intermediate (I), and long (L) based on their length. Each sensillum houses a combination of chemosensory neurons and a single mechanosensory neuron. Typically, L- and S-type sensilla contain 4 neurons, whereas I-type sensilla have 2. S-type sensilla are responsible for detecting water, sweet compounds, bitter substances, and cations like Ca2+ and Na+, whereas I-type sensilla mainly detect sweet and bitter tastes. L-type sensilla detect water and sweet compounds, with additional GRNs sensitive to low concentrations of Na+. These GRNs transmit signals to the brain’s subesophageal zone, which processes taste information and generates behavioral responses (Scott, 2018). Given its highly conserved nature, the fly’s taste system offers critical insights into the evolutionary and ecological significance of taste perception.

Beyond basic taste modalities, the perception of fatty acids has become an important area of research in Drosophila. Fatty acids are vital for energy storage, cell membrane structure, and signaling pathways, making their detection essential for metabolic homeostasis (De Carvalho and Caramujo, 2018). Recent studies have shown that Drosophila can detect and respond to fatty acids, which influence their feeding behavior and dietary choices (Ahn et al., 2017, Brown et al., 2021, Pradhan et al., 2023a). The ability to sense fatty acids involves a combination of gustatory receptors (GRs; GR32a, GR33a, GR66a, and GR64e) and ionotropic receptors (IRs; IR25a and IR56d), which detect a broad spectrum of chemical cues, including fatty acids and cholesterol (Ahn et al., 2017, Kim et al., 2018, Pradhan et al., 2023b, Pradhan et al., 2025).

Research has revealed that Drosophila distinguishes between short-chain fatty acids, medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs), and long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs). For instance, hexanoic acid (a 6-carbon short-chain fatty acid) and octanoic acid (an 8-carbon MCFA) elicit avoidance behaviors mediated by bitter-sensitive GRNs (Ahn et al., 2017, Brown et al., 2021). However, behavior also depends on the concentration of fatty acids; for example, lower concentration of hexanoic acid is sensed by sweet neuron, while higher by bitter neuron. These responses suggest that fatty acids can activate bitter pathways, typically associated with the detection of harmful substances. However, the response to fatty acids is not uniform. Some fatty acids, particularly those with longer carbon chains, may elicit both attractive and aversive responses depending on concentration and context. Oleic acid (an 18-carbon monounsaturated fatty acid) and hexanoic acid, eg, trigger both attraction and aversion, highlighting the complexity of fatty acid perception (Masek and Keene, 2013, Pradhan et al., 2023b, Dey et al., 2023). Specifically, in the case of hexanoic acid, GRs mediate the perception of toxic concentrations, whereas IRs are responsible for sensing attractive concentrations (Pradhan et al., 2023b).

Arachidic acid (ARCH), an LCFA with 20 carbon atoms, is found in plant-based oils, such as peanut oil, perilla oil, and corn oil. This fatty acid has gained attention due to its potential health implications. Excessive intake of LCFAs, such as ARCH, has been linked to negative health outcomes, particularly cardiovascular disease (Baum et al., 2012, Visioli and Poli, 2020). Furthermore, while ARCH is not a dominant dietary component for Drosophila, its ecological significance may still be worth considering. Saturated LCFAs like ARCH can influence the structure of microbial biofilms and may affect the growth of yeast and bacteria that Drosophila may reject. In this way, even minor constituents like ARCH may indirectly modulate the chemical landscape that shapes Drosophila behavior, development, and microbiota composition. Moreover, if Drosophila has the sensory or metabolic capability to detect and respond to ARCH, it might influence feeding rejection or even lifespan. Although ARCH is not well-studied in this context, exploring its presence in natural food substrates and potential signaling roles could open interesting avenues for understanding dietary lipid processing and ecological adaptation in Drosophila. Therefore, understanding how ARCH is perceived by Drosophila can provide valuable insights into its ecological and health significance.

This study aims to assess whether ARCH elicits positive or negative sensory responses in D. melanogaster using behavioral and physiological assays. We also aim to investigate the molecular and neural mechanisms underlying ARCH taste perception. Specifically, we will explore the involvement of bitter-sensitive GRNs and specific IRs, such as IR7g, IR25a, IR51b, IR67a, and IR76b, in mediating the detection of ARCH. By examining these pathways, we seek to better understand the sensory mechanisms involved in the detection of LCFAs and their potential ecological and evolutionary significance. Ultimately, our findings may provide insights into the broader implications of fatty acid sensation in other organisms, including humans.

Binary Food Choice Assay

We conducted binary food choice assays using a previously described method (Aryal et al., 2022b). The experiment commenced by subjecting a cohort of 50 to 70 flies (aged 4-7 days, comprising both males and females) to an 18-hour fasting period within a controlled humid chamber. The subsequent procedures involved the preparation of 2 distinct food sources, each containing 1% agarose as the base and supplemented with 2 mM sucrose. Only 1 of these sources also incorporated various concentrations of ARCH. To differentiate between these 2 food sources, blue food coloring dye (0.125 mg/ml of brilliant blue FCF) was introduced to one, and red food coloring dye (0.1 mg/ml of sulforhodamine B) to the other. These solutions were evenly distributed within the wells of a 72-well microtiter dish (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 438733), alternating between the 2 options. Approximately 50 to 70 starved flies (in 1% agarose for 16-18 hours [humidity: 50%-60%, temperature: 25°C, and light/dark: 12 hours/12 hours]) were then introduced to the plate within approximately 30 minutes of food preparation. The flies were allowed to feed at room temperature for 90 minutes within a dark, humid environment to maintain consistent conditions. Subsequently, the tested flies were carefully sacrificed at −20°C for further analysis. The coloration of their abdomens was observed and categorized as blue (NB), red (NR), or purple (NP) based on visual assessment. The preference index (PI), a value derived from the combinations of dye and tastant, was computed for each fly. The formula used for this calculation was (NB − NR)/(NR + NB + NP) or (NR − NB)/(NR + NB + NP). A PI of either 1.0 or −1.0 indicated a significant preference toward one of the food alternatives, while a PI of 0.0 signified no bias among the flies toward either option.

Tip Recordings

The electrophysiological procedure, specifically the tip-recording assay, was conducted in strict accordance with established protocols (Moon et al., 2006). Satiated flies of both genders, aged 4 to 7 days, were gently anesthetized on ice to facilitate the procedure. A reference glass electrode containing Ringer's solution (3 mM CaCl2, 182 mM KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris base, and 1 N HCl) was meticulously inserted into the thorax of the flies and gradually advanced toward the proboscis of each specimen. This process was repeated over multiple days to ensure the reliability and consistency of the recorded results.

To stimulate the sensilla, a recording pipette with a tip diameter ranging from 10 to 20 µm was affixed to a preamplifier. The pipette was filled with a mixture of chemical stimulants dissolved in a 30 mM TCC solution, which served as the electrolyte solution. Signal amplification was accomplished through a Syntech signal connection interface box and a band-pass filter with a frequency range of 100 to 3,000 Hz. These amplified signals were recorded at a frequency of 12 kHz and subsequently analyzed using Autospike 3.1 software provided by Syntech. Each subsequent recording had a stimulation interval of approximately 1 minute. Only spikes occurring between 50 and 550 ms were counted. The average action potential frequencies (spikes per second) for the responses are shown.

Survival Assay

Survival evaluations were conducted in accordance with established methodology (Shrestha et al., 2022). For the survival study, groups of 10 male and 10 female flies (aged from 4-5 days) were meticulously chosen. These flies were provided with food containing various concentrations of ARCH, along with 1% agar and 1% sucrose. The viability of the flies was carefully monitored and assessed at 12-hour intervals. After each evaluation, the flies were transferred to fresh vials containing the same food source to maintain the continuity of the experiment. These survival investigations enable us to evaluate fly viability and determine the influence of ARCH concentration on survival rates. The median lethal time (LT₅₀) was estimated using the following formula:

LT50 = (First − Initial/2)/(First − Second) × Interval + FirstHour. Where Initial is the total number of flies at the start of the experiment, First is the number of flies alive at the earlier observation time (FirstHour), Second is the number of flies alive at the subsequent observation time, and Interval is the duration (hours) between these 2 observations.

RT-PCR Analysis

For gene expression analysis, whole-body samples were collected from 40 adult flies of the mutant and control strains. In addition, labellum samples were dissected from approximately 40 adult flies of the Gr5a-GAL4/UAS-hid and Gr33a-GAL4/UAS-hid lines. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and first-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with AMV reverse transcriptase (Promega). RT-PCR was conducted using gene-specific primers for Ir7g, Ir51b, and Ir67a, with tubulin serving as an internal reference gene. The primer sequences were as follows:

Amplification products were resolved on agarose gels, and the observed band sizes were consistent with the expected amplicons: Ir7g (1,758 bp), Ir51b (1,705 bp), Ir67a (1,641 bp), and tubulin (464 bp).

Quantification and Statistical Analyses

The data were processed and figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (RRID: SCR 002798). Each experiment was independently replicated on different days, with the number of trials depicted as data points on the graphs. Error bars on the graphs represent the standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparisons among multiple datasets, single-factor ANOVA, followed by Scheffe's post hoc analysis, was employed. All statistical analyses were conducted using Origin (Origin Lab Corp., RRID: SCR 002815). In the figures, asterisks indicate statistical significance: *P < .05; **P < .01.

RESULTS

ARCH Reduces the Lifespan of the Fruit Fly

In the complex ecological dynamics that organisms face, they must navigate a variety of environmental factors, encountering numerous compounds, some of which may pose potential health risks if ingested. ARCH, a long-chain saturated fatty acid found in both plants and animals, has garnered attention due to its health implications. Although it is naturally present in many dietary sources, excessive consumption of ARCH has been linked to adverse health outcomes (Calder et al., 2019). Given the evolutionary importance of distinguishing and avoiding harmful substances, we investigated how D. melanogaster, a model organism, responds to various concentrations of ARCH. We assessed the survival responses of wild-type to ARCH toxicity by providing them with 1% sucrose or 1% sucrose mixed with different concentrations of ARCH (10−5%-10−1%) over a 13-day period. Controls were given 1% sucrose (positive control) or subjected to complete starvation with 1% agar (negative control) (Fig. 1A). Our results revealed a clear relationship between fruit fly survival rates and the ARCH concentration. At high concentrations (10−1%), fruit fly survival was significantly reduced compared with the control group fed with 1% agar, indicating marked toxicity. In contrast, lower concentrations (10−5%) of ARCH were associated with higher survival rates compared with higher dose but still exhibits the lower level of toxicity. This suggests that flies exhibit a nuanced survival to different concentrations of ARCH exposure. The median lethality time (LT50) for flies fed 1% sucrose was 238 ± 4.81 hours, which was significantly longer than the LT50 of 37.3 ± 1.38 hours recorded for the group exposed to 10−1% ARCH, indicating rapid toxicity at high doses. Interestingly, within the toxic range, ARCH exposure resulted in a shorter lifespan than starvation alone. However, survival rates improved (LT50: 201.2 ± 4.92 hours) at lower doses, such as 10−5% ARCH in comparison with its higher dose. Additionally, intermediate concentrations of ARCH (10−3% and 10−2%) modestly enhanced survival rates, with LT50 values of 169.67 ± 11.78 and 181 ± 5.40 hours, respectively. These findings suggest that ARCH can have harmful effects in dose-dependent manner. Given the pronounced sexual dimorphism in D. melanogaster and the well-documented physiological and behavioral differences between males and females (Lushchak et al., 2023), we hypothesized that their responses to ARCH might differ. ARCH Activates Bitter-Sensing GRNs in S-Type Sensilla

Following our investigation into the effects of ARCH on survival, we shifted our focus to understanding whether ARCH directly stimulates neuronal responses in the labellar GRNs. The labellum, a key gustatory organ in Drosophila, consists of both taste hairs and taste pegs and has been extensively studied for its role in taste detection (Shrestha and Lee, 2023). As mentioned in the “Introduction”, each hemisphere of the labellum houses 31 taste sensilla, categorized into 3 types based on their length: L, I, and S (Fig. 1B) (Hiroi et al., 2002). To pinpoint which specific sensilla are responsive to ARCH, we analyzed all 31 sensilla using 10−3% ARCH (Fig. 1C). Our findings revealed that only the S6 and S7 sensilla showed significant neuronal activation in response to 10−3% ARCH, with minimal or no responses observed in other S-, I-, or L-type sensilla. To determine the specific GRNs required for sensing ARCH, we used genetic manipulation to hyperpolarize targeted neurons. This was achieved by expressing Kir2.1, an inwardly rectifying potassium channel, to inhibit neuronal activity (Fig. 1D, E) (Paradis et al., 2001). We selectively inactivated specific classes of GRNs (Montell, 2021): Class A (Gr64f-GAL4, sweet-sensing GRN) (Dahanukar et al., 2007), Class B (Gr33a-GAL4, bitter-sensing GRN) (Moon et al., 2009), Class C (ppk28-GAL4, water-sensing GRN) (Cameron et al., 2010), and Class D (ppk23-GAL4, calcium/pheromone-sensing GRN) (Lee et al., 2018). Among these, inhibiting the bitter-sensing Class B GRNs significantly reduced action potential firing; in contrast, the other GRNs (Class A, C, and D) and the control flies displayed similar neuronal firing patterns. These results provide that ARCH-induced aversive response is predominantly mediated by bitter-sensing GRNs.

We next tested whether sensilla differ in their sensitivity to various concentrations of ARCH (ranging from 10−5%-10−2%). Tip recordings were performed on S7, I8, and L6 sensilla (Fig. 1F). Our results showed that the S7 sensilla in wild-type flies exhibited a significant increase in action potential firing rates in response to escalating concentrations of ARCH, with the highest neuronal activity occurring at 10−3% ARCH. In contrast, L6 and I8 sensilla showed no significant responses across the tested concentration range. These findings suggest that ARCH is selectively sensed by the neurons in S-type sensilla, which likely transmit aversive signals to the brain.

Together, our electrophysiological and genetic analyses provide strong evidence that ARCH detection in Drosophila is mediated specifically by bitter-sensing GRNs located in the S-type labellar sensilla. Given the known role of these GRNs in detecting other aversive or toxic compounds, their activation by ARCH suggests a conserved neural mechanism underlying taste-mediated avoidance. This response likely serves an adaptive role, enabling flies to identify and reject harmful substances. Our findings thus highlight the specialized function of bitter-sensing GRNs not only in taste aversion but also in broader chemosensory defense strategies essential for survival.

Five IRs (IR7g, IR25a, IR51b, IR67a, and IR76b) Are Essential for the Neuronal Response to Stimulus ARCH

We determined that 10−3% ARCH induces an aversive response and activates bitter-sensing GRNs. To identify the molecular sensors involved in detecting ARCH, we screened available mutant libraries of IRs and bitter-sensing GRs from the most responsive sensillum, S7 (Fig. 1C). Our IR library included broadly expressed IRs, such as IR25a and IR76b (Lee et al., 2018), along with IRs associated with specific stimuli, including acetic acid (IR7a) (Rimal et al., 2019), high salt (IR7c and IR60b) (Sang et al., 2024, McDowell et al., 2022), low salt (IR56b) (Dweck et al., 2022, Sang et al., 2024, Asefa et al., 2024), calcium (IR62a) (Lee et al., 2018), cantharidin (IR94f) (Pradhan et al., 2024), sour taste (IR51b, IR94a, and IR94h) (Shrestha et al., 2024), and alkali (IR47a, IR52a, and IR92a) (Pandey et al., 2023). Similarly, the GR library consisted of broad bitter molecular sensors, such as GR32a, GR33a, and GR66a (Shrestha and Lee, 2021a, Shrestha and Lee, 2021b), as well as GRs like GR47a, GR89a, and GR93a (Lee et al., 2009, Shrestha et al., 2022). To further explore the involvement of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, known for their roles in sensory perception (Al-Anzi et al., 2006, Cheng et al., 2010, Fowler and Montell, 2013, Gong et al., 2013, Jeong et al., 2016, Kang et al., 2012, Kang et al., 2010, Kim et al., 2010, Kwon et al., 2010, Liu et al., 2007, Zhang et al., 2013b), we examined several TRP channel mutants (Fig. S4). Despite their importance in various sensory functions, all TRP channel mutants tested showed normal neuronal responses to 10−3% ARCH, indicating that TRP channels are not essential for sensing ARCH. This finding highlights the complexity of the molecular mechanisms underlying fatty acid detection in Drosophila.

In our effort to identify the specific receptors required for bitter neurons to sense ARCH, we employed an RNAi-based approach targeting both broadly expressed ionotropic co-receptors, such as Ir25a and Ir76b, as well as tuning receptors like Ir7g, Ir51b, and Ir67a (Fig. 2D and S3B). Knockdown of these Irs in bitter neurons resulted in a significant reduction in action potential firing on S6 and S7 sensilla in response to 10−3% ARCH, further confirming the essential role of these receptors in the perception of ARCH by bitter neurons. Notably, when examining ppk23-GAL4-labeled neurons, which are involved in sensing other aversive compounds such as pheromone (Lu et al., 2012) and calcium/sodium salt (Lee et al., 2018, McDowell et al., 2022), the action potential response remained similar to control flies, indicating that these receptors are specifically required in bitter-sensing neurons for accurate detection of ARCH.

Next, we conducted dose-dependent physiological analyses. We confirmed that the response to ARCH across concentrations ranging from 10−5% to 10−1% was significantly impaired in the IR mutants in all doses (Fig. 2E). However, these deficits were rescued when we expressed wild-type cDNA of the respective IRs in the mutants. Specifically, we rescued the suppressed response in Ir25a2 and Ir76b1 mutants by expressing their cDNA under their specific GAL4 drivers or the bitter-sensing Gr33a-GAL4 (Figs. 2F and S3C). Similarly, expressing the wild-type cDNA of IR7g, IR51b, and IR67a under Gr33a-GAL4 restored the neuronal defects observed in these mutants. These findings, combined with dose-dependent impairments in neuronal responses and successful genetic rescue experiments, underscore the critical role of these 5 IRs—IR7g, IR25a, IR51b, IR67a, and IR76b—in mediating the detection of ARCH in Drosophila. Together, these results provide compelling evidence that these IRs function within bitter-sensing GRNs to detect ARCH and may mediate the aversive response to ARCH.

ARCH Acts as an Antifeedant

The integration of physiological measurements with behavioral assays is critical for validating research findings and understanding how sensory responses translate into actions (Aryal et al., 2022b). To investigate the relationship between taste receptor activity and behavioral responses, we employed an unbiased binary food choice assay (Aryal et al., 2022b) designed to explore the effects of ARCH on the feeding behavior of D. melanogaster. In this experiment, flies were presented with a choice between 2 mM sucrose alone and 2 mM sucrose mixed with various concentrations of ARCH (Fig. 3A). In the absence of ARCH, flies showed a neutral response. However, when ARCH was present, flies displayed an aversion irrespective of the concentration tested (10−5%-10−1%), confirming that ARCH acts as an antifeedant compound. To further dissect the cellular mechanisms for this aversive response, we used genetically modified flies expressing Kir2.1, a tool to specifically inhibit bitter-sensing GRNs. We found that blocking these GRNs significantly reduced the flies' avoidance of ARCH; in contrast, the inhibition of other GRN types did not affect their feeding behavior (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we observed changes in feeding behavior in flies with loss-of-function mutations in specific Ir genes, including Ir7g, Ir25a, Ir51b, Ir67a, and Ir76b, during the food choice assay (Fig. 3C). These mutants showed reduced avoidance or even subtle attraction to ARCH, further emphasizing the importance of these IRs in mediating ARCH aversion.

To validate the behavioral defects observed, we utilized RNAi driven by the Gr33a-GAL4 and ppk23-GAL4 systems (Fig. 3D), as we tested them in electrophysiology (Fig. 2D). Consistent with our electrophysiological findings, RNAi knockdown of candidate genes in the bitter-sensing GRNs (Gr33a-GAL4) revealed that these receptors are primarily required for the aversion to ARCH, while pheromone/calcium-sensing GRNs (ppk23-GAL4) are not essential (Fig. 3D). Recovery experiments using the GAL4/UAS system provided additional evidence for the role of the targeted receptors in feeding behavior (Fig. 3E). Genetic rescue experiments using the appropriate UAS constructs under their respective GAL4 drivers confirmed that the deficits observed in the mutants could be fully recovered (Fig. 3E), further validating the key role of these receptors in mediating the behavioral response to ARCH. Together, the behavioral and physiological data presented here reinforce each other, providing strong evidence that specific IRs are essential for the detection of ARCH and the regulation of feeding behavior.

IR7g, IR25a, IR51b, IR67a, and IR76b Are Necessary but Not Sufficient to Elicit ARCH-Induced Responses

The data presented in Fig. 2, Fig. 3 suggest that ARCH-responsive IRs function as ligand-gated ion channels. To test whether the identified IRs are sufficient to confer sensitivity to ARCH, we employed ectopic expression using the GAL4/UAS system in Drosophila. As a first step, we performed RT-PCR analyses to assess the expression patterns of Ir7g, Ir51b, and Ir67a transcripts in a wild-type background (Fig. S6A-C). To target distinct GRN populations, we used two GAL4 drivers: Gr5a-GAL4, which labels sweet-sensing neurons located in L-type sensilla (eg, L4 and L6) (Fig. 4A), and Gr33a-GAL4, which labels bitter-sensing neurons in I-type sensilla (eg, I8 and I9) (Fig. 4B). Following ectopic expression of the IRs in these neurons, we performed tip-recording assays to assess neuronal responses to ARCH. We observed that none of the modified flies exhibited detectable responses to ARCH in either L-type or I-type sensilla, as compared with wild-type controls. These results indicate that while the candidate IRs are necessary for ARCH detection in their native context—namely, in S-type sensilla—they are not sufficient to confer sensitivity when expressed in nonresponsive GRNs.

This suggests that additional molecular components—such as other unidentified IRs or specific cellular factors—may be required to form functional receptor complexes capable of mediating ARCH detection. Given that only a subset of S-type sensilla responds to ARCH, our findings support the conclusion that these IRs are necessary but not sufficient, and that ARCH sensing likely relies on a multimeric receptor complex or context-dependent mechanism.

IR67a Is Specifically Required for Sensing ARCH, but Not Other Bitter Compounds

The role of IR67a in sensing bitter compounds was previously unknown (Aryal et al., 2022a, Dhakal et al., 2021, Sang et al., 2024, Shrestha et al., 2024, page number). Notably, we previously reported that IR7g and IR51b are not required for detecting bitter compounds such as caffeine, quinine, strychnine, denatonium, lobeline, chloroquine, berberine, papaverine, coumarin, and umbelliferone (Pradhan et al., 2024). To investigate whether IR67a is involved in detecting other aversive substances besides ARCH, we conducted electrophysiological recordings with a variety of bitter compounds, including berberine, caffeine, chloroquine, coumarin, denatonium, lobeline, papaverine, quinine, strychnine, and umbelliferone (Fig. 5). Our findings showed that Ir67a mutant flies displayed normal neuronal responses to all tested bitter compounds, similar to control flies, with 1 notable exception—ARCH (Fig. 5). This suggests that IR67a is not broadly required for general bitter taste perception but is specifically critical for sensing ARCH. ARCH Inhibits Sugar Responses

Bitter compounds can directly activate bitter-sensing GRNs and simultaneously inhibit sugar-sensing GRNs (French et al., 2015). Bitter compounds can also affect sugar perception through 2 known pathways. There are 2 primary mechanisms involved in bitter avoidance. One involves the binding of bitter compounds to an odorant-binding protein OBP49a in the endolymph, which inhibits sugar-activated GRs (Jeong et al., 2013), and the other entails the stimulation of bitter GRNs, which then activate GABAergic interneurons (Chu et al., 2014). These interneurons subsequently inhibit the activity of sweet GRNs.

Our findings emphasize the critical role of bitter-sensing GRNs in detecting ARCH, as inactivation of these neurons significantly reduced avoidance behavior (Fig. 3B), consistent with their involvement (Fig. 1D). To assess whether ARCH can also inhibit sugar responses, we performed tip recordings from the L4 and L6 sensilla, which are devoid of bitter-sensing GRNs. When stimulated with 100 mM sucrose, control flies exhibited robust neuronal responses, with 64.33 ± 6.21 and 65.50 ± 4.35 spikes/s in L4 and L6, respectively (Fig. 6). However, co-application of 10−3% ARCH significantly reduced sugar-evoked responses to 13.45 ± 2.87 (L4) and 20.66 ± 5.31 (L6) spikes/s. This inhibition was absent in Obp49a1 mutants, which failed to show a significant reduction in sugar responses in the presence of ARCH (Fig. 6). Although Obp49a1 mutants displayed slightly reduced sucrose responses compared with controls, the difference was not statistically significant. DISCUSSION

Fatty acid perception plays a critical role in shaping dietary preferences, metabolic health, and ecological interactions. ARCH, a fatty acid found in vegetable oils such as peanut and perilla oil, is involved in lipid metabolism and cellular structure. However, excessive intake of ARCH poses health risks. In humans, high levels of saturated fats, including ARCH, are linked to elevated cholesterol, metabolic disorders, and cardiovascular complications. Our study on D. melanogaster similarly demonstrated increased lethality at high ARCH doses, reinforcing its toxic potential when consumed in excess.

We found that ARCH stimulates bitter-sensing GRNs, triggering aversion in flies. Ablation of other GRN types (sugar-, water-, and calcium/pheromone-sensing) did not affect ARCH avoidance, highlighting the importance of bitter GRNs in detecting this fatty acid. Through electrophysiological and behavioral assays, we identified 5 essential IRs required for ARCH detection: IR7g, IR25a, IR51b, IR67a, and IR76b. Previous studies have shown that IR25a and IR76b are co-expressed in bitter-sensing GRNs and are broadly expressed across multiple GRN subtypes, including those involved in bitter, sweet, and salty taste detection (Lee et al., 2018). Their widespread expression has been functionally validated in several studies, supporting their presence and co-receptor roles in bitter GRNs. In contrast, for IR7g and IR51b, direct evidence of expression through enhancer-GAL4 lines or FISH-based localization is currently lacking. However, our previous work (Dhakal et al., 2021, Pradhan et al., 2024) and this work demonstrated their expression in bitter GRNs through RT-PCR analyses using wild-type labella and labella in which bitter-sensing GRNs were selectively ablated. These transcriptional findings are supported by functional assays, where loss- or gain-of-function manipulations of IR7g and IR51b led to significant changes in bitter responses, indicating their physiological relevance in bitter GRNs. Our previous work demonstrated that IR7g is essential for detecting compounds such as cantharidin and cholesterol (Pradhan et al., 2024, Pradhan et al., 2025). Importantly, its function does not appear restricted to a narrow ligand spectrum but instead contributes to the tuning of multiple sensilla types, a feature often associated with co-receptor activity. Similarly, IR51b mediates the detection of a diverse set of structurally distinct compounds, including aversive amino acids, nitrogenous waste products, and acidic or basic tastants as well as cantharidin and cholesterol (Aryal et al., 2022a, Dhakal et al., 2021, Pradhan et al., 2024, Shrestha et al., 2024, Pradhan et al., 2025, page number). Its requirement across multiple stimulus classes suggests that IR51b does not act within a single narrowly tuned pathway but instead provides broader support for sensory transduction. Taken together, these findings support a model in which IR7g and IR51b function as context-dependent co-receptors or relatively broadly tuned receptors, expanding the chemical repertoire of specific sensilla rather than serving as universal partners like IR25a and IR76b. While higher-resolution techniques such as FISH or immunohistochemistry would provide more definitive cell-specific localization, the convergence of transcriptional and functional data provides reasonable confidence in their presence within these neurons. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the current study and highlight it as an important direction for future investigation.

Regarding IR67a, to our knowledge, this is the first report implicating it in chemosensory function. Although its role in taste had not been previously established, we confirmed its functional involvement in aversive responses through both RNAi knockdown and specific GAL4/UAS rescue experiments in the bitter-sensing GRNs. These experiments demonstrate its relevance in chemosensation. However, like IR7g and IR51b, definitive evidence of IR67a's co-expression in bitter GRNs will require further validation. Addressing this gap will be essential for fully elucidating the molecular logic of bitter GRNs.

These findings indicate that the perception of ARCH in Drosophila relies on the coordinated activity of these specific IRs, forming a minimal molecular framework for its detection. Despite extensive screening, we were unable to replicate ARCH sensing in neurons unresponsive to it, suggesting that additional receptors or heteromultimeric complexes may be involved.

Comparisons with previous studies on other fatty acids further underscore the complexity of fatty acid perception. For example, low concentrations of hexanoic acid induce attraction via sweet-sensing GRNs, while high concentrations trigger aversion through bitter GRNs (Pradhan et al., 2023a, Dey et al., 2023). Additionally, IR56d has been implicated in sensing MCFAs (Ahn et al., 2017, Brown et al., 2021). Our findings on ARCH align with this broader role of IRs in mediating fatty acid detection.

In conclusion, the ability to detect and avoid harmful concentrations of ARCH is crucial for survival, serving as both an antifeedant and a toxic deterrent in Drosophila. This sensory adaptation likely evolved to protect organisms from dietary hazards. The ecological and evolutionary implications of these findings extend beyond insects, given the widespread occurrence of fatty acids in natural and human-altered environments. Understanding the molecular basis of fatty acid sensing not only advances chemosensory biology but also offers insights into dietary regulation and metabolic health, underscoring the need for further research into these vital sensory mechanisms.
