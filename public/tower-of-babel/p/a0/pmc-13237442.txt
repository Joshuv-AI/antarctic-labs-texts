Outcomes of PTCY-Based Haploidentical Transplants and Donor Factors Affecting Outcomes: A 12-year Experience from India
Authors: Nishant Jindal, Anant Gokarn, Malini Garg, Komal Kumbhalwar, Selma D Silva, Meenakshi Singh, Sadhana Kannan, Libin Jacob Mathew, Sumeet Mirgh, Akanksha Chichra, Lingaraj Nayak, Avinash Bonda, Sachin Punatar, Bhausaheb Bagal, Navin Khattry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOutcomes of haploidentical transplantation have been reported to be comparable to HLA-matched transplants in several studies; however, data from low- and middle-income countries remain limited. In this retrospective study, we evaluated outcomes of 71 patients who underwent haploidentical transplantation for malignant indications using post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) between July 2010 and March 2022 and analyzed donor factors influencing outcomes. All patients received peripheral blood stem cell grafts with standard PTCy/calcineurin inhibitor/mycophenolate mofetil prophylaxis. Graft failure occurred in seven patients (9.8%), with a higher incidence observed in those with 0-1 mismatch in the graft versus host direction (p=0.011). The incidences of grade II-IV acute graft versus host disease (GVHD) and chronic GVHD were 42.3% and 47.7%, respectively. Overall transplant related mortality (TRM) was 42.3%. Donor killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptor haplotype BX with 2DS2 and the Bw6Bw6 ligand in recipients were associated with a reduced risk of relapse. The 5-year overall survival was 31.9%. On multivariate analysis, female donors were associated with improved overall survival (hazard ratio 0.465 [95% confidence interval 0.222-0.974], p=0.042). Transplant outcomes were primarily limited by a high incidence of TRM. These findings warrant validation in larger multicenter studies.Keywords: PTCY, Haploidentical Transplants, Outcomes, Donor factors

Introduction

Allogeneic transplant remains the only curative option for several hematological malignancies. A significant proportion of eligible patients do not have an HLA matched donor. Haploidentical transplant offers an alternative as almost all patients will have one or more potential haploidentical donors. Several strategies have been employed to overcome the HLA barrier associated with haploidentical transplants including ex vivo T cell depletion, anti-thymocyte globulin and post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy). In vivo T cell depletion using PTCy has emerged as one of the most viable platforms to perform haploidentical transplants1. It is safe, feasible and relatively inexpensive. There has been an exponential rise in the number of haploidentical transplants being done using PTCy across the world2,3. A similar rise has been seen in India (ISBMT unpublished data). The outcomes of haploidentical transplants in hematological malignancies have been reported to be comparable to HLA matched transplants in several studies4-6.

Haploidentical transplant is associated with delayed engraftment, a higher incidence of poor graft function and impaired immune reconstitution resulting in a higher incidence of infectious complications. This makes haploidentical transplants more challenging, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where the incidence of carbapenem resistant blood stream infections is higher (State of the world's antibiotics 2021: A global analysis of antimicrobial resistance and its drivers) and newer antibacterial drugs are not available7. Antiviral drugs such as foscarnet and letermovir are expensive and not easily available in these countries. Cellular therapy for viral infections is still in its infancy and is not available for clinical use. Another concern is the use of non-leukodepleted blood products in non-academic centers which may lead to a higher incidence of donor specific anti-HLA antibodies8,9. The increased cost of supportive care required for managing post-transplant complications makes haploidentical transplant far more expensive than HLA-matched transplants. These challenges are unique to LMICs and can lead to inferior outcomes. There is limited literature on outcomes from LMICs for hematological malignancies10-12. In this retrospective study, we report the outcomes of patients who underwent haploidentical transplant at a single center in India and analyze the donor factors affecting outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Patients

This is a single center retrospective study that included patients who underwent haploidentical transplants for malignant conditions between 1 July 2010 and 31 March 2022. Patients with high risk or relapsed acute leukemia, chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) with T315I mutation or failure of second generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors, relapsed refractory Hodgkin's lymphoma and high-risk myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) were considered eligible for haploidentical transplant. For patients who underwent transplant before February 2019, low resolution HLA typing was done. From March 2019 onwards, high resolution HLA typing was performed for all patients and donors. In patients who had undergone transplant before 2019, high resolution HLA typing was performed retrospectively. Killer-cell immunoglobulin-like Receptor (KIR) typing was available for 66 (93%) patient-donor pairs. In patients who underwent transplant before July 2020, KIR typing was performed retrospectively. All patients provided an informed consent and the study was approved by the Institutional Ethics committee (IEC III) of Tata Memorial Centre, Mumbai (IRB no. 901210).

Donor selection

Donor selection was based on age, gender, kinship, and fitness. Younger donors were preferred. Male donors were preferred over females for male patients. Siblings and children were preferred over parents as donors. The total number of HLA mismatches and KIR typing were not considered while choosing donors.

Transplant procedure

Allogeneic transplant was performed according to standard guidelines and institutional policies. All patients were admitted in HEPA-filtered single rooms for the duration of their hospital stay. Standard infection control protocols were followed. G-CSF mobilized peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC) graft was used in all but 2 patients for whom bone marrow grafts were used. The conditioning regimen was based on an assessment of disease risk, organ function, and comorbidity index. graft versus host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis included PTCy 50 mg/kg on days 3 and 4, calcineurin inhibitors from day 5 onwards, and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) from day 5 to day 35. All patients received antifungal prophylaxis with posaconazole until day 100 post-transplant or until discontinuation of immunosuppression, whichever occurred later. Posaconazole trough levels were monitored weekly, with a target concentration of > 0.7 mg/L. Antiviral prophylaxis with acyclovir was initiated at the start of conditioning and continued for up to one-year post-transplant or until discontinuation of immunosuppression, whichever occurred later. Pneumocystis prophylaxis with cotrimoxazole was commenced after neutrophil engraftment and continued for one-year post-transplant or until cessation of immunosuppression, whichever occurred later. Routine antibacterial prophylaxis was not used. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) was monitored weekly after start of conditioning and twice a week in patients with CMV copies > 150 copies/mL in peripheral blood. A pre-emptive approach was used for management of CMV reactivation post-transplant. No patient received letermovir prophylaxis as the drug was not available during the study period. Cyclosporine taper was started on day 60 in patients with high-risk disease and on day 90 in patients with standard risk disease. In patients with acute leukemia, bone marrow was performed on day 30 in those with pre-transplant residual disease and on day 90 and at 1 year post transplant in all patients. Real-time polymerase chain reaction PCR for BCR-ABL was monitored monthly in peripheral blood in patients with CML and Ph-positive acute leukemia. In patients with Hodgkin's lymphoma, PET-CT was done at 3 months and 1 year post transplant.

Definitions

Neutrophil engraftment was defined as absolute neutrophil count > 500/mcL for more than 3 days and platelet engraftment by platelet count > 20,000/mcL for more than 7 days in the absence of platelet transfusions. Acute GVHD and chronic GVHD were graded according to modified Glucksberg and NIH criteria, respectively. CMV reactivation was defined as > 1,000 copies/mL in the peripheral blood. Progression free survival (PFS) was defined as time from transplant to progression/relapse or death from any cause. Overall survival (OS) was defined as time from transplant to death due to any cause.

Prognostic factors

Donor factors were analyzed for potential impact on graft failure, acute and chronic GVHD, cumulative incidence of relapse and survival. These included age of the donor, gender, kinship, HLA mismatches in host versus graft (HvG)/graft versus host (GvH) direction, DRB1 mismatch, ligand-ligand/receptor-ligand mismatch (L-L, R-L mismatch), and donor KIR haplotype. Patient KIR ligand was also evaluated for a potential impact on outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables and proportions were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test and the chi-square or Fisher's exact test, respectively. Survival was computed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival between groups was compared using the log-rank test. For outcome analyses, CD3+ cell dose was dichotomized using the cohort median value as the cut-off. Similarly, other continuous scale variables were categorized based on their respective median values. Cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) was computed with transplant-related mortality (TRM) as competing risk and was compared using the Fine-Gray model. Variables were included for multivariate analysis if p < 0.10 on univariate analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analysis was performed using SPSS software version 23.

Results

Seventy-one patients were included. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Median age was 24 years (2-54 years). Seventy three percent were males. Acute myeloid leukemia was the most common indication for transplant (40.8%) followed by (acute lymphoblastic leukemia [ALL] [29.6%]). CML (10), Hodgkin Lymphoma (8) and MDS (3) were the other indications for transplant. Fifteen (21.1%) patients had active disease at the time of transplant. Thirty-six percent of patients had high or very high disease risk index. Donor was a parent in 30 patients (42.2%), sibling in 29 patients (40.9%), and offspring in 12 patients (16.9%). Median donor age was 34 years (8-60 years). Ten patients (14%) were positive for donor specific antibodies and none underwent desensitisation. Nearly 90% patient-donor pairs were CMV sero-positive. All but 2 patients received reduced intensity regimens. Conditioning regimens used were Fludarabine (Flu)-Treosulfan (30 mg/m2 to 42 mg/m2) with or without 2 Gy TBI (n=24), Flu-Treosulfan-Thiotepa (n=1), Flu-Melphalan140 (n=22), Flu-TBI (7.2 Gy/8 Gy) (n=18), Flu-TBI (4 Gy) (n=1), Flu-TBI (12Gy) (n=1) and Flu-Busulfan (6.4-9.6 mg/kg IV Busulfan) (n=4). The median CD34 dose infused was 5.35 × 106 CD34 cells/kg patient body weight and CD3 dose was 143.90 × 106 CD3 cells/kg patient weight.

Engraftment and graft failure

Sixty-five patients (91.5%) achieved donor engraftment. Median time to neutrophil engraftment was 18 days (10-57 days) and to platelet engraftment was 17 days (8-170 days). Seven patients had graft failure, 6 patients had primary failure, and 1 developed secondary graft failure after initial engraftment. Of the patients who successfully achieved donor engraftment, two had chimerism levels below 95% on day 28 post-transplant (90% and 39%). The remaining patients all had chimerism levels greater than 95% at day 28. The impact of number and vector of HLA mismatch at HLA A, B, C, DRB1, DQB1 loci on engraftment and graft failure was analyzed. There was a higher risk of failure in transplants with 0-1 mismatches in GvH direction (HLA mismatch directionality favoring HvG response). There were 6 patients with 0-1 mismatches in the GvH direction and 2-5 mismatches in the HvG direction. Of these 6 patients, 3 had graft failure (50%). The risk was significantly higher as compared to those with 2 or more mismatches in the GvH direction (4/65 i.e. 6.2%) (p=0.011). In the entire cohort, there were 2 patients who were homozygous at all 5 loci (0 mismatches in the GvH direction and 5 mismatches in the HvG direction). Of these, 1 had failure and the other patient had early progression with loss of chimerism. There was a higher incidence of failure in patients who received a lower CD3 dose. Among the 36 patients who received < 143.9 × 106 CD 3 cells/kg, 6 had failure (16.7%) as compared to 2.9% in the 35 patients who received a higher CD3 dose (p=0.107). Zero-1 HLA mismatches in the GvH direction (with 2-5 mismatches in the HvG direction) remained a significant factor for graft failure on multivariate analysis (hazard ratio (HR) 27.732 [95% confidence interval (CI) 2.437-315.585], p=0.007).

Acute GVHD and CMV reactivation

The cumulative incidence of grade II-IV acute GVHD was 42.3% and that of grade III-IV GVHD was 19.7%. Eleven patients (15.5%) had steroid refractory acute GVHD. There was no difference in the incidence of clinically significant acute GVHD based on age or gender of the donor, female to male transplant, kinship, or the number of HLA mismatches in the GvH direction. CMV reactivation (> 1,000 copies/mL) was seen in 50/71 patients (70.4%).

Chronic GVHD

The incidence of chronic GVHD was assessed in the 44 patients who survived for more than 100 days post-transplant and had achieved donor chimerism. Chronic GVHD was seen in 21/44 patients (47.7%) and was extensive in 14/44 (31.8%) of patients. It was significantly higher in patients who received a higher CD3 dose. Of the 19 patients who received < 143.9 × 106 CD3 cells/kg, chronic GVHD was seen in 21.1% of patients as compared to 68% in the 25 patients who received more than 143.9 × 106 CD3 cells/kg (p=0.002).

Transplant related mortality

Twenty-five patients died within 4 months of transplant and 23 of these deaths were due to TRM (32.3%). Seven (30%) of these were prior to engraftment. Fifteen of the 23 patients (65%) had acute grade II-IV GVHD. Among the remaining 8 patients, sepsis was the cause of death in 5, central nervous system toxoplasmosis in 1, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder in 1 patient. Eighteen patients had sepsis as the immediate cause of death with a majority of these being gram-negative bacterial sepsis. Three patients succumbed to a probable fungal pneumonia and three had disseminated adenovirus infection leading to mortality. Another patient had slippage of chimerism for which donor lymphocyte infusion was given, subsequently developed GVHD, and died of sepsis. Overall TRM in the cohort at the time of analysis was 42.3%. There was a trend towards lower TRM with female donors (27.3% vs. 49%, p=0.087) and this benefit was irrespective of the kinship. Patients with acute GVHD had a significantly higher risk of TRM (58.3% vs. 25.7%, p=0.005). There was no difference in the incidence of TRM between patients who underwent transplant from 2010 to 2015 (n=28) and 2016 to 2022 (n=43) (p=0.16).

Relapse

At two years, CIR was 22.14%. The CIR was higher with donor AA haplotype (44.2%) (SHR 3.33 [95% CI 0.96-11.4], p=0.05) and BX without 2DS2 (33.3%) (SHR 2.94 [95% CI 0.94-9.2], p=0.06) as compared to BX with 2DS2 (14.7%) (Figure 1A). The risk of relapse was lower in patients with Bw6Bw6 ligands (12.2%) (SHR 0.19 [95% CI 0.03-1.00], p=0.050) and Bw4Bw6 ligand (25.9%) (SHR 0.60 [95% CI, 0.20-1.8] p=0.37) as compared to those with Bw4Bw4 KIR ligand (42%) (Figure 1B). On multivariate analysis, Bw6Bw6 KIR ligand in patients was associated with a lower risk of relapse (SHR 0.2 [95% CI 0.04-0.96], p=0.045). No other donor factors including age, KIR Receptor-Ligand, KIR Ligand-Ligand, or DRB1 mismatch in the GvH direction were found to be associated with the risk of relapse.

Discussion

The ease and feasibility of PTCy have led to a rise in the number of haploidentical transplants being performed across the world. The outcomes of haploidentical transplant using PTCy have been reported to be comparable to those of HLA-matched transplant in several studies from multiple centers from the west4-6,13. There is limited literature from LMICs10-12. Despite acceptable rates of GVHD, the risk of infections due to delayed engraftment and poor immune reconstitution is a major concern. In this retrospective analysis we report the outcomes of 71 patients who underwent haploidentical transplant at our center from 1 July 2010 to 31 March 2022 at a tertiary cancer institute in India.

In our cohort, the risk of graft failure was 9.8%, which is similar to what has been reported from other centers14. The risk of failure was significantly higher in patients with 0-1 mismatches in the GvH direction (50% vs. 6.2%). Our cohort included 2 patients who were homozygous at all 5 loci (0 mismatches in the GvH direction and 5 mismatches in the HvG direction). Of these 2, 1 had failure and 1 had early progression with rapid fall in chimerism. In these patients (0-1 mismatches in the GvH direction), there are a higher number of mismatches in the HvG direction as compared to the GvH direction possibly resulting in a higher risk of failure. Homozygosity at mismatched loci in patients has been found to be associated with higher risk of failure in patients undergoing 8/10 or 9/10 mismatched unrelated donor transplants15,16. This situation is rare in haploidentical transplants as it is very rare to have homozygosity at all 5 loci. In Japan, the incidence of homozygosity is higher due to preserved haplotypes. In a series from Japan, the authors did not find a higher incidence of failure in patients with HLA homozygosity who received stem cells from heterozygous donors17. Despite HLA haploidentical transplant, these patients received standard CNI with methotrexate as GVHD prophylaxis, as the risk of GVHD was deemed low due to absence of any mismatches in the GvH direction. The notable difference between our patients and the Japanese cohort is the use of PTCy in our patients. PTCy depletes donor alloreactive T cells, and this may further increase the risk of failure in patients where the risk is already high due to unopposed HLA mismatches in the HvG direction.

The incidence of grade II-IV acute GVHD was 42.3% and that of grade III-IV GVHD was 19.7%. The incidence of acute GVHD was higher than what has been reported in most studies. An important factor contributing to the high incidence of acute GVHD may be the use of PBSC graft in nearly all patients (69/71). In a retrospective analysis of nearly 700 patients, Bashey et al. reported a higher incidence of acute GVHD in patients receiving PBSC graft. The incidence of grade II-IV acute GVHD was 44% with PBSC graft, which is similar to our cohort18. Similar results were reported from EBMT analysis where the risk of grade II-IV and grade III-IV acute GVHD with PBSC graft was 38% and 14%, respectively, and was significantly higher than BM graft19. Extensive chronic GVHD was seen at 47.7%, similar to what has been observed in other studies4,19. Chronic GVHD rates were higher in patients who received a higher CD3 dose. There has been conflicting literature on the impact of CD3 cell dose in patients undergoing haploidentical transplant using PTCy. Mussetti reported a higher incidence of all-grade chronic GVHD in patients receiving a higher CD3 dose. This difference was seen only with PBSC graft20. A recent CIBMTR analysis did not find any difference in chronic GVHD based on the infused CD3 dose21.

In our cohort, we found a very high risk of early TRM (32.3% at 120 days post-transplant). This is higher than what has been reported from most centers (10-20% at 12 months)4,6,14. A metanalysis showed higher incidence of non-relapse mortality with haploidentical transplant as compared to HLA-matched transplant (HR 1.36 [95% CI 1.12-1.66])13. A similarly high TRM has been reported from another large center from India where the authors reported 45% non-relapse mortality at 100 days post-transplant10. The high TRM in our setting is mostly related to GVHD and infections. Sixty-five percent of the patients with early TRM had acute GVHD and nearly 40% had steroid refractory GVHD. Use of steroids and immunosuppression results in increased risk of viral, bacterial, and fungal infections which ultimately results in high risk of mortality in these patients. Early TRM remains a major challenge in haploidentical transplants, especially in resource-limited settings. Infectious complications are a major contributor to early mortality, with a higher incidence of carbapenem-resistant organisms. Access to newer antibacterial and antiviral agents is often restricted by cost or availability, while cellular therapy for refractory viral reactivation is not yet in clinical use. These challenges are further compounded by the high cost of supportive care. Strategies aimed at improving immune reconstitution and preventing viral reactivations are required to improve outcomes. Use of CD45 RA-positive T cell depleted prophylactic donor lymphocyte infusions and anti-CMV prophylaxis are two such strategies which are currently evolving.

The risk of relapse was lowest in patients who received graft from donors with BX with 2DS2 KIR haplotype as compared to BX without 2DS2 and AA haplotype. This finding has been reported in other series also. In a multicenter study, the use of donors with KIR B haplotypes was associated with improved relapse-free and overall survival in T cell replete unrelated transplants22. Similar results were reported by Oevermann et al. in children with ALL who underwent ex vivo T cell depleted haploidentical transplantation23. Activating NK cell haplotypes can reduce the risk of relapse by NK cell-mediated graft versus leukemia effect without increasing the risk of GVHD. We also found a lower risk of relapse in patients with Bw6Bw6 KIR ligands. This finding is intriguing, because of the differential interaction of HLA-Bw4 and HLA-Bw6 alleles with KIR receptors expressed on NK cells. Only the HLA-B alleles that carry the HLA-Bw4 epitope protect target cells from NK cell attack and leukemic cells with Bw6Bw6 may be more susceptible to NK cell-mediated cell kill24. There was no difference in the risk of relapse based on ligand-ligand or receptor-ligand mismatch. These findings appear exploratory given the limited sample size and borderline p-values and need be confirmed in a larger multi-centre dataset.

Female donors were associated with improved OS, irrespective of recipient gender or donor kinship. Outcomes were superior with mothers compared to fathers, and with sisters compared to brothers, largely due to a lower incidence of TRM in female donor transplants (27.3% vs. 49.0%, p=0.087). Previous studies have reported reduced GVHD and improved survival with maternal donors, likely attributable to fetal-maternal tolerance; however, these studies were not based on the PTCy platform25-27. To date, there are no reports demonstrating superior outcomes with female donors regardless of kinship in PTCy-based haploidentical transplantation. This finding from our study therefore requires validation in larger cohorts.

The interpretation of this study is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size. The association between 0-1 mismatches in the GvH direction and graft failure is based on a very small subgroup (n=6). Additionally, the heterogeneity of the study population, including patients with varied diagnoses and disease status at the time of transplant, may have influenced the analysis of donor-related factors affecting transplant outcomes.

Conclusion

Transplant outcomes in LMICs are limited by a high incidence of early TRM. Fewer mismatches in the GvH direction (0-1) leading to HLA mismatch directionality favoring HvG response may predispose to failure in a haploidentical setting. Donor KIR haplotype BX with 2DS2and Bw6Bw6 ligand in the patient are associated with a lower risk of relapse. Female donors were associated with a lower TRM and superior outcomes. Findings of this study need to be validated in a larger multicenter study.
