Comparison of mesh and non-mesh repair in adolescent inguinal hernia surgery using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database: a nationwide retrospective cohort study
Authors: Min Seung Kim, So Ra Yoon, Ho Jong Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study was performed to analyze the long-term outcomes of inguinal hernia repair in adolescent patients and assess the effectiveness of using mesh with data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database.MethodsUsing the NHIS claims data between 2005 and 2023, 15,808 adolescents aged 10–19 years who underwent inguinal hernia surgery were identified. Patients were categorized by mesh use, and postoperative outcomes were analyzed using statistical and Cox regression models.ResultsAmong 15,808 patients, 810 (5.1%) underwent mesh repair, whereas 14,998 (94.9%) did not. Mesh use increased significantly with age and was more frequent in males. There were no significant differences in readmission or reoperation rates after 1 year. However, mesh repair showed higher readmission (4.32% vs. 3.01%, P = 0.03) and reoperation (3.95% vs. 2.69%, P = 0.03) rates after 5 years. Older age was an independent risk factor for both outcomes. Recurrence analysis revealed no significant differences between the 2 techniques.ConclusionNHIS data showed no clear long-term recurrence benefit of mesh repair for adolescent inguinal hernia. Given data limitations and potential selection bias, routine mesh use is not recommended, and individualized decision-making is advised. Further prospective studies are needed, particularly for older adolescents.

INTRODUCTION

Inguinal hernia is a common condition encountered in general surgical practice worldwide, and surgical repair is the standard treatment. The HerniaSurge Group has developed international guidelines for managing inguinal hernias, which recommend the preferential use of mesh, regardless of the use of an open or laparoscopic approach [12]. However, these guidelines are primarily intended for use in adults. Conversely, pediatric surgeons generally conduct high ligation to repair indirect inguinal hernias in children.

Adolescence is a transitional period of physical development between childhood and adulthood. Owing to the unique physiological characteristics of this developmental stage, determining the most appropriate surgical approach, whether pediatric or adult, for adolescents with inguinal hernias remains challenging. Treatment decisions often vary depending on whether the operating surgeon specializes in general or pediatric surgery [3]. Ongoing efforts are aimed at better understanding these clinical trends and establishing treatment recommendations for adolescent inguinal hernias; however, no definitive consensus has been reached [4].

The aim of this study is to analyze the long-term outcomes of inguinal hernia repair in adolescent patients and assess the effectiveness of using mesh with data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database.

METHODS

Data source

A nationwide claims database provided by the NHIS in South Korea was used. This database encompasses comprehensive Korean Health Insurance and Medical Aid program information. This includes patient demographic data and diagnostic and procedural codes.

The Institutional Review Board of the National Health Insurance Service of Ilsan Hospital (No. 2024-03-003) approved this study, which was conducted using the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Study design

Data from the NHIS claims database between 2002 and 2023 were analyzed. Overall, 648,254 patients who underwent inguinal hernia surgery were identified using the procedure codes Q2753, Q2754, Q2755, and Q2756. To establish a 5-year follow-up period, 394,112 patients who underwent surgery between 2005 and 2018 were selected. From this cohort, 15,808 patients aged between 10 and 19 years at the time of surgery, characterized as adolescents according to World Health Organization criteria, were included in the final analysis.

The data extracted included patient characteristics such as age at surgery, sex, income quintile, place of residence, laterality of the hernia (unilateral or bilateral), and whether a surgical mesh was used, as identified by mesh material codes (Supplementary Table 1). Postoperative outcomes assessed included hernia recurrence and hospital readmission. Readmission patients were those who underwent primary inguinal hernia surgery and were readmitted with disease codes (K4090, K4091, K4020, K4021, K4000, K4001, K4010, K4011, K4030, K4031, K4040, and K4041). Reoperation patients were readmission patients who underwent surgery with procedure codes (Q2753, Q2754, Q2755, Q2756, QA753, QA754, QA755, and QA756).

Statistical analysis

Patients were classified into 2 surgical groups based on the use of mesh during inguinal hernia surgery: mesh and non-mesh groups. Postoperative outcomes were compared between the 2 groups.

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square and Fisher exact tests, while continuous variables were compared using the independent Student t-tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for readmission and reoperation at 1 and 5 years postoperatively. Mesh use (yes vs. no) was the main exposure of interest. For the multivariable analyses, covariates were selected a priori based on predetermined clinical relevance and data availability within the NHIS claims database. Accordingly, age at surgery (continuous), sex, income quintile, residential area, and laterality of hernia repair (unilateral vs. bilateral) were considered clinically meaningful variables and were included as predefined covariates in all multivariable models, regardless of their statistical significance in the univariable analyses. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using tests based on Schoenfeld residuals, and no meaningful violations of the assumption were detected. The Cochran-Armitage trend test was used to evaluate trends in the proportion of mesh use across 1-year age intervals. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The SAS software (ver. 9.4, SAS Institute) was used to conduct all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Overall, 15,808 patients underwent inguinal hernia repair, of whom 810 (5.12%) underwent mesh repair, and 14,998 (94.88%) underwent non-mesh repair. Table 1 shows a comparison of patient characteristics based on mesh usage. Most patients were male (79.54%), and mesh repair was conducted more frequently in males than in females (5.60% vs. 3.28%, P < 0.01). The mean age at surgery was 13.63 ± 3.11 years, with a significant difference between the mesh and non-mesh groups (17.21 ± 1.84 years vs. 13.44 ± 3.05 years, P < 0.01).

When analyzed at 1-year age intervals from 10 to 19 years, the proportion of mesh use progressively increased with age, a trend confirmed by the Cochran-Armitage test (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1). Patients in the fifth income quintile had the largest proportion of all surgeries (42.21%), whereas those in the first income quintile had the highest rate of mesh use (11.32%; P < 0.01). No significant differences in mesh use were observed according to the residential area (P = 0.74). Unilateral repair was far more common than bilateral repair (97.66% vs. 2.34%); however, mesh repair was significantly more frequent in bilateral cases (11.35% vs. 4.97%, P < 0.01).

Table 2 shows readmission and reoperation rates at 1 and 5 years postoperatively based on mesh usage. No significant differences were observed at 1 year (readmission, P = 0.36; reoperation, P = 0.83). However, the mesh group showed significantly higher readmission (4.32% vs. 3.01%, P = 0.03) and reoperation (3.95% vs. 2.69%, P = 0.03) rates at 5 years than at 1 year.

Patients were further classified based on the timing of readmission after inguinal hernia repair, and the associated factors were analyzed (Table 3). For readmissions within 1 year, univariate Cox regression identified older age as a significant risk factor (HR, 1.06; P = 0.01), with no significant associations observed in the multivariate analysis. For readmissions within 5 years, univariate analysis showed that mesh use (HR, 1.44; P = 0.04) and older age (HR, 1.08; P < 0.01) were significant risk factors, whereas female sex was associated with a reduced risk (HR, 0.75; P = 0.02). In the multivariate model, only older age remained statistically significant (HR, 1.074; P < 0.01).

Table 4 shows the results of the Cox regression analysis for reoperation. For reoperation within 1 year, the univariate analysis identified older age as a significant risk factor (HR, 1.06; P = 0.03), with no significant associations observed in the multivariate analysis. Univariate analysis identified mesh use (HR, 1.48; P = 0.03) and older age (HR, 1.07; P < 0.01) as significant risk factors for reoperation within 5 years, while female sex was a protective factor (HR, 0.73; P = 0.02). In multivariate analysis, older age (HR, 1.07; P < 0.01) remained a significant risk factor.

Table 5 shows the results of the reanalysis using specific diagnostic and procedural codes to identify cases of recurrent inguinal hernia, thereby correcting for potential confounding factors from other causes of readmission and reoperation. The codes utilized in this reanalysis included K4091 (unilateral recurrent inguinal hernia), K4021 (bilateral recurrent inguinal hernia), K4001 (bilateral recurrent inguinal hernia with obstruction), K4011 (bilateral recurrent inguinal hernia with gangrene), K4031 (unilateral recurrent inguinal hernia with obstruction), and K4041 (unilateral recurrent inguinal hernia with gangrene) for diagnosis, alongside QA753 (recurrent herniorrhaphy with resection of intestine), QA754 (recurrent herniorrhaphy with resection of intestine and posterior repair), QA755 (recurrent herniorrhaphy), and QA756 (recurrent herniorrhaphy with posterior repair) for procedures. In this reanalysis, the readmission or reoperation rates did not significantly differ between the mesh and non-mesh groups at 1 or 5 years (1-year readmission, P = 0.82; 1-year reoperation, P = 0.43; 5-year readmission, P = 0.76; and 5-year reoperation, P = 0.65). Owing to the small number of recurrent cases, Cox proportional hazards regression could not be performed.

DISCUSSION

Adolescence is a transitional stage between childhood and adulthood. Surgeons who perform inguinal hernia repair in adolescents face the challenge of deciding whether a mesh should be used to reinforce the groin. This concern may stem from the unclear and poorly understood underlying causes of inguinal hernias in this age group. In young children, approximately 99% of inguinal hernias are indirect, congenital, and caused by patent processus vaginalis. In contrast, adult inguinal hernias are generally considered acquired lesions [567]. However, limited information is available regarding the etiology of inguinal hernias in adolescents. Consequently, treatment decisions often vary depending on whether the operating surgeon is a general or pediatric surgeon, and their level of experience [3].

National healthcare service data spanning approximately 10 years were analyzed in this study to investigate the patterns and outcomes associated with mesh use in adolescent inguinal hernia repairs. Overall, a mesh was used in 5.12% of adolescent patients. In comparison, a recent meta-analysis of 21 studies and 3,816 adolescents with inguinal hernias showed that 753 (19.7%) patients underwent mesh repair [8]. While this is a noteworthy comparison, a direct comparison is challenging owing to the methodological differences between the 2 studies. However, there are no similar large-scale, multi-institutional retrospective studies in Korea, and similar retrospective studies are scarce worldwide.

Long-term follow-up revealed that the outcomes associated with mesh repair were not superior to those associated with non-mesh techniques. At 1 year, readmission or reoperation rates did not significantly differ between the 2 groups. However, at 5 years, the mesh group exhibited significantly higher rates of readmission and reoperation than at 1 year (Table 2). Cox regression analysis further revealed that mesh use was a significant risk factor for readmission and reoperation within 5 years in univariate analysis (Tables 3, 4). A significant age imbalance was observed between the mesh and non-mesh groups (Table 1), which is an important confounding factor, and to mitigate this, age was included as a continuous covariate in all multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. After adjustment, age remained an independent predictor of readmission and reoperation; however, the effect of mesh use no longer reached statistical significance (Tables 3, 4). These results suggest that the unadjusted difference between the 2 groups may be primarily due to the age imbalance rather than mesh use. However, even in this result, the association with mesh repair was not superior to that with non-mesh techniques.

Even after accounting for several potential confounding variables, the finding that the mesh group had significantly higher rates of readmission and reoperation at 5 years than the non-mesh group raises important questions about the need for mesh use in adolescent hernia repair (Table 2). Similar concerns were raised in previous studies. Reistrup et al. reported that non-mesh repair yields low recurrence rates and suggested that mesh implantation should be avoided in adolescents who still have growth potential [8]. According to a Cochrane review, mesh repair carries potential risks, including infection, deep tissue injury, and nerve damage [9]. In addition, Kim et al. [10] observed significantly higher rates of chronic pain in late adolescent males undergoing mesh repair than in those undergoing non-mesh repair (9.0% vs. 1.7%, P = 0.023). Mesh migration has not yet been reported in adolescents; however, several studies involving adults have shown mesh migration or erosion into adjacent organs, such as the urinary bladder and intestines, following inguinal hernia repair [11121314]. These findings indicate that mesh implantation carries a risk of long-term foreign body reactions and complications. Ongoing tissue growth and anatomical changes in adolescents may increase the risk of mesh migration or erosion.

Despite these disadvantages, the international guidelines for inguinal hernia management developed by the HerniaSurge Group recommend mesh use primarily to reduce recurrence rates in adult patients. In the present study, the causes of readmissions and reoperations could not be clearly identified owing to data limitations. However, analysis of recurrence using specific diagnostic and procedural codes (K4091, K4021, K4001, K4011, K4031, K4041, QA753, QA754, QA755, and QA756) was possible, and we compared the recurrence rates after adolescent hernia repairs between the mesh and non-mesh groups. The 5-year reoperation rates were 0.25% and 0.34% in the mesh and non-mesh groups, respectively, with no significant differences (Table 5). In a recent meta-analysis of adolescents with inguinal hernias, recurrence rates of 0%–0.6% were reported for mesh repair and 1.6%–1.9% for non-mesh repair [8]. Criss et al. [15] also found no statistically significant difference in recurrence between adolescents and young adults who underwent mesh and non-mesh repair, whereas Fan and Yao [16], Taylor et al. [17], and Gasior et al. [18] demonstrated that high ligation alone was associated with low long-term recurrence rates and minimal chronic symptoms. Collectively, these findings indicate that the recurrence rates after mesh and non-mesh repairs in adolescents are low.

In our study, older age was identified as a significant risk factor for readmission and reoperation. Mesh use also increased significantly with age and was particularly high among those aged 17–19 years (Fig. 1 and Tables 3, 4). This observation aligns with those of previous studies reporting that adolescents aged 16–17 years have a higher risk of recurrence after inguinal hernia repair [19]. Holzmacher et al. [20] found that the incidence of direct hernias increases with age in adolescents and young adults, suggesting that their occurrence during adolescence may represent a physiological transition from pediatric to adult abdominal wall characteristics, thereby prompting the consideration of muscle or mesh reinforcement.

Compared with prior studies, our research is unique in that it focuses exclusively on adolescents, and nationwide data were used to provide long-term follow-up outcomes for more than 15,000 patients. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged owing to the characteristics of the dataset. First, some key information needed to derive the results was unavailable owing to limitations in the data available from the NHIS claims database. The type of surgical technique (laparoscopic, robotic, totally extraperitoneal, transabdominal preperitoneal, high ligation, or posterior wall repair) could not be identified using the available procedure codes. Only readmission and reoperation events were assessable; other postoperative complications could not be evaluated. Detailed information on the causes of readmission and reoperation was unavailable. Additionally, the prescription data for analgesics or antibiotics were not presented because it was unclear whether the medications were administered for surgical complications, postoperative pain management, or unrelated conditions. Finally, clinical variables, such as hernia size and surgeon expertise (general or pediatric surgeon), could not be captured.

In conclusion, inguinal hernia repair using mesh did not demonstrate a clear long-term recurrence benefit in adolescents based on NHIS data. Given the limitations inherent in claims data and the potential for selection bias, mesh use should be individualized, and its routine use cannot be recommended. Further prospective studies are warranted to clarify its role in this population, particularly to determine whether individualized treatment strategies are appropriate for older adolescents.
