Artificial intelligence for brain-to-speech decoding in paralysis: a systematic review
Authors: Sanae Belfrouh, Fatima Zahra Salmam, Rahhal Errattahi, Mohamed Hanine, Waeal J Obidallah, Hisham Alhulayyil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe loss of communication constitutes a critical challenge for people living with paralysis. Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) paired with artificial intelligence (AI) provide an opportunity to restore this ability. This systematic review examined the use of AI to decode speech from brain signals through both invasive and non-invasive neural interfaces. Using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we reviewed 115 studies published between 2019 and 2025 to extract data on acquisition protocols, signal preprocessing, and AI architectures. The quality of each study was evaluated using Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2). The results indicated that the invasive approach achieved a higher median multiclass classification accuracy than the non-invasive method (77.7%, interquartile range (IQR): 61.4–93.2% vs. 73.0%, IQR: 49.3–89.2%; N = 82 studies with comparable multiclass metrics), computed from the best-performing model per study across heterogeneous task types, vocabulary sizes, and predominantly subject-dependent evaluation paradigms (84.3% of studies). However, this narrow gap in raw accuracy (4.7% points) should not be interpreted as a direct cross-modality performance ranking, as it obscures substantial differences in task complexity (invasive studies typically decoded larger vocabularies and continuous speech), evaluation paradigm, and participant population. Additionally, the hybrid convolutional neural network/recurrent neural network (CNN/RNN) architecture and transformers outperformed traditional classifier models. Nevertheless, the quality assessments showed significant limitations; notably, 62.6% of the studies evaluated had a high risk of selection bias due to patient characteristics, and only six studies (5.2%) validated results in paralyzed individuals–all relying on invasive modalities. Among these, classification accuracy ranged from 47.1% to 90.0%, while word error rates for continuous speech decoding ranged from 25.6% to 58.8%, demonstrating feasibility but with substantial variability across paralyzed cohorts. No non-invasive study has demonstrated functional speech decoding in paralyzed populations. This validation gap represents the most urgent translational priority in this field. We proposed a decision framework to address accuracy, cost constraints, and clinical applicability.Keywords: Brain-computer interface, Speech neuroprosthesis, Neural decoding, Artificial intelligence, Paralysis rehabilitation, Systematic review

Introduction

The ability to communicate is a fundamental aspect of human existence, and its loss represents one of the most devastating consequences of severe paralysis. The brain-computer interface (BCI) field directly addresses this constraint by aiming to restore high-bandwidth communication for individuals experiencing intense paralysis (locked-in syndrome or anarthria), for whom traditional assistive technologies are ineffective. This ambition finds its voice in Brain-to-Speech Decoding, the translation of neural correlates of speech intention into intelligible acoustic or textual outputs [1] . The significance of this technology transcends mere substitution as it promises to restore expressive autonomy by mapping complex linguistic planning directly from the central nervous system [2].

In order to avoid ambiguity, we define several core terms used throughout this review. Brain-to-speech decoding refers to the process of translating brain signals into linguistic representations, which may take two forms: text-based outputs, involving classification into discrete phonemes, words, or sentences; or acoustic outputs, involving the direct synthesis of audible speech from neural activity. A speech neuroprosthesis is defined here as the complete system, comprising a neural recording device and the decoding algorithms, designed to restore functional communication. The literature distinguishes three speech paradigms: overt speech, the production of articulated speech with audible output; imagined (covert) speech, the internal rehearsal of words without muscular activation; and attempted speech, the volitional effort to articulate in individuals with motor paralysis who can no longer produce overt output. Of these, attempted speech is of greatest clinical relevance because it most closely reflects the conditions under which speech neuroprostheses are intended to operate.

Restoration of speech relies on the recording and analysis of brain activity associated with the execution of articulations and encoding of phonemes [3]. The methods employed for this recording exhibited significantly different reliability capabilities, primarily because of variations in fidelity and invasiveness. For instance, invasive techniques such as stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG), electrocorticography (ECoG), and microelectrode arrays yield a strong signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and provide an accurate representation of High-Gamma Activity (HGA), which is crucial for real-time speech decoding. Non-invasive methods, such as electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG), are the most common and are more accessible to patients; however, they also have weaker signal strengths and lower spatial resolution owing to the volume conduction of electrical and magnetic fields through the scalp and skull. Deep Learning (DL) has revolutionized the technology used to decode neural activity using Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs). DL moves away from Support Vector Machines (SVM) toward more advanced Sequence-to-Sequence Models, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). The development of DL has highlighted the fundamental relationship between Artificial Intelligence (AI) and brain–computer interfaces (BCIs). DL manages the high dimensionality and time-series nature of neural activity. Brain-to-speech interfaces differ from other types of BCIs, primarily those focusing on motor BCIs, where the primary goal is to classify limb movements rather than the continuous high-throughput generation of speech.

Although previous systematic reviews have identified several barriers to successful translation of laboratory findings to clinical application, they have been limited in scope and methodology. For example, Su et al. (2025) [4] focused exclusively on EEG-based speech imagery decoding and encoding without addressing invasive modalities, Alzahrani et al. (2024) [5] emphasized classification tasks such as directional words and vowel identification without assessing clinical translation readiness, and Tates et al. (2025) [6] restricted their scope to the speech imagery paradigm without evaluating clinical applicability to the paralyzed populations. Critically, none of the existing reviews employed standardized quality assessment tools such as QUADAS-2 to evaluate methodological rigor, nor did they provide a unified framework relating the choice of neural modality and AI architecture to clinical applicability.

This systematic review of brain-to-speech decoding synthesizes 115 studies and rigorously addresses critical research questions through a quantitative analysis of the fidelity-performance dichotomy. In addition, it employs the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) framework [7] to identify pervasive patient selection biases that hinder the effective translation of research findings into clinical practice. In conclusion, this work presents a necessary prescriptive framework for constraint-based decision-making, aimed at determining the optimal neural modality and artificial intelligence architecture for creating assistive neural technologies for individuals with paralysis.

Methods

We performed this systematic review in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [8] to promote transparency and reproducibility. We focused on AI-powered brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) for speech decoding, identifying original research conducted and published between January 1, 2019, and September 18, 2025.

Search strategy

Literature retrieval was conducted using four electronic databases: Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and IEEE Xplore. The initial search was performed across Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science. A supplementary validation search was subsequently conducted on IEEE Xplore to ensure comprehensive coverage of engineering conference and transaction literature relevant to the BCI and signal-processing domains. This supplementary search was motivated by the recognition that, despite substantial indexing overlap between Scopus/Web of Science and IEEE-published venues, a dedicated IEEE Xplore search could capture additional conference proceedings not fully indexed in the primary databases. The search was restricted to peer-reviewed publications to ensure methodological rigor, reproducibility, and adherence to the PRISMA guidelines, which recommend clearly defined and reproducible search strategies across established bibliographic databases.

The search strategy was structured using Boolean logic and tailored keywords to capture the domains of brain-computer interfaces, speech decoding (including imagined speech), and artificial intelligence/machine learning applications. Only studies published in English were included. Furthermore, these publications must come from peer-reviewed journals or conference proceedings and be relevant to the fields of neuroscience, engineering, and computer science.

However, the exclusion criteria outlined several conditions that disqualified some studies. Non-speech-related brain-computer interface (BCI) applications, such as those focusing on emotion detection, were excluded. Studies focused exclusively on overt speech decoding that do not relate to covert, imagined, or attempted speech paradigms or do not have application to restoration of communication in populations with paralysis were also excluded. Studies employing overt speech as a developmental paradigm for validating decoding architectures intended for eventual use in non-overt speech were retained. Additionally studies lacking quantitative performance metrics were not included. Reviews, editorials, and theoretical studies that did not include experimental data were excluded. Finally studies that relied exclusively on non-cerebral modalities such as electromyography (EMG) or peripheral nerve recordings were excluded. Studies employing multimodal setups that included at least one cerebral recording modality (e.g., EEG combined with EMG) were retained.

Data extraction

A specific Data Extraction Template was created and used during this review to standardize the systematic and comprehensive extraction of key aspects from the analyzed studies. The first part of the template contained the fundamental characteristics of each study, such as the author’s name, year of publication, and what the researchers aimed to accomplish in that particular study. The next portion of the template extracted demographic and clinical information regarding the subjects participating in the studies, including characteristics such as sample size, health status of the subject population, and age.

Following the collection of participant information, the template collected information about how the neural acquisition data were obtained, specifically outlining what types of neural signals were recorded (i.e., EEG or ECoG) and listing what equipment and methods were used to obtain and process the neural acquisition data before it was acquired. Finally, the Template lists the Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods applied to the neural acquisition data, specifically, how the AI methods obtained features from the data, what models were used (i.e., Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), and Support Vector Machine (SVM)), and what classification tasks were performed on the data.

Finally, the last part of the template evaluates the performance of the AI methods applied to the neural acquisition data by utilizing well-established performance measures (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score). The relevance of the bias risk indicators in the reviewed studies was evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool to objectively evaluate the methodological quality and reliability of the reviewed studies. Performance metrics were applied in the context of each experimental environment, including the preprocessing strategy, model architecture, and volume of data used. Additionally, this section emphasizes modality-specific advantages, emerging trends, and performance trade-offs.

For the quantitative performance comparison, we extracted the highest reported multiclass classification accuracy (three or more classes) from each study using the best-performing model or configuration. When a study reported both pairwise (one-vs-one) and multiclass accuracy, the multiclass value was used to ensure comparability. Binary classification tasks (e.g., speech vs. rest) were excluded from the median computation due to differences in chance-level baselines. Studies reporting only non-accuracy metrics (e.g., word error rate, Pearson correlation, BLEU scores) or non-classification outcomes (e.g., phase-locking values, spectral power differences) were discussed qualitatively but excluded from the accuracy based comparison. No weighting was applied when multiple studies used the same dataset (e.g., KARA ONE), as each study contributed a distinct methodological pipeline (preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification approach) and therefore represented an independent evaluation of a different AI architecture on the same data. The evaluation paradigm (subject-dependent vs. subject-independent) was recorded for each study and is analyzed separately in the Results section.

Results

Study selection

A systematic search identified 560 records across four databases: Scopus (), PubMed (), Web of Science (), and IEEE Xplore (). After removing 23 duplicate records, 537 unique titles and abstracts were screened. This initial screening excluded 257 records, primarily focusing on general motor imagery not specific to speech or employing non-AI/ML analysis techniques. Full-text reports were obtained from the remaining 280 reports; however, 64 could not be retrieved. The eligibility of 216 full-text reports was assessed. A total of 101 reports were excluded at this stage based on predefined criteria: 38 were not focused on speech-related BCIs, 22 did not employ AI or machine learning techniques, 17 used non-brain signals (e.g., EMG-only), 20 lacked quantitative performance metrics, and four involved unrelated populations or conditions (e.g., non-human animal studies). Consequently, 115 studies were included in the final qualitative and quantitative syntheses. The systematic search and selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Open-source datasets

The availability of open-source datasets that cover a wide variety of brain-to-speech decoding methods will help advance this field by creating common ground among researchers. Datasets can be used to train and assess models by employing standardized procedures. They also provide a means to compare different approaches to signal processing and apply machine learning algorithms.

The 115 included studies referenced 14 unique, publicly available datasets for brain-to-speech decoding research. The dataset most commonly used in the literature is KARA ONE (2015) [121]. It consisted of 64-channel EEG recordings made while participants said phonemes or syllables. This dataset has been used in six studies ( [3], [57], [46], [52], [85], [79]). Regarding invasive modalities, the Angrick 2024 dataset [54] provides a critical point for translationally relevant research. This dataset included high-density ECoG recordings from one paralyzed individual who could not speak (anarthric). The recordings contained 128 channels of neural activity captured during 1570 trials of six-word speech production. Importantly, this dataset contains data from the exact patient population in which the clinical deployment of BCI systems is expected. In contrast to other datasets, this study was not based on recordings obtained from healthy volunteers.

The datasets also record the neural activity associated with a variety of speech tasks, including imagining speech, speaking aloud, and covertly producing speech. The participant sample sizes varied from one subject [54] to 20 subjects [25], and the electrodes varied from eight-channel wearable devices to 140-channel clinically used arrays. The linguistic content recorded in these studies covers a variety of languages, including English, Dutch, Spanish, Mandarin, Chinese, and Arabic. A summary of the properties of each dataset studied, including the type of recording, recording parameters, and types of stimuli where the data may be accessed, and how often each dataset was referenced in the studies included in our review, is presented in Table 6 .

These public datasets are important for the development and comparison of BCI systems and for promoting reproducible, innovative, and collaborative research efforts to restore communication among people with paralysis.

Despite the growing availability of open-source datasets, several critical limitations persist. First, only one dataset (Angrick 2024 [54]) includes recordings from a paralyzed participant; all others were collected from healthy volunteers or epilepsy patients, limiting their relevance for clinical deployment. Second, few datasets provide standardized subject-independent evaluation splits (with the DAIS dataset being a notable exception [25]), making cross-study comparison of generalization performance difficult. Third, aligned articulatory data (e.g., electromagnetic articulography or ultrasound tongue imaging) are absent from all datasets, precluding multimodal articulatory-neural analyses. Fourth, adoption of standardized neurophysiology formats such as Neurodata Without Borders (NWB) remains limited to two datasets (HEscs and DSPi-IEEG), with the majority distributed in proprietary Matlab formats. Finally, no dataset provides longitudinal recordings from same participants within multiple sessions over weeks or months, which are needed to evaluate decoder stability over time.

Reproducibility beyond dataset availability

While dataset availability is a necessary condition for reproducibility, it is not sufficient. We assessed additional dimensions of reproducibility across the 115 included studies.

Open code availability: Only 11 studies (9.6%) provided fully publicly accessible source code or linked to a code repository, and an additional 8 studies (7.0%) offered partial code availability (e.g., related repositories, code expected to be released, or supplementary materials). The remaining 96 studies (83.5%) had no publicly identifiable code, severely limiting the ability to verify or replicate reported results.

Dataset sharing: Beyond the 14 public datasets identified in Table 6, only 8 studies (7.0%) made their experimental data publicly available, and 3 studies (2.6%) offered data upon request; the remaining 104 studies (90.4%) did not share data.

Predefined train/validation/test splits: The vast majority of studies defined their own data splits, with no standardized partitioning across studies using the same dataset. Among the 14 public datasets, only the DAIS dataset [25] provided a recommended subject-independent evaluation split.

Reporting completeness: A number of studies left out methodological details that would be needed to replicate their work. Common omissions included the exact order in which preprocessing steps were applied relative to the train/test split—a point that matters directly for assessing data leakage—as well as hyperparameter search ranges and how many random seeds or repetitions were run. It was also frequent to see only point estimates for the main performance metrics, with no standard deviations or confidence intervals alongside them.

External replication: Not a single study in our review reported an independent replication of a previously published result using the same dataset, pipeline, and evaluation protocol. Several studies did work with overlapping datasets - six used KARA ONE, for instance—but each one applied a different pipeline. That makes side-by-side comparison possible, but exact replication is still missing. Taken together, these observations point to a reproducibility problem in brain-to-speech decoding that goes well beyond whether datasets are openly available.

The reported acquisition hardware reflects the strategic tension between clinical-grade accuracy and experimental accessibility. High-density non-invasive EEG systems, particularly those using Brain Products Quick-CAP/ActiCap, were employed in 16 studies (13.9%). Participant populations and clinical relevance

Most studies (, 69.6%) relied on healthy volunteers to establish the initial proof-of-concept (computed from the Summary Table 5, ). Only 35 studies (30.4%) included a clinical population. Of the clinical studies, 25 (21.7%) included non-paralyzed cohorts, predominantly patients with epilepsy undergoing ECoG/sEEG monitoring (e.g., [13] and [18]), or neurosurgical patients. Critically, only nine studies (7.8%) included participants with severe paralysis (anarthria, tetraplegia, or motor neuron disease) as the primary target population for communication restoration [1, 16, 54, 60, 65, 101, 103, 105, 108]. One additional study [27] included a mixed cohort of three ALS patients alongside seven healthy controls.

All nine studies targeting communication restoration in paralyzed participants employed highly invasive methods, such as ECoG or intracortical arrays. No EEG or MEG studies have demonstrated functional speech-decoding outcomes in permanently paralyzed clinical cohorts. The exclusive reliance on invasive ECoG/intracortical methods for robust decoding in paralysis suggests that high-frequency components (HGA) and localized spatial specificity obtained via direct cortical contact are necessary to resolve the complex neural correlates of speech. Although EEG research provides valuable methodological advancements, its translational impact remains limited until non-invasive techniques demonstrate the requisite signal integrity and robustness for high-fidelity decoding.

Translational stratification

To contextualize the clinical relevance of the included evidence, we stratified the 115 studies into three translational tiers.

Tier 1: Proof-of-concept (healthy volunteers). Most studies (N = 80; 69.6%) recruited only healthy participants performing speech tasks under controlled laboratory conditions. These studies demonstrate technical feasibility, but their findings do not transfer directly to clinical populations, since neural speech representations may differ considerably in individuals with neurological injury or degeneration.

Tier 2: Clinically adjacent (neurosurgical monitoring patients). Twenty-five studies (21.7%) used invasive recordings from epilepsy patients undergoing pre-surgical monitoring or from patients undergoing awake craniotomy for tumor resection. These studies benefit from high quality intracranial signals but are limited by short recording windows (typically 1–3 sessions), electrode placements dictated by clinical need rather than speech-optimal coverage, and a participant population that does not reflect the intended end-users of speech BCIs.

Tier 3: True end-user validation (individuals with paralysis or speech loss). Only nine studies (7.8%) [1, 16, 54, 60, 65, 101, 103, 105, 108] involved participants with actual paralysis, anarthria, or motor neuron disease. One additional study [27] included a mixed cohort of three ALS patients alongside seven healthy controls. These are the most clinically relevant studies but also the most constrained: sample sizes were typically N = 1, recording conditions varied across sites, and performance metrics are difficult to compare across different levels of residual motor function. Every Tier 3 study used an invasive modality (ECoG, intracortical arrays, or sEEG); no non-invasive study has yet validated speech decoding in a paralyzed individual.

This stratification exposes a pronounced translational gap: nearly 70% of the evidence base reflects idealized conditions in healthy volunteers, while the population that speech BCIs are ultimately built for accounts for fewer than 8% of studies.

Feature extraction

Feature extraction follows a significant chronological development pattern: early methods employed classic statistical properties, while recent approaches utilized sophisticated deep learning representations compatible with language models. Early methods primarily sought to quantify oscillatory behaviors (e.g., power spectrum) in neural signals; however, the need for reliable decoding of complex tasks has led to a strategic shift away from traditional signal processing methods (e.g., wavelets) and toward more advanced model-based approaches for generating features. This progression has shifted from static hypothesis-driven signal analysis to dynamic data-driven encoding that is compatible with large-scale high-capacity neural network architectures. A graphical summary of the temporal variations across different feature extraction techniques by time period (pre-2022 and post-2022) is provided in Fig. 4.

A substantial methodological shift was observed in this study. There was an almost five-fold increase in the number of Deep/Learned Feature methods used after 2022 (from N = 5 before 2022 to N = 25 after 2022). This preference for learned features indicates a continued growth in the capacity of models to analyze raw or lightly processed neural signals to develop features that are consistent with both linguistic and acoustic features (e.g., GPT feature sets for semantic reconstructions [12]). Additionally, the number of wavelet/time-frequency feature methods has increased substantially and is frequently employed as a transformation layer before input into Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) (e.g., [25], [64]).

Classification approaches

The classification paradigm has evolved rapidly with advancements in feature extraction and deep learning. Models must handle the inherent temporal dependencies and high dimensionality of the neural signals related to continuous speech. Although traditional, computationally inexpensive methods remain important for baseline comparisons and binary tasks, the field overwhelmingly favors complex architectures designed to capture nonlinear, time-varying relationships. A significant trend is the emergence of advanced sequence models: transformers, large language models (LLMs) architectures, and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been used in 18 studies (15.7%). These models are disproportionately represented in invasive (ECoG/sEEG) studies, reflecting the necessity of high-capacity models to decode the fine spatial and temporal details offered by high-gamma data for continuous synthesis (e.g., the use of transformers for sEEG-based decoding [61]).

Evaluation paradigms: subject-dependent vs. Subject-independent

The evaluation paradigm employed in the included studies represents a critical factor influencing reported performance and clinical interpretability. We classified each study according to whether models were evaluated using subject-dependent (SD) protocols, in which training and testing data originate from the same individual via within-subject cross-validation, or subject-independent (SI) protocols, in which models are tested on individuals entirely excluded from training.

Subject-dependent evaluation was the dominant approach, employed in 97 studies (84.3%). Only five studies (4.3%) evaluated exclusively using SI or cross-subject generalization. Ten studies (8.7%) reported results under both SD and SI paradigms [2, 12, 21, 26, 36, 50, 66, 67, 106, 117], including Dash et al. (2020) [117], who reported a mean correlation of r = 0.80 under SD versus r = 0.06 under SI for continuous jaw kinematics decoding from MEG, representing a near-complete collapse in performance under cross-subject conditions. The remaining three studies (2.6%) did not clearly specify the evaluation paradigm used.

Among the ten studies reporting both paradigms, SI performance was consistently and substantially lower than SD performance. Vorontsova et al. (2021) [66] reported up to 84.5% accuracy under SD versus 10–13% under SI for 9-class classification, and 88% (SD) versus 52–53% (SI) for binary classification. Dash et al. (2023) [36] reported 80%+ for imagined speech under SD versus 20% under SI using MEG. Tang et al. (2023) [12] found that cross-subject fMRI decoders performed barely above chance compared to within-subject models. Einizade et al. (2022) [50] reported a mean SD accuracy of 35.5% versus 41.3% for SI in an EEG study, representing a rare case where SI marginally outperformed SD, potentially attributable to regularization effects with limited per-subject data. LaRocco et al. (2023) [21] reported 97% mean accuracy and an F1 score of 0.55 under both paradigms, though the low F1 score relative to accuracy suggests class imbalance that may obscure true generalization ability. Dash et al. (2020) [117] reported a mean Pearson correlation of r = 0.80 for subject-dependent jaw kinematics regression from MEG signals versus r = 0.06 for the subject-independent model, demonstrating a near-total breakdown of generalization in a continuous articulatory decoding task.

These findings indicate that the median accuracy values reported in this review (Sect. 3.6.5) are derived predominantly from SD evaluation and likely overestimate the performance achievable in clinical deployment, where rapid generalization to a new user with minimal calibration data is essential. The near-exclusive reliance on SD paradigms (84.3%) represents a significant methodological limitation of the current literature. Future studies should report performance under both SD and SI protocols and adopt standardized cross-subject evaluation benchmarks to enable meaningful assessment of clinical readiness.

Model performance

The performance metrics are highly heterogeneous across modalities. Of the 115 included studies, 82 reported multiclass classification accuracy (3 classes) suitable for quantitative comparison; the remaining 33 studies reported non-classification metrics (e.g., word error rate, Pearson correlation, BLEU scores), binary outcomes, or non-standard measures and were therefore excluded from the median computation (see Sect. 2.6 for full criteria). Among the 82 comparable studies, invasive modalities (ECoG, sEEG, intracortical; N = 18) achieved a median accuracy of 77.7% (IQR: 61.4–93.2%), while non-invasive modalities (EEG, MEG, fMRI, hybrid; N = 64) achieved a median accuracy of 73.0% (IQR: 49.3–89.2%). The difference of 4.7% points, while modest in magnitude, must be interpreted in the context of substantial differences in task complexity: invasive studies typically decoded larger vocabularies (up to 420 words for intracortical recordings [60]) and continuous speech, whereas non-invasive studies predominantly classified 4–13 discrete words or phonemes. When examining EEG-only studies (N = 57), the median accuracy was 71.8% (IQR: 47.7–88.8%). MEG studies (N = 5) achieved a notably higher median of 88.0%, although this estimate is limited by the small sample size (Fig. 6).

Distribution of multiclass classification accuracy (3 classes) by neural recording modality (N = 82 studies). Each dot represents one study’s best-performing model. Box plots show the median, IQR, and 1.5 IQR whiskers. Studies reporting only binary, pairwise, or non-accuracy metrics were excluded

Caveat on evaluation paradigm. The modality-level median accuracies reported above are derived almost entirely from subject-dependent (SD) evaluation (84.3% of studies). Because only five studies employed exclusively subject-independent (SI) protocols—none of which used invasive modalities for multiclass classification—it is not currently possible to determine whether the invasive advantage persists under cross-subject conditions. Similarly, the architecture-level comparisons (e.g., CNN/RNN vs. transformers) are confounded by the near-universal use of SD evaluation; architectures that excel at capturing individual-specific neural patterns may not retain their advantage when required to generalize across users. These comparisons should therefore be interpreted as reflecting performance under idealized, subject-specific calibration conditions rather than clinically realistic deployment scenarios.

Limitations of the cross-modality comparison. The median-based comparison between invasive and non-invasive modalities pools studies with heterogeneous task designs, vocabulary sizes (from 3 phonemes to 420 words), speech paradigms (imagined, overt, and attempted), recording conditions, and participant populations (healthy volunteers vs. epilepsy patients vs. paralyzed individuals). We did not perform a sensitivity analysis stratified by vocabulary size, paradigm, or evaluation protocol because the number of studies within each stratum was too small for robust subgroup analysis—only six studies involved paralyzed participants, for example, and only five used exclusively subject-independent evaluation. The reported median accuracies should therefore be read as descriptive summaries of the current literature, not as evidence of a definitive performance ranking between modalities. A meaningful cross-modality comparison will require future studies to adopt standardized tasks, vocabulary sizes, and evaluation protocols.

Quantitative comparison. The comparable median accuracy between invasive and non-invasive modalities (77.7% vs. 73.0%) should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent decoding capability. Invasive modalities maintain a fundamental advantage through direct cortical access, superior signal-to-noise ratio, and access to high-gamma activity, which collectively enable decoding of larger vocabularies and continuous speech at clinically relevant throughput rates. Non-invasive methods, while achieving comparable accuracy on simpler classification tasks, are constrained by volume conduction, lower spatial resolution, and signal fidelity limitations that currently preclude high-throughput continuous decoding. Discussion

This systematic review synthesized data from 115 studies that included various methods and results for brain-to-speech decoding using different neural recording technologies and machine learning architectures. Our work shows that many new technologies are being rapidly developed in this field. However, there is a distinction between demonstrating the capabilities of technology under laboratory conditions and developing clinically relevant communication devices based on such technologies. Therefore, instead of simply discussing the extent of neural decoding capabilities, our focus was on determining how best to balance competing demands: achieving high signal fidelity, managing model complexity, ensuring clinical applicability, and maximizing user accessibility. To meet these objectives, we provide a prescriptive decision framework for guiding researchers and clinicians, as they select the most effective technologies for their specific applications.

Summary of evidence and the modality-performance trade-off

Our findings show a hierarchical structure in decoding performance related to the spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the acquisition method, and invasive methods (ECoG and sEEG) have shown consistent superiority over non-invasive methods (EEG).

Among the 82 studies reporting comparable multiclass accuracy (3 classes), invasive methods achieved a median of 77.7% (IQR: 61.4–93.2%), while non-invasive methods achieved 73.0% (IQR: 49.3–89.2%), a difference of only 4.7% points. However, this narrow gap in raw accuracy should not be interpreted as a direct performance ranking, as it is confounded by substantial differences in task complexity: invasive studies typically decoded substantially larger vocabularies and continuous speech, whereas non-invasive studies predominantly classified 4–13 discrete words. Additionally, the lowest WER values for continuous speech synthesis reported in the literature (25.6% [65]) were achieved exclusively using invasive methods. This performance advantage depends on the capacity to acquire high-frequency neural activity (high-gamma, 70–170 Hz), which is correlated with both the kinematics of articulation and acoustic properties of speech [49, 54]. While non-invasive EEG provides the highest accessibility, its performance on comparable classification tasks is constrained by limited spatial resolution, muscular and ocular artifacts, and attenuation of high-frequency cortical signals by the skull and scalp. Consequently, there was also a difference in the complexity of the AI models used in each modality. Owing to the large number of features and the rich nature of the data collected from ECoG/sEEG, deep learning architectures are possible, including transformers, GANs and Hybrid CNN-RNNs [1, 58, 61], which can learn the long-term dependencies of time in speech, as well as the complex nonlinear mapping required to continuously synthesize speech from neural activity. However, the majority of EEG studies have employed significantly less complex AI models, such as traditional classifiers (e.g., SVM and LDA) and simple CNNs, when using lower-dimensional data for tasks, including binary classification or classification of a small vocabulary [2, 30]. This disparity in AI model complexity and the corresponding modality-driven differences in performance demonstrate that there is no universal AI architecture and that the best AI architecture will always depend on the amount of information available in the neural signal being processed. Importantly, both the modality comparisons and the architecture comparisons presented above must be qualified by the near-exclusive reliance on subject-dependent evaluation across the literature. With 84.3% of studies training and testing on the same individual, the reported performance hierarchy—invasive over non-invasive, deep learning over classical ML—may partly reflect the capacity of complex models to memorize individual-specific neural signatures rather than to learn generalizable speech representations. Until a sufficient number of studies report cross-subject performance stratified by modality and architecture, the comparative conclusions drawn here should be treated as provisional.

As this framework illustrates, there are no one-size-fits-all solutions to these applications, and the suggested pairings should be revisited as the evidence base matures—particularly as more studies adopt subject-independent evaluation and validate in paralyzed cohorts. High-performance prosthetics (Tier 1) require an invasive approach with a requirement of very complex AI models being able to realize their full capabilities. Non-invasive hardware is required to achieve widespread access (Tier 3); therefore, computationally simple and robust models that trade off performance in favor of practicality are favored. Tier 2 is the middle ground of the translational tier and is where all new algorithms go through validation and preparation for clinical use. The translational stratification presented in Sect. 3.5.1 further underscores that the performance figures reported in this review should be interpreted with caution. The median accuracies, architecture comparisons, and modality rankings are overwhelmingly derived from Tier 1 and Tier 2 studies. Whether these patterns hold for Tier 3 end-users is a separate question, and an urgent one. These individuals may have altered cortical representations—from prolonged disuse of speech musculature, cortical reorganization after stroke, or progressive neurodegeneration—none of which are captured by experiments on healthy volunteers. The nine Tier 3 studies in our corpus show that clinical deployment is feasible, but they are too few and too heterogeneous to draw firm comparative conclusions. The field needs longitudinal studies in people with paralysis that track performance over time, not cross-sectional snapshots from healthy participants.

Critical caveats: the chasm between laboratory performance and clinical generalizability

One of the most important findings of this review is the gap between the populations used to develop these models and their intended users. Our QUADAS-2 evaluation found that 72 studies (62.6%) had a high risk of bias in “Patient Selection,” driven primarily by reliance on healthy volunteers. In practice, this means the accuracy figures reported in most papers come from young, neurotypical individuals with intact speech motor control—and will almost certainly overestimate what these models can achieve in patients with paralysis. This overestimation is further compounded by the near-exclusive reliance on subject-dependent evaluation paradigms (84.3% of studies), which inflate reported accuracy by exploiting individual-specific neural patterns that may not generalize to new users in clinical settings. Furthermore, the comparable median accuracy between invasive (77.7%) and non-invasive (73.0%) modalities is misleading without accounting for task complexity: invasive studies decoded substantially larger vocabularies, and therefore the raw accuracy gap understates the true performance advantage of invasive recordings for clinically relevant communication restoration.

Speech production after a brainstem stroke or ALS is fundamentally changed because of: (i) cortical reorganization as a result of injury, (ii) degeneration of neurons as a result of disease progression, and (iii) disuse-induced plasticity of the remaining cortex [101]. Because of these differences, models developed with data from healthy brains cannot be assumed to be generalized to the cohort of paralyzed patients. All six studies where successful speech decoding and/or communication was demonstrated in patients with anarthria utilized invasive stimulation modalities [1, 16, 54, 65, 101, 105]. Both the better signal quality and the dense, local representations of ECoG and intracortical array stimulation recordings likely make them more resistant to the effects of the above-mentioned pathophysiological changes. Decoding rates in the studies mentioned in this section ranged from 15.2 words/min [65] to 47.5 words/min [1], both of which are less than half the typical rate of a person’s natural speech, i.e., 150–160 words/min. The availability of training data for paralyzed patients has been very limited. For example, the Angrick (2024) study used a total of only 1,570 trials recorded from one ALS patient [54]. Most high-performance architectures were trained on datasets collected from many more trials of non-paralyzed individuals. As previously stated, we strongly advocate for the field to require validation of these models in the target clinical populations. While the proof-of-concept in healthy subjects is still useful, claims of translational relevance must be supported with data collected from paralyzed patients.

Future directions

To close the gap between the potential of laboratory-based technology and the real-world applications of this technology, researchers will need to follow the aforementioned paths.

Targeted Clinical Validation: Future research should continue with a targeted longitudinal validation using paralyzed participants. This is contingent on successful collaboration between those in AI research communities and clinical neurology/neurosurgery communities.

Computational Efficiency and Latency: In order to maintain the fluency of conversation, natural speech requires a decoding time of less than 200 ms and a word rate of 150–160 words/min. Although our review did not systematically extract computational performance metrics, the reported architectures for paralyzed patients (e.g., transformer and RNN-based pipelines) rely on external computing resources and do not yet meet these real-time requirements. Thus, there exists a gap between the ability of algorithms to perform decoding and the ability to deploy such algorithms on portable or totally implanted devices. Therefore, future research should focus on developing ultra-low-power architectures that enable real-time inference on devices that can be deployed on the body. Future systematic evaluations should also benchmark computational latency, memory footprint, and power consumption alongside decoding accuracy.

Training Data Constraints and Mitigation Strategies: Current high-performing architectures need thousands of labeled trials, but paralyzed participants can only provide limited recording sessions—fatigue and clinical burden make long data collection impractical. Transfer learning from healthy-subject data, fine-tuned with a handful of patient recordings, is one way to reduce this burden. Model distillation and quantization could bring large networks onto edge devices, while federated learning would let multiple clinical sites pool data without sharing raw recordings. Neuromorphic hardware may eventually handle the real-time processing these systems demand. Of these, rapid-calibration paradigms that cut per-session data requirements look the most immediately actionable, though their decoder stability over time still needs validation in longitudinal studies.

Standardization of Reporting: Standardized reporting of clinically relevant metrics would significantly improve the communication of results across BCI research domains. To support cross-study comparison, particularly concerning continuous speech generation, it is recommended to report clinically relevant metrics (such as ITR and WER) in addition to the common metrics reported, such as accuracy.

Robust Personalization and Adaptation: Decoders must be designed to account for the non-stationarity of neural signals and the unique neuropathological characteristics of individual users. Few-shot unsupervised adaptation techniques are critical for achieving the long-term feasibility of BCIs.

Limitations

The limitations of this systematic review should be acknowledged. First, we restricted our search to English-language publications only, which meant that important studies could have been excluded because they were written in another language. Second, the retrieval ended on September 18, 2025; therefore, our analysis did not include the most recent developments in AI in this rapidly changing area. A third limitation is that there is considerable variability among the performance metrics used by the study authors, as many researchers report their accuracy, word error rate, precision, recall, etc., but do not use the same measures, making it difficult to compare their findings directly. Fourth, although we had an extremely detailed evaluation using the QUADAS-2 quality assessment tool to assess the methodological quality of reviewed studies, when we adapted this tool from diagnostic studies to neural engineering studies, we needed to exercise some subjectivity in interpreting the assessment criteria. Furthermore, formal inter-rater reliability statistics (e.g., Cohen’s ) were not computed; quality assessment was performed primarily by one reviewer with independent verification on a subset, which may introduce subjectivity in borderline cases. Fifth, although we stratified studies by translational stage (Sect. 3.5.1), the small number of Tier 3 studies (N = 9) precluded formal subgroup analysis of performance by participant population, limiting our ability to assess whether findings from healthy volunteers generalize to the intended end-user population. Sixth, although multiple studies using the same public dataset (e.g., six studies used KARA ONE) were treated as independent methodological evaluations—each contributing a distinct preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification pipeline—we did not perform a sensitivity analysis reducing the weight of repeatedly reused datasets. This may introduce a dataset-specific bias into the aggregate performance estimates, as models evaluated on the same underlying neural recordings may not represent fully independent evidence.

Conclusion

This systematic review of 115 studies demonstrated that the field of AI for decoding brain-to-speech is now at a technical and ethical juncture. While there have been substantial advances in the capabilities of AI models and the accuracy of decoding—particularly when utilizing high-fidelity invasive signals such as electrocorticography (ECoG)—significant gaps remain between these technical achievements and clinical viability. The QUADAS-2 risk of bias assessments revealed pervasive Patient Selection bias (62.6% High Risk) due to the predominant use of non-paralyzed cohorts, which severely limits the clinical generalizability of reported accuracy metrics relative to the target anarthric population. Furthermore, 84.3% of studies relied exclusively on subject-dependent evaluation, meaning that reported accuracy values likely overestimate clinical performance where cross-subject generalization is required. To navigate the transition from laboratory science to therapeutic reality, this review recommends adopting a constraint-based decision-making framework that explicitly aligns technical development with clinical constraints and patient needs.

Within this framework, High-Fidelity Voice Restoration should prioritize invasive ECoG or intracortical arrays coupled with computationally efficient, real-time streaming AI to achieve chronic stability and ultralow latency for both voice prosthetics and neural interfaces. In contrast, Accessible Assistive Systems based on non-invasive EEG should strategically pivot toward optimizing low-complexity models for robust, low-channel command selection (Tier 3), rather than chasing performance metrics that are currently restricted to surgical approaches. Ultimately, the future of this field depends on prioritizing clinical rigor: mandatory validation protocols are needed for all studies claiming translational relevance, ensuring that the promise of communication restoration is realized through tested, verifiable efficacy in paralyzed patient cohorts.
