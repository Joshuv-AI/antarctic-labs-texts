School is more than a place to learn: An intersectoral assessment of adolescent well-being prior to and after the COVID-19 pandemic in the WHO European Region
Authors: M. Limburg, M. Cronin, M. Black, J.C. Inchley, D. Jourdan, C. Jung-Sievers, P. McHale, E. Rehfuess, M.-C. Tsai, N.J. Gray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAdolescents' lives around the world were significantly disrupted during school closures to contain the COVID-19 pandemic. As well as the learning losses that ensued, commentators have noted the pandemic's often negative impact on a wide range of issues including mental health and nutrition. This study sought to understand how adolescent well-being changed following the pandemic period across the WHO European region.Study designUsing data from two international surveys for countries in the WHO European region, Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) and Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), this secondary analysis examines adolescent well-being pre- and post-pandemic, as well as associations with socioeconomic status (SES) and school closure duration.MethodsThe UN H6+ framework for adolescent well-being informed selection of indicators from both surveys, mapped against its five domains: 1) Good health and optimum nutrition, 2) Connectedness, positive values and contribution to society, 3) Safety and a supportive environment, 4) Learning, competence, education, skills and employability, and 5) Agency and resilience.Following selection of one indicator per domain for analysis, overall trends between 2018-2022 and 2014–2018 were described, and outcomes by most and least deprived quintiles of SES compared. Logistic fixed effect regression models were employed to analyse the change between 2018 and 2022 for the binary variables in domains 1,2,3 and 5. Linear fixed effect regression models were used for continuous variables in domain 4. Finally, measures of SES and school closure duration were added as interaction terms to the models.ResultsA decrease in adolescent well-being was seen across all domains between 2018 and 2022, apart from domain 1 (physical activity) which showed an increase. These changes were statistically significant for all except domain 3 (bullying). High family support decreased by 17% (OR: 0.827; 95% CI: 0.720 to 0.949) and feeling pressured by schoolwork increased by 40% (OR: 1.402; 95% CI: 1.242 to 1.581). PISA average scores for maths and reading decreased, by 15.153 (95% CI: −22.229 to −8.078) and 12.482 (95% CI: −20.184 to −4.779), respectively. SES had a greater association with assessment scores for maths (p = 0.027), and science (p = 0.002) in 2022 than in 2018, resulting in a growing inequity gap. None of the included well-being indicators showed a statistically significant association for every 30 days of school closure duration.ConclusionsAdolescent well-being declined during the pandemic; this was especially seen in education outcomes for adolescents from the most deprived backgrounds. Leveraging existing health and education datasets enables an intersectoral approach to support those who have fallen behind. Future pandemic response decisions, such as school closures, must carefully balance protection from infectious diseases with the impacts on education and student well-being.

Introduction

Adolescence is a crucial period of development that shapes future well-being. Health and education have significant mutual benefits: healthy students are ready to learn, and those with a good education can reap the ‘triple dividend’ of good health as adolescents, future adults, and for the children of those who later become parents [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Yet, globally, inequalities in adolescent health and well-being are increasing [5], with socioeconomic circumstances a key factor affecting various health outcomes during adolescence, including mental health and overweight [6].

In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic led to the closure of schools worldwide, disrupting many adolescents’ education trajectories. As the pandemic took hold in Europe, students faced cycles of school closure and reopening: schools were closed for more than 100 days in many European countries, with a maximum of 341 days in Italy, among the 40 countries analysed as part of this paper [7]. Even once schools reopened, school life was further disrupted through various combinations of restrictive infection prevention measures, such as splitting classes into cohorts, testing regimens and mask mandates, active in some countries until mid-2022. School closures and infection prevention measures have been widely studied: commentators expressed concern about many possible impacts and unintended consequences on adolescent health and well-being, including limited contact with peers, reduced physical activity, and learning loss [[8], [9], [10]]. Yet the impact of these disruptions on adolescent well-being remains imperfectly understood, particularly in the context of existing socioeconomic inequalities.

School is not just a place to learn but also a place to promote health and well-being, according to the World Health Organisation's definition of a ‘health-promoting school’ [11]. The pastoral support of schools to students – including the provision of free school meals or safeguarding vulnerable children and adolescents – was reduced or absent during closures and times of preventive measures in place such as cohorting [8]. While remote or hybrid learning was embraced by countries across Europe, a relatively affluent world region, as a solution for students unable to attend school in person, it also presented new challenges for adolescents [12], exacerbating educational inequities and the ‘digital divide’ [13].

In August 2020, a multi-UN-agency (UN H6+) group published a conceptual framework for adolescent well-being, including five inter-connected domains: 1) Good health and optimum nutrition; 2) Connectedness, positive values and contribution to society; 3) Safety and a supportive environment; 4) Learning, competence, education, skills and employability; and 5) Agency and resilience [14] (Box 1). Methods

Study design and participants

Secondary analysis was undertaken using PISA and HBSC datasets for 40 out of 53 countries of the WHO European region (refer to supplementary file 1), up to 38 in PISA and 37 in HBSC datasets depending on the availability for each indicator. Of the 40 countries in the analysis, 31 are classified as high income and 9 as upper-middle income as per the World Bank Atlas method [27]. A graphical overview of the analysed countries and their income classification can be seen in supplementary file 2.

PISA is a global study, repeated every three years, of 15-year-olds which particularly focusses on formally assessing reading, mathematics and science knowledge and skills, hosted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Nationally representative target populations are ensured through a two-stage stratified sample design with sampling of schools from a national list, followed by student sampling within schools. The study primarily utilises surveying of students, who also undergo the PISA assessment, and school principals, while a smaller number of countries also issue questionnaires to teachers and parents. PISA is primarily administered through computer-based testing, though depending on circumstances surrounding specific countries or economies, an element of paper-based testing remains. Its most recent cycle included 81 countries, of which 38 were members of the WHO European region; study cycles 2015 (n = 236,619), 2018 (n = 313,278) and 2022 (n = 298,078) were utilised for this analysis.

HBSC is a WHO collaborative, repeated cross-sectional survey of adolescent health and well-being conducted every four years amongst nationally representative samples of school children aged 11, 13 and 15 years. The survey is school based, using self-report questionnaires, with whole classes being the primary sampling unit. The most recent cycle was conducted in 37 countries across the WHO European Region and Canada. Data from the 2014 (n = 200,129), 2018 (n = 227,441) and 2022 (n = 279,117) survey cycles were included in this analysis.

Selection of indicators

All indicators available in the two datasets were carefully reviewed and mapped against the five well-being domains of the UN H6+ Framework [14]. Following this initial mapping exercise, relevant indicators were selected to populate each of the five domains (Table 1). Subsequently, one indicator per domain was selected for inclusion in the analysis, based on data availability to facilitate trend analysis and data quality (see indicators in bold in Table 1). Additionally, indicators were chosen in line with the literature review undertaken and what was deemed to have the greatest impact on adolescent well-being. On occasion, where indicators had equal data quality and availability as well as similar justification through the literature, authors met to discuss the most appropriate indicators for analysis. For instance, family support was chosen over peer support after careful consideration of the context of the pandemic. Variables

All variables obtained from HBSC data are based on self-reports through survey responses obtained in a school setting. The questionnaire used for collection of the HBSC data has undergone piloting on a national and cross-national basis to ensure it is a validated and reliable measurement tool [28]. PISA educational assessment scores for maths, reading and science are based on standardised, primarily computer-based, tests undertaken in a school-based setting.

For domain 1 (Good health and optimum nutrition), physical activity was assessed using a single item from HBSC and defined as participation in leisure time vigorous physical activity (VPA) 2 or more times per week [29]. The most recent WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour recommend children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 should undertake VPA at least three days per week. However, since older survey cycles used a ‘two to three times a week’ classification, the value for VPA was set at ‘two days or more’ for this analysis to enable comparison across survey years.

For domain 2 (Connectedness, positive values and contribution to society), the HBSC perceived family support measure was used. This is a subscale of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, which includes four items on different aspects of family support: getting help, receiving emotional support, being able to talk about problems and willingness to help with decision making [30]. Adolescents rank their agreement with the four questions on a scale of 1 to 7, with a mean score calculated to create the high family support indicator, defined as a mean score of ≥5.5 across the four questions.

For domain 3 (Safety and a supportive environment), exposure to bullying was calculated from the HBSC bullying victimisation scale, adapted from Olweus (1996) [31]. Adolescents were asked how often they had been bullied at school in the past couple of months. A cut-off of '2 to 3 times a month or more' was used to reflect a regular pattern of exposure and the risks this carries to physical and mental health [32].

For domain 4 (Learning, competence, education, skills and employability) we used the PISA assessment in mathematics, reading and science. The measures focus not only on basic proficiency but also on the ability to reflect and evaluate knowledge before applying the gathered skills to real-world problems [33]. A mean score was computed for each subject based on 10 questions completed by students with total scores ranging from 0 to 850.

Domain 5 (Agency and resilience) was represented by a single item from HBSC which asks about the extent to which adolescents feel pressured by schoolwork (not at all/a little/some/a lot). It is a key indicator for understanding resilience in schoolchildren, while also being indicative of overall school experience [34]. Responses were categorised into a binary indicator defined by “some/a lot” of schoolwork pressure vs. “not at all/a little” schoolwork pressure.

Pandemic period

The pandemic period was the primary exposure under review, the pre-pandemic survey cycle 2018 was used as the baseline year and compared to the change which occurred in the post-pandemic period utilising the survey cycle 2022.

Socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic status (SES) was calculated differently for the two datasets. PISA utilises the composite index of Economic, Social and Cultural Status (ESCS) throughout its questionnaires, gathering data from both students and parents across parental educational attainment, parental occupation status and household income to formulate the composite measure [35]. HBSC utilises the Family Affluence Scale (FAS) to analyse inequality, a scale that has been internationally validated over 25 years and is now widely used. The most recent iteration of this scale, FAS-III, asks adolescents six questions ascertaining their family affluence, such as owning vehicles, going on holiday or having their own bedroom [36].

Number of days of school closures due to COVID-19

The number of COVID-19 school closure days were obtained from the WHO PHSM (Public Health and Social Measures in Response to COVID-19) database severity index for all WHO European region countries included in the analysis [37]. Only days with a national-scale Level 4 school closure, “suspension of in-person teaching on all levels”, were counted. The recorded number of school closure days in the 40 countries included in the model ranged from 0, in Finland, to 341, in Italy, with a median number of 115.5 days across the region.

Of note, this classification led to some countries showing very low numbers of school closure days due to variations in levels of restrictions across institutions or subnational regions. For example, Finland is represented with 0 school closure days in this measure, as early childhood education facilities remained open throughout the pandemic, while elements of primary, secondary and tertiary in-person teaching in the country were suspended for up to 57 days. Despite its limitations, using the WHO PHSM database ensured a consistent classification of school closures across all included countries. Alternative methods were considered, particularly self-reported school principal data through the PISA survey. However, this data showed extreme variability within countries, while country level averages showed high discrepancy from the WHO data, for example in Italy, where 341 days from the WHO PHSM database were contrasted by an average of 111 days reported by school principals through PISA.

Data analysis

Preliminary analysis reviewing summary statistics for each outcome variable was undertaken, specifically the frequency and percentage of binary variables and the mean and standard deviation of continuous variables, for 2018 and 2022.

Descriptive analysis looking at the percentage change between the pre-pandemic (2018) and post-pandemic (2022) period for each indicator by country was initially explored. These are further compared to the 2015 and 2014 study cycles for PISA and HBSC respectively to assess longer-term trends and whether there has been any acute change in indicators over the pandemic period. The findings were presented in bar plots. The 2018 to 2022 trend data is furthermore disaggregated according to socioeconomic status. Both ESCS and FAS were used to create quintiles within respective countries, allowing for a visualisation of the changes experienced in the 20% of the population with the lowest socioeconomic status compared to the 20% of the population with the highest socioeconomic status, showcasing changes between these two groups over time.

Regression models were used to understand whether the differences observed in well-being outcomes between 2018 and 2022 were statistically significant, with 2018 serving as the baseline reference year. Logistic fixed-effects regression models were used to analyse the binary outcome variables from the HBSC data for domains 1,2,3 and 5 while linear fixed-effect regression models were employed for PISA assessment scores in Domain 4.

A single variable model was initially employed for each of the investigated indicators looking at the observed change between 2018 and 2022. Fixed effect models were incorporated with country as a clustering variable but not as a fixed effect, this allowed us to control for country-specific clustering. For logistic regression models, coefficients were exponentiated and presented as odds ratios (ORs) to facilitate interpretation of the association between survey year and each binary outcome. For the linear models, coefficients were expressed as mean differences in outcome scores, representing the average change in PISA assessment scores between 2018 and 2022.

An interaction term representing SES was then incorporated into each model to understand the changing association of SES on well-being indicators between 2018 and 2022. Where indicators were from HBSC data, the FAS scale was used to represent SES, for PISA data the ESCS variable was used. A further model for each indicator included the number of school closure days in each country divided by 30 (allowing for a predictor per month of COVID-19 closure) and this model also included year as a variable. Since there were no COVID-19 closures in 2018, the resulting model effectively captures the effect of school closures on the differences seen between pre- and post-pandemic results. Multi-collinearity was checked through use of the Variance inflation factor for each fixed-effects regression model. Although some models incorporating the FAS interaction did have a VIF slightly above 10, a higher threshold was accepted due to the expected multi-collinearity from interaction terms. Q-Q plots were used to check for normality of residuals for linear fixed-effect regression models.

The primary model examining the pandemic period did not adjust for confounding variables beyond the clustering across countries, as its purpose was to assess whether there was a significant change in well-being during this time, rather than to establish a causal link with the pandemic itself. However, when specifically analysing the impact of the 30-day COVID-19 closure, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) was used to identify potential confounders. Age was the only identified confounder as it was associated with both the outcome variables of interest and the exposure of COVID-19 closure days. Age was effectively controlled for by design, as surveys across both years included adolescents of the same age. Furthermore, a country-wide measure was used for the number of COVID-19 closure days, therefore this measure did not vary by age.

Results

Descriptive analysis of changes in adolescent well-being across the pandemic period

Domain 1: good health and optimum nutrition

Compared to pre-pandemic levels in 2018, the percentage of children reporting having engaged in vigorous physical activity slightly increased in 2022, from 71.0% to 74.0% in 2022 (Table 2). This slight increase was observed in the majority of countries, 25 out of 37 (67.6%) (Fig. 1a). However, besides one outlier, Malta, which experienced a reduction of 51.0%, all results, both positive and negative, were under 10% signifying that no major changes in physical activity occurred over the pandemic period. This is in keeping with trends seen from 2014 to 2018, where only small changes (in both directions) were observed across countries. Similarly, when examining the inequity gap no consistent pattern emerges. Domain 2: connectedness, positive values and contribution to society

The percentage of adolescents experiencing high family support decreased from 71.3% in 2018 to 67.2% in 2022 (Table 2). This observation was relatively consistent across countries, as 32 out of 36 countries with available results (88.9%) experienced a decrease, with more than a third of countries seeing a drop of 10% or more (Fig. 1b). This decreasing trend was not evident in the 2014 to 2018 survey cycles, with 17 (56.0%) of countries experiencing an increase in high family support during this period. For the majority of countries (61.1%), those from the lowest quintile saw a greater decrease in family support between 2018 and 2022 (Fig. 2b).

Domain 3: safety and a supportive environment

The percentage of children experiencing bullying remained relatively similar in 2022 (10.8%) in comparison to 2018 (10.3%) (Table 2). 27 out of 36 countries (75.0%) reported increases compared to pre-pandemic levels from 2018. This trend was not apparent when comparing the 2014 and 2018 survey cycles, where an overall decrease was observed. Those from the most affluent 20% experienced a greater increase in exposure to bullying during the pandemic period in 28 out of the 36 countries (77.8%) compared to the least affluent 20% (Fig. 2c).

Domain 4: learning, competence, education, skills and employability

Two of the key proficiencies in educational development in adolescents, mathematic reasoning and reading comprehension saw strong decreases during the pandemic period across the WHO European region. Mathematics assessment scores decreased from 476.8 in 2018 to 461.6 in 2022 and reading assessment scores decreased from 468.5 in 2018 to 455.9 in 2022 (Table 2). Mathematics assessment scores fell in all except one country, Kazakhstan, of the 38 (97.4%) with available trends for the pandemic period, while reading assessment scores decreased in all except 4 out of 38 countries (89.5%), i.e. Italy, Israel, Serbia and Romania. This is not in keeping with trends observed prior to the pandemic, with more variability seen in countries between 2015 and 2018 in these two assessment categories. Conversely, assessment scores in the sciences did not demonstrate a clear trend, with 20 out of 38 countries (52.6%) showing a decrease in their results.

These decreases during the pandemic period appear to have been particularly driven by poorer performance in adolescents from the lowest socioeconomic quintile (Fig. 2d–f). For maths as well as science scores, this pattern presented itself in 33 out of 38 countries (86.8%), while for reading it was still pronounced with 27 out of 38 countries (71.1%) showing the lowest quintile experiencing a greater negative change.

Domain 5: agency and resilience

Pressure exerted on students by schoolwork showed marked increases across the WHO Europe region. In 2018, 36.1% of adolescents reported feeling pressured by schoolwork, increasing to 44.2% in 2022. This increase in schoolwork pressure was seen in 33 out of 37 countries (89.2%), with only Malta, Albania, Lithuania and Finland experiencing reductions (Fig. 1g). There has however been a longer-term trend in this increase, with 27 out of the available 34 countries (79.4%) experiencing an increase in the previous reporting period between 2014 and 2018. Increases observed between 2018 and 2022 were more pronounced in the most deprived quintile in 26 out of 37 countries (70.3%) (Fig. 2g).

Changes in adolescent well-being by socioeconomic status between the pre- and post-pandemic period (2018–2022)

The association of socioeconomic status, as measured by the FAS scale, remained constant between 2018 and 2022 for well-being indicators from domains 1,2,3 and 5. Inequities did grow in the pandemic period when looking at educational attainment measures in domain 4. Socioeconomic status as measured by ESCS did have a greater association in 2022 compared to 2018 for maths, reading and science. However, a significant change was seen only for maths (coef: 2.912; 95%CI: 0.348 to 5.475) and science (coef: 4.622; 95%CI: 1.793 to 7.450), but not reading (coef: 2.205; 95%CI: 0.408 to 4.817).

Association of 30 days of school closure on the change in adolescent well-being (2018–2022)

Overall, 30 days of school closure did not have a clear differential association on the change in well-being indicators from 2018 to 2022. There was no quantifiable association between 30 days of COVID-19 school closure and the odds of adolescents engaging in vigorous physical activity between 2018 and 2022, with the OR being close to 1 (OR: 0.999; 95% CI: 0.958 to 1.042; p-value: 0.971). The odds of receiving high family support decreased by 2.6%, being bullied increased by 2.9% and being pressured by schoolwork decreased by 3.5% for every 30 days of school closure. However, these results were not statistically significant at the 5% level (Table 3). A negative association was observed between 30-day increments of school closure and performance in maths, reading and science assessments, however, again, these observed effects were not statistically significant at the 5% level.

Discussion

Using contemporary data from two international school surveys, across 40 European countries, we have found that adolescent well-being (across the domains of the UN H6+ framework) decreased after the pandemic (2018–2022).

The HBSC and PISA datasets were used in combination to explore their utility for this purpose and to raise awareness of their existence and complementarity among a multi-professional audience in health, where HBSC is rooted, and education, where PISA is rooted. There were a multitude of indicators in both datasets that could be mapped to each of the UN H6+ framework domains, and the choices made by the team were pragmatic to showcase a variety of aspects of well-being. Existing literature also contributed to the choices, where pandemic publications could be found on each topic from the European region. Going forward, the curators of these surveys could reflect upon the holistic nature of well-being, and create relevant questions where gaps exist, particularly in Domain 5, agency and resilience. Working in an intersectoral way, data from two major datasets such as HBSC and PISA provide a holistic view of adolescent well-being.

The decrease in well-being was seen across four of the five domains, while the indicator within Domain 1 (physical activity) showed an increase, and statistical significance was shown for all changes throughout the pandemic period apart from Domain 3 (exposure to bullying). Changes in well-being were not equally distributed between and within countries. Decreases in the domains of connectedness (family support) and agency (pressure from schoolwork) were experienced more by those from the most deprived backgrounds, whereas a poorer sense of safety and security (increased bullying) was reported by those from the least deprived backgrounds.

Adolescents are receiving less family support, feeling under more pressure from schoolwork, and are performing worse in reading and maths in comparison to the pre-pandemic analysis. Adolescents are, however, engaging in slightly more vigorous physical activity compared to the pre-pandemic period. Although this result differs from previous findings in the literature [[16], [17], [18]], possible explanations for the increase in physical activity include engagement in outdoor play and unstructured activities outside the school environment [38]. Trends in feeling pressured from schoolwork were present prior to the pandemic with an increase also observed between 2014 and 2018; this finding is consistent with existing literature [39]. Trends which were not observed in the pre-pandemic period were the decrease in high family support and decrease in maths and reading scores. Due to the pandemic, distance learning was widely implemented. While some studies have found that the pandemic led to a decline in all forms of bullying [23,40], others have reported an increasing trend in countries with fewer social restrictions, such as limited school closures [41,42]. This is consistent with the findings of the analysis, which showed non-statistically significant changes in bullying exposure between the pre- and post-pandemic period.

The most significant change in socioeconomic inequity was seen in PISA maths and science scores (Domain 4, education), with adolescents from the most deprived backgrounds experiencing a more pronounced decrease in scores than their more affluent peers between 2018 and 2022. Adolescents from the least affluent 20% also experienced a greater increase in feelings of pressure from schoolwork and a decrease in high family support when compared to the most affluent 20%. However, previous research had found that family support declined more during the pandemic among adolescents from high SES backgrounds compared to those from lower SES [43]. A possible explanation for our findings is that low SES parents, due to external circumstances, tend to be less involved in their children's education, which may in turn also contribute to increased academic stress among adolescents from those backgrounds [44]. Adolescents from the most affluent 20% experienced a greater increase in exposure to bullying while at school. However, when examining the absolute numbers and previous literature, young people from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds continue to be at a higher risk of experiencing bullying [45].

The impact of the duration of school closures did not appear to explain trends observed in adolescent well-being. There are several reasons why this may have occurred; firstly, the bluntness of the measurement used from the WHO data – which did not reflect within-country variability of school closures. Additionally, other school-based measures as well as national pandemic-related policies may have also impacted upon adolescent well-being, with differential impact on young people depending on their circumstance. The exposure of school closures and other measures impacted every country differently, making it more difficult to disentangle the extent to which school closures themselves impacted the results.

Strengths

Our study benefitted from the combination of two major international datasets, allowing for both a significant coverage of the WHO European region, as well as holistically exploring adolescent well-being through the availability of a breadth of indicators to allow for mapping across the UN H6+ framework. The reliability of the data used in the analysis is provided by these two studies’ robust data collection methods, which have been refined over more than 20 years of regular study cycles. Further insight into the trend data and widening inequalities was provided through both studies utilising validated and continuously improved upon scales as markers for socioeconomic status, FAS for HBSC and ESCS for PISA respectively.

The use of the UN H6+ framework allowed for holistic consideration of the important connection between adolescent well-being, health and educational experience. Utilising two large-scale datasets to evidence adolescent well-being across the WHO European region in these domains allowed us to illustrate the wider factors that contribute to exam performance and school achievement, as well as the potential unintended consequences of necessary pandemic-related policies in both the short and long-term for adolescents.

Limitations

Both datasets obtain their results in a cross-sectional manner every three, for PISA, and every four years, for HBSC, not allowing to follow the progress of individual students throughout their school experience. Further analysing this data in aggregated trends opens any interpretation to ecological fallacy, especially pertinent in the individuality of every student's school experience and well-being throughout their adolescence.

Measurements obtained in the 2022 study cycles occurred after pandemic-related school closures and nationwide lockdowns. This means our results were not able to explore the well-being of students during school closures, but only the evolution of their well-being thereafter. Furthermore, even after re-opening of schools, various combinations of restrictive infection control measures, present in some countries until mid-2022, such as splitting classes into cohorts, testing regimens and mask mandates continued to severely impact student's lives and school experiences. The potential impact of these measures and a comparison to full school closures was not possible with the datasets and tools available as part of this analysis.

While utilising the WHO PHSM index to determine national-scale Level 4 school closures allowed for reliable quantification of radical closures, it may not have truly represented the different approaches taken by countries in tackling the pandemic, not allowing any nuance to regional differences or specific measures or policies adopted by governments concerning adolescents. Furthermore, specific school types of interest, in this case secondary schools, may have been underrepresented in our measure, where countries did not officially declare a national-scale closure of all school types.

The 2022 PISA examination cycle saw mathematics return as a main area of focus for the first time since 2012, leading to a re-assessment of the contents of the examination and changing key components of the framework, additionally being the first reassessment of the mathematics framework since PISA made computer-based assessment the primary delivery method of testing mathematical literacy, though some countries remained with a paper-based version. While this is a regular and thorough process undertaken throughout the history of PISA assessments, it cannot be excluded that these changes may have introduced some variability to the maths results seen in 2022. All variables used via HBSC in Domains 1,2,3 and 5 were self-reported by adolescents which increases the potential for bias (e.g. recall, social desirability). However, self-report also allows adolescents to genuinely report on their own feelings and behaviours which is essential for gaining a holistic understanding of young people's experiences.

It is important to note that a causal model was not used for this analysis; therefore, it is not possible to attribute the decrease in well-being observed in this period to specific factors. Additionally, when examining the impact of the 30-day COVID-19 closure measure, residual confounders are likely present that are impacting on the observed estimates. It is also important to highlight that logistic regression models were used for the analysis of binary outcome variables due to non-convergence of log-binomial models. Where prevalence of outcomes are high, the odds may be inflated when comparing to relative risk estimates [46].

Policy implications

Well-being and education are mutually reinforcing [3], so it comes as no surprise that these complementary datasets show consistent negative trends for most indicators across the five domains of the UN H6+ model. School is more than a place to learn; it is also, for example, a place for social connection with peers and trusted adults, and a source of welfare services (including school meals). We need to make sure that our schools, especially in the wake of the pandemic and the ever-present threat of another health crisis, form the foundation of healthy lives [2]. The interesting, not necessarily intuitive, findings in this study suggest that physical activity may have increased while schools were closed. Further studies need to explore what the nature of that activity was, whether the observed change was a “bounce back” effect following lifting of national restrictions, and how we can learn from this to sustain those activity increases in our schools – not least to counter the increasing trends in obesity among our children and youth.

The education scores – and most worryingly, the widening inequities observed - threaten the longer-term employment and economic opportunities for adolescents in low socioeconomic groups and the other health benefits that accrue from a good education [4]. The need to deploy effective catch-up measures for those who are most affected by education losses must include intersectoral efforts to also promote wider well-being and not simply focus on educational outcomes. One of the hypotheses for the post-pandemic increasing pressure of schoolwork is that adolescents, particularly from low SES backgrounds, could not benefit from the continuity of remote learning. They lacked the space in their homes, the technology or connectivity, and family support to engage; therefore, when schools reopened, their losses were greater. Using intersectoral partnerships, working in parallel on the social determinants of health - and of learning – is necessary for their recovery [47,48].

These datasets are useful for policy making by education system managers, human resources specialists, and health and education professionals themselves. They can also mobilise civil society, including parents and communities - and empower adolescents themselves to shape their own future.

Future research

Since PISA and HBSC are not longitudinal surveys but rather repeated cross-sectional studies, a key limitation is that different cohorts of participants are surveyed each time, preventing the tracking of individual changes over time. Therefore, we suggest that future research seeking to explore adolescent well-being more comprehensively adopt a longitudinal design based on Ross et al.'s framework. Domain 5, concerning agency and resilience, would particularly benefit from additional focus in the design of both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, as our understanding of what constitutes adolescent resilience and the consequences for both learning and developmental behaviours grows. We recommend that future studies incorporate validated resilience measures. For instance, the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) includes sample items such as “Able to adapt to change” and “Tend to bounce back after illness or hardship” [49,50]. Both the educational and health communities would benefit from allowing adolescents to reflect on their autonomy, adaptability and coping mechanisms, in everyday life, as well as during exceptional circumstances, like a global health emergency.

While this study explored pre- and post-pandemic trends across the domains of adolescent well-being, as well as attempting to connect these with the most severe form of pandemic-related intervention, full school closures, we were neither able to examine the impact, nor the effectiveness, of individual control measures within schools. Further research into the effects of full school closures, their appropriate duration, as well as the more variable infection control measures in use at schools, which still carried the potential for significant disruption to adolescent lives, is required by both educational and health literature. While this analysis was looking at a discrete period of time, future research could look at longer time trends using interrupted time series analysis methods which may be useful to further understand the differential impact of the pandemic period.

We noted that some countries did not follow the negative trend for specific indicators. It would be beneficial to further explore these cases at country level. This approach may make it possible to match data relating to educational policies with data relating to the well-being of pupils. We could also group countries into clusters which have similar educational policies. These efforts could lead to identifying the educational practices that have a positive influence on well-being in crisis situations, whatever their origin.

Conclusion

Our paper showed that adolescent well-being decreased across the WHO European region during the pandemic period, with the greatest decline seen in education outcomes for adolescents from the most deprived backgrounds. Leveraging existing health and education datasets supports an improved understanding of the impacts felt by students and enables an intersectoral approach to support those who have fallen behind. Utilising this data is crucial for informing decisions about future pandemic or emergency responses, including weighing up the benefits of school closures against their consequences for student learning and overall well-being.
