Total ablative stereotactic body radiotherapy to primary and oligometastatic renal cell carcinoma in medically inoperable cases: An institutional analysis
Authors: Shyam Singh Bisht, Tejinder Kataria, Sorun Shishak, Deepak Gupta, Venkatesan Kaliyaperumal, Jyoti Wadhwa, Manav Suryavanshi, Susovan Banerjee, Kushal Narang, Shina Goyal, Deepak Rathi, Mayur Mayank, Rajesh Selvaraj, Anubhav Khandelwal
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has been found to be an effective and safe modality with excellent oncological outcome in medically inoperable primary renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and oligometastases. There is scarcity of data on the synchronous delivery of SBRT to primary and oligometastatic RCC in patients unfit for nephrectomy. Here, we report the findings of a retrospective study of prospectively collected data on “total ablative SBRT.”Methods:Oligometastatic RCC patients with intact primary tumors were enrolled between May 2021 and June 2022. SBRT was synchronously delivered to the primary tumor and metastases. Demographics, treatment, oncologic outcomes, and toxicity were assessed. Kaplan–Meier estimates were generated for oncologic outcomes. The primary endpoint of this study was feasibility and tolerability.Results:Eleven patients were enrolled between May 2021 and June 2022. One patient died at 2 months after SBRT due to viral pneumonitis (possibly COVID pneumonia). Nine patients (82%) had metastatic disease, while 2 (18%) were stage II. The average maximal diameter of primary was 68.7 mm (range, 23–128 mm). The SBRT doses for primary and metastasis ranged from 40 to 55 Gray (Gy) in 5 to 7 fractions and 22 to 40Gy in 2 to 5 fractions, respectively. The median follow-up period was 10.5 months (Range: 4–15 months). Response assessment was available for ten patients. Local control, marginal control, regional control and initial oligometastatic control (OMC) rates were 100%. OMC declined to 87.5% as one patient had recurrence in irradiated subcarinal lymphnode at 7 months. The metastatic control rate was 80% and loco-regional progression-free survival was 8 months (range, 4–15 months). Toxicities were minimal and manageable. At the last follow–up, 7 of 11 patients were alive with an overall survival of 63.5%. Six patients received systemic therapy after SBRT.Conclusions:Synchronous delivery of SBRT to primary and oligometastatic sites in patients unfit for nephrectomy was feasible and tolerable with good locoregional control. The total ablative SBRT strategy needs to be explored in similar cohorts.

INTRODUCTION

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the eighth most common cancer worldwide and accounts for approximately 90% of all kidney malignancies.[1,2] Incidence of localized cancer has risen, probably due to increased access to abdominal imaging. Both radical nephrectomy (RN) and partial nephrectomy, also known as nephron-sparing surgery (NSS) are accepted standards of care. EORTC 30904 trial, which randomized patients with RCC ≤5 cm to RN versus NSS showed that after exclusion of patients with positive surgical margins, NSS was associated with equivalent overall survival (OS) compared with RN.[3] For older patients particularly those with pre-existing comorbidities, high risk for anesthesia and chronic kidney disease, nonsurgical treatments are offered including radiofrequency ablation and cryotherapy.[4] These techniques provide lower survival and have significant limitations when compared to partial nephrectomy.[5] These techniques typically can treat only smaller lesions located away from the ureter and vascular structures because of the risk of heat-sink effects and stricture and/or fistula development.[6] The safety and effectiveness of stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) have been proven in oligometastatic RCC lesions.[7-9] SBRT to primary kidney tumor has also been found to be an effective and safe modality showing excellent oncological outcome in medically inoperable patients with localized cancers regardless of the location and size of tumor.[10,11] SBRT has been recommended as a category 2B treatment option for stage I kidney cancer in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. Studies on SBRT are mainly restricted to oligometastatic patients in the postnephrectomy setting or to primary tumor in nonmetastatic cohort with comorbidities. The indications of debulking nephrectomy in metastatic patients has undergone refinement.[12-14] However, there are many oligometastatic patients who are medically unfit for debulking surgery/chemotherapy and their optimal management is unclear.

It has also been observed that complete eradication of tumor burden with SBRT was associated with better survival in patients with oligometastatic RCC.[15] There is scarcity of data on the synchronous delivery of SBRT to primary and oligometastatic sites.

In this pilot study, the initial experience of SBRT directed at primary RCC and oligometastatic sites, referred as ‘total ablative SBRT,’ is reported The primary endpoint was tolerability (defined as no Grade III or above toxicity) and feasibility (able to complete SBRT to primary and metastatic sites without >7 days of gap).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical details of prospectively collected eleven patients between May 2021 and June 2022 were analyzed. The inclusion criteria of the study were patients being unfit for surgery or oligometastatic (up to three metastases) RCC with life expectancy >6 months, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Score (ECOG PS) 0-3, and patient’s refusal for surgery. Medical inoperability was defined by the referring urologist after the pre-anesthesia workup. All cases were discussed in the multi-disciplinary tumor meeting Written informed consent was obtained from all patients explaining the low level of evidence for “total ablative treatment.” Sixteen patients were cleared for “Total ablative SBRT” approach, out of which only eleven returned for treatment.

Respiration-induced target motion assessment was done before the simulation scan. If the motion of the tumor was more than 1 cm than either fiducial tracking with Cyberknife-VSI (Accuray®) or deep inspiratory breath hold (DIBH) (Elekta) with Active Breathing Co-Ordinator strategy was employed. Each patient was immobilized in the supine position with Vacloc (Vacloc, Medtek, Orange City, IA). Radiotherapy planning computed tomography (CT) scans (1 mm slice thickness) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abdomen (when feasible) were acquired. Gross tumor volume (GTV) was defined as the visible extent of the tumor on the planning CT image. An internal target margin was added to the GTV to account for residual excursion when treated in DIBH. Finally, a planning target volume (PTV) margin of 5 mm was added to account for setup uncertainty. After performing radiation planning CT scan, secondary exclusion was considered as a broad area of contact with bowel loops where delivering biologically effective dose (BED) of more than >72 Gray (Gy) with alpha/beta value of 10 (Gy10) was not possible without exceeding bowel constraints. The prescribed dose was selected based on the highest dose within dose constraints of the organ at risk. The aim was to deliver BED of more than 72Gy10 in a maximum of 5 to 7 fractions to primary tumor. Different fractionations used with BED of more than 72Gy10 were 40Gy in 5 fractions (BED 72Gy10), 45Gy in 5 fractions (BED 85.5 Gy10), 50Gy in 5 fractions (BED 100 Gy10), 55Gy in 5 fractions (BED 115.5 Gy10) and 49Gy in 7 fractions (BED 83.3 Gy10). Dose was prescribed to 80% isodose line encompassing PTV with at least 95% of PTV volume receiving 100% of the dose using 6 MV X-rays. The SBRT plan was created with a radiotherapy planning system (Monaco, Elekta or IDMS, Accuray Inc.,) and delivered using Elekta Synergy-S or Cyberknife-VSI.

SBRT was delivered synchronously to the primary kidney tumor and oligometastases. Patients with at least 6-month follow-up after SBRT were evaluated. After SBRT, patients were referred to a medical oncologist for systemic therapy but only six patients agreed to systemic therapy/immunotherapy.

The response was assessed 3–4 months after SBRT with follow-up CT scans with or without positron emission tomography (PET), according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Metabolic response was also assessed when PET-CT was available. Local control (LC), oligometastatic control (OMC), marginal control (MC), regional control (RC), locoregional control (LRC), locoregional progression-free survival (LR-PFS), OS, and toxicities were assessed. LC was defined as freedom from local progression, OMC was defined as freedom from ablated oligometastatic progression, MC was defined as freedom from recurrence in the area beyond the PTVs but within the PTV plus one cm and RC was defined as freedom from recurrence beyond PTV plus 1 cm and freedom from new regional lymph node metastases (renal hilum and para-aortic region). LRC was defined as a combination of LC, MC, and RC. LR-PFS was defined as the time from the start day of SBRT to any instance of local/marginal/regional recurrence. OS was defined as the time from the start of SBRT to death from any cause. Toxicity was scored according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v5). Time-to-event outcomes were calculated from the 1st day of SBRT to the day on which an event was confirmed. Kaplan–Meier estimates were generated for oncologic outcomes. Descriptive analysis was used to describe demographic, patient and tumor characteristics. Central tendency (Median) was used to described target volumes, doses and standardized uptake value (SUV) of tumor.

RESULTS

The patient and tumor characteristics are shown in Tables 1 and 2. Eleven patients (9 males and 2 females) were treated at our institute between May 2021 and June 2022 with the intent of total ablative therapy. One patient died at 2 months after SBRT due to viral pneumonitis (possibly COVID pneumonia) and was thus excluded from response analysis. The median age was 69 years (range, 46–84 years). All patients had at least one comorbidity (diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, stroke, postmyocardial infarction, deep venous thrombosis, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic renal disease, and morbid obesity) and 8 patients (72.7%) had 2 or more comorbid conditions. seven patients (63.6%) had ECOG Performance Status of 2 and four patients (36.4%) had performance status of one. The symptoms at presentation were flank pain (n = 5, 45%), backache (n = 3, 30%), and hematuria (n = 2, 20%). One patient presented with seizures and two with motor weakness. Majority (n = 9, 82%) had clear cell histology, remaining 2 (18%) had papillary RCC. The average maximal diameter was 68.7 mm (range, 23–128 mm) with mean SUV 10.4 (range, 5.4–22). Nine patients (82%) had metastatic disease, while 2 (18%) had stage II disease. Both stage II patients were medically inoperable due to multiple comorbidities. Metastatic sites were brain (n = 3), skeletal (n = 4), lymph nodes (mediastinal = 1, para-aortic = 2, aortocaval = 2), and adrenal (n = 1). Among the 9 metastatic patients, 7 were International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) Intermediate risk and 2 were poor risk disease. SBRT was delivered by linear accelerator with DIBH motion management in 7 cases and by CyberKnife-VSI (Accuray, Sunnyvale) in 4 cases.

Target dose characteristics for primary tumor and oligometastatic lesion are shown in Table 3. Representative plans with pre and post-SBRT images are shown in Figures 1 and 2. The delivered SBRT doses for kidney tumor ranged from 40 to 55Gy in 5 to 7 fractions (40Gy/5 fractions, BED 72 Gy10 in 2 patients; 49Gy/7 fractions, BED 83.3 Gy10 in one patient; 45Gy/5 fractions, BED 85.5Gy10 in 2 patients; 50Gy/5 fractions, BED 100Gy10 in 3 patients, and 55Gy/5 fractions, BED 115.5Gy10 in 2 patients). The mean GTV volume was 118.3 cc (16–377cc) and mean PTV volume was 202.91 cc (45–500 cc). Contact of PTV with bowel was seen in 7 patients (point contact in 4 cases, large than 5mm contact in 3 cases) and average of maximum bowel dose was 31.8Gy (5.4–48Gy). The mean target volume coverage was 96%. The median of the mean PTV and GTV doses was 48.6 Gy and 51.2Gy, respectively. The delivered dose to brain metastasis was 30Gy in 3 fractions, bone metastasis received 22–30 Gy in 2–3 fractions, lymph node metastasis 40 Gy in 5 fractions, and adrenal glands received 40 Gy in 5 fractions. The median overall treatment duration was 8 days (range: 5–14 days). SBRT was delivered on consecutive days except in one patient where it was performed every alternate day. The median follow-up period for all evaluable patients (n = 10) was 10.5 months (Range: 4–15 months). At the time of data cutoff, three patients had died and only one patient died from metastatic disease (IMDC poor risk). One patient died of myocardial infarction, and another died due to COVID pneumonitis. All ten patients were assessed for response at ablative sites (primary and oligometastatic sites) as per RECIST criteria. At primary site, best response was partial response in four patients (40%) and stable disease in 6 patients (60%) which was maintained at last follow-up scan. In eight oligometastatic patient, metastatic site SBRT resulted in best response as complete response in two (2 out of 8), partial response in three patients, stable disease in three patients. In one patient, disease progression was noted at subcarinal node at 7 months, after an initial stable response. Pattern of metabolic responses at primary sites is shown in Table 4. All ten patients underwent baseline PET-CT scan and only eight had posttreatment PET-CT scans. More than 50% decrease in maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) was observed in 4 patients including one complete metabolic response and <50% decrease in SUVmax was observed in remaining four patients. None had increase in metabolic activity in irradiated kidney tumor. LC, initial OMC, and MC at a median follow-up of 10.5 months were 100%. OMC declined to 87.5% as one patient had local recurrence in irradiated subcarinal lymph node at 7 months (dose of 40 Gy in five fractions planned). The same patient was also detected to have progression at distant sites (right occipital, mediastinal lymph node and bilateral lungs). One patient developed pelvic bone metastasis. Thus, the regional and metastatic control rates were 100% and 80% (2 of 10 failure), respectively, and LR-PFS was 8 months [range, 4–15 months, Figure 3].

At a median follow-up of 10.5 months, 7 patients were alive resulting in an OS of 70%. Six cases received systemic therapy (Pazopanib = 3, Cabozantinib + Nivolumab = 1, Pembrolizumab + Axitinib = 1, Everolimus + Lenvatinib = 1). Toxicities were reported in two patients, one patient with grade I diarrhea, abdominal pain with grade II nausea, who received 49Gy in 7 fractions (BED 83Gy) due to large tumor volume (500 cc). Another patient with grade 1 nausea received 45Gy in five fractions (BED 85.5Gy). The median creatinine level at baseline and atthe last follow-up was 1.6 (Range: 0.9–3.7, mg/dL) and 2.1 (0.8–4.2, mg/dL), respectively. The median GFR before and after SBRT were available only for 3 patients.

DISCUSSION

RCC is the most common type of adult kidney cancer and the incidence is increasing. Historically, there were disappointing results of preoperative and postoperative radiation in RCC.[16] This is because of the poor results of conventional radiation due to inappropriate doses, primitive techniques and most importantly radio-resistance of tumor cells to standard fractionation.[17] This resistant can be overcome by the ablative dose of radiation leading to endothelial damage and activation of ceramide pathway.[18] Hence, there appears to be a renaissance of radiotherapy for RCC with the use of SBRT. The first report of SBRT targeting primary RCC was published in 2003.[19] Subsequently, there has been an increased utilization of SBRT for primary RCC with excellent LC.[20-22] There have been numerous publications on the effectiveness of SBRT in postnephrectomy oligometastatic setting.[23-26] It has also been used to delay or defer systemic therapy in some low and intermediate IMDC risk groups.[27] However, such publications are mainly confined to postnephrectomy patients.

The current study was carried out in oligometastatic cohort with intact kidney cancer, medically unfit for debulking surgery. The authors tested the feasibility of the concept, “total ablative therapy,” i.e., SBRT to both primary tumor and oligometastatic sites followed by systemic therapy when suitable. The primary endpoint was tolerability and feasibility. This approach was feasible as there were no unplanned break during the delivery of SBRT. Patients received synchronous or sequential (two patients) delivery of SBRT to oligometastatic site including primary tumor, and all completed total ablative therapy in median of 8 days (Range: 5–14 days). Despite tumors as big as 12 cm, tolerability was good as none of the patients developed grade III or higher toxicity. The possible reasons for low toxicity may be small sample size, careful case selection and excluding patients with large area of contact with intestine to reduce the bowel dose. One patient could not complete the first follow-up and died due to viral pneumonitis, possibly COVID pneumonitis during pandemic and was excluded from the response evaluation. Majority of kidney SBRT series have reported very low rates of toxicities.[11,28,29] The International Radiosurgery Oncology Consortium for Kidney (IROCK) published a thorough meta-analysis in 2022. They showed good LC with manageable toxic effects. There were no grade 3 toxic effects or treatment-related deaths. One (1%) patient developed an acute grade 4 duodenal ulcer and late grade 4 gastritis.[30] Tang et al. prospectively tested the feasibility and efficacy of SBRT in all oligometastatic sites and none received systemic therapy. Only One patient had local failure and median progression-free survival was 22.7 month.[27]

We evaluated the kidney motion before simulation and adopted different motion management strategies such as abdominal compression, DIBH, and tumor tracking with CyberKnife. Setup for oligometastatic lesions was based on the location of the lesion. Three patients had brain metastasis and underwent CyberKnife-based frameless stereotactic radiosurgery. CT scan done in the nephrogenic phase was used for delineation of renal GTV but in patients with deranged renal parameters, MRI abdomen and PET-CT scan was utilized. The rationale of using SBRT in RCC is the lower α/β ratio, although many studies have used a value of α/β10. In our dose comparison, we applied the α/β ratio value of 10, although many preclinical studies have assumed an α/β ratio for renal cell cancer of 2.6–6.9 Gy.[31] In the current study, for primary tumor dose prescription we applied risk adaptive fractionation strategy taking into consideration primary tumor size, proximity to bowel (no contact, focal contact of <5 mm or broad contact) and location of the lesion. In one patient, SBRT was delivered in seven fractions due to a very large tumor size (12 cm) and larger area of contact with the bowel (3 cm). Most of the studies have treated smaller lesions. The average maximal diameter of the primary tumor in our study was 68.7 mm (range, 23–128 mm). Several retrospective and a few prospective studies have assessed SBRT for primary renal cancer. However, treatment doses lack consistency, with a range from 12 to 60Gy in 1 to 5 fractions.[32] There is currently no established standard fractionation schedule, although a 3–5 fraction schedule is favored by most experienced centers[33] and emerging role of single fraction schedule. Doses in our study ranged from 40 to 50 Gy in 5 fractions. We progressively tried to increase the dose with experience as studies have demonstrated better outcome with a BED of more than 100 Gy10.[34] Ponsky et al. in their study demonstrated that a dose of 40Gy in 5 fractions may be inadequate for long-term control.

With a median follow-up of 13.7 months, all evaluable patients showed radiologically stable disease (80%) or partial remission (20%), however, biopsies at the median of 9 months’ posttreatment remained positive in the majority of cases (64%).[35] In our study with small sample size and no local failure, we were not able to demonstrate dose effect on LC. Although the follow-up duration is short, we reported local and MC of 100% and none of the patients progressed at primary site at a median follow-up of 10.5 months and the results are in agreement with the reported outcomes in the literature. Yamamoto et al. from Japan reported impressive 5 years local and LRC of 94% and 88%, respectively, with OS of 68%.[36] The IROCK demonstrated a LC rate of 98% for all tumors with OS of 70% and cancer-specific survival of 92%. When they evaluated the effect of SBRT on larger tumors (T1b, >4cm) rates of LC and cancer specific survival did not decline.[37,38] In the recently published individual patient data meta-analysis, 5 years’ LC rates of 95%[30] were reported. It is pertinent to note the excellent short term LC despite large primary tumors in our series. In our study, the mean maximum diameter of tumor was 68 mm (range 23–128 mm). All except 2 cases (maximum tumor dimensions of 23 mm and 36 mm) had primary tumor >4 cm (T1b/T2), mean GTV volume was 118cc (16–377 cc). 100% LC in such large tumors highlights the efficacy of SBRT even in large tumors. Best OMC was 100% at initial follow-up but one patient reported subcarinal nodal recurrence (7 months after SBRT) along with regional (mediastinal failure) and distant failure (right occipital lymph node and bilateral lungs) resulting in OMC of 87.5% at last follow-up, although other ablative sites were stable. The probable cause of nodal recurrence after SBRT may be lower coverage (84%) accepted to meet strict tracheobronchial constraints. Six of eight patients received systemic therapy and one patient reported distant failure, along with locoregional failure (LRC 78%; LR-PFS: 8.5 months). The value and timing of metabolic imaging in RCC still are unclear due to low sensitivity and specificity but may be helpful in detection of metastasis.[39] In our study, all patients (n = 11) had baseline PET-CT (Median of SUVmax: 10.4 [range, 5.4–22]) and 8 underwent response evaluation with PET-CT scan (Median of SUVmax: 5.5 [3–12.4]). The median reduction in SUVmax value at primary tumor was 43% (range, 5%–73%). Figure 4 shows Pseudoprogression 8 months post SBRT, with complete loss of enhancement at 14 months post SBRT. PET may have value in patients with deranged renal parameter and low GFR as CT without contrast may be inappropriate in target delineation and response assessment.

One of the reasons for low usage of SBRT in RCC is the perception of treating urologists regarding the poor efficacy of radiation in RCC and lack of randomized data. With the NCCN recommendation of category 2b for SBRT in cases of medically inoperable RCC and the consensus statement from consortium on kidney cancers for SBRT to RCC, usage is likely to improve.[33] To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the utilization of SBRT in RCC in India.

The limitations of this study were its small sample size, heterogeneous cohort as two patients with nonmetastatic kidney tumor were also included, different BED for primary tumor, heterogeneous tumor sizes, nonuniform and incomplete systemic therapy, and short follow-up. We are also not able to collect posttreatment DTPA-GFR in all patients. We observed that SBRT maintained its efficacy on LC even with large tumors (>7 cm).

CONCLUSION

This retrospective study suggests the feasibility and tolerability of the total ablative SBRT concept, synchronously targeting both primary and oligometastatic sites in selected patients. Good local/LRC rates even in large RCCs with very low toxicity rates were reported in this study. PET avidity may not be a reliable predictor of response. Total ablative SBRT strategy needs to be explored in similar cohort.
