Upper limb mucormycosis in a patient with acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a case report
Authors: Hesam Alitaleshi, Mohammadreza Abbaszadeh, Younes Noshadi, Leila Zanjani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Mucormycosis, a fungal infection, usually involves the rhino-orbital-cerebral cavities in immunocompromised patients. The upper limb is one of the most infrequent sites for mucormycosis infection.Case presentation:We present a rare and atypical case of upper limb mucormycosis in an immunocompromised patient undergoing chemotherapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia. The diagnosis was confirmed through histopathological examination. Prompt and aggressive intervention – including extensive surgical debridement and intravenous antifungal therapy – led to the successful preservation of both the patient’s life and limb.Clinical discussion:This case is notable for successful limb salvage without amputation, despite deep tissue involvement. Prompt recognition, early surgical intervention, and appropriate antifungal therapy were key to the favorable outcome. Considering mucormycosis in immunocompromised patients, along with early diagnostic workup, biopsy, specific fungal cultures (when available), antifungal therapy, and timely surgical intervention, can lead to limb and life salvage.Conclusion:The present study has revealed the importance of early diagnosis and management of uncommon site infections with mucormycosis in immunocompromised individuals, particularly in hematologic malignancies.

Introduction

Mucormycosis is a rare but serious fungal infection. Previous studies reported an annual incidence of 1.7 cases per one million individuals[1]. More recent data indicate that the overall prevalence of all forms of mucormycosis in the United States accounts for less than 0.01% of total hospitalizations[2]. There is no epidemiological study on the prevalence of mucormycosis in Iran. However, Vaezi et al., in their review study, reported 98 cases of mucormycosis documented in Iran over a 25-year period[3]. It primarily affects immunocompromised patients, including those with diabetes mellitus, hematologic malignancies, and individuals who have undergone organ or hematopoietic cell transplantation. Patients with hematologic malignancies account for about 17% of all mucormycosis cases[3,4]. Consistent with global data, the most common risk factor reported in Iranian studies was diabetes mellitus, particularly uncontrolled diabetes[4,5]. Other risk factors included immunosuppression, malignancy, and corticosteroid use[5,6]. The rhinocerebral cavity is the most common site of mucormycosis infection; however, the lungs, skin, and gastrointestinal tract can also be involved[7]. Upper limb involvement in mucormycosis is rare and presents unique challenges due to the complex anatomy and function of the upper limb. Primary cutaneous Mucormycosis affecting the limbs has been reported in the literature as case reports[8]. Additionally, a limited number of muscular mucormycosis cases have been reported in the literature, with hematologic malignancies being the most common predisposing factor[9]. This study presents a patient with upper limb mucormycosis with muscle involvement in an immunocompromised patient with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[10].

Case presentation

A 27-year-old man had been undergoing chemotherapy for B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, diagnosed 3 months ago. He had no history of diabetes, smoking, or alcohol use. He was a farmer. His body mass index was 24.7 kg/m2. He had received induction chemotherapy, including cyclophosphamide (750 mg/m2), vincristine (1.4 mg/m2), and doxorubicin (60 mg/m2). He presented with a tender, necrotizing skin lesion on the volar proximal forearm, measuring 3 × 4 cm, with an erythematous margin and a necrotic center (Fig. 1A). The skin lesion had been present for 2 weeks. It initially appeared as a small erythematous patch on the volar proximal forearm and then gradually became painful and extended to the dorsal surface. Central necrosis developed as the lesion progressed. Throughout this period, the patient had no fever, no systemic symptoms, no rhinocerebral involvement (facial swelling, sinus symptoms, or neurological deficits), no pulmonary symptoms, and no decreased consciousness. There was no history of trauma, burns, prior surgery, or injections in the affected area. It had been 1 month since his last chemotherapy.

There was no clinical evidence of pulmonary, sinus, rhinocerebral, cerebral, or disseminated involvement, and thus no suspicion of disseminated or rhinocerebral disease. However, because rhinocerebral mucormycosis is the most common presentation in Iran, an otorhinolaryngology consultation was obtained, which ruled out sinus and airway involvement. Initial laboratory tests (CBC, liver function tests, BUN, creatinine, urinalysis, and blood culture) showed no abnormalities, and the blood culture was negative.

Radiography of the affected limb revealed no osseous involvement (Fig. 1B and C). Ultrasound showed no fluid collection or abscess, only findings suggestive of cellulitis. PET or FDG scans were unavailable at our center to rule out disseminated disease, but there were no clinical signs of systemic spread to warrant such imaging.

The patient received empiric antibiotics and underwent a needle biopsy, which demonstrated a probable angioinvasive fungal infection. He was then prepared for urgent surgical debridement. MRI was not performed due to concerns about delaying surgery, given the rapidly progressive nature of the lesion and the importance of timely intervention in suspected mucormycosis. A surgical consultation was then obtained.

On examination, the patient’s general condition was good, with no evidence of sinus, lung, or other organ involvement. The skin lesion, with an erythematous border and necrotic center, was visible, leading to the decision for surgical exploration. Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent necrotizing tissue excision.

During surgical exploration, deep soft tissue involvement was found, revealing that the lesion had extended to the flexor muscle compartment (Fig. 2A). Consequently, debridement of the flexor muscle compartment with healthy borders was performed, including the skin, subcutaneous fat, and involved parts of the muscle with a healthy border. The total excision volume was 3 × 1.7 × 0.7 cm, and it was sent for histopathological examination. The results revealed non-pigmented pauciseptate ribbon-like hyphae with right-angle branching in the background of necrosis and chronic inflammation (Fig. 2B). These changes were observed in the muscle specimen, along with regenerative changes.

According to the pathology report, both skin and muscle samples demonstrated pauciseptate hyaline hyphae with angioinvasion, consistent with mucormycosis. The epidermis was hyperplastic, with focal muscle degeneration and necrosis. Species-level identification (e.g., Rhizopus, Mucor, Lichtheimia) was not possible without fungal culture or PCR, which were not performed because they were unavailable at our center.

Serial CBC was monitored daily. The initial CBC was normal, but over the following days, the WBC progressively decreased, indicating severe neutropenia during antifungal treatment. Importantly, surgical debridement was performed before the onset of neutropenia.

Preoperative blood cultures were negative, and intraoperative tissue cultures for bacteria were also negative.

Following debridement, the wound was managed with conventional sterile dressings using normal saline twice daily until the wound bed was suitable for grafting. Dressings were changed twice daily to monitor progression or new lesions. Negative-pressure wound therapy was not used.

A week later, the patient underwent split-thickness skin graft surgery. During this time, he received intravenous amphotericin B.

Based on the diagnosis, he received intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) for 4 weeks. Daily BUN and creatinine tests were conducted to monitor kidney injury.

The treatment proceeded without notable complications, and the patient was discharged in good condition. At 1, 3, and 6 months post-graft, no recurrence was observed (Fig. 3). His leukemia treatment was monitored by the hematology-oncology team. He did not return for further surgical follow-up due to continued active chemotherapy. The range of motion of the elbow was full and similar to the contralateral side, he had no pain, and he performed personal activities independently. Unfortunately, he passed away due to fever, neutropenia, and pneumonia during his neutropenic period after chemotherapy, 12 months after the surgery.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the few case reports of successful limb salvage without amputation in upper limb mucormycosis. The clinical course is often rapidly progressive, although rare osteomyelitis has been reported in indolent presentations[11].

The importance of early diagnosis and rapid intervention lies in the potential for limb salvage and patient survival. Amputation cases have been reported, ranging from shoulder disarticulation[12] and hip disarticulation[13] to digit and metacarpal amputation[14]. A critical point is prompt patient presentation and timely intervention; in the case leading to shoulder disarticulation, surgery was performed after approximately 20 days. In contrast, appropriate debridement and antifungal therapy can achieve limb salvage[15,16].

Immunocompromised patients are typically affected by mucormycosis after penetrating trauma, burns, or injection[17]. Although immunocompromised status is emphasized, cases have also been reported in healthy patients without immunodeficiency or diabetes following road traffic accidents[15], blast injury[13], or extensive burns[18]. These should be kept in mind as predisposing factors.

Mucormycosis accounts for about 10% of infections in patients with hematologic disorders[19]. The diagnosis is challenging due to a wide range of clinical manifestations, such as rhino-orbital, skin, bone, and soft tissue involvement. Additionally, there are several differential diagnoses, such as aspergillosis. These variable manifestations and a wide range of differential diagnoses can make the diagnosis challenging and delayed[9]. Differential diagnoses include necrotizing fasciitis, invasive aspergillosis, ecthyma gangrenosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, vasculitis, and drug-induced reactions. Histopathological confirmation of mucormycosis (pauciseptate hyphae with right-angle branching) excludes these entities. Patients with mucormycosis have a high mortality rate of about 70%; however, skin involvement has the lowest mortality rate compared with other sites of infection. Skin infections can progress to the deeper layers and develop osteomyelitis. Reported cases of mucormycosis-related osteomyelitis have primarily been attributed to direct inoculation or iatrogenic contamination. However, in immunocompromised patients, hematogenous dissemination has also been documented as a potential route of skeletal involvement[20,21]. Furthermore, mucormycosis, by invading the blood vessel endothelium, can cause thrombosis and disseminated disease[22]. According to its aggressive nature, limb mucormycosis almost always leads to limb amputation, and there are few cases in the literature with successful limb salvage[17]. However, early diagnosis and crucial management, including surgical debridement and systemic therapy with antifungal agents such as amphotericin B, can make limb salvage possible[23]. In addition to antifungal therapy and surgical debridement, other treatment modalities, such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy, have been reported in the management of mucormycosis[18].

Patients with deep fungal invasion should be assessed for other sites of infection, as soft tissue involvement (e.g., muscle) may result from disseminated disease (e.g., from the lungs). Given the importance of rapid diagnosis, especially in immunosuppressed or diabetic patients, mucormycosis should be considered in any suspicious lesion, particularly those with rapid progression and central necrosis. Empirical antifungals, specific fungal cultures (if available), prompt biopsy, and timely surgery can be limb- and life-saving.

Conclusion

Due to the high rate of mucormycosis infection in patients with hematologic malignancies, it should be carefully considered and managed in these immunocompromised individuals. Early clinical suspicion, surgical intervention, and histopathological assessments are crucial in improving patient outcomes and significantly reducing the mortality rate.
