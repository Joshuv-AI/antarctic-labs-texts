Implementation of a midwifery preceptor program in Sierra Leone: a qualitative evaluation using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research
Authors: Nicholas M. Raposo, Olivia Yang, Alice Konyani, Chrisencia Owoko, Adelaide Debrah, Jennina Rose Viera, Mustapha Sonnie, Julie Mann, Brittney J. van de Water
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSierra Leone has the world’s third highest incidence of maternal mortality, with 443 deaths per 100,000 live births. Strengthening the country’s midwifery workforce is essential to providing adequate maternal healthcare and reducing preventable perinatal mortality. In support of this goal, we developed and implemented a midwifery preceptor program (MPP) to train experienced midwives to effectively mentor new and student midwives. The aim of this study was to qualitatively evaluate barriers and facilitators to the implementation of the MPP using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).MethodsAt the end of the first year of MPP implementation, all preceptors, facilitators, and stakeholders from both implementation sites in Sierra Leone were invited for virtual, individual interviews; 26 participated. Using deductive content analysis, interview transcripts were coded according to the five CFIR domains — innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals, and process — and an added sixth domain: future directions. Barriers and facilitators to MPP implementation were assessed per domain.ResultsParticipants identified barriers and facilitators to MPP implementation across all six domains. Facilitators included a well-designed program that comprehensively integrated theory and practice, the support of program facilitators, and effective stakeholder collaborations. Barriers included limited resources and time constraints, which occasionally made it difficult to balance professional responsibilities with program activities. Nonetheless, participants advocated for formalizing the MPP to sustain the program’s impact.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the MPP is a valuable innovation for enhancing midwifery education in Sierra Leone, which in turn can help to improve perinatal health outcomes. By identifying key barriers and facilitators to implementation, this study provides actionable insights to inform the adaptation, scaling, and sustainability of the MPP not only in Sierra Leone but also in other resource-limited settings facing similar workforce challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44263-025-00202-5.

Background

Sierra Leone has one of the poorest maternal health indicators globally, with a maternal mortality rate of 443 per 100,000 live births [1]. Strengthening midwifery care, which can substantially reduce perinatal mortality in low- and medium-income countries, is integral to addressing high rates of preventable maternal mortality in Sierra Leone [1, 2]. The country needs approximately 3000 midwives to provide minimally adequate maternal healthcare services, yet it has fewer than 500 [3]. To address this critical workforce shortage, the national government and midwifery schools in Makeni and Bo have collaborated since 2010 to increase enrollment of midwifery students in Sierra Leone [4].

In addition to increasing the quantity of midwives, improving maternal health in Sierra Leone depends on providing high-quality midwifery care aligned with the competencies recommended by the International Confederation of Midwives [2]. According to the Nurses and Midwives Council of Sierra Leone, novice students develop into professional midwives through an educational program that combines 40% theory with 60% clinical skill building [5]. A common and globally recognized approach to cultivating this clinical competence is preceptorship, whereby experienced clinicians provide guidance and support to students or newly qualified practitioners as they transition into professional practice [6]. Such structured clinical teaching programs are essential to producing a competent midwifery workforce that meets national and international standards [5].

Seed Global Health (Seed), a nonprofit organization that supports healthcare workforce training in resource-limited countries, has been working to strengthen midwifery education in Sierra Leone since 2021 [7]. In response to the pressing need for improved clinical teaching, Seed developed a four-step midwifery preceptor program (MPP) to facilitate the transfer of essential competencies for quality midwifery care [8]. Consistent with the goal of implementation science, which aims to “promote the adoption and integration of evidence-based practices and interventions into routine health care and public health settings,” the purpose of this study was to qualitatively evaluate barriers and facilitators to implementation of the MPP using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [9, 10]. To achieve this, we interviewed preceptors, facilitators, and other stakeholders closely involved with the first year of the program.

Methods

Setting

Seed implemented the MPP at Makeni Regional and Bo Government Hospitals, in collaboration with their associated schools of midwifery [8]. Makeni Regional Hospital is the referral hospital for the five northern districts of Sierra Leone and the primary training site for midwifery students from the School of Midwifery Makeni. Bo Government Hospital is the regional hospital in the Southern Province, where another school of midwifery is located. Both schools offer direct-entry diploma programs, as well as certificate programs for nurses seeking midwifery training. Students were trained at the regional hospitals, as well as other public health units and community health clinics. Although midwives at these facilities were expected to precept students, there was no formal preceptor role or training. The lack of adequate and effective clinical education in the hospitals negatively affected students’ skill acquisition.

Program design and implementation

Prior to this study, Seed designed the MPP as a 1-year program to prepare experienced midwives to instruct, mentor, assess, and orient new and student midwives in perinatal care [8]. The MPP was built on the principles of preceptorship and incorporated evidence-based strategies such as low-dose, high-frequency training and objective structured clinical examinations. Twenty experienced midwives — 10 each from Makeni and Bo — were selected for the program following an application and interview process. The program included an opening retreat, 12 weeks of skills- and team-building to improve teamwork and communication, 12 weeks of preceptor training in the clinical setting, and 7 weeks of supervised preceptorship. Pre- and post-program assessments measured competence and confidence in clinical and teaching skills.

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive approach to evaluate MPP implementation practically and comprehensively using the language and perspectives of those who were closely involved with the first year of the program [11, 12]. We followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research [13].

Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research

CFIR 2.0 served as the guiding framework for our interviews, deductive content analysis, and evaluation of MPP implementation [10]. CFIR provides a practical structure for systematically evaluating barriers and facilitators, tailoring implementation strategies, adapting interventions, and explaining program outcomes. It has been widely applied in healthcare settings, including those in low-income contexts [14]. CFIR comprises five domains — innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals, and process — and supports the addition of constructs to capture salient themes beyond these [10].

Participants

We used purposive sampling to recruit individuals directly involved in the first year of MPP implementation. All preceptors (n = 20), facilitators (n = 5), and stakeholders (n = 15) were eligible and there were no exclusion criteria. The authors recruited these individuals on-site in Sierra Leone and via email. The sample size was not predetermined; the final sample included all 26 individuals – 18 preceptors and eight facilitators or stakeholders – who responded and consented to participate (Table 1). Participants were compensated for their time.

Participant characteristics

Data collection

Individual interviews took place via Zoom (Version 6.0.10, Zoom Communications, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) between May and June 2024, after the first year of MPP implementation [15]. We obtained written informed consent prior to audio recording and transcribing the interviews using Zoom functions. Two authors (N. R. and O. Y.) conducted interviews in English, the official language in Sierra Leone, using an interview guide developed from the CFIR domains (Supplementary Material 1: Interview guide). Interviews lasted 30 to 60 min. Each interviewer reviewed and edited their corresponding transcripts for accuracy before uploading to Dedoose, a web-based application for organizing and analyzing qualitative data (Sociocultural Research Consultants, LLC, Los Angeles, CA, USA) [16].

Reflexivity

This study involved a diverse research team, including nurses and midwives from the USA (N. R., O. Y., J. M., B. v. d. W.) and Sierra Leone (A. K., C. O., A. D., J. R. V.). Throughout the project, team members carefully considered personal, professional, academic, and sociocultural differences and power imbalances among the team and between researchers and participants. US-based researchers continuously reflected on their positionality, consciously sought to mitigate power differentials, and worked to center the voices of Sierra Leonean authors and participants. Sierra Leonean researchers played a crucial role in contextualizing the research, facilitating cross-cultural understanding, and ensuring fidelity to the perspectives of local midwives.

Analysis

Three authors (N. R., O. Y., B. v. d. W.) deductively coded the transcripts according to constructs within the five CFIR domains (Supplementary Material 2: CFIR codebook) [10]. Each of the first 20% of transcripts (n = 5) was independently coded by two authors. The authors then reviewed coded excerpts together to ensure consistent application of codes and to discuss coding discrepancies collaboratively until consensus was achieved. In these discussions and as supported by CFIR, the authors agreed that one additional domain — future directions — was necessary to capture an emerging theme in the data. The remaining transcripts were independently coded, after which the team collectively reviewed coded excerpts to determine barriers and facilitators per domain.

Results

In their qualitative evaluations of MPP implementation, participants described barriers and facilitators across all five CFIR domains and the added sixth domain. Innovation domain: “A whole package; simple but comprehensive”

According to participants, the MPP was a novel and necessary innovation for midwifery clinical education. The blend of in-depth learning, skill building, and continuous mentorship seemed an intuitive approach to participants. Although some described the program as stressful, serious, or complex, participants felt well supported by program facilitators, who delivered the program in a way that was thoughtful and easy to follow.

“It’s a full package that really instills all the competencies that a learner should have to be able to practice midwifery.” (Site 1 Preceptor #1)

“It’s a full package that really instills all the competencies that a learner should have to be able to practice midwifery.” (Site 1 Preceptor #1)

“People remember about 5 or 10% of what they’re told, but 90% of what they do, so having opportunities for simulation and debrief embedded throughout the entire program, and then mentorship opportunities throughout the workday – I think the combination of those two things in an ongoing way is the best educational opportunity that I think we could design for anyone, especially for clinical skills.” (Facilitator #4)

“People remember about 5 or 10% of what they’re told, but 90% of what they do, so having opportunities for simulation and debrief embedded throughout the entire program, and then mentorship opportunities throughout the workday – I think the combination of those two things in an ongoing way is the best educational opportunity that I think we could design for anyone, especially for clinical skills.” (Facilitator #4)

Participants noted that the MPP was challenging but worthwhile. To minimize participant burden, the program was designed to be delivered in the hospitals where the midwives worked; however, several participants reported difficulties balancing class attendance or activities with patient care responsibilities. Additionally, some participants said the yearlong commitment was a significant demand on their time and emphasized the motivation necessary to see the program through to completion.

“The program – it’s not easy, you know; one year is not just one month or one week. Every week, sometimes we go twice. They really take the program in detail. We touched every area of midwifery that we think is necessary and worth knowing. We have gaps there; we try to fill those gaps with some new things.” (Site 1 Preceptor #5)

“That was just my challenge, that balancing. How am I to balance work, balance precepting, or supervising, and going for class?... You have this document that you need to do, you have test tomorrow, you know, it was so stressful.” (Site 2 Preceptor #9)

“That was just my challenge, that balancing. How am I to balance work, balance precepting, or supervising, and going for class?... You have this document that you need to do, you have test tomorrow, you know, it was so stressful.” (Site 2 Preceptor #9)

Despite its challenges, the MPP was much more comprehensive than prior training models, which were often shorter in duration and narrower in scope. As a prerequisite to effective student teaching, facilitators helped midwives to recognize weaknesses in their own practice and offered a comprehensive, standards-based program to improve clinical mastery. Owing to the program’s rigor and timespan, participants felt empowered with the knowledge, skills, and attitudes necessary to precept effectively.

“The previous programs were focusing on how to teach students but not enhancing the skills of the preceptors. While ours is both: the skills and how to teach.” (Facilitator #2)

Outer setting domain: “Hands together to reduce maternal and child mortality”

Participants identified several external environmental factors that impacted implementation of the MPP. Key among these was effective collaborations between Seed, the national government, the midwifery schools, and the regional hospitals.“We work through the partners. It’s a partnership program. So the partners that are in the facility, the school, they will come together. Then we decide, what do they want to achieve for the year? What is the problem that you want us to help them solve?... So that’s how the program is developed: it’s purely based from the partners’ perspective.” (Stakeholder #1)

“We work through the partners. It’s a partnership program. So the partners that are in the facility, the school, they will come together. Then we decide, what do they want to achieve for the year? What is the problem that you want us to help them solve?... So that’s how the program is developed: it’s purely based from the partners’ perspective.” (Stakeholder #1)

Given Seed’s leading role in orchestrating key partnerships and leading the initiative, some participants voiced concern about the program’s viability in the absence of a partner like Seed. Specifically, they questioned who would oversee the program and who would provide funding to sustain and scale the program after Seed.“I’m just hoping that there will be resources.... I’m not foreseeing that when Seed will leave our country, there will be continuity. I’m not sure, but I am hoping for the better.” (Site 2 Preceptor #4)

Inner setting domain: “We fight with everything we have: teamwork, passion, resources”

Interviewees recalled characteristics of the inner setting that warranted implementation of the MPP, including negative workplace dynamics and outdated practices. One participant specified that “communication [and] teamwork was a very big challenge” (Stakeholder #1), while another said that “we have been practicing this archaic midwifery, and we have been seeing the results of our old practice” (Site 1 Preceptor #9). However, implementation of the MPP was constrained by a lack of space for preceptorship activities, insufficient material resources, and limited work infrastructure.“When we had classes here at the hospital, there is no space for us to get these classes... You can’t practice a skill if you can’t touch a patient because you don’t have gloves or you don’t have a way to properly sanitize because you just don’t have soap... There were days when we didn’t have oxytocin available... or we didn’t have needles or syringes available... How can you suture, you know, if you don’t have suture kits?” (Facilitator #4)

Building on the successful implementation of the MPP in Makeni and Bo, participants advocated for expanding it nationally and internationally. Highlighting an urgent need to scale the program, one participant noted:

“There are other hospitals that I think are still struggling with the skills and knowledge…So by the time they send patient to our hospital, it’s late. It’s [too] late for us to intervene.” (Site 2 Preceptor #4).

Several recommendations were made to enhance future iterations of the program. One participant recommended refresher training to maintain connections among cohorts and ensure the most up-to-date practices. Another suggested that reducing the frequency of meetings from weekly to monthly could improve sustainability and ease replication of the program. Overall, participants felt the intervention was highly adaptable and, with appropriate support and contextual adjustments, could be replicated in various settings. They also highlighted the multifaceted impact of the intervention, noting its direct influence on preceptors as well as the broader, indirect benefits for colleagues, students, and patients.

“If this program continues, it will benefit both the students, the patient, and even the colleagues or staff and to make the positive impact. Yes. So, we think this program is very necessary and we hope it will continue to be sustaining so other people can come on board.” (Site 1 Preceptor #5)

Discussion

This study found that the MPP was widely valued for its comprehensive and rigorous approach to enhancing midwifery education. Key factors supporting successful implementation included supportive stakeholders, dedicated facilitators, and committed preceptors, who together fostered a positive culture around the program. Participants recognized the program’s demands but consistently viewed it as worthwhile for professional growth. Importantly, there was strong enthusiasm for sustaining the MPP in current sites and expanding it nationally and beyond.

The primary barriers identified to implementing the MPP related to the program’s duration and scope. Unlike prior professional development programs in this setting, which were brief and lacked sustained follow-up, the MPP was higher-frequency and longer lasting, which demanded significant commitment from all parties. However, evidence shows that serial, targeted interventions combined with mentorship or supportive supervision improve knowledge, clinical performance, skill retention, and health outcomes more effectively than isolated interventions delivered via traditional training approaches in many settings [17–21].

Competing professional and program demands were another salient barrier. As with the MPP, challenges related to staffing shortages, heavy workloads, work infrastructure gaps, and inadequate clinical support were documented with implementation of a midwifery educational program in Kenya [22]. Time constraints and conflicting priorities remain pervasive barriers to educational program implementation in other sub-Saharan African contexts [23]. Addressing these barriers necessitates increased resource allocation and supportive policy frameworks.

Concerns regarding the MPP’s sustainability without ongoing external support highlight the importance of local and national ownership for long-term viability. Embedding the program within national nursing and midwifery education systems and securing endorsement from governmental bodies are critical steps for sustained impact and scaling. One model for these efforts is the Nurse Education Partnership Initiative, a capacity-building program that was formally adopted by the Malawi Nurses and Midwives Council after garnering support from a coalition of nursing and midwifery schools [24].

The MPP’s comprehensive and well-organized design facilitated its implementation within clinical settings. Conducting trainings within the practice setting with the support and mentorship of educators fostered an enabling learning environment, essential for effective clinical education [25, 26]. Evaluations of comparable initiatives in the region have found that embedding education within the clinical setting is feasible, effective, and particularly beneficial for implementation work in resource-limited settings [22].

Although outcome evaluations for this first iteration of the MPP are forthcoming, initial observations suggest that the program has enhanced preceptors’ knowledge, competence, and confidence, as found with similar programs implemented in Kenya and Nigeria [27]. Participants’ enthusiasm to sustain and expand the MPP reflects perceptions of its personal, professional, and broader public health benefits. Leveraging identified facilitators while addressing barriers will be vital for sustaining and scaling the MPP in settings across and beyond Sierra Leone.

This study is not without limitations. Although English is the official language of Sierra Leone and the language used in all hospitals and universities, it is not the first language of many Sierra Leoneans; accordingly, nuances may have been missed in interviews with some participants. Additionally, the interview guide may have limited discussion of barriers and facilitators beyond the CFIR domains. Few participants responded substantively to the final, open-ended interview question, which was intended to mitigate this limitation. Finally, we cannot rule out selection bias among interview participants. We invited all individuals involved in program implementation to an interview; it is difficult to determine whether those who responded are significantly different or have significantly different perspectives than those who did not.

Conclusions

The findings show that the MPP represents a valuable innovation for enhancing midwifery education, which in turn can contribute to improved perinatal health outcomes. Stakeholders, including preceptors and facilitators, expressed strong support for the program and enthusiasm for its expansion across and beyond Sierra Leone. Based on these findings, targeted adaptations addressing barriers and leveraging facilitators to program implementation can enhance the efficacy and effectiveness of future iterations of the MPP. These insights provide a foundation for scaling the MPP in Sierra Leone and similar resource-limited settings.
