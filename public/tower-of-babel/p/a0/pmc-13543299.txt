Optimized Contrast‐Enhanced Micro‐Computed Tomography for Simultaneous Imaging of the Ductus Arteriosus and Pulmonary Arteries During the Perinatal Transition
Authors: Elahe Alizadeh, Patricia D. A. Lima, Rachel E. T. Bentley, Ashley Y. Martin, Stephen L. Archer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContrast‐enhanced micro‐computed tomography (micro‐CT) provides high‐resolution, three‐dimensional visualization of small vascular structures in situ, enabling detailed analysis of morphological changes in the perinatal vasculature. The ductus arteriosus (DA) connects the aorta and pulmonary artery (PA), diverting placentally oxygenated blood away from the lungs in utero. Both DA and PA undergo rapid changes in tone with the first breath during the transition from fetal to neonatal oxygen conditions. This study aimed to optimize contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT protocols to enable simultaneous visualization of the DA and pulmonary vasculature during this transition in fetal rabbits. Two contrast agents, an iodine‐based agent (Omnipaque, 300 mg/mL) and two forms of a barium sulphate suspension, were compared to determine which optimally opacified the DA and pulmonary circulation in fetal rabbit kits (n = 22) from new‐term New Zealand white rabbits (n = 4). Micro‐CT imaging was performed to assess DA patency and changes in PA caliber across a wide range of vessel diameters from ~50 µm to > 1 mm under hypoxia versus normoxia in anesthetized kits. Omnipaque provided adequate opacification of the DA but limited visualization of the pulmonary vasculature. However, unfiltered 25% barium sulfate in 3% gelatin achieved robust, simultaneous visualization of the DA and pulmonary circulation. This agent optimally enhanced contrast in both large vessels and small PAs, enabling simultaneous assessment of DA constriction and pulmonary arterial vasodilation within 20 min of breathing oxygen. This study established an optimal contrast agent and micro‐CT imaging strategy for post‐mortem assessment of DA and pulmonary vascular morphology during the perinatal transition.

Introduction

During fetal life, the ductus arteriosus (DA) plays a critical role in directing blood that has been oxygenated by the placenta away from the developing lungs and into the systemic circulation via the DA, which connects the pulmonary artery (PA) to the aorta. With the first breath, the newborn's circulatory system must quickly adapt to obtaining oxygen through respiration via the lungs, rather than relying on placental oxygenation [1]. This perinatal transition is characterized by opposing changes in vascular tone in the DA and pulmonary circulation in response to increased oxygen exposure [1, 2]. The rise in oxygen tension at birth (from fetal PO2 < 40 mmHg to postnatal PO2 ≈ 100 mmHg) directly stimulates DA smooth muscle cell contraction by inhibiting voltage‐gated potassium channels, thereby activating L‐type calcium channels and raising intracellular calcium, leading to rapid vasoconstriction and functional closure of the DA within the first hours of life [3, 4]. DA constriction is reinforced by a change in production of vasodilatory versus vasoconstrictor prostaglandins at birth to favor vasoconstriction [5]. In contrast, the pulmonary arterial circulation simultaneously undergoes vasodilation in response to increased oxygen tension, mediated in part by activation of PA smooth muscle cell potassium channels and reduction in cytosolic calcium [6] and by increased production of nitric oxide and other vasodilators, which reduce pulmonary vascular resistance and facilitate pulmonary blood flow [3]. Through these rapid changes in vascular tone, which occur within seconds to minutes of the first breath, oxygen redirects pulmonary blood flow through the newly ventilated lungs post birth, eliminating fetal right‐to‐left shunting. DA functional closure typically occurs within the first hours of life in full‐term infants and is followed within days‐weeks by anatomical closure characterized by intimal thickening, smooth muscle cell proliferation, and eventual formation of the fibrous ligamentum arteriosum [3, 7, 8].

While this transition occurs successfully in most term infants, it frequently fails in premature and low‐birthweight newborns, leading to conditions such as persistent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), which are major causes of morbidity and mortality in preterm infants [7, 8]. Current treatments for PDA (prostaglandin synthesis inhibitors or interventional closure) and PPHN (inhaled nitric oxide) are ineffective in up to half of cases and may cause significant toxicities [7, 8, 9]. Since impaired O2‐sensing mechanisms in the DA and PA contribute to the pathogenesis of PDA and PPHN, these pathways warrant further investigation to identify potential therapeutic targets. These clinical challenges highlight the need to develop novel therapeutic strategies and methods for evaluating experimental therapies in animal models of congenital cardiovascular disorders [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. For example, investigating mitochondrial O2‐sensing mechanisms in PA and DA smooth muscle cells provides a framework for understanding the opposing oxygen responses of the DA and PA. To support the development of mitochondrial‐targeted therapies for PDA and PPHN, multiomics comparison of mitochondrial proteomics in the fetal DA and PA were integrated with quantitative imaging approaches [14]. This strategy required optimization of CT vascular imaging and analysis methods to reliably visualize the DA and PA in experimental models of PDA and PPHN. It is our hypothesis that closing the knowledge gap regarding optimal treatments for PDA and PPHN can be accelerated by advancing preclinical imaging of the DA and pulmonary circulation in small‐animal models.

High‐resolution imaging is essential for visualizing small and dynamic vascular structures in preclinical cardiovascular research [15, 16, 17, 18]. Micro‐computed tomography (micro‐CT) provides three‐dimensional (3D) imaging of small animal tissues with near‐micron spatial resolution (effective resolution ≤ 100 µm) [19, 20] and facilitates medical quality assessments of cardiopulmonary diseases in preclinical models. Micro‐CT has been widely used for lung imaging due to intrinsic air‐tissue contrast in the thorax [20, 21], though visualization of the cardiopulmonary vasculature remains technically challenging. These challenges arise primarily from cardiac and respiratory motions, as well as the small caliber of key vessels such as pulmonary arteries (PAs), ranging from ~20 to 200 µm for distal to > 1 mm for proximal) in rodents [22, 23], and ≈0.5 mm for the DA in fetal rabbit kits [24].

Despite advances in imaging technologies, current approaches remain limited in their ability to simultaneously visualize the DA and pulmonary arterial vasculature at high spatial resolution in small‐animal models. This limitation constrains the development and evaluation of novel therapeutics for PDA and PPHN, as it restricts the ability to assess treatment effects on both vascular systems within the same specimen [25]. Histological methods provide detailed structural information but do not preserve 3D anatomy [26]. Imaging modalities such as conventional CT lack sufficient spatial resolution for small‐animal models, typically achieving resolutions on the order of 0.5–1 mm, which is inadequate for resolving small‐caliber vessels such as the DA and distal PAs. Similarly, while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables non‐invasive, longitudinal assessment of cardiovascular anatomy and function, its spatial resolution is generally lower than that of micro‐CT, limiting visualization of the smallest pulmonary vessels in neonatal animal models [18, 27]. Whereas micro‐CT systems provide substantially improved spatial resolution, existing micro‐CT approaches that do not use contrast agents lack sufficient enhancement to resolve large vessels and distal pulmonary branches within the same specimen [22, 28, 29].

Contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these limitations, particularly using intravascular contrast media to enhance soft tissue contrast and address the inherently low X‐ray attenuation of soft tissues [29, 30]. In vivo and ex vivo micro‐CT approaches have been applied to visualize rodent airways, including the trachea and bronchial tree, as well as pulmonary and systemic vasculature [22, 29]. Micro‐CT has been used in preclinical models of respiratory diseases, like murine coronavirus infection, to detect lung consolidation, demonstrating its utility for high‐resolution assessment of pulmonary pathology [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. In addition, as part of ongoing efforts to develop novel therapeutic strategies for PDA and PPHN [14, 37], previous work has demonstrated that ex vivo micro‐CT imaging using an iodine‐based contrast agent (Omnipaque 300 mg of Iodine/mL (I/mL) in 3% gelatin) can reliably visualize structural changes in the DA associated with oxygen exposure in fetal rabbit models [24]. However, these approaches remain limited by incomplete opacification of the pulmonary vasculature, particularly the smaller distal vessels, preventing simultaneous, high‐fidelity assessment of the anatomy of the DA and pulmonary arterial tree.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to develop and optimize a contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT protocol capable of simultaneously visualizing the DA and pulmonary vasculature ex vivo. Multiple iodine‐ and barium‐based contrast agents, along with different perfusion strategies were evaluated to achieve uniform vascular filling across a broad range of vessel diameters. Moreover, micro‐CT acquisition parameters were optimized, and quantitative image analysis techniques were implemented to characterize structural differences in small‐caliber neonatal vessels under hypoxic and normoxic conditions. We report a protocol and imaging platform that enables reliable, high‐resolution 3D assessment of the perinatal cardiopulmonary system during the perinatal transition. Although limited to ex vivo imaging, this protocol may facilitate future investigations into the mechanisms and treatment of neonatal cardiopulmonary disorders.

Materials and Methods

Reagents Preparation

All contrast agents were freshly prepared and maintained at ~37°C before perfusion and used within 30 min of preparation to minimize precipitation or sedimentation. Barium sulfate powder and gelatin (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were used to prepare a 25% w/v barium sulfate suspension in 3% w/v gelatin. Gelatin was first dissolved in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) at 50°C–60°C under continuous stirring. Next, barium sulfate powder was slowly added while stirring continued to ensure a homogenous suspension. Barium‐based agents are restricted to non‐survival studies due to the cardiovascular toxicity of intravascular barium, including induction of arrhythmias, hypertension, and direct cardiac injury in animal models [38, 39]. Two versions of this solution were compared: (1) Filtered barium/gelatin perfusate: the suspension was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) to remove large particles and reduce viscosity, improving perfusion efficiency. (2) Unfiltered barium/gelatin perfusate: used to preserve higher particulate content, though with increased risk of vessel clogging or rupture.

Omnipaque (Iohexol 300 mg of iodine/mL (I/mL); GE Healthcare) was mixed with 3% gelatin prepared as described above. Omnipaque is a widely used clinical contrast agent available in various iodine concentrations, which provides stable contrast enhancement for up to an hour, making it suitable for high‐quality in vivo CT imaging in small‐animal models [40, 41].

For each animal, three luer‐lock syringes were prefilled, free of air bubbles, with the following solutions: (1) heparinized (10 I.U./20 g bodyweight) PBS (2 mL) to flush the vasculature and remove residual blood while preventing coagulation, (2) 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; 8 mL) to fix and preserve tissue architecture, and (3) one of the contrast agents (8 mL), either Omnipaque or barium sulphate suspensions. All solutions were warmed to 37°C prior to perfusion.

Animal Model

Pregnant New Zealand White rabbits at 29–30 days of gestation (n = 4), were anaesthetized with intravenous induction of alfaxalone (4 mg/kg) and maintained with 2.5%–4% isoflurane. Rabbit kits (n = 22) were delivered via caesarean section, removed from the amniotic sac, and their placentas were ligated. Prior to the onset of respiration, kits were assigned to one of two oxygen exposure conditions: hypoxia or normoxia. Hypoxic kits were immediately anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) before their first breath (to maintain fetal hypoxia), whereas normoxic kits were exposed to 100% O2 for 20 min to simulate the transition to normoxia [14, 24], followed by administration of the same anesthetic dose. Once the kits lost the toe pinch reflex, a midline sternotomy and then perfusion were performed.

Administration of Contrast Agents

For perfusion, kits were placed in the right lateral decubitus position, and a left thoracotomy was performed to prevent bleeding through the umbilical vasculature. A right femoral arteriotomy was made to facilitate the exit of blood and perfusate. Transcardiac perfusion was performed by inserting a 25‐gauge butterfly catheter < 1.5 mm into the left ventricle while the heart was still beating. Solutions were infused manually at a constant rate of 2 mL/min to fill vessels uniformly in the following sequence: heparinized PBS, 4% PFA, and finally, the contrast agent. Following perfusion, the kits were positioned on ice with angled‐elevated heads to minimize contrast agent leakage. They were then transferred to the imaging facility for high‐resolution micro‐CT scanning. Micro‐CT Image Acquisition

Micro‐CT images were acquired using a VECTor4CT (MILabs B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands), a preclinical tri‐modality imaging platform equipped with a cone‐beam X‐ray CT system for high‐resolution, volumetric imaging (voxel size < 70 μm). The scanner accommodates horizontal positioning of the specimen on a fixed bed while the X‐ray source rotates around it, enabling both total‐body and organ‐focused acquisitions. Scans were performed at 50 kVp acceleration voltage, 430 μA X‐ray tube current, and 40 ms exposure time under two distinct acquisition modes: 1.Total‐body scan mode: This mode was used to scan intact rabbit kits in situ. This modality enabled anatomical assessment of overall contrast distribution and vascular opacification throughout the body, including heart, lungs, and DA. A full‐body scan was acquired using 1400 projections during a 360° rotation. Reconstructions were performed using MILabs reconstruction software v11.0, applying a Hann projection filter, generating 3D datasets with a 50 μm isotropic voxel grid. The voxel numbers were converted into Hounsfield units using a premeasured calibration factor.2.Focused (ultra‐high resolution) scan mode: For detailed evaluation of the pulmonary vasculature ex vivo, the heart and lungs were carefully dissected from the same contrast‐perfused kits that underwent total‐body micro‐CT imaging. The dissected tissues were gently washed in cold PBS to remove residual contrast agent and blood, then immediately scanned in focused mode. This protocol involved extended image acquisition with 3600 projections per scan under the same X‐ray settings (50 kVp, 430 μA, 40 ms). The data were reconstructed at a higher spatial resolution using a 40 μm voxel grid, enabling visualization of fine structures such as distal PAs and DA.

Image Post‐Processing

Reconstructed slice data and maximum intensity projection (MIP) images were processed using PMOD 3.9 (PMOD Technologies Ltd., Zurich, Switzerland) to generate horizontal and vertical long‐axis and short‐axis views. Images were manually converted to a DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format, as a standard format for storing and exchanging medical images and related information. DICOM files were then imported into OsiriX Lite (Pixmeo, Geneva, Switzerland) for 3D rendering. Imalytics Preclinical 3.1.1.3 (Gremse‐IT, Aachen, Germany) was used for advanced volume rendering, 3D visualization, and semi‐automated segmentation of anatomical structures [42].

Different parameters were quantified from segmented micro‐CT datasets for each lung across eight kits. Measurements included aortic diameter, DA diameter and patency, PA caliber, as well as the number of vessels and total lung vasculature in the lungs of near‐term hypoxic and term normoxic rabbit kits.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in vessel size and vascular distribution between two groups were evaluated using unpaired two‐tailed t‐tests. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 11 for macOS (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). The number of kits received each contrast‐agent group are indicated in Figure 1b.

Results

Comparison of Contrast Agents and Optimization of Perfusion Protocol

We optimized the preparation of contrast media and perfusion techniques to achieve uniform filling of all small neonatal arteries (ranging from ~50 µm to > 1 mm) with the contrast agents. We evaluated several contrast agents, including barium‐based agents and iodine‐containing clinical agents, such as Omnipaque, which are suitable for contrast‐enhanced CT imaging in small‐animal models [40, 43].

Based on our previous findings using Omnipaque 300 mg of iodine/mL (I/mL) in 3% gelatin [24], we conducted additional experiments to assess its distribution in the DA and pulmonary vasculature of term rabbit kits. Omnipaque provided strong and consistent opacification of the DA, enabling reliable visualization of its morphology and connection to the aorta during in situ CT scans in both hypoxic and normoxic conditions (Figure 2a,b). However, the pulmonary vascular opacification was incomplete, particularly in distal vessels. Increasing perfusion pressure, limited by the risk of vascular damage at higher pressures, did not improve small vessel opacification. Thus, Omnipaque, under the current protocol (with 3% gelatin), is suitable for DA imaging ex vivo but insufficient for comprehensive pulmonary vascular visualization.

Representative contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT images of fetal rabbit kits following Omnipaque administration under (a) hypoxic and (b) normoxic conditions. In situ: Omnipaque enabled high‐contrast visualization of the DA under both fetal (hypoxia) and neonatal (normoxia) oxygen conditions. Ex vivo: Despite clear visualization of the DA, pulmonary vascular filling remained incomplete, particularly in distal vessel regions, limiting the utility of Omnipaque for whole‐lung vascular imaging in this model.

To address this limitation, we modified the protocol by employing a lower‐viscosity agent (a 25% barium sulfate solution in 3% gelatin). Although barium sulfate is inappropriate for administration in live animals due to its toxic effects on the heart and vasculature [38, 39], it offers several advantages for non‐survival studies, including excellent radiodensity and low viscosity [43, 44]. To optimize this contrast agent and minimize the risk of particulate embolism or inconsistent vessel filling, barium sulfate was administered either as filtered or unfiltered solutions.

Filtered 25% barium sulfate in suspended 3% gelatin allowed easier perfusion through the left ventricle, which permitted adequate visualization of the pulmonary vasculature in both hypoxic and normoxic conditions ex vivo (Figure 3a,b). However, DA opacification with the filtered barium solution was inconsistent in in situ scans, limiting its utility for assessing DA morphology and patency (Figure 3a,b). These results suggest that although filtration improves perfusion feasibility, likely through reduced particulate concentrations and lower viscosity; however, these changes may reduce the agent's ability to traverse or be retained within the DA. Thus, while filtered barium may be useful for imaging PAs, it is suboptimal for simultaneous visualization of the DA and pulmonary vasculature.

Filtered barium enables visualization of the pulmonary circulation but not the ductus arteriosus (DA) in fetal rabbits in situ. Representative micro‐CT images of fetal rabbit kits perfused with filtered barium sulfate contrast agent under (a) hypoxia and (b) normoxia conditions (n = 2 kits per group). While the pulmonary vasculature is adequately opacified in ex vivo scans, the DA is not consistently visualized in total‐body (in situ) scans. Circles indicate the expected anatomical location of the DA, which appears faint or absent, thereby limiting reliable assessment of its morphology and patency or closure.

We next evaluated unfiltered 25% barium sulfate in 3% gelatin to determine whether it could provide simultaneous, high‐quality visualization of both the DA and pulmonary vasculature. Imaging focused on visualizing of DA and its continuity with the aorta, as well as the branching of resistance level PAs throughout the lungs, including PAs with diameters from < 50 µm to > 1 mm. Representative micro‐CT images (n = 8; n = 4 hypoxic kits and n = 4 normoxic kits from n = 2 pregnant rabbits) demonstrate consistent simultaneous opacification of the DA and pulmonary vasculature in kits under both oxygen conditions in in situ and ex vivo scans (Figure 4a,b). In situ images reveal DA patency under hypoxic condition, maintaining continuity between the PA and the aorta, whereas the DA is fully closed in normoxic kits, consistent with the normal postnatal transition. Notably, in 2 of 4 kits under hypoxia, these morphological features were also clearly preserved and identifiable in ex vivo scans after dissection of the heart and lungs. This technique provided high‐quality opacification of both the DA and pulmonary vasculature in all kits, enabling qualitative assessment of vascular morphology and suitable for quantitative morphometric analysis.

Morphological Changes of the DA and PAs Under Hypoxia and Normoxia

Omnipaque in 3% gelatin demonstrated preferential opacification of the DA with minimal visualization of the PAs under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions. In contrast, filtered barium showed the opposite pattern, with strong PA opacification and limited filling of the DA. Unfiltered barium, however, provided a uniformly high opacification and complete visualization of both structures under both oxygen conditions (Figure 4). Under hypoxia, the DA remained patent, maintaining continuity between the PA and the aorta. With normoxic exposure, the DA appeared constricted and functionally closed. Concurrently, pulmonary arterial branching differed between the two conditions: normoxic lungs showed more numerous distal branches clearly visualized due to the perfusion of the newly ventilated lungs and pulmonary circulation, whereas hypoxic lungs displayed fewer distal branches with relatively more prominent proximal vessels. Quantitative Analysis of Pulmonary Vascular Morphology

Following image reconstruction, we assessed the utility of several DICOM‐compatible platforms to identify those most effective for quantifying vascular features in neonatal lungs [45, 46]. Measurements included DA diameter and patency as well as the caliber of PAs in neonatal (normoxic) versus fetal (hypoxic) rabbit kits. Prior to any measurement and vascular segmentation, the heart was cropped from the image to exclude the confounding signal resulting from accumulation of large amounts of contrast in the cardiac chambers. Imalytics Preclinical 3.1.1.3 (Gremse‐IT, Aachen, Germany) was exploited to measure the sizes of the aorta and the proximal left and right PAs in each scan to determine physiologically appropriate size ranges (bins selection) for segmentation [42]. Across the eight kits shown in Figure 4, the aortic diameter was 1.48 ± 0.05 mm (mean ± SE). The proximal PAs measured 0.68 ± 0.03 mm for the smaller branch and 0.86 ± 0.03 mm for the larger branch. These measurements provide baseline anatomical reference values that support the interpretation of downstream analyses, like PA diameter distributions and segmentation thresholds. Based on these sizes, pulmonary vessels segmentation was defined in four diameter bins and color‐coded as follows: 0.05–0.1 mm (green), 0.1–0.3 mm (blue), 0.3–0.5 mm (red) and > 0.5 mm (pink). Figure 5a,b shows representative color‐coded segmentations from one kit per group (n = 4 per group), illustrating differences in pulmonary vessels size and distribution in hypoxic versus normoxic conditions.

Two other parameters were quantified across those same eight kits: lung volume and lung total vasculature (as a surrogate for lung volume). Quantitative image analysis (Figure 5c) showed a higher mean lung volume in the normoxic group compared to the hypoxic group (855.7 ± 103.2 mm3 vs. 584.6 ± 44.0 mm3; p = 0.05). Also, the total counts of segmented pulmonary vessels was ~62% higher in normoxic lungs than in hypoxic lungs (Figure 5d). These findings indicate an outstanding increment in lung volume and pulmonary vasculature perfusion under normoxic versus hypoxic conditions.

Volumetric quantification of small vessel counts across four segmentation bins (Figure 5e) demonstrated that absolute vessel counts were higher under normoxic conditions across all diameter ranges, with the most pronounced increase observed in mid‐sized, intrapulmonary vessels (0.1–0.3 mm). This pattern is consistent with the larger lung volumes measured in normoxia and supports oxygen‐induced pulmonary vasodilatation having occurred during the 20 min these kits breathed 100% oxygen. Specifically, oxygen rapidly dilates the pulmonary vasculature most notably small, intrapulmonary PAs ranging in diameter from 0.1 to 0.3 mm. In contrast, hypoxic lungs exhibited reduced representation of both distal resistance‐level arteries and larger proximal vessels (> 0.5 mm).

Discussion

This study provides an optimized contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT protocol enabling simultaneous, high‐resolution visualization of the DA and pulmonary vasculature in fetal rabbit kits under controlled oxygen conditions. Hypoxic kits were immediately anesthetized prior to the first breath to prevent postnatal circulatory transition, whereas normoxic kits were allowed to respire in an oxygen‐enriched chamber before anesthesia. Among the contrast agents evaluated, unfiltered 25% barium sulfate in 3% gelatin provided the most consistent and comprehensive vascular opacification, supporting reliable assessment of both DA morphology and pulmonary arterial branching across a broad range of vessel diameters ex vivo.

Using this approach, distinct structural differences were observed between hypoxic and normoxic conditions. In hypoxic kits, the DA remained patent, whereas in normoxic kits the DA was constricted and functionally closed. In parallel with normoxic DA constriction, the pulmonary vasculature demonstrated vasodilatation, manifest on CT imaging as increased branching complexity under normoxic conditions, with improved visualization of more small distal pulmonary arterial segments. These findings are consistent with the well‐established physiology of the circulatory system transition at birth, in which increased oxygen exposure is associated with DA constriction and vasodilatation of intrapulmonary arteries.

Accurate quantitative assessment and segmentation of the pulmonary vasculature requires both robust analysis software and optimized imaging parameters, particularly when resolving vessels across a range of diameters [45, 46]. Quantitative analysis revealed that normoxic lungs had a greater perfused volume and a higher count of detectable pulmonary vessels compared to hypoxic lungs. Quantification of PA caliber indicated that most PA vessels in both groups fell within the 0.1–0.3 mm diameter range. Notably, differences in counts of PAs between oxygen conditions were primarily driven by an oxygen‐induced increase in mid‐sized (0.1–0.3 mm) PAs, whereas variations in smaller (0.05–0.1 mm) and larger (> 0.3 mm) vessels were less pronounced. This pattern suggests that oxygen exposure is associated with vasodilatation and enhanced detectability of mid‐sized pulmonary arterial branches within the lung parenchyma, rather than uniform changes across all intrapulmonary arteries.

The ability to resolve these differences using micro‐CT highlights the sensitivity of the optimized imaging and segmentation approach. Unlike iodine‐based contrast agents such as Omnipaque, which preferentially enhanced larger vessels but insufficiently opacified the pulmonary vasculature, the barium‐gelatin formulation enabled consistent filling of both large and small vessels without evident perfusion‐related artifacts. This property is particularly advantageous for non‐survival imaging studies and permits comprehensive 3D characterization of vascular architecture. However, Omnipaque remains advantageous for in vivo applications due to its clinical approval, rapid systemic distribution and clearance, making it well‐suited for longitudinal and translational imaging studies.

Limitations

Although the protocol described in this study provides exceptionally high spatial resolution for detailed three‐dimensional visualization of the DA and pulmonary vasculature, it can only be used post‐mortem. In contrast, MRI is a valuable non‐invasive imaging modality that enables longitudinal and functional assessment in living animals. However, access to preclinical MRI systems is limited in many research laboratories, and the spatial resolution achievable with MRI is generally lower than that of micro‐CT for imaging these small cardiovascular structures. Thus, the two imaging modalities are complementary, with MRI being better suited for longitudinal and functional studies and contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT providing high‐resolution anatomical detail for terminal morphological investigations. Because micro‐CT provides structural information only, however, it does not directly assess blood flow or functional shunting; therefore, interpretations regarding DA closure and pulmonary vascular adaptation are based on morphological findings.

Additional limitations should also be considered. Segmentation and quantification of complex pulmonary vascular networks remain technically challenging, particularly for PAs smaller than 50 µm, and may be influenced by image resolution and threshold selection. Although Omnipaque is approved for in vivo use, the protocol described here incorporates gelatin to retain the contrast agent within the vasculature during post‐mortem imaging; therefore, this preparation is not applicable to in vivo studies. Finally, while emerging artificial intelligence‐based approaches may improve the scalability and reproducibility of vascular segmentation, further validation against established histological measurements is required.

Conclusions

This study established a robust contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT framework for 3D visualization and quantitative assessment of structural changes in the DA and pulmonary vasculature during the perinatal transition. By enabling simultaneous, high‐resolution imaging across a wide range of vessel diameters, this approach supports detailed characterization of oxygen‐associated vascular remodeling under controlled experimental conditions.

This platform may facilitate future investigations into the mechanisms underlying vascular remodeling and adaptation and support preclinical evaluation of therapeutic strategies targeting DA‐related pathologies, including PDA and PPHN. In addition, the optimized imaging and segmentation framework may be adaptable to other applications requiring comprehensive assessment of vascular architecture in small‐animal models.
