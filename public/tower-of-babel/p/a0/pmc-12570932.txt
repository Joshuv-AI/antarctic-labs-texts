Combination of a novel TCR Vβ chain-directed selective T cell activator with standard of care therapy for head and neck cancer improves antitumor responses and promotes regression of checkpoint-refractory tumor models
Authors: Francesca Rosato, Ginette Santiago-Sanchez, Kellsye P Fabian, Michelle R Padget, Jonelle K Lee, Clint Tanner Allen, Zhen Su, Madan Katragadda, Jacques Moisan, Andrew Bayliffe, Jeffrey Schlom, James W Hodge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) treatment, alone or in combination with standard anticancer therapies, has led to important progress in the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Yet, a significant proportion of patients with carcinogen-associated HNSCC (human papillomavirus (HPV)−) develop disease relapse or progression. Effective treatments for patients who have failed standard of care (SOC) treatment are lacking. STAR0602, a selective, bifunctional T cell agonist comprising an antibody targeting Vβ6 and Vβ10 T cell receptors fused to human interleukin-2, has demonstrated clinical activity in anti-programmed death-ligand 1 resistant tumors. A murine surrogate molecule, mSTAR1302, has been shown to induce tumor regression in multiple syngeneic tumor models and enhance antitumor activity in ICB-refractory settings. This study investigates the therapeutic benefit of mSTAR1302 combined with SOC in the mouse oral carcinoma (MOC)1 and MOC2 HNSCC tumor models.MethodsC57BL/6 mice bearing MOC1 or MOC2 tumors were treated weekly with mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody to determine antitumor efficacy and survival benefit. Immune populations and their effector functions were characterized by flow cytometry and cytokine assays. The tumor microenvironment’s immune architecture was analyzed by multiplex immunofluorescence. Gene expression analysis was conducted to gain an understanding of the mechanism of action of the combination therapy.ResultsCombination therapy with mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-PD-1 promoted robust antitumor activity, higher numbers of mice with complete resolution of tumors, and significantly prolonged survival compared with mSTAR1302 monotherapy or SOC treatment in MOC1 and MOC2 tumors. Tumor-free animals from cohorts treated with the combination therapy demonstrated protection against tumor rechallenge and an overall increase in antigen-specific T cells. Tumor growth inhibition was associated with the expansion of Vβ13 CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, characterized by heightened cytotoxic and effector potentials, in both the tumor and the periphery. The therapeutic effect elicited by the combination therapy was strongly dependent on interferon-γ expression. Thus, the combination of mSTAR1302 and α-PD-1 enabled control of tumors that progressed following platinum-containing treatment.ConclusionThese findings provide a rationale for the combination of STAR0602 and SOC therapy in the clinical setting for patients with recurrent or metastatic HPV− HNSCC.Keywords: Chemotherapy, Immunotherapy, T cell Receptor - TCR, Head and Neck Cancer, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor

Introduction

The global incidence of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs) continues to rise in many countries, with a predicted 30% annual increase in incidence by 2030.1 HNSCCs account for approximately 90% of all head and neck cancers and originate from the mucosal epithelia of the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx.2 Risk factors commonly linked to HNSCCs encompass tobacco and alcohol use, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, and Epstein-Barr virus infection.3 In contrast to virally driven oropharyngeal carcinomas that carry a fair prognosis, advanced-stage carcinogen-associated HNSCC carries a poor prognosis, with nearly half of all patients relapsing within 2 years despite maximum multimodal standard of care (SOC) treatment.4

Currently, based on the results of Keynote-048,5 6 the programmed death 1 (PD-1) inhibitor pembrolizumab alone or in combination with cisplatin and fluorouracil (FU) chemotherapy (based on programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) combined positive score (CPS)) is used as first-line treatment for patients with recurrent or metastatic HPV− HNSCC. Objective responses and clinical benefit will be observed in roughly 30% of patients. In June 2025, results from Keynote-6897 (NCT03765918) led to the approval of pembrolizumab for perioperative use in patients with surgically resectable advanced-stage HNSCC based on the observation of superior event-free survival with neoadjuvant plus adjuvant treatment added to SOC compared with placebo. Yet, even with the addition of perioperative pembrolizumab, 35% of patients with advanced-stage HNSCC will experience a recurrence within 2 years. Data-driven determination of how to increase objective response rates with initial treatment and how to treat patients with relapsed HNSCC who have progressed following one or more SOC treatments is a time-sensitive unmet need. Novel immunomodulatory therapies designed to exploit different mechanisms of immune cell engagement are therefore being evaluated clinically.8

Recently, a first-in-class bifunctional T cell receptor (TCR)-directed antibody fusion molecule “STAR0602” was described.9 10 STAR0602 is a bifunctional therapeutic molecule designed to selectively target, activate, and expand a subset of αβ T cells predominantly expressing the Vβ6 chain of the TCR. RNA sequencing analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) across several cancers revealed that Vβ6 TCR-expressing T cells are among the most frequently occurring Vβ chain T cell subsets and are common to all solid tumors, including HNSCCs.11 STAR0602 was therefore designed as an antibody-fusion molecule comprising a Fab fragment targeting the Vβ6 TCR, fused to a native human interleukin-2 (IL-2) cytokine, both of which are fused to a human IgG1 Fc region. With this molecule design, STAR0602 conditionally agonizes both the TCR and IL-2 receptors (IL-2R) in cis on the same Vβ6 T cell. The currently ongoing STARt-001 phase 1/2 monotherapy trial of intravenously infused STAR0602 has reported clinically meaningful antitumor activity in anti-PD(L)-1-resistant tumors, with an acceptable tolerability and safety profile in patients with advanced antigen-rich solid tumors (NCT05592626).12 A murine surrogate molecule of STAR0602, named mSTAR1302, was developed to target and expand the most abundant murine Vβ T cell subset (Vβ13) and comprises the same human IL-2 antibody fusion design. The simultaneous binding of TCR and IL-2R by STAR0602 and mSTAR1302 elicited vigorous antitumor activity in human ex vivo or murine syngeneic tumor models, respectively.9

In this study, we describe for the first time the antitumor efficacy of mSTAR1302 in combination with cisplatin and α-PD-1 in syngeneic models of highly (mouse oral carcinoma (MOC)1) and poorly (MOC2) immunogenic HPV− MOCs. The addition of the dual T cell agonist mSTAR1302 resulted in a potent antitumor response and long-term immunological protection compared with SOC treatment, which consisted of cisplatin plus α-PD-1. Combination therapy induced robust expansion and activation of T cells at the tumor site and in peripheral tissues, promoting tumor regression and a prolonged survival benefit over SOC or mSTAR1302 monotherapy. The multimodal therapy regimen presented in this study was found to be strongly dependent on interferon (IFN)-γ responses, and cytotoxic cells, including antigen-specific T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and M1 macrophages, were critical in the antitumor response induced by the combination therapy. Moreover, a robust antitumor activity was observed in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-refractory MOC1 tumors that had progressed during SOC treatment, demonstrating superior overall survival with acceptable safety and tolerability. While the prior study9 defined STAR0602 as a novel Vβ-directed IL-2 fusion molecule with broad preclinical activity, our work uniquely demonstrates its translational potential in HNSCC. By combining the murine surrogate mSTAR1302 with cisplatin and α-PD-1, we show superior efficacy in checkpoint-refractory models, providing a rationale for STAR0602 as part of multimodal therapy in recurrent or metastatic HPV− HNSCC.

Materials and methods

Reagents

mSTAR1302 was provided by Marengo Therapeutics (Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA) under a Collaborative Research and Development Agreement with the National Cancer Institute (NCI) (Bethesda, Maryland, USA). Cisplatin was obtained from West-Ward Pharmaceuticals (Eatontown, New Jersey, USA). Anti-mouse PD-1 (clone RMP1-14-CP153), CD8α (clone 2.43), CD4 (clone GK1.5), NK1.1 (clone PK136), and IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2) were purchased from Bio X Cell (Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA).

Cell lines and animals

Syngeneic C57BL/6 MOC1 and MOC2 cell lines were obtained from R Uppaluri at Washington University (St Louis, Missouri, USA) and were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium/F12 at a 2:1 mixture supplemented with 5% fetal calf serum (Fisher Scientific, Houston, Texas, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; Millipore, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA), 400 ng/mL hydrocortisone, and 5 mg/mL insulin (Sigma Chemical, St Louis, Missouri, USA). Murine colon MC38 carcinoma cells were cultured as described.13 All cell lines were used at low passage (<20) and tested negative for Mycoplasma (MycoAlert Kit, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). C57BL/6 mice were bred and maintained at the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, Maryland, USA) under pathogen-free conditions. All animal studies were approved and conducted in accordance with an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved animal protocol and using Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE1) reporting guidelines.14

Tumor models and in vivo experiments

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 8–12 weeks were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) in the right flanks with 5×106 MOC1 or with 1×105 MOC2 cells resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and mixed at 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, Massachusetts, USA). Mice were randomized into treatment groups (n=8–15 mice per group) when tumors reached 180–200 mm3 on day 12 (MOC1) or 100–120 mm3 on day 10 (MOC2). Treatment of MOC1 tumors was initiated with mSTAR1302 at a dose of 1 mg/kg, delivered intraperitoneally (i.p.), on days 12, 19, and 26. 5 mg/kg cisplatin and 200 µg α-PD-1 were injected i.p. on days 16, 23, and 30. According to the same treatment schedule, MOC2 tumor-bearing animals were injected with mSTAR1302 (1 mg/kg, i.p.) on days 10, 17, and 24 and subsequently treated with cisplatin (5 mg/kg, i.p.) and α-PD-1 (200 µg, i.p.) on days 14, 23, and 30. For depletion studies, MOC1-bearing mice were treated with α-CD8, α-CD4, and α-NK1.1 (100 μg, i.p.) antibodies on days 8, 9, and 10 post-tumor inoculation, followed by weekly injections for the duration of the experiment. Anti-IFN-γ (100 µg, i.p.) antibody was administered on days 10, 12, and 14, followed by three times weekly injections thereafter. Tumors were monitored two times per week, and tumor volume was determined as length×width2/2. At least 100 days after tumor resolution, naïve and tumor-free mice were implanted or rechallenged, respectively, with 5×106 MOC1 or 3×105 MC38 cells as described above and monitored up to 25 days post-rechallenge. Experiments on first-line and second-line therapy strategies were conducted using 8–12 week-old female C57BL/6 mice inoculated s.c. in the right flank with 5×106 MOC1 or 1×105 MOC2 cells resuspended in PBS and mixed at a 1:1 ratio with Matrigel. Mice were randomized into control and treatment groups (n=40 mice per group) when tumors reached 150–180 mm3 on day 12 (MOC1) or when they measured 80–100 mm3 on day 8 (MOC2). Treatment of MOC1 tumors with 5 mg/kg cisplatin and 200 µg α-PD-1 was administered i.p. on days 14 and 21, while MOC2 tumor-bearing mice were injected with SOC on days 8 and 15. Treated and untreated mice were randomized a second time on day 28 (MOC1) or day 21 (MOC2) into treatment groups (n=11 mice per group) after first-line therapy failure, defined as progression of tumor growth after two cycles of SOC therapy.15 Second-line treatment with mSTAR1302 (1 mg/kg, i.p.) and α-PD-1 (200 µg, i.p.) started on day 28 (MOC1) or day 21 (MOC2) and was delivered weekly until the end of the study. Mice were euthanized when tumor length reached 20 mm, tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3, if body weight loss of >20% was observed or tumor ulceration covered ≥50% of the surface. Mice were kept under observation daily for clinical signs, including hunched posture, lethargy, rough hair coat, sneezing, sniffling, labored breathing, discharge from eyes or nose, and/or lack of appetite.

Flow cytometry

Tumors and spleens from untreated and treated groups were excised on day 20 post-MOC1 tumor implantation and single-cell suspensions were obtained through mechanical and enzymatical dissociation. Cells were suspended in flow stain buffer composed of PBS and supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin, followed by a viability staining with Live/Dead fixable aqua dye set (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) diluted in PBS for 30 min on ice in the absence of light. Cells were washed with PBS and stained with anti-mouse CD16/32 FcR block antibody (Clone 2.4G2; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) for 10 min on ice. The following primary conjugated antibodies from BioLegend (San Diego, California, USA) were used: CD206-PECy7 (C068C2), CD279-PECy7 (29F.1A12), CD38-AF488 (90), CD4-BV605 (clone RM4-5), CD44-BV421 (IM7), CD80-AF647 (16–10 A1), CTLA-4-BV421 (UC10-4B9), F4/80-BV421 (BM8), Granzyme B-AF700 (QA16A02), TCR Vβ8.1, 8.2-FITC (KJ16-133.18). Additionally, CD11b-PerCP/Cy5.5 (M1/70), CD3-APC/Cy7 (17A2), CD62L-BV711 (MEL-14), and CD8-PerCP/Cy5.5 (53 6.7) antibodies were purchased from BD Biosciences. CD45-BV786 (30-F11), FoxP3-PE (FJK-16s), and Ki67-PE/Cy7 (B56) were obtained from Invitrogen (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Antibody staining of cell surface markers was conducted for 30 min on ice in the dark. Intracellular staining was performed using the FoxP3/Transcription Factor Kit (eBioscience, San Diego, California, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were acquired on an Attune Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher), and all analyses were performed using FlowJo V.10.8.2 (Ashland, Oregon, USA). The following conjugated primary antibodies were used to stain cisplatin-treated cells: TRAIL-R2-PE (clone MD5-1) and PD-L1-BV421 (clone 10F.9G2) from BioLegend, FAS-ligand PE/Cy7 (clone MFL3) from Invitrogen and H-2Kb-BV786 (clone AF6-88.5) from BD Biosciences. Live/dead fixable aqua staining from Thermo Fisher was used to exclude dead cells. Cell populations were gated on FSC-A×SSC A followed by single cells discrimination by FSC-A×FSC hour. Immune cell populations were identified and counted according to the following gating strategies: CD4+ T cells: Live/CD45+/CD3+/CD4+; CD8+ T cells: Live/CD45+/CD3+/CD8+; CD4+ effector memory T (Tem) cells: Live/CD45+/CD3+/CD4+/TCR Vβ8.1, 8.2+/CD44+/CD62L–; CD8+Tem cells: Live/CD45+/CD3+/CD8+/TCR Vβ8.1, 8.2+/CD44+/CD62L–; CD4+ central memory T (Tcm) cells: Live/CD45+/CD3+/CD4+/TCR Vβ8.1, 8.2+/CD44+/CD62L+; CD8+ Tcm cells: Live/CD45+/CD3+/CD8+/TCR Vβ8.1, 8.2/CD44++/CD62L+; regulatory T cells (Tregs): Live/CD45+/CD4+/CD25+/FoxP3+; M1 macrophages: Live/CD45+/CD11b+/CD38+/CD206–; M2 macrophages: Live/CD45+/CD11b+/CD38–/CD206+.

Cytokine analyses

Cytokine analysis was performed on tumor lysates and sera harvested at day 20 post-MOC1 tumor inoculation using the Olink Target 48 Mouse platform (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) by the Clinical Support Laboratory, Frederick National Laboratory (NCI, Frederick, Maryland, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tumor lysates were generated following homogenization of tumors in PBS using the gentle-MACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Sera were obtained from blood samples collected via mandibular bleed. Tumor supernatants and sera were stored at −20°C until analysis.

OPAL immunofluorescence

MOC1 tumors were harvested 19 days post-tumor implantation, preserved in Z-fix (Anatech, Richmond, British Columbia, Canada), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (VitroVivo Biotech, Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA). Slides with tumor sections from treated and untreated groups were stained using Opal 4-Color Manual IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following antibodies from Abcam (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) were used: anti-CD38 (ab216343), anti-CD4 (ab183685), anti-CD8 (ab217344), anti-F4/80 (ab6640), and anti-FoxP3 (ab215206). Slides were scanned with an Axio Scan Z1 Slide Scanner and Zen software (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Stained tumors were analyzed using automated cell counting based on Otsu thresholding and quantification of the number of nuclei in each region of interest. For each group, 10 representative images from each tumor were included in the analysis. A secondary-only staining method was used as a negative control.

Isolation and ex vivo peptide stimulation of CD45+ cells

For analysis of antigen-specific lymphocytes, tumors and spleens were excised on day 27 post-MOC1 tumor inoculation or on day 25 post-MOC2 tumor inoculation and processed to obtain single-cell suspensions. For each group, equal numbers of live cells were pooled from each tumor, and immune cells were isolated using Mouse CD45 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Tumor-derived and spleen-derived CD45+ single cell suspensions were plated in the presence of H2-Kb-restricted p15E604-611 (KSPWFTTL) peptide (1 µg/mL) or control HIV-gag (SQVTNPANI) peptide (1 µg/mL) for 16 hours, at 37°C and 5% CO2. For the analysis of TILs, antigen-presenting cells (APCs) were isolated from naïve mice, and a 5:1 ratio of APC to T cell was used in the assay. At the end of incubation, the Mouse IFNγ/IL-2/tumor necrosis factor alpha FluoroSpot Plus assay (Mabtech, Nacka Strand, Sweden) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Spots were imaged and enumerated using the Mabtech IRIS 2 FluoroSPOT reader (Mabtech). Analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism V.10.4.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA), and data were expressed as spot-forming units per 3×105 splenocytes and 1×105 TILs.

RNA analysis

MOC1 tumors were harvested 19 days post-tumor implantation and processed into single-cell suspensions by mechanical and enzymatical dissociation. Total tumor RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and the isolated RNA was analyzed using the nCounter PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, Washington, USA) by the Genomics Laboratory at the Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer Research. Gene expression analysis was conducted using the nSolver analysis software V.4.0.70 (NanoString Technologies) and Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen). Results were normalized to control housekeeping genes and adjusted to untreated samples as the categorical reference value. An abundance of immune cell populations was identified in treated groups using ROSALIND (ROSALIND, San Diego, California, USA) and the Nanostring Cell Type Profiling Module.

Tumor lysis assay

Spleens were excised from non-tumor-bearing female C57Bl/6 mice and single-cell suspensions were obtained by mechanical dissociation. Ammonium Chromide Potassium Lysis buffer (Quality Biological; Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA) was used to lyse red blood cells. NK cell isolation was performed by negative selection using a mouse NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 5×103 MOC1 tumor cells were plated per well in a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight at 37°C. MOC1 cells were exposed to cisplatin (5 µM) for 24 hours, and incubated with purified NK cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 25:1 for 18 hours. NK-mediated cell lysis was measured using an impedance-based xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis instrument (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA) and values were normalized to untreated samples.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.8.2 (GraphPad Software). One-way or two-way analysis of variance was performed to compare more than two groups with Tukey’s post-hoc analysis for correction. Survival proportions were calculated with the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Fisher’s test was applied to pathway analyses. The non-parametric Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was was applied to flow cytometry histograms to compare distributions between two groups in FlowJo V.10.8.2 (BD Biosciences). P values less than 0.05 were considered significant and are listed in each figure (*p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001). Error bars in each graph indicate mean±SEM.

Results

mSTAR1302, cisplatin and α-PD-1 combination therapy exerts antitumor activity in MOC1 and MOC2 murine oral squamous cell carcinoma models

Patients with metastatic or unresectable HNSCC are currently treated with the α-PD-1 antibody pembrolizumab or pembrolizumab-chemotherapy (platinum±FU). In this study, we investigated whether the addition of mSTAR1302 to the SOC therapy (consisting of cisplatin+α-PD-1±5 FU) could improve tumor suppression in a mouse model of HPV− HNSCC. To this end, we used the C57BL/6 syngeneic MOC models as they display genetic alterations that highly mirror those of human carcinogen-induced oral cancers.5 6 16 17

MOC1-bearing mice were treated with mSTAR1302 once a week, staggered with weekly injections of cisplatin and α-PD-1 (figure 1A). When mSTAR1302 was administered as monotherapy, it showed antitumor activity compared with the untreated control (p=0.003; figure 1B) but did not result in tumor-free animals (figure 1C) or improved survival (figure 1D). Similarly, systemic treatment with cisplatin and α-PD-1 slowed tumor growth (p=0.005; figure 1B) and improved overall survival (p=0.02; figure 1D) compared with untreated tumors; however, tumor resolution was not observed in this cohort (figure 1C). Treatment of MOC1 tumors with cisplatin+α-PD-1+5 FU did not show any benefit in tumor growth inhibition compared with cisplatin+α-PD-1, confirming the efficacy of a SOC regimen without 5-FU (figure 1B inset). Hence, the present study refers to SOC therapy as the combination of cisplatin+α-PD-1 without 5-FU. The triple combination of cisplatin, α-PD-1, and mSTAR1302, however, induced a strong inhibition of tumor growth compared with the untreated control, SOC, or mSTAR1302 monotherapy (p<0.0001; figure 1B). Treatment with the combination therapy resulted in complete tumor resolution in three out of nine animals (figure 1C) and significantly enhanced overall survival compared with the untreated (p=0.0005), SOC therapy (p=0.002), or mSTAR1302 monotherapy (p=0.01) groups (figure 1D). Analysis of an alternative treatment schedule revealed that the combinatorial and synergistic effects of cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 in tumor suppression were diminished when the three agents were administered concurrently once a week (online supplemental figure S1). The cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination provided protection from tumor growth when tumor-free mice were challenged with MOC1 tumors for a second time, as opposed to naïve control mice implanted with the same tumors (figure 1E). The safety profile of all the treatments was monitored, and the animals were kept under observation for weight loss as an indication of severe toxicity. None of the combinations resulted in adverse side effects throughout the entire study duration (figure 1F).

As the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy demonstrated tumor suppression and increased overall survival in the immunogenic MOC1 tumor model, we further investigated its antitumor activity in a second, poorly immunogenic MOC2 tumor model. In contrast to MOC1, this cell line generates aggressive tumors with low PD-L1 and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I expression.16 17 Animals treated with SOC exhibited a degree of tumor growth inhibition compared with untreated controls (p<0.0001) or mSTAR1302 monotherapy (p=0.002) (figure 1G). The addition of mSTAR1302 to cisplatin and α-PD-1, however, resulted in a greater suppression of tumor growth compared with the control group (p<0.0001) and the SOC-treated and mSTAR1302-treated groups (p<0.0001). Similarly to what was observed for MOC1-bearing animals, the treatments were well-tolerated by mice inoculated with MOC2 tumors and treated with the triple combination therapy, confirming the safety and tolerability of the regimen (online supplemental figure S2).

Taken together, these data indicate that the combination therapy of mSTAR1302, cisplatin and α-PD-1 could (1) potentiate the antitumor activity of SOC, (2) provide long-term immune protection and (3) extend overall survival in immunogenic MOC1 as well as delay tumor growth in poorly immunogenic MOC2 tumor-bearing animals.

Combination therapy with mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-PD-1 induces greater antitumor activity compared with monotherapy or doublet therapy in the MOC1 tumor model

Platinum compounds (cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin) belong to the most active drugs in the treatment of solid tumors, but their systemic administration often results in significant adverse effects, particularly as a result of dosing with cisplatin.18 Therefore, we evaluated whether the doublet combination therapy of α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 would be sufficient to inhibit MOC1 tumor growth compared with the cisplatin+mSTAR1302 or the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination. To this end, the treatments were given to MOC1-bearing mice according to the schedule illustrated in figure 1A. The individually administered mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-PD-1 monotherapies did not demonstrate a substantial benefit in tumor growth control compared with the untreated group (p=0.02, p=0.6, p>0.9, respectively; figure 2A). Additionally, none of the animals treated with single agents showed tumor resolution at the end of the study (figure 2B), and a limited improvement in median overall survival (mOS) was observed over the untreated control (figure 2C). Conversely, we observed a potent inhibition of MOC1 tumor growth with the doublet combination therapies cisplatin+mSTAR1302 and α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 (figure 2A). Cisplatin+mSTAR1302 significantly induced tumor regression compared with untreated controls, mSTAR1302, and cisplatin monotherapies (p<0.0001). The antitumor response resulted in a survival benefit compared with untreated (p<0.0001), cisplatin (p<0.0001), and mSTAR1302 (p=0.01) single therapies. Likewise, the α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy was potent against MOC1 tumors, with a significant inhibition of tumor growth compared with untreated (p<0.0001), mSTAR1302 (p=0.0007), and α-PD-1 (p<0.0001) monotherapies (figure 2A). Notably, the α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 resulted in two out of nine mice being tumor-free at the end of the study (22.2% tumor resolution), along with the triple combination cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 (44.4% tumor resolution, figure 2B), and a significant increase in mOS (p=0.0001 over untreated, p=0.014 over SOC, p=0.02 over mSTAR1302 monotherapy; figure 2C). In conclusion, the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 therapy achieved the greatest control over tumor growth and a superior survival benefit compared with monotherapy and doublet combination therapies.

Combination therapy with mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-PD-1 increases infiltration of immune cells with a cytotoxic profile in the MOC1 tumor model

HPV− HNSCC tumors are among the most highly immune-infiltrated cancer types. High levels of immune infiltration are, however, often accompanied by high degrees of immunosuppression, which is frequently mirrored by a high Treg/CD8+ T cell ratio within the tumor microenvironment (TME).19 We therefore decided to elucidate the changes in immune components induced by the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy within the TME by multiplex immunofluorescence. MOC1 tumor-bearing mice were treated according to the schedule illustrated in figure 1A until day 19 post-tumor implantation, when tumors were excised and stained to identify immune cell populations. Immunofluorescent staining revealed that tumors from the cohort treated with the triple combination therapy showed a fourfold increase in CD8+ T cells within the tumor lesion compared with the untreated and SOC-treated cohorts (p<0.0001; figure 3A,B). Similarly, treatment with mSTAR1302 monotherapy resulted in a higher presence of CD8+ T cells infiltrating the tumor compared with the control groups (p<0.0001). In contrast, the frequency of CD4+ T cells remained consistent across the SOC or mSTAR1302 monotherapy groups but showed a 1.5-fold increase in the combination therapy cohort.

Next, we proceeded to evaluate the presence of F4/80+ tissue-resident macrophages, characterized by the expression of the surface marker CD38, as it has been associated with a proinflammatory, M1 phenotype.20 Analysis of double F4/80+CD38+ across tumor sections (overlap of green and red staining, figure 3A) revealed a higher frequency of macrophages for animals treated with SOC therapy (p=0.0003), mSTAR1302 (p=0.0003), and triple combination therapy (p<0.0001) over untreated controls. Still, no significant difference was observed among the treated groups (figure 3B). In contrast to CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, F4/80+CD38+ macrophages appeared to be predominantly concentrated in specific areas of the tumors, rather than being evenly dispersed across the entire lesions. Lastly, we examined the frequency of CD4+FoxP3+ T cells (as indicated by the overlap of green and red staining, figure 3A,B) across the groups. We observed that treatment with cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 had no significant impact on CD4+ Tregs compared with the untreated cohort in the tumor.

A transcriptome analysis was performed with bulk MOC1 tumors from each treatment cohort. Cell type profiling confirmed that the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy significantly increased the presence of cytotoxic cells within the TME compared with the untreated control and, in contrast to cisplatin+α-PD-1 treatment or mSTAR1302 monotherapy, with a significant preponderance of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (figure 3C). Accordingly, gene expression analysis revealed a remarkable upregulation of Gzmb (granzyme B) and Gzma (granzyme A) at the transcript level in the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 cohort, alongside an increased expression of cytokines and chemokines contributing to positive regulation of the immune response (figure 3D). Among these, IFN-γ was identified as one of the most upregulated genes in the combination therapy group relative to untreated tumors. In contrast, several genes implicated in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and angiogenesis, such as Syt17, Ceacam1, and Itgb4, among others, were shown to be downregulated by several folds in the combination therapy cohort compared with other treated cohorts. These findings confirm the observed tumor regression (figure 1B) and the higher frequency in CD8+ T cells within the TME (figure 3A,B) in cohorts treated with the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy compared with the control, SOC, or mSTAR1302 groups.

Treatment with mSTAR1302 or combination therapy with mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-PD-1 promotes preferential expansion of CD8+ T cells and M1 macrophages in the TMEs

We further characterized the immune infiltrative landscape of MOC1 tumors in untreated and treated cohorts by flow cytometry. In relation to the total tumor weight, the combination therapy cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 and mSTAR1302 administered as a single agent promoted an overall increase in the frequency of CD45+ cells within the TME, with a notable expansion of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (figure 4A, online supplemental figure S3). Within the T cell subsets, we noted a sevenfold increase in the TCR Vβ13+ CD8+ T cell population and a 4-fold increase in the TCR Vβ13+ CD4+ T cell subset induced by mSTAR1302 as monotherapy or in the combination group (figure 4B). Frequencies of effector (Tem; CD44+CD62L−) and central memory (Tcm; CD44+CD62L+) Vβ13+ CD8+ T cells increased in the mSTAR1302 monotherapy and combination groups compared with the untreated control, although the increase was not statistically significant (figure 4C). Similarly, the frequencies of effector and central memory Vβ13+ CD4+ T cells were significantly higher in the mSTAR1302-treated and combination-treated tumors (p=0.0061, p=0.0087, respectively) compared with untreated tumors. The proliferative ability (Ki67+) of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells was consistently higher in tumors treated with mSTAR1302, either as a single agent or as part of triple combination therapy (figure 4D). Most strikingly, the functional capability of activated CD8+ T cells (CD44+GrzB+) in the combination group significantly increased by fourfold over all the other cohorts (untreated, p=0.0001; SOC; p=0.0007, mSTAR1302, p=0.0005, figure 4E), as elucidated by gene expression analysis as well. We detected a decrease in the PD-1 surface expression of all CD8+ T cells following treatment with mSTAR1302 or cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302, while it remained constant on CD4+ T cells across different treatment groups (figure 4F). Moreover, the T cell expansion in these cohorts shifted the balance between CD8+ T cells and suppressive Treg population in the TME, thereby improving antitumor activity (figure 4G). Expansion and activation of Vβ13+ CD8+ and CD4+ T cells was not associated with an increase in exhaustion markers (PD-1, Tim-3) on the cell surface at the indicated point in time (online supplemental figure S4). Similar observations in the frequency of immune cell populations following triple combination therapy resulted from flow cytometry analysis of peripheral, spleen-derived CD45+ cells, characterized by a marked expansion of Vβ13+ CD8+ and CD4+ T cells with highly proliferative activity and cytotoxic potential (online supplemental figure S5). We quantified the presence of intratumoral myeloid (CD11b+) cells in all cohorts and observed that tumors treated with the triple combination therapy present a higher frequency of macrophages (CD11b+F4/80hi) characterized by a more pronounced proinflammatory M1 phenotype (CD38+CD206−), as opposed to protumorigenic M2 macrophages (CD38−CD206+). Although not statistically significant, the combination therapy improved the M1/M2 ratio compared with the untreated or SOC-treated cohorts, shifting the balance toward more potent antitumor activity (figure 4H).

Overall, these results indicate that the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy drives a substantial expansion of Vβ13+ CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, characterized by high proliferative potential and cytotoxic capability. At the same time, proinflammatory tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) expand alongside the lymphocytes and adopt a more proinflammatory phenotype that ultimately results in enhanced primary tumor control.

Combination therapy with mSTAR1302, cisplatin, and α-PD-1 promotes the release of immunostimulatory cytokines and increases tumor antigen-specific immune cells in the TME

Proinflammatory cytokines are key contributors to cancer immunotherapy, influencing the balance between tolerance and immunity, and thereby affecting the clinical outcome.21 We interrogated tumor supernatants from the different cohorts for selected immune factors, aiming for a comprehensive understanding of cytokine-mediated inflammatory processes. An analysis of intratumoral mediators revealed that tumors treated with cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 were enriched in cytokines, chemokines and factors associated with both the innate and adaptive immune responses (figure 5A). IL-2 and IFN-γ levels were significantly increased in the combination therapy cohort compared with the untreated group (p=0.001, p=0.0008, respectively), the cisplatin+α-PD-1 group (p=0.0026, p=0.0013), and the mSTAR1302 group (p=0.019, p=0.002). Similarly, the granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor-2 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 Ccl2 levels were elevated following treatment with cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302, thereby providing a suitable environment for the expansion of M1-polarized macrophages described in figure 4H. The observed increase of Ccl4, Ccl11 and Ccl17 in the TME of MOC1 tumors treated with the triple combination (2-fold, 3-fold and 60-fold increase compared with other cohorts, respectively) points to the high levels of NK and T cell infiltration occurring within the tumors, given their strong association with CD8+ T cell recruitment and activation,22 as observed also in figure 3A,B. However, elevated levels of Ccl22, considered a potent chemoattractant for Tregs and immune suppression, were also shown in tumors from the combination therapy cohort. Consistent with these observations, we observed elevated levels of the aforementioned cytokines and chemokines in the sera of treated mice, supporting a robust immune response and tumor regression (online supplemental figure S6).

We next evaluated the antigen specificity of TILs and peripheral immune cells involved in the antitumor response. We performed FluoroSpot assays with the MOC1-derived, H-2Kb-restricted T cell p15E antigen, an immunodominant tumor rejection epitope highly expressed in various tumor cell lines.23 24 To this end, CD45+ TILs and splenocytes were isolated from tumors and spleens, respectively, and harvested on day 27 post-tumor implantation. Unstimulated TILs and splenocytes collected from each treatment cohort were examined for their functional status by IFN-γ production in response to p15E (figure 5B,C). CD45+ TILs from tumors in the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 cohort produced higher levels of IFN-γ compared with untreated, SOC- or mSTAR1302-treated groups. Similarly, peripheral immune cells collected from the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy group were more responsive to p15E antigen stimulation. IFN-γ production from p15E-specific splenocytes was significantly elevated relative to that in the untreated control (p=0.0007), cisplatin+α-PD-1 (p=0.0009) and mSTAR1302 (p=0.0076) groups. Notably, a comparable response was observed in MOC2 tumors treated with the triple combination therapy, in which both TILs and splenocytes demonstrated antigen specificity after exposure to the tumor p15E peptide (online supplemental figure S7).

Cytotoxic cells and IFN-γ are required for the antitumor activity elicited by the combination therapy with mSTAR1302, cisplatin and α-PD-1

Immunofluorescence data (figure 3A,B), transcriptome analysis (figure 3C,D), and flow cytometry interrogation of immune cells (figure 4) suggested that the observed antitumor response in MOC1 tumor-bearing mice treated with the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy was mediated by cytotoxic cells and highly dependent on IFN-γ response. Therefore, in vivo depletion studies were performed to investigate the functional contribution of T cells, NK cells, and IFN-regulated cells to the therapeutic effect. Individual depletion of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells significantly diminished the ability of cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 treatment to control tumor growth (p<0.0001, figure 6A). In addition, tumor suppression was significantly impacted when NK cells were depleted (p=0.049) and following IFN-γ depletion (p<0.0001). Complete tumor resolution mediated by the triple combination therapy was entirely abolished when mice were depleted of CD8+ T cells or IFN-γ response (0/11) and was partially lost in mice depleted of CD4+ T cells and NK cells (1/11) (figure 6B). Consequently, the survival benefit achieved by the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 treatment was strongly reduced in each depletion group (figure 6C). The contribution of NK cells to the antitumor activity observed with the triple combination therapy was further investigated in vitro in NK-mediated killing assays of MOC1 tumor cells following treatment with cisplatin for 24 hours (online supplemental figure S8). As previous studies demonstrated that cisplatin increases cell surface levels of markers that aid the antitumor immunity,25 we observed changes in MOC1 phenotype (MHC class I, TRAIL-R2, Fas ligand and PD-L1) induced by cisplatin that ultimately resulted in enhanced killing by purified NK cells, as opposed to untreated MOC1 cells.

Combination therapy with mSTAR1302 and α-PD-1 after SOC failure elicits antitumor effect and promotes recapture of antitumor responses in ICB-refractory tumors

First-line treatment in patients with HPV− HNSCC often fails to achieve a complete response, and residual disease or progressive disease (PD) might persist.26 More than 80% of patients with recurrent or metastatic disease progress on ICB treatment, and, at the time of tumor recurrence, overall survival is generally 6–12 months.27 28 Patients with persistent disease may qualify for second-line therapy with timely initiation after detection of PD or relapse.29 We therefore investigated the potential of mSTAR1302 as a single agent or in combination with α-PD-1 in promoting antitumor activity in those tumors that had progressed during SOC treatment. In this model, cisplatin was not included in the treatment regimen, due to its low tolerability in patients undergoing second-line treatment and the risks of irreversible kidney damage, neurotoxicity, and ototoxicity resulting from its cumulative nature.30 Mice were treated with two cycles of SOC therapy and, at tumor progression (day 28), second-line therapy with α-PD-1, mSTAR1302, or doublet therapy with α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 was administered once a week (figure 7A). Treatment with α-PD-1 or mSTAR1302 monotherapy did not show improvements in tumor growth inhibition in comparison to the SOC regimen (figure 7B,C). On the contrary, α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 resulted in better control of MOC1 tumors that had advanced under the SOC regimen (p=0.02, figure 7B) and in 20% of tumor resolution at day 45 (2/10 tumor-free animals, figure 7C). Mice treated with the α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy following front-line treatment failure maintained stable disease, with control over tumor volume below 500 mm3, or presented shrinkage of primary lesions. Moreover, this combination therapy did not elicit adverse effects on the health of the animals, as evaluated by changes in animal weight (online supplemental figure S9). Similarly, treatment with mSTAR1302 and α-PD-1 following SOC therapy failure has demonstrated its antitumor activity in the immunologically cold MOC2 tumor model (online supplemental figure S10), in which the growth of primary lesions resulted significantly diminished in comparison to untreated control group (p<0.0001), α-PD-1 single agent (p=0.003) or mSTAR1302 monotherapy (p=0.023). These results indicate that there still may be a substantial therapeutic advantage with α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 combination therapy for those tumors that appear α-PD-1 resistant and show disease progression on a SOC regimen.

Discussion

In recent years, immunotherapy has led to significant improvements in survival and disease control for patients with HNSCC, particularly in the recurrent or metastatic setting. While HPV+ HNSCC is more sensitive to treatment with favorable survival, HPV− HNSCC urgently requires more effective therapies.31 The phase III KEYNOTE-048 clinical trial (NCT02358031) has led to the approval of pembrolizumab monotherapy or pembrolizumab plus platinum and 5-FU as first-line treatment in patients with locally advanced disease. This decision includes CPS in the treatment algorithm.5 6 More recently, the KEYNOTE-689 (NCT03765918) trial demonstrated a significant clinical improvement for patients with newly diagnosed, resectable, locally advanced HNSCC who had been treated with SOC and pembrolizumab, given as neoadjuvant and adjuvant.7 Despite the overall survival advantage of pembrolizumab-based therapies, however, neither treatment option significantly improved objective response rate or progression-free survival (PFS), emphasizing the unmet need in this population. Recent reports suggest that a combination of two immunotherapies may improve treatment response among patients with HNSCCs compared with current treatment regimens, highlighting a positive correlation between clinical response and the recruitment and expansion of CD8+ TILs exhibiting stem-like and tissue-resident gene programs.32,34

In the present study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of a dual T cell activator (mSTAR1302) in combination with the current SOC treatment for recurrent or metastatic HPV− HNSCC in a syngeneic murine tumor model. Unlike Hsu et al,9 which established STAR0602 as a novel therapeutic class, our work demonstrates its translational utility in combination with SOC therapy for checkpoint-refractory HNSCC. The MOC1 cell line was used as a tumor model, given that it generates tumors with immunogenic features that resemble HNSCC tumors in patients with oral cancer.35 We have previously demonstrated the efficacy of a SOC regimen consisting of an α-PD-1 antibody and cisplatin, administered in the absence of 5-FU, in treating MOC1 tumors.36 Hence, the present study refers to SOC therapy as the combination of cisplatin+α-PD-1 without 5-FU. Our results confirm and extend previous studies, in which mSTAR1302 was shown to induce the de novo expansion of Vβ13 T cell subsets in the periphery and in tumors, resulting in a novel memory-like effector phenotype. This ultimately rejuvenates antitumor T cell responses and leads to potent antitumor activity in several in vivo models.9 11 We demonstrated that the addition of mSTAR1302 to cisplatin and α-PD-1 results in enhanced MOC1 tumor control, extended overall survival, and increased rate of tumor resolution in tumor-bearing mice compared with the current SOC or mSTAR1302 monotherapy (figures1BD and 2). We identified a synergistic effect between the three agents when mSTAR1302 was given before cisplatin and α-PD-1 (figure 1), as opposed to concurrent administration (online supplemental figure S1). In this study, the concomitant delivery of chemotherapy and mSTAR1302 was shown to negatively impact the mSTAR1302-induced proliferation of Vβ13 T cells and did not result in tumor control or a survival benefit in the combination therapy group. The antitumor response generated by the triple combination therapy exhibited durable protection from tumor rechallenge, suggesting an immunological memory response partly associated with the expansion of p15E tumor antigen-specific T cell clones (figures1E 5B). Concurrently, T cell stimulation induced by α-PD-1 in the combination therapy appears to be essential for tumor control, as the doublet cisplatin+mSTAR1302 therapy resulted in a lower tumor-free survival rate (figure 2). Given the advantage demonstrated in tumor resolution, the α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 therapy may provide a therapeutic option for those cases where cisplatin-induced toxicity must be avoided.

Emerging evidence suggests that ICB-mediated clinical responses rely on tumor infiltration by T cells that can recognize and kill cancer cells.37 For instance, the intensity of pre-existing CD8+ T cell infiltrates at the tumor margin in patients with melanoma has been found to be predictive of clinical response to α-PD-138 and α-CTLA-4 therapies.39 Thus, therapeutic modalities that promote the expansion and infiltration of more persistent Tem cells with low exhaustion potential into tumors might improve overall patient prognosis and clinical responses. Treatment with cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 resulted in a higher frequency of CD8+ and CD4+ T cell infiltrates within the TME of MOC1 tumors in comparison to untreated or SOC groups (figure 3A,B). Expansion and proliferation of T cells, both in the tumor and the periphery, appear to be driven by the presence of mSTAR1302, as tumors treated with mSTAR1302 monotherapy exhibit a similar degree of Vβ13 TIL expansion. The higher density of intratumoral T cells induced by mSTAR1302 correlated with tumor regression and extended overall survival, thereby reinforcing the rationale for its combination with therapies that target the PD-1/PD-L1 axis (figures1 2).

Here, we demonstrate that the immune infiltration observed in the combination therapy group was associated with cytolytic activity and activation of effector signaling pathways (figures3C,D and 4CE). mSTAR1302 induced the expansion of Vβ13 T cells that expressed a central and effector memory immune signature (CD44+ and CD62L− or CD62L+), and a substantial increase in proliferative (Ki67+) and cytotoxic (GrzB+) potential. The selective expansion of this smaller subset of clonally diverse T cell populations as a distinct modality of T cell activation retains several advantages over other mechanisms of activation.9 As opposed to agonist anti-CD3 antibodies, which engage pan T cell populations with high affinity and avidity, mSTAR1302’s mechanism of action is poised to minimize overactivation and potential toxicity observed with polyclonal T cell redirection, such as severe cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, and on-target, off-tumor toxicity.40 41 The significant upregulation of GrzB, as shown by gene expression analysis and flow cytometry of tumors treated with the triple combination therapy, plays a crucial role in the effective antitumor response (figures3D 4E). Several studies have shown that increased GrzB expression, especially in the ICB therapy setting, correlates with durable clinical benefit and prolonged PFS survival.42 43 In contrast to non-selective TCR agonists (eg, anti-CD3 monoclonal antibodies) or high dose lymphokines (eg, high dose IL-2/aldesleukin), the combinations with mSTAR1302 tested here were well tolerated and resulted in no adverse effects on the health of treated animals (figure 1F, online supplemental figures S1, S9 and S10), further supporting the potential for clinical trials of STAR0602 in this combination regimen. We confirmed the crucial involvement of cytotoxic cells in the antitumor response by conducting depletion studies in which depletion of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, or NK cells resulted in the loss of antitumor activity by the cisplatin+α-PD-1+mSTAR1302 regimen, therefore concluding that these immune populations are primarily responsible for tumor control in MOC1 tumors (figure 6). The inflamed transcriptional state, characterized by a remarkable increase in the expression of granzyme B, granzyme A, and IFN-γ genes by activated immune cells in the combination group, is associated with chemokine expression, the upregulation of downstream cytotoxic molecules, and effector functions (figures4E 5A). These tumor-specific cells appear to be strongly dependent on IFN-mediated responses, as a nearly complete loss in antitumor efficacy was observed when the triple combination therapy was administered to IFN-depleted tumor-bearing mice (figures3D 6). Our experimental results indicate that type I IFN activation and TME invasion by T and NK cells are crucial events occurring in MOC1 tumors treated with the triple combination therapy. Consequently, antitumor activity and a survival benefit were observed compared with SOC therapy, mSTAR1302 monotherapy, or PD-1–PD-L1 blockade alone. As TAMs can induce resistance to ICB therapy through immunoinhibitory signals,44 we investigated changes in the composition and phenotype of the myeloid compartment following combination therapy. This identified a substantial increase in proinflammatory M1 macrophages on treatment, as opposed to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, which remained unchanged in frequency (figures3A 4H). These results suggest that combination therapy also promotes a more immunologically active microenvironment and reprograms immune cells to secrete increased amounts of proinflammatory chemokines that aid the cytotoxic immune response, ultimately leading to tumor control. Moreover, previous studies demonstrated that a high frequency of polymorphonuclear-myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) in peripheral blood strongly correlates with poor overall survival in patients with advanced head and neck cancer.45 As T cell suppressive activity was found to be higher in PMN-MDSCs compared with monocytic (M)-MDSCs, we evaluated the presence of these two populations in all cohorts. We observed a decrease in the presence of PMN-MDSCs in tumors treated with the triple combination therapy, as opposed to M-MDSCs (online supplemental figure S4B).

For the first time, this study demonstrates that the use of mSTAR1302 in second-line therapy settings for recurrent or metastatic HPV− HNSCC could achieve tumor regression and control after initial disease progression (figure 7, online supplemental figure S10). The development of therapies for patients who do not respond to PD-1/PD-L1 therapy in earlier stages and experience PD is an urgent need in clinical oncology.46 Notably, STAR0602 demonstrated clinically meaningful antitumor activity in α-PD(L)-1-resistant and antigen-rich, high-tumor-mutational burden solid tumors, according to the latest update from the Phase 1/2 STARt-001 (NCT05592626).47 48 Among 42 patients who had received prior anti-PD(L)-1 treatment, the disease control rate with STAR0602 was 45% (n=19 of 42), and included an overall 52% (20/38) of patients presenting tumor regression. In addition, STAR0602 has entered a second combinational trial with a Trop-2-directed antibody-drug conjugate in a multicenter Phase 1/2 clinical study (STARt-002, NCT06827613) for patients with metastatic hormone receptor-positive, human EGF receptor 2-negative breast cancer and metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, as per a recent press release.49 Moreover, immunotherapy prior to curative surgery could constitute a promising strategy for treating locally advanced HNSCCs, as recently demonstrated by the KEYNOTE-689 trial,7 and the preclinical data reported here support the use of STAR0602 plus concurrent pembrolizumab for patients in the locally advanced setting as well.

In summary, our investigations reveal the immune-mediated mechanism of enhanced tumor control by mSTAR1302 combined with SOC therapy in a preclinical setting. In the HPV− HNSCC tumor model MOC1, the triple combination therapy demonstrated tumor regression, an increased rate of disease-free survival, long-term immune protection from tumor rechallenge, and activity in an ICB-refractory setting as opposed to mSTAR1302 monotherapy and SOC treatment with cisplatin + α-PD-1. While the MOC model represents an immunogenic and aggressive carcinoma highly similar to human HNSCC,49 further investigation in an orthotopic oral carcinoma model50,53 may extend the understanding of immune tumor control in relation to human HNSCC treated with mSTAR1302+cisplatin+α-PD-1.

Correlative studies from patients with α-PD(L)-1-resistant tumors (CPS<1) enrolled in ongoing trials with STAR0602 will provide further insights for the potential addition of this therapeutic molecule to frontline therapy or to second-line therapy after disease progression on or following platinum-containing treatment.
