What are the true medical concerns facing unsheltered homeless adults?
Authors: Randall Kuhn, Jessie Chien, Evan Michael Shannon, Sonali Saluja, Giovanni Righi, Mohammad Damra, Coley M King, Lillian Gelberg, Benjamin F Henwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEstimates of behavioral and physical health conditions among unsheltered populations are often used to guide policy initiatives for healthcare and housing, yet robust, disease-specific prevalence estimates remain limited. We address this gap by leveraging multiple data sources to generate more reliable estimates of specific disease conditions for unsheltered adults.MethodsWe triangulate data from three sources in Los Angeles County, which has the largest unsheltered population in the US: a probability-based survey of adults experiencing homelessness (n = 898), and electronic health records from two field-based medical providers (n = 1683; n = 527). We report univariate estimates by source and summarize across sources.ResultsAcross sources, median prevalence was 40% for mental health conditions (range: 40%-61%) and 33% for substance use conditions (range: 31%-43%). Post-traumatic stress disorder and major depression were the most common mental health conditions (median 22%), and stimulant use disorder was the most common substance use condition (median 17%). Physical health conditions were more common than both mental health and substance use conditions overall (median 57%; range: 49%-73%).ConclusionThese findings underscore that housing and health care are complementary components of care and support, integrating field-based medical services with permanent housing investments.Keywords: unsheltered homelessness, prevalence, health surveys, electronic health records

Introduction

Despite strong empirical evidence that the lack of affordable housing is the primary driver of homelessness in the United States (U.S.),1 debates framing mental illness, substance use, or individual behavior as “the real cause” continue to shape public policy.2,3 This debate was reignited in December 2025 when the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) released a Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO) claiming that 75% of unsheltered adults have a substance use condition and 78% have a mental health condition. While some have cast doubt on these statistics, little peer-reviewed evidence exists on the specific health conditions affecting unsheltered populations. We address this limitation by triangulating complementary survey and EHR data to provide the first disease-specific estimates of behavioral and physical health conditions for the unsheltered population of Los Angeles (LA) County, California, the largest in the U.S.

Background and existing estimates

Historically, information on the treatment needs of unsheltered populations has been limited. HUD defines unsheltered homelessness as living outside or in places not meant for human habitation, such as vehicles, tents, or abandoned buildings. Most research has focused on individuals residing in shelters or temporary housing. A recent systematic review found that few U.S.-based studies report prevalence estimates of mental health or substance use conditions among unsheltered populations.4 Similarly, an ongoing review of 17 EHR-based studies quantifying specific medical conditions among homeless adults revealed that none were conducted in predominantly unsheltered populations or distinguished between sheltered and unsheltered groups.

Among studies that report unsheltered status, findings vary widely and are often based on small or highly selected samples. For example, a longitudinal, clinic-based study of older homeless adults in Oakland, California, included 162 predominantly unsheltered individuals and found higher rates of mental health problems compared to sheltered participants: 71% reported a history of mental illness, 16.7% had a prior psychiatric hospitalization, and 60.5% had an illicit drug use disorder.5 In contrast, a medical-records-based study of 4034 unsheltered Veterans reported substantially lower prevalence, with 33.5% diagnosed with a mental health condition and 28.2% with a substance use condition, but did not report specific conditions or severity.6

The study cited in the HUD NOFO, conducted by the California Policy Lab (CPL), used vulnerability assessment data from homeless service providers in 15 states, reporting that 75% of unsheltered adults have a substance use condition and 78% have a mental health condition.7 Following publication, the authors issued two methodological notes clarifying that assessments were conducted among individuals that “case managers believe are medically vulnerable or disabled enough to qualify for fully supportive housing or housing”, resulting in a sample more medically vulnerable than the broader homeless population. They also noted that the self-reports reflected lifetime experiences and did not rely on any clinical criteria. The authors pointed instead to their 2021 study in LA County that linked street outreach data to safety-net health system diagnosis codes, finding that 17% of unsheltered individuals had a severe mental illness, including 7.3% with a psychotic spectrum disorder (no substance use estimates were reported).8

The past 5 years has seen a concerted effort to leverage new and existing data sources to generate better estimates of health conditions affecting unsheltered populations. During this time, LA County has seen the nation's largest scale-up of field-based medicine services for homeless populations. Through the California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal (CalAIM) initiative, LA Care Health Plan has empaneled 23 providers to develop comprehensive field-based medicine programs and bill field services as primary care, with considerable emphasis on high-quality EHR data.9 This study is one product of these efforts.

New data, new insights

We triangulate health condition prevalence estimates from survey and EHR data for unsheltered homeless adults aged 18+. We note that because most unsheltered adults frequently cycle indoors, and those who do tend to have poorer health, we report estimates for all individuals in the data, irrespective of unsheltered status at the time of assessment, in order to avoid selection on better health.

The first data source is the Periodic Assessment of Trajectories of Housing, Homelessness, and Health Study (PATHS), an ongoing monthly survey of LA County's homeless population.10 Compared to prior studies, PATHS offers several advantages, including coverage of a large, geographically diverse segment of the unsheltered population and of individuals not engaged with housing or healthcare systems. All respondents were unsheltered at the time of recruitment. We report self-reported endorsements of substance use, mental, and physical health conditions for 898 active respondents between August 2024 and August 2025. Details of PATHS data collection methods appear in the supplementary materials with full wording of health condition endorsements in Appendix Table S1.

To address potential recall and social desirability bias in PATHS survey data, we separately estimated disease condition prevalence using EHR data for unsheltered patients of two LA County field-based medicine providers: Venice Family Clinic (VFC), a Federally Qualified Health Center that operates the County's oldest field medicine program, and Healthcare in Action (HIA), the largest field medicine program in California. For both providers, we included patients classified as experiencing unsheltered homelessness based on a clinician-assessed housing trajectory screening or the use of any Z59.02 ICD code. For VFC, we included patient data for 2016-2023; estimates were nearly identical in pre- and post-COVID periods. For HIA, data covered 2025.

Health conditions were coded using a common ICD crosswalk intended to mirror the categories reported for PATHS, with the full set of ICD codes shown in Appendix Table S2. For both practices, we included only patients with at least 2 clinical encounters to ensure adequate reporting of conditions. Health conditions may have been recorded across multiple patient encounters that occurred when patients were sheltered or unsheltered, thus further justifying the inclusion of patients who were ever unsheltered rather than based on a point-in-time unsheltered status.

Across all three data sources, we summarized the prevalence of physical health, mental health, and substance use conditions in all data sources, and synthesized median and weighted mean estimates. We report the prevalence of any physical health, mental health, or substance use condition based on any reported conditions in that disease category. We also report detailed prevalence for any conditions with prevalence ≥2% in any source.

Key results

Weighted mean demographic estimates of the PATHS and EHR samples combined were similar to those of the overall unsheltered population in LA County11 (reported in Appendix Table S3). Differences across the three sources reflect differences in population and location.

Mental health conditions

Across the three sources, we estimated a median prevalence of any mental health condition of 40%. PATHS yielded higher estimates of any mental health condition compared to the EHR-based sources (61% vs 40%), largely reflecting greater sensitivity to common but clinically meaningful conditions such as anxiety (40%), depression (33%), and post-traumatic stress disorder (28%). Rates of schizophrenia/severe psychotic disorder were consistent across sources, ranging from 7% to 12%, compared to 1.2% in general US adult population.12

Substance use disorders

The estimated prevalence of substance use disorders ranged from approximately 31% to 43% (including alcohol and drug use; tobacco excluded), with a median prevalence of 33%. Estimates of opioid use disorder were highly consistent across sources, from 9% to 11%, compared to 0.5% for the general population.12 Greater variation was observed for alcohol use disorder (7%-11%) and stimulant use disorder (13%-27%), particularly between the two EHR systems. In both cases, PATHS estimates fell near the midpoint of the observed ranges (11% for alcohol use disorder, 19% for stimulant use disorder), suggesting that variability across EHR-based estimates may reflect differences in geography, patient mix, or clinical documentation practices rather than systematic measurement bias.

Physical health

Across all sources, the prevalence of physical health conditions was higher than that of mental health or substance use, with a range of 49%-73%. For physical health, PATHS generally produced lower prevalence estimates than the EHR-based sources, particularly for hypertension (11% compared to 26% for VFC and 29% for HIA). Other high-prevalence conditions included diabetes (6%-11%) and asthma (5%-16%). Estimates of HIV prevalence were consistent across sources, ranging from 3% to 5%.

Discussion

Using three independent data sources from the nation's largest unsheltered homeless population, we find that the prevalence of mental health and substance use conditions was about half of the estimates reported in the December 2025 NOFO issued by HUD. In other words, a substantial share of unsheltered adults have no documented behavioral health concern; they simply lack housing. Many others face a broad spectrum of health conditions, including both less severe behavioral disorders along with many physical conditions requiring complex care. We do observe a substantial burden of more severe behavioral health conditions, such as psychotic spectrum disorders and opioid or stimulant use disorder, affecting about one-quarter of the total population.

The HUD NOFO claimed extraordinarily high behavioral health burdens to cap funding for permanent supportive housing (PSH) at 30% and redirect funds toward behavioral health care, policing, encampment resolution, and involuntary psychiatric treatment.13 We argue that the broad mix of health conditions observed in our study suggest that we should view housing and healthcare not as competing priorities, but rather as complements along a continuum of care. First, prior research suggests that substance use is frequently a consequence of prolonged homelessness rather than its primary cause,14-16 reinforcing the importance of homelessness prevention, early intervention, and rapid access to housing. Second, conditions such as schizophrenia and opioid use disorder are best treated in community-based, non-institutional settings using a mix of long-acting pharmacologic treatments and psychosocial interventions,17-19 which are increasingly delivered through field-based programs capable of engaging individuals disconnected from traditional care systems.20 Finally, many of these services can be delivered on the street but are most effective when paired with the housing stability afforded by PSH. Some conditions cannot yet be treated without housing, including those with complex adherence requirements, with drug resistance concerns, requiring refrigeration, and with significant side effects like excessive toileting.21,22

In California, field-based medical programs increasingly leverage enhanced care management funding to provide navigation into PSH, leveraging the trust engendered by meeting people where they are at with high-value medical treatments and respectful care. Yet this potentially robust pipeline of care is threatened by recent changes to Medicaid financing and eligibility.23 While there is only limited evidence that medical programs can effectively serve as a conduit to housing,24 the solutions presented by the NOFO as alternatives to PSH are either far less well-established (eg, encampment resolution) or have been consistently associated with harms (eg, policing, involuntary commitment).25,26

Several limitations warrant consideration. PATHS relies on self-reported diagnoses, and EHR data reflect access to care and documentation practices, potentially introducing measurement bias. Our estimates did not capture the severity or acuity of conditions. Findings from LA County may not fully generalize to other regions, particularly for rates of stimulant and opioid use disorders. But given the long average duration of homelessness in LA County and the association between increased duration and substance use, these likely reflect an overestimate compared to many parts of the US.14

While HUD rescinded the NOFO shortly after its release, a revised policy is expected shortly. Durable progress in addressing unsheltered homelessness requires investments that reflect the actual distribution of need. The proposed cuts to PSH could return up to 45 000 people back into homelessness.27 This redirection of funds creates an artificial tradeoff between PSH and health services, despite evidence that both are essential. In LA County and beyond, new financing and coordinated service delivery systems can increasingly engage clients in meaningful pipelines of care that connect people in real time to the services they need in the short run while advancing toward housing as the ultimate goal.
