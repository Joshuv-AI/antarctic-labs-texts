Metabolomic fingerprinting of Indonesian beetroot across Java agroecological zones: A tool for geographical authentication and quality assessment
Authors: Elazmanawati Lembong, Siti Nurmilah, Gemilang Lara Utama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe metabolomic composition of beetroot (Beta vulgaris L.) is strongly shaped by environment, supporting geographical origin authentication. This study reports the first untargeted metabolomic profiling of Indonesian beetroot from Bandung (BDG), Semarang (SMG), and Malang (MLG) using liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-Q/TOF-MS). A total of 92 metabolites were identified, including flavonoids, stilbenoids, alkaloids, glycosides, and phenolic derivatives. Multivariate analyses—principal component analysis (PCA), analysis of variance (ANOVA), and sparse partial least squares discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA)—achieved clear regional separation. Biomarkers were region-specific: BDG enriched in 2-hydroxy-3,4-dimethoxy-isoflavam-7-O-β-glucoside (loading weight > 0.8), MLG in 6-hydroxy-2-[2-(4′-methoxyphenyl)ethyl]chromone and decuroside VI (loading weight > 0.5), and SMG in licochalcone A and thalictricoside (loading weight > 0.5). Network analysis highlighted biosynthetic clustering (phenylpropanoid–stilbenoid in BDG, chromone–glycoside in MLG, chalcone–flavonoid in SMG), reflecting adaptation to altitude, humidity, and UV exposure. Findings provide metabolite markers for authentication, quality control, and region-specific cultivation strategies.Keywords: Beetroot (Beta vulgaris L.), LC-Q/TOF-MS metabolomics, Geographical discrimination, Metabolite profiling, Marker compounds

Introduction

Metabolomics offers a powerful, non-targeted approach for the comprehensive characterization of local plant varieties, providing valuable insights into their biochemical composition and functional properties (Hao et al., 2025). By identifying small molecules that serve as biochemical fingerprints, metabolomics captures both genetic and environmental influences on plant tissues (Pinu, 2018). For crops such as beetroot (Beta vulgaris L.), metabolomic profiling connects biochemical diversity to regional cultivation practices and functional attributes.

Beetroot is a rich source of betalains, particularly betaxanthins, which have been shown to exhibit antimicrobial and anticancer activities (Spórna-Kucab et al., 2023). Its role as a functional food and natural colorant is driving demand globally, including in Indonesia. Previous studies, particularly those utilizing 1H NMR-based metabolomics, have demonstrated that beetroot metabolite levels vary according to factors such as harvest time and climate, affecting key compounds including choline, betaine, amino acids, and nitrates (Giampaoli et al., 2021). LC-Q/TOF-MS offers greater sensitivity than NMR, enabling detection of low-abundance metabolites valuable for origin authentication.

Environmental factors—collectively known as the “terroir” effect—influence plant metabolic profiles (Dawid & Hille, 2018). These factors contribute to distinct regional metabolic signatures that can be used for geographic authentication and quality assessment (Sun et al., 2021). Indonesia's diverse agroecological zones offer ideal conditions for studying terroir-driven metabolic variation in beetroot.

In the context of nutritional security and functional food development, a detailed metabolomic characterization of beetroot holds significant promise. Beetroot is especially noted for its high nitrate content, associated with cardiovascular health benefits, as well as its diverse antioxidant compounds (Varshney & Mishra, 2022). Mapping the metabolite composition of beetroot across different regions can reveal how environmental factors influence the levels of these bioactive compounds. Such insights not only enable geographical authentication but also inform cultivation practices aimed at enhancing nutritional and functional qualities in local beetroot varieties.

Beyond quality control and authentication, metabolomics also plays a critical role in understanding plant responses to environmental stress. Functional metabolomics has revealed how plants dynamically adjust their metabolite profiles under abiotic stress conditions such as drought, salinity, and temperature extremes (Dawid & Hille, 2018). In beetroot, such stress responses are particularly relevant, as environmental pressures can impact not only crop yield but also the concentration of valuable compounds like betalains and nitrates (Giampaoli et al., 2021).

As concerns over climate change and environmental sustainability grow, the role of metabolomics in supporting sustainable agriculture becomes increasingly important. Metabolite profiling can inform the development of cultivation strategies that align environmental conditions and agronomic inputs with optimal metabolic outcomes. Identifying specific environmental factors that promote the biosynthesis of health-promoting compounds enables the design of cultivation systems that are both high-quality and environmentally sustainable. This is especially significant for Indonesian agriculture, where a wide range of agroecological zones demands localized, science-based strategies.

This study provides the first LC-Q/TOF-MS-based untargeted metabolomic comparison of Indonesian beetroot from three major agroecological zones: Bandung, Semarang, and Malang. While previous research on beetroot metabolomics has largely focused on temperate regions, little is known about tropical environments where unique climatic and soil conditions may alter phytochemical composition. By characterizing metabolite profiles from each region, identifying region-specific markers, and assessing their potential for geographical discrimination, this work addresses a critical knowledge gap. The findings are necessary for developing reliable tools for geographic authentication, establishing regionally adapted quality standards, and informing cultivation strategies that optimize functional metabolite traits under Indonesian growing conditions.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

This study employed beetroot (Beta vulgaris L. var. rubra) samples cultivated in three prominent highland regions of Indonesia: Lembang (Bandung Barat Regency, West Java), Bandungan (Semarang Regency, Central Java), and Batu (Batu City, Malang, East Java). These regions were selected based on their favorable agroecological conditions, particularly high altitudes exceeding 900 m above sea level (MASL), which are considered optimal for beetroot cultivation.

Lembang is located at elevations ranging from 1300 to 2000 MASL, with average daily temperatures between 17 and 22 °C and annual rainfall of approximately 2000–3000 mm. The predominant soil type in this region is andosol, characterized by high fertility and substantial organic matter content. In Bandungan, elevations vary between 900 and 1500 MASL, with daily temperatures of 20–27 °C and annual precipitation reaching up to 3500 mm. The area features latosol and regosol soils, both derived from volcanic parent materials. Batu, situated at 900–1700 MASL, experiences average temperatures of 18–25 °C and annual rainfall ranging from 1500 to 2500 mm, and is dominated by inceptisol and andosol soils that support intensive horticultural production.

The cultivar used in this study was crimson globe, a beetroot variety commonly cultivated in Indonesia due to its adaptability to highland environments and favorable agronomic traits, such as a rounded shape and deep red pigmentation. Harvesting was conducted at approximately 100 days after sowing to ensure physiological maturity and the optimal accumulation of bioactive compounds, including betacyanins and phenolics.

Manual harvesting was performed in the early morning using sharp tools to minimize mechanical damage. Postharvest handling included washing the tubers with clean water to remove soil and debris, followed by air-drying under shaded conditions to reduce surface moisture. Leaves and stems were trimmed, leaving approximately 1 cm of petiole to reduce microbial spoilage risk. The tubers were then sorted to select only healthy, intact specimens and stored under refrigerated conditions (0–4 °C) to preserve bioactive compound stability prior to extraction.

A standardized postharvest protocol was implemented across all sites to reduce variability due to handling practices, thereby ensuring that any observed differences in bioactive compound levels could be attributed to environmental and geographical factors. From each sampling location, 10 to 15 beetroots were collected. To minimize micro-environmental bias, tubers were selected from multiple plots within the same cultivation area. Only visually uniform tubers, free from physical damage or disease, were included. A composite sample for each site was prepared by thoroughly mixing the selected tubers to ensure homogeneity. A stratified random sampling method was applied, wherein each cultivation area was divided into several distinct plots (strata), and random sampling was conducted within each plot to account for spatial variability. Tubers exhibiting deformities, decay, or atypical sizes (either excessively small or large) were excluded to maintain sample representativeness.

Sample preparation and liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry analysis

Beetroot extracts were prepared by macerating the raw material in ethanol containing 2 % citric acid (1:10 b/v) for 24 h at room temperature under airtight, light-protected conditions. The extract was filtered through filter paper, and the filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C. Concentrated extracts were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. When necessary, solid-phase extraction was applied for further purification. All extractions and analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3, biological replicates) to ensure reproducibility. Final filtrates were stored in amber vials at 4–8 °C until analysis (Lembong & Utama, 2020).

Antioxidant compounds, including polyphenols, flavonoids, and alkaloids, were profiled using liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-Q/TOF-MS). Separation was achieved on a reverse-phase C18 column with a gradient mobile phase of water (0.1 % formic acid) and acetonitrile or methanol (0.1 % formic acid). The injection volume was 2–10 μL, and the flow rate was 0.3–0.5 mL/min (Lee et al., 2005). Mass spectrometry employed electrospray ionization in both positive and negative ion modes, with full-scan acquisition (m/z 50–1200) for broad metabolite detection. High-resolution mass data enabled accurate molecular formula prediction, and Auto-MS/MS fragmentation supported structural elucidation. Compound identification was performed by matching fragmentation spectra with reference databases including METLIN, MassBank, and GNPS.

Data pre-processing

Raw LC-Q/TOF-MS data were initially processed using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis software (version 10.0) to perform peak detection and spectral deconvolution. The resulting data were then imported into Agilent Mass Profiler Professional (version 15.1) for comprehensive feature extraction and alignment. Parameters were set to a minimum absolute abundance threshold of 5000 counts to exclude low-intensity background noise, a retention time alignment window of 0.1 min, and a mass accuracy threshold of 10 ppm to ensure high precision in feature matching across samples. The curated feature list was exported for downstream statistical analysis and uploaded to the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) for further processing. Features detected in fewer than 80 % of samples within any experimental group were excluded to improve data robustness and reduce the influence of sparsely detected variables. To correct for systematic differences in total ion abundance across samples and ensure comparability, data were normalized by scaling each feature intensity to the total ion current of its respective sample, thereby adjusting for variations in overall signal intensity. Subsequently, the data were transformed using a base-10 logarithmic transformation to stabilize variance and approximate a Gaussian distribution. Finally, Pareto scaling was applied to reduce the impact of dominant variables while preserving the integrity of the data structure for multivariate statistical analysis.

Multivariate and statistical analysis

A comprehensive multivariate statistical workflow was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 to identify region-specific discriminant metabolites across different geographical origins. Initial unsupervised clustering was explored through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reveal inherent grouping patterns among the samples. To identify metabolites significantly varying across regions, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test was conducted, applying a false discovery rate (FDR) correction to control for multiple testing.

To pinpoint the most discriminatory features contributing to geographical classification, sparse partial least squares discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) was employed, with component tuning based on the balanced error rate and optimal feature selection guided by cross-validation. Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) using Euclidean distance and Ward's linkage was used to generate heatmaps, highlighting differential metabolite accumulation patterns across regions. Additionally, PatternHunter analysis was conducted to detect metabolites exhibiting similar abundance profiles, revealing potential biochemical relationships through correlation. Tentative identification of discriminant metabolites was achieved by matching accurate mass values (within a 10 ppm threshold) against online databases available, MS/MS fragmentation spectra were compared with spectral libraries to enhance annotation confidence.

Results and discussion

Compound features summary and overview

LC-Q/TOF-MS-based metabolomic profiling of beetroot samples from three distinct Indonesian regions—Bandung (BDG), Semarang (SMG), and Malang (MLG)—revealed pronounced metabolic differentiation, forming a robust chemical basis for geographical origin discrimination. Across all samples, 92 unique metabolites were identified, with clear region-specific distributions (Table 1). Semarang and Malang exhibited the highest metabolic diversity (46 metabolites each), while Bandung had 38. The presence of 26 unique metabolites in Bandung, 18 in Semarang, and 16 in Malang highlights strong local biochemical signatures, whereas the 7 metabolites common to all regions likely represent core beetroot constituents that are less influenced by environmental modulation.

Metabolite feature of LC-Q/TOF-MS profiling.

Bandung samples exhibited significantly elevated sucrose concentrations and high levels of complex flavonoids such as 8-C-β-D-[−2-O-(E)-p-Coumaroyl]-glucopyranosyl-2-[2-hydroxy]propyl-7-methoxy-5-methyl-chromone. Elevated sucrose levels can be mechanistically linked to increased carbon fixation and assimilate partitioning via enhanced activity of sucrose phosphate synthase (SPS) and invertase enzymes, both of which are promoted under high soil fertility and consistent moisture regimes (Baxter et al., 2003; Bharali & Baruah, 2022). Bandung's volcanic soils, rich in potassium and other micronutrients, may stimulate SPS activity, while stable humidity minimizes respiratory carbohydrate losses, leading to greater carbohydrate storage. The observed flavonoid enrichment is consistent with upregulation of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and chalcone synthase (CHS) in the phenylpropanoid pathway—processes often induced by moderate light stress and nutrient availability (Li et al., 2023).

Semarang samples contained senbusine B, a diterpenoid alkaloid absent from other regions, alongside high levels of 7-hydroxy-3′,4′-dimethoxy-isoflavan-5′,2′-di-O-β-D-glucoside and N-methyl laurotetanine. The exclusive detection of Senbusine B may be associated with the activation of diterpene biosynthetic pathways, including diterpene synthases and downstream tailoring enzymes. Such pathways are widely reported to be transcriptionally regulated under abiotic stress, suggesting that stress-induced metabolic reprogramming could underlie its presence, particularly elevated temperatures and soil salinity in Semarang's coastal lowlands (Abidin et al., 2013; Rahmawati et al., 2013). Salinity stress can enhance jasmonic acid and ethylene signaling, both known to trigger secondary alkaloid biosynthesis (Ali & Baek, 2020). Microbial pressure in the rhizosphere may further upregulate defensive alkaloid production via systemic acquired resistance (SAR) signaling (Bakker et al., 2013). The documented anticancer potential of diterpenoid alkaloids (Wada & Yamashita, 2019) raises the possibility that Semarang beetroots represent a unique nutraceutical resource.

Malang samples were enriched in 7-hydroxy-3′,4′-dimethoxy-isoflavan-5′,2′-di-O-β-D-glucoside and oleracein A, both associated with antioxidant defense. The sandy silt loam soils of Malang, characterized by slightly acidic pH and low organic matter (Pratiwi et al., 2022). can impose nutrient stress that triggers polyphenol biosynthesis. Betacyanin derivatives like oleracein A are synthesized via the betalain pathway from tyrosine through the intermediates betalamic acid and cycloDOPA (Xing et al., 2008), serving as ROS scavengers and membrane stabilizers under fluctuating thermal conditions (Wang et al., 2023). Isoflavans, derived from the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, may also be induced through stress-related activation of isoflavone synthase (IFS), providing complementary antioxidant protection (Yoon & Park, 2014).

Exploratory data analysis

The PCA score plot showed a clear separation among beetroot samples from BDG, MLG, and SMG, with PC1 (69.8 %) and PC2 (28.2 %) together explaining 98 % of the total variance (Fig. 1). The tight clustering of replicates highlights strong analytical reproducibility and biological consistency. PERMANOVA confirmed significant group differences (F = 336.26; R2 = 0.99116; p = 0.005), indicating that nearly all observed variance can be attributed to geographical origin. These results underscore that environmental and agronomic factors exert strong regulatory effects on beetroot secondary metabolism.

(a) PCA score plot revealed a clear separation among beetroot samples from BDG, MLG, and SMG; (b) The biplot revealed a clear separation among beetroot samples along with their 10 dominant associated metabolites, indicating relationships between sample groups and key metabolites.

The PCA biplot revealed that specific metabolites contributed to separation by origin. BDG samples clustered negatively along PC1, SMG samples grouped positively along both PCs, while MLG samples localized to the lower right quadrant. The orientation of vectors for metabolites such as d-nantenine, piperolactam-C, lignans, maltol, and gentiatibetine suggests their important role in distinguishing regional profiles.

The elevated levels of lignans in SMG samples, such as lignan and irilone, align with prior studies indicating that abiotic stressors—such as temperature fluctuations and soil composition—can upregulate phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Qaderi et al., 2023). These antioxidants and phytoestrogenic compounds often serve protective roles under environmental stress. The coastal lowland climate of Semarang, characterized by higher temperatures and potential salinity, may induce lignin-related pathways as part of a defense mechanism (Giampaoli et al., 2021). This aligns with previous reports of lignan upregulation under fluctuating abiotic conditions, where they act as ROS scavengers and membrane stabilizers (Debnath et al., 2024; Nawfetrias et al., 2024).

MLG samples showed strong associations with alkaloids such as d-nantenine piperolactam-C, piperolactam-C9:3, and other alkaloid derivatives consistent with the role of alkaloid biosynthesis as a defense against environmental stressors. Alkaloids are typically derived from amino acid pathways (e.g., tyrosine, tryptophan), with biosynthesis regulated by stress-responsive phytohormones including jasmonic acid and salicylic acid. Alkaloids such as d-nantenine are frequently associated with plant–microbe interactions in nutrient-poor soils, which may explain their neuroprotective and antimicrobial roles (Zhang et al., 2021). Cooler temperatures and higher UV exposure at altitude may upregulate alkaloid and lactam pathways through enhanced expression of cytochrome P450 enzymes and UV-B photoreceptors, leading to protective metabolite accumulation. Moreover, elevated ultraviolet exposure at higher elevations may stimulate lactam production as a photoprotective strategy (Koski et al., 2024).

BDG samples were enriched in maltol, gentiatibetine, and malonylated compounds. Unlike SMG and MLG, BDG's volcanic-andosol soils are highly fertile, rich in organic matter and minerals, and coupled with a humid tropical climate. These conditions likely favor primary metabolism over energetically costly secondary pathways. The higher relative abundance of maltol—a degradation product linked to carbohydrate metabolism—suggests stronger allocation to sugar turnover and carbon cycling. Gentiatibetine and malonylated metabolites may represent conjugated storage forms, as malonylation is a common modification that increases metabolite solubility, facilitates vacuolar sequestration, and provides stabilization under pH fluctuations (Clemensen et al., 2020). Rather than reflecting a “simpler” phytochemical profile, BDG's metabolite signature may indicate an ecological trade-off, where nutrient sufficiency reduces the necessity for defensive secondary metabolite accumulation.

Overall, the distinct separation of beetroot samples based on their metabolomic profiles supports the application of LC-Q/TOF-MS-based metabolomics as a reliable approach for verifying geographical origin. The identification of region-specific metabolites opens opportunities for developing traceability systems and quality benchmarks based on regional signatures. Furthermore, these insights may inform region-specific agricultural practices and breeding strategies aimed at enhancing the content of bioactive compounds.

Hierarchical clustering heatmap

Fig. 2 presents a hierarchical clustering heatmap of metabolites detected in beetroot samples from three distinct Indonesian regions, revealing distinct region-specific metabolic signatures. The clustering patterns, based on relative metabolite abundances, highlight the strong influence of geographical origin on beetroot metabolomic composition. Similar terroir-driven metabolite differentiation has been reported in other crops, where climate, soil, and cultivation practices collectively shape secondary metabolism (Cassago et al., 2021).

Hierarchical clustering heatmap of metabolites detected in beetroot samples collected from three distinct regions in Indonesia: Bandung (BDG), Semarang (SMG), and Malang (MLG).

BDG samples clustered together with elevated levels of marker metabolites concentrated in the bottom-left quadrant of the heatmap. Bandung's cool, humid climate and fertile volcanic soils may favor strong primary metabolism, supplying abundant carbon skeletons for secondary pathways. This can channel carbon flux into the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathways through key enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and chalcone synthase (CHS), whose activity is often modulated by environmental factors including nutrient status and moisture availability. Flavonoids, and to some extent alkaloids, function as reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavengers and defense metabolites under humid conditions. This observation is consistent with previous reports that variation in environmental parameters can drive differences in the accumulation of specialized metabolites such as flavonoids, chlorogenic acids, and sugars, particularly at local geographic scales (Padilla-González et al., 2021).

SMG samples exhibited an intermediate clustering pattern, positioned between BDG and MLG. This reflects Semarang's transitional agroecological conditions, spanning humid lowlands and drier uplands. Such environmental heterogeneity may trigger overlapping metabolic responses, leading to a “hybrid” metabolomic profile. For example, regions with more humid conditions showed enhanced photosynthesis, photorespiration, and respiration to support the biosynthesis of nitrogen metabolites, while an increase in defense metabolites from the shikimate–phenylpropanoid pathway was also detected as an environmental stress response (Zhao et al., 2015). The clustering suggests that SMG beetroots may flexibly modulate both primary and secondary metabolism, balancing growth-related and stress-related biochemical demands.

MLG samples clustered distinctly in the upper-right quadrant, defined by upregulation of metabolites associated with stress adaptation. Malang's upland environment, with cooler temperatures, lower soil fertility, and higher UV radiation exposure, likely stimulates both phenolic and alkaloid biosynthesis. UV radiation in particular is a well-established inducer of flavonoid and phenolic pathways, mediated through activation of UV-B photoreceptors and downstream transcription factors that regulate phenylpropanoid enzymes. In addition, cooler highland climates may slow primary metabolism, redirecting resources toward protective secondary metabolites. Comparable altitude-dependent metabolite enrichment has been reported in Andean plants, where environmental stressors drive accumulation of phenolics and alkaloids for photoprotection and oxidative stress resistance (Padilla-González et al., 2021).

The heatmap also highlights several metabolite clusters uniquely abundant in one region while significantly downregulated in others. These region-specific compounds may serve as reliable biochemical markers for determining the geographical origin of beetroot. Such findings support the development of metabolomics-based authentication tools and the establishment of region-specific quality standards. Additionally, understanding these metabolite patterns can inform location-specific cultivation strategies aimed at enhancing the accumulation of desirable bioactive compounds. This application of metabolomics for food authentication and regional branding is increasingly recognized for its value in supporting geographical indications and promoting product traceability (Zhang et al., 2025).

Differential metabolite analysis

Fig. 3 presents the results of one-way ANOVA-based differential metabolite analysis, revealing statistically significant compounds that differentiate beetroot samples from the three regions. Several metabolites exhibited highly significant differences, suggesting strong region-specific biochemical adaptations shaped by environmental conditions. These findings highlight not only the discriminatory power of metabolomics but also the mechanistic basis of beetroot's metabolic plasticity in response to terroir.

Differential metabolite analysis one-way ANOVA-based, highlighting statistically significant features (metabolites) that discriminate beetroot samples across the three geographical regions: Bandung (BDG), Semarang (SMG), and Malang (MLG).

Among the identified metabolites, gentiatibetine emerged as the most statistically significant (−log₁₀(p) > 10), with elevated abundance particularly in Malang samples. Gentiatibetine is a betaine derivative, and betaines are well-documented osmoprotectants that accumulate under drought or salinity stress. These compounds function as compatible solutes, stabilizing proteins and membrane structures, protecting photosynthetic machinery, and maintaining osmotic balance without interfering with enzymatic activity (Singh et al., 2015). The highland environment of Malang, characterized by drier conditions and greater exposure to abiotic stressors, is consistent with enhanced betaine accumulation as a stress-adaptive strategy. While gentiatibetine has been previously reported in species such as Moringa oleifera with neuroprotective and antioxidant properties (Rahayu & Timotius, 2022) and in Cosmos caudatus extracts (Firdaus et al., 2021) its elevated abundance in beetroot underscores a conserved stress-related function across plant taxa.

Stilbostemin B, belonging to the stilbene class of polyphenols, also showed strong discriminatory power. Stilbenes are synthesized through the phenylpropanoid pathway and act as phytoalexins, protecting plants against pathogen infection and oxidative stress. Their biosynthesis is strongly induced by environmental factors such as UV radiation, temperature fluctuations, and pathogen exposure (Valletta et al., 2021). The differential abundance of stilbostemin B among the studied regions therefore reflects both climatic modulation and possible local pathogen pressures, fitting within the well-documented role of stilbenes as environmentally responsive secondary metabolites.

The detection of p-hydroxyacetanilide (pHA) is more complex. While commonly known as paracetamol in medicine, its appearance in plant metabolomics datasets is unusual and could be attributed to endogenous phenolic derivatives, environmental contamination, or in-source fragmentation artifacts during LC-Q/TOF-MS. Nonetheless, the varying abundance of this feature in SMG and MLG samples raises the possibility of stress-related aromatic compound metabolism. Phenylamide derivatives in plants are often linked to stress signaling and pathogen defense (Cho & Lee, 2015). Thus, while further validation is needed, this feature may point toward stress-induced modifications of phenolic metabolism rather than a direct biosynthesis of acetaminophen in beetroot.

Another metabolite of interest was 7-hydroxy-3′,4′-dimethoxy-isoflavan-5′,2′-di-O-β-D-glucoside, a rare isoflavan glycoside with reported antioxidant and estrogenic activities. Isoflavans derive from the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway and are known to accumulate in response to UV stress, nutrient availability, and soil composition (Patil et al., 2024). Their presence in beetroot, particularly in environmentally variable regions, may reflect an adaptive upregulation of antioxidant defenses under fluctuating oxidative stress.

Interestingly, several significantly altered metabolites clustered in BDG and SMG samples, suggesting that the more humid and temperate climates of West and Central Java favor the accumulation of phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related compounds. This is consistent with reports that environments promote enhanced phenylpropanoid metabolism, which provides both antioxidant defense and structural reinforcement under pathogen-prone conditions (Lü et al., 2025). By contrast, the MLG cluster was enriched in stress-related osmoprotectants and protective secondary metabolites, reflecting the harsher highland climate.

Taken together, the ANOVA results provide quantitative support for the divergence observed in clustering patterns and emphasize how environmentally modulated pathways—particularly osmoprotectant accumulation, phenylpropanoid/stilbene biosynthesis, and flavonoid glycosylation—drive regional metabolomic signatures in beetroot.

Discriminative marker identification

To further validate the differentiation of beetroot samples by region, a sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) was performed. Fig. 4 highlights the top metabolites driving separation between Bandung (BDG), Malang (MLG), and Semarang (SMG). Unlike the univariate ANOVA approach, sPLS-DA provides multivariate resolution by ranking features according to their discriminatory power and linking them to region-specific biosynthetic trends (J. Zhao et al., 2022). This combined strategy increases biomarker robustness, as demonstrated in food authenticity and terroir metabolomics studies (Trainor et al., 2017).

Top loading weight metabolites selected by the sPLS-DA model, ranked according to their loadings values, which reflect their importance in discriminating among beetroot samples from (a) Bandung (BDG), (b) Malang (MLG), and (c) Semarang (SMG).

Among the strongest discriminants, stilbostemin B and licarin B showed markedly higher intensities in BDG samples (Fig. 4a). Both compounds belong to the phenylpropanoid pathway, which is tightly regulated by environmental stress cues such as UV exposure, temperature, and pathogen pressure. Stilbenes in particular are phytoalexins, synthesized via the phenylpropanoid to stilbene synthase branch, and function in pathogen defense and ROS scavenging (Bai et al., 2018). The cooler and more humid highland climate of West Java likely promotes oxidative and biotic stress conditions that induce phenolic biosynthesis, consistent with studies showing that metabolite variation in high-altitude regions often reflects plant adaptation to UV stress and water scarcity (Liang et al., 2024). Licarin B, a lignan derivative, similarly arises from phenylpropanoid metabolism and is associated with lignification and structural reinforcement under stress. The concerted upregulation of these phenolics in BDG thus reflects adaptive modulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway in response to regional ecological drivers.

Another key metabolite, hispidulin 7-(6-E-p-coumaroyl-β-D-glucopyranoside), was more abundant in BDG samples. Hispidulin, a flavone, undergoes glycosylation and acylation, which enhance its stability and solubility in vacuoles. Functionally, its accumulation is part of a broader flavonoid-mediated photoprotection mechanism. Flavones absorb UV-B radiation and stabilize cellular ROS balance, supporting plant survival under high-altitude conditions. This fits with prior metabolomics studies showing flavonoid enrichment in plants exposed to strong UV and variable water availability (Wang et al., 2020). Thus, hispidulin glycosides serve as both protective pigments and antioxidants, reinforcing the climatic adaptation hypothesis.

Several moderate discriminants—1-galloyl-gluc, polycathine, and dihydrocoumarin—were also enriched in BDG and SMG. Galloyl derivatives are linked to tannin biosynthesis, strengthening cell walls against pathogens and herbivores, while dihydrocoumarin derives from coumarin metabolism, which contributes to allelopathy and defense. Their presence reflects an ecological strategy where more humid environments favor enhanced phenylpropanoid flux. Importantly, the clustering of these metabolites across BDG and SMG reinforces the hierarchical clustering patterns (Fig. 2) and ANOVA results (Fig. 3), supporting their role as complementary regional markers.

In MLG samples, 2′-hydroxy-3′,4′-dimethoxy-isoflavan-7-O-β-D-glucoside, decuroside VI, and flazin were among the strongest contributors (Fig. 4b). Isoflavan glycosides are uncommon in beetroot but derive from the isoflavonoid branch of the phenylpropanoid pathway, often linked to stress responses in legumes. Their detection here suggests either rare metabolic flexibility or annotation overlap, but their consistent recurrence across loadings indicates robustness as markers. Decuroside VI, an iridoid glycoside, reflects monoterpenoid metabolism, often stimulated by abiotic stress and known for anti-inflammatory properties. Iridoid glycosides have been reported to accumulate under drought and temperature stress, consistent with MLG's drier highland environment. Flazin, a pyrrole alkaloid associated with nitrogen metabolism and oxidative balance, further suggests that soil nutrient availability and agroecological pressures in East Java shape beetroot secondary metabolism. These findings emphasize genotype–environment interactions in modulating diverse pathways beyond phenylpropanoids.

In SMG samples, licochalcone A was identified as a strong discriminant (Fig. 4c). Licochalcone A, a retrochalcone flavonoid, is known for potent antioxidant and antimicrobial properties and is typically induced under UV and arid stress (Chen et al., 2017). Its enrichment in SMG suggests adaptation to specific microclimatic factors such as high solar intensity or soil composition. Additional compounds like cyanidin 3-glucoside—a canonical anthocyanin—were also elevated in SMG. Anthocyanins not only provide pigmentation but act as ROS scavengers and pH-sensitive pigments, making them sensitive terroir indicators (Wang et al., 2025). Their modulation in SMG aligns with studies in grape and berry metabolomics where anthocyanin shifts trace local environmental variation.

Unexpectedly, piperine was also detected at higher levels in SMG. While typically associated with Piper species, such findings highlight the challenges of metabolite annotation in untargeted LC-MS. Rather than concluding true biosynthesis, this may represent structural analogs or misannotation, a common issue reported in untargeted metabolomics. Nonetheless, its consistent separation power suggests it—or a structurally related compound—may still serve as a useful statistical marker, albeit requiring validation with MS/MS and authentic standards.

Overall, the sPLS-DA model demonstrates that region-specific metabolite markers in beetroot are primarily rooted in phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and iridoid pathways, with additional contributions from nitrogen-related alkaloids. These metabolites not only provide discriminatory power but also reveal mechanistic links between environmental pressures (humidity, UV intensity, soil conditions) and metabolic adaptation. Importantly, this aligns with broader terroir metabolomics frameworks in crops such as grapevine, coffee, and tea, where local environments reprogram secondary metabolism to produce distinctive chemical signatures.

Correlation-based pattern hunter analysis

To further investigate metabolite co-expression networks and validate the discriminatory capacity of the top-ranked compounds identified through the sPLS-DA loadings, a correlation-based pattern hunter analysis was conducted. This analysis utilized the top loading weights from loadings 1-3–(Fig. 5). The objective was to identify metabolite pairs whose intensity patterns are highly correlated—either positively or negatively—thereby revealing potential biosynthetic, regulatory, or environmental associations. Correlation-based metabolomics is a powerful strategy for discovering co-regulated metabolites and inferring functional biochemical modules, especially when paired with tools like pattern hunter or weighted gene co-expression network analysis (Liu et al., 2022).

Pattern Hunter correlation analysis using the top loading weights compounds from (a) loadings 1, (b) loadings 2, and (c) loadings 3.

In BDG, licarin B exhibited strong positive correlations with stilbostemin B, hispidulin 7-(6-E-1-galloyl-glucose), 1-galloyl-glucose, and polyanthine. These compounds are primarily phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-derived, suggesting coordinated regulation of phenolic metabolism. Such clustering is consistent with the activity of the phenylpropanoid pathway, where PAL, C4H, and downstream enzymes funnel precursors into stilbenoids and flavones. Co-expression of stilbenoids with glycosylated flavonoids has been observed in grape and peanut, where stilbene synthase is activated under biotic and abiotic stress (Bartzis et al., 2017). The enrichment of these metabolites in BDG, which has a humid temperate climate, suggests that constant pathogen pressure and high humidity may favor the induction of phenylpropanoid defenses. The co-occurrence of β-carboline and balaphonin—alkaloids associated with plant defense—further supports the hypothesis that the BDG metabolome reflects stress-induced reprogramming. Notably, adenosine's correlation should be interpreted cautiously: as a primary metabolite central to nucleotide and energy metabolism, its fluctuation may indicate increased turnover of ATP/AMP signaling during stress rather than a direct secondary metabolite role.

In MLG, a highland and comparatively drier environment, 6-hydroxy-2-[2-(4′-methoxyphenyl)ethyl]chromone showed strong correlations with decuroside VI, flazin, and 2-formyl-5-(hydroxymethyl)pyrrole. Chromones are often linked to antioxidant responses, and their co-expression with glycosides suggests modulation of detoxification and ROS-scavenging systems. Glycosylation of phenolics has been reported as a major adaptive mechanism for plants facing oxidative stress (Behr et al., 2020). The negative correlation with 7-methoxycoumarin and p-hydroxyacetanilide may indicate metabolic channeling away from coumarin-related pathways under MLG conditions, perhaps due to altered substrate allocation within phenylpropanoid metabolism.

In SMG, licochalcone A displayed strong positive correlations with thalicticoside, piperine, kingianoside I, and astragaline. Licochalcone A is a prenylated chalcone, and its co-variation with flavonoid glycosides suggests activation of chalcone isomerase–mediated branches of the flavonoid pathway. Chalcones and glycosylated flavonoids are commonly co-induced by UV-B and pathogen stress (Jiao et al., 2022), supporting the notion that SMG's lower altitude but higher UV exposure may enhance flavonoid-based photoprotection. Conversely, its strong negative correlation with isatan B, maltol, gentiatibetine, and betaine suggests region-specific metabolic trade-offs, where osmolyte-associated pathways (e.g., betaine and gentiatibetine) may be downregulated in favor of flavonoid biosynthesis. Such antagonistic correlations are consistent with modular regulation of plant secondary metabolism, where specific pathways dominate under distinct environmental pressures. Importantly, several metabolites such as melatonin, toralactone, and guanine were consistently present across all three networks. Melatonin in plants functions as a redox regulator and growth modulator, frequently acting as a central hub in stress signaling. Its recurrent appearance in correlation networks may indicate its role as a core metabolic node integrating environmental responses (Hannah et al., 2010).

Overall, the correlation-based pattern hunter analysis not only validated the discriminatory metabolites highlighted by sPLS-DA but also revealed biosynthetic and regulatory coherence underlying region-specific metabolomes. The Bandung network emphasized phenylpropanoid–stilbenoid clustering, the Malang network highlighted chromone–glycoside antioxidant modules, and the Semarang network reflected chalcone–flavonoid coordination with trade-offs in osmolyte biosynthesis. These patterns align with ecological drivers such as humidity, UV exposure, and soil conditions, reinforcing the concept of terroir-mediated metabolic adaptation. By integrating multivariate modeling with correlation-based network profiling, this study provides a mechanistic framework for interpreting metabolomic fingerprints in beetroot and underscores the utility of metabolite networks in food authentication and functional crop improvement (Amara et al., 2022).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effective use of untargeted LC-Q/TOF-MS-based metabolomics to discriminate the geographical origin of Indonesian beetroot. A total of 92 metabolites were identified, revealing clear region-specific profiles closely linked to environmental conditions. Distinct marker compounds—such as 2-hydroxy-3,4-dimethoxy-isoflavam-7-O-β-glucoside (Bandung), 6-hydroxy-2-[2-(4′-methoxyphenyl)ethyl] chromone and decuroside VI (Malang), and licochalcone A and thalictricoside (Semarang)—emerged as potential biochemical indicators of provenance. These metabolic variations reflect the influence of climate, soil, and altitude, highlighting the terroir effect on beetroot secondary metabolism. By integrating metabolomic data with environmental context, this work supports food authentication, traceability, and sustainable agricultural practices, while laying a foundation for targeted LC-MS assays to verify origin. Future studies incorporating seasonal variation and genetic diversity will further refine authentication strategies and enable optimization of bioactive compound production in beetroot and other high-value crops.
