Predictive factors for the success of the Paul glaucoma implant: a one-year multicenter prospective study
Authors: Elena Millá, Javier Moreno-Montañés, Susana Duch, Aitor Lanzagorta, Jesús Téllez, José Vicente Piá, Elena Arrondo, Jorge Vila, Alfonso Antón, Javier Bóveda, Jordi Loscos, The Spain’ Paul Glaucoma Implant Group (SPGG)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Paul Glaucoma Implant (PGI) is becoming increasingly used in many countries. The primary aim of this study was to identify the most important pre-, intra- and post-operative factors associated with successful intraocular pressure (IOP) control at one year. A total of two hundred and fifty -one eyes from 239 glaucoma patients undergoing PGI surgery were included in this prospective, multicentre, longitudinal study. Clinical data were collected on a REDCap platform using a standardised surgical protocol. Variables were recorded pre-, intra- and postoperatively. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify predictors of complete success at one year. These results are expressed as Odds Ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was also performed. Mean IOP was significantly reduced from 25.3 ± 8.8 mmHg preoperatively to 13.1 ± 3.9 mmHg at one year (p < 0.001). The medication burden decreased from 3.03 ± 1.12 to 0.78 ± 0.96 (p < 0.001). Most complications were minor and self-limiting. Factors associated with complete success in univariable or multivariable analysis were: use of intraoperative antimetabolites (mitomycin or 5-fluorouracil) [OR 2.59; 95% CI 1.18–5.67], combined phacoemulsification-PGI surgery [OR 4.38; 95% CI 1.10–17.36] and removal of the ripcord after 4–5 months [OR 2.36; 95% CI 1.07–5.18]. High myopia was only associated in the univariable analysis (OR 0.50; CI 0.30–0.85; p = 0.010). Previous glaucoma surgery, hypertensive phase, surgeon experience and diagnosis of non-open angle glaucoma were not significantly associated with complete success. This study concluded that the effectiveness of the PGI can be enhanced by implementing certain strategies that have not shown similar benefits in Ahmed or Baerveldt glaucoma devices.Keywords: Paul glaucoma implant, Glaucoma drainage device, Intraocular pressure, Prognostic factorsSubject terms: Diseases, Risk factors

Introduction

Glaucoma drainage devices (GDD) are widely used in the treatment of glaucoma that does not respond to medications or filtrating surgery. GDDs have shown a similar efficacy in IOP control as standard trabeculectomy1,2. These implants facilitate the egress of aqueous humor to a subconjunctival reservoir, bypassing the physiological outflow pathways. Among GDDs, two main categories exist: valved (e.g., Ahmed Glaucoma Valve) and non-valved (e.g., Baerveldt Glaucoma Device, Molteno implant). While valved devices offer early intraocular pressure (IOP) control with reduced hypotony risk, non-valved devices often achieve better long-term pressure reduction at the cost of a higher risk of early postoperative hypotony3. The Paul Glaucoma Implant (PGI) (Advanced Ophthalmic Innovations, Singapore, Republic of Singapore) is a free-flow GDD with a plate similar in shape and size (297 mm2), to the Baerveldt Glaucoma Device (BGD)(Johnson & Johnson Vision, Jacksonville, FL, USA), albeit with a significantly narrower tube with an internal diameter of 127 microns, in contrast to BGD tube of 300 microns. The PGI is functional immediately, as the tube is not ligated as with a BGD, but rather uses an intraluminal ripcord to provide an IOP gradient sufficient to avoid hypotony4–6. Owing to its specialized design—featuring a thinner tube and a larger effective filtration area while maintaining filtration efficiency—the PGI has shown an enhanced safety profile.

The PGI is progressively positioning itself within the current glaucoma surgical algorithm as a valuable option for moderate to advanced glaucoma cases, particularly when filtering surgery is contraindicated or has failed, and when a long-term solution with lower endothelial risk is desirable. Its surgical implantation technique closely resembles that of other widely used devices, such as the Baerveldt or Ahmed implants, making it accessible to experienced surgeons. Owing to its demonstrated efficacy and favorable safety profile, it is increasingly being considered as a primary GDD option when a GDD is required. Furthermore, in light of recent publications, the PGI may be expanding its indications beyond those traditionally reserved for GDD, potentially broadening its role in surgical glaucoma management7. However, given that the structural design of the PGI differs substantially from the other non-valved, large-bore GDDs, it is plausible that the mechanisms underlying its success may also differ. Therefore, predictive factors associated with the efficacy of traditional GDDs, such as the Baerveldt or Ahmed implants, may not be fully applicable or extrapolable to the PGI. This underscores the need for specific, dedicated studies to determine the most relevant clinical variables influencing PGI outcomes, allowing us to optimize its use in real-world settings. In this study we analyzed the effect on IOP through the results of The Spain Paul Glaucoma Implant Group at 1 year follow-up. The main goal of our analysis was to identify the critical preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative factors related to the success of PGI as a mean of IOP control.

Table 4 presents the results of univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses examining the association between various preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative factors and the likelihood of achieving complete surgical success at one year. In the univariable analysis, high myopia [Odds ratio (OR): 0.50; and 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 0.30–0.85; p = 0.010] was significantly associated with a lower chance of success. Combined surgery with phacoemulsification (OR 3.61; CI 1.30–10.36; p = 0.017) and the use of antimetabolites (OR 3.24; CI 1.68–6.27; p < 0.001) were positively associated with success (Fig. 3A–D). In the multivariable model, after adjusting for confounding variables, the factors that remained statistically significant were: combined PGI–phacoemulsification surgery (OR 4.38; CI 1.10–17.36; p = 0.036); the use of intraoperative antimetabolites such as Mitomycin C (MMC) or 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) (OR 2.59; CI 1.18–5.67; p = 0.018), and the removal of the intraluminal ripcord, particularly after month 4 (OR 2.36; CI 1.07–5.18; p = 0.033). IOP decreased from 18.2 ± 6.8 mmHg before ripcord extraction to 14.5 ± 5.4 mmHg after ripcord extraction (p < 0.001). Regarding the use of antimetabolites, the results were consistent for both MMC and 5-FU. However, 5-FU demonstrated greater efficacy in lowering IOP during the early postoperative period, whereas MMC was associated with more pronounced IOP reductions in the later follow-up visits.

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) remained overall stable during the 12-month follow-up period in the 149 eyes analyzed with complete data at 1 year (baseline: 0.45 ± 0.026; 12 months: 0.47 ± 0.027; p = 0.237). When stratified by surgical approach, the 127 eyes undergoing standalone PGI implantation showed no significant change in BCVA (baseline: 0.45 ± 0.029; 12 months: 0.44 ± 0.029; p = 0.672). In contrast, the 22 eyes that underwent combined surgery with phacoemulsification demonstrated a significant improvement in BCVA, increasing from 0.44 ± 0.057 at baseline to 0.61 ± 0.067 at 12 months (p = 0.004).

Discussion

The popularity of PGI has been increasing progressively in recent years and more and more publications are reporting its effectiveness and good safety profile. One of the possible reasons for its increasing acceptance is its free flow design that allows it to function from the first postoperative day. The narrower internal diameter of the tube, coupled with an intraluminal ripcord, allows immediate flow while having a pressure gradient sufficient to avoid hypotony. Thus, no ligation of the tube is necessary, as is the case with the non-valved BGD, and the risk of sudden decompression at the time of spontaneous suture lysis is avoided. Furthermore, the natural evolution of surgery in recent times has been towards minimal invasiveness and this has been reflected in the advent of minimally invasive bleb surgery (MIBS) implants, promoting increasingly thinner tube designs. In this sense, PGI has followed this line by presenting a tube of smaller caliber than the previous GDDs like BGD or Ahmed ClearPath.

In this multicenter study conducted in Spain we included more than 200 patients. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date involving patients receiving a PGI. The mean decrease in IOP in our study was 48.2%, which is between the 42.9% published by Koh8 and the 60% published by Jose4. And our findings are also consistent with those of Weber et al., who reported a mean IOP reduction of 43.4% and 41.5% at 1 and 2 years respectively in European cohorts undergoing PGI implantation, with a substantial decrease in medication use and a favorable safety profile9,10. The reduction in the number of medications was 74.2%, which is also similar to other published studies4,8.

Our primary objective was to identify the factors associated with successful IOP control at one year of follow-up, using both univariable and multivariable analyses. One of the most significant factors found was the use of antimetabolites (MMC or 5-FU). However, the use of antimetabolites in other GDDs still remains controversial11. Alvarado et al. found that the adjunctive use of both intraoperative MMC and postoperative 5-FU during Ahmed valve implantation was associated with higher success rates12, in contrast to the findings of Costa et al.13. Similarly, Irak et al. reported that MMC did not provide any significant benefit in BGD surgery14, which differs from the conclusions of De Sousa et al., who reported a positive effect15.

In MIBS such as XEN or Preserflo, antimetabolites have been effective in decreasing IOP16,17. Our results indicate that antimetabolites are effective in enhancing the effectiveness of PGI, more in line with the effect observed in MIBS than in traditional GDD surgeries. It should be noted that the lumen of the Paul tube is 127 microns and with a 6/0 Prolene ripcord it is reduced by about 70–80 microns, making the final internal diameter similar to XEN63 or Preserflo. This fact could mean that PGI acts in a similar way as a MIBS device to which a plate has been added to increase the filtration area. Perhaps another reason why we observe a positive effect from the use of antimetabolites in PGI surgery might be because the type of capsule formed is different from that of other classic GDDs18, given the special design and particular functioning of PGI. The time and mechanism of maturation of the bleb of this implant is still unknown, but according to our results, it benefits from the use of intraoperative antimetabolites.

Another significant finding of this study is that PGI is more effective in combined surgery compared to solo surgery in contrast with other GDDs that tend to have worse outcomes in combined surgery. In this line, Wardani et al. found less IOP reduction with Phaco-BGD compared to solo BGD19. Again, our results are more similar to the ones reported with MIBS that seem to achieve better IOP results in combined surgeries. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, combining PGI with cataract surgery may enhance the hypotensive effect, possibly due to the additional angular opening achieved through phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation within the capsular bag.

One of the most interesting findings of this study is the optimal timing for ripcord removal. Large-caliber nonvalved implants require a larger intraluminal ripcord to avoid severe postoperative hypotonia20. However, there is limited evidence on its hypotensive efficacy or the optimal time for its removal. An et al., found that ripcord removal did not increase the success criteria for BGD, but decreased the number of hypotensive drops21. While some authors recommend ripcord removal between 1 and 3 months postoperatively or shortly after peritubular ligation dissolves15,17,18, our findings suggest that the optimal timing may be around the 4th to 5th postoperative month. In clinical practice, this implies that if IOP remains suboptimal during the early postoperative period, it may be preferable to manage the patient with topical hypotensive agents and defer ripcord removal until reaching the 4–5 month window.

In our study, the frequency of the early hypertensive phase, considering this between the first and third postoperative month, was about 17%, which is lower than the 50–80% found with the BGD or Ahmed valve22,23. This suggests that there may be less inflammation around the plate due to slower aqueous outflow in the immediate postoperative period due to the narrower tube and ripcord. In the current study, the presence of a hypertensive phase did not influence complete success. However, some studies suggest that the hypertensive phase in other GDDs correlates with higher IOP at 1 year and greater need for postoperative medication24,25.

Finally, in our study the trend for high myopia to be a risk factor for failure did not reach statistical significance in a multi-factor-adjusted analysis. However, we found that at 1 week, 1 month and 1 year postoperatively, the success criteria were worse than those of non-high myopia. One reason that IOP might be higher in high myopia eyes, and thus success reduced, would be that steroid elevation of IOP may be greater in high myopia. Jung et al., found that the development of hypertensive phase was significantly higher in the high-myopia group than in the non-high myopia group with Ahmed implant but with non-significant differences in cumulative probability of success at 1 year26. In our study, we found no significant relationship between the hypertensive phase and high myopia (OR 1.19; 95%CI 0.48–2.90, p = 0.714), although in all postoperative months the success criteria were lower in high myopia than in non-high myopia. One reason could be that surgeons are more reluctant to use antimetabolites in high myopic patients to avoid hypotony.

The longest follow-up study available to date on the Paul Glaucoma Implant is the retrospective review conducted by Tan et al.27, which evaluated the 3-year outcomes in 48 eyes. The study demonstrated a sustained reduction in IOP (mean IOP of 14.9 ± 4.1 mmHg at 36 months) and a significant decrease in the number of hypotensive medications, with 75% of eyes achieving complete success. Although hypotony occurred in 17% of cases, most were self-limiting. Notably, the authors did not use internal or external tube occlusion; instead, they employed a modified flow-modulating technique called the “stability system,” which involved placing a pericardial patch graft over the plate and injecting cross-linked viscoelastic beneath it to fill the reservoir and delay flow initiation. This study provides important long-term evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of the PGI. In comparison, in our series the highest rate of hypotony (IOP < 6 mmHg) was observed at the first postoperative visit (8.4%), and decreased progressively over time, with no cases recorded at 12 months. This lower and transient incidence of hypotony may be related to the use of an intraluminal ripcord in our surgical protocol, which provides immediate but controlled flow, potentially reducing the risk of early overfiltration.

One potential limitation of this study is the involvement of multiple surgeons across different centers, which may introduce variability in surgical technique and postoperative management. This includes differences in the discretionary use of postoperative medications and the timing of interventions such as needling or suture release, which could influence outcome consistency. However, this diversity also reflects real-world clinical practice, thereby enhancing the external validity and applicability of the results to a broader patient population. Additionally, the absence of a control group using other glaucoma drainage devices (GDDs) limits the ability to directly compare outcomes and contextualize the performance of the PGI within the broader range of available surgical options. Finally, the 12-month follow-up period does not allow for assessment of long-term efficacy or late complications, such as endothelial failure or device exposure, which are important considerations in evaluating the durability and safety of glaucoma implants.

In conclusion, PGI achieves significant reductions in both IOP and the number of medications after 1 year of follow-up. Our recommendations to improve IOP control with PGI would be: intraoperative use of antimetabolites, ripcord removal around 4 to 5 months postoperatively, and perform a combined surgery with phacoemulsification if needed, as these factors enhance absolute success in our study. To date, no studies have specifically addressed how to optimize the use of the PGI, and applying the same criteria used for large-caliber GDDs may not be appropriate in the case of the PGI. We believe that PGI exhibits characteristics of both MIBS in terms of safety and traditional GDDs in terms of efficacy. Finally, longer-term studies are needed to determine whether these success factors remain consistent or change over time, and to address other issues such as the type of capsule formed, its maturation time, or what factors may determine longer-term survival.

Methods

Design and data evaluated

This is a prospective multicenter study of a cohort of glaucoma patients from Spain who underwent PGI surgery with one-year prospective follow-up, initiated in March 2021. The data was extracted from the medical records of the different participating hospitals between March 2021 and January 2024. Each participating center added the study data to a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) platform created for this purpose. Data included preoperative, demographic features, factors related to the surgical procedure, and postoperative evolution in the first 24 h and subsequent visits at 1 week and 1, 3, 6 and 12 months.

At the preoperative visit, sociodemographic data and medical information of patients were collected. Concerning the surgical procedure, data collected included combined or solo PGI surgery, use of antimetabolites, use of 6/0 Prolene ripcord and complications.

Mitomycin C (0.01%, 0.02%, or 0.04%) or 5-Fluorouracil was applied either via subconjunctival sponges for 2 min (followed by irrigation) or by subconjunctival injection, depending on the conjunctival, Tenon’s, and scleral characteristics. MMC 0.04% was only used with sponges. Lower concentrations or 5-FU were chosen for thin sclera like in high myopia or poor conjunctival quality, while higher concentrations were used in those with thick Tenon’s or prior fibrosis.

During follow-up visits, data was collected to assess the success of the surgery (IOP and hypotensive treatments), complications, and the procedures freely chosen by surgeons for the correct evolution of the patients such as removal of the ripcord, antimetabolite injection, capsule drainage etc.

Intraoperative modifications and postoperative management were left to the experience of each surgeon, such as the choice of the ocular quadrant or the use and dosage of antimetabolites.

In the postoperative period, all patients received topical steroids for at least the first 3–4 months and the introduction of postoperative hypotensive medication was left to the discretion of each surgeon. Finally, each surgeon chose the appropriate time to remove the ripcord according to IOP evolution and the characteristics of each specific case.

Participants

A total of 251 PGI surgeries were performed by 29 glaucoma surgeons across 20 participating hospitals. The surgeons had a mean of 19.5 ± 7.7 years of experience in glaucoma surgery. Prior to the study, all surgeons received standardized training in the PGI implantation technique conducted by an experienced instructor.

The study enrolled adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with medically uncontrolled glaucoma who were scheduled for PGI implantation. Inclusion criteria comprised eyes with IOP inadequately controlled despite maximally tolerated medical therapy, with or without prior ocular surgery. Both primary and secondary types of glaucoma were eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria included active ocular infection or inflammation, a history of extensive conjunctival scarring precluding device implantation, prior GDD implantation in the same eye, and insufficient follow-up data. Eyes with neovascular glaucoma, uveitic glaucoma with ongoing inflammation, or significant corneal endothelial compromise deemed at high risk for decompensation were also excluded.

Statistical analysis

Longitudinal changes in IOP and medication use were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, accounting for within-subject correlations and missing data. Adjusted means and standard deviations (SD) were estimated for each follow-up visit.

The success of the surgery was defined as complete success (IOP between > 4 and ≤ 18 mmHg without hypotensive treatment in 2 consecutive visits after the 1st month visit) and qualified success (similar to complete success criteria, but with supplemental medical treatment). In both definitions, success also required the absence of additional glaucoma surgeries and no progression to visual loss to no light perception.

To assess the association between different variables and the complete success of the surgery after 1 year, univariant and multivariable adjusted logistic regression analyses were conducted. These results are expressed as Odds Ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

To evaluate the association between the timing of ripcord removal and the success of the surgery after 1 year, cubic regression splines adjusted for the same variables were used. Notably, our comparison focused on identifying the optimal timing for ripcord removal by evaluating outcomes in patients who had the ripcord withdrawn before versus those who maintained good IOP control at one year but with the ripcord still in place.

The chi-square test was used to compare the frequency of complete success according to the use of Mitomycin C and 5-Fluorouracil, history of previous glaucoma surgery, presence of high myopia, and combined surgery (phacoemulsification + PGI) over a number of follow-up periods.

Additionally, Kaplan–Meier survival curves for 1 year follow-up after IOP surgery are presented. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analyses were performed using STATA 17.0. The study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Navarra, Pamplona, Spain. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research of the Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (Number HCB/2021/1106) and was accepted by the other participating hospitals. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) prior to their inclusion in the study. All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
