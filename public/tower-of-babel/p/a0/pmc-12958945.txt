Harm reduction and pharmacy practice: a scoping review of services for people who use drugs provided by pharmacy staff
Authors: Javiera Navarrete, Emily Berg, Christine Hughes, Essi Salokangas, Marliss Taylor, Janice Y Kung, Jeffrey A Johnson, Elaine Hyshka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThe roles of pharmacy staff have expanded to include public health functions, such as delivering harm reduction services for people who use drugs (PWUD), particularly unregulated substances and non-medical drug use, in response to an ongoing drug overdose crisis. Nonetheless, their involvement across the full spectrum of harm reduction services remains underexplored. Introduction

Harm reduction is both a set of health promotion and risk reduction strategies and a philosophy of care rooted in pragmatism, human rights and respect for autonomy.1 In the context of substance use, it seeks to minimise the negative consequences associated with drug use while supporting the rights of people who use drugs (PWUD) to make decisions about their health and well-being.1 Harm reduction services include, for example, safer drug use education, syringe exchange programmes, low-threshold opioid agonist treatment (OAT), access to prevention, treatment and care for sexually transmitted and blood-borne infections (STBBI), drug checking and supervised consumption sites.1 2 Evidence shows that these programmes improve health and social outcomes, mitigate stigma, increase access to services2,7 and offer a cost-effective public health response.8 9 As overdose deaths and drug-related harms continue to rise,10 expanding access to harm reduction services is essential. One approach is to integrate these services into existing systems, such as pharmacies and the wider scope of pharmacy practice.

Pharmacy staff, including pharmacists, pharmacy technicians and assistants, are among the most accessible healthcare providers. They work in hospital settings as part of multidisciplinary teams, in community-based locations with extended hours or through outreach models that connect directly with communities.11 12 In recent years, pharmacy practice has expanded to include public health roles,13 such as responding to the drug overdose crisis and related harms by providing sterile injection supplies,14 distributing naloxone kits,15,17 supporting access to hepatitis C virus (HCV) testing and treatment18 and participating in safer supply programmes.19 20 Although pharmacy staff are uniquely positioned to act as a key point of contact with healthcare, especially in communities with limited access to specialised services,21 their current involvement in delivering harm reduction services for PWUD, including how these services are implemented and integrated into pharmacy practice, remains underexplored.

Previous reviews have explored pharmacy-based strategies targeting substance use-related harms but have not directly addressed the provision of care for PWUD from a harm reduction perspective. Existing reviews have focused on specific interventions like naloxone distribution,15 16 needle and syringe programmes,14 methadone prescribing22 and buprenorphine (BUP) treatment.23 24 A recent scoping review by Pope et al found that pharmacists in the USA are generally supportive of harm reduction efforts, though engagement varied significantly.25 Since pharmacy staff are increasingly involved in addressing substance use-related harms and caring for PWUD, it is crucial to synthesise the breadth and depth of the existing literature on harm reduction services. Mapping this area of research is essential to provide an overview of existing implementation approaches, identify key gaps and inform future evaluations of the feasibility and sustainability of such services. Therefore, this scoping review seeks to fill this gap by systematically mapping studies on the implementation of harm reduction services offered by pharmacy staff for PWUD.

Methods

Study design and protocol

The scoping review methodology, with its exploratory and descriptive nature, allows mapping of evidence sources and identification of research gaps, making it suitable for summarising evidence.26 27 The work followed Arksey and O’Malley’s five-stage framework,28 enhanced by Levac et al,29 reported per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses protocol extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (online supplemental file 1)30 and was registered on the Open Science Framework registries.31 A comprehensive protocol was previously published.32

Search strategy

The medical librarian (JYK) developed and executed comprehensive searches in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, CINAHL, Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection on 4 June 2024, and updated on 25 July 2025. Relevant keywords and controlled vocabulary were carefully selected. The MEDLINE search was peer-reviewed by a second medical librarian before being translated to the other databases (online supplemental file 2). In addition to subscription databases, the team reviewed the first 200 results from Google Scholar, a reasonable number to screen given the overlap with Web of Science.33 Articles were imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation) for management and screening.

Screening and study selection

The study selection focused on peer-reviewed literature that described or evaluated harm reduction services for PWUD (particularly unregulated substances and non-medical drug use) provided by pharmacy staff (table 1). To be included, articles had to describe an implementation where pharmacy staff had direct interaction with PWUD. Given our focus on unregulated and non-medical drug use, articles were excluded if they focused on services related to tobacco, alcohol, cannabis use, opioid stewardship, such as for the treatment of acute and chronic pain, or services not described as harm reduction (eg, explicitly requiring abstinence as a programme outcome or excluding people who continue to use drugs). Studies were also excluded if they lacked details about the service and its implementation, the role of pharmacy staff or only reported patient outcomes (eg, experiences or attitudes), and were incomplete or yet to be published (eg, conference abstracts). When implementation or service outcomes were not explicitly labelled as such by study authors, they were identified through reported findings related to service delivery, pharmacy staff roles or implementation processes.

Eligibility criteria

PWUD, people who use drugs.

This review searched for all services aimed at reducing substance use harm for PWUD, including treatment programmes when labelled as harm reduction or explicitly referencing harm reduction as part of their approach. While OAT programmes for opioid use disorder (OUD) are not universally regarded as harm reduction, particularly when they are abstinence-based or coercive, there remains a significant opportunity to align treatment with harm reduction principles, fostering more person-centred and non-judgmental models of care.34

Articles were screened in two phases. Two team members (JN and EB) independently screened study titles and abstracts for eligibility. Then, both team members reviewed the full text of articles identified as potentially relevant, including their reference lists. During full-text screening, discrepancies related to three articles were resolved through discussion and consensus between reviewers (JN and EB). The protocol included a third reviewer (EH) for consultations on final decisions when consensus was not reached between the two reviewers, but this was not required.

Data extraction and synthesis

A data extraction tool (Excel, V.16.39, Microsoft) was developed to record key information about the included articles. The list of included articles was divided into two parts. Two team members (JN and EB) extracted the data, each handling half of the articles and then reviewing the data extracted by the other reviewer for accuracy. Following the data extraction, a team meeting was held to discuss preliminary findings and refine the data extraction tool with all members. A second round of data extraction, incorporating newly added variables, followed the same approach. A descriptive analysis was conducted, including a numerical overview of the included articles by type and distribution, as well as a narrative synthesis. Articles were grouped and synthesised based on the harm reduction service reported.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

After removing duplicates, 7815 records were screened. After title and abstract screening, a total of 198 records were included for full-text screening, and their reference lists were also searched. This process resulted in 43 articles being included for analysis (figure 1).

Study characteristics

Most studies were retrospective (n=9), followed by cross-sectional (n=8) and interventional (n=6). The majority were conducted in the USA (n=27) followed by the UK (n=15), and over half were published after 2021 (n=23). Pharmacy staff roles were typically described as collaborative and integrated into multidisciplinary teams (n=34), with pharmacists’ roles reported more often (n=41) than those of pharmacy technicians and assistants (n=8) (online supplemental file 3).

Harm reduction services

Seven categories were identified: (1) STBBI care (n=14); (2) needle and syringe programmes (n=9); (3) naloxone distribution and overdose education (n=8); (4) medication treatment for OUD (n=8); (5) mental healthcare and information services (n=2); (6) drug-checking (n=1); and 7) referrals to medical/social services (n=1).

STBBI care

STBBI testing and treatment

One study described a hospital-based programme for Indigenous communities,35 and another a mobile pharmacy and clinic.36 Through standing orders35 or new state legislation,36 pharmacy staff provided STBBI laboratory panels for syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, trichomonas, HIV, hepatitis B virus (HBV) and HCV35 and pregnancy testing35 or offered point-of-care HIV and HCV testing.36 Pharmacy staff also provided treatment for sexually transmitted infections (STI),35 36 HCV35 36 and HIV.36 Partner therapy for chlamydia, gonorrhoea and trichomoniasis,35 HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis36 and STI prevention treatment (eg, doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis) were also offered.36 Additionally, pharmacy staff provided tailored education on safe injecting practices,35 assistance with medical appointments,35 provided harm reduction supplies35 and dispensed/prescribed naloxone.35 36 The programmes documented 23 new STI cases,35 21 and 9 new HCV diagnoses35 36 and 1 new HIV case.36 In one study, Indigenous nurses helped follow-up and treat syphilis cases.35

HCV testing and direct-acting antiviral treatment

Within the nine studies focusing on HCV, three reported on a community pharmacy-based care model,37,39 two described an outreach care model,40 41 two compared community pharmacy models with those in other settings42 43 and two described treatment programmes.44 45 The testing roles described included performing dried-blood spot testing,38 39 42 ordering antibody laboratory testing,37 performing phlebotomy40 and referring patients to other providers.37 42 Pharmacists in all studies participated in HCV treatment care in various capacities.37,45 Additional roles included supervised OAT and HCV therapy,38 39 43 monitoring therapy outcomes,37,45 assessing post-treatment outcomes,40 41 administering immunisations for hepatitis A virus and HBV,41 as well as providing overdose medication and HIV PrEP.40 None of these studies reported substance use as a criterion for excluding participants.

The pharmacy-led HCV care pathways effectively provided testing and treatment for PWUD,39 41 42 improving the reach and impact of HCV treatment,38 44 45 detecting more cases than standard care42 and demonstrating feasibility.37 Completion of treatment rates ranged from 49% to 86%.37 38 41 44 45 Although the pharmacist-led model was deemed the most expensive compared with other models, it proved to be cost-effective.42 43 Manca et al reported a 7.8% increase in case detection at pharmacies versus standard care.42 Most studies indicated services were free for patients,37,3942 43 while pharmacists either received incentives38 or billed for HCV services.40 41

Challenges reported included establishing HCV eligibility, reimbursement, practice legislation and lack of patients’ knowledge or documentation of HCV history.40 Facilitators included the pharmacy scope of practice, willingness to cover uncompensated care, as well as incorporating trauma-informed and harm reduction approaches to care.40

HIV education, PrEP and antiretroviral therapy

Among the studies on HIV, one examined the impact of HIV education for people who inject drugs (PWID),46 one assessed PrEP access for PWID47 and one evaluated the provision of HIV antiretroviral therapy (ART) for PWUD.48

Lewis et al found that participants who received services from pharmacy staff trained in harm reduction were significantly more likely to use sterile syringes.46 Participants’ surveys were available in both English and Spanish.46 Similarly, Bazzi et al supported HIV PrEP access for PWID who experienced homelessness.47 Pharmacists worked as part of a multidisciplinary team, dispensing and repackaging PrEP into 7 day supplies and providing on-site medication support to improve adherence and reduce patient stress, with non-stigmatising, flexible care.47 Metcalfe et al reported that pharmacist-led ART monitoring increased access to ART from 29% to 59%.48 Pharmacists tailored their services to meet patient needs, offering flexibility and ongoing support for medication adherence.48

Needle and syringe programmes

Nine studies described community pharmacy-based needle and syringe programmes for PWID.49,57 Most programmes reported facilitating needle and syringe disposal through returns or providing sharp containers.4952,56 Pharmacists provided information or referrals to testing for STBBI, as well as housing support services.53,55 They educated patients on safe disposal49 and safer drug use.55 56 Pharmacists also provided additional dispensed prescriptions for OAT and other controlled medications.53 56 57 Still, Fuller et al described limited interaction between pharmacy staff and clients during transactions.50 Some programmes offered free sterile supplies,52,55 sold them without a prescription,49 50 or a combination of both.51 56

Some programmes coordinated extensive networks that included various settings.49 51 56 57 Lurie et al analysed the costs of one network and found that community pharmacy models were less costly than needle and syringe programmes operating as standalone services.51 However, all settings were likely cost-saving to society when considering annual HIV incidence rates of over 2.1%.51 Since programmes have different funding models and target groups, Lurie et al suggest that they should complement each other.51

Implementation challenges included remuneration,52 57 availability of a private room,57 referring clients to external services57 and training.52 53 Concerns about their clients50 52 56 or the experience49 were rarely reported by pharmacists. Recommendations included clients’ incentives for returning supplies, staff training, advertising, cheaper or free injecting equipment, more time to provide advice and a private consultation area.56

Naloxone distribution and overdose education

Naloxone programmes were implemented in various settings, including community pharmacies,58 59 outpatient pharmacies,60 specialty pharmacies,61 the Veterans Affairs Healthcare System network62 63 and hospitals.64 65 In most cases, pharmacists dispensed naloxone and provided patient education on overdose prevention.5860,65 Pharmacists’ roles were described as part of a multidisciplinary team of providers.6062,65 However, only one of the six studies mentioned that members of a harm reduction non-profit organisation played a key role in developing the protocol.64 The service was generally free for patients and funded through grants,62,65 although in some cases, patients paid for it via insurance or out-of-pocket expenses.58,61

Studies showed increased rates of naloxone co-prescribing60 and kits dispensed,5863,65 as well as recorded successful opioid overdose reversals.61 Moore et al noted that 87% of all take-home naloxone orders were dispensed, with heroin use the most common reason.65 Conversely, Duval et al identified low uptake among pharmacists, mainly due to a lack of requisitions and limited knowledge about harm reduction.59

Implementation challenges included accessing patients’ information and naloxone cost.60 64 Success factors included leadership support, partnering with a harm reduction organisation and having staff champions like a pharmacist and a social worker.64

Medication treatment for OUD

In the eight studies on medication treatment for OUD models, six focused on BUP treatment66,71 and two on OUD medication management.72 73 Pharmacy staff roles included dispensing therapy,66 68 69 independently prescribing medications,73 monitoring therapy,66 67 69 70 72 73 observing self-administration,68 ordering and/or interpreting drug screen results,69 coordinating the transition of patients to other care settings (eg, outreach to office-based treatment services, hospital to community pharmacy),67 73 providing other harm reduction supplies and services to patients,67 68 72 73 educating and counselling patients,66,7072 73 delivering therapy directly to patients living in supportive housing accommodations67,69 or to community organisations66 and supporting wound care and overdose response.70 When reported, services were covered by patients’ private insurance66 or other medication benefits and grants for low-income patients.66,69

All models incorporated a multidisciplinary approach, where pharmacy staff collaborated with other healthcare providers, including physicians, registered nurses, practicing nurses, psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers and staff from community organisations.66,7072 73 Some models integrated a community-participatory approach, connecting pharmacy staff with supportive housing staff67,69 and non-profit public health harm reduction organisations.66 70

In all models, pharmacy staff offered low-barrier access to OAT. Ly et al noted their programme offered optional non-punitive urine drug screenings for follow-up.67 Jessop et al described how pharmacy staff modified the pharmacy setup, were mindful of their language and communication and connected people with additional resources.66 Studies presented BUP as a harm reduction tool for patients not aiming for abstinence69 and a low-threshold model following harm reduction principles.70 At the same time, Llewelyn-Williams et al highlighted patient readiness and support as one of the pillars of the pharmacist’s approach.73 Whereas Messmer et al reported that their mobile programme, based on the Indigenous Leader Outreach Model, enabled them to meet people where they are.70

Programmes were considered successful in increasing access to BUP for patients diagnosed with OUD66 67 70 72 and supporting patients in adhering to treatment.67 68 Tran et al reported that patients who received a consultation from the multidisciplinary care team experienced shorter stays and lower 30-day readmission rates compared with those who did not.72 The role of a pharmacist in the team was highlighted as contributing to the success of enhanced OUD medication use.72

Mental healthcare and information services

One study described pharmacists’ activities within a multidisciplinary team aiming at providing tailored, comprehensive mental healthcare for eligible patients.74 Pharmacists completed over 1100 visits, with the most common medical interventions being monitoring or initiating BUP (33%).74 Other roles included triaging outpatient consults, placing referrals to higher levels of care and ordering naloxone kits and laboratory blood panels.74

Scott et al implemented an information service on-site for staff and clients of a harm reduction drug agency.75 The pharmacist offered information and advice on non-prescribed drug use, drug testing, adverse drug reactions, harm reduction strategies and drug-related health issues, among others, on a drop-in basis. Integrating a pharmacist into a multidisciplinary team working with a harm reduction organisation was found to be feasible and enhanced access to care for PWUD.75

Drug-checking

Guirguis et al implemented a pharmacist-led drug-checking service at a harm reduction community centre.76 The pharmacist used a portable spectrometer to check substances on-site, providing real-time analysis. They provided counselling, education and referrals to providers and worked closely with the staff.76 The design involved engaging current users of the centre, staff, individuals with lived and living experience of substance use and decision-makers. The pharmacist provided an appropriate evaluation of the drug-checking analysis, review of risk assessments and tailored harm reduction advice.76 Combining drug-checking services with legal, clinical, social and multidisciplinary frameworks had valuable implications for PWUD’s engagement in harm reduction services.76

Referrals to medical/social services

A study expanded a service programme for PWID in community pharmacies, providing referrals to medical and social services, sterile injection supplies and harm reduction education.77 Pharmacy staff were trained on HIV, HCV, addiction and non-judgemental engagement using a harm reduction approach.77 The intervention group was less likely to share injecting supplies and more likely to see a clinician and use pharmacy services.77 Materials were available in English and Spanish. Rudolph et al emphasised trust-building, community partnerships and participatory approaches as success factors to address healthcare disparities.77

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to summarise research that described or evaluated the implementation of harm reduction services for PWUD provided by pharmacy staff. Our results show that pharmacy staff perform a wide range of roles and participate in various harm reduction models of care for PWUD, and that included studies generally reported positive findings regarding the feasibility, effectiveness and safety of these services.

Pharmacy staff were involved in a broad spectrum of harm reduction services, often as part of multidisciplinary care models. Their roles varied significantly across settings and jurisdictions, ranging from autonomous functions, such as independently prescribing HCV treatment35 43 and managing a drug-checking programme,76 to collaborative practice agreements with physicians or psychiatrists to manage medications. These findings align with previous research reporting suitable harm reduction services for pharmacy practice78 and highlighting the value of coordinated, low-barrier networks that include pharmacy teams in substance use care, such as opioid stewardship79 and OUD medication management.23 24 80 81 Existing evidence suggests that pharmacists view themselves as playing a role in health promotion for PWID,82 overdose prevention83 and accessible care for PWUD.21 Marley et al reported that community pharmacists have also expressed willingness to engage in innovative services, such as fentanyl or xylazine testing.21 Our findings support the role of pharmacy staff as an accessible point of care for harm reduction services and underscore the need to support the roles of all pharmacy team members, including technicians and assistants, within harm reduction, ensuring their contributions are aligned with its core principles and fully integrated into equitable models of care.

Many studies described their services as low-barrier or low-threshold, often including financial coverage to address PWUD’s structural vulnerabilities. However, only two studies targeted equity-deserving populations, including pregnant and postpartum individuals with OUD60 and Indigenous communities.35 Moreover, there was high variability in how studies reported participants’ demographics, including sex, gender, race and ethnicity. These findings suggest that services may overlook the unique needs and barriers faced by women, Indigenous peoples, 2SLGBTQIA+ and racialised PWUD.84 Additionally, a few studies described formal partnerships with individuals with lived or living experience of substance use or community organisations. Without meaningful community involvement, services risk reproducing systemic power imbalances and excluding the voices of those affected. Grounding harm reduction efforts in community-engaged approaches is one way to ensure that interventions are not only evidence-based but also equitable, person-centred and trusted by those they aim to support.

While intervention approaches varied, most studies focused on addressing opioid use-related harms, reflecting a broader trend in the North American literature, where opioid-related harms constitute a major public health concern.85 86 The escalating toxicity of the unregulated drug supply, driven by an adaptable and ever-expanding market, has played a central role in shaping this landscape in the region.85 However, the use of different synthetic and non-synthetic substances such as amphetamines, cocaine and benzodiazepines also remains prevalent.10 While implementing these services is crucial, the harms related to the use of other substances and polysubstance use should not be overlooked. A comprehensive harm reduction approach in pharmacy practice should consider addressing this broader context. Overall, the implementation of other potentially relevant pharmacy-led harm reduction services for PWUD, such as off-label prescribing to manage withdrawal symptoms,87 safer supply19 20 88 or support for off-label androgen use,89 was absent in the included peer-reviewed literature.

This review identified key barriers to implementing harm reduction services in pharmacy settings, including physical layout, remuneration, reimbursement and service costs. These findings align with previous work by Rawal et al, who reported that reimbursement and patients’ access issues hindered the implementation of pharmacy-based naloxone and BUP services.17 24 Stigma among pharmacy staff towards PWUD and PWID has also emerged as a persistent barrier, contributing to inconsistent service availability and exacerbating existing disparities in care for patients.1721 24 82 83 90,93 Encouragingly, several studies addressed this issue by providing pharmacy staff with clinical training on substance use as well as education on non-stigmatising communication and community engagement. A few recent studies go further, explicitly integrating harm reduction as a guiding philosophy of care.35 36 70 73 These interventions detail implementation processes and prioritise person-centred, non-judgmental engagement, demonstrating that a more comprehensive and ethically grounded model of pharmacy practice is feasible, and offering concrete examples of how such principles can be embedded in pharmacy practice. To fully leverage the evolving role of pharmacy staff in caring for PWUD, it is essential to incorporate harm reduction principles into pharmacy education and support pharmacy staff in delivering equitable, person-centred care.94

This review identified critical gaps in the literature reviewed. Most research was conducted in the USA, primarily focused on opioid-related harms, and the role of pharmacy technicians and assistants was underexplored. Few studies addressed the design or delivery of services tailored for equity-deserving populations, and the perspectives of people with lived and living experience were rarely incorporated into programme design. Validated implementation and evaluation frameworks were infrequently employed in the included studies, limiting the extent to which outcomes could be consistently reported and compared across studies. While more recent studies report important progress in integrating harm reduction as a guiding philosophy of care, engaging people with lived experience in programme design and adopting collaborative and comprehensive approaches to pharmacy service delivery, these practices remain limited in scope and are not consistently reported across the literature.

The findings of this review highlight significant considerations for practice and point to opportunities for future research to enhance the role of pharmacy staff as essential providers in the public health response to the drug overdose crisis. Overall, harm reduction services delivered by pharmacy staff should be tailored to local contexts and community needs. Collaborative models of care, particularly those involving interdisciplinary partnerships and engagement with people with lived and living experience, appear central to service delivery. In addition, the variability in reporting on implementation processes underscores the need for an evaluation-oriented culture within pharmacy programmes, including documenting implementation strategies and outcomes to support learning, scalability and sustainability. To support this, future research would benefit from systematically measuring and reporting implementation and service outcomes using validated implementation science frameworks. Moving forward, developing a coordinated and sustainable harm reduction approach will require integrating person-centred care and harm reduction principles into pharmacy education, practice, research and policy.

To our knowledge, this is the first review to systematically map the literature on the implementation of harm reduction services for PWUD provided by pharmacy staff. This scoping review followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, and its protocol was registered before publication and screening to ensure transparency of the process and reduce potential bias. Our comprehensive search strategy was developed by a health sciences librarian and peer-reviewed by a second librarian. However, some limitations of this review should be noted. First, this review is not a systematic literature review; therefore, a quality appraisal was not performed. Second, non-academic literature, including clinical guidelines, policy briefs and reports, was excluded from the analysis. Thus, this review may not have included programmes that have not been reported in peer-reviewed literature. Future work may use the findings of this scoping review as a guide to perform a quality assessment of studies in this field and to conduct a comprehensive review of non-academic literature.

Conclusion

PWUD face unique challenges in accessing care, and pharmacy staff are well-positioned to provide harm reduction services. This review summarises the growing involvement of pharmacy staff in delivering a broad spectrum of harm reduction services for PWUD, especially the different roles pharmacists play, as well as their integration into multidisciplinary care models. However, the current literature does not explore further the role of other pharmacy staff, and existing evidence indicates that these services are not tailored to equity-deserving populations that use drugs. Future implementation efforts could expand to other pharmacy staff roles, while prioritising the principles of harm reduction to better support the expanding role of pharmacy staff in public health efforts addressing substance-related harms.
