Safety of Fondaparinux After Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin in Bleeding-Complicated High-Risk Pregnancies: A PSM Cohort Study
Authors: Hongjun Yang, Yuehan Gao, Xiao Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-risk pregnancies (those complicated by antiphospholipid syndrome, rheumatic disease, or adverse obstetric histories) increase the risk of thromboembolic events, for which low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) is commonly used. However, LMWH can cause bleeding complications, leading to consideration of alternative anticoagulants. Fondaparinux (FPX), a selective factor Xa inhibitor, has been suggested as an alternative, but its safety and efficacy in pregnancy remain unclear. This study aimed to compare maternal and neonatal outcomes between high-risk pregnant women switched from LMWH to FPX due to bleeding complications and those who continued LMWH.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary hospital from January 2018 to June 2024. Pregnant women on LMWH who developed LMWH-related bleeding were switched to FPX and resumed LMWH once bleeding resolved. The comparison group consisted of women who continued LMWH without complications. After propensity score matching (PSM), maternal and neonatal outcomes were compared between the two groups using chi-square tests for categorical variables and rank-sum tests for continuous variables.ResultsA total of 159 high‑risk pregnant women were included before propensity score matching (124 in the LMWH group and 35 in the FPX group). After 2:1 matching, 62 patients (40 receiving LMWH and 22 receiving FPX) were analyzed with well‑balanced baseline characteristics between groups. In the matched cohort, rates of miscarriage, gestational age at delivery, live birth, Apgar scores, and neonatal intensive care unit admission were comparable between the two groups (all p > 0.05). Postpartum blood loss within 48 hours was significantly lower in the FPX group (p = 0.016), whereas neonatal birth weight was modestly lower (p = 0.019). Overall, FPX demonstrated a safety profile comparable to LMWH without an increase in adverse maternal or neonatal outcomes.ConclusionThe results are suggestive that FPX may be a safe and effective alternative for pregnant women intolerant to LMWH, associated with reduced postpartum hemorrhage and no increase in adverse outcomes. Further validation through multicenter and prospective studies is needed.

Introduction

Pregnancy confers a heightened risk of thromboembolism due to physiologic hypercoagulability, venous stasis, and endothelial changes, with risk further amplified among women with antiphospholipid syndrome, rheumatic disease, or adverse obstetric histories.1 To prevent maternal and fetal complications in these high-risk settings, anticoagulation is essential.2 Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) remains the standard of care, favored for minimal placental transfer and a lower risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia compared with unfractionated heparin.3,4 However, LMWH can precipitate clinically relevant bleeding—such as vaginal bleeding or retroplacental hematoma—necessitating dose adjustments, temporary interruption, or alternative strategies to balance thromboprophylaxis against hemorrhage.5 This underscores the need for alternative anticoagulants that maintain efficacy while minimizing adverse events in vulnerable pregnant populations.

Despite the widespread use of LMWH, bleeding complications remain a significant clinical challenge, occurring in up to 2–5% of treated pregnancies and potentially leading to miscarriage, placental abruption, or preterm delivery, with global data indicating even higher major bleeding rates of 6.5–7.7% in therapeutic anticoagulation cohorts.6–8 Fondaparinux (FPX), a selective factor Xa inhibitor, has emerged as a promising alternative in non-pregnant populations due to its predictable pharmacokinetics, lack of requirement for routine monitoring, and lower incidence of thrombocytopenia.9,10 However, evidence on the safety and efficacy of FPX in pregnancy is limited. Although a related meta-analysis found no significant differences in pregnancy outcomes compared with LMWH, the included FPX studies were largely case reports and small cohorts, with recent meta-analyses reporting maternal bleeding incidence around 3.2%.11,12 Consequently, whether switching to FPX during pregnancy due to bleeding tendency affects pregnancy outcomes—particularly neonatal outcomes—remains insufficiently explored.

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study evaluated the safety and efficacy of switching from LMWH to FPX in high-risk pregnant women with LMWH-related bleeding complications, compared to those continuing LMWH. Using propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline characteristics, we provided robust real-world evidence on maternal and neonatal outcomes—including gestational complications, delivery parameters, and postpartum events—motivated by the need to validate FPX as a viable alternative for guiding clinical anticoagulation management.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective cohort study collected data from January 2018 to June 2024 at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University Dezhou Hospital. Inclusion criteria were patients who initiated LMWH therapy before or during pregnancy. Exclusion criteria included twin or multiple pregnancies and non-medical termination of pregnancy and bleeding events attributable to causes unrelated to LMWH (such as placenta previa, trauma, or underlying coagulopathies independent of anticoagulation). All patients initially received LMWH treatment. During this period, some patients switched to FPX therapy due to factors such as retroplacental hematoma and vaginal bleeding. Therefore, we divided all patients into two groups: the LMWH group, consisting of patients who continued LMWH throughout; and the FPX group, comprising patients who initially received LMWH and subsequently switched to FPX due to retroplacental hematoma, vaginal bleeding, or other factors, and who, after symptom resolution, switched back to LMWH for continued treatment.

Baseline Characteristics

The study assessed a comprehensive set of baseline pre-pregnancy clinical characteristics, including age at delivery, gravida, parity, abortion history, history of cesarean delivery, assisted reproductive technology, rheumatic diseases (antiphospholipid syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren’s syndrome, and no definite diagnosis), and adverse obstetric history (ambryonic arrest, intrauterine fetal demise, neonatal death, and fetal malformations or chromosomal abnormalities). Pregnancy-related clinical characteristics evaluated included hypothyroidism, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, amniotic fluid status (normal, polyhydramnios, oligohydramnios), premature rupture of membranes, fetal growth restriction, fetal distress, gestational week at initiation of anticoagulant therapy, aspirin therapy, progesterone therapy, immunosuppressive therapy, and immune indicators (anti-nuclear antibody, anti-cardiolipin antibody, anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibody, lupus anticoagulant, protein C, protein S, antithrombin, and blocking antibody). For patients switched to FPX, additional characteristics recorded were the gestational week of FPX initiation, reasons for switching (eg, retroplacental hematoma, vaginal bleeding), and duration of FPX medication. Pre-delivery blood test indicators comprised hemoglobin, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, plasma prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, thrombin time, and fibrinogen. Pregnancy outcomes assessed included gestational weeks at pregnancy termination, mode of pregnancy termination (vaginal delivery, cesarean section, abortion), anesthesia regimen (none, neuraxial, general), postpartum blood loss within 48 hours, anemia, placental adhesion, upper respiratory tract infection, live birth rate, neonatal birth weight, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes, and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission.

Endpoints

This study primarily compared adverse pregnancy outcomes, maternal perinatal adverse complications, and neonatal adverse complications between the LMWH and FPX groups. Pregnancy outcomes assessed included gestational duration at pregnancy termination, mode of pregnancy termination (vaginal delivery, cesarean section, abortion), postpartum blood loss within 48 hours, anemia, placental adhesion, upper respiratory tract infection, live birth rate, neonatal birth weight, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes, and NICU admission.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized with descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square test (frequency and percentage); continuous variables, using non-parametric rank-sum tests (median and interquartile ranges [IQR]).

To enable a more reliable comparison of pregnancy outcomes between the LMWH and FPX groups, we conducted 2:1 PSM using pre-pregnancy baseline characteristics, with the aim of minimizing baseline differences and enhancing between-group comparability. Covariates included age at delivery, gravida, parity, abortion history, history of cesarean delivery, assisted reproductive technology, rheumatic diseases (eg, antiphospholipid syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren’s syndrome), and adverse obstetric history (eg, ambryonic arrest, intrauterine fetal demise). Matching was conducted using nearest neighbor caliper matching without replacement (caliper 0.02), ensuring a standardized mean difference (SMD) <0.2 for balance assessment. After PSM, comparisons between the two groups were performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and non-parametric rank-sum tests for continuous variables.

Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Characteristics of the FPX Group

Discussion

In this propensity score-matched retrospective cohort, switching from LMWH to FPX in high-risk pregnancies complicated by LMWH-related bleeding was associated with generally comparable maternal and neonatal outcomes and lower postpartum blood loss in the matched analysis. These findings suggest that FPX may be a clinically useful alternative in carefully selected patients who cannot continue LMWH because of bleeding complications. However, given the retrospective single-center design and limited sample size, the present results should be interpreted cautiously. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm safety, identify the most appropriate candidates for switching, and define optimal implementation strategies in obstetric anticoagulation practice.

LMWH is the standard prophylaxis for venous thromboembolism in pregnancy but may induce bleeding complications due to partial platelet factor 4 (PF4) interaction and variable anticoagulant responses.3,6 FPX, a fully synthetic factor Xa inhibitor, exhibits minimal PF4 binding and no cross-reactivity with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, providing a more predictable anticoagulant effect.9,13 In the context of retroplacental or vaginal bleeding, FPX’s selective mechanism may limit hematoma expansion compared with LMWH, making it a rational rescue strategy.10,14 Nevertheless, potential adverse effects include placental passage in trace amounts, maternal bleeding, and low neonatal birth weight in some reports.12,15 Close clinical monitoring is recommended, including serial ultrasound for hematoma assessment, complete blood count, coagulation parameters, and fetal growth surveillance. Given its favorable pharmacokinetics and safety profile in small cohorts, FPX represents a viable option where LMWH intolerance occurs, though long-term neonatal safety requires further high-quality evidence.6,16 Additionally, although FPX was administered during the bleeding episode and symptoms improved in our study, we cannot infer that FPX hastens hematoma resolution. The improvement may instead reflect LMWH interruption and supportive care. Prospective studies with predefined imaging intervals and standardized assessments are needed to determine whether FPX offers benefit beyond stopping LMWH.

Our post-matching analysis revealed no significant differences in most maternal and neonatal outcomes between the FPX and LMWH groups, including gestational age at delivery, live birth rates, Apgar scores, and NICU admissions (all p>0.05). However, the FPX group exhibited lower postpartum blood loss within 48 hours (p=0.016) and neonatal birth weight (p=0.019). The reduced blood loss may reflect FPX’s targeted factor Xa inhibition, which could offer a more controlled anticoagulant effect during the peripartum period, minimizing hemorrhage without increasing thrombotic risks.17 The lower birth weight in the FPX group might be influenced by baseline imbalances, such as higher rates of immunosuppressive therapy (72.7% vs. 12.5%) and abnormal immune indicators, which persisted post-matching and are known to affect fetal growth.18 Importantly, miscarriage rates were similar (2.9% in FPX vs. 1.6% in LMWH), suggesting FPX does not exacerbate early pregnancy loss in high-risk cases.

Our results are consistent with recent meta-analyses and reviews on FPX in pregnancy. A 2023 meta-analysis of fondaparinux versus LMWH in patients with recurrent miscarriage found no significant differences in key pregnancy outcomes (eg, live birth rate: OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.85–1.47) or adverse events (eg, maternal bleeding: OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.68–1.24), though it noted limited data on neonatal metrics like birth weight, with only small cohorts contributing to pooled estimates.11 Similarly, a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis reported comparable safety profiles for FPX in pregnant women, including low rates of maternal bleeding (pooled incidence 3.2%, 95% CI 1.8–4.6%) and no increased fetal complications (eg, congenital anomalies: OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.55–1.10), but highlighted the need for more robust comparative data in high-risk subgroups.12 In contrast to our observation of lower neonatal birth weight in the FPX group (p=0.019), a 2025 state-of-the-art review observed variable neonatal birth weights with FPX (ranging from 2.5–3.2 kg across studies), possibly due to population differences such as varying rates of rheumatic comorbidities or obstetric histories.15 Our study extends these findings by incorporating PSM to address confounders, providing stronger comparative evidence in a real-world setting with high-risk features like rheumatic diseases.

The observed benefits of FPX switching, such as reduced postpartum bleeding, may stem from its pharmacokinetic advantages, including a longer half-life and no need for monitoring, which allow for stable anticoagulation without the heparin-induced platelet activation seen with LMWH.19 In patients with bleeding complications, FPX’s minimal interaction with PF 4 could reduce hematoma progression, as evidenced by studies showing improved subchorionic hematoma resolution when FPX is combined with conventional therapy in cases of protein S deficiency, aligning with our FPX group characteristics (eg, switching primarily due to retroplacental hematoma or vaginal bleeding).20 The neonatal weight difference might relate to indirect effects, such as higher progesterone and immunosuppressive use in the FPX group, which could influence placental function and nutrient transfer.7 However, no direct teratogenic effects of FPX have been reported in recent reviews.6,15

Clinically, our findings support FPX as a viable rescue therapy for LMWH-intolerant high-risk pregnancies, particularly in those with rheumatic diseases or adverse obstetric histories.21 This could inform guidelines, such as updating recommendations for anticoagulation switching in bleeding scenarios, potentially reducing unnecessary terminations or prolonged hospitalizations by optimizing combined antiplatelet and anticoagulation approaches.7,22 For example, the median 35-day FPX duration in our cohort suggests short-term use is sufficient to resolve bleeding without long-term risks, aligning with EULAR guidelines for antiphospholipid syndrome management and evidence questioning prolonged LMWH in recurrent placenta-mediated complications.18 Overall, these results advocate for personalized anticoagulation strategies to optimize maternal-fetal outcomes in vulnerable populations.

This study benefits from its real-world design, incorporating propensity score matching to minimize confounding and provide robust comparative insights into FPX as a rescue therapy in high-risk patients who develop bleeding signs during LMWH use. The comprehensive assessment of maternal and neonatal outcomes adds practical value to the limited evidence base on FPX use. That said, while propensity score matching helps balance key variables, it may not fully account for all potential confounders, potentially leaving some residual imbalances. Furthermore, the single-center design offers meaningful preliminary evidence, and future expansions to multi-center studies could further explore and validate these findings in diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion

Switching to FPX in high-risk pregnancies with LMWH-related bleeding appears safe and effective, offering a practical alternative without compromising outcomes. These insights advocate for FPX’s integration into obstetric anticoagulation protocols, enhancing care for vulnerable patients.
