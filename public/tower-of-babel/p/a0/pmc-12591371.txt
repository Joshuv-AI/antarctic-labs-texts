Gradual Increase in Lung Cancer Risk Due to Particulate Matter Exposure in Patients With Pulmonary Function Impairments: A Nationwide Korean Database Analysis
Authors: Jongin Lee, Joon Young Choi, Jeong Uk Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer remains a significant public health concern, with external environmental factors, such as particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5), playing a crucial role in its development. This study aimed to assess the interaction between cumulative exposure to PM2.5 and lung function impairment, focusing on the risk of lung cancer in a large Korean population.MethodsData from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 2007 to 2020 were analyzed with linkage of the national cancer registry. A total of 43,973 participants, including 386 lung cancer cases, were included, with spirometry data available to classify lung function into categories. PM2.5 exposure was quantified using geocoded address information. Logistic regression models with interaction terms were used to evaluate lung cancer risk according to PM2.5 exposure levels and in interaction of lung function categories.ResultsA significant increase in lung cancer risk was observed with higher PM2.5 exposure levels, with odds ratios (ORs) of 1.41, 1.59, and 1.68 for the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th quartiles, respectively in multivariable models. Stratified analysis showed that the interaction between PM2.5 exposure and obstructive lung function significantly increased lung cancer risk, with ORs of 2.74 for spirometric obstruction and 3.43 for preserved ratio impaired spirometry in the highest PM2.5 quartile.ConclusionThis study suggests the carcinogenic risk of PM2.5 exposure, particularly in individuals with obstructive lung function impairments.

INTRODUCTION

In 2020, nearly two million new cases were reported globally, and the number is expected to increase dramatically by 2050.1 The effects of smoking need no further emphasis, as the effects of smoking are well known,2 and other occupational and environmental factors have been linked to the development of lung cancer. For example, female residents near coal-fired power plants have higher incidence rate of lung cancer compared to the standard population.3 Therefore, efforts to manage these external factors associated with lung cancer development are necessary to prevent cancer and reduce the risk of cancer development.4

In 2013, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified air pollution as a group 1 carcinogen that is definitely related to the incidence of lung cancer.5 Particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) literally means small particles less than 2.5 micrometres in diameter, which are emitted into the air by human activities such as diesel engines, road dust, agriculture and various industrial activities.6 Triggering cellular damage from free radical peroxidation, abnormalities in intracellular calcium homeostasis, and inflammatory damage associated with the release of various cytokines are described as the main mechanisms of PM2.5-induced respiratory effects.7 The mechanisms by which the response induced by PM2.5 exposure mediates the development of lung cancer have been reported in detail to date.89

While environmental influences play a role in the development of lung cancer, personal susceptibility also has an impact. The risk of lung cancer increases if a person has previously acquired a disease that can leave permanent damage to the lungs, such as a tuberculosis infection. In the case of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the mechanisms leading to its development are known to be shared with lung cancer.10 The mutual risk between lung cancer and COPD may be influenced by sharing a major risk factor, such as smoking.11 However, a significant increase in lung cancer risk was also found in a cohort comprising only non-smokers with COPD; suggesting that COPD may act as an independent risk factor for lung cancer even after controlling for shared risk factors.12 The severity of lung function decline itself is also associated with the development of lung cancer. In a cohort study, the risk of lung cancer increased in a gradient with the severity of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) decline.13

While air pollution is a known cause of lung cancer, as noted above, individual susceptibility may be related to the risk of developing lung cancer. It is reasonable to speculate that the effects of exposure to air pollution may vary depending on individual susceptibility. In particular, as mentioned above, understanding this interaction between two known risk factors is critical because lung function decline could amplify the impact of PM2.5 exposure and accelerate cancer development in vulnerable populations. Furthermore, there are few studies that have investigated the interaction quantitatively. In this context, the aim of this study was to evaluate the risk of lung cancer according to the degree of lung function in a large Korean population, focusing on air pollutants, especially PM2.5.

METHODS

Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES)

The KNHANES is a survey conducted by establishing a sample that is representative of the entire population of South Korea.14 In the case of spirometry in KNHANES, all adults aged 20 and over were surveyed from 2007 to 2009, but only adults aged 40 and over were surveyed from 2010 onwards. For this study, participants in the KNHANES from 2007 to 2020 were eligible for inclusion in the study, with a total number of 104,426 individuals surveyed.

Demographic characteristics

Sex and age are collected, and for smoking, respondents are categorized as never smokers if they have smoked less than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. To adjust for socioeconomic factors, household income, educational level, and occupation were investigated. Household income was categorized into quintiles, with slightly different numerical cut-off values based on the distribution of household income in each survey year.

Spirometry

In all KNHANES survey years, spirometry was performed on all adults aged 40 years and older unless there were unavoidable reasons. In 2020 and 2021, pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were not performed due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Forced vital capacity (FVC) and FEV1 from spirometry were available for KNHANES subjects who underwent PFTs. Only pre-bronchodilator values are available for analysis. For predicted values, the Korean normalized prediction equation was used.15 In this study, lung function status was assessed using the predicted ratio of FVC and FEV1 investigated regardless of symptoms. FVC < 0.8 was classified as spirometric restriction, FEV1/FVC < 0.7 was classified as spirometric obstruction, and FEV1/FVC > 0.7 but FEV1 < 0.8 was defined as preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm). A total of 44,037 subjects with available smoking history and PFT information were included in the study.

Assessment of exposure to air pollution

The Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCDC) has address information of subjects who participated in KNHANES. The addresses of all subjects participating in KNHANES since 2007 were converted to latitude-longitude coordinates, which were modelled based on air pollution concentrations, meteorological information, and satellite modelling, and combined with air pollution exposure information.

Each participant's KNHANES participation date was recorded and matched with air pollution information at the participant's address. The KCDC has compiled air pollution information for each KNHANES participant assessed. The methodology for incorporating air pollution information into KNHANES can be found in a separate journal article.16

As this study addresses the association with lung cancer development, it is reasonable to consider long-term exposures. However, since the air pollution database in Korea was established in 2005, 5-year air pollution concentration data were unavailable for a large number of subjects. In total, 31,716 subjects had both 2-year and 5-year exposure data while 43,973 subjects had 2-year exposure data. Considering that the missing rate would be 27.9%, and the correlation between the 5-year exposure assessment and the 2-year exposure for fine PM exposure was analyzed to be very high (Fig. 1), two-year exposures were considered to be more representative of longer-term exposure to PM2.5. The total amount of PM2.5 exposure was converted to an annual average and divided into quartiles to assess risk.

Diagnosis of lung cancer

To determine whether participants in KNAHENS subsequently developed lung cancer, their personal identification numbers were used to check whether they were diagnosed with lung cancer at the National Cancer Registration Program run by the National Cancer Center. Almost all newly diagnosed cancer patients are enrolled in the registration program. Of the 44,037 people in the study, we searched for those with International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) code C34 in the cancer registration data and identified 450 lung cancer diagnoses. In addition to the ICD-10 code, cancer registries record additional information such as histological type and stage. The authors verified that the diagnosis code assigned was appropriate for primary lung cancer by looking at the histological type and staging data in the cancer registry.

Of the 450 individuals diagnosed with lung cancer, 386 were diagnosed with lung cancer after participation in KNHANES and 64 were identified as having participated in KNHANES after their lung cancer diagnosis. To account for temporal follow-up, we excluded 64 individuals whose lung cancer diagnosis preceded the study. As a result, 43,973 individuals were ultimately included in the analysis, of whom 386 were confirmed to have been diagnosed with lung cancer (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics are presented according to the presence or absence of a lung cancer diagnosis. All variables except age were categorical, and chi-square tests were used to determine statistical significance. Exposure to PM2.5 was also distributed according to each demographic characteristic, and the distributions of the disorders analyzed: restriction, obstruction, PRISm and lung cancer were tested.

Crude and multivariate logistic regression was performed to determine the risk of developing lung cancer according to PM2.5 exposure, and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated. The same multivariate models were then stratified by the presence or absence of each impairment to determine the interaction of PM2.5 levels with pulmonary function on the risk of developing lung cancer. The interaction term was assumed to be the product of PM2.5 exposure levels and each impairment in the unstratified multivariate model, and the resulting p value was taken as an indicator of the presence or absence of an interaction. The significance level of the P value was chosen to be < 0.05, and for the ORs calculated, statistical significance was determined with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board of Yeouido St. Mary’s Hospital exempted this study as it was conducted using a pre-existing database established by the Korean government (No. SC23ZISE0076). In general, informed consent was obtained from all the study participants when they participated in the KNHANES.

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics of those with and without lung cancer diagnosis showed that age was significantly higher in those with lung cancer, with a mean age of 63.12 years for those diagnosed with lung cancer and 53.38 years for those without lung cancer. Although a higher proportion of the study population was female, more men were diagnosed with lung cancer, 241 (62.4%). In terms of smoking history, 173 (44.8%) of those diagnosed with lung cancer were current smokers, compared to only 24.5% of those without lung cancer. Household income and education also showed significant differences in relation to lung cancer diagnosis. Regarding occupation, 52.6% of those diagnosed with lung cancer were unemployed (Table 1).

Stratified analysis was performed according to pulmonary function impairment. The presence of restrictions was associated with an increased OR for lung cancer only in the fourth quartile (OR, 1.94; 95% CI, 1.08–3.50), and the change in OR with and without restrictions was not significant. With P for interaction = 0.538, restriction did not appear to interact with PM2.5 exposure on lung cancer risk.

However, for spirometric obstruction and PRISm, the variation in ORs across strata was very large. When looking at PM2.5 fourth quartile, the OR was 1.35 (95% CI, 0.87–2.04) in those without obstruction, which was not statistically significant, but increased dramatically to 2.74 (95% CI, 1.66–4.52) in those with obstruction, with P for interaction = 0.021, and the interaction term was statistically significant. DISCUSSION

Consistent with previous reports, this study suggests that PM2.5 exposure is a significant risk factor for lung cancer. The effect of high concentrations of PM2.5 exposure was significantly increased in the presence of spirometric obstruction or PRISm. In contrast, in the case of restrictive impairment, there was no interaction between PM2.5 exposure and increased lung cancer risk. Individuals with reduced pulmonary function, such as those with COPD or other chronic lung conditions, may be more vulnerable to the harmful effects of PM by the following mechanisms.1718

First, people with pre-existing lung conditions often experience chronic inflammation and increased oxidative stress in their lung tissue.19 Exposure to PM can further exacerbate this inflammation, creating an lung environment more likely to undergo malignant transformation.20 And reduced lung function can impair the ability to clear inhaled particles due to decreased ciliary function, allowing more PM to remain in the lungs and exert a longer-term detrimental effect.2122 A reduced capacity to clear ambient PM allows it to reach deeper into the peripheral areas of the lungs, which are more difficult to clear compared to the central airways. Followed by the mechanisms above, we could observe the interaction from obstructive lung function impairment on the carcinogenic effect by the exposure to PM2.5. However, our findings suggest that the interaction was not observed for subjects with restrictive impairment. This is because the restrictive impairment can also be related to non-pulmonary chronic illnesses, such as diabetes, congestive heart failure, obesity and hypertension, in addition to decreased lung functions.23

Given that the dataset in this study is considered a dynamic cohort, with new subjects added each year, a survival analysis could have been considered, as it would have been possible to identify subsequent lung cancer events. However, as KNHANES is based on voluntary participation, individuals with mobility difficulties due to chronic illness or accidents may have been missed from the sample.24 In a typical cohort study, the exposed and unexposed groups are binary,25 but this study differs from a typical cohort design in several ways. First, PM2.5 exposure levels were assigned as annual averages of the cumulative values for the two years prior to KNHANES participation. Although two years of exposure were included in the study, the effects are not limited to two years, as the effects of PM2.5 on a subject are likely to be lifelong. Indeed, the correlation between the two-year and five-year PM2.5 exposures was found to be highly significant, as depicted in Fig. 1.

In addition, immortal time bias is a problem when applying common survival analysis methods.26 All subjects were exposed to some level of PM2.5 prior to the exposure assessment, and PM2.5 is not only a precursor to lung cancer but can also be associated with other diseases such as cardiovascular disease, some of which can be fatal. As KNHANES is essentially a survey of healthy individuals, it is possible that some individuals may have already died from PM2.5 exposure in the past and be missed in the study. Therefore, it is difficult to find an appropriate effect size using conventional survival analysis methodology. Therefore, logistic regression was used in this study. When calculating ORs, there is a risk that a high proportion of cases can skew the value in the direction of a larger value,27 but in this study, the number of lung cancer cases was 386, which is very rare (less than 0.01%), so there is no risk of skewing the OR by a large number of cases.

Regarding the increase the risk of lung cancer by low-level PM2.5 exposure, the study of seven European cohorts, demonstrated that prolonged exposure to ambient PM2.5 is linked to an increased incidence of lung cancer, even at levels lower than the EU limits.28 A longitudinal multicenter study involving the same cohorts found that prolonged exposure to PM2.5 levels below the current annual limits was linked to a higher risk of lung cancer mortality.29 Our models for lung cancer incidence based on PM2.5 exposure (shown in Table 4) indicated that higher quartiles of exposure were associated with a gradual increase in the risk of developing lung cancer. The 2nd quartile of PM exposure showed a significantly higher risk of lung cancer recurrence compared to the 1st quartile, which served as the reference in the analysis. A study in the New England, the US, confined to area annual concentrations < 10 μg/m3 or daily concentrations < 30 μg/m3 showed PM2.5 was associated with increased mortality.30 In 2021, the World Health Organization updated its air quality guidelines, significantly tightening the exposure standard for PM2.5 to an annual average of < 5 μg/m3.31 This study supports this tightening of exposure levels. However, as this was an analysis of a population with high overall PM2.5 exposure levels, further research is needed to determine if populations exposed to lower concentrations show similar trends to this study.

This increased risk association with low PM exposure, was not statistically significant in subgroups (spirometric restriction, obstruction, and PRISm) in our study. A significant association with lung cancer was only evident when comparing the reference group to the 4th quartile of exposure in each respective subgroup. This could be largely attributed to the smaller sample sizes and reduced statistical power. Given the observed trend of a gradual increase in OR, these findings should be validated in larger populations with chronic lung diseases. Of the total 43,973 study subjects, 5,717 had obstruction and 2,483 were categorised as PRISm. Subgroup analyses were performed on these individuals, which significantly reduced the statistical power. Considering only the point estimates, the risk of lung cancer increased with increasing PM2.5 exposure.

This study has several limitations. First, because the data do not give information on pathological subtype and concurrent genetic alterations, we could not distinguish the effect from innate carcinogenic risks, such as the development of adenocarcinoma from young female population in East Asian ethnic group and epidermal growth factor receptor mutations.32 Even though this would provide strong evidence in this study, we could not use this information in this study using secondary data from the national database. The categorization of subjects according to lung function in this study relied solely on spirometry and not on physician diagnosis based on the presence or absence of symptoms. In addition, the test was performed without the use of bronchodilators, so it is likely that many patients without meaningful obstructive lung diseases were included in the obstruction group. Despite the absence of post-bronchodilator PFT values, we believe that our representation of the spirometric obstruction group still retain meaningful clinical significance, as pre-bronchodilator obstruction shows a high correlation with COPD. Only 8.5% of individuals did not exhibit fixed airway obstruction in previous study.33 Nevertheless, future studies representing COPD groups with fixed airway obstruction, assisted by physician diagnosis, will be necessary to demonstrate the association with lung cancer due to PM exposure.

Regarding the duration of the exposure assessment, we had to use 2-year estimates instead of 5-year estimates because KNHANES does not have enough data for 5-year estimates. However, as shown in Fig. 1, there was little difference between the assessment durations. PM2.5 exposure in a given period can be interpreted as a proxy for lifetime exposure to PM2.5. For example, a study in Norway estimated PM2.5 exposure for four years and it showed a significant increase in lung cancer deaths.34 Moreover, although UK Biobank has only one year estimate of PM2.5 exposure in 2010, it provides evidence that the exposure is associated with lung cancer incidence.35 However, it is the limitation of this study that it could not use genetic data like the study using the UK Biobank as well as detailed occupational exposure and the reliability of spirometry tests. And more, the exposure assessment was based on the address when the study subjects enrolled for KNHANES investigation, random errors could occur by movement before and after the investigation time point. Further studies need to focus on individualized risk factors above all.36

In conclusion, this study suggests the previously known carcinogenicity of PM2.5 exposure to lung cancer, and that the effect is more pronounced in those with obstructive lung dysfunction. This could be seen as an environmental health gap.37 It is necessary that efforts to reduce PM2.5 exposure may prioritize those with more vulnerable medical conditions, and therefore global efforts to reduce PM2.5 exposure should continue.
