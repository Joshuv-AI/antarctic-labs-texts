ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the efficacy and safety of guselkumab for the treatment of moderate to severe scalp psoriasis in individuals with diverse skin tones?FindingsIn this randomized clinical trial of 108 participants with moderate to severe scalp psoriasis, through 48 weeks of treatment, approximately 80% of VISIBLE Cohort B study participants achieved absent or very mild scalp disease, and approximately 70% achieved complete scalp clearance; mean percentage improvements in scalp surface area and Psoriasis Scalp Severity Index were greater than 92%. Most adverse events were mild or moderate; infections were the most common adverse events (reported in 33.3% of guselkumab-randomized participants), but no serious infections were reported.MeaningGuselkumab is a highly effective, durable scalp psoriasis treatment across all skin tones.

Introduction

Scalp psoriasis, which affects up to 80% of patients with plaque psoriasis,1,2,3 is associated with pain, pruritus, and flaking scale.3,4 In some patients, scalp psoriasis can cause alopecia and pigmentation changes extending beyond the scalp to the neck and forehead.4,5,6,7 Because lesions on the scalp, neck, and face are highly visible, patients often experience social isolation, negative body image, and limitations on work and daily activities, which can have debilitating effects on quality of life.4,8 Scalp psoriasis can cause considerable burden in people with skin of color, including Asian and Black individuals.8,9 Scalp psoriasis can be more severe and nuanced to treat in Black individuals because of specific hair textures and hair washing and styling practices.10,11,12,13

VISIBLE (Varying Skin Tones in Body and Scalp Psoriasis: Guselkumab Efficacy and Safety) is an ongoing, phase 3b, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of guselkumab in 2 cohorts of participants who self-identified their race or ethnicity as a category other than White (ie, individuals with skin of color).14 VISIBLE Cohort A enrolled participants with moderate to severe plaque psoriasis.15 VISIBLE Cohort B is dedicated to participants with moderate to severe scalp psoriasis across all objectively measured skin tones. Herein, we present VISIBLE Cohort B results through week 48.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Cohort B of the VISIBLE randomized clinical trial enrolled participants from 45 sites (32 in the US and 13 in Canada). The protocol (Supplement 1) was approved by appropriate institutional review boards or independent ethics committees. Advarra in Columbia, Maryland, is the central US institutional review board. Prior to any study-related activity, all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, current Good Clinical Practice, and International Council on Harmonisation guidelines, applicable regulatory requirements, and sponsor policy. We followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline. Data were collected from September 2022 to June 2024.

The primary objectives of VISIBLE Cohort B were to evaluate guselkumab efficacy and safety vs placebo in participants with moderate to severe scalp psoriasis. The study was designed to enroll at least 50% of participants with Fitzpatrick skin type (FST) IV to VI.

Detailed inclusion criteria have been reported previously.14,15 Briefly, VISIBLE Cohort B included participants (18 years or older) who self-identified their race or ethnicity as a category other than White and who had one of the following: (1) a diagnosis of plaque psoriasis (with or without psoriatic arthritis) for at least 6 months before the first administration of study drug, (2) an expert panel–confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis based on clinical photographs, or (3) biopsy-confirmed psoriasis. Participants reported race or ethnicity using a questionnaire that included predefined categories and the option for open-ended response. Participants in VISIBLE Cohort B were required to have moderate to severe scalp psoriasis, defined as scalp surface area (SSA) involvement of at least 30%, Psoriasis Scalp Severity Index (PSSI) of at least 12, and scalp-specific Investigator’s Global Assessment (ss-IGA) of at least 3, and were candidates for phototherapy or systemic treatment for psoriasis. There were no minimum requirements for overall body surface area (BSA) involvement, Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), or IGA, but participants were required to have at least 1 psoriasis plaque outside the scalp to ensure differentiation from other scalp disorders, including seborrheic dermatitis.4,7

Eligible participants were randomized (3:1) to receive subcutaneous guselkumab, 100 mg, or placebo from week 0 to week 16 using a computer-generated schedule; an interactive web response system was used to generate codes to facilitate allocation concealment and blinding. To balance representation across racial and ethnic backgrounds, permuted block randomization with stratification by FST I to III or IV to VI was used. Participants randomized to guselkumab received the study drug at weeks 0 and 4, then every 8 weeks. Participants randomized to placebo crossed over and received injections of guselkumab, 100 mg, at weeks 16 and 20, then every 8 weeks. The active treatment period continued through week 48, then all participants entered an unblinded long-term extension period through week 112 (Figure 1A).

Assessments

As described for VISIBLE Cohort A,15 participants’ skin tone was determined at screening using colorimetry to assess constitutive pigmentation at a non–sun-exposed anatomic location, with results fitted to a I to VI scale consistent with FST.16,17 Efficacy was assessed using ss-IGA, PSSI, and SSA. The ss-IGA evaluates scalp psoriasis severity based on plaque redness, thickness, and scaliness, from 0 (absence of disease) to 4 (severe disease).18 PSSI measures the extent of scalp psoriasis involvement and severity of lesional erythema, induration, and desquamation, from 0 to 72, with 0 indicating no scalp psoriasis.19,20 SSA measures the percentage of the scalp affected by psoriasis; the surface area of 1 thumbprint is equivalent to 1% of SSA.21

Patient-reported outcome measures included the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), Scalp Itch Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), and Psoriasis Symptoms and Signs Diary (PSSD). DLQI evaluates the effect of skin problems on symptoms and feelings, daily activities, leisure, work or school performance, personal relationships, and treatment (range, 0-30; scores of 0 or 1 indicate no impact of disease on quality of life).22,23 The Scalp Itch NRS is a single-item instrument that participants used to rate their worst level of itching during the past 24 hours from 0 (no scalp itch) to 10 (worst scalp itch imaginable).24 Reductions of 3 to 4 points are generally considered meaningful improvements.24 Participants used the PSSD to rate symptoms (itch, skin tightness, burning, stinging, pain) and signs (dryness, cracking, scaling, shedding or flaking, redness, bleeding) experienced in the past 7 days from 0 (absent) to 10 (worst imaginable); total scores range from 0 to 100.25,26

Key safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), AEs leading to discontinuation, serious AEs (SAEs), infections, and injection site reactions. AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, version 25.1.

Sample Size

A sample size of 100 participants based on a 3:1 randomization ratio has greater than 90% power to detect differences between guselkumab and placebo for the coprimary end points, assuming a 2-sided α of .05. Power calculations were based on end point achievement rates from previous studies of guselkumab vs placebo (ss-IGA score of 0 or 1 [ss-IGA 0/1]: 83.4% vs 14.5% and 80.6% vs 10.9% at week 16) and secukinumab vs placebo (90% or greater improvement in PSSI [PSSI 90]: 52.9% vs 2.0% at week 12).27,28,29

Statistical Analysis

Efficacy was assessed for all participants who were correctly randomized in VISIBLE Cohort B. The coprimary end points were the proportions of participants in the guselkumab vs placebo groups who achieved ss-IGA 0/1 (absence/very mild) and PSSI 90 from baseline at week 16. Multiplicity-controlled major secondary end points (at week 16) tested in a fixed sequence order included percentage changes from baseline in PSSI and SSA, achievement of ss-IGA 0 and PSSI 100, time to PSSI 90, change from baseline in DLQI, 4-point or greater reduction from baseline in Scalp Itch NRS (among those with a baseline score of at least 4), change from baseline in PSSD symptoms score, and a PSSD symptoms score of 0 (among those with a baseline score of at least 1). Two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. The proportion of participants achieving a DLQI of 0 or 1 (DLQI 0/1; among those with a baseline DLQI of greater than 1) was a prespecified secondary end point that was not multiplicity controlled; thus, the P value comparing guselkumab to placebo at week 16 was considered nominal.

The coprimary, binary major secondary, and DLQI 0/1 end points were analyzed using a Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel χ2 test stratified by FST I to III and IV to VI. Participants who discontinued study agent due to lack of efficacy, worsening psoriasis, or use of a prohibited psoriasis treatment prior to a designated study visit were considered not to have achieved the binary end point, and nonresponder imputation was used for missing data. Continuous major secondary end points with more than 1 postbaseline efficacy assessment were compared using a mixed-effect model for repeated measures (MMRM), with explanatory variables of treatment group, visit, baseline efficacy end point score, FST (I to III or IV to VI), an interaction term of visit with treatment group, and an interaction term of visit with baseline efficacy end point score. Zero change from baseline was assigned after participants discontinued study agent due to lack of efficacy, worsening psoriasis, or use of a prohibited psoriasis treatment (intercurrent events rules). After applying intercurrent events rules, missing data were not explicitly imputed and were accounted for in the MMRM under a missing-at-random assumption. Change from baseline to week 16 in Scalp Itch NRS and DLQI were assessed using only 1 postbaseline efficacy assessment and analyzed with an analysis of covariance model with treatment group, baseline efficacy end point score, and FST of I to III or IV to VI as explanatory factors; missing data were not imputed.

Safety was summarized by treatment group for all randomized and treated participants who received at least 1 dose of the study agent. SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc), was used for the analysis.

Results

Participants

A total of 108 participants were randomized to receive guselkumab (n = 81) or placebo (n = 27) and were included in the safety analysis population. The efficacy analysis population included 102 participants (guselkumab, n = 76; placebo, n = 26), who were correctly randomized into VISIBLE Cohort B (6 participants who should have been included in VISIBLE Cohort A were excluded from VISIBLE Cohort B efficacy analyses). In the efficacy population (Table 1), 1 participant (1.0%) self-identified as American Indian or Alaska Native, 39 (38.2%) as Asian, 11 (10.8%) as Black, 39 (38.2%) as Hispanic or Latino, 5 (4.9%) as Middle Eastern, 6 (5.9%) as multiracial, and 1 as other (1.0%). Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between groups. The mean (SD) age was 42.5 (13.6) years, and mean (SD) body weight was 88.1 (23.9) kg; 58 participants (56.9%) were male, and 64 (62.7%) had FST of IV to VI. Mean (SD) psoriasis duration was 11.3 (10.6) years. Baseline disease characteristics were consistent with extensive scalp psoriasis, with a mean (SD) overall BSA of 16.6% (14.4%). Ninety-one participants (89.2%) were previously treated with topical therapies; however, only 12 (11.8%) previously underwent phototherapy, 18 (17.6%) received nonbiologic systemics, and 13 (12.7%) received biologics. Through week 48, 100 of 108 participants (92.6%) continued treatment with study agent, including 78 of 81 (96.3%) randomized to guselkumab and 22 of 27 (81.5%) randomized to placebo (Figure 1B).

Efficacy

At week 16, all primary and major secondary efficacy end points were met. Treatment with guselkumab provided statistically significantly greater improvements compared with placebo for all outcomes. Improvements in guselkumab-treated participants increased over time and were maintained through week 48 (Figure 2A-F). At week 16, in the guselkumab vs placebo group, respectively, ss-IGA 0/1 was achieved by 52 of 76 (68.4%) vs 3 of 26 (11.5%) participants (P < .001), and PSSI 90 was achieved by 50 of 76 (65.8%) vs 1 of 26 (3.8%) (P < .001). By week 48, more than 80% of guselkumab-randomized participants had achieved ss-IGA 0/1 and PSSI 90 (Figure 2A-B). The median (IQR) time to PSSI 90 was 11.6 (6.0-15.3) weeks in the guselkumab group and was not achieved prior to week 16 in the placebo group (P < .001). At week 16, complete scalp clearance was achieved by significantly higher proportions of participants in the guselkumab vs placebo group (ss-IGA 0, 44 of 76 [57.9%] vs 1 of 26 [3.8%]; P < .001; PSSI 100, 45 of 76 [59.2%] vs 1 of 26 [3.8%]; P < .001). By week 48, approximately 70% of guselkumab-randomized participants achieved complete scalp clearance (ss-IGA 0, 51 of 76 [67.1%]; PSSI 100, 52 of 76 [68.4%]) (Figure 2C-D). Mean percentage improvements in PSSI and SSA from baseline to week 16 were significantly greater with guselkumab vs placebo (least-squares mean [LSM] changes: PSSI, 87.6% [95% CI, 81.8%-93.4%] vs 37.8% [95% CI, 27.5%-48.1%]; P < .001; SSA, 86.6% [95% CI, 79.7%-93.4%] vs 33.4% [95% CI, 21.1%-45.7%]; P < .001). Guselkumab efficacy increased and was maintained through week 48, when guselkumab-randomized participants achieved mean (SD) percentage improvements in PSSI and SSA of 94.6% (12.2%) and 94.8% (16.2%), respectively, and 51 (67.1%) achieved ss-IGA 0 (Figure 2E-F).

Post hoc analyses showed high rates of scalp clearance, regardless of whether participants had high BSA (≥10% and IGA ≥3) (n = 43) or low BSA (≥2% to <10% and IGA = 3) (n = 26) involvement at baseline. At week 48, among guselkumab-randomized participants with high and low BSA at baseline, respectively, ss-IGA 0/1 was achieved by 40 (93.0%) and 19 (73.1%), and ss-IGA 0 was achieved by 31 (72.1%) and 15 (57.7%); mean (SD) percentage improvements in PSSI were 96.5% (7.4%) and 90.1% (18.2%), and mean (SD) percentage improvements in SSA were 96.1% (12.7%) and 91.2% (22.4%), respectively.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

At week 16, the LSM change from baseline in DLQI was significantly greater with guselkumab compared with placebo (–9.7 [95% CI, −11.1 to −8.2] vs –2.2 [95% CI, −4.8 to 0.4]; difference, –7.5 [95% CI, −10.4 to −4.6]; P < .001). Among participants with a baseline DLQI of more than 1, DLQI 0/1 was achieved at week 16 by 33 of 75 participants (44.0%) in the guselkumab group and 3 of 26 (11.5%) in the placebo group (P = .003). At week 48, more than 60% of all participants had achieved DLQI 0/1 (Figure 3A), indicating psoriasis had no impact on their quality of life.23

Among participants with a baseline Scalp Itch NRS score of at least 4, 50 of 72 participants (69.4%) in the guselkumab group and 6 of 25 (24.0%) in the placebo group achieved a reduction of at least 4 points at week 16 (P < .001). Mean Scalp Itch NRS scores decreased (improved) to 2.0 or less with continued guselkumab treatment through week 48 (Figure 3B). The mean change from baseline in PSSD symptoms score was significantly greater with guselkumab vs placebo at week 16 (LSM change, –44.8 [95% CI, −50.6 to −39.1] vs –8.3 [95% CI, −18.4 to 1.9], respectively; difference, –36.5 [95% CI, −48.1 to −25.0]; P < .001), with most guselkumab-treated participants achieving clinically meaningful improvements (≥40-point reduction).30 Meaningful improvements in PSSD symptom scores were maintained through week 48 (Figure 3C). Among participants with baseline scores of at least 1, at week 16, 16 of 75 (21.3%) in the guselkumab group achieved a PSSD symptoms score of 0, compared with 1 of 26 (3.8%) in the placebo group (P = .04). By week 48, PSSD symptoms score of 0 was achieved by 26 of 75 participants (34.7%) in the guselkumab-randomized group and by 8 of 23 (34.8%) in the placebo-to-guselkumab group.

Adverse Events

Through week 16 in the guselkumab and placebo groups, at least 1 AE was reported in 29 (35.8%) and 3 (11.1%) participants, respectively, and infection AEs were reported in 12 (14.8%) and 1 (3.7%) participant(s), respectively (Table 2). Through week 48, infections were reported in 27 of 81 (33.3%) guselkumab-randomized participants. Infections reported in more than 1 participant included upper respiratory tract infection, COVID-19, nasopharyngitis, influenza, urinary tract infection, and viral infection; all were mild or moderate. There were no serious infections. No participants had AEs leading to discontinuation. SAEs were reported for 2 participants randomized to guselkumab; angina pectoris was reported in a participant with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, obesity, hepatic steatosis, and gout; and 1 participant had pancreatitis. One guselkumab-treated participant had a mild injection site reaction. Through week 48, there were no participants with inflammatory bowel disease, major adverse cardiovascular events, cancer, serumlike sickness or anaphylaxis, or active tuberculosis.

Pooled safety results from Cohort A and Cohort B of the VISIBLE trial (158 guselkumab-randomized participants) show that the most common AEs through week 48 were upper respiratory tract infection (n = 20 [12.7%]), COVID-19 (n = 9 [5.7%]), and nasopharyngitis (n = 8 [5.1%]). There were no guselkumab-treated participants with inflammatory bowel disease, major adverse cardiovascular events, cancer, serumlike sickness or anaphylaxis, or active tuberculosis.

Discussion

Results from Cohort B of the VISIBLE randomized clinical trial demonstrated that guselkumab is highly effective for the treatment of moderate to severe scalp psoriasis in individuals with skin of color, inclusive of all objectively measured skin tones. At week 16, guselkumab was superior to placebo for the coprimary and all major secondary end points. By week 48, mean improvements in both SSA and PSSI were greater than 94% for guselkumab-randomized participants, and approximately 70% of participants achieved complete scalp clearance. Participants also achieved continuous, clinically meaningful improvements in self-reported psoriasis symptoms and health-related quality of life, underscoring the robust treatment responses in this diverse population of participants across all skin tones.

The high levels of scalp psoriasis clearance observed with guselkumab in VISIBLE Cohort B expanded on results from the pivotal VOYAGE 1 and VOYAGE 2 phase 3 trials.18,31 Consistent with results from VISIBLE Cohort B, in VOYAGE 1 and 2, rates of achieving absent or very mild scalp psoriasis at week 16 were significantly higher with guselkumab vs placebo (ss-IGA 0/1: 81.8% vs 12.4%; P < .001; ss-IGA 0: 65.3% vs 8.4%; P < .001).18 On average, baseline scalp disease in VISIBLE Cohort B was more severe and extensive than in the VOYAGE study populations.18,31 Despite having had psoriasis for an average of more than 10 years at baseline, VISIBLE participants were undertreated compared with VOYAGE participants, with lower rates of prior exposure to nonbiologic systemics (17.6% vs 63.2%) and biologics (12.7% vs 20.7%),32 and likely had developed chronic systemic inflammation that could impair response to treatment.33 Prior undertreatment in VISIBLE participants could be due to health care disparities or reluctance to use biologics for a primary complaint of moderate to severe scalp disease.

Itch has historically been an underrecognized symptom of psoriasis,34,35 but recent patient surveys have identified itch, especially of the scalp, as the most burdensome, severe, and intolerable disease feature,34,35,36 with potentially debilitating effects on psychosocial well-being, sleep quality and quantity, and daily activities.34,35,37 On average, VISIBLE Cohort B participants rated their scalp itch at baseline as severe (7.6 on a 10-point scale), their overall psoriasis symptoms as severe (PSSD symptoms score of 63.4), and the impact of disease on their life as moderate to severe (DLQI of 14.2).23,24,26 After just 3 doses of guselkumab, participants achieved a mean scalp itch score of 2.5 (mild) at week 16, mean PSSD symptoms score decreased (improved) by more than 40 points (clinically meaningful improvement),30 and 44% of participants reported psoriasis had no impact on their life (DLQI 0/1).23 These patient-reported outcomes continued to improve through week 48.

The AEs across both VISIBLE Cohort A15 and Cohort B were consistent with the well-established guselkumab safety profile. Safety findings in VISIBLE were generally comparable to those from large-scale pooled safety analyses of more than 4000 study participants with psoriatic disease treated with guselkumab for up to 5 years.38,39,40 Pooled results across both VISIBLE cohorts show that the most common AEs through week 48 in guselkumab-randomized participants were upper respiratory tract infection, COVID-19, and nasopharyngitis. These findings are similar to results through week 48 from VOYAGE 1 and 2, in which nasopharyngitis, upper respiratory infection, and headache were most common.27,28,41

VISIBLE Cohort B is one of very few clinical trial populations dedicated to individuals with scalp psoriasis, and the only scalp psoriasis study, to our knowledge, to exclusively include participants with skin of color. In addition to previously reported novel design elements of the overall VISIBLE study that promoted participant recruitment and retention,14,15 other unique features of VISIBLE Cohort B include no specified minimum requirement for total BSA involvement, and that participants were allowed to continue stable, concurrent use of over-the-counter medicated and nonmedicated shampoos and topical moisturizers throughout the study.

Strengths and Limitations

Traditional skin clearance assessments, such as PASI and IGA, do not capture dyspigmentation as a measure of psoriasis disease severity. However, patients with higher FST of IV to VI are disproportionately affected by postinflammatory pigment alteration (PIPA),42,43 which contributes to the impact of psoriasis in individuals with skin of color.11 The negative effects of PIPA can be especially burdensome in patients with scalp psoriasis, which often extends to highly visible locations on the forehead, neck, and face.4,7 Therefore, VISIBLE Cohort B included assessments of changes in Skin Discoloration Impact Evaluation Questionnaire (SDIEQ) scores.44 The mean baseline SDIEQ scores indicated that skin discoloration had a moderate effect on quality of life; however, after 3 doses of guselkumab, mean scores were consistent with just a mild effect, with improvements maintained through week 48 (eFigure in Supplement 2). During the ongoing VISIBLE open-label extension (weeks 48 to 112), PIPA will continue to be a key exploratory outcome, assessed based on changes in SDIEQ and Postinflammatory Dyspigmentation Area and Severity Index (PIDASI),45 and objective analyses of cross-polarized clinical photographs. The study’s exploratory end points and robust data will help address knowledge gaps, such as the impact of PIPA, to enhance care for individuals across the skin tone spectrum.

Conclusions

Cohort B of the VISIBLE randomized clinical trial provided important clinical results demonstrating that guselkumab is highly effective for the treatment of moderate to severe scalp psoriasis in individuals with skin of color across the spectrum of objectively measured skin tones. At week 48, mean SSA and PSSI improvements of more than 94% were observed and were associated with clinically meaningful reductions in psoriasis symptoms and quality-of-life improvements.
