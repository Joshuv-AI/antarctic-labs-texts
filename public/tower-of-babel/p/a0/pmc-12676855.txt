Elimination of pathogenic Aeromonas and Flavobacterium strains in the aquatic environment using CaviPlasma
Authors: Miroslava Palíková, Věra Vaibarová, Pavel Sťahel, Jan Čech, Ivona Toulová, Ivana Mikulíková, Hana Novotná, Jiří Pikula, Jan Mendel, Pavel Rudolf, Blahoslav Maršálek, Ivana Papežíková
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe key limiting factor for recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) is maintaining good water quality, which requires keeping the system free of bacterial pathogens. In this study, we investigated the potential of CaviPlasma water treatment technology for the industrial-scale disinfection of RAS water.MethodsWe focused on the remediation of model suspensions containing Aeromonas salmonicida, Aeromonas hydrophila, and Flavobacterium psychrophilum, which are important fish disease causative agents that represent significant health risks for reared fish. The CaviPlasma technology utilized is based on advanced oxidation processes induced by plasma discharge in fast-flowing liquids, synergized with the hydromechanical effects of cavitation.ResultsOur results demonstrated strong biocidal efficiency of CaviPlasma water treatment without chemical or medical additives. F. psychrophilum was completely eliminated immediately after treatment, even at the lowest plasma dose (8.4 kJ/L). Aeromonads displayed slightly higher resistance; instant elimination of aeromonads was achieved with a plasma dose of 25.2 kJ/L, or 33.6 kJ/L. However, during post-treatment incubation, the concentration of live aeromonads gradually decreased to zero, even at the lowest plasma dose.ConclusionsOur pilot study revealed that CaviPlasma technology is a promising sanitization method with potential for use in recirculation aquaculture systems (RAS). It offers approximately m3/h treatment performance and the potential to greatly limit the environmental burden and long-term side effects, such as acquired bacterial resistance due to persistent chemical residues associated with common sanitization technologies that utilize antibiotics or chemical treatment of RAS.Keywords: Recirculation aquaculture systems, Water treatment and sanitization, Aeromonads, Flavobacteria

Introduction

The importance of aquaculture as a food source for human consumption is continuously increasing [1]. In response to a reduction in suitable water sources for facilities with high stocking densities, a range of recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) has been developed, utilizing a series of treatment processes to allow the reuse of water. The key limiting factor of RAS is maintaining good water quality, particularly keeping it free of bacterial pathogens. Bacterial pathogens represent the main health risk for reared fish [2], with bacteria from the genera Aeromonas and Flavobacterium being among the most important and frequent in freshwater aquaculture.

While bacterial infections are usually treated with antibiotics and chemotherapeutics, the current trend is to limit their use due to their persistence in the environment, which can increase bacterial resistance and, consequently, promote a further increase in their use in human medicine [3–5] or may negatively affect the biofilter and disturb water quality [6]. As a result, new methods are being sought to promote aquacultural health without negatively affecting RAS and the environment.

The ozone, hydrogen peroxide or UV treatment, commonly referred to as the advanced oxidation processes (AOP), have been intensively tested [7, 8] for remediation of contaminated water. The plasma technologies for water treatment [9–12] belong also to the group of AOP and represent a promising potential for biological and environmental applications, including microbial inactivation [9–11]. The plasma-treated liquid is commonly referred to as PAW (plasma activated water), see, e.g., the recent focused review on PAW in agriculture [12]. Contrary to the standard chemical treatment, e.g., hydrogen peroxide, or pharmaceuticals, the plasma technologies or PAW offer several advantages. The biologically and chemically active agents can be produced directly in the treated liquid using electric discharge and solely from the treated liquid or ambient air, i.e. no chemicals are involved [12]. The plasma in contact with water [13] produce a mixture of highly reactive species, e.g., •OH radical, singlet oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, ozone and in the presence of nitrogen also nitrogen oxides, which are referred to as reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS). Moreover, plasmas in contact with water often generate also strong UV radiation, solvated electrons and electric fields [13]. Moreover, Graves [14] concluded, that “plasma-generated RONS should minimize resistance development”. However, until recently, there have been significant technological barriers preventing adoption of plasma technologies in large-volume applications, such as the RAS: (a) The low throughput of plasma-liquid systems, recently overcome with the invention of CaviPlasma technology [15–17], (b) acidification of treated water, when nitrogen gas is present in discharge atmosphere [18]. The energy efficiency of plasma treatment is also important parameter of plasma-liquid systems [19].

The CaviPlasma (CP) technology resolved these limitations by the synergistic effect of hydrodynamic cavitation in fast-flowing liquid and electric discharges generated within the water vapour inside the cavitation cloud [20], which prevents acidification (slight increase of pH was observed). The technologically significant throughput of units of m3/h was achieved at laboratory-scale units. Hydrodynamic cavitation resulted in the energy-efficient production of a low-pressure gaseous environment inside the treated liquid, known as the cavitation cloud, which results in lower breakdown voltage facilitating technical implementation. Moreover, the dynamics of the cavitation cloud enhance the energy/treatment efficiency leveraging the efficient gas-liquid transport of reactive species into the treated liquid due to the significantly elevated pressure of collapsing cavities, which also imposes a mechanical burden on the suspended microorganisms, enhancing the overall biocidal effect of CP technology.

The strong biocidal effects on CP treatment have been successfully tested in direct flow-through remediation of water contaminated with cyanobacterial biomass [16] and the biocidal effect of plasma pre-treated water [17]. Moreover, the CP treatment of Microcystis aeruginosa contaminated water has been found not only capable of elimination this cyanobacteria strain with no signs of revitalisation, but also without any increase in microcystin concentration in the treated water [16].

We can provide a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the biocidal effects of CP thanks to our recent studies on RONS dynamics in CP-treated water [20–22]. There are at least four mechanisms involved in direct (short-term) and indirect (long-term) action on the microorganisms. The resuspended bacterial cells suffer from the direct exposure to UV radiation and also from the attack of •OH radicals or singlet oxygen (1O2) that are present in the active discharge zone together with alternating electric fields. There is also a mechanical stress and •OH radicals present at the collapsing end of the cavitation cloud, where vapour-liquid transition occurs. The indirect CP action then follows through post-treatment reactions with generated hydrogen peroxide and ozone. The presence of short-lived agents was confirmed by several analytical methods. The optical emission spectroscopy confirms, besides the atomic hydrogen, the presence of atomic oxygen and molecules of OH, resulting from the dissociation of water vapours in the gaseous active discharge zone. It also reveals that a substantial part of the CP radiation falls within the UV spectrum around 310 nm (•OH emission bands, from approximately 280 nm to 360 nm) [20]. The presence of singlet oxygen, hydroxyl radical, or nitric oxide radicals (through the presence of nitrites, when the treated medium already contained nitrates) was also confirmed using a spin-trapping technique with the EPR spectrometry [21]. The chemiluminescence of luminol probe also confirmed the presence of •OH radicals in the liquid-phase in the active discharge/cavitation zone with the estimated lifetime of 1.4 ms [22]. Moreover, the decay of •OH radicals was detected also in post-discharge, wake stream several centimetres behind the cavitation cloud collapse [22]. The analysis by Machala et al. [23] suggests also the presence of other reactive species, such as HO2 or nitrogen oxides and their acids, when nitrogen is present in the discharge atmosphere. In CP-treated water we have quantified also the long-lived H2O2 and O3 molecules resulting from the plasma-chemical reactions in active discharge zone. They were detected in CP-treated water using colorimetric assays. Our investigations revealed that the plasma-chemically produced hydrogen peroxide can persist for days to months in the treated water in the concentration of a few tens of mg/l [21].

In the present study, we tested the biocidal effect of CP technology to explore its potential in aquaculture as a tool for the treatment of inflow water and preventing disease outbreaks. For this experiment, strains of Aeromonas salmonicida, Aeromonas hydrophila and Flavobacterium psychrophilum were selected as representatives of agents causing economically important bacterial fish diseases. We have also measured the persistence of water-soluble peroxides in the treated water and bacterial suspensions as the end-user feasible marker of the treated-liquid potential to the residual biocidal activity. This case study reveals the feasibility of transitioning RAS maintenance to sustainable systems without chemicals and antibiotics. Further development of AOP technologies using the biocidal effects of plasma treatment of water could provide us with encouraging confidence in the future of aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains tested

The bacterial strains used in this study were carefully selected to represent common waterborne pathogens. These included Aeromonas salmonicida ATCC 33,658, Aeromonas hydrophila CCM 7232, and Flavobacterium psychrophilum ATCC 49,418. A. salmonicida and A. hydrophila were cultivated for 24 h at 17 °C on Columbia blood agar containing 3.9% Columbia Blood Agar Base (Oxoid) and 5% defibrinated sheep blood. F. psychrophilum was cultivated for 72 h at 17 °C on Tryptone Yeast Extract Salts (TYES) agar containing 0.4% tryptone (Tryptone, Oxoid), 0.04% yeast extract (Yeast Extract, Oxoid), 0.02% CaCl2 × 2H2O (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.05% MgSO4 × 7H2O (Merck KGaA), 0.05% glucose (Erba Lachema), 1.3% bacteriological agar (Agar No. 1, Oxoid) and 5.0% bovine foetal serum (BFS) (Biosera, France). Before the analysis, all strains were at least once subcultivated: one single colony was picked with sterile bacteriological loop, transferred onto new sterile agar medium and cultivated at 17 °C for 24 and 72 h, respectively. For the analysis of F. psychrophilum, entire loop of the grown culture was transferred into 250 ml of TYES broth containing 0.4% tryptone (Tryptone, Oxoid), 0.04% yeast extract (Yeast Extract, Oxoid), 0.02% CaCl2 × 2H2O (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.05% MgSO4 × 7H2O (Merck KGaA), 0.05% glucose (Erba Lachema). For the analysis of A. salmonicida and A. hydrophila, entire loop of the grown culture was transferred into 250 mL of BHI (Brain Heart Infusion) broth containing 3.7% Brain Heart Infusion Broth (Oxoid). Broth cultures were cultivated under static conditions for 24 h at 17 °C. Next, 30 mL of the broth culture was inoculated into 2970 mL of tap water in sterile bottles to obtain a bacterial concentration of approximately 106 of colony forming units (CFU) per mL and mixed well. For testing, a 500 mL aliquot of these resuspended cells, with no treatment, served as a positive control, while 2.5 L was used for the CaviPlasma treatment. The treatment and positive control samples of resuspended cells were of equal concentration on the day of treatment.

Plasma treatment procedure of bacteria-contaminated water

This study employed a robust CP reactor configuration with a closed-loop hydraulic circuit and an integrated water reservoir, see Fig. 1 [20], ensuring the precision and reliability of the experimental setup. The high-pressure water pump (Calpeda MXHM 205/A) generated a high-velocity water stream in the reaction chamber using a Venturi nozzle. The hydrodynamic cavitation cloud was then initiated in the Venturi nozzle throat, propagated downstream, and spanned approximately 14 cm. The reaction chamber with a Venturi nozzle was made of a polycarbonate tube with an inner diameter of 10 mm and a minimal nozzle throat diameter of 6 mm. The system of two metallic electrodes was inserted into the reaction chamber. The first cylindrical electrode was made of a titanium rod, 4 mm in diameter, including insulation. The electrode was positioned in the Venturi nozzle. The second electrode was made of a chrome-plated stainless-steel pipe, 10 mm in diameter, and placed 12 cm away from the nozzle electrode. In the described setup, both metallic electrodes were exposed to the cavitation cloud, and the electric discharge burning between the metallic electrodes bridged the cloud. This resulted in a discharge starting as a spark discharge and subsequently reigniting as a glow-like discharge [20]. The electric discharge inside the cavitation cloud was sustained by alternating high voltage applied to the electrodes with the frequency of 31 kHz and peak values in the range of 4–8 kV.

CaviPlasma treatment setup. The hydraulic circuit consists of reaction chamber with Venturi nozzle connected to the water pump and the water tank. The electric circuit consists of a pair of electrodes energized using high voltage (HV) generator with applied power monitored using wattmeter

For presented experiments the batches of 2.5 L of microbially contaminated water were poured into the tank and treated. Plasma treatment was performed directly on the resuspended cells, which were flown through the active plasma-cavitation zone in the closed-loop hydraulic circuit described above. The five steps of increasing plasma doses were tested. The specific input energie (SIE) delivered to the unit volume of the resuspended cells was increased from 8.4 kJ/L to 42 kJ/L in 8.4 kJ/L steps. These SIE correspond to the treatment times from 21 s to 105 s in 21 s steps and 1 kW of input power. The flow rate was set to 1 m3/h, which corresponds to approximately 2 to 12 passages of resuspended cells through the active plasma-cavitation zone. After the treatment at particular plasma dose, the sample of 500 mL was collected into sterile bottle.

Re-cultivation of bacterial strains after plasma treatment

Treated/untreated resuspended bacterial cells of Aeromonas salmonicida/Aeromonas hydrophila were incubated at 17 °C for 7 days. Bacterial cells of Flavobacteirum psychrophilum were incubated at 17 °C for 15 days because of its slow growth. Bacterial concentrations were measured throughout the cultivation process (at 0, 1, 2 and 7- day interval for Aeromonas spp. and 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 8 and 15-day interval for Flavobacterium psychrophilum) using the plate count method. The samples were mixed well to obtain a homogenous suspension and diluted using phosphate-buffered saline in a ten-fold dilution series up to −7. Next, 100 µL of each dilution was spread onto Columbia blood agar (A. salmonicida, A. hydrophila) or TYES agar (F. psychrophilum) and cultivated at 17 °C for 24 and 72 h, respectively. After cultivation, numbers of colonies on countable plates were counted and multiplied by dilution factor to obtain the number of colony-forming units (CFU) per mL of undiluted sample. The colonies were identified by Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionisation Mass Spectrometry (MALDI TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, colonies not identified as tested bacterial species were not included.

Measurement of peroxide concentrations in CP-treated water and resuspended bacterial cells

Water-soluble peroxides were assessed in the samples of resuspended bacterial cells, positive control, and plain tap water (negative control) at 2 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 168 h after CP treatment. Between measurements, all samples were stored in the dark in closed test tubes at 4 °C. Peroxides were measured colorimetrically using the Pierce™ Quantitative Peroxide Assay Kit (Thermofisher Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with standards produced by preparing a serial dilution of 30% hydrogen peroxide (Millipore Sigma, USA) at a concentration range of 0–1000 µmol/L. Based on the results of preliminary tests, all samples were diluted ten-fold in distilled water before measurement to prevent any bleaching effect on the dye from excessive peroxide. Next, 200 µL of the kit’s working solution was added to 20 µL of sample/standard and incubated for 15 min at laboratory temperature. Then, the absorbance was measured at 595 nm on a Cytation 3 multimode reader (BioTek, USA).

Results

The CP treatment immediately eliminated F. psychrophilum in the treated suspension, with no signs of recovery over the observation period (Table 1), even for the lowest of tested plasma doses, i.e., SIE of 8.4 kJ/L. The treatment of Aeromonas spp. showed the presence of viable cells directly after the treatment up to the SIE dose of 25.2 kJ/L. For higher tested plasma doses, complete elimination was achieved for both Aeromonas strains. However, even the lowest plasma dose (SIE of 8.4 kJ/L) was sufficient to significantly reduce the amount of both Aeromonas strains 1 day after the treatment. Moreover, the lowest tested plasma dose lead even to complete elimination of A. salmonicida two days after the treatment (see Table 2) and 7 days after the treatment for A. hydrophila (see Table 3). For all samples, the longer treatment times, and the higher plasma doses resulted in the complete elimination of Aeromonas spp., either immediately or in a shorter period after the treatment (compare Tables 2 and 3). Although aeromonads and F. psychrophilum have been effectively eliminated by CP treatment, post-treatment bacterial cultivation revealed several non-pathogenic bacterial taxa in all tested samples: Delftia acidovorans, Stenotrophomas maltophilia, Sphingomonas sp., Acidovorax temperans, Brevundimonas intermedia, Pseudomonas mosselii, Microbacterium oxydans.

Discussion

The RASs provide optimal conditions for reproduction of bacteria, allowing them to survive in aquatic environments once shed from their hosts [2]. Eliminating pathogens from RAS water could help prevent disease outbreaks, minimising economic losses caused by infectious agents. The most common method used to control RAS pathogen loads is UV irradiation, partly due to its simple installation and relatively low costs. However, some bacterial taxa have been shown to tolerate very high UV doses. For example, Sharrer et al. [24] have shown that F. psychrophilum requires UV doses four times higher than the recommended 30 mJ/cm2 to reduce its presence to log 5. Consequently, several other technologies have been applied to enhance the effect of UV irradiation, including chlorination, membrane filtration (MF), and ozonation [25]. However, these methods have several drawbacks, with chlorination, for example, producing by-products that are potentially toxic, or high turbidity, which can shield pathogens from UV exposure [26]. MF technologies, though widely used in many sectors, are considered capital-intensive [27], and ozone can present a health risk for both fish and humans due to its toxicity, even at low levels [24, 28].

In this study, we evaluated the biocidal effect of CP technology in eliminating pathogenic bacteria often found in RAS. For the plasma treatment, suspensions of bacterial strains, A. salmonicida, A. hydrophila, and F. psychrophilum, were selected, as these bacteria can cause severe economic losses in affected farms. The aeromonads are the causative agents of typical/atypical furunculosis of salmonid fish, while F. psychrophilum causes bacterial coldwater disease/rainbow trout fry syndrome. Aeromonads are among the most common bacteria found in the freshwater environment [29], ubiquitous in the water column and the upper sediment layers [30] and are also a common component of the fish gut microbiome. As a result, they cannot be fully eliminated; however, their environmental load, and thus the likelihood of disease, could be significantly reduced through appropriate intervention.

Our results demonstrated that the three bacterial strains examined showed differing susceptibility to CP treatment. While F. psychrophilum was completely eliminated after the 21 s treatment (SIE 8.4 kJ/L), aeromonads showed greater resistance; A. hydrophila was completely eliminated after the 63 s treatment (SIE 25.2 kJ/L) and A. salmonicida even after the 84 s treatment (SIE 33.5 kJ/L). Shorter treatment (21–42 s treatment for A. hydrophila and 21–63 s for A. salmonicida) did not kill all bacteria in the sample, but during the post-treatment storage of resuspended bacterial cells in the thermostat, the number of viable bacteria gradually decreased to zero (Tables 2 and 3), probably due to the persistence of generated reactive oxygen species in the treated water (see Fig. 2). It has been shown that alongside short-lived ROS with a half-life of microseconds to seconds, long-lived species are produced in plasma-treated water [21]. The long-lived species provide the CP-treated water with residual disinfection capacity. This indirect action of CP has been shown in the study of Čech et al. [17] confirming that CP-treated water added into the culture medium significantly inhibited the growth of cultured cyanobacteria and green algae. In the present study, this effect was also found significant. Notably, while the immediate pathogen elimination efficiency increased with higher SIE (Tables 1, 2 and 3), similar effects were also achieved for low SIE treatments through post-treatment reactions. For example, the Aeromonas salmonicida treatment requires a SIE of more than 25.2 kJ/L for the complete immediate elimination of pathogens. However, similar levels of bactericidal efficiency were also achieved for the lowest tested SIE of 8.4 kJ/L when the samples underwent post-treatment reactions with reactive species generated in the treated resuspended cells. This effect is significant, as it could result in a substantially shorter, more energy-efficient treatment with the potential to reduce the costs of integrating CaviPlasma technology into RAS. However, this delayed bactericidal effect could be rendered inefficient in real field conditions depending on the particular RAS implementation. Generally, the treated inflow water is expected to be mixed with the water in the RAS tank. This could result in the excessive dilution of CP-treated water and the loss of long-term biocidal potential of CP-treated water. Our results indicate a safe margin for eradication of these pathogens within CP-treatment, even if the treated water is subsequently diluted. The 84 s treatment with SIE 33.5 kJ/L, or more should be considered effective and able to immediately eliminate all bacterial species tested.

Post-treatment bacterial cultivation revealed a range of non-pathogenic bacterial taxa in all tested samples. Many questions arise from the presence of these microorganisms, including their potential source, the elucidation of their reproduction following treatment, and the relevance of these taxa for fish health. One possible source could be the untreated tap water used for the experiment. While several authors have reported the presence of the above-mentioned taxa in drinking water [31–34], we did not find them in the positive untreated controls. This could be due to the overwhelming loads of Aeromonas spp./Flavobacterium sp., which may have resulted in the suppression of other species. However, given that the microorganisms were not detected in the untreated tap water, contamination of the treated samples during their passage through the CP device appears to be a more logical explanation. Given that the isolated bacteria are capable of biofilm formation [33–36], we assume that a biofilm was present in the outflow section of the device and that microorganisms were released from it to the treated samples of resuspended cells.

A similar conclusion was reached by Johani et al. [37] while working on endoscope working channels, the authors concluded that, as biofilm microbial cells were embedded in an extracellular polymer matrix, this acted as a barrier and protected them, to a great extent, from the effects of antibiotics, detergents, and disinfectants. Furthermore, Attramadal et al. [38] showed that while UV inactivates most bacteria in RAS, it cannot always inactivate all, and such selective inactivation can lead to the proliferation of opportunistic pathogens that could destabilise the microbial community.

The presence of biofilm-forming bacteria in the treated water implies that biofilm-forming fish pathogens could also escape elimination. This poses a potential risk in practical applications of CaviPlasma tretment. It has been shown that all three species of tested bacteria (A. salmonicida, A. hydrophila and F. psychrophilum) are able to form biofilms which may act as long-term reservoirs from which bacteria are released to water [39–41]. Since the dose of 33 kJ/L and higher immediately killed all bacteria of the three species tested, this dose is likely to kill all bacteria released from the biofilm formed between the water inflow and CaviPlasma device. The risk would only be posed by the biofilm formed in the outflow section of the device, which represents relatively small area.

It should be noted that this study only describes the effects of CP on selected important fish bacterial pathogens commonly found in RAS and that other issues may still need addressing before the method can be adopted. These include factors such as the effect of CP-treated water on fish, the impact of treated water on the RAS biofilter, and issues related to the generation of peroxides in the water.

Recently, plasma activated water has been successfully used to reduce bacterial loads in RAS for Nile tilapia larval culture. Plasma activated water had an effect comparable to use of conventional antibiotic treatment, without negatively affecting fish growth and feed conversion ratio [42]. We suggest that future studies examine the effect of CP-treated water on different fish species and age categories, as well as on fish eggs and sperm. In the case of favourable results, CP treatment of water used for incubating fish eggs could help reduce mortality, and associated economic losses, caused by rainbow trout fry syndrome in salmonid farms.

CP treatment does not selectively target pathogens, which may have several implications. For example, adverse effect on biofilter microbial community could be expected due to the presence of long-lived reactive species in plasma-treated water. This problem could be easily overcome by installing the inflow of plasma-treated water remote from the biofilter. When the treated water does not enter the RAS system directly through the biofilter, its impact on the biofilter would be significantly reduced due to the dilution and a negligible influence on the biofilter could be achieved depending on the ratio of inflow water to the total RAS capacity. In addition, rapid degradation of reactive species could be expected in RAS water due to its high organic load and related microbial activity. The work of Odehnalova et al. shows high stability of hydrogen peroxide in deionized and tap water. In deionized water, H2O2 content decreased to 64% of the initial concentration after 60 days. In tap water, zero concentration was reached after 21 days [21]. In high-intensity RAS, however, hydrogen peroxide was shown to be degraded much faster, with a half-life of approximately 30 min [43], mostly due to microbial activity [44]. A similar assumption concerning effect of water dilution and rapid peroxide degradation may also be valid regarding the effect of plasma-treated water on fish health condition.

Conclusions

The direct CaviPlasma treatment of inoculated water resulted in outstanding efficacy, eliminating all three tested bacterial species. While some factors still require further research, the use of CaviPlasma treatment appears to be a highly promising method for reducing the causative agents of important fish bacterial diseases in RAS and the environmental burden of antibiotics and other chemotherapeutics in freshwaters.
