Ammonium and nitrate fluxes drive metabolic functional shifts in tap water biofilms
Authors: Binessi Edouard Ifon, Wei Zhang, Zhongbo Zhou, Okugbe Ebiotubo Ohore, Yeyuan Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen availability critically influences biofilm development in drinking water distribution systems (DWDSs), yet the distinct impacts of ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻) on biofilm and pathogen risks remain unclear. Using bench-scale annular reactors simulating DWDSs, we investigated how ammonium depletion/supplementation (AD/AS) and nitrate depletion/supplementation (ND/NS) regulate biofilm profiles over 28 days. Water chemistry analyses and functional annotation (KEGG, FAPROTAX) revealed that AS and NS increased total nitrogen retention (AS: 2.046–2.261 mg/L; NS: 10.625–11.800 mg/L) and biofilm prokaryotic cell abundance. AS enriched biosynthesis and virulence pathways (e.g., amino acid transport, energy production), while AD activated stress-response mechanisms (e.g., DNA repair, carbohydrate metabolism). NS favored Gram-negative bacteria, stress-tolerant taxa, and pathogenic genera (e.g., Acinetobacter, Mycobacterium), whereas ND upregulated organic nitrogen scavenging. Nitrogen supplementation preferentially enriched genera including WHO-listed critical-risk pathogens, which correlated strongly with inorganic nitrogen levels (Mantel r > 0.5, P < 0.05). In contrast, genera including high/medium-risk pathogens exhibited increased relative abundance under depletion via organic nutrient reliance. These findings demonstrate that NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ differentially regulate biofilm pathogenicity: NH₄⁺ drives growth and virulence, while NO₃⁻ enhances structural resilience and redox flexibility. Introduction

Microbial biofilms in drinking water distribution systems (DWDSs) represent dynamic ecosystems that critically impact water quality and public health (Liu et al. 2016). These surface-associated communities harbor diverse microbial populations, including opportunistic pathogens such as Legionella pneumophila and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which pose significant risks to immunocompromised individuals and can lead to outbreaks of waterborne diseases (Margot et al. 2024; Ward et al. 2018). Understanding the environmental factors that shape biofilm development and pathogenic potential is therefore critical for mitigating these risks.

Among the drivers of biofilm dynamics, availability of inorganic nutrients, particularly nitrogen compounds, plays a critical role (Geetha et al. 2017; Mangalea et al. 2017; Xiao and Waheed 2019). Nitrogen enters drinking water sources through agricultural runoff and urban wastewater effluent discharge, which create fluctuating nutrient regimes that can profoundly alter biofilm development (Geetha et al. 2017). Ammonium is also released into DWDSs during chloramine decay when chloramination is practiced as a disinfection method. A major group of biofilm-forming microbes in DWDSs is the autotrophic nitrifying bacteria, which utilize nitrogen-based compounds—including ammonia, nitrate, and nitrite—as energy sources, with ammonia serving as the preferential compound for biomass production (Liu et al. 2016). This makes nitrogen not just a nutrient but a critical ecological filter that structures biofilm communities.

To assess biofilm microbial diversity and function in DWDSs, tools like FAPROTAX (Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), Bugbase, and COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups) are routinely employed to map taxonomic data to functional traits, including metabolism, pathogenicity, and biofilm formation (Kanehisa 2011; Tatusov et al. 2003; Wang et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2022). This integrated analytical approach is critical for deciphering the link between microbial community structure, functional potential, and associated health risks. Drawing on insights from bioinformatics databases such as KEGG and COG, research has elucidated the specific impacts of ammonia and nitrate on DWDS biofilms.

Within the nitrogen pool, ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻) exert distinct effects on biofilm function and composition through divergent metabolic pathways (Wang et al. 2024a, b; Zhang et al. 2020). Ammonia (NH₃-N) in DWDSs upregulates central metabolic pathways (e.g., amino acid and nucleotide synthesis), enhancing extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) secretion, a key factor in biofilm adhesion and resistance to chlorine disinfection (Sun et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024a, b). This, in turn, promotes the proliferation of conditional pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and the accumulation of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). Nitrate, by contrast, acts both as a nitrogen source and a terminal electron acceptor in anaerobic respiration, shaping EPS-organic matter (EPS-OM) composition and altering microbial ecological network interactions (Wang et al. 2024a, b). KEGG annotations further reveal that nitrate supplementation enriches nitrogen metabolism and denitrification pathways while increasing the biofilm’s pathogenic potential (Li et al. 2025). COG classifications have linked nitrate-sensing gene clusters (e.g., narX/narL and nigh) to the modulation of biofilm formation in specific pathogens like Burkholderia pseudomallei, a mechanism that directly alters the relative abundance of pathogenic taxa (Mangalea et al. 2017). Collectively, both nitrogen forms act as key ecological filters that alter microbial community function and structure, ultimately impacting pathogen persistence and water quality safety. Despite these observations, a critical knowledge gap remains: how do ammonium/nitrate depletion vs. supplementation drive divergent patterns of community assembly and pathogen enrichment in DWDS biofilms? This knowledge gap hinders the development of targeted strategies for managing biofilm-associated risks in DWDSs.

The functional consequences of nitrogen fluctuations extend across multiple biological dimensions, from cellular processes and metabolic pathways to virulence factor expression. For instance, nitrogen repletion can stimulate biosynthetic pathways and microbial growth, while nitrogen limitation may trigger stress responses and alternative nutrient acquisition strategies (Mitchell and Silhavy 2019). Ammonia in DWDSs has also been shown to increase bacterial virulence factors levels and promote the growth of some conditional pathogenic bacteria by regulating biofilm microbial metabolic pathways (Sun et al. 2025). However, the specific functional adaptations of biofilms to NH₄⁺ vs. NO₃⁻ remain insufficiently characterized, as existing research often conflates nitrogen’s general role with the unique effects of individual nitrogen forms.

To address these gaps, this study employed bench-scale biofilm annular reactors (BARs) to investigate the influence of ammonium and nitrate fluxes on DWDS biofilm microbiome structure and the resultant consequences on biofilm functional profiles over a 28-day development period. We integrated high-throughput sequencing with water chemistry analyses to test three central hypotheses: first, that ammonium supplementation would preferentially enrich pathogenic taxa through enhanced biosynthetic capacity; second, that nitrate availability would promote stress-tolerant communities via redox-mediated selection; and third, that nitrogen form would dictate distinct patterns of pathogen enrichment. The findings provide new mechanistic insights into nitrogen-mediated biofilm regulation, with important implications for managing microbial risks in drinking water systems.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup and sample collection

Biofilms were developed on pipe material coupons mounted on rotors within bench-scale BARs (effective volume 2.5 L) designed to simulate drinking water distribution system conditions (Fig. S1). A BAR consisted of a coupon-holding rotor (140 mm outer diameter × 140 mm height) accommodating 16 coupons of four common pipe materials: galvanized steel (GS), stainless steel (SS), polyethylene (PE), and unplasticized polyvinyl chloride (UPVC), each measuring 1.5 cm × 14 cm. The BAR maintained a flow rate of 7.35 mL/min, replicating a 5.7-h hydraulic residence time in DWDSs (Rożej et al. 2015), and generated shear forces equivalent to a DN300 pipe system (0.61 m/s flow velocity) using a speed-controlled motor.

Two nitrogen regimes were tested: (1) Ammonium treatments: Depletion (AD; ≈0.004 mg/L NH₄⁺-N) vs. supplementation (AS; 0.5 mg/L NH₄⁺-N); (2) Nitrate treatments: Depletion (ND; ≈0.011 mg/L NO₃⁻-N) vs. supplementation (NS; 10 mg/L NO₃⁻-N). To reduce cross-interference between biofilms, PE and UPVC were incubated in Reactor 1 (R1), while SS and GS were placed in Reactor 2 (R2). Although this separation was necessary, it may have introduced an unavoidable reactor effect. To minimize such inter-reactor variability, the AS treatment was conducted using a single BAR for all materials.

Laboratory tap water was sourced and nitrogen levels were reduced using ion exchange resins: T-24H (Kehees Beijing Technology Co., Ltd.) for NH4+ removal and D890 (Zhengguang Industrial Co., Ltd.) for NO3⁻ removal, respectively, packed in chromatographic columns (25 mm × 500 mm). The D890 resin was used directly, while the T-24H resin was activated with hydrochloric acid beforehand. The pretreated water was used for the AD and ND experiments directly, while NH4+ and NO3⁻ were re-dosed to the required concentrations from NH4Cl and KNO3 stock solution for the AS and NS experiments, respectively. Sodium hypochlorite was added to maintain a residual chlorine concentration of approximately 0.05 mg/L in all systems.

To assess water quality and biofilm metagenomics, a total of 140 samples were collected in duplicate from the water and coupons. Water quality parameters including NH4+-N, NO3−-N, and NO2−-N were analyzed using microplate and spectrophotometric methods in line with Chinese National Standard GB/T 5750.5–2006 (Miranda et al. 2001). Additional assessments included total organic carbon (TOC), total nitrogen (TN), and dissolved organic nitrogen (DON); dissolved oxygen (DO) and pH were measured with a portable analyzer, while turbidity and residual chlorine were evaluated with specific meters. Biofilm samples were collected weekly by sonicating coupons in phosphate buffer solution (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄).

Biofilm EPS analysis

Extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) were extracted using a thermal extraction technique (Waheed et al. 2017). Briefly, the biofilm was harvested via centrifugation at 4000 rpm (4 °C, 30 min). The resulting pellet was resuspended in 0.05% NaCl and incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. Following centrifugation at 4000 rpm (4 °C, 15 min), the supernatant was collected as the EPS fraction. The protein (PN) content in EPS was determined using the Lowry method with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (Salgar-Chaparro et al. 2020), while the polysaccharide (PS) content was measured via the phenol-sulfuric acid method (Rożej et al. 2015).

Flow cytometric analysis of biofilm cells

Detached biofilm suspensions were homogenized and passed through a 40 µm cell strainer to remove large particulates and residual aggregates prior to analysis. Samples were stained with SYBR Green I (total cells) and propidium iodide (PI) (membrane-compromised cells) using the BacLight™ viability kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol, then incubated in the dark to ensure consistent fluorescence development. Flow cytometric acquisition was conducted using a volumetrically calibrated instrument with fluorescent counting beads as internal standards (Nescerecka et al. 2018). Data analysis employed a sequential gating strategy: forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) thresholds excluded electronic noise and non-cellular debris, followed by fluorescence gates to differentiate stained microbial populations from background signals. Dual-staining patterns (SYBR Green I and PI) classified intact (live) and cell membrane-compromised (dead) cells. Absolute cell concentrations derived from flow cytometry were normalized to biofilm area or biomass, yielding microbial abundance as cells cm⁻2 or cells g⁻1 for direct comparison with biofilm structural metrics and complementary microscopy data.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

For microbial community analysis, 300 mL water samples and 40 mL biofilm suspensions (collected from coupons) were filtered through 0.2 µm polycarbonate Nucleopore® membranes (47 mm diameter, Whatman, Kent, UK). The filters from duplicate samples were stored in individual 5 mL tubes at −20 °C until processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the GenElute™ Bacterial Genomic DNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with lysozyme pretreatment for enhanced cell lysis. DNA quality was verified and concentrations were determined spectrophotometrically using an Eppendorf (Germany) Biophotometer (Rożej et al. 2015).

The V4–V5 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA genes were amplified using universal primers 515 F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 926R (5′-CCGYCAATTYMTTTRAGTTT-3′) targeting both bacteria and archaea. Amplicons were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (BMK Cloud, Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Raw paired-end reads were processed using QIIME 2 (version 2023.2). Adapter and primer sequences were removed, and reads were quality filtered to exclude sequences containing ambiguous bases, average Phred quality scores < 20, or abnormal lengths. Based on per-base quality profiles, forward and reverse reads were trimmed and truncated prior to downstream analysis.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred using the DADA2 plugin within QIIME 2, which performs quality filtering, dereplication, error-model learning, denoising, paired-end read merging, and chimera removal using the consensus methods (Adyari et al. 2025; Chun et al. 2017; Quast et al. 2013). Only high-confidence, non-chimeric ASVs were retained. Representative ASV sequences were taxonomically assigned using a naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA v138 reference database, trimmed to the amplified V4–V5 region. Taxonomic assignments were accepted at a confidence threshold of ≥ 70%. Non-target sequences (chloroplasts, mitochondria, and eukaryotes) were removed prior to downstream analyses. ASV abundance tables were generated from quality-filtered read counts and normalized to relative abundance (%) for comparative analyses.

Functional potential was predicted using multiple complementary approaches, building on the analytical tools introduced in the Introduction (FAPROTAX, KEGG, COG, BugBase). PICRUSt2 was employed to infer gene family and pathway profiles from ASV data (Douglas et al. 2020). Briefly, ASV representative sequences were aligned to a reference multiple-sequence alignment using HMMER, followed by phylogenetic placement into a reference tree using EPA-NG and GAPPA. Gene family copy numbers (e.g., KEGG Orthologs) were inferred using castor based on phylogenetic relationships (Kanehisa 2011). Predicted gene family abundances per sample were calculated by integrating ASV copy number predictions with ASV abundance tables. Pathway abundances were subsequently inferred using MinPath, producing predicted gene family profiles and metabolic pathway abundances for each sample.

FAPROTAX was used to assign putative ecological functions based on curated taxonomic-function relationships at genus or species levels, particularly targeting nutrient cycling processes (Yang et al. 2022). KEGG and COG annotations were used to classify predicted gene families into metabolic pathways and orthologous functional categories, respectively (Kanehisa 2011; Tatusov et al. 2003). BugBase was applied to predict organism-level phenotypes, including Gram staining characteristics, biofilm formation, pathogenic potential, mobile genetic element content, oxygen utilization, and oxidative stress tolerance (Wang et al. 2020). BugBase analysis included normalization based on predicted 16S rRNA gene copy numbers, estimation of trait relative abundances across coverage thresholds (0–1, increment = 0.01), and selection of optimal thresholds based on maximal variance across samples.

Statistical analyses and displays were conducted in R (version 4.4.3) and GraphPad Prism. Differences in taxonomic and functional profiles among treatments were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) following verification of normality and homogeneity of variance. Student’s t-test was used to examine other significant differences. The packages "Circlize" and "ComplexHeatmap" in R were used to visualize the distribution of different biofilm functional activities and processes between experimental conditions. Mantel tests based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices were used to evaluate correlations between microbial functional structures and environmental variables, with significance assessed using 9,999 permutations.

All sequence data were deposited in the NCBI Short Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession numbers PRJNA1347285 and PRJNA1347785.

Results

Shifts in water quality parameters and biofilm EPS dynamics under nitrogen availability

Ammonium supplementation (AS) consistently maintained elevated NO₃⁻-N levels (1.62–1.70 mg/L) compared to depletion conditions (AD: 1.37–1.46 mg/L; P < 0.05) (Table S1). This was accompanied by a significantly higher DON (0.06 to 0.11 mg/L) under AD versus AS (0.01–0.08 mg/L) (P < 0.05). In addition, DO was slightly higher in AD (5.56–5.88 mg/L) compared to AS (5.10–5.30 mg/L). Moreover, the biofilm EPS exhibited an increasing trend in AD, from day 7 to 28 (PN: 2.33–4.07 mg/L; PS: 3.80–6.20 mg/L). This was not observed under AS; however, significantly higher PN-EPS was produced in AS than AD (P < 0.05).

As shown in Table S2, nitrate supplementation (NS) elevated the DON levels (0.42–0.73 mg/L) significantly compared to nitrate depletion (ND: 0.03–0.15 mg/L, P < 0.05). TOC displayed marked depletion in ND (5.47 to 1.38 mg/L), while remaining relatively stable under NS (2.34–3.00 mg/L). DO was relatively stable in ND (5.67–6.04 mg/L), while NS showed a temporal spike on day 14 (8.58 mg/L), likely due to variation in tap water quality. However, EPS production patterns under nitrate treatments showed temporal divergence. ND conditions promoted progressive PN accumulation (0.68–5.84 mg/L from day 7–21), mirroring AD trends, while NS preferentially stimulated polysaccharide (PS) production while suppressing PN secretion.

Influence of nitrogen species on biofilm prokaryotic cell abundance and composition

Of the 8,751 ASVs generated after DADA2 processing, all were classified as known bacteria (Fig. S2). Genus-level assignment was achieved for 6,218 ASVs (71.1%). At the species level, 1,680 ASVs (19.2%) were confidently assigned to known (cultured/named) species, while an additional 680 ASVs (7.8%) were assigned to uncultured species (e.g., “uncultured Bacteroides sp.” or similar placeholders in SILVA). This yielded a total of 2,360 ASVs (27.0%) classified to species level (known + uncultured). Consequently, the remaining 6,381 ASVs (73.0%) remained unclassified or ambiguous at the species level (e.g., assigned only to genus with “sp.”, “uncultured bacterium”, candidate divisions, or no species designation). Although these rates align with typical expectations for short-read 16S rRNA (V4-V5 region) data processed with DADA2 and the SILVA v138 database in environmental biofilm samples, species-level resolution is often limited due to sequence conservation and database completeness. Therefore, genus-level patterns were used as the primary basis for metabolic functional and pathogen-related inferences in this study.

As shown in Fig. 1a, biofilm cell abundance exhibited significant nitrogen-dependent variations. AS resulted in 1.8-fold higher prokaryotic cell counts compared to AD (P < 0.05). Gram-negative bacteria demonstrated consistent dominance across all nitrogen treatment regimes (P < 0.05, Fig. 1b), with their relative abundance peaking at 83.27% under NS conditions compared to 61.40% in ND. In particular, Proteobacteria (mainly Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter) accounted for 68.3% of the Gram-negative enrichment under NS, likely due to their enhanced denitrification capabilities (Zhang et al. 2020).

In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria (predominantly Firmicutes and Actinobacteria) showed limited responsiveness to nitrogen fluctuations, maintaining relatively stable populations between 18.6–37.9% across treatments (Figs. 1b and S3a). Temporal analysis (Fig. S3a) revealed progressive Gram-positive enrichment in all treatments except NS, where their sustained suppression (19.2 ± 1.4%) suggests nitrate availability creates particularly unfavorable conditions for their growth.

Functional activity shift in biofilm communities under ammonium treatments

Within metabolic processes, amino acid transport and metabolism (M1) showed the most substantial increase under AS (64.7% vs 35.26% in AD), followed by energy production and conversion (M5: 63.0% vs 28.9%) (Fig. 2c). Additionally, AD exhibited marked upregulation of carbohydrate transport and metabolism (M3: 62.3% vs 36.7% in AS) (Fig. 2c). These findings align with the known preference of microorganisms for ammonium as a nitrogen source due to its direct assimilability into amino acids and nucleotides (Yuan et al. 2023). Genetic information processing (GIP) displayed an inverse pattern, with AD biofilms showing substantially higher activity (63.87%) compared to AS (36.12%) (Fig. 2a). Detailed examination revealed that transcription (GIP4: 57.40%) and translation-related functions (GIP5: 58.40%) were particularly enhanced under AD (Fig. 2c), suggesting increased protein turnover and RNA-mediated regulation under nitrogen-limited conditions. Organismal systems analysis further distinguished the conditions, with AS enriched for endocrine system (OS5; 73.15%) and environmental adaptation (OS6; 88.65%), contrasting with AD's emphasis on nervous system (OS9; 91.60%) and immune function (OS8; 86.02%) (Fig. 2c). Notably, disease-associated pathways diverged significantly: AS favored cardiovascular diseases (HD2; 76.73%); infectious disease (HD6; 55.33%), and neurodegenerative disease (HD7; 61.70%) signatures, while AD demonstrated strong associations with immune diseases (HD5; 53.49%) and drug resistance (HD3; 51.31%) pathways (Fig. 2c).

Functional activity shift in biofilm communities under nitrate treatments

Nitrate availability induced distinct functional profiles that differed from ammonium-mediated responses (Fig. 2b and d). NS conditions resulted in significant enrichment of lipid transport and metabolism (M8: 61.56%) compared to ND (38.33%), along with enhanced energy production (M5: 61.04% vs 38.94%) (Fig. 2d). Additionally, ND upregulated amino acid transport (M1; 65.3%) and carbohydrate metabolism (M3; 63.2%) (Fig. 2d).

These patterns suggest nitrate’s dual role as both nitrogen source and electron acceptor in respiratory pathways. The cellular processes under nitrate regimes showed unique characteristics (Fig. 2d). NS biofilms prioritized cell growth and death (CPS2: 74.79%), motility (CPS3: 65.67%) and intracellular trafficking (CPS6: 62.36%), while ND conditions enhanced membrane transport (CPS7: 56.72%) and defense mechanisms (CPS5: 70.32%) and cell cycle control, division, chromosome partitioning (CPS1: 67.82%). Compared to NS, ND dominated all GIP pathways such as ribosomal biogenesis, DNA replication/repair, and transcription (Fig. 2b). These differences may reflect alternative survival strategies under varying nitrate availability. Organismal systems under NS emphasized endocrine, circulatory, and aging pathways (OS5, OS2, OS1), contrasting with ND’s activation of digestive and immune systems (OS4, OS8) (Fig. 2d). Remarkably, human disease-related pathways exhibited particularly notable differentiation, with NS conditions leading to a substantially greater proportion (HD; 79.20%) compared to ND (Fig. 2b). NS conditions were associated with higher prevalence of infectious disease pathways (HD6: 67.87% vs 32.12% in ND) and neurodegenerative disease signatures (HD7: 72.72% vs 27.27%). This pattern raises important questions about the potential health implications of a high nitrate level in water systems.

Shifts in pathogenic and stress-tolerant biofilm communities between treatments

Nitrogen availability distinctly shaped biofilm pathogenicity and stress resilience (Figs. 1c, S3b). However, these patterns reflect relative abundance shifts rather than absolute changes in pathogen load. AS induced the most significant shifts in pathogenic taxa, increasing their relative abundance by 2.3-fold compared to AD (P < 0.05).

Nitrate treatments showed more complex effects on pathogen compositional dynamics (Figs. 1c and S3b–d). While NS increased stress-tolerant taxa by 1.7-fold (P < 0.05), its potential impact on pathogens was relatively variable. Although the broad functional category Human_pathogens_all showed no significant change, Human_pathogens_pneumonia and Exoparasites were predicted to increase by > 2.5-fold at day 21 while Intracellular parasites first decreased but subsequently increased by 3.3-fold by day 28 (Fig. S3c).

The Mantel test analyses revealed nitrogen regime-specific associations between bacterial functional groups and environmental parameters, highlighting distinct ecological drivers under varying nutrient conditions (Fig. 3). Under AD, Human_pathogens_pneumonia exhibited strong correlations with organic nutrients (TOC, DON) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3a), suggesting organic source scavenging as a survival strategy, consistent with microbial adaptations to nutrient limitation (Loi et al. 2024). Concurrently, knallgas bacteria (hydrogen-oxidizing chemolithotrophs) showed significant ties to EPS proteins, pH, and dissolved oxygen (DO), implying their role in biofilm matrix stabilization and redox homeostasis under nutrient-limited conditions, aligning with observations of biofilm structural resilience under nutrient-limited conditions (Schwermer et al. 2008). This aligns with studies showing that nitrogen availability enhances exopolysaccharide synthesis, facilitating pathogen colonization and nutrient retention in biofilms (Imchen et al. 2022). Nitrate deprivation further emphasized organic nutrient dependencies, as both Human_pathogens_pneumonia and potential pathogens correlated with organic nutrient sources (Fig. 3c–d), reflecting a compensatory shift toward extracellular matrix production to sequester residual nutrients, mirroring nitrogen scavenging strategies in E. coli under nitrogen stress (Siri et al. 2024). Notably, Human_pathogens_all and potential pathogens exhibited significant associations with EPS polysaccharides/proteins and inorganic nutrients (NO₃⁻-N, NH₄⁺-N) (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Nitrogen form and availability shape community structure and metabolic function

Our results establish a fundamental link between nitrogen regime and the foundational characteristics of biofilms. Consistent with the established principle that ammonium is the preferred nitrogen source for microbial assimilation (Yuan et al. 2023), we found that its deprivation (AD), alongside nitrate deprivation (ND), severely limited microbial proliferation. This underscores nitrogen’s role as a key growth-limiting nutrient. Beyond abundance, the chemical form of nitrogen specifically influenced the biofilm’s matrix composition. ND, AD, and AS promoted progressive protein accumulation, while NS preferentially stimulated polysaccharide production and suppressed protein secretion. This temporal divergence in EPS production demonstrates nitrogen-dependent regulation of biofilm matrix composition. This matrix remodeling directly influences biofilm cohesion, porosity, and stability, ultimately governing its functional capacity.

The community structure that emerged under all nitrogen treatments was dominated by Gram-negative bacteria, particularly Proteobacteria. This dominance can be directly attributed to their structural and metabolic superiority in nitrogen processing (Mitchell and Silhavy 2019). Their specialized porins for nutrient uptake, possession of efficient nitrate reductases (Nap and Nar), and innate metabolic flexibility provide a competitive advantage. This is exemplified by the significant enrichment (68.3%) of Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter under NS, a regime that leverages their denitrification capabilities (Zhang et al. 2020). In contrast, the constrained response of Gram-positive bacteria (predominantly Firmicutes and Actinobacteria) highlights their disadvantage imposed by their thick peptidoglycan layer and energy-intensive nitrogen acquisition pathways, e.g., glutamine synthetase-glutamate synthase cycle (Mitchell and Silhavy 2019; Wang et al. 2020).

This nitrogen-driven selection of community members directly translated into pronounced potential functional shifts, aligning with prior findings that ammonium fluctuations drive microbial functional shifts (Loi et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2015). AS promoted biosynthetic processes, while AD activated a scavenging and stress adaptation mode. AS enhanced nitrogen metabolism, stimulating amino acid biosynthesis, protein assembly, and ATP-generating respiration. Conversely, AD triggered carbon substrate redirection, lipid storage, carbohydrate transport and metabolism pathway activation to sustain energy conservation. AS preferentially stimulated nitrogen assimilation pathways, as reflected by the pronounced upregulation of amino acid transport and metabolism (M1) and energy production and conversion (M5), indicating enhanced anabolic activity and increased metabolic energy demand to support nitrogen incorporation. In contrast, AD shifted the metabolic profile toward carbohydrate transport and metabolism (M3), suggesting a compensatory reliance on carbon substrates for energy generation under nitrogen-limited conditions. Collectively, these patterns demonstrate metabolic reprogramming in response to ammonium availability, where AS promoted biosynthetic nitrogen metabolism, whereas AD induced carbon-centered energy acquisition strategies to maintain cellular homeostasis.

Similarly, nitrate availability tuned energy metabolism: NS enhanced respiratory pathways and energy metabolism (lipid β-oxidation, electron transport), consistent with previous findings (Mangalea et al. 2017); while ND triggered nitrogen-sparing (amino acid recycling, glyoxylate shunt) and stress response (DNA repair, ribosomal biogenesis) metabolism, supporting the stress-induced mutagenesis hypothesis (Loi et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2025). Results suggest that NS promoted a shift toward oxidative and biosynthetic metabolism. Enhanced lipid metabolism implies increased membrane remodeling, signaling lipid synthesis, or β-oxidation for ATP generation. Concurrent enrichment of energy production pathways indicates elevated respiratory or oxidative phosphorylation activity, reflecting improved redox balance and higher metabolic efficiency. In microbial systems, this often corresponds to increased growth, respiration, and potentially nitrate-dependent electron transport processes (Mangalea et al. 2017).

The Mantel test analyses further solidify these distinct ecological drivers, revealing how nitrogen regime shapes specific associations between bacterial functions and environmental parameters. For instance, AD potentially induced organic sources scavenging as a survival strategy, consistent with microbial adaptations to nutrient limitation (Loi et al. 2024). Concurrently, the link between knallgas bacteria (hydrogen-oxidizing chemolithotrophs) and EPS proteins under nutrient-limited conditions suggests a role in maintaining redox homeostasis and biofilm matrix integrity (Schwermer et al. 2008), illustrating how community structure and function converge to adapt to nitrogen constraints. Our study relies on 16S rRNA gene sequencing and predictive functional profiling, which provide insights into potential functional trends but do not directly measure specific genes or confirm pathogen viability. Ultimately, validating and generalizing these findings requires further metagenomic or culture-based studies to assess the actual observations.

Nitrogen as a selective force in pathogenic ecology and risk

The most significant implication of our findings lies in the clear role of nitrogen as a potential selective force shaping the biofilm’s pathogenic profile. We observed a strong, multi-faceted response of pathogenic taxa to ammonium fluxes. AS acted as a potential pathogen promoter, directly stimulating virulence gene expression via nitrogen regulatory (Ntr) systems, enhancing quorum sensing (Mangalea et al. 2017), and boosting the production of a protective protein-rich matrix. This synergistic mechanism explains the specific enrichment of opportunistic pathogens like Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter under AS conditions.

Nitrate treatments showed more complex effects on pathogen compositional dynamics, reflecting its role in respiratory metabolism rather than direct assimilation. NS supported pathogen niches (Human_pathogens_pneumonia) in mature biofilms favorable for denitrification, and was linked to disease pathways (infectious/neurodegenerative), likely through ROS-mediated damage and nitrosamine accumulation (Lin et al. 2023). Conversely, ND conditions were associated with a higher predicted abundance of virulence and immune evasion factors, suggesting alternative invasion strategies under nutrient stress.

Critically, these dynamics led to a clear enrichment of genera including bacterial pathogens according to WHO priority classification. The dominance of the relative abundance of genera including critical-risk pathogens under both ammonium and nitrate supplementation is an important finding, indicating that nitrogen repletion, regardless of form, selectively favors the genera including the most dangerous pathogenic taxa due to their superior metabolic versatility. For instance, Acinetobacter thrives in nutrient-rich environments by utilizing diverse nitrogen sources for biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) expression (Eales et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020). This creates a competitive environment where stress-adapted genera, like Staphylococcus and Escherichia (including high/medium-risk pathogens), are outpaced as their energy investment in stress-response pathways (e.g., DNA repair, efflux pumps) becomes a disadvantage (Siri et al. 2024). The broader ecological associations between environmental parameters and genera including risk-associated pathogens under nitrogen supplementation, revealed by Mantel tests, further highlight nitrogen as a potential ecological driver for these priority pathogens.

Overall, our findings imply that nitrogen availability is a fundamental ecological lever controlling genera associated with bacterial pathogen risk. This insight points toward actionable strategies for drinking-water system management, such as prioritizing ammonium control to monitor and potentially suppress critical pathogens and considering dual-phase nitrogen management to disrupt stable, pathogen-friendly communities. These approaches could be particularly effective when integrated with existing disinfection protocols, potentially enhancing overall biofilm control while reducing disinfectant demand. While 16S rRNA data robustly inferred functional shifts in this study, and has been successfully applied in prominent microbial functional studies (Lu et al. 2024; Rushimisha et al. 2024), future work employing shotgun metagenomics will be crucial to directly profile the functional genes and pathways, thereby validating the metabolic predictions inferred here. Future work should also employ an approach integrating total cell counts or biofilm biomass measurements to estimate absolute abundance of actual pathogen loads and the WHO stratified health-risk species.

Conclusion

This study establishes that the form and availability of nitrogen are fundamental ecological drivers that dictate the structure, function, and pathogenic risk of biofilms in drinking water distribution systems. NH₄⁺ supplementation promotes virulence gene expression and biofilm formation and competitively enriches critical-risk pathogens (e.g., Acinetobacter, Mycobacterium), as reflected by their increased relative abundance. Conversely, NO₃⁻ supplementation enhances biofilm structural resilience via polysaccharide synthesis and redox flexibility, offering a potential tool for managing biofilm redox states, albeit with a risk of supporting stress-tolerant and denitrifying pathogens. Furthermore, nitrogen depletion shifts the competitive advantage to taxa employing stress-response strategies, including high/medium-risk pathogens (e.g., Staphylococcus, Escherichia), highlighting that nutrient scarcity alone does not eliminate risk but merely reshapes it.

These insights provide a new, mechanistic foundation for managing microbial risks in DWDSs. We advocate moving beyond viewing nitrogen merely as a contaminant to be minimized, toward its strategic use as a lever for microbial control. This involves: i) Ammonium-centric interventions to suppress critical pathogens by limiting their metabolic reliance on NH₄⁺-fueled growth and virulence; ii) Nitrate-driven optimization of biofilm redox functions to enhance structural stability without amplifying pathogen risks; and iii) Dynamic nutrient management tailored to disrupt pathogen-biofilm synergies, thus preventing the stable establishment of any single high-risk consortium.

Overall, tailoring nitrogen regulation represents a promising, targeted approach for advancing water safety. By integrating these principles with existing disinfection practices, utilities can potentially achieve superior biofilm control while reducing operational demands. Future research employing shotgun metagenomics across a gradient of nitrogen concentrations is essential to validate these functional relationships and define precise, actionable thresholds for pathogen mitigation in diverse drinking water ecosystems.
