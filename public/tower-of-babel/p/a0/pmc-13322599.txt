Trend and Linearity Determination of Left Ventricular Mechanics in Cardiovascular Risk Factor Population with Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Akhil Mehrotra, Mohammad Shaban, Shubham Kacker, Mohammad Shadab, Naveen Chandra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:4-Dimensional XStrain speckle tracking echocardiography (4DXStrainSTE) is an advanced cardiac imaging technique that synthesizes speckle tracking echocardiography (STE) data obtained from the apical three-chamber (3CH), four-chamber (4CH), and two-chamber (2CH) views, thereby enabling a comprehensive evaluation of multiple left ventricular strain parameters (MLVSP) within a three-dimensional framework.Methods:This study aimed to evaluate the impact of cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) on MLVSP using 4DXStrainSTE. Nine strain parameters were analyzed: global circumferential strain (GCS), global circumferential strain rate (GCSR), global radial strain (GRS), and global radial strain rate (GRSR) at the mitral valve (mv) and papillary muscle (pap) levels, along with global longitudinal strain (GLS). A total of 500 Indian adults (aged 18–60 years; both sexes) were enrolled. Participants were categorized into four CVRF Risk Categories (1, 2, 3, and 4) according to the cumulative number of risk factors. The inclusion criterion was a 4-dimensional left ventricular ejection fraction (4D-LVEF) ≥50%.Results:The impact of CVRFs on LV strain parameters displayed a significant decline in GLS, GCS at pap level, GCSR at mv and pap level, substantiated by trend analysis. Mild-to-moderate linear association across MLVSP was identified. Multivariate regression illustrated significant associations of GLS and GCSR at mv level with GLS across the risk categories.Conclusions:This study illustrates a comprehensive evaluation of the consequences of CVRFs on MLVSP in individuals with preserved ejection fraction.

INTRODUCTION

Currently, GLS assessment via two-dimensional speckle tracking echocardiography (2D STE) remains one of the most extensively investigated techniques worldwide.[1] The American Society of Echocardiography recommends a normal left ventricle (LV) GLS value of > −20% ± 2%.[2] Three-dimensional STE (3D STE) enables single-apical acquisition of the LV, thereby reducing acquisition time and allowing a comprehensive evaluation of strain parameters.[3] However, its clinical application is constrained by suboptimal temporal and spatial resolution, which can impair tracking accuracy. In addition, multi-beat acquisitions may introduce stitching artifacts between subvolumes, further compromising speckle tracking analysis.[45]

4-Dimensional XStrain speckle tracking echocardiography (4DXStrainSTE) is an innovative and advanced imaging modality that integrates Tomtec GMBH’s 3D/4D imaging platform with Beutel™ computational technology.[6] It synthesizes STE data from apical three-chamber (3CH), four-chamber (4CH), and two-chamber (2CH) views to achieve a comprehensive 3D reconstruction of LV strain parameters. By capitalizing on the superior temporal and spatial resolution of 2D imaging, this technique effectively mitigates the inherent limitations of full-volume 3D STE.[7] While single-plane 3D echocardiography still suffers from lower resolution compared to 2D imaging,[8] 4DXStrainSTE overcomes these constraints and offers an accessible and user-friendly solution for LV strain analysis.

Previous studies have documented the impact of CVRFs on GLS,[910] though primarily using 2D STE. A thorough literature search revealed no prior studies that comprehensively evaluated the influence of CVRFs on multiple LV strain parameters (MLVSP) through rigorous statistical techniques, namely, trend analysis, Pearson’s correlation, and multivariate regression for linearity assessment. This investigation was therefore designed to address this important methodological gap.

METHODS

Study center

This original research was conducted over 28 months (September 2021 to December 2023) at Prakash Heart Station, Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh, India.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Prakash Heart Station (Approval No. IEC/PDC/PHSD/2021:01,02). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study’s aims. The research procedures were carried out in alignment with the International Conference on Harmonization–Good Clinical Practice standards and adhered to the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

This was a single-center, observational, prospective study involving 500 Indian adults aged 18–60 years, of both sexes. Participants were classified into four CVRFs categories: category 1, 2, 3, and 4 [Figure 1]. Inclusion criteria stipulated that all participants maintain a 4D LVEF ≥50%.

Category 1

This group included 152 healthy individuals (aged 18–60 years; both sexes) without any CVRFs, exhibiting normal resting electrocardiograms (ECG), 4DXStrainSTE findings with 4D LVEF ≥50%, and negative treadmill stress tests (TST).

Category 2, 3, and 4

This cohort comprised 348 adults (aged 18–60 years; both sexes) with one or more CVRFs, all in sinus rhythm and exhibiting a 4D LVEF ≥ 50%. Comprehensive clinical and medical assessments were conducted by a cardiac physician, with meticulous documentation of the CVRF profile for each participant.

Echocardiography

All participants underwent a comprehensive cardiac evaluation using conventional two-dimensional (2D) transthoracic echocardiography and 4DXStrainSTE. Examinations were conducted on the MY LAB X7 4D XStrain system (ESAOTE, Italy) equipped with a 1–5 MHz transducer. All acquisitions were performed by the experienced study authors, with measurements obtained in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.[21]

Conventional echocardiography

2D, M mode, pulse wave doppler, continuous wave doppler, and tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) modalities were employed to evaluate standard cardiac parameters, including left ventricular (LV) volumes, systolic and diastolic function, LV mass, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF%), cardiac output (CO), cardiac index, early diastolic (E) and late diastolic (A) velocities, E/A ratio, early myocardial relaxation velocity (E′), and the E/E′ ratio.

Four-dimensional X-Strain echocardiography

Image acquisition

Apical 4CH, 3CH, 2CH, and short-axis (SX) views at the mitral valve (mv) and papillary muscle (pap) levels were obtained [Figure 2]. Each cine loop comprised at least three cardiac cycles, recorded at 40–75 frames per second and stored digitally for offline analysis.

Illustration of 4DXStrain speckle tracking echocardiography (4DXStrainSTE). Representative image demonstrating the 4DXStrainSTE technique used to assess myocardial deformation. Strain parameters, including global longitudinal strain, global circumferential strain, and global radial strain, are derived from multiplanar apical and short-axis views. Corresponding strain rate values – GCSR and GRSR – are also computed using the same imaging planes. GCS = Global circumferential strain, GCSR = Global circumferential strain rate, GLS = Global longitudinal strain, GRS = Global radial strain, GRSR = Global radial strain rate

High-quality image acquisition was prioritized. Optimal ECG tracing was ensured by adjusting electrode placement on the chest and selecting the lead from the physio menu of the echocardiography system that yielded the smoothest waveform with well-defined R/Q waves and minimal noise. Traces exhibiting prominent P or T waves were avoided to prevent gating interference during 4DXStrainSTE acquisition. Participants were instructed to hold their breath at end-expiration to minimize translational and respiratory artifacts. The LV was positioned to fill the majority of the imaging sector, avoiding foreshortening. Particular care was taken to exclude papillary muscle bulges when tracing the endocardium.

High-quality SX views at the mv, pap, and LV apex levels were acquired to facilitate accurate computation of circumferential strain, circumferential strain rate, radial strain, and radial strain rate.[22] Clear delineation of cardiac borders was confirmed throughout the cardiac cycle.

Offline analysis of acquired images

Offline analysis was conducted using XStrain 4D™ software[23] (Esaote S.p.A. - R&D; Via di Caciolle, Florence, Italy / Via E. Melen, Genoa, Italy).[23] The offline speckle tracking measurements were implemented blinded to group assignment and were accomplished by one independent expert echocardiographer who was not involved in the image acquisition and had no knowledge of examination dates and other echocardiographic or clinical data. The LV endocardial and epicardial borders were automatically delineated from 13 equidistant tracking points, supported by the Aided Heart Segmentation tool.[23] The Beutel computational platform, integrated into XStrain™ 4D, synthesizes 2D STE data to reconstruct a 3D/4D LV model, enabling automated derivation of LV strain parameters.[23] A standardized 17-segment Bull’s-eye plot was generated for visualization.

RESULTS

Demographic data

In Category 1 [Table 1], 152 healthy individuals were enrolled (103 males and 49 females). The mean age was 29.5 ± 10.24 years for males and 34.16 ± 9.95 years for females, with females being significantly older than males (P < 0.01). Males exhibited significantly greater weight, height, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure (BP), and heart rate (HR) compared to females (P < 0.01).

In the pooled data, there were 348 participants (204 males, 144 females). The mean age of males was 49.85 ± 12.73 years, and that of females was 54.92 ± 12.58 years. Males had significantly higher weight, height, BMI, and diastolic BP (DBP) than females (P < 0.01), whereas systolic BP (SBP) and HR were significantly higher in females (P < 0.01).

Conventional echocardiography data

Routine 2D echocardiography was employed to obtain conventional measurements.

In male subjects of Category 1 [Table 1], left atrial size, interventricular septal diameter in diastole (IVS d), left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVID d), left ventricular posterior wall diameter in diastole (LVPW d), left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), LV mass in diastole (LV Mass d), CO, mitral E/A ratio, lateral wall TDI E′ velocity, and 2D fractional shortening (FS) were significantly greater than in females (P < 0.01). Conversely, E-point septal separation (EPSS), lateral wall TDI E/E′ ratio, and 2D left ventricular ejection fraction (2D-LVEF) were greater in females (P < 0.01, P > 0.05, and P < 0.05, respectively).

Pooled data values for EPSS, IVS d, LVID d, LVPW d, LVEDV, LV Mass d, CO, TDI E′, and TDI E/E′ ratio were significantly higher in males (P < 0.01, P < 0.05). In contrast, left atrial size, mitral E/A ratio, 2D-LVEF, and 2D-FS were significantly higher in females (P < 0.01).

4-Dimensional XStrain speckle tracking data

Left ventricle strain data

A comparative analysis of LV strain parameters between category 1 and the pooled category [Table 2] revealed a highly significant reduction in GLS and GCS at the pap level in the pooled category (P < 0.001) and a significant decline in GCSR at the mv and pap level (P < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

2D STE remains a widely employed, noninvasive modality for evaluating LV systolic function, as well as strain, volumetric indices, and rotational mechanics – including twist, torsion, and rotation.[2223] However, inherent limitations persist across 2D, 3D, and 4D STE platforms.[22] The most recent innovation, XStrain™ 4D, was developed to improve the precision of LV contractile function assessment,[22] although its adoption in routine clinical practice remains limited.

Four-dimensional versus two-dimensional speckle tracking echocardiography

Prior studies have demonstrated that 4D STE offers superior accuracy in quantifying LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and strain parameters compared to conventional 2D echocardiography.[24] This advantage largely stems from the geometric assumptions inherent to 2D-derived LVEF calculations.[24] Indeed, several reports have shown that LVEF and LV volumes measured by 2D imaging tend to be overestimated relative to RT3DE or 4D modalities.[2526] In the present study, we employed 4DXStrainSTE to perform LVEF and volumetric assessments within a true 3D/4D analytical framework.

Earlier work by Takahashi et al.[9] and the STAAB cohort[27] examined the influence of CVRFs on LV strain using 2D echocardiographic platforms. However, 2D-based methods may compromise strain accuracy due to spatial and geometric limitations.[728]

Reproducibility studies

Previous investigations[829] have demonstrated minimal variability in reproducibility assessments. Measurements of global LV three-dimensional multiple strain parameters, such as GLS, GCS, and GRS, showed low intra- and interobserver variation.[8] Similarly, Leischik et al.[29] conducted an almost identical study using two-dimensional strain echocardiography. Their results indicated the highest intraobserver reproducibility for GLS in the 4-chamber view (mean deviation: 9% ± 13.6%, P = 0.624, P = 0.003), followed by GCS (13.3% ± 8.3%, Ρ =0.406, P = 0.068) and the lowest reproducibility for GRS (26.3% ± 30.1%, P = 0.391, P = 0.08). For interobserver measurements, GLS from the 3-chamber view exhibited the smallest deviation (11.9% ± 9.5%, Ρ =0.513, P = 0.017), followed by CS (15.2% ± 12.0%, P = 0.263, P = 0.249) and the least consistent readings in RS (35.9% ± 46.3%, P = 0.382, P = 0.088).

Correspondingly, in the current study, we have additionally illustrated in 20 healthy adult subjects (males 15, females 5) very low intra- and interobserver variability [Table 6].

Clinical significance of cardiovascular risk factors on left ventricle strain

GLS has emerged as a sensitive and prognostically valuable marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, often surpassing GCS and GRS in predictive capacity. This superiority likely reflects the vulnerability of subendocardial, longitudinally oriented myocardial fibers to early pathological insults during the initial stages of myocardial dysfunction.[3031] Conversely, circumferential strain – reflecting mid-wall circumferential fibers – typically remains preserved until later disease stages.[32] Radial strain, representing myocardial wall thickening in the radial dimension, is comparatively less sensitive to early changes.[33] Strain rate parameters provide complementary insights into the velocity of myocardial deformation, thereby refining the characterization of contraction and relaxation kinetics.

CVRFs are highly prevalent among older adults and, if unaddressed, predispose individuals to overt cardiovascular disease (CVD).[34] The cumulative presence of multiple CVRFs has been implicated in the development of subclinical myocardial dysfunction.[35] Importantly, impaired GLS has been reported even in individuals with preserved LVEF, underscoring its value as an early marker of myocardial impairment.[36] Moreover, multiple studies have linked an increasing burden of CVRFs with stepwise elevations in cardiovascular mortality risk.[3738]

Although prior investigations have explored the relationship between CVRFs and strain, primarily GLS using 2D STE,[927] our study detected significantly lower GLS and GCS at the papillary level in pooled CVRF categories compared with the low-risk group (P < 0.01). While reductions in GCS at the mitral level, GRS at both mitral and papillary levels, and GRSR were also observed in the CVRF group, these differences did not achieve statistical significance (P > 0.05).

Furthermore, trend analysis demonstrated a significant, progressive decline in GLS, GCS at the pap level, GCSR at mv and pap level across ascending CVRF categories, consistent with worsening myocardial deformation. Pearson’s bivariate correlation revealed predominantly mild linear associations among strain indices, while multivariate regression identified significant associations between GLS and GCSR at the mitral level with GLS.

GLS remains the most widely adopted strain parameter in both clinical and research settings.[39] However, there is a notable paucity of studies systematically evaluating the impact of CVRFs on GCS and GRS. Our study has rigorously examined the impact of CVRFs on GCS, GRS, GCSR, and GRSR using an innovative 4DXStrain STE platform.

CONCLUSIONS

This study investigated the consequences of CVRFs on MLVSF in subjects with 4D LVEF of ≥ 50%. There was a highly significant reduction of GLS and GCS at pap level (P < 0.01) and a significant decline of GRSR at mv and pap level (P < 0.05).

A robust statistical approach – including trend analysis, Pearson’s correlation, and multivariate regression – demonstrated that ascending CVRF categories were associated with a significant stepwise decline in GLS and GCS at the papillary level (P < 0.01), GCSR at the mv and pap level (P < 0.05), indicating progressive myocardial dysfunction. Other parameters showed nonsignificant or inconsistent patterns (P > 0.05). Pearson’s correlation revealed predominantly mild linear associations among strain parameters, while multivariate regression identified significant associations of GLS and GCSR at the mv level with GLS.

This study is a comprehensive single-center investigation to evaluate MLVSP in the context of CVRFs. The results highlight distinct differences in LV strain profiles between CVRF-affected individuals and healthy controls, supporting the clinical utility of XStrain™ 4D STE as a sensitive tool for detailed ventricular mechanics assessment, particularly for GLS and related strain parameters.

Limitations of the present research

This was a single-center study conducted on a modest number of healthy Indian adults and individuals with CVRFs. Therefore, the values generated cannot be extrapolated to other ethnic groups such as Africans, Europeans, or Americans.The specific ultrasound system used plays a significant role in strain evaluation, and values may differ when using other echocardiographic platforms due to software algorithm variability.[40]A major limitation lies in the lack of standardization of GLS values across vendors, which can affect strain measurements.[4142]The study did not incorporate validation against cardiac magnetic resonance tagging (CMR-TAG) or feature tracking (CMR-FT),[43] which remain the gold standards for strain analysis.

Suggestion for future research

In the future, a larger multicentric study should be conceptualized to validate the magnitude of adverse impact on the outcomes of cardiovascular risk factors on LV strain. Since trivial aberrations in LV ejection fraction, LV strain, and rotation mechanics can be detected at a premature stage of various diseases, and these variations can be utilized for having knowledge about escalating prognostic value for predicting morbidity and mortality4D XStrain STE has not yet been universally embraced, and its utility is still limited to research settings. Therefore, future research studies utilizing XStrain 4D may augment its use in various clinical scenarios and showcase its advantages as compared to traditional techniques.
