Challenges in Diagnosis and Management of Crohn's Disease in Bangladesh With Long Term Follow‐Up
Authors: Imtiaz Abdullah, Imran Hasan, Mantaka Rahman, Shoma Hayat, Nowshin Papri, Nusrat Fatema, Ahmedul Kabir, Zhahirul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCrohn's disease (CD) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) with significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We reported a case of a 20‐year‐old Bangladeshi male with an 8‐month history of severe upper abdominal pain, bowel discomfort, and intermittent low‐grade fever. Initial investigations showed mild anemia and elevated inflammatory markers. Magnetic resonance enterography revealed distal small bowel thickening, suggestive of IBD. Colonoscopy and biopsy confirmed chronic ileitis without granulomas and an ulcer at the terminal ileum. Despite the absence of granulomas, clinical symptoms, elevated fecal calprotectin, and high C‐reactive protein (CRP) led to a diagnosis of CD. The disease later progressed to a perianal abscess, a fistulizing complication. The patient received three doses of infliximab but subsequently discontinued therapy. He remained untreated for 3‐years and subsequently developed an intestinal perforation requiring right hemicolectomy with ileostomy. Following ileostomy reversal, adalimumab was advised. This case highlighted the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of CD in Bangladesh.

Introduction

Crohn's disease (CD) is a chronic autoimmune form of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that primarily affects the gastrointestinal tract, and commonly presents with clinical symptoms like abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, and fatigue. According to a recent global burden study, an estimated 3.83 million people were living with IBD in 2021, representing an 85% increase from 1990 (2.17 million cases). However, the age‐standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) showed a slight decline, from 48.02 per 100,000 in 1990 to 44.88 per 100,000 in 2021 [1]. CD was once regarded as uncommon in South Asia, most specifically in Bangladesh. However, the region has seen a marked increase in the incidence and prevalence of CD, likely due to factors like growing urbanization, dietary shifts, and lifestyle changes [2]. While specific data on the incidence of CD in Bangladesh is very limited, studies from neighboring countries suggest that the incidence is lower than Western countries possibly due to underdiagnosis or underreporting [3]. Moreover, misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis is a frequent challenge in CD diagnosis due to non‐specific clinical symptoms that overlap with conditions like irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), intestinal tuberculosis, and ulcerative colitis. This makes delay in treatment management as well.

Among CD phenotypes, fistulizing disease represents a severe manifestation of transmural inflammation; however, its pathogenesis remains a therapeutic dilemma, reflecting the interplay of epithelial barrier disruption, microbial translocation, and dysregulated immune repair that complicates further diagnosis. Moreover, inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐13) drive epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, myofibroblast activation, and matrix metalloproteinase upregulation, leading to fistula formation in CD [4]. Evidence reported that, despite targeted biologic therapy, recurrence is common, necessitating vigilant screening (including for malignancy) and combined Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)‐guided surgical management [5].

Globally standard therapies for CD, including corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and biologics, are often costly and out of reach for many. It remains a critical barrier, particularly as advanced treatments like biologics are generally unaffordable for the average patient without insurance support. In Bangladesh, where private health insurance is uncommon, the financial burden on families can be overwhelming, leading to treatment interruptions and suboptimal outcomes. This situation is compounded by limited availability of specialized healthcare providers and IBD‐focused treatment facilities, further underscoring the need for systemic improvements to make CD management more accessible and affordable in this region. In this report, we have presented a case of CD to emphasize the diagnostics and therapeutic challenges in Bangladesh where such cases are rarely documented.

Case Presentation

A young male (20 years) from Bangladesh was presented with persistent upper abdominal pain for 7–8 months. He was a nonsmoker, nondiabetic, and normotensive, with no significant past medical history and no familial history of CD and IBD. His primary complaints were sharp, non‐radiating upper abdominal pain, confined above the umbilicus, lasting for 30–40 s, occurring frequently throughout the day. The patient also reported bowel discomfort characterized by loose, watery stools, with occasional low‐grade fever and no bulk formation for around 8 months. Laboratory investigations revealed mild microcytic hypochromic anemia (Hemoglobin: 10.9 g/dL) and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP: 36.9 mg/L; Table 1). The patient was suspected to have either intestinal tuberculosis or Crohn's disease, and further investigations were recommended for confirmation.

Differential Diagnosis and Challenges for CD Detection

Intestinal TB was ruled out with negative results from a Gene Expert test, QuantiFERON‐TB Gold test, and chest X‐ray. Imaging studies including Computed Tomography (CT) and abdominal ultrasonography showed no abnormalities. Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) test was performed to rule out any hepatobiliary conditions; however, Magnetic Resonance (MR) enterography revealed distal small bowel thickening. Moreover, colonoscopy showed second‐degree hemorrhoids and significant inflammation at the ileocecal valve, with an ulcer at the terminal ileum suggesting a primary diagnosis of IBD (Figure 1).

Investigations

Biopsy of the terminal ileum was performed that indicated partly ulcerated mucosa with granulation tissue and chronic ileitis, raising the suspicion of CD (Table 2). Patient's initial fecal calprotectin level was 95 μg/g, indicating mild inflammation. Treatment with Ofloxacin and Ornidazole for 14 days reduced the CRP to 2.59 mg/L; however, fecal calprotectin increased to 1437 μg/g, suggesting ongoing inflammation. Budesonide was introduced as steroid therapy, but CRP rose to 3.93 mg/dL, and fecal calprotectin further increased to 1459.39 μg/g (Table 1). Subsequently, the patient developed a perianal abscess, confirmed by transrectal ultrasound, and was treated with Cefixime for 1 week to address the abscess. No surgical drainage was required. After evaluating the entire clinical picture, including persistent inflammation, the development of a perianal abscess, and the biopsy findings, the diagnosis of CD, specifically the fistulizing variety, was made by the gastroenterologist.

Treatment

After considering all the complexities and severity of the condition, it was decided to initiate biologic therapy with infliximab, administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg at zero, two, and 6 weeks, followed by maintenance therapy every 8 weeks. Once the patient was stabilized on infliximab, the plan was to introduce Azathioprine, at a dose of roughly 2 mg/kg to maintain remission. However, before starting Azathioprine, it was essential to measure the patient's thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) level, as individuals with low or absent TPMT activity are at a higher risk for severe side effects, such as bone marrow suppression.

Unfortunately, TPMT testing was not available in Bangladesh. However, arrangements were made to send the patient's sample to a laboratory outside the country. The TPMT result came back normal. With this confirmation, Azathioprine was started as planned, in conjunction with the ongoing infliximab therapy. After 8 weeks of initiation of this treatment regimen, the patient showed significant improvement and was relatively in good condition.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Although the symptoms were under control after taking three doses of infliximab, therapy could not be continued beyond three doses due to circumstantial constraints. Patient remained untreated for most of the next 3 years, receiving only intermittent symptomatic management. After 3 years in 2025, the patient was again admitted to the hospital with severe acute abdominal pain and was diagnosed with intestinal perforation at the terminal ileum, a probable complication of CD. Emergency surgical intervention was performed, including a right hemicolectomy and creation of an ileostomy to maintain bowel function.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimens (ileum, cecum, appendix, and ascending colon) revealed multiple ulcers, transmural inflammation, perforation, and serositis of the ileum, consistent with CD and its complication. subsequently, reversal of the ileostomy was also successfully done after 3 months of perforation repair (Table 2). After all surgical intervention, consulting with multiple gastroenterologists, the patient was advised to restart biologic therapy along with azathioprine. Considering the discontinuation of prior infliximab therapy and the potential for anti‐drug antibody formation, adalimumab was recommended as the preferred biologic agent for moving forward (Figure 2).

Discussion

This case report intends to emphasize the challenges in diagnosing and managing complicated CD with limited resources in a country like Bangladesh. CD is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune condition that can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract with characteristics of skip lesions. The terminal ileum and colon are usually the most prevalent sites of lesions. The diagnosis of CD is basically challenging due to non‐specific symptoms, which often overlap with conditions like IBS, intestinal tuberculosis, and ulcerative colitis. For example, a study from developing countries highlighted this diagnostic dilemma between CD and intestinal tuberculosis due to nonspecific signs and symptoms and similar clinical presentations and overlapping radiological findings [6]. In this case report, the diagnostic journey for this particular patient was also challenging, which began with unremarkable imaging studies with clinical correlates, including chest X‐ray, CT scan, and MRCP. In this diagnostic trajectory, the turning point came when MR enterorrhaphy revealed distal small bowel thickening, raising suspicion for CD. Colonoscopy confirmed significant inflammation at the ileocecal valve and an ulcer at the terminal ileum, with biopsies indicating chronic ileitis. However, the absence of granulomas created diagnostic complexity, especially in Bangladesh, where intestinal tuberculosis is more common [7]. Raised fecal calprotectin levels further supported the Crohn's diagnosis despite the lack of definitive histopathological features. A few days later, after developing the perianal abscess, a fistulizing complication of CD, the diagnosis became clearer towards CD. However, the management of the fistulizing variant of CD in this patient was complex, particularly after the lack of response to antibiotics. Infliximab, a TNF inhibitor, was chosen as the mainstay of treatment, aligning with current best practices for moderate to severe CD, especially in the presence of complications like fistulas [8].

Although current international guidelines advocate early biologic therapy (infliximab and adalimumab) and treat‐to‐target monitoring (endoscopy and fecal calprotectin) to achieve mucosal healing and reduce further complications to improve the patient's quality of life [9]. In contrast, many low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMIC) settings, including Bangladesh, have limited access to biologics therapy, endoscopy, and therapeutic drug monitoring. In those settings, due to limited access to biologics and objective monitoring, CD management is confined to steroid‐ and thiopurine‐based, symptom‐driven care, which is linked to delayed escalation and higher complication and surgery rates. To address the CD burden in LMICs, care should prioritize clinical diagnosis with basic inflammatory markers and affordable steroid‐thiopurine therapy. Early detection and management of complications, including timely surgical referral and nutritional support, are also essential.

The management of fistulizing CD with biologics is well‐established, with multiple studies reporting efficacy in inducing and maintaining remission (35%–45%) and reducing the need for surgical intervention. Notably, adalimumab is associated with a more favorable safety profile, showing approximately 38% lower risk of adverse events compared to infliximab [10, 11, 12]. Introducing Azathioprine as maintenance therapy after stabilizing the patient on infliximab aims to sustain long‐term remission and reduce relapse risk. This immunosuppressant is commonly combined with biologics to enhance efficacy and prevent antibody formation against the biologic agent [13].

Globally, emerging therapies such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) have shown promise in refractory CD and fistulizing complications by promoting tissue oxygenation, reducing inflammation, and facilitating mucosal healing. In a retrospective cohort of 61 patients with CD in Croatia treated with HBOT, disease activity indices decreased markedly; 94.1% of conservatively managed patients achieved remission, and surgically treated patients experienced faster postoperative recovery [14]. The oxygen therapy has also improved bowel peristalsis (28%–30%) and reduced postoperative complications (~30%), supporting its role as an adjunctive therapy to optimize outcomes [11, 14]. Furthermore, a systematic review of 18 studies reported an overall response rate of 81.9%, with complete fistula healing in 47.6% and partial healing in 34.3% of patients, while adverse events were predominantly minor. These findings revealed HBOT as a promising adjunct within the expanding therapeutic landscape of CD. Yet, such advanced modalities remain largely inaccessible in resource constrained settings including Bangladesh and many South Asian LMICs, highlighting persistent global inequities in access to specialized care and underscoring the gap between evolving evidence and real‐world treatment availability [11].

CD is a relapsing–remitting IBD that can lead to significant complications if not adequately treated. It can affect any part of GIT, but most commonly within the distal ileum of the small intestine. Nearly half of patients required surgery within 10 years from confirmation of diagnosis [15]. Consistent with previously published observations that therapeutic discontinuation, particularly of immunosuppressants or biologics, is associated with increased risk of persistent fistulizing disease, relapse, and surgical intervention in CD [16, 17]. In this particular case study, the patient also developed a terminal ileal perforation after discontinuation of infliximab therapy and remaining untreated for the next 3 years, receiving only symptomatic care. Patient underwent emergency right hemicolectomy with ileostomy, followed later by successful reversal of ileostomy. Post‐operatively, restarting biologic therapy was advised. Given the possibility of anti‐drug antibody development after prior infliximab discontinuation, adalimumab was recommended, consistent with guideline‐based practice.

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing CD in resource‐limited settings like Bangladesh, where conditions such as intestinal tuberculosis can complicate diagnosis. The development of intestinal perforation in this patient highlights the severe complications that may arise from delayed or interrupted therapy. Early and accurate diagnosis, coupled with timely initiation of appropriate treatment, may reduce the risk of such complications and help patients avoid repeated surgical interventions. Improved access to advanced diagnostics and biologic therapies remains crucial for optimizing outcomes in complex cases of CD.
