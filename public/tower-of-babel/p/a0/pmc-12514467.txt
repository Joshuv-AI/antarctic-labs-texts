Dataset on characterisation of microbiome of prostate tissue and expressed prostatic secretions
Authors: Olayemi O. Akinnola, Abosede E. Samuel, Conrad A. Omonhinmin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most prevalent cancer in men, particularly affecting those of Black African descent. Nigeria currently has the fourth highest risk for PCa mortality in the world. The microbiome of the prostate has emerged as a critical factor in understanding the aetiology and progression of prostate diseases, such as prostate cancer (PCa), benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) , benign stromal hyperplasia (BSH) and prostatitis (PRO). This study to comparatively characterise the microbiome present in prostate tissue and expressed prostatic secretion (EPS) from 30 study subjects diagnosed with PCa, BPH, BSH and PRO and sampled from the urology clinic of Lagos State University Teaching Hospital Ikeja. Bacterial species community composition and diversity were analysed based on 16S rRNA metagenome nucleotide data to ensure the accuracy, reproducibility, and broader applicability of microbiological and genomic research. Data information allows for precise identification of organisms at the species or strain level, essential for verifying experimental results and comparisons of the isolated organism's genome with related strains, providing insights into genetic diversity, virulence factors, and metabolic pathways of the sample population microbiome.

Background

One of the most prevalent malignant tumours in males is prostate cancer, which affects men of Black African descent more frequently and severely than men of other races [7], linked to factors such as age, ethnicity, genetics, diet, infection, and lifestyle. Investigations have documented the presence of microorganisms in the prostate, including viruses, bacteria, and parasites [3]. The virulence factors that microorganisms create can cause chronic inflammation and uncontrolled cell proliferation, upstaging the interest in the relationship between the microbiome and cancer [3]. This bidirectional relationship can promote malignancies with the ability to change the microbiota of the organs while impacting the aggressiveness of the cancer.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study area and sample collection

Thirty (30) study participants were enrolled from the urology clinics at Lagos State University Teaching Hospital, Ikeja (6.5904°N, 3.3423°E) Lagos States. The selected healthcare centre is a referral healthcare facility with patients from across Nigeria. Written informed consents were obtained from all subjects and samples were assigned an arbitrary subject number. Demographic characteristics, such as age, address, ethnicity, level of education, smoking, alcohol intake, and clinical variables were also obtained for all subjects using structured questionnaires. Adult males with biopsy- confirmed prostate disorders were included in this study while males with recent history of antibiotics use gastrointestinal blockage disorders and participants on catheter were excluded. Prostate tissue (PT) samples were aseptically collected via needle biopsy from the removed prostate specimen [6]. For bacterial DNA analysis, the samples were stored with DNA/RNA shield buffer Zymo® at −80 °C until extraction. Clean catch expressed prostatic secretions (EPS) were carefully collected following a prostatic massage into two 5.0 ml sterile tubes per subject. the tubes were stored at −20 °C until DNA extraction [5].

DNA extraction and targeted library sequencing

DNA extraction from the expressed prostatic secretions and prostate tissue pellets were performed using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit® following the manufacturer's instructions. Bacterial DNA concentrations were measured with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [5,8]. The extracted DNA samples were stored at −20 °C until PCR amplification and the Genomic DNA were sequenced at Inqaba Biotech, West Africa. The DNA was PCR amplified using universal primers 27F and 1492R, which target the V1-V9 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene [2]. The resulting amplicons were barcoded with PacBio M13 barcodes for multiplexing via limited-cycle PCR. Following amplification, the barcoded amplicons were quantified, pooled in equal amounts, and purified using AMPure PB beads. A PacBio SMRTbell library was then created from the pooled amplicons according to the manufacturer's protocol. The sequencing primer annealing and polymerase binding were performed as per the SMRTlink software protocol to prepare the library for sequencing on the PacBio Sequel IIe system.

Quality control for samples and data

The quality of the paired end reads generated were checked using FastQC. The raw sequencing data were analysed using QIIME version 1.9.1. Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed using split_libraries.py. The sequences were filtered using a cut off quality score of 25 and were then clustered into an open reference operational taxonomic unit (OTU) table using pick_ open_reference_otus.py and taxonomically classified. The sequences were filtered using filter_taxa_from_ otu_table.py to remove chloroplasts, and diversity analyses were performed using core_diversity_analyses.py. Quality filtering of reads was performed with filter\_and\_trim function from DADA2 package. Then, reads were merged with vsearch. All the data files (reads in FASTQ format) were deposited in NCBI SRA database under the project accession number PRJNA1209092.

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequencing reads were filtered, and alpha and beta diversity indices were computed using QIIME. The filtered sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with 97% similarity using UPARSE [4]

Limitations

The major limitation of this study is the limited sample size.

Ethics Statement

The study was approved by the Covenant Health Research Ethics Committee – CHREC (CHREC /376/2024) and the Lagos State Teaching Hospital Research Ethics Committee – LREC (LREC/06/10/2452) . All subjects gave written informed consent for sample collection and personal data processing.
