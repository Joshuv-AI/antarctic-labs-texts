Point-of-Care EEG Artificial Intelligence Measure of Seizure Burden Associates With Clinical Outcome at Discharge
Authors: Josef Parvizi, Michelle Armenta Salas, Mariel Kalkach Aparicio, Sahar F. Zafar, Masoom Desai, Suganya Karunakaran, Archit Gupta, Baharan Kamousi, Lawrence J. Hirsch, Aaron F. Struck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Point-of-care (POC) electroencephalography (EEG) enabled with artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms hold the potential to address gaps in EEG access and interpretation. We assessed the clinical association of the patterns recognized by one of such systems.Design:Secondary cohort analysis of the retrospective multicenter Seizure Assessment and Forecasting with Efficient Rapid-EEG (SAFER-EEG) study.Setting:EEG and clinical data were gathered from three academic centers with access to POC EEG (Ceribell, Sunnyvale, CA) as part of their standard of care. We used a bedside seizure burden (SzB) monitor (“bedside” algorithm) and a more sensitive online algorithm (“portal” algorithm) to determine SzB. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at discharge was selected as the outcome.Patients:Four hundred enrolled adult patients, 359 with complete outcome and clinical data.Interventions:Time in seizure and peak SzB assessed by AI.Measurements and Main Results:Per the bedside algorithm, 39.8% of patients had peak 5-minute SzB greater than 0%. With every additional hour of seizure detected by the bedside algorithm, patients were more likely to have unfavorable outcome (mRS > 3) at discharge (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.98; 95% CI, 1.11–4.29). Compared with 5-minute SzB = 0%, prolonged activity per the bedside algorithm (peak 5-min SzB ≥ 90%) was associated with a 3.4-fold increase in aOR of poor outcome. Every 30 seconds of activity in the maximum hourly SzB of the bedside algorithm was associated with increased odds of unfavorable outcome (aOR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.00–1.03). Combining outputs from the algorithms increased the strength of associations with outcome, particularly in patients with peak 5-minute SzB greater than or equal to 90% (aOR, 4.4; 95% CI, 1.66–12.69).Conclusions:AI-estimated SzB is associated with functional outcomes at discharge in a dose-response fashion, even after controlling for clinical cofounds. This provides initial evidence of the clinical utility of AI algorithms for detecting clinically important EEG patterns.

KEY POINTS

Question: Are artificial intelligence (AI)-derived seizure burden estimates from point-of-care Ceribell electroencephalography (EEG) clinically relevant and associated with functional outcomes in hospitalized adults?

Findings: In a retrospective analysis of data from a multicenter study of 359 patients, increasing AI-detected seizure burden was independently associated with worse discharge outcomes. Combining outputs from two AI algorithms further strengthened these associations, especially at higher seizure burdens.

Meaning: AI-enabled EEG systems can provide clinically relevant information by quantifying SzB, supporting their potential use for rapid bedside assessment and risk stratification.

Seizures and ictal-interictal continuum (IIC) patterns are common in hospitalized patients (1–4). The majority of critically ill patients do not have overt signs of seizures such as convulsions (5), and suffer from “nonconvulsive” also known as electrographic seizures, and thus, the only way to detect seizures in these patients is with electroencephalography (EEG) (6).

Several studies have demonstrated a relationship between seizure burden (SzB) and poor clinical outcomes (7–9). In particular, Payne et al (8) demonstrated that, in pediatric patients, not only were seizures associated with poor outcomes, but also peak SzB was more tightly associated with outcomes in a clear “dose-response” fashion. This finding has helped redefine status epilepticus (10). Electrographic status epilepticus is now defined to include 20% or greater SzB within any given hour. The underlying hypothesis is that seizures are metabolically demanding and that the reserve capacity of the brain can be depleted, resulting in secondary brain damage (11, 12). Evidence of these metabolic demands in critically ill patients comes from oxygenation probes (13), microdialysis (14), and fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (15, 16). These findings could be extrapolated to the IIC patterns, which can behave similarly to status epilepticus in terms of secondary brain injury (14, 16, 17).

While continuous long-term EEG monitoring on its own has not been shown to impact outcome (18, 19), time to EEG and timely detection and treatment of seizures and related patterns have beneficial clinical effects (20–22). Due to logistical and financial constraints of conventional EEG infrastructure (23), timely access to EEG and its interpretation is either limited (24) or significantly delayed across major hospitals where time to EEG has been systematically investigated (25, 26). These barriers are being overcome. The 2HELPS2B scale and other similar tools can help triage patients at risk for seizure based on shorter EEG samples (27). Recently, two multicenter studies showed the utility of a Food and Drug Administration-cleared and commercialized point-of-care (POC) EEG to be used as a rapid screening of at-risk patients within minutes (26) and to accurately forecast with the 2HELPS2B scale based on EEG tracings recorded with this POC EEG system (28). Further, the system is coupled with an artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm that monitors the SzB with reported high sensitivity and specificity for detecting continuous seizure activity (100% and 93%, respectively) (29). More recent assessments of the algorithm have shown high accuracy rate, ranging from around 80% to 96%, for detecting and ruling-out definite and possible electrographic status epilepticus at different SzB thresholds (30).

While access to EEG may be shortened with POC EEG devices, the interpretation of recordings with these devices may still encounter similar barriers as conventional EEG. Using AI interpretation of EEG is known to aid and augment the diagnostic process (31). However, it remains to be determined if AI-generated interpretation of the EEG is associated with outcomes in a manner similar to traditional analysis of conventional EEG by human experts.

This study was designed to explore this issue by analyzing data from patients enrolled in the Seizure Assessment and Forecasting with Efficient Rapid-EEG (SAFER-EEG) study. For this SAFER study, we have previously characterized the potential utility of the POC EEG for assessing seizure risk (28), and explored the association of access to POC EEG with shorter stay in the ICU (32). The primary question in this analysis is to explore the extent to which AI-generated EEG interpretations, based on POC EEG recordings, are associated with patient outcomes, regardless of human expert assessments.

METHODS

We performed secondary cohort analysis of the retrospective multicenter SAFER-EEG study. The original study included adult patients from four academic centers, namely Massachusetts General Hospital, University of New Mexico, University of Wisconsin (UW), and Yale New Haven Health System, who were hospitalized and received EEG as part of their standard of care from January 2018 to June 2022. We selected the cohort examined with the POC EEG system (Ceribell, Sunnyvale, CA). In the sites included, the POC EEG data guided the clinical decision-making in each case, although AI outputs were either not available or not used.

Institutional Review Board Approval and Patient Consent

The SAFER-EEG study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at each study site under the study name “SAFER: Seizure Assessment and Forecasting with Efficient Rapid-EEG.” The UW site IRB served as the IRB of record (IRB study number: 2021-1022, approved on September 03, 2021). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, patient consent was waived. The data user agreement for the SAFER-EEG study provided the right to the data science team at Ceribell to analyze the de-identified study data using their proprietary AI algorithm. The procedures of the study were in accordance with the ethical standards of each institution and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Inclusion Criteria

In the primary SAFER-EEG study, hospitalized patients undergoing EEG were sequentially enrolled into two cohorts based on the first EEG type: greater than or equal to 1 hour of POC EEG or greater than or equal to 4 hours of conventional EEG. At sites with both modalities, POC EEG was typically used when conventional EEG was unavailable, such as after hours or on weekends. For this secondary analysis, inclusion required available outcome data (pre-EEG and at discharge) and raw POC EEG recordings for AI analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968). No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied.

Machine Learning Algorithms

Two separate AI algorithms were run post hoc on the POC EEG data and used to derive our exposure metrics. These algorithms were trained and validated (29) separately from any of the data reported here. The first one is a SzB monitoring algorithm (Claritγ) that runs both at the bedside as well as on the cloud (accessible through a web portal). This algorithm measures the severity of SzB every 10 seconds using a rolling 5-minute window of the preceding EEG recording. Sensitivity and specificity of an older version of this algorithm was studied systematically (29). Our current analysis is based on the output of a newer version of the algorithm. The second algorithm is a more sensitive algorithm (Insights Bar; Ceribell) that only runs on the cloud and it is accessible only through the web portal. Given the higher sensitivity of the portal algorithm, some highly epileptiform activities in the IIC may be included in the final output measure that are not included in the bedside algorithm.

Supplementary Figure 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968) shows a representative EEG, including the 5-minute SzB trace that would be available through the recorder at the bedside, and the AI-output accessible through the portal. Further details of the algorithms are provided in the Supplementary Figure 3 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968) and the Machine Learning section of the Supplementary Material (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968).

Bedside and Portal AI Outputs

In our report, we will refer to Claritγ output as “bedside AI output” and to Claritγ + Insights Bar outputs as “portal AI output.” In clinical practice, the Insights Bar algorithm is not available at the bedside and is not presented by itself on the web portal, either, without the presence of Claritγ output. Clinicians reviewing the EEGs remotely on the web portal would have access to the output of both algorithms, while the clinicians at the bedside would only have access to the output of the first AI algorithm. Therefore, in the presentation of the results, we do not report findings with only the second algorithm.

Algorithm-Derived Seizure Metrics

Time in Seizure (Time of Seizure and % of Total Duration)

Estimated as the cumulative time in seizure throughout the recording from the 10-second binary output of the algorithms. If multiple recordings were done for one subject, the total time with positive findings was accumulated across all available recordings. The time was converted to hours and to a percentage of the total recording time.

Peak Seizure Burden (per 5-min)

Using a 5-minute moving window with 10-second steps, we estimated the SzB for the bedside and the portal algorithms. We determined the maximum SzB for each subject, which could happen at any point during the recording. We estimated the impact in clinical outputs across different SzB thresholds (≥ 1%, ≥ 5%, ≥ 10%, ≥ 20%, ≥ 50%, and ≥ 90%), when compared with patients with SzB of zero throughout their entire recording.

Peak Hourly Seizure Burden

We also estimated the seizure burden using 1-hour windows, the hourly seizure burden (hSzB), more commonly used in other studies (8). Similar to the 5-minute SzB calculation, we used a rolling moving average across the 10-second binary outputs to estimate the hSzB and used 5-minute step sizes to update the hSzB output. We determined the maximum hSzB as the peak value that happened over any 1-hour window during the entire recording.

Functional Outcome Assessment

The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at discharge was used to assess neurologic functional outcome. Scores were extracted or estimated from admission and discharge notes by trained research coordinators under the supervision of the national and site principal investigators. The mRS ranges from 0 (no disability) to 6 (death); scores of 4–6 were classified as unfavorable outcomes, consistent with prior studies (7, 17, 32). Outcome data were available only at discharge, which may introduce bias, as longer hospital stays could reflect ongoing rehabilitation and improved condition at discharge.

Statistical Analysis

For continuous variables, we reported means ± sds and medians with interquartile ranges; categorical variables were summarized as proportions. Associations between poor outcomes and seizure metrics (time in seizure, peak SzB groups) were assessed with separate multivariable logistic regression models adjusting for relevant confounders (Supplementary Methods, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968). Comparisons across SzB groups were corrected using the Bonferroni-Holm method (α = 0.05). Models controlled for age 65 years old or older, pre-EEG Glasgow Coma Scale less than or equal to 8, pre-EEG mRS greater than or equal to 4, time to EEG (< 24 vs. ≥ 24 hr), number of neurologic diagnoses and comorbidities, and primary diagnostic category (stroke, anoxic encephalopathy, other acute brain injury [ABI], epilepsy, or systemic/other) (27, 33–36). To evaluate immortality bias, we used permutation tests using the lmPerm package in RStudio (Posit Team 2025), to evaluate whether there were significant differences between survivors and patients who passed away in the recording duration and the algorithm estimated seizure metrics. Finally, we examined the correlation between recording duration and seizure findings using Spearman rank correlation test.

From 400 adult patients in the initial POC EEG cohort, data from 41 patients were excluded from this analysis according to unbiased inclusion criteria mentioned above (Supplementary Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968). The most common primary diagnoses were nervous system injuries or other nonnervous system illnesses without a history of epilepsy (33.7%), history of epilepsy without major ABI (23.9%), and ABI including ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke findings (18.1%).

Time in Seizure and Outcomes

Based on the bedside AI output, 60.1% of the patients (n = 216) did not have any seizure activity throughout their monitoring with POC EEG, while 12.8% (n = 46) had a peak 5-minute SzB greater than or equal to 50% (i.e., burden of seizure activity over the course of preceding 5 min exceeded 50%). Just over half of these (n = 25/46, 54.3%) reached a peak 5-minute SzB of at least 90%, indicating possible status epilepticus. Based on data from the portal AI outputs, we found that 28.7% of patients (n = 103) did not have positive findings on either algorithm throughout their recordings. Further, 18.9% (n = 68) had peak 5-minute SzB greater than or equal to 50%, with most (n = 49, 72.1%) reaching a peak 5-minute SzB greater than or equal to 90%. Figure 1 summarizes all subjects across each major diagnostic group and their time in seizure per the bedside and portal algorithms, while Supplementary Table 1 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968) shows grouping of time in seizure and outcome at discharge. When examining the impact of survival on time in seizure, we did not find any significant differences across seizure metrics and survival status (Supplementary Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968).

In the multivariable model, we found that time in seizure was a significant contributor to the odds of poor outcome. With every additional hour with detections per bedside AI output, we found a nearly two-fold increase in the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of poor outcome (aOR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.11–4.30; p = 0.058). Similarly, when normalizing time in seizure as the proportion of the entire recording, we found a trend of increased odds for every 10% of the recording with seizure per the bedside AI output (p = 0.094). Using the portal output, we found that patients’ odds of unfavorable outcome were 1.44 (95% CI, 1.08–2.14; p = 0.058) for every hour with positive findings. Further, when we normalized the times to the recording duration, we measured a 22% relative increase in odds for every 10% of recording with positive findings (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

Peak Seizure Burden and Outcomes

Five-Minute Seizure Burden

Supplementary Table 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968) summarizes patients by peak 5-minute SzB. Compared with those without seizures, bedside AI detections of peak SzB greater than or equal to 50% and greater than or equal to 90% were associated with 2.7-fold (padj = 0.023) and 3.4-fold (padj = 0.031) higher odds of poor outcome, respectively. With the portal AI, corresponding aORs were 3.4 (padj = 0.011) and 4.4 (padj = 0.024). To ensure that our findings are not driven by time-dependent confounds. We first addressed the issue of mortality bias by analyzing whether those patients who died during the hospitalization had indeed any significantly different seizure activity findings per the AI and whether the subjects who died had any different EEG recording duration. As shown in Supplementary Table 4 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968), we did not find any differences in recording duration, peak 5-minute SzB, cumulative time in seizure, or peak 1-hour SzB. Next, we explored whether subjects with longer length of stay (LOS) had in fact longer recording duration in the POC EEG. For this, we examined the correlation between recording duration and LOS. We found no significant correlation between the recording duration and LOS (Spearman rank correlation, rho = 0.067; p = 0.204). Additionally, we examined whether the day of EEG in respect to the day of admission was consistently different between patients with different seizure findings. As shown in Supplementary Figures 6 and 7 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968), we found no relationship between day of EEG recording and LOS. Overall, we found that most EEGs started close to admission time, but some were started later on (i.e., at random intervals) during the patient’s hospital stay.

Peak Hourly Seizure Burden

Using the peak hSzB instead of the 5-minute SzB, we found again a significant association between the maximum value and the odds of poor outcome at discharge. Using the output from the bedside, we found an aOR of 1.02 (95% CI, 1.00–1.03; padj = 0.050) for every percent increase in maximum hSzB, that is, for every 30 seconds with seizure per the bedside AI output. Further, when using the combination of the two AI outputs on the portal, we found that there was a corresponding 1% relative increase in odds of poor outcome for a similar hSzB increase (aOR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.00–1.02; padj = 0.031). Our data also revealed that the probability of mRS greater than or equal to 4 at discharge as peak hourly SzB increases, for the bedside AI output and the combination of bedside and portal AI outputs (Supplementary Fig. 5, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H968).

DISCUSSION

Our findings suggest that the output of POC EEG AI algorithms (trained on thousands of EEG samples entirely independent from the current dataset) can be directly correlated, in a dose-response manner, with the clinical outcome, even after adjusting for confounding clinical factors. These findings are consistent with prior research examining the impact of SzB and severity on neurologic outcomes, the cumulative effects of seizures and status epilepticus, and the threshold effect; that is, SzB becomes additive once a certain metabolic threshold is crossed.

What sets our study apart is that the EEG signals were acquired rapidly with a reduced-montage device and independently from trained EEG technicians, and, more importantly, interpreted by AI algorithms without considering whether the expert neurologists would have agreed with it. In line with prior research (17, 35), our findings highlight the potential of AI-coupled EEG systems to address existing gaps in not only access to EEG recordings but also in their clinically meaningful interpretation, particularly in emergency or critical care settings.

Although preliminary, our findings suggest that the measure of SzB captured by the POC EEG algorithms, in adult patients, may indeed be useful to identify patients that warrant a change in management, especially those with high SzB (e.g., > 50%). For example, previous validation of the POC EEG system have shown impact in management from quick review of the EEGs via its sonification feature (26), and in more recent studies assessed the utility of the system in stroke code differential diagnosis (37). Although these studies did not include the AI algorithm derived metrics, we consider that the metrics presented here would provide clinically relevant information given previous validation of the algorithm against expert review. However, we are mindful that this claim requires validation in future prospective studies and testing this claim was not the aim of our current study. This information added to the rapid deployment of POC systems would provide a valuable tool when incorporated in neurocritical care workflows.

As noted, we did not compare the performance of the POC EEG AI algorithms to the performance of neurology experts reading the same EEGs. We emphasize that several studies of the Claritγ algorithm (29, 30) have validated its relative accuracy and have shown that the measure of high SzB is associated with either ictal or IIC patterns. Nonetheless, the specificity of the algorithm varies at different SzB thresholds and, hence, we acknowledge that in some cases the patterns identified as seizures by the AI may have been nonseizure (i.e., false positive). However, the majority of these patterns may still be pathologic (29) and could likely contribute to neuronal injury. Future studies are needed to determine if the so called false positive detections by the AI may indeed be clinically relevant.

Our study has limitations. The AI algorithms were applied to a POC EEG system with ten electrodes covering frontal, temporal, and occipital regions—equivalent to standard bitemporal chains in the 10–20 system. While limited parasagittal or midline coverage may miss some seizures or IIC patterns, such focal parasagittal seizures are rare (37, 38). Our findings show that seizure activity detected by this reduced montage is clinically meaningful; however, whether smaller or more focal seizures—potentially undetected by this system—carry similar clinical significance remains to be determined.

We are also aware of the difficulties in explaining the poor outcome of patients solely based on higher measures of SzB and independently from the severity of their underlying disease, particularly without incorporating treatment information. However, separate studies, including a controlled observational multicenter Does Use of Rapid Re-sponse EEG Impact Clinical Decision Making trial (26), have documented changes in clinical decisions on the basis of POC EEG information available at the bedside (39, 40). While another subanalysis of the SAFER-EEG data that focused on ICU cohorts showed a significant difference in patient outcomes between those who received POC EEG and those who waited for conventional EEG (32). We also acknowledge that careful attention to time-dependent analysis is critical for examining the association between SzB and clinical outcomes. Because seizure exposure accrues over time and can be truncated by competing events such as death or recovery, analytic approaches must explicitly account for time-varying exposure, time-dependent confounding, and potential immortal time or selection biases. Failure to do so may yield distorted associations that reflect differences in opportunity for exposure rather than true biological effects. In our study, we did not include outcome assessments at uniform time points across all participants, which limited certain time-based approaches. However, in supplementary analyses, we did not find a significant association between patient mortality, study duration, or time to EEG and the seizure burden measures.

Last, in the analysis of our data, we adjusted for relevant clinical covariates and found a clear dose-response relationship with both cumulative time in seizure and peak SzB. As such, our data are in line with previous studies (14, 41) and provide novel evidence supporting a causal relationship between the measure of SzB as estimated by an AI-coupled POC EEG system and clinical outcome.
