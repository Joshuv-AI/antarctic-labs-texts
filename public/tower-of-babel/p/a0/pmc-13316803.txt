Anatomy Dissection and Early Career Engagement: A Narrative Review of Undergraduate Medical Education in Rwanda
Authors: Darius Benimana, Schadrack Habumuremyi, Jean Paul Rugambwa, Olivier Kubwimana, Julien Gashegu, Olivier Uwishema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAt the start of their professions, trainees pursuing bachelor's degrees in medicine and surgery participate in a long program that constitutes mainstream medical education. The practicalities surrounding anatomical study, whether by dissection, using cadaveric specimens, or prosection, is essential. However, it requires infrastructure and enough specimens, amongst other paraphernalia, making it challenging to teach effectively in low‐resource settings. This narrative review explores the importance of anatomy dissection courses for undergraduate students at the University of Rwanda and how its influences support early career involvement.MethodsUnrestricted search was conducted in Google Scholar and PubMed/MEDLINE and other online databases. This focused‐on anatomy education and dissection courses for undergraduate students.ResultsThe significance of utilizing the clinical skills learned of undergraduate students whilst boosting self‐confidence to become future successful surgeons. Additionally, the research abilities exhibited by undergraduate students were utilized to enhance their professional development and gain knowledge of anatomical variations when writing case reports. Moreover, various obstacles coincide with the pursuit of anatomical study in Rwanda that affects students enrolling in such anatomy dissection classes. These comprise a dearth of undergraduate students, poor infrastructure of standard laboratories, and insufficient numbers of cadavers.ConclusionSince there is currently evidence that early involvement has a favorable impact on a student's career, we should discover methods to include medical students in dissection courses.

Introduction

Medical education comprises a complex domain that requires trainees to be involved at very early career stages [1]. Integrating student participation in healthcare initiatives and tactics to enhance the sustainability of healthcare delivery is essential in the medical field [1]. Undergraduate medical students in several Sub‐Saharan African (SSA) universities commonly learn through active and passive observation, utilizing digital aids to augment anatomical study [2, 3]. Participating in undergraduate students while in anatomy dissection classes contributes to the development of tutee‐tutor interprofessional relations and trust. In the early years of medical study, it aided in the learning of the human body, enhancing student anatomical comprehension correlating with practical skills learned [4].

Conversely, the students' enthusiasm in surgical tasks is heightened by their participation in dissection classes [5]. According to a study of 185 students and 8 surgical residents, the sessions they received bolstered their enthusiasm in pursuing a surgical career [5]. This would demand preclinical dissection abilities from students who want to become surgeons.

At the University of Rwanda, anatomy dissection courses are designed for residents and master's students, and it is challenging to engage undergraduate students in medical studies through dissection [6]. Meanwhile, a few of these individuals join postgraduate classes, aiding their professional development. There is a scarcity of data pertaining to undergraduate student engagement in SSA, where available data focuses on surgical residents undertaking anatomy [3].

A study by Gashegu et al. (2023) showed that dissection was a unique experience for all trainees, including undergraduate medical students, resident postgraduate trainees, and even medical doctors joining the course [7]. This is an excellent illustration of how increasing the number of medical undergraduates taking dissection courses would greatly advance their careers and professional development.

In Rwanda, most of the students they don't do cadaveric dissection but study on the only prosected bodies in the laboratories [8]. Since some literatures have strongly reported to support that undergraduate students should engage in the cadaveric dissection, to improve their anatomy knowledge and understanding [9, 10, 11]. This review provides the current engagement of undergraduate students regarding their engagement in the cadaver dissection, challenges and proposes recommendations to enhance the early career engagements by the medical students in Rwanda.

Research Question

“What is the importance of the anatomy dissection courses on early career engagement of the undergraduate students at the University of Rwanda?”

Search Strategy

The literature‐based narrative review was performed employing electronic search databases PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar, and other online databases. Literature published in English which identified adhered to Rwandan anatomy practices and education, focusing on dissection courses for respective undergraduate students. We used the following keywords for searching: “Anatomy dissection”, “Rwanda”, “Undergraduate”, and “Anatomy dissection”. There were no results of studies done on undergraduate students in Rwanda.

Anatomy Dissection Courses at the University of Rwanda

In August 2020, the Departments of Surgery and Anatomy at the University of Rwanda organized anatomy dissection courses intending to impact surgical safety and competencies [12]. Surgical residents from general surgery, plastic surgery, orthopaedic surgery, urology, neurology, ENT, and obstetrics and gynaecology have been actively impacted by their learning outcomes through anatomy courses advocating dissection [12].

At the University of Rwanda, the medical curriculum includes three anatomy modules that cover all parts of the human body, and they are taught during the first and second academic years of undergraduate study [13]. At the postgraduate training level of all surgical discipline curricula, the recall anatomy module relevant to several surgical disciplines is taught in Year 1. However, its theoretical nature and lack of exposure to real anatomy structures have been its drawbacks [13].

In addition to surgical residents, master's students studying clinical anatomy also participated in the dissection course, which affected their learning and comprehension [12]. However, some undergraduate medical students typically enrol in these courses primarily for observation and learning; they answer some of the structural questions posed in the course, which affects their capacity to learn, engage in active thinking, and practice.

Benefits of Early Career Engagement Through Anatomy Dissection Courses

The benefits of dissection classes for undergraduates are substantial, as they increase their student‐teacher interprofessional trust, learning, and achievements [14]. As students may clearly view the layers of the human body, fascia, bones, tendons, arteries, veins, major nerves, and even minuscule structures, taking dissection courses enhances their grasp of human anatomy generally [15]. Each individual student should integrate their learned knowledge from theoretical study, the information garnered from observing the anatomical table, and the practical skills from dissection [16].

Second, by watching a video or witnessing the actual execution of surgical surgery in person, trainees may improve their clinical abilities while also retaining and deepening their comprehension. Moreover, it has been shown that students have a clear understanding of some of the surgical terminology and techniques now used by surgeons and residents [17].

Utilising undergraduate students' clinical skills is aided by anatomical variations, which are also discovered during dissection courses. When students' anatomical variations are identified, they become more aware of them, ask questions about them, and even learn how to present case reports more effectively [18].

Higher confidence in medical practice is another important outcome, which is attained by improving one's knowledge of human anatomy. This increases one's confidence and trust in medical procedures. Additionally, they become more confident in their medical acts because of their exposure to specific medical cases and surgical lessons [19].

Challenges and Solutions to Improving Anatomy Dissection Among Medical Students in Rwanda

An inadequate number of undergraduate students regularly attend the dissection courses at the Anatomy Department at the University of Rwanda [7]. A few of them join medical residents to do dissection courses during their holidays and free leisure time. Even possessing a small gross anatomy facility to accommodate all students in dissection courses presents a hurdle [20].

Engaging all students, even undergraduates, in dissection courses has been hampered by reports of insufficient cadavers utilised in instruction [7]. Despite increased efforts, obtaining cadavers that may instruct all students remains a challenge [21].

Another issue is that the University of Rwanda's undergraduate curriculum does not include dissection classes or provides little opportunity to practice skills during their training years [22].

Most of the studies from the sub‐Saharan Africa reported the shortage of the supplies compared to the student ratio [23], leading to the most of the schools reports the inadequate numbers for the effective dissection [24, 25]. This situation is not far beyond Rwandan current issue regarding the limited engagement of the Rwandan undergraduate students' engagements in cadaveric dissection.

Expanding the gross laboratory and possibly purchasing new supplies to accommodate the participation of undergraduate medical students in the dissection courses may require more funding [6]. Many students taking dissection classes will have more room because of this increase in dissection space.

Limitations and Future Perspectives

These challenges, along with stakeholders and tactics to increase student participation, would be implemented in every discipline. To increase the number of undergraduate students who consistently enroll in these courses, there is a need for more knowledge and involvement of the students to join these courses [26].

To increase the availability of cadavers for student instruction, it is increasingly important to raise public knowledge about corpse donation. Additionally, undergraduate students' preclinical programs would require more participation in dissection courses [27].

Enhancing a different approach to help students develop their dissection abilities during their clinical years would aid in their skill development. This may include wherever possible the sessions of the cadaver dissection at the university, dissection videography and open‐access online courses designed to provide University of Rwanda students with an understanding of dissection [2, 28]. Students' understanding of structural anatomy and even their proficiency in medical practical sessions would both benefit from this.

This study synthesizes only secondary literature rather than primary engagement and involvement, in addition to the limited literature on Rwandan medical students' engagement in cadaveric dissection. It does not solely discuss the actual number of students, their experiences, and primary reasons based on university levels and other stakeholders.

Undergraduate students should be the subject of research studies to determine how they feel about taking anatomy dissection classes, identify the difficulties they encounter, and offer potential solutions to those issues [29].

Additionally, universities and policymakers in Rwanda should improve public awareness among the population to increase body donations to learning institutions, thereby reducing supply burdens. Improved funding and investment in the availability of cadaveric dissection for undergraduate students should also help not only in Rwanda but in other settings.

Conclusions

Undergraduate student participation in dissection courses is crucial in Rwanda. Early career participation in cadaveric dissection entails skill development, confidence building, and trust leverage. It is also necessary to implement more cutting‐edge strategies aimed at enhancing anatomy instruction in Rwanda, as well as those based on various approaches to involving undergraduate students in practical skills. Some challenges still exist, such as limited supplies and funding. Curriculum reform, public cadaver donation campaigns, and the integration of other innovative teaching methods must be implemented to support undergraduate students' access to anatomy dissection classes.
