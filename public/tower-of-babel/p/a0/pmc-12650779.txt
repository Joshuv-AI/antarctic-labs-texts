Long-standing neck mass: A rare case of vagal paraganglioma
Authors: Maryam Sadiq, Sarmad Ali, Awais Tariq, Junaid Azad, Jahangir Sarwar Khan
Published: 2025

Introduction

Paragangliomas (PGLs) of the head and neck are rare, and although the exact incidence is difficult to estimate, they account for less than 0.5 % of all head and neck malignancies. They may come from the jugular paraganglia, vagal, tympanic, or carotid body [1]. Three genes, succinate dehydrogenase complex subunits ‘D’, ‘B’, and ‘C’ (SDHD, SDHB, and SDHC), are known to be linked to hereditary vulnerability to head and neck paragangliomas. SDHD and SDHB, the genes most commonly implicated, are also predisposing to phaeochromocytoma [2]. Most paragangliomas are thought to be benign tumors, although between 6 and 19 % have been shown to have malignant behavior and cause distant metastases [3]. The vagus nerve is the source of less than 5 % of these head and neck PGLs [4]. The typical reported age upon diagnosis is 45 years, and there is a clear female predominance [5]. The vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) is the dominant nerve of the parasympathetic division of the autonomic nervous system and an excellent illustration of an endocrine tumor connected to the vagus nerve is a vagal PGL (VPGL) [6]. Vagal paragangliomas grow in the retrostyloid compartment of the parapharyngeal space. They arise from an island of paraganglion tissue generated from the neural crest that is positioned on the vagus nerve [7]. Vagal paragangliomas can very rarely form on the nerve's distal end. Some of these tumors grow down into the neck, while others grow upward toward the base of the skull [8].

Signs and symptoms of paraganglioma happen when the tumor releases too much adrenaline or noradrenaline. Some paraganglioma tumors, on the other hand, are asymptomatic—that is, they don't produce excess noradrenaline or adrenaline rather present with a painless, slowly enlarging mass in the lateral neck or may produce symptoms arising from the compression of its adjacent structures. To make the diagnosis, an imaging modality evaluation is required. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA), computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and B-mode sonography with color-coded Doppler sonography are among the commonly used imaging techniques [10].

•Surgery: It can be associated with mortality and morbidity. A multidisciplinary team is required to perform the surgery, which involves exposing the parapharyngeal space and retracting the digastric and stylohyoid muscles.

Other treatment options for paraganglioma include: Chemotherapy, Ablation therapy, and Targeted therapy [11]. Surgical excision of these tumors is advocated as the definitive treatment although they often present a surgical challenge because of their closeness to the internal and external carotid arteries, the internal jugular vein, the lower cranial nerves, and their tendency to expand to the base of the skull [12]. Alternatively, radiation therapy has also been advocated. The relative efficacy of one modality over the other remains unclear [13].

We present a case of a 55 years old female with a rare vagal paraganglioma. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [14].

Case presentation

A 55-year-old female, who was normoglycemic, normotensive, and a non-smoker, presented to the surgical outpatient department (OPD) with a long-standing complaint of a right-sided neck mass that had been gradually enlarging for the past 10 years. The mass was painless and had progressively increased in size, from the size of an almond to that of a mango. There were no associated symptoms such as palpitations, tremors, voice changes, dysphagia, weight loss, fever, anorexia, headaches, tachycardia, or shortness of breath. The patient also had no significant medical or surgical history.

Upon examination, a 12.5 cm × 12 cm swelling was noted on the right side of the neck. The mass was non-lobulated, firm, non-compressible, and immobile, firmly fixed to the underlying structures. It caused slight deviation of the trachea to the left, but the overlying skin appeared normal. Her vital signs were stable and within the normal range. Indirect laryngoscopy (IDL) revealed a thickened tongue but a normal oropharyngeal isthmus. However, the IDL mirror could not pass beyond the tongue, limiting visualization of structures beyond the oropharynx.

To confirm the clinical diagnosis, several diagnostic investigations were performed. A fine needle aspiration (FNAC) was conducted, which yielded hemorrhagic aspirates. Cytological analysis showed moderately cellular smears with cohesive groups and clusters of epithelioid cells, exhibiting central round-to-oval nuclei and occasional prominent nucleoli. Additionally, spindle cells with elongated, wavy nuclei were scattered throughout. These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of paraganglioma.

An ultrasound of the neck revealed a large hyperechoic mass on the right side, with areas of necrosis and internal vascularity observed on Doppler imaging. The mass appeared separate from the right submandibular gland and the right lobe of the thyroid, which were compressed and elevated by the mass.

A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the neck further supported the diagnosis as shown in Fig. 1. The scan revealed a well-defined, enhancing soft tissue lesion in the right carotid space, supplied by multiple prominent feeding arteries. Two small aneurysms were located deep within the lesion, confirming the presence of a vagal paraganglioma.

Given the large size of the paraganglioma, surgical excision was selected as the treatment option. Under aseptic conditions and general anesthesia, a 5 cm elliptical incision was made on the right side of the neck. During the procedure, a firm and hard swelling was identified, tethered to the carotid artery and jugular vein. The mass was carefully lifted with its capsule intact, and the surrounding arteries and veins were meticulously separated and ligated. Hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed in reverse order, with a drain placed to ensure proper drainage.

Following tumor excision, the patient was closely monitored in the postoperative period with particular attention to airway patency, swallowing function, and vocal cord mobility, given the involvement of the vagus nerve. Standard monitoring included neurological examinations, pain control, and wound assessment. Fiberoptic laryngoscopy was performed to evaluate vocal cord movement, while swallowing assessments were undertaken to detect aspiration risk. Supportive measures such as speech and swallowing therapy were initiated when needed. The patient was also scheduled for regular follow-up imaging to monitor for recurrence and long-term surveillance of cranial nerve function. The patient's postoperative recovery was uneventful, and she remained stable throughout. The drain was removed on 2nd post-operative day and the patient was discharged on the third postoperative day with appropriate schedule for regular follow-up imaging to monitor for recurrence and long-term surveillance of cranial nerve function.

The histopathological specimen consisted of an un-oriented grayish white mass measuring 11 × 7 × 6 cm and its examination revealed an encapsulated neoplasm comprising of nest of tumor cells arranged in a zellballen pattern with intervening vessels as shown in Fig. 2. Individual cells were epithelioid to spindle shaped with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm along with salt and pepper pattern chromatin, moderate nuclear pleomorphism with occasional bizarre nuclear forms. 5/10 HPF (high power field) mitosis was seen. The patient was discharged on the third postoperative day with appropriate follow-up care instructions.

Discussion

Vagal paragangliomas are most commonly found in the parapharyngeal space, but there is evidence indicating that cervical paragangliomas can also arise along the cervical trajectory of the vagus nerve [5]. Vagal paragangliomas (VPG) often remain asymptomatic and are frequently diagnosed incidentally during imaging for unrelated conditions. When symptomatic, the most common presentation, seen in nearly half of patients, is a neck mass as in our case [13]. These patients present with cranial nerve palsies in about 50 % of cases [14]. Head and neck paragangliomas are rarely associated with catecholamine hypersecretion, so urinary catecholamine screening is typically reserved for cases with symptoms like tachycardia, hypertension, or a family history of paraganglioma [12]. Our patient, like many others, exhibited no signs of catecholamine hypersecretion. Genetic testing is typically performed in patients with risk factors such as age over 50, a family history of paragangliomas, extra-adrenal or metastatic tumors, or elevated dopamine and methoxytyramine levels. However, in this case, it was deemed unnecessary due to the absence of any significant personal, medical, or family history [15].

Histologically, paragangliomas display a “Zellballen” pattern, characterized by nests of epithelioid or polygonal cells with or without atypia, mitosis, and capsular invasion, surrounded by reticular fibers and numerous blood vessels [16]. The preoperative diagnosis of vagal paraganglioma, though challenging, can be achieved through a combination of clinical evaluation and radiographic imaging [14]. Advanced imaging techniques, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), conventional angiography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are valuable tools for the diagnosis of vagal paragangliomas [17]. Octreotide scintigraphy can detect hidden distant lesions, small recurrences, multicentric locations and in some functioning tumors, it can also assess the tumor's secretory activity as these tumors express large numbers of somatostatin receptors [18]. Our patient also underwent similar investigations. Her contrast CT scan showed a lesion measuring 6.3 × 7.3 × 11.3 cm (AP × TR × CC) displacing the right common, internal and external carotid arteries medially. The right internal jugular vein was significantly compressed anteriorly. The lesion was supplied by multiple prominent feeding arteries with two small aneurysms located deep within the lesion. It was located deep to the right sternocleidomastoid muscle, which was significantly compressed outwards. Inferiorly the lesion was causing compression upon the right lobe of thyroid gland with intact fat planes between the lesion and thyroid gland. A couple of prominent subcentimeter right level IIA cervical lymph nodes were also seen. Visualized brain parenchyma was unremarkable. Orbits and base of skull soft tissues were normal. No abnormality seen in oral cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, larynx and hypopharynx. No concerning osseous lesion was seen. She additionally underwent FNAC and ultrasound as well suggesting the same diagnosis. Due to a lack of somatic symptoms, octreotide scintigraphy was not performed. Surgical removal is the primary treatment for these tumors, often necessitating the sacrifice of the vagus nerve and potentially causing damage to other nerves. This leads to common postoperative complications, including vagal, hypoglossal, and glossopharyngeal paralysis. While therapeutic embolization can reduce tumor size and help control blood loss during surgery, it is seldom regarded as a definitive treatment [17]. Radiation therapy serves as an alternative to surgery, effectively halting tumor growth in some cases, but it may cause neurological complications and, rarely, completely eradicate the tumor. It is particularly beneficial for elderly patients or when tumors are large and inoperable. Unilateral radiotherapy may be recommended after surgery on the contralateral side to prevent bilateral damage to the vagus nerve [19]. In our patients, surgery was carried out using a straightforward trans-cervical approach as the tumor, despite its large size, did not extend significantly upwards or invade the skull base. During surgery, only minimal disruption of normal anatomical structures occurred, limited to the resection of the digastric and styloid muscles and rupture of the styloid process. There was no need for a midline mandibulotomy, temporal bone removal, or posterior fossa craniotomy. Complete removal of the tumor while preserving the integrity of the involved vagus nerve was not feasible, as the neoplasm was encapsulating the nerve. These findings align with literature reports, which indicate that nerve-sparing procedures can be successfully performed in only 5–8 % of patients [20]. In comparison, techniques aimed at preserving facial nerve function during vestibular schwannoma resection—particularly those performed without electrocautery—have demonstrated up to a 95 % rate of postoperative hearing improvement. For benign tumors, neurosurgeons should therefore strive for optimal surgical outcomes by prioritizing maximal tumor removal while making every effort to preserve nerve function whenever possible [21]. Nerve-sparing procedures, however, are not associated with a higher risk of tumor recurrence or improved functional outcomes, as preserving anatomical integrity does not necessarily guarantee functional preservation [22]. Up to 30–40 % of head and neck paragangliomas are associated with germline mutations, most commonly in the SDH gene complex, and may also occur with syndromes such as MEN2 or VHL. Genetic counseling and testing are therefore advised, particularly in young or familial cases. In our case, although the tumor appeared sporadic, the patient was counseled regarding genetic testing for surveillance and family risk assessment [23].

Although complete surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for vagal paragangliomas, the procedure carries a considerable risk of postoperative morbidity, particularly involving cranial nerves and vascular structures. However, in our case, the postoperative course was uneventful, with the patient showing stable recovery without any neurological or vascular complications. The patient was monitored for airway safety, swallowing, and vocal cord function, with neurological and laryngoscopic assessments performed as needed. She recovered without complications or distress and regained strength. She was discharged on the third postoperative day, with plans for regular imaging and long-term cranial nerve surveillance.

This case report is limited by its single-patient nature, which may not fully represent the broader spectrum of vagal paraganglioma presentations and outcomes. Additionally, genetic testing was not performed, which could have provided further insight into the tumor's etiology and potential for recurrence. Finally, the absence of a comparison with other treatment modalities, such as radiation therapy, restricts our ability to determine the most optimal treatment for similar cases.

Conclusion

Vagal paragangliomas, though rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of progressively enlarging neck masses. This case reinforces the importance of thorough clinical evaluation, radiological imaging, and FNAC in establishing a diagnosis. Surgical excision remains the mainstay of treatment for resectable tumors, despite the potential risk of cranial nerve and vascular complications. Our report adds to the existing literature by documenting a rare presentation and its successful surgical management, thereby contributing to the collective understanding of these uncommon tumors.
