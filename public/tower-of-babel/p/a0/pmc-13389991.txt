Catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) was frequently deferred during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic1; however, the safety of ablation in patients with COVID-19 identified during hospitalization remains unclear. Using the Japanese Registry of All Cardiac and Vascular Diseases–Diagnosis Procedure Combination (JROAD-DPC) database, we compared in-hospital outcomes after AF ablation according to COVID-19 status. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines and was approved by the institutional review board of the National Hospital Organization Higashihiroshima Medical Center (approval no. 2024-8). An opt-out option was provided on the institutional Web site.

Of the 175,215 patients undergoing AF ablation between April 2020 and March 2023, 190 (0.11%) had documented COVID-19 during hospitalization. Baseline characteristics were largely similar between patients with and without COVID-19 (age 68.5 ± 12 years vs 67.9 ± 10.6 years; weight 65.3 ± 15.2 kg vs 65.8 ± 14.6 kg; male sex 67.4% vs 68.5%; CHADS2 score 1.5 ± 1.0 vs 1.4 ± 1.0; chronic pulmonary disease 2.1% vs 2.4%). However, congestive heart failure was more prevalent (52.6% vs 45.2%) and the mean hospital bed count was lower (511 ± 223 vs 575 ± 257) among patients with COVID-19.

Cardiac tamponade, pneumonia, and in-hospital death were more frequent in patients with COVID-19, whereas stroke and thromboembolism were similar between groups (Table 1). In multivariable analyses, COVID-19 positivity was independently associated with the primary composite outcome, defined as in-hospital death, thromboembolic or ischemic events, pneumonia, intensive care unit admission, respiratory support, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation use (odds ratio [OR] 2.41; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.60–3.61). Hypertension (OR 1.17; 95% CI 1.13–1.22), renal disease (OR 1.24; 95% CI 1.14–1.35), chronic pulmonary disease (OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.26–1.56), older age (OR 1.04 per 10-year increase; 95% CI 1.02–1.06 per 10-year increase), and body mass index < 18 kg/m2 (OR 1.16; 95% CI 1.06–1.26) were also positively associated with the composite outcome. Patients with COVID-19 had longer hospital stays (13.4 ± 18.7 days vs 5.0 ± 3.7 days; P < .0001) and higher in-hospital costs ($22,865 ± $10,444 vs $17,954 ± $3298; P < .0001).

Patients undergoing AF ablation are generally considered relatively healthy, and baseline clinical characteristics were largely comparable between groups. Nevertheless, those with COVID-19 experienced worse in-hospital outcomes. Prior studies have identified AF as a cardiovascular condition associated with adverse outcomes among patients with COVID-19,2 and the present findings extend this observation to patients undergoing elective procedures.

Because COVID-19 infection was identified at some point during the index hospitalization, the temporal relationship between infection and the ablation procedure could not be determined. Some patients may have had recent or unrecognized infection before admission, whereas others may have acquired COVID-19 during prolonged hospitalization after procedural complications. This uncertainty is important when interpreting the observed associations, as higher rates of complications such as cardiac tamponade or pneumothorax may partly reflect reverse causation. At the same time, the findings highlight the potential risk of in-hospital COVID-19 acquisition during elective invasive procedures.

Although COVID-19 has been associated with increased thromboembolic risk,3 no excess thromboembolic events were observed in this cohort. Routine and intensive periprocedural anticoagulation used during AF ablation may have mitigated COVID-19–related thrombotic risk.4

These findings have implications for procedural decision making during current or future pandemics. Even in clinically stable populations, the coexistence of elective invasive procedures and infectious disease exposure may be associated with unfavorable in-hospital outcomes.

This study has several limitations, including its observational design, reliance on administrative data, lack of detailed clinical information such as the timing of infection relative to the procedure, and the small number of patients with COVID-19.

In conclusion, COVID-19 infection identified during hospitalization for elective AF ablation was uncommon but was associated with worse in-hospital outcomes.
