Diagnostic Yield and Clinical Utility of Coronary Angiography Versus Coronary Function Testing in Women With Angina and Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries
Authors: Natasha Cigarroa, Nida Latif, Marah Maayah, Arshjot Khokhar, Steffne Kunnirickal, Alexandra Schwann, Kaitlin R. Maciejewski, Natalija Odanovic, Carolyn M. Mazure, Erica Spatz, Steven Pfau, Alexandra Lansky, Samit M. Shah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApproximately 50% of women referred for invasive coronary angiography have angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries, which includes coronary microvascular dysfunction, vasospastic angina, and other vasomotor disorders. We sought to determine the real‐world diagnostic yield of invasive coronary angiography and coronary function testing in women with angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries.Methods and ResultsFrom 2018 to 2023, we enrolled 198 women who underwent either coronary angiography (CA) alone (n=99) or coronary function testing (CFT; n=99). Mean±SD age was 62±10 years (CA alone) compared with 57±10 years (CFT). Coronary angiography was interpreted as nonobstructive coronary artery disease more frequently after CA alone (79% versus 52%). Of the women who underwent CFT, 82% (N=81) were found to have vasomotor disorders, including coronary microvascular dysfunction (27%), vasospastic angina (32%), mixed coronary microvascular dysfunction/vasospastic angina (16%), endothelial dysfunction (10%; without spasm), elevated resting flow (2%), or symptomatic myocardial bridging (4%). Compared with women undergoing CA alone, medications were changed more frequently after CFT at 24 hours (41% versus 65%; P=0.001) and between 24 hours and 30 days (30% versus 44%; P=0.04) with intensification of antianginal therapy (79% versus 92%; P<0.0001) and increased use of calcium channel blockers (36% versus 63%; P<0.0001).ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that women presenting with suspected ischemic heart disease undergoing CA alone only received an anatomic diagnosis, whereas >80% of women undergoing CFT received a specific diagnosis of a coronary vasomotor disorder and greater intensification of antianginal therapy.

Stable ischemic heart disease (SIHD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in women. 1 As many as 50% to 70% of women who undergo clinically indicated angiography are found to have nonobstructive coronary arteries compared with 30% to 50% seen in their male counterparts. 2 Because of sex differences in vascular biology, the prevalence of obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) is lower for women in all age groups compared with men, but women are more likely to present with chest pain and anginal equivalents. 3 , 4 Angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) is an umbrella term used to describe a mismatch in the demand‐supply of coronary artery blood flow in the absence of flow‐limiting stenosis. 2 The 2 primary subtypes of ANOCA include vasospastic angina and coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), but other syndromes, such as symptomatic myocardial bridging and elevated resting flow, have been increasingly recognized. 5 , 6 Vasospastic angina is defined as symptomatic angina with an associated vasomotor disorder, including endothelial dysfunction, coronary vasospasm, and/or microvascular vasospasm (Data S1). Although noninvasive and invasive testing for diagnosing ANOCA are recommended by the 2021 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association chest pain guidelines and the 2023 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines for management of chronic coronary disease, many patients do not undergo further diagnostic evaluation. 7 , 8 , 9

In a patient self‐reported assessment, over a third of women with ANOCA were found to live with symptoms without a diagnosis for >3 years. 10 Underdiagnosis of ANOCA may lead to increased morbidity, adverse cardiovascular events, and higher health care costs. 2 , 11 Recently, the British Heart Foundation Coronary Microvascular Angina (CorMicA) trial demonstrated a significant benefit in patient‐reported outcomes (including angina and quality of life) with medical therapy stratified by the underlying endotype for patients with ANOCA. 12 However, in clinical practice, ANOCA is often not diagnosed, which precludes the implementation of targeted medical therapy. 13 To describe the real‐world management of women with ANOCA, we evaluated the diagnostic yield of invasive coronary function testing (CFT) in comparison with coronary angiography (CA) alone, and changes in medical therapy following the procedure in both groups.

Methods

Deidentified data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request pending approval from Yale University Institutional Review Board.

Study Population

Participants were consecutively selected from a local registry of women with known or suspected ischemic heart disease who underwent clinically indicated invasive coronary angiography with coronary function testing at Yale New Haven Hospital between February 2018 and March 2023. We used a purposeful sampling approach to select a comparison group who underwent coronary angiography alone without CFT, referred to as the CA alone cohort. Inclusion criteria were composed of women with cardiac symptoms who presented for clinically indicated cardiac catheterization to evaluate for SIHD. SIHD was defined as patients with suspected cardiac ischemia without unstable symptoms or evidence of an acute coronary syndrome. This included patients with cardiac‐related symptoms, including stable angina, new‐onset angina, dyspnea on exertion, with or without abnormal stress test results, and patients with persistent symptoms after percutaneous coronary intervention despite relief of obstructive coronary artery disease. 14 Exclusion criteria were obstructive coronary artery disease on coronary angiography based on the presence of >50% stenosis or <50% with a fractional flow reserve ≤0.80, or referral for coronary angiography for preoperative testing or evaluation of cardiomyopathy. A waiver of informed consent was obtained from the Yale University Institutional Review Board.

End Point Definition

The primary outcome of interest was the postprocedure diagnosis, as described in the procedure report. For patients undergoing CFT, all invasive physiology findings were recorded and grouped for analysis to compare baseline medications against a final angina‐associated diagnosis (Figure 1; Figure S1). Secondary outcomes included changes of cardiac medications at 24 hours after the procedure and at 30 days. Change to the cardiac medication regimen was defined by either the addition or the subtraction of a medication used to manage cardiac disease. Medications were then stratified by class (preventative, antiplatelet, and antianginal) to compare frequency of use before and after angiography. Preventative therapies included angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and statin therapy. Antiplatelet agents included aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitors. Antianginal therapy was composed of ranolazine, ivabradine, nitrates, calcium channel blockers, and β‐blockers.

Coronary Function Testing

Invasive CFT was performed using a standardized protocol (Data S1). 15 Invasive coronary angiography was followed by acetylcholine testing to assess for endothelial dysfunction/coronary vasospasm, and guidewire‐based coronary thermodilution testing to assess for CMD by deriving the index of microcirculatory resistance and coronary flow reserve. Results were reported in the cardiac catheterization report in the medical record and made available to the patient's primary cardiologist immediately after the procedure. CMD was defined as index of microcirculatory resistance ≥25 and/or coronary flow reserve ≤2.5; epicardial vasospasm was defined as ≥90% visual diameter stenosis after intracoronary administration of acetylcholine with either ischemic electrocardiographic changes or reproduction of the presenting chest pain; microvascular spasm was defined as ≤90% epicardial narrowing after acetylcholine administration but with ischemic electrocardiographic changes and chest pain; endothelial dysfunction was classified as epicardial narrowing >0% and <90% after acetylcholine administration; symptomatic myocardial bridging was defined on the basis of pressure wire assessment of the myocardial bridge during dobutamine administration; and elevated resting flow was defined as coronary flow reserve <2.5 with index of microcirculatory resistance <25 and baseline thermodilution mean transit time <0.50. 6

Clinical Characteristics

We retrospectively collected patient demographics, comorbidities, preprocedure diagnostic testing, procedure details, including postprocedure diagnosis, and postprocedure changes to medications. Data were extracted from the medical record by 2 independent reviewers, (N.C. and N.L.), and discrepancies were resolved by a third adjudicator.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using mean and SD for continuous variables and count and percentage for categorical variables. Analysis of differences in characteristics between cohorts was performed using t‐test for continuous variables, and χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables. Rate ratios were used for the difference in incidence of baseline medication use between groups. Logistic models were used to find association of diagnosis with change in medications at 24 hours, association of baseline medications with diagnosis of CMD, association of baseline medications with diagnosis of vasospastic angina, among the coronary function testing cohort only. Medication classification at baseline, 24 hours, and 30 days was compared between cohorts. The risk ratio was calculated by unconditional maximum likelihood estimation (Wald), and χ2 P values are presented. P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Analysis was preformed using R statistical software (© 2022 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Packages used included MASS, Hmisc, rms, gtsummary, and epitools. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

Results

The study sample included 198 women who were categorized by the diagnostic approach, CA alone (N=99), or CFT (N=99; Figure 1). The mean±SD age was 62±10 years (CA alone) versus 57±10 years (CFT; P=0.001), with no significant difference in racial distribution (White, 79% versus 72%; Black, 14% versus 11%; P=0.4; Table 1). Preprocedure comorbidities of heart failure, diabetes, sleep apnea, depression, anxiety, prior percutaneous coronary intervention, and active smoking did not significantly vary between each group (Table 1). A history of hypertension (76% versus 58%; P=0.008) and family history of coronary artery disease (68% versus 38%; P<0.001) were significantly higher in the CA alone cohort (Table 1). The most common presenting symptoms in both CFT and CA alone were chest pain (76.7% versus 64.6%) and dyspnea on exertion (22.2% versus 27.2%), respectively (Table 1). Preprocedure medications varied across both groups, with increased baseline use of β‐blockers (54% versus 37%; P=0.02) and aspirin (59% versus 42%; P=0.02) in the CA alone group. Increased rates of calcium channel blocker use (26% versus 44%; P=0.007), nitrates (14% versus 37%; P<0.001), and ranolazine (3% versus 12%; P=0.016) were present at baseline in the CFT group (Table 2).

Most patients in both groups had prior stress testing (85% versus 90%), but the result was abnormal more frequently in the CA alone group compared with the CFT group (83% versus 46%; P<0.001), and there was a higher incidence of ischemia in the CA group (70% versus 27%; P<0.001; Table 3). The mean number of prior stress tests (1.5±1.2 versus 1.8±1.1; P=0.079) and emergency department presentations (1.3±2.1 versus 2.6±8.5; P=0.14) was similar in both groups. Inpatient referral for coronary angiography occurred more frequently in the CA alone group (39% versus 16%; P<0.001; Table 3). Procedural characteristics included a higher rate of radial access in the CA alone group compared with the CFT group (96% versus 83%; P=0.003). Coronary angiography showed normal coronary arteries in 21% of CA alone versus 48% in CFT (P<0.001); and nonobstructive coronary artery disease in 79% CA alone versus 52% CFT (P<0.001; Table 3). Additional information, including fractional flow reserve, left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure, and resting full‐cycle ratio, was recorded for the CFT group when available (Table S1). In patients who underwent CFT, 82% of patients (N=81) were found to have coronary vasomotor disorders. In addition to a description of epicardial arteries, postprocedure diagnoses in the CFT cohort included CMD (27%), vasospastic angina (32%), mixed CMD/vasospastic angina (16%), elevated resting flow (2%), and symptomatic myocardial bridging (4%; Figure 1). The most common procedural complication included spasm of the arterial access site, which more frequently occurred in the CA alone group (7%) compared with 1 (1%) in the CFT group. There were no significant differences in the date of death, myocardial infarction, or hospitalization for cardiac cause at 30 days (Table 3). Additionally, CFT provided additional granularity with a variety of overlapping physiological endotypes (Figure S1).

Changes to medications at 24 hours after angiography occurred in 41% of the women in the CA alone groups compared with 65% of the CFT group (P=0.001). At 30 days, the proportion of women undergoing additional medication changes was 30% versus 44% for CA alone compared with CFT (P=0.04), respectively (Figure 2). There was no significant association between preprocedure medical therapy and the postprocedure diagnosis in patients undergoing CFT (Table S2). The clinical utility of CA alone and CFT was assessed in terms of modification of postprocedure medical therapy regimens (Table 4). Preventative therapy in CA alone and CFT did not significantly differ at baseline (67% versus 68%; P=0.88), 24 hours (74% versus 74%; P=1.00), and 30 days (75% versus 78%; P=0.62). Antiplatelet therapy did not significantly differ at baseline (60% versus 46%; P=0.06); the number of patients receiving this class of medications after CA alone was higher at 24 hours (68% versus 47%; P<0.001) and at 30 days (63% versus 46%; P=0.02). Antianginal therapy did not significantly differ at baseline (69% versus 78%; P=0.15), but there was intensification of antianginal therapy in the CFT group compared with CA alone at 24 hours (79% versus 92%; P<0.0001) and at 30 days (79% versus 91%; P=0.02). Specifically, calcium channel blockers were more commonly prescribed after CFT compared with CA alone at 24 hours (36% versus 63%; P<0.0001) and at 30 days (39% versus 63%; P<0.0001; Table 4).

Discussion

In women with suspected SIHD and nonobstructive coronary arteries undergoing invasive coronary angiography, we found that 82% patients who underwent invasive CFT had a coronary vasomotor disorder contributing to a final angina‐related diagnosis. Compared with women who underwent CA alone, those with a CFT‐established diagnosis were more likely to have a change in medical therapy immediately postprocedure and at 30 days, with intensification in antianginal therapy after CFT. There was no association between the preprocedure medication regimen and the eventual diagnosis, suggesting that empiric antianginal therapy before the procedure was not targeted to specific ANOCA endotypes. Our findings suggest that the use of coronary angiography alone (without CFT) in women with SIHD may result in underrecognition and undertreatment of underlying coronary vasomotor disorders. When compared with the CorMicA trial, the frequency of postprocedure medical therapy changes in our real‐world study was slightly lower (48.7% CorMicA control versus 41% CA alone; 87.8% CorMicA intervention versus 65% CFT group). 12 A lack of an actionable diagnosis in patients undergoing CA alone may have led to lower rates of antianginal therapy. Specifically, with a CFT‐confirmed diagnosis, there was greater use of calcium channel blockers, suggesting increased recognition of vasospastic angina, which was present in 32% of our participants. Our results follow those of the recently published British Heart Foundation Coronary Microvascular Function and CT Coronary Angiogram (CorCTCA) trial, where participants were randomized to disclosure of the results of CFT or blinding of the results; there was greater use of antianginal therapy after disclosure of the CFT result (77%) compared with the blinded group (41%), and those authors also reported greater use of calcium channel blockers after disclosure of the CFT results. 21 For preventative therapies, such as statins or angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker, there was no significant difference between groups in our study, CorCTCA, or CorMicA. Although not clearly linked to relief of angina, the prognostic benefit of these therapies in women with ANOCA is currently under investigation in the Women’s Ischemia Trial to Reduce Events in Non‐Obstructive CAD (WARRIOR) trial. 22 Most patients in our analysis had undergone at least 1 stress test, and many had previously been evaluated in the emergency department. Before referral for CA or CFT, most patients were on at least 1 antianginal agent, but there was no correlation between the preprocedure medication regimen and the CFT‐confirmed diagnosis. Because these patients were referred on the basis of clinical indications and with persistent symptoms of angina, this underscores the limited effectiveness of empiric medical therapy in women with suspected ischemic heart disease.

This study was underpowered for cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction and death, which have a low incidence in this population. In our study, there were no occurrences of myocardial infarction or death in either study group at 30 days (Table 3), and the rate of rehospitalization for cardiac causes did not vary significantly. Additionally, as a retrospective study, we were not able to formally assess angina burden at baseline or relief of angina after CA or CFT. Ongoing prognostic studies, such as the Determining the Cause of Coronary Vasomotor Disorders in Patients With Ischemia and No Obstructive Coronary Artery Disease (DISCOVER INOCA) multicenter prospective registry, will further define the long‐term hazard of ANOCA from both the standpoint of patient‐reported outcomes and “hard” cardiovascular end points, 23 and future randomized trials should focus on determining the most effective diagnostic and treatment strategies for women with angina.

Should CFT become the standard of care for women with suspected ANOCA? Our results are hypothesis generating and suggest that both the diagnostic yield and downstream care of invasive coronary angiography can be improved with the added granularity of a CFT‐defined vasomotor diagnosis. In real‐world practice, coronary vasomotor disorders are overlooked and often misdiagnosed as noncardiac symptoms, which can affect treatment satisfaction and quality of life. 21 Furthermore, access to CFT is currently limited by both operator expertise and resource scarcity. Acetylcholine, a key component for the diagnosis of coronary vasospasm and endothelial dysfunction, is not widely available. 24 This disparity predominately affects women and highlights the importance for increasing access to both invasive and noninvasive methods of diagnostic testing. Noninvasive test modalities, such as positron emission tomography myocardial perfusion imaging or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, are alternatives to invasive testing and should be used routinely in the diagnostic evaluation of women with suspected ANOCA, especially to evaluate for CMD.

ANOCA is more prevalent in women than men, and the pretest probability of finding obstructive CAD is lower in women in every age group. A sex‐specific diagnostic approach would prioritize the use of CA with CFT in women who are unlikely to have obstructive CAD to help determine the underlying diagnosis and guide medical therapy. The 2023 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines for chronic coronary disease have a grade 2a recommendation for invasive wire‐based testing and provocative testing for patients with documented ischemia on stress testing and suspected ANOCA to guide stratified medical therapy. 8 However, in our sample, only 27% of the women undergoing CFT were documented to have ischemia with stress testing, which is similar to prior reports from other centers. 25 , 26 Currently, invasive CFT is the gold standard diagnostic study for patients with ANOCA, but advances in noninvasive imaging will be helpful to expand the recognition of vasomotor disorders and guide medical therapy. 9

Limitations

We note several limitations to this study. The decision to refer patients for invasive coronary function testing as opposed to angiography alone suggests an inherent selection bias that is difficult to account for given the study's retrospective design. However, all of these patients were cared for in the same health system, and the cohorts were demographically similar. We hope these data will set the foundation for future randomized controlled trials.

The results of diagnostic coronary angiograms were collected using the procedure report by the performing operator, and core laboratory analysis was not performed to confirm the absence of significant epicardial CAD. Furthermore, there may have been physiologically significant epicardial coronary artery disease in the CA alone group that was not appreciated during angiographic assessment. Although our CFT program is decentralized and titration of medical therapy is determined by referring providers, it is possible that patients in the CFT group were more closely followed up before referral for CFT. As a result, medication changes after procedure could reflect a more symptomatic or more intensively treated population. The lack of formal angina assessment is a limitation, but patient‐reported outcome measures are not routinely assessed in clinical practice, and there is no dedicated measure for women with SIHD or suspected ANOCA. These pilot data should inform a future prospective study where a formal symptom assessment (inclusive of symptoms that women with ischemic heart disease may experience) will be performed at baseline and after changes in antianginal therapy. Finally, the study was not designed or powered to prospectively evaluate outcomes such as repeat hospital presentation or major adverse cardiovascular events.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that women presenting with SIHD undergoing coronary angiography alone frequently received a diagnosis of nonobstructive CAD, whereas those undergoing CFT were frequently diagnosed with coronary vasomotor disorders. CA alone results in fewer changes in medical therapy after procedure and at 30 days compared with women who underwent CFT. Prior studies have shown improved outcomes with stratified medical therapy for patients with ANOCA, and our study demonstrates targeted medication changes occurred more frequently after a CFT‐confirmed diagnosis. Ongoing studies will help determine the safety of CFT and long‐term prognosis of vasomotor phenotypes, and future work will evaluate the impact of CFT‐guided medical therapy on angina relief and long‐term outcomes in women.
