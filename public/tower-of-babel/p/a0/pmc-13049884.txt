Targeting epigenetic networks to overcome cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer: from mechanisms to clinical translation
Authors: Maoyan Tang, Yu Wang, Yaya Xie, Jia Li, Danni Ding, Chan Li, Qiaochu Chen, Fengjuan Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer patients with platinum resistance face a dismal five-year survival rate of only 30%, owing to the limited efficacy of current therapeutic options. Existing research often focuses on individual epigenetic modifications, which hampers a systematic understanding of resistance mechanisms and their clinical translation. To address this, our review integrates current evidence to propose an “Epigenetic Resistance Stability Network” model, which comprises three core components: (i) four foundational layers, each with distinct roles in cisplatin resistance. These include DNA methylation (e.g., SFRP5 hypermethylation activating the Wnt pathway), non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), RNA modifications, and histone modifications, which govern gene expression, post-transcriptional regulation, RNA function, and chromatin architecture, respectively. (ii) three interactive networks: “DNA methylation/miRNA feedback loops,” “lncRNA/histone modification cascades,” and “circRNA/m6A synergistic stabilization.” These networks sustain the resistant phenotype by interlinking key nodes across the four foundational layers. (iii) a theoretical framework encompassing two parts: the “Epigenetic Compensation Network” theory (explaining how tumors evade single-target therapies) and the “Epigenetic Temporal Regulation” model (outlining resistance progression: “miRNA response → methylation consolidation → chromatin stabilization”). Building on this model, we outline a translational roadmap involving “biomarker validation (e.g., SFRP5 methylation, circITGB6),” “combination therapy development,” and “personalized intervention,” offering novel insights for overcoming cisplatin resistance and improving patient outcomes in ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is one of the three most common gynecological malignant tumors, with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSC) being the most common subtype. Globally, there are approximately 1.314 million new cases of ovarian cancer each year, resulting in 207,000 deaths annually, making it the leading cause of death among gynecological malignant tumors [1]. The current first-line treatment regimen consists of cytoreductive surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy [2], cisplatin (cis-[PtCl₂(NH₃)₂]) inhibits transcription and replication by forming DNA adducts and induces apoptosis [3]; however, approximately 85% of patients with epithelial ovarian cancer experience recurrence within six months of treatment (a condition defined as “cisplatin resistance”) [4, 5]. Although novel therapeutic strategies and targeted therapies, including intraperitoneal administration, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, and anti-angiogenic agents, have been introduced, the five-year survival rate for patients with advanced ovarian cancer (OC) remains approximately 30%. This underscores the imperative to conduct an in-depth analysis of the mechanisms underlying drug resistance [6]. Currently, clinical prediction of platinum resistance relies on empirical assessment and lacks precision biomarkers; in terms of treatment, single targeted drugs are prone to tumor compensatory drug resistance, and there is a scarcity of combination regimens. Therefore, exploring translatable biomarkers and combination strategies from epigenetic networks is the key to overcoming clinical dilemmas. An in-depth analysis of drug resistance mechanisms is urgently needed. Known drug resistance mechanisms include reduced drug accumulation, enhanced DNA damage repair, and apoptosis pathway inactivation [7]. Epigenetic dysregulation acts as an important upstream driver of these mechanisms [7]. Cisplatin’s anti-tumor activity has been extensively studied in various tumor cell lines and experimental animal models [8]. Thus, this review uses cisplatin as a model to summarize the epigenetic mechanisms underlying cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer.

While DNA sequences constitute the basis of genetic information, their faithful execution requires regulation by an additional layer of signaling information, the study of which is termed “epigenetics” [9, 10]. This concept has undergone several redefinitions since Conrad Waddington first defined it in 1942 as phenotypic variation independent of genotypic changes [11]. The modern definition has since been refined to “mechanisms that maintain stable differences in gene expression states under an identical genetic background” [9]. In HGSC, the epigenetic landscape—when compared to normal tissues or chemosensitive tumors—is characterized by a paradoxical coexistence of genome-wide hypomethylation and promoter-specific hypermethylation, a profile strongly linked to cisplatin resistance [12, 13]. The core regulatory mechanisms of epigenetics include DNA methylation, whereby the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) family transfers methyl groups to the fifth carbon of cytosine residues, leading to promoter hypermethylation, gene silencing, and altered function of tumor-related genes [14]. The realm of non-coding RNA (ncRNA) regulation encompasses microRNAs (miRNAs), which inhibit gene expression by targeting the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) of mRNAs, as well as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs) that regulate gene expression by competitively binding to miRNAs. Furthermore, RNA modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) and 5-methylcytosine (m5C), are dynamically regulated by “writer” (e.g., METTL3, NSUN2), “eraser” (e.g., ALKBH5), and “reader” proteins (e.g., IGF2BP1/2, YTHDF1/2, YBX1), which modulate RNA splicing, stability, and translation [15–17]. Additionally, histone acetylation—regulated by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and deacetylases (HDACs)—is associated with chromatin relaxation and transcriptional activation upon increased levels. In contrast, histone methylation, controlled by histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and demethylases (HDMTs), typically leads to chromatin compaction and transcriptional repression when elevated [18] (Fig. 1).

Taking cisplatin resistance as a model, this review transcends the limitations of prior studies focusing solely on a single epigenetic modification. It integrates multi-dimensional mechanisms including DNA methylation, ncRNAs, histone modifications, and RNA modifications, systematically dissects three categories of cross-regulatory networks, and innovatively proposes the “theory of epigenetic compensatory networks” and the “temporal regulatory pattern”. The study aims to identify clinically translatable predictive biomarkers and novel combination therapeutic strategies based on network regulation to overcome drug resistance.

DNA methylation-mediated gene expression regulation

Remodeling of DNA methylation patterns (hypermethylation and hypomethylation) is closely associated with cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer through the bidirectional regulation of gene expression.

Hypermethylation silences tumor suppressor genes

Hypermethylation of gene promoters is one of the key mechanisms driving drug resistance, and DNMT inhibitors (azacitidine, decitabine) can reverse abnormal methylation to enhance cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity [19]. In ovarian cancer, SULF1 is epigenetically silenced through hypermethylation of CpG sites in its Exon 1A region—a process mediated by DNMTs—coupled with increased histone H3 methylation [20]. This silencing consequently leads to reduced sensitivity of tumor cells to cisplatin [20]. Clinical data from the study demonstrated that the chemotherapy response rate was 90% in patients with high SULF1 expression (n = 30), significantly higher than the rate of 63% observed in patients with weak/moderate expression. Importantly, this epigenetic silencing could be reversed by treatment with DNMT inhibitors such as 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine [20]. Secreted Frizzled-Related Protein 5 (SFRP5) loses its function due to promoter hypermethylation. This loss activates the Wnt pathway and AKT2 signaling, thereby driving epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and chemoresistance [21]. The pro-apoptotic factor FBXO32 loses its pro-apoptotic function due to hypermethylation-induced silencing. Conversely, its overexpression reduces the cisplatin IC₅₀ from 1.447 µg/mL to 0.892 µg/mL, thereby reversing chemoresistance [22]. Analysis of clinical ovarian cancer samples (n = 96) revealed that the methylation frequency of FBXO32 was 29.3% in patients with advanced disease, and this methylation status could independently predict poor survival [22]. In HGSC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, decitabine (DAC) significantly demethylates 10.6% of CpG sites. This demethylation, in turn, activates the CSK gene—a negative regulator of the Src family kinase pathway. Clinical data show that high CSK expression is associated with better prognosis in HGSC patients, suggesting it may serve as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target [23]. In addition, hypermethylation-induced silencing of genes such as Tribbles 2 (TRIB2) and E3 ubiquitin ligase Mahogunin Ring Finger 1 (MGRN1) contributes to drug resistance by regulating the cell cycle and apoptosis [24–26].

In terms of biomarker potential, PAX9 methylation levels in the drug-resistant cell line OVCAR3-R were six times higher than those in the sensitive strain (60% vs. 10%). In clinical settings, high PAX9 methylation (≥ 29.74%) in platinum-resistant patients was significantly associated with shorter overall survival [27]; FKBP1B methylation decreased by 13% after treatment with 5-Aza, and expression was restored. The IC50 of cisplatin decreased from 2 µg/mL to 1.3 µg/mL, with sensitivity approaching that of sensitive strains [27]. In addition, the hypermethylated status of genes such as HPS4, LZTFL1, FARS2, SGMS2, ABHD2, and RNF144A can serve as biomarkers for clinical staging of ovarian cancer [19]. Such hypermethylation events target tumor suppressor pathways and are important drivers of primary resistance.

Hypomethylation activates drug resistance genes

Global hypomethylation (compared with cisplatin-sensitive HGSC cells) and local hypomethylation at the promoters of drug-resistant genes act synergistically. These two forms of hypomethylation drive cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer by activating the expression of drug resistance-related genes. Among the 1488 differentially methylated sites in HGSC ascites-resistant cells, 84% were hypomethylated in intergenic regions and introns, challenging the traditional notion that promoter methylation is dominant [12]; studies have demonstrated that high-concentration cisplatin (7 µM) can induce differential expression of 387 genes, which are enriched in DNA damage repair pathways. The IL6/KLF4 network integrates epigenetic remodeling and transcriptional changes, thereby providing a novel mechanistic explanation for cisplatin resistance [12]. Hypomethylation of CpG sites within the MSX1 gene reduces its expression, impairing cisplatin-induced apoptosis and p21 activation, thereby promoting cisplatin resistance in HGSC [28]. The gene encoding Myelin and Lymphocyte protein (MAL) is highly expressed in EOC due to low promoter methylation and is significantly associated with cisplatin resistance, making it a potential marker for resistance [29]. Drug-resistant gene TMEM88 promoter hypomethylation upregulates its expression, escapes cisplatin toxicity by downregulating cyclin D1, c-Myc, and inhibiting the Wnt pathway to induce cell dormancy [30]. In addition, promoter hypomethylation of the GSN gene is associated with high expression of its product, plasma gelsolin (pGSN), and chemoresistance [31].

5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) is the primary oxidized methyl modification generated by the oxidation of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) via TET family DNA dioxygenases [32]. In contrast, 5mC primarily mediates gene repression (i.e., methylation) by recruiting methyl-binding proteins, whereas 5hmC exerts gene regulatory effects through mechanisms such as regulating chromatin accessibility and inhibiting the recruitment of 5mC-binding proteins [33]. Whole-genome siRNA screening revealed that TET2 knockdown significantly enhances resistance to PARPi (such as olaparib) and cisplatin in BRCA2-deficient cells; TET2 promotes the degradation of stalled replication forks by generating 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), and its absence protects replication fork stability and induces cross-resistance to cisplatin, a process associated with APE1 recruitment. This finding suggests that 5hmC can serve as a resistance biomarker, while TET2 activation or APE1 inhibition may provide potential targets for reversing chemotherapy resistance [34] (Fig. 2).

In summary, although DNA methylation can bidirectionally regulate drug resistance-related genes and establish a foundation for resistance at the gene expression level, DNMT inhibitors—which target DNA methylation exclusively—reverse abnormalities at only 10.6% of CpG sites [23]. This suggests that DNA methylation may require crosstalk with other epigenetic layers to maintain stable drug resistance, potentially through the formation of feedback loops with miRNAs to further reinforce the drug-resistant phenotype (see sectionThe “cascade amplification” of LncRNA and histone modifications for details).

NcRNA mediated regulation at the post-transcriptional level

NcRNA participates in ovarian cancer cisplatin resistance by regulating apoptosis, autophagy, signaling pathways, and DNA damage repair, forming a complex regulatory network.

MiRNA regulation

MiRNAs exert bidirectional regulatory effects on cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer by targeting apoptosis- and autophagy-related molecules, as well as core nodes of signaling pathways [17].

MiRNAs: bidirectional resistance regulators

Downregulated mirna: loss of antagonistic inhibition

High expression of anti-apoptotic proteins is an important cause of cisplatin resistance. MiR-130a [35] and miR-137 [36] can specifically bind to the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) of the anti-apoptotic protein XIAP, reducing its expression level and enhancing cisplatin-induced apoptosis. Experimental data showed that transfection of miR-137 mimics significantly increased the apoptosis rate of SKOV3 cells, whereas A2780 cells with miR-137 knockdown exhibited a decreased apoptosis rate. Furthermore, miR-137 is downregulated in ovarian cancer tissues and exhibited a negative correlation with XIAP protein levels, suggesting it may serve as a potential biomarker for chemosensitivity [36]. In addition, miR-146a-5p [37], miR-142-5p [38], miR-335-5p [39] reverse drug resistance phenotypes by targeting anti-apoptotic genes such as baculoviral IAP repeat-containing 3 (BIRC3), B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2), and BCL2-like 2 (BCL2L2).

Beyond the aforementioned mechanisms, miR-29c-3p [40], EGR1-miR-152 [41], miR-133a [42], hsa-miR-105-1 [43], miR-7 [44], miR-199a [45], miR-146b [46], miR-370 [47], and miR-18a-5p [48] can also contribute to cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer by targeting other distinct related proteins, as summarized in Table 1.

Overexpressed mirna: actively promotes drug resistance

Some miRNAs are abnormally overexpressed in ovarian cancer, promoting cisplatin resistance by suppressing tumor suppressor genes or drug resistance-related pathways, which is the opposite of their traditional tumor suppressor function.

MiR-21-3p is overexpressed in ovarian cancer cells, where it specifically binds to the 3’-UTR of the NAV3 gene to suppress its expression, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways and enhancing cisplatin resistance [49]. Clinical data show that among 101 late-stage patients, those with high miR-21 expression had significantly shorter survival times. Mechanistically, miR-21 deficiency can relieve translational inhibition of the tumor suppressor protein PDCD4 and simultaneously downregulate the anti-apoptotic protein c-IAP2, thereby promoting cell apoptosis and reversing cisplatin resistance [50]. In platinum-resistant ovarian cancer cells, miR-483-3p expression is significantly upregulated, inhibiting its expression by targeting the 3’UTR of PRKCA mRNA, thereby helping cells evade cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity [51].

Such overexpressed miRNAs promote drug resistance phenotypes by actively inhibiting tumor suppressor pathways or enhancing detoxification mechanisms. Their high expression may serve as potential biomarkers for poor clinical prognosis. Therapeutic agents targeting these miRNAs, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), could represent novel strategies to reverse drug resistance (Table 1).

MiRNAs influence cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer through related signaling pathways

The association between miRNAs and cisplatin resistance may involve changes in multiple intracellular signaling pathways.

Abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is a key mechanism in miRNA-mediated cisplatin resistance [52], in which PTEN, as a core negative regulator of the pathway50, becomes a common target of multiple miRNAs [53]. For example: miR-93 [54], miR-214 [55], miR-205-5p [56], miR-4461 [57], miR-625-3p [58], etc., inhibit its translation by targeting the 3’-UTR of PTEN, thereby releasing the inhibition of the AKT/mTOR pathway, leading to sustained activation of the pathway and inhibition of cisplatin-induced apoptosis. The dual regulatory mechanism of miR-625-3p: it not only targets PTEN to activate the pathway but also suppresses apoptosis by downregulating the pro-apoptotic protein BAX and upregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, thereby inhibiting the apoptosis response from multiple dimensions and enhancing drug resistance [58]. In addition, let-7d-5p directly targets HMGA1, relieving its inhibition of the p53 pathway, reducing the ability of cells to form colonies after cisplatin treatment, and significantly increasing the sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to cisplatin, providing a new direction for reversing chemotherapy resistance [59]. Human MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (hMSC-EVs) deliver miR-18a-5p, which targets and inhibits the expression of nucleus accumbens-associated protein 1 (NACC1). This inhibition downregulates the AKT/mTOR pathway, thereby reversing cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer [60]. Clinically, low expression of miR-18a-5p in patient tissues and serum is associated with shorter survival, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker [60].

Abnormal activation of the Ras-MAPK pathway is another key driver of cisplatin resistance. MiR-634 directly targets the core molecule CCND1 and downstream components of the Ras-MAPK pathway, reducing their expression levels. After overexpression of miR-634 in cisplatin-resistant cells, cell viability significantly decreased upon treatment with 80 µM and 125 µM cisplatin [61]. Patient-derived resistant primary cells treated with miR-634 exhibited reduced viability upon treatment with 15 µM and 30 µM cisplatin, and concurrently enhanced sensitivity to carboplatin and doxorubicin, suggesting that the miR-634 delivery system could serve as a combination therapy strategy to reverse multidrug resistance [61]. The S100 calcium-binding protein A7 (S100A7) is highly expressed due to low promoter methylation, promoting pathway activation and cell proliferation and invasion by activating Ras. miR-330-5p targets the 3’-UTR of S100A7 to inhibit its expression, blocking this activation process and weakening the drug-resistant phenotype [62].

NF-κB drives cisplatin resistance by forming a feedback loop with non-coding RNA. NF-κB-p65 binds to the miR-429 promoter to promote its transcription, and miR-429 inhibits the calcium-sensing receptor (CASR), forming a positive feedback loop between p65, miR-429, and CASR, thereby enhancing cisplatin resistance [63]. NF-κB binds to the miR-200b/c promoter to promote its transcription. miR-200b/c targets mortalin to inhibit Akt/Erk phosphorylation, forming a negative feedback loop that partially reverses drug resistance [64]. miR-874-3p directly downregulates the anti-apoptotic protein XIAP, blocking the NF-κB/Survivin pathway’s drug resistance signal [65]. In a study of 132 cases of primary ovarian tumors, it was found that the expression levels of miR-141 were significantly elevated in patients with non-serous ovarian cancer who were resistant to treatment. The high expression of miR-141 was found to induce cisplatin resistance by inhibiting KEAP1 and activating NF-κB. This suggests that combining NF-κB inhibitors with platinum-based therapies could reverse resistance in patients with high miR-141 expression [66].

Abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway drives the malignant phenotype and cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, with miRNAs participating in regulation by targeting key molecules in the pathway. As reported by Zhengyi Ruan et al., downregulation of miR-181c and upregulation of MEST in ovarian tumor tissues can predict cisplatin resistance. Notably, extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) can efficiently deliver miR-181c to target the MEST/Wnt pathway, thereby reversing drug resistance with low toxicity and enabling precise therapeutic translation [67]. The deletion of the RNA-binding protein RBMS3 on chromosome 3p confers cisplatin resistance to epithelial ovarian cancer cells, and its anticancer mechanism may involve the activation of the miR-126-5p/β-catenin/CREB-binding protein (CBP) signaling pathway, providing evidence for chromosome deletion as a predictor of cisplatin resistance [68]. The tumor suppressor gene miR-506-3p not only inhibits EMT but also targets Zeste homologue 2 (EZH2) to block its activation of β-catenin, thereby enhancing the response of serous ovarian cancer cells to PARP inhibitors and cisplatin [69]. MiR-219-5p blocks protective autophagy mediated by the Wnt pathway by downregulating HMGA2, forming dual effects (pathway inhibition and autophagy blockade) for reversing drug resistance [70].

In addition, other pathways are associated with cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer. For example, STAT3 binds to the miR-216a promoter to promote its transcription, and miR-216a targets PTEN to form a “STAT3/miR-216a/PTEN” pro-resistance loop, which can serve as a screening marker for CDDP treatment non-response [71]. MiR-509-3p and miR-509-3-5p synergistically target YAP1 to reduce its expression and enhance cisplatin-induced apoptosis. Furthermore, following their co-transfection in ES-2 cells, the cisplatin IC₅₀ decreased to 27.6% of the control level, accompanied by an increased apoptosis rate. Both miRNAs are downregulated in recurrent clear cell ovarian cancer and exhibit a negative correlation with YAP1, indicating that they contribute to drug resistance by regulating YAP1 [72].

MiRNAs form a complex drug resistance regulatory network by targeting core pathways including PI3K/AKT, Ras-MAPK, NF-κB, and Wnt/β-catenin, as well as key nodes such as PTEN, CASR, and XIAP. This provides theoretical support for “miRNA-targeted therapy combined with chemotherapy”. Future research should focus on exploring the value of miRNAs as clinical screening biomarkers and the translational application of exosome delivery systems (Fig. 3).

MiRNAs regulate the reduction of platinum drug accumulation in cells

Reduced intracellular accumulation of platinum-based drugs is a classic mechanism of drug resistance. miRNAs influence cisplatin concentration by regulating drug efflux pumps (ABC transporters) and detoxification systems (GST/GSH) [73]. The ATP-binding cassette subfamily (ABC subfamily) reduces intracellular drug concentrations through efflux [74], such as MDR1 (P-glycoprotein 1) encoded by the ABCB1 gene, which can pump out cisplatin through ATP hydrolysis [75]; whereas overexpression of glutathione S-transferase (GST) and GSH induces resistance by inactivating cisplatin [76].

MiR-186 [77] and miR-133b [78] reduce cisplatin inactivation by regulating GST-π. Multiple studies support a direct association between ABC and acquired cisplatin resistance transporters. MiR-514 [79] inhibits members of the ABC family, such as ABCA1 and ABCA10, thereby increasing intracellular cisplatin concentration to restore sensitivity. MiR-128 targets ABCC5 and Bmi-1. After overexpression in SKOV3/CP drug-resistant cells, the cisplatin IC50 significantly decreased. In nude mouse experiments, cisplatin concentration in tumors increased from 1903.7 to 3709.5 ng Pt/g, with ABCC5 and Bmi-1 expression reduced by 46% and 39%, respectively [80]. In the cisplatin-resistant cell line A2780/DDP, high expression of miR-130a promotes resistance through dual mechanisms: upregulating MDR1 (drug efflux) and downregulating PTEN (pathway activation) [81]. The above mechanism reveals that miRNAs, through precise regulation of the efflux and detoxification systems, have become key targets for reversing cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer.

MiRNAs regulate DNA damage repair

Enhanced DNA damage repair capacity is a key mechanism underlying cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, and miRNAs regulate this process by targeting core molecules in the repair pathway. RAD51, as a core protein of homologous recombination (HR), assembles nucleoproteins onto single-stranded DNA and catalyzes homologous sequence exchange to complete repair [82]. MiR-506 inhibits RAD51 translation by binding to its 3’-UTR, reducing RAD51 protein levels to block HR repair and enhance cisplatin sensitivity [83]. MiR-98-5p reduces miR-152 production by inhibiting Dicer1, thereby relieving the enhancing effect of miR-152 on RAD51 mRNA stability and translation, forming a cascade pathway that promotes HR repair and drug resistance [84]. In tissues from 84 patients with EOC, the expression of let-7e in the chemotherapy-resistant group was significantly lower than that in the chemotherapy-sensitive group. let-7e downregulates BRCA1 and RAD51 to inhibit DNA double-strand break repair; after let-7e overexpression, the cisplatin IC50 values in A2780 and C13K cells decreased to 3.66 µM and 9.66 µM, respectively. The area under the curve (AUC) of let-7e for predicting platinum-taxane resistance reached 0.826 (with a sensitivity of 82.4% and specificity of 82.9%), which was superior to that of BRCA1 and RAD51. Moreover, let-7e was identified as an independent predictive factor (OR = 19.696, P = 0.001), suggesting that it could serve as a potential biomarker for predicting clinical chemotherapy response [85]. MiR-136 affects DNA repair efficiency through an unknown mechanism and indirectly contributes to cisplatin resistance [86].

PARP1 acts as a DNA damage sensor, and when its activity is inhibited, it can produce a synthetic lethal effect with HR repair-deficient cells [87]. MiR-216b targets PARP1 to reduce its expression. After overexpression of miR-216b in drug-resistant cells, cell viability decreases and apoptosis increases. In 51 specimens, the expression levels of miR-216b and PARP1 were negatively correlated, and PARP1 overexpression can reverse sensitivity [88].

The above mechanism reveals that miRNAs participate in drug resistance by interfering with HR repair efficiency, PARP1 signaling, and cascade pathways, providing direction for the clinical translation of “miRNA combined with PARP inhibitors” in HR-deficient ovarian cancer (Fig. 4).

LncRNA: drug-resistant ceRNA and metabolic regulator

LncRNAs participate in drug resistance as ceRNAs or by directly regulating protein function, involving apoptosis, metabolism, and signaling pathways.

Apoptosis and endogenous competitive RNA network

In apoptosis regulation, after cisplatin-induced DNA damage, the p53-dependent LncRNA PANDAR inhibits apoptosis and promotes drug resistance through a dual mechanism; on the one hand, it binds to serine/threonine-rich protein 2 (SFRS2) to block p53 phosphorylation, reducing the cisplatin-induced apoptosis rate in A2780 cells from 35% to 11%, with the IC50 increasing by 1.5-fold [89]; on the other hand, through the transmission of chemotherapy-induced extracellular vesicles (Chemo-EVs), binding to SRSF9 upregulates the ratio of SIRT4/SIRT6 mRNA, further inhibiting apoptosis and enhancing drug resistance, especially playing a key role in patients with p53 mutations [90]. Linc00312 activates the Bcl-2/Caspase-3 signaling pathway to promote apoptosis and antagonize cisplatin resistance [91]. UCA1 acts as a ceRNA to bind miR-27a-5p, relieving inhibition of ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 2 N (UBE2N) to upregulate BIM and enhance cisplatin sensitivity [92]. In addition, UCA1 can also regulate cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer by activating the miR-143/FOSL2 pathway [93].

LncRNA CCAT1 upregulates survivin by sequestering miR-454, forming a “lncRNA/miRNA/apoptosis protein” resistance axis [94]. Analysis of 1656 ovarian cancer samples revealed that CASC10 was highly expressed in cisplatin-resistant HGSC and associated with poor prognosis. Knockdown of CASC10 reduced the viability of drug-resistant OVCAR3CIS cells by 30%, and when combined with cisplatin, the cell viability further decreased to 56%. Critically, liposome-encapsulated CASC10-siRNA combined with cisplatin exhibited significant synergistic efficacy without toxicity in tumor-bearing mice (the mechanism involves the regulation of cell cycle and DNA damage response pathways), demonstrating clear clinical translational value [95].

Metabolic reprogramming

LncRNAs reshape tumor metabolism to adapt to cisplatin stress. In cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells, LncRNA H19 is highly expressed. H19 stabilizes Nrf2 to activate GSTP1, promoting GSH synthesis to clear cisplatin toxicity. Clinical data indicate that H19 expression levels are significantly associated with cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer patients and can serve as a biomarker for predicting resistance [96]. LncRNA RPRM is highly expressed in cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells, which promoting the enrichment of RNA helicase Dead-box helicase 3 X-linked (DDX3X) on Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) mRNA, thereby enhancing de novo serine synthesis to provide the metabolic substrates required for tumor cell proliferation [97]. LncRNA CTSLP8 promotes cisplatin resistance by enhancing glycolysis: it specifically binds to pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) to upregulate c-Myc expression, which in turn increases glycolytic activity. In SKOV3 cells, overexpression of CTSLP8 led to increased cell viability after cisplatin treatment, with the SUVmax value in the drug-resistant group reaching 18.8 (vs. 5.7 in the sensitive group); conversely, CTSLP8 knockdown reduced cell viability and restored cisplatin sensitivity [98]. TMEM147-AS1 adsorption of let-7b relieves inhibition of Aurora Kinase A (AURKA) and maintains lipolysis homeostasis through the interaction between AURKA and DDX5, supporting drug resistance [99].

Signal pathway regulation

LncRNAs can modulate the activity of core signaling pathways, such as STAT3, Notch1, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR, by directly binding to pathway molecules or indirectly regulating pathway-related genes, ultimately shaping the drug-resistant phenotype of tumor cells. LncRNA CHRF promotes cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer cells by positively regulating miR-10b and thereby activating the process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and the STAT3 signaling pathway. In cisplatin-resistant ES2 cells, knockdown of CHRF significantly restored cisplatin sensitivity, as evidenced by the reversal of the EMT phenotype and a decrease in the phosphorylation level of STAT3 [100]. Overexpression of MALAT-1 activates the Notch1 signaling pathway and enhances cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer cells [101]; PRKAR1B-AS mediates OC chemotherapy resistance through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [102]. PART1 is highly expressed in OC cell lines resistant to DDP. The transcription factor YY1 induces lncRNA PART1 to sponge miR-512-3p, thereby increasing CHRAC1 expression and conferring cisplatin resistance to ovarian cancer cells [103]. In addition, CRNDE and arginine-rich splicing factor 1 (SRSF1) are co-expressed at high levels in ovarian cancer drug-resistant cells. Further experiments show that CRNDE induces drug resistance in ovarian cancer cells through the SRSF1/T-cell intracellular antigen-1 (TIA1) pathway. Combining cisplatin with CRNDE inhibitors or SRSF1 inhibitors may become a new therapeutic approach [104] (Fig. 5).

CircRNA regulates cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer

CirRNAs are covalently closed circular single-stranded ncRNAs that lack a 5’ cap and 3’ poly(A) tail, making them resistant to degradation by nucleases and deadenylation and decapping, and thus highly stable [110]. This characteristic allows them to remain intact for long periods in complex biological samples such as serum and tissue, laying the foundation for their clinical application as tumor biomarkers [111].

CircRNAs contribute to drug resistance by regulating the tumor microenvironment or protein ubiquitination. Highly expressed circITGB6 forms a ternary complex with IGF2BP2 and FGF9 mRNA to induce M2 macrophage polarization, thereby participating in the cisplatin resistance process. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting circITGB6, when combined with cisplatin, can reduce M2 macrophage infiltration and prolong the survival of tumor-bearing mice. Clinical data from 119 °C patients showed that patients with high circITGB6 expression had significantly shortened overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) (P < 0.05), suggesting that circITGB6 can serve as a potential indicator for evaluating OC prognosis [112]. CircNUP50 promotes drug resistance through a dual mechanism: it directly binds to p53 and UBE2T to accelerate p53 ubiquitination and degradation, while also acting as a sponge for miR-197-3p to upregulate G3BP1 and enhance this process; the co-delivery of cisplatin and si-circNUP50 via a nanomedicine system (Psc@DPP) effectively reduces tumor burden in mouse models and reverses drug resistance phenotypes [113]. In addition, Circ_C20orf11, which is highly expressed in the serum of drug-resistant patients, alleviates the inhibition of the 14-3-3 zeta (YWHAZ) pathway by adsorbing miR-527, while weakening macrophage M2 polarization, promoting cell proliferation, and enhancing cisplatin resistance [114]. CircFoxp1 sequesters miR-22/miR-150-3p to upregulate CEBPG and FMNL3, thereby enhancing the proliferation of EOC cells and their resistance to cisplatin. The expression level of circFoxp1 in serum exosomes from 112 EOC patients was significantly higher than that in 82 healthy controls (P < 0.001), and its expression in 46 cisplatin-resistant patients was also higher than that in 76 cisplatin-sensitive patients (P < 0.001). The diagnostic area under the curve (AUC) of circFoxp1 reached 0.914 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.867–0.960), and high circFoxp1 expression was associated with poor survival. Additionally, circFoxp1 knockdown combined with cisplatin could reduce tumor volume, providing a basis for non-invasive diagnosis, drug resistance stratification, and therapeutic targeting of EOC [115]. Circ-VPS13C binds to miR-106b-5p and upregulates YWHAZ to induce autophagy. Knocking down circ-VPS13C increases the apoptosis rate of drug-resistant cells and reduces the IC50 of cisplatin [116] (Fig. 6).

The above studies indicate that abnormal expression of circRNAs can serve as potential biomarkers for predicting drug resistance and assessing prognosis, while intervention strategies targeting circRNAs provide a new therapeutic direction for clinically reversing cisplatin resistance.

M6A modification regulates cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer

M6A modification participates in drug resistance through dynamic regulation of RNA stability, and its mechanism involves multiple levels, including oncogene regulation, post-transcriptional modification, and maintenance of stem cell phenotype.

At the level of oncogene regulation, oncogene RIPK4 was found to be highly expressed in drug-resistant EOC tissues in the EOC cohort. Its carcinogenic mechanism may involve METTL3 mediating m6A modification of RIPK4 mRNA, YTHDF1 recognizes this modification and maintains the stability of RIPK4 mRNA, thereby activating the NF-κB pathway. After silencing METTL3 or YTHDF1, the apoptosis rate increased and the cisplatin IC50 decreased [117]. The insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1 (IGF2BP1) and METTL3 synergistically stabilize the m6A-modified apoptosis inhibitor baculovirus apoptosis-related protein repeat sequence (BIRC5) mRNA and enhance chemotherapy resistance [118]. YTHDF1 binds to m6A-modified FZD7 mRNA, enhancing its translation and activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Silencing YTHDF1 restores cisplatin sensitivity [119]. Additionally, following cisplatin exposure, nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) activates MST4, which in turn phosphorylates protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) and binds to METTL3, leading to increased levels of METTL3-R36me2 and mRNA m6A methylation at the N6 position, thereby recruiting to DNA damage sites to promote RAD51-mediated homologous recombination repair; inhibition of PRMT5 or METTL3 disrupts this process, enhancing cisplatin toxicity [120].

In addition, at the post-transcriptional modification level, the oncogene Intermediate filament family orphan 1 (IFFO1) can inhibit the migration of β-catenin to the cell nucleus to weaken drug resistance; however, it is inhibited by HDAC5 during transcription and downregulated by METTL3/YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification after translation, ultimately promoting tumor progression and chemotherapy resistance [121]. Similarly, overexpression of m6A-modified lncRNA RHPN1-AS1 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, accelerating ovarian cancer cell invasion and cisplatin resistance [122].

In terms of stem cell phenotype regulation, YTHDF1 promotes the translation of m6A-modified TRIM29, thereby maintaining the stem cell-like phenotype of cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells [123]. Additionally, the demethylase AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) forms a positive feedback loop with HOXA10: HOXA10 transcriptionally activates ALKBH5, and ALKBH5 in turn maintains HOXA10 mRNA stability. Meanwhile, ALKBH5 demethylates the m6A modification of JAK2 mRNA. This demethylation reduces YTH domain family protein 2 (YTHDF2)-mediated JAK2 mRNA degradation, thereby activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [124]. In A2780 cells, overexpression of ALKBH5 significantly increased the cisplatin IC50; however, knockdown of either HOXA10 or ALKBH5 reversed this drug-resistant phenotype (Fig. 7) [124].

The above findings indicate that inhibitors targeting YTHDF1 or METTL3 can reverse m6A-mediated drug resistance, and the interaction between ALKBH5 (erasure protein) and HOXA10 provides a target for JAK2/STAT3 pathway intervention.

M5C regulates ovarian cancer cisplatin resistance

M5C modification participates in ovarian cancer cisplatin resistance by regulating RNA stability and function, with Y-box binding protein-1 (YBX1) playing a key role as a central molecule.

YBX1 is highly expressed in recurrent OC, and its abnormal expression is associated with acquired cisplatin resistance [125]. YBX1 not only promotes the transcription of DNA repair-related genes, but also cooperates with proteins such as CHD4, MTA1, and MTA2 to regulate chromatin remodeling, laying the foundation for drug resistance phenotypes [126]. YBX1, as an m5C-reading protein, recognizes the m5C modification of CHD3 mRNA and enhances its stability, promoting CHD3 protein expression. Through chromatin remodeling, it enhances HR repair capacity, inhibits apoptosis, and drives drug resistance [127]. YBX1 and MATR3 synergistically stabilize CCNL2 mRNA through m5C modification, enhancing its expression and promoting ovarian cancer cell proliferation and cisplatin resistance. Experiments showed that knocking down CCNL2 in HEY cells reduced the cisplatin IC₅₀ from 4.83 µg/mL to 2.79 µg/mL; while YBX1 inhibition combined with cisplatin significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in the PDX model and restored cisplatin sensitivity [128].

The regulatory network of m5C remains poorly understood, and further exploration is needed to elucidate the roles of modifying enzymes such as NSUN2 and other target genes. However, existing studies support the notion that inhibiting YBX1 enhances platinum sensitivity, providing a new target for reversing acquired resistance.

NcRNAs contribute to cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer through multidimensional ways: MiRNAs regulate resistance by targeting apoptotic molecules [35–39] and signaling pathway nodes; lncRNAs exert effects via the ceRNA mechanism [92]; circRNAs, by virtue of their high stability, regulate macrophage polarization [112] or protein ubiquitination [113]. However, it is important to note that ncRNAs can also interact with other epigenetic layers to further stabilize the drug-resistant phenotype. For instance, the function of circPLPP4 depends on m6A modification to maintain its stability [129]; lncRNA HOTAIR not only acts as a ceRNA to sequester miRNAs but also regulates chromatin structure by modulating histone-modifying enzymes [130, 131]. This cross-layer crosstalk is the core of the “cross-regulatory mechanism” and also the key to understanding the long-term stability of cisplatin resistance [130, 131].

Histone modification-chromatin structure level

Histones are the core basic proteins of eukaryotic chromatin, classified into H1, H2A, H2B, H3, and H4. Reversible modifications of their N-terminal tails (e.g., acetylation, methylation) play a key role in regulating gene expression [132]. In ovarian cancer, histone modifications contribute to cisplatin resistance by reshaping chromatin structure and regulating the transcription of genes associated with drug resistance, with acetylation and methylation being the most extensively studied modifications [133].

Acetylation

Acetylation modification regulates gene expression by dynamically modulating chromatin structure [134, 135]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α regulates aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A3 (ALDH1A3) upregulation in HGSC cells, leading to increased H3K27ac modification levels in the PITX1 promoter region, which directly reduces cell sensitivity to cisplatin [136].

The synergistic treatment strategy combining HDAC inhibitors with cisplatin has become a research focus. The HDAC inhibitor panobinostat can block the binding of Actin-like 6 A (ACTL6A) to the SWI/SNF complex, and when combined with cisplatin, it significantly reduces tumor size in xenograft models [137]. HDAC inhibitors (panobinostat, LMK235) enhance the cytotoxic effects of cisplatin by regulating survivin, APAF-1, and p21; in cisplatin-resistant A2780CisR cells, the IC50 decreased from 19.7 µM to 6.53 µM after 48 h of preincubation, and 72 h of preincubation completely reversed resistance; in CaOV3CisR cells, 10 nM panobinostat completely reversed resistance. Long-term low-dose HDACi also inhibits the development of resistance and has low toxicity to normal cells; however, the combination of HDACi with HSP90i and cisplatin was not superior to the combination of HDACi with cisplatin alone, providing a strategy for clinically reversing and preventing cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer [138]. Additionally, APCS-540 exhibits dual effects on glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3B) and HDAC. Treatment with 0.6 µM APCS-540 alone significantly reduced cell viability. When combined with cisplatin in drug-resistant cells, it reversed drug resistance, extending the median survival time in tumor-bearing mice from 21 days to 35 days and increasing survival rates by 66%, offering a new direction for clinical treatment [139].

In summary, these research findings provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer and lay the theoretical foundation for developing therapeutic strategies targeting acetylation modifications.

Methylation

Histone methylation alters the condensed/loose state of chromatin primarily through the coordinated action of HMTs and HDMTs, thereby regulating the transcription of cisplatin resistance-related genes [18]. This section will focus on key methylation sites such as H3K79, H3K9, and H3K27, and elaborate on the mechanisms of action of their corresponding regulatory molecules in drug resistance.

The study found that CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β (C/EBPβ) recruits DOT1L to mediate H3K79 methylation, thereby activating drug resistance genes such as RICTOR and NEIL3. Notably, knocking down C/EBPβ or using a DOT1L inhibitor (SGC0946) can restore cisplatin sensitivity [140]. In terms of regulating platinum drug sensitivity, the histone demethylase ZNF711 recruits JMJC-domain-containing histone demethylase 2 A (JHDM2A) to bind to the SLC31A1 promoter, thereby reducing H3K9me2 levels to activate its expression and enhance cisplatin uptake. However, following ZNF711 silencing, drug resistance increases, and the H3K9me2 inhibitor BIX-01294 can reverse this effect [141]. Lysine demethylase KDM3A regulates drug resistance through dual epigenetic mechanisms: demethylation of H3K9me2 activates Sox2 to maintain cancer stem cell characteristics; demethylation of p53-K372me1 inhibits p21, activates Bcl-2, and promotes drug resistance and anti-apoptosis; its deficiency can induce cell cycle arrest, senescence, apoptosis, and reverse drug resistance, and is positively correlated with Sox2 expression in clinical tissues, providing a new direction for targeted therapy [142].

Notably, enhancer of EZH2 promotes cell proliferation by enhancing H3K27 methylation modification, and its inhibitor can enhance cisplatin sensitivity [130].

Cross-regulation mechanism: the core hub linking epigenetic layers

The previous sections have summarized the independent functions of epigenetic layers associated with ovarian cancer drug resistance. However, epigenetic regulation is not solely the result of individual modifications; rather, it forms a network through three core cross-regulatory patterns that collectively maintain the stability of the drug-resistant phenotype.

DNA methylation and MiRNA bidirectional feedback loop

Positive regulation: DNA methylation silences tumor suppressor MiRNAs

In drug-resistant cells, hypermethylation of tumor-suppressing miRNA promoters leads to their silencing, forming a positive feedback loop of “DNA methylation/miRNA silencing/activation of drug resistance genes.” For example, miR-130b is downregulated in cisplatin-resistant cells due to hypermethylation of its promoter. This downregulation renders it unable to effectively target and silence colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), ultimately promoting drug resistance [143]. Similarly, promoter methylation of miR-143 [144] and miR-200b/c [145] reduces their expression, causing them to lose their ability to target and inhibit DNMTs, which indirectly exacerbates abnormal DNA methylation accumulation and reinforces the drug resistance cycle. DNMT3A mediates hypermethylation of the miR-199a promoter (85.9% in SKOV3 vs. 22.7% in IOSE386 cells), which silences this tumor suppressor and thereby upregulates its target, Discoidin Domain Receptor 1 (DDR1). The reversibility of this axis was demonstrated by 5-Aza-dC treatment, which reduced methylation to 40.6%, restored miR-199a-3p, and downregulated DDR1. This was functionally confirmed by DDR1 knockdown, which reduced the cisplatin IC₅₀ from 36.35 µM to 20.25 µM, increased apoptosis, and reversed miR-199a-3p inhibitor-induced resistance, supporting the conclusion that this axis is a key driver of cisplatin resistance [146].

Reverse regulation: MiRNA targets DNMT to block abnormal methylation

Some miRNAs can directly target members of the DNMT family to negatively regulate DNA methylation levels, forming a dynamic balance with positive regulation to maintain the stability of drug-resistant phenotypes. For example, the lowly expressed miR-152 and miR-185 target DNMT1, inhibiting the methylation modifications it mediates, and this process does not depend on DNMT inhibitors, forming an independent “miRNA/DNMT/DNA methylation” regulatory loop [147].This reverse effect can partially offset the abnormal silencing effect of DNA methylation on tumor suppressor genes, and together with the positive regulatory mechanism, maintains the stable state of the drug-resistant phenotype.

The “cascade amplification” of LncRNA and histone modifications

LncRNA regulates histone modification enzyme expression through the ceRNA mechanism, forming a cascade reaction that amplifies drug resistance signals, with the HOTAIR-mediated regulatory axis being the key mechanism.

HOTAIR adsorption of miR-138-5p relieves its inhibition of EZH2, resulting in a 3.5-fold and 2.1-fold increase in EZH2 mRNA and protein levels, respectively, in the drug-resistant cell line A2780/DDP compared to sensitive cells [131]. Overexpression of EZH2 further consolidates the drug-resistant phenotype by enhancing H3K27me3 modification to silence drug-resistant suppressor genes [130]. PNA3, designed to target the 89-mer region where HOTAIR binds to EZH2, can specifically reduce their interaction by 80% without affecting the binding of normal RNAs. After pHLIP-mediated delivery, PNA3 can accumulate in the acidic tumor microenvironment; when combined with cisplatin, it significantly reduces the volume of drug-resistant ovarian cancer tumors (P = 0.02) and increases the survival rate of mice to 64%, thereby providing a novel “lncRNA-targeted therapy + chemotherapy” regimen for solid tumors [148].

In summary, the HOTAIR/miR-138-5p/EZH2-H3K27me3 cascade amplifies the downstream effects of histone modifications through upstream regulation of LncRNA, becoming a key mechanism of drug resistance. Targeting molecules upstream and downstream of this axis may provide a precise strategy for clinically reversing platinum resistance.

CircRNA and m6A modification “co-stabilization”

The stability of circRNA depends on m6A modification, which in turn regulates drug resistance through signaling pathways. M6A-modified circPLPP4 is stabilized by METTL3 methylation, acting as a sponge for miR-136 to upregulate PIK3R1 and thereby activating the PI3K/AKT pathway. In 166 clinical samples, high circPLPP4 expressions were associated with drug resistance and poor prognosis. Targeting circPLPP4 with ASO in combination with cisplatin reduced tumor volume in PDX models [129], revealing the “empowering” role of m6A modification in circRNA function and the core role of circRNA-linked RNA modification and signaling pathways.

In summary, DNA methylation/miRNA feedback loops enable the crosstalk between gene expression and post-transcriptional regulation, while lncRNA/histone modification cascades facilitate the connection between post-transcriptional regulation and chromatin structure—collectively, these two mechanisms transform drug resistance from a short-term response to a long-term stable state. Additionally, the circRNA/m6A cooperative stabilization further enhances the robustness of this regulatory network through RNA function modulation. Furthermore, the cross-regulatory mechanism not only provides multi-target combination strategies for therapy, such as targeting the HOTAIR-EZH2 axis, combining DNMT inhibitors with miR-152 mimics, and inhibiting m6A to reduce circPLPP4 stability, but also highlights the importance of combined detection of biomarkers—for instance, the combined use of biomarkers like SFRP5 methylation and miR-137, which overcomes the limitations of single-molecule detection.

Innovative theoretical framework

Based on the cross-regulatory mechanisms discussed earlier, two core theoretical frameworks are extracted: the “Epigenetic Compensatory Network Theory” focuses on the compensatory characteristics of the cross-regulatory network, explaining the essence of single-target intervention failure; the “Epigenetic Temporal Regulatory Pattern” explores the stage-specific characteristics of the cross-regulatory network, clarifying the dynamic rules of drug resistance development and providing insights for subsequent clinical interventions.

Epigenetic compensation network theory

Tumor cells form an “epigenetic compensation network theory” by constructing a cross-feedback network of DNA methylation, non-coding RNA, and histone modifications. They use multi-level functional complementarity and closed-loop regulation to resist single-target intervention and maintain stable drug resistance phenotypes.

At the DNA methylation level, cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer exhibits a synergistic compensation pattern characterized by “hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes (compared with cisplatin-sensitive cells) and hypomethylation of drug resistance-related genes (compared with cisplatin-sensitive HGSC cells)”. Specifically, SFRP5 is silenced due to promoter hypermethylation, which in turn activates the Wnt/AKT2 signaling pathway; MAL, on the other hand, is activated due to promoter hypomethylation, thereby enhancing platinum resistance. These two genes complement each other in function, forming a fundamental compensatory mechanism [21, 29]. Concurrently, miR-152 targets and inhibits DNMT1, while DNMT1, in turn, methylates the miR-152 promoter to silence it, forming a negative feedback loop that stabilizes methylation abnormalities. This results in the use of DNMT inhibitors (such as DAC) alone only demethylating 10.6% of CpG sites, making it difficult to completely reverse resistance [23, 147]. Functional redundancy buffers exist among histone modifications: EZH2 inhibitors can block H3K27me3 modification but cannot affect H3K79me2 modification mediated by C/EBPβ-DOT1L, which continues to activate drug-resistant genes such as RICTOR, maintaining the stability of the drug-resistant phenotype [140].

A single DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitor can only reverse the methylation of 10.6% of CpG sites [23]. While single-target intervention can disrupt local processes, the cross-regulatory networks will compensate through other levels. Clinical practice requires combined strategies to overcome buffering resistance, such as “DNMT inhibitors combined with miR-152 mimics” to break the methylation-miRNA feedback loop, or “EZH2 inhibitors combined with DOT1L inhibitors” to block redundant histone modifications [23, 140, 147].

Epigenetic temporal regulation patterns

The development of cisplatin resistance exhibits stage-specific and stress-induced dynamic characteristics, rather than being intrinsic to cells [149]. As post-transcriptional regulatory molecules, miRNAs possess the property of rapidly regulating gene expression without requiring changes in DNA sequence, making them the “primary responders” of cells to external stress. The early stage of cisplatin resistance is dominated by the rapid response of miRNAs [150]; for instance, miR-130a and miR-137 enhance apoptosis by targeting the anti-apoptotic protein XIAP, and the apoptosis rate of SKOV3 cells increases after transfection with miR-137 mimics - this stage relies on the rapid regulatory effect of miRNAs [35, 36]. As the treatment cycle extends, epigenetic modifications gradually accumulate and stabilize. DNA methylation, as an heritable marker, requires cell division to consolidate and becomes the dominant mechanism [151]. For example, in the drug-resistant cell line OVCAR3-R, PAX9 methylation levels are six times higher than those in sensitive strains. In clinically platinum-resistant patients, high PAX9 methylation (≥ 29.74%) is associated with shorter survival, and methylation patterns gradually stabilize over time [27]. Under long-term drug exposure, cells maintain drug-resistant phenotypes through the stable remodeling of chromatin structure [152]. C/EBPβ recruits DOT1L to mediate H3K79me2 modification, continuously activating drug-resistant genes such as RICTOR, while the DOT1L inhibitor SGC0946 can significantly restore sensitivity [140].

These processes indicate that drug resistance, in response to cisplatin stress, forms a dynamic phenotype of cells via epigenetic reprogramming characterized by the sequence of “rapid response, gradual consolidation, and stable maintenance”—rather than being an intrinsic property of cells. For early-stage chemotherapy, miRNA mimics can be used to enhance apoptotic sensitivity [36]. For mid-stage treatment, combining with DNMT inhibitors can reverse methylation consolidation. For example, decitabine demethylates and activates the CSK gene, which is associated with better prognosis [23]. For patients with advanced drug resistance, targeting chromatin remodeling is required (e.g., the DOT1L inhibitor SGC0946 blocks H3K79 methylation) [140] (Fig. 8).

Potential biomarkers and therapies of epigenetic modifications in ovarian cancer

Potential biomarkers and therapies

Epigenetic modifications provide targets for predicting and reversing drug resistance, exhibiting significant clinical translational potential [13].

At the DNA methylation level, hypomethylation of the PARP4 promoter at site cg18582260 (AUC = 0.86 compared to cisplatin-sensitive patients) can predict cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, providing a basis for clinical decision-making [153]. Among 105 ovarian cancer patients who received platinum-based chemotherapy, the methylation rate of the SFRP5 promoter was 44.4% (52/117). The rate of favorable chemotherapy response in patients with SFRP5 promoter methylation was 59.6%, which was significantly lower than the 95.2% in patients without methylation (p < 0.001); thus, the methylation status of SFRP5 can serve as a biomarker for screening and drug resistance prediction [21]. There is global DNA hypermethylation in high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) and platinum-resistant HGSC. Among these, the demethylating drugs azacitidine and decitabine can reverse this epigenetic abnormality, and synergistically enhance the cytotoxic effect of cisplatin by inducing apoptosis or G2/M cell cycle arrest, respectively [19].

The field of short-chain RNAs has yielded abundant research findings. Abnormal methylation levels of miR-7 are closely associated with patients’ progression-free survival (PFS) and can be used for the evaluation of chemotherapy efficacy [44]. MiR-486-3p enhances cisplatin sensitivity by targeting TMIGD2; in clinical settings, high TMIGD2 expression is associated with short patient survival, and detecting its expression enables the screening of patients at high risk of drug resistance [154]. The combination of miR-379-5p and cisplatin can significantly reduce the enrichment of cancer stem cells (CSCs). In the OVCAR3 CSC population, it increases the cisplatin-induced cell death rate to 59.3% and simultaneously reduces the volume and weight of xenograft tumors significantly, providing a basis for potential combination therapy strategies targeting CSCs-related cisplatin resistance ⁠ [155]. In contrast, downregulated expression of miR-29a/b/c enhances drug resistance; restoring its expression can reduce the tumorigenicity of cancer cells and induce “sensitization” to cisplatin treatment, making it suitable for patients with poor initial response [156].

LncRNAs also hold significant clinical value. For instance, lncRNA CTD-2288O8.1 plays a key role in cisplatin resistance; its high expression is associated with poor patient prognosis and can serve as a predictive indicator for immunotherapy response [157]. Three lncRNAs—lnc-PVT1, lnc-TUG1, lnc-MEG3—exhibit high sensitivity and specificity in predicting the response of ovarian cancer patients to cisplatin. Detecting their expression can guide the selection of individualized treatment regimens [158]. The m6A-regulated lncRNA risk model (including CACNA1G-AS1, ACAP2-IT1, etc.) has an AUC of 0.625 for predicting 5-year OS, which is superior to that of a single biomarker ⁠ [159]. LINC02776 is induced by HIF-1α, highly expressed in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, and associated with poor prognosis. After LINC02776 knockdown, the cisplatin IC₅₀ of A2780 cells decreases from 20.21 µM to 11.56 µM, and the olaparib IC₅₀ drops from 60.15 µM to 36.81 µM—making it a potential target for reversing platinum and PARP inhibitor resistance [160].

In the field of circRNAs, circITGB6 and circFoxp1 have become potential targets for non-invasive prediction and reversal of cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, with core advantages of high stability in serum exosomes, strong correlation with drug resistance prognosis, and preclinically effective intervention strategies [112, 115]. Similarly, circ-VPS13C sequesters miR-106b-5p to upregulate YWHAZ and induce autophagy; after its knockdown, the apoptosis rate of drug-resistant cells increases and the cisplatin IC₅₀ decreases, making it also a potential target for cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer [116].

In the field of histone modifications, HDAC inhibitors synergize with cisplatin to enhance antitumor efficacy [161]. A recent study revealed that the epigenetic inhibitor JIB-04 downregulates the expression of the MDS1 and EVI1 Complex locus (MECOM) by targeting the demethylation of H3K27me3 in its promoter region, and reverses cisplatin resistance in a dose-dependent manner (with cisplatin IC₅₀ reduced by approximately 7-fold), providing a novel therapeutic strategy for MECOM-amplified ovarian cancer⁠ [162]. Preclinical studies have shown that EZH2 inhibitors (e.g., GSK343) or EZH2 silencing can significantly enhance the sensitivity of ovarian cancer to cisplatin [69, 130, 131]. Although no clinical data are available, existing evidence supports their use as a potential combination regimen for EZH2-highly expressed drug-resistant ovarian cancer [163]. BET proteins, represented by BRD4, regulate transcription by binding to acetylated lysines of histones; their inhibitors can suppress oncogenic pathways such as FOXM1 and JAK/STAT, and reverse the sensitivity of drug-resistant cells to cisplatin and PARP inhibitors [164, 165]. Additionally, the combination of BET inhibitors (e.g., JQ1, I-BET-762) with cisplatin can also target ALDH-positive cancer stem cells to produce a synergistic effect, and their clinical safety and efficacy are currently being explored [166].

Epigenetic therapeutic regimens

Single-agent epigenetic therapy for ovarian cancer centers on DNMT inhibitors, while HDAC inhibitors have limited single-agent efficacy and rely on combination strategies. Among them, decitabine and azacitidine have shown prominent clinical performance: in Phase II trials, decitabine combined with carboplatin achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 35% in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer patients, with a median PFS of 3.7 months and a median OS of 14 months in 30 evaluable patients [167–169]; azacitidine as a single agent had an overall response rate of 13.8%, with fatigue and myelosuppression as the most common adverse events [170]. Notably, pan-epigenetic modulators exhibit broad-spectrum toxicity due to non-specific targeting, highlighting the need for developing highly selective subtype inhibitors [169]; although HDAC inhibitors have limited single-agent efficacy, combination regimens (e.g., belinostat combined with decitabine) can enhance therapeutic sensitivity [171, 172].

Combination therapy strategies have demonstrated distinct clinical potential. For platinum-resistant patients: when azacitidine was combined with valproic acid and carboplatin, 18.8% of patients achieved stable disease or minor response, but the study was terminated early due to a high rate of grade 3 or higher adverse events [170]; in 10 patients with recurrent EOC treated with decitabine plus NY-ESO-1 vaccine and liposomal doxorubicin, 6 achieved stable disease or partial clinical response with favorable safety, as only 1 patient experienced grade 3/4 adverse events [173]; although guadecitabine (30 mg/m²) combined with carboplatin did not meet the primary endpoint compared to physician’s choice of chemotherapy, it significantly improved the 6-month PFS rate (37% vs. 11%, P = 0.0027) without differences in OS [174]. Moreover, decitabine combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors could reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment in ID8 Trp53-/- tumor-bearing mice, with a greater survival extension than conventional chemotherapy, and this regimen has now entered clinical trials (NCT02915523)⁠ [175]. For platinum-sensitive recurrent patients, the regimen of bevacizumab (BV) combined with gemcitabine and carboplatin (GC), followed by BV maintenance until progression, extended PFS from 8.4 months to 12.4 months (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.484, P < 0.0001); in addition, its objective response rate (ORR) and duration of response were both superior to those of GC combined with placebo, and although the incidence of grade 3 or higher hypertension and proteinuria was higher, the risks were manageable during treatment [176] (Table 3).

Their results will provide additional evidence for the epigenetic treatment of platinum-resistant ovarian cancer.

Conclusion

Platinum resistance in advanced ovarian cancer results in a five-year survival rate of only 30%. Despite the promising potential of emerging therapies such as bevacizumab and PARP inhibitors [172], there remains a clinical lack of precise predictive biomarkers and effective strategies to reverse drug resistance. Epigenetics provides a critical perspective to break through this predicament. Epigenetics can restore cancer cell sensitivity by targeting DNMTs to reverse hypermethylation, regulating histone modifications, or utilizing non-coding RNAs to intervene in drug resistance pathways [8].

This study elucidates the core epigenetic mechanisms of cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer. Its key innovations are the discovery of three epigenetic crosstalk patterns and the proposal of the “Epigenetic Compensatory Network” theory and the “Epigenetic Temporal Regulatory Pattern”, which provide a theoretical framework for clinical translation.

Challenges in clinical translation include: (1) Unresolved mechanistic questions: The specifics of epigenetic crosstalk and its role in immune cell regulation within the tumor microenvironment are not fully defined. (2) Toxicity and efficacy profiles: Pan-inhibitors suffer from toxicity issues, while the clinical benefits and appropriate use of subtype-selective inhibitors remain debated [178–180]. (3) Inadequate preclinical models: Commonly used cell lines are genetically discordant with typical HGSOC, which calls for the development of clinically relevant models. (4) Biomarker limitations: The predictive value of single biomarkers is suboptimal. Their clinical adoption is further impeded by non-standardized assays of inconsistent sensitivity [181].

Future research requires breakthroughs in multiple dimensions: (1) Mechanistic deepening: (i) Systematically investigate epigenetic crosstalk, prioritizing understudied RNA modifications (e.g., m5C, m7G) [22, 143, 182]. (ii) Leverage single-cell and spatial transcriptomics to dissect how epigenetic heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment drives chemoresistance [183–185] and to uncover novel microenvironmental targets. (2) Optimizing preclinical models: Establish more clinically relevant research systems by adopting HGSOC-specific models to validate the translational relevance of discovered mechanisms. (3) Advancing translational technologies: Develop integrated, multi-omics liquid biopsy platforms to resolve the standardization issues plaguing biomarker detection [186–189]. (4) Therapeutic strategies: Focus on “low-toxicity and high-efficacy” precision combination regimens: (i) Strengthen preclinical and clinical studies on the combination of epigenetic drugs and PARP inhibitors, and explore the mechanisms of action of subtype-selective inhibitors; (ii) Investigate the toxicity-reducing and efficacy-enhancing value of Chinese herbal monomers (e.g., Toosendanin [190], Wedelolactone [191]); for example, exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles achieved an 87% tumor inhibition rate in drug-resistant models with no organ toxicity [172, 192, 193]; (iii) Build AI-driven dynamic treatment models for real-time regimen optimization.
