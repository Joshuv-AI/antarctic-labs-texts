Crystal structure of the class V GTP-binding RNA aptamer bound to its ligand: GTP recognition by a topologically complex intermolecular G-quadruplex
Authors: Helena Stafflinger, Konstantin Neißner, Saskia Bartsch, Alexander K Pichler, Karolina Bartosik, Karthikeyan Dhamotharan, Rupert Abele, Elke Duchardt-Ferner, Ronald Micura, Hermann Schindelin, Jens Wöhnert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA aptamers obtained from in vitro selection experiments provide unique insights into the structural properties and ligand recognition capabilities of RNA in general. In order to investigate the relationships between RNA sequence information content and structural complexity with ligand affinity and specificity, the Szostak group previously identified eleven different families of GTP-binding aptamers varying widely in their sequence conservation patterns as well as in their secondary structure complexity and topology. The class V GTP aptamer has a particularly high affinity for GTP. Here, we report the high-resolution X-ray crystal structure of this aptamer–GTP complex. Remarkably, GTP is found to be integrated into one layer of a two-layered G-quadruplex, thereby rationalizing the high ligand affinity and the observed specificity. The G-quadruplex is extended on one side by a noncanonical tetrad layer containing three non-G nucleotides and on the other side by a Watson–Crick base pair stacking on top of an unpaired adenine. Interestingly, the G-nucleotides forming the G-quadruplex originate from two asymmetric bulges separated by an 8 base pair A-form helix, resulting in a complex and previously unobserved G-quadruplex topology.

Introduction

The SELEX protocol, established in the early 1990s by the pioneering work of the groups of Szostak and Gold [1–3], enables the identification of nucleic acid sequences with user-specified functional properties from large random sequence libraries by combining targeted selection and exponential amplification steps. The application of SELEX has been particularly successful in the identification of RNAs capable of binding to a wide variety of different ligands, ranging from metal ions to many chemically diverse small molecules, all types of biomacromolecules, whole cells, and even different tissue types [4–9]. Due to their remarkable aptitude in binding their various ligands with high affinity and specificity these RNAs have been termed aptamers [2, 10]. In certain instances, the target specificity and affinity of aptamers exceed even those of antibodies. A particularly impressive example for this is the classical theophylline binding aptamer [11]. This aptamer is able to strongly discriminate between theophylline and caffeine—two small molecules that differ only with regard to the presence of a single methyl group [11]. Due to these remarkable recognition capabilities, RNA aptamers found widespread applications for instance as specific sensors, diagnostic tools and were even successfully turned into clinically applicable drugs [12–14]. However, aptamers were also important from a more fundamental perspective since they provided an early illustration of the folding capabilities of RNA molecules in general and demonstrated that it is possible to construct structurally complex binding pockets for a wide variety of small molecules from only four chemically distinct nucleotide building blocks [15–17]. In this regard, they presaged the discovery of the naturally occurring riboswitches, which combine a highly specific small molecule binding domain—the so-called aptamer domain—with an expression platform domain to regulate gene expression on the transcriptional or the translational level, predominantly in bacteria [18–23].

In order to delineate the relationships between RNA sequence and structural complexity on one hand with ligand specificity and affinity on the other hand the Szostak group evolved 11 different aptamer families for the same ligand—the nucleotide triphosphate GTP [24, 25]. The resulting aptamers differ widely in sequence, size, information content, and secondary structure. The aptamers revealed a relationship between information content as quantified by the number of conserved nucleotides in the binding pocket and ligand affinity. Surprisingly, the high-affinity aptamers were not more specific than the aptamers with a lower ligand affinity [25]. So far, three-dimensional structures are only available for two of these aptamers in complex with their common ligand GTP [26, 27]. Both the structures of the class I aptamer and the class II aptamer bound to GTP reveal ligand binding pockets with a complex architecture build around a number of base triplets and base quartets but with no obvious structural homologies to each other. The two structures also differ with regard to the GTP recognition mode. The class I aptamer recognizes the Hoogsteen edge of the bound GTP, which base pairs with the Watson–Crick edge of a guanine nucleotide in the RNA [26]. In the class II aptamer, the nucleobase of the ligand is an integral component of an intermolecular base triple with a cytosine and an adenine nucleotide of the RNA. In this configuration, the C forms a Watson–Crick base pair with the bound GTP and the Watson–Crick face of the A recognizes the sugar edge of the ligand [27]. Notably, despite the more extensive base pairing interactions between the RNA and the ligand, the previously reported affinity of the class II aptamer for GTP (KD ∼ 400 nM) is significantly lower than the one of the class I aptamer (KD ∼ 76 nM) [25]. Neither aptamer shows interactions of the RNA with the ribose or triphosphate moieties of the ligand [26–28]. Another remarkable feature of the class II aptamer is a structurally important base quartet stabilized by a protonated adenine nucleotide forming the floor of the ligand binding pocket. The pKa of the adenine in this base quartet is >9.0. This corresponds to a structurally induced pKa shift compared to adenines in single stranded RNAs of more than 5 pH units [27]. Overall, the structures of these two aptamer–GTP complexes nicely illustrate the structural versatility of RNA and underscore their value as model systems to understand the biophysical and conformational properties of RNA molecules in a broader sense.

The class V aptamer from the same selection was reported to possess the second highest affinity for GTP of all eleven aptamer classes (KD ∼ 17 nM) and a more complex secondary structure (Fig. 1) than the class I and II aptamers [25]. While the class I and II aptamers consist of simple stem–loop structures, in the class V aptamer three helical stems separate two highly asymmetric internal bulges. According to nucleotide conservation and mutational data, both internal bulges contain nucleotides involved in ligand binding [25, 29]. Previous nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments with this system did not allow disentangling the overall topology and did not yield a full structural description of the aptamer–ligand complex due to its size and limited spectral quality. However, in conjunction with experiments investigating analog binding and metal ion specificity as well as mutational data, these experiments suggested that the class V aptamer might recognize GTP by the formation of a G-quadruplex structure into which the ligand is incorporated [29].

G-quadruplexes are noncanonical nucleic acid structural elements formed by sequences rich in stretches of consecutive guanine nucleotides [30–34]. In these motifs, guanine residues use Hoogsteen base pairing to form multiple circular base quartets that stack on top of each other and thereby form coordination sites for monovalent cations such as potassium or sodium ions. Formally, at least two stacked guanine quartets, which are also called tetrads in this context, are required to form a G-quadruplex structure. To identify potential G-quadruplexes from nucleic acid sequences it is often assumed that they conform to consensus sequences of the form G≥2–NX–G≥2–NX–G≥2–NX–G≥2 with X in a range between 1 and 7 nucleotides [35, 36]. However, structural studies of G-quadruplexes have shown that different deviations from this consensus sequence and structure can be tolerated and extend the structural complexity of these motifs [37]. In particular, the stretches of G residues can be interrupted by loops and bulges [38]. Furthermore, nucleotides other than G can form additional base triplets or tetrads that stack on or even in-between the G-tetrads [39–41].

G-quadruplex structures with particularly complex topologies and pronounced deviations from the simple consensus sequence given above were observed for fluorescence-light-up RNA aptamers. These aptamers bind to fluorogenic dyes resembling the chromophore of the green-fluorescent protein or are derived from thiazole orange [38, 42–44]. In addition, Curtis and Liu identified potential G-quadruplex motifs in human genomic DNA and RNA that bind to and are stabilized by GTP [45]. However, their sequences corresponded to simple canonical G-quadruplex types and their affinities for GTP were characterized by KD’s > 100 μM. This is in the range of physiologically relevant intracellular GTP concentrations but much higher than the previously reported KD of the class V aptamer for GTP [25, 45]. Later, simple DNA motifs containing (4n − 1) G residues in the quadruplex core were described. These motifs were called G-vacancy bearing quadruplexes and it was suggested that guanosine and guanine nucleotides could be directly integrated into one of the G quartets [46, 47]. One of these motifs bound a dAdG dinucleotide and cGAMP, a cyclic dinucleotide, with KD’s in the low micromolar range and its solution structure revealed a simple all-parallel architecture [47].

Here, we describe a high-resolution X-ray crystal structure for the class V GTP aptamer bound to GTP. We show that GTP is indeed bound to the aptamer as an integral part of a two-layered intermolecular G-quadruplex which is extended on one side by an additional mixed nucleotide tetrad and on the other side by an additional Watson–Crick G:C base pair stacking on an unpaired adenine residue, respectively. The nucleotides contributing to the formation of the G-quadruplex and its noncanonical extension layers on both sides are located in two asymmetric internal bulges, separated by an 8 bp A-form double helix. To the best of our knowledge such an overall topology is unprecedented with respect to previously determined G-quadruplex structures. In conjunction with extensive mutation and deletion experiments, the structure helps to rationalize previous biophysical and biochemical observations as well as the high ligand affinity. Notably, the complex structure of this aptamer–GTP complex is well beyond the current prediction capabilities of AlphaFold 3 [48].

Materials and methods

RNA sample preparation by in vitro transcription

All full-length class V GTP aptamer variants were synthesized by in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase with linearized plasmid DNAs as templates [49]. The RNAs were transcribed as fusion constructs with a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozyme sequence to generate homogenous 3′-ends after auto-cleavage of the primary transcripts [50]. After transcription and auto-cleavage, the target RNAs were purified by preparative urea PAGE and desalted using PD-10 columns (GE Healthcare) according to standard protocols. The RNAs were then washed first with a 10 mM EDTA solution, followed by washing with water and concentrated using Vivaspin™ concentrators with a 3 kDa MW cutoff (Sartorius).

RNA solid-phase synthesis

Chemical synthesis of truncated RNA variants was performed on an automated DNA/RNA synthesizer (H6 K&A or ABI 392) at a 1.0 µmol scale using standard phosphoramidite chemistry. 2′-O-TOM or 2′-O-TBS standard RNA nucleoside building blocks and 2′-O-TBS 1000 Å CPG solid supports were purchased from ChemGenes. Detritylation, coupling, capping, and oxidation reagents were dichloroacetic acid/1,2-dichloroethane (4/96), phosphoramidite/acetonitrile (100 mM), and benzylthiotetrazole/acetonitrile (300 mM), Cap A/Cap B (1/1) [Cap A: 4-(dimethylamino)pyridine/acetonitrile (500 mM), Cap B: acetic anhydride/sym-collidine/acetonitrile (2/3/5)], and iodine (20 mM) in tetrahydrofuran/pyridine/H2O (35/10/5), respectively. Solutions of phosphoramidites and tetrazole were dried over activated molecular sieves (3 Å) overnight.

The solid-supported RNAs were treated with 1,8-diazabicycloundec-7-en (DBU) in acetonitrile (1.0 M and 0.5 ml) for 5 min at room temperature, then washed with acetonitrile and dried. The CPG beads were transferred to a screw-capped vial and incubated with a mixture of aqueous methylamine (40%, 0.5 ml) and aqueous ammonia (28%, 0.5 ml) for 2 h at 40°C. The supernatant was removed and the solid support was washed three times with H2O/THF (1.0 ml, 1/1). The combined supernatant and washings were evaporated to dryness and the residue was dissolved in a solution of tetrabutylammonium fluoride in tetrahydrofuran (1.0 M and 1.5 ml) to remove the 2′-O-silyl protecting groups. After incubation at 37°C for 16 h, the reaction was quenched by addition of triethylammonium acetate/H2O (1.0 M and 1.5 ml, pH 7.4). Tetrahydrofuran was removed under reduced pressure and the sample was desalted by size-exclusion column chromatography (GE Healthcare, HiPrep™ 26/10 Desalting; Sephadex G25) eluting with H2O. Collected fractions were evaporated and the RNA was dissolved in H2O (1 ml).

Crude RNA was purified by anion exchange chromatography on a semi-preparative Dionex DNAPac® PA-100 column (9 mm × 250 mm) at 80°C with a flow rate of 2 ml/min [eluent A: 20 mM NaClO4 and 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) in 20% aqueous acetonitrile; eluent B: 0.6 M NaClO4 and 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) in 20% aqueous acetonitrile]. Fractions containing RNA were evaporated and the residue redissolved in 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate solution (10–20 ml), loaded on a C18 SepPak Plus® cartridge (Waters/Millipore), washed with H2O, and then eluted with acetonitrile/H2O (1/1).

Crude and purified RNAs were analyzed by anion exchange chromatography on a Dionex DNAPac® PA-100 column (4 mm × 250 mm) at 80°C with a flow rate of 1 ml/min. A gradient of 0%–45% B in 60 min was applied; eluent A: 20 mM NaClO4 and 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) in 20% aqueous acetonitrile; eluent B: 0.6 M NaClO4 and 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) in 20% aqueous acetonitrile. HPLC traces were recorded at an UV absorption of 260 nm.

For mass spectrometric analysis of RNA samples (∼200 pmol) were diluted with an aqueous solution of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt dihydrate (Na2H2EDTA) (40 mM, 15 µl). H2O was added to obtain a total volume of 30 µl. The sample was injected onto a C18 XBridge column (2.5 µm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm) at a flow rate of 0.1 ml/min and eluted using a gradient from 0% to 100% B at 30°C (eluent A: 8.6 mM triethylamine, 100 mM 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoroisopropanol in H2O; eluent B: methanol). RNA was detected by a Finnigan LCQ Advantage Max electrospray ionization mass spectrometer with 4.0 kV spray voltage in negative mode. For further experiments, the chemically synthesized RNAs were dissolved in H2O, desalted and concentrated using PD-10 columns (GE Healthcare) and centrifugal concentrators as described above.

RNA crystallization and data collection

To obtain a homogenously folded RNA for crystallization 6 mg/ml RNA in 25 mM Bis–Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl and either 5 mM MgCl2 or MnCl2 were heated for 10 min at 95°C prior to the addition of a 1.3-fold molar excess of GTP followed by cooling on ice for 10 min. Crystallization screens were set up in 24-well plates at 18°C using the hanging-drop-vapor-diffusion method with commercially available screening kits (Hampton Research). For all constructs, the crystallization buffer and the RNA solution were mixed in an equal ratio and crystals were obtained after 3–7 days in several conditions. Selected conditions were optimized with respect to pH, additives and salt concentration. Final crystals for the class V GTP aptamer in the presence of MnCl2 for experimental phasing were grown in 40 mM sodium cacodylate pH 6.0, 50 mM spermine, 80 mM KCl, and 10% methylpentanediol (MPD). Crystals of the G61A mutant were grown in the presence of MgCl2 under similar conditions. For the class V (UU) variant, crystals were obtained at a pH of 7.0, 12 mM spermine, 80 mM NaCl, and 30% MPD. Crystals were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and diffraction data were collected at 100 K on a Dectris Eiger 16 M detector at the P13 beamline at Deutsches Elektronen-Synchrotron (DESY), Hamburg, Germany [51]. The wavelength for the MnCl2 crystal was set to 1.892 Å to exploit the anomalous scattering of the Mn K-edge. For the other crystals, the wavelength was set to 0.976 Å. All diffraction data were processed with XDS [52], and due to anisotropic diffraction of the Class V (UU) RNA the data were corrected with STARANISO [53].

Structure determination

The structure of the class V aptamer (MnCl2 containing crystals) was solved by a combination of molecular replacement and single-wavelength anomalous diffraction. An initial search model encompassing residues (2–7, 14–29, 34–39, and 53–68) was used for molecular replacement with Phaser [54] as implemented in CCP4 [55] which yielded four solutions, in agreement with packing calculations and noncrystallographic translational symmetry. The resulting model was preliminarily refined in Phenix [56] and SAD experimental phasing was conducted with Phaser-EP as implemented in Phenix. This resulted in a heavy atom model with eight major sites (two per chain in the vicinity of the phosphodiester groups of residues 40, 52, and 53) and a figure of merit of 0.576. The resulting phases were improved with Phenix Resolve incorporating NCS restraints, yielding a highly complete model. Alternative rounds of model building in Coot and refinement in Phenix and REFMAC [57] in CCP4 at a resolution of 2.50 Å resulted in the final model (Table 1). The structure of the class V (G61A) variant was solved by molecular replacement with Phaser (CCP4), using the final model of the wildtype class V aptamer. This resulted in a clear solution with similar packing of the four RNA strands. In the class V (UU) crystal, only one RNA strand was found in the asymmetric unit after molecular replacement with the same model as predicted by packing considerations. Alternative rounds of model building in Coot and refinement in Phenix as well as CCP4 at a resolution of 2.05 Å for the G61A variant and 1.60 Å for the UU variant generated the final models (Table 1).

Isothermal titration calorimetry

To determine the ligand binding affinity of the various class V GTP aptamer variants for GTP as well as for GTP derivatives, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements were performed. GTP derivatives were obtained commercially (Jena Bioscience) and ITC experiments were performed using a MicroCal iTC200 calorimeter (Malvern Panalytical) in a buffer containing 50 mM Bis–Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl as well as 2 mM MgCl2 or MnCl2 at 20°C. For all measurements the reference power was set to 11 µcal s−1 and the high feedback mode was selected. For the titration, GTP or its derivatives were titrated into the RNA solution in 20 separate injections with a stirring speed of 750 rpm. After an initial delay of 120 s, the first injection of 0.2 µl with a duration of 0.4 s, which was excluded from the data analysis, was followed by 19 injections of 2 µl with a duration of 4 s. The spacing between the injections ranged from 180 to 300 s. The ligand and RNA concentrations were adjusted in ranges of 10–50 µM for the RNA and 50–500 µM for the ligand, according to the binding affinities. The thermograms were processed and fitted via a one-site binding model in Origin7 (Origin Lab). All measurements were conducted in triplicates and results are given as the mean value with standard deviation, respectively. An overview of the results of all ITC measurements is given in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S1).

SEC-MALS

Size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS) was performed at 4°C using a TSKgel G3000SWXL column (15 ml, Tosoh Bioscience), a light-scattering detector (TREOS) and a refractometer (Optilab rEX) from Wyatt Technology and a UV detector, HPLC pump, and degasser from Jasco. The system was equilibrated with 3 column volumes of buffer (50 mM Bis–Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl, and 2 mM MgCl2). The buffer was filtered through 0.1 µm pore size Durapore PVDF membrane filters (Merck) following a recirculation through the system for 20 h at 0.5 ml/min to improve the baseline by removing air bubbles and particles by degasser and pre-injection filter (0.1 µm). The light scattering detector was calibrated by using monomeric bovine serum albumin (Merck) with a dn/dc value of 0.185 ml/g. Prior to the size-exclusion chromatography, flash cooling of the RNA was performed in the presence of a 2.5-fold molar excess of GTP. For molecular weight determination, 100 µl of a 20 or 100 µM solution of the RNA–GTP complex was injected. The obtained signals were processed with the ASTRA software package version 5.3.4.13 (Wyatt Technology). The RNA concentration was determined by the refractometer using a refractive index increment dn/dc of 0.172 ml/g at 20.5°C and 658 nm [58]. The UV detection wavelength was set to 295 or 300 nm depending on the RNA concentration.

NMR spectroscopy

NMR spectra were collected on a Bruker Avance 600 MHz NMR-spectrometer equipped with 5 mm cryogenic triple resonance TCI-N probes and a 700 MHz spectrometer equipped with a quadruple resonance QCI-P cryogenic probe using Bruker standard pulse sequences. Measurements were performed in 50 mM Bis–Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 5% D2O at 15°C. NMR data were recorded and processed using TOPSPIN version 3.7.0 (Bruker). To ensure homogenously folded samples, the RNA was heated prior to the addition of a 1.5- and 5-fold molar excess of GTP and cooled on ice for every measurement.

SAXS data acquisition and analysis

SAXS measurements of the free class V GTP aptamer (3 mg/ml) and in complex with GTP (6 mg/ml), respectively, were performed in 50 mM Bis–Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl, and 2 mM MgCl2 at 293 K at the EMBL P12 BioSAXS beamline at PETRAIII at DESY, Hamburg, Germany [59] equipped with a Pilatus 6 M photon counting detector at a sample-detector distance of 3 m. For the ligand-bound sample, a 1.3-fold excess of GTP was added to the pre-heated RNA. The mixture was cooled on ice and washed using Vivaspin™ concentrators with a 3 kDa MW cutoff (Sartorius), using the flow-through as a blank for the SAXS measurements. For each sample, 40 frames with an exposure time of 0.095 s at a wavelength of 1.240 Å were recorded. The maximum dimension, Dmax, the radius of gyration, Rg, the forward scattering intensity, I(0) and the Porod volume, Vp, were obtained from ATSAS 3.2.1 [60] and RAW 2.3.0 [61]. Furthermore, for the RNA–GTP complex an ab initio model was created using the program DAMMIN [62] and aligned with chain A of the wildtype crystal structure (PDB 9HRD) using CIFSUP with distance measurements defined based on the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm [63]. A detailed overview of the SAXS data acquisition and analysis parameters is given in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S2).

Results

Crystallization and structure solution

The crystallization constructs for the class V aptamer GTP complex are closely based on the sequence and secondary structure originally published by Szostak and coworkers [25] (Fig. 1B). The only difference is a G:C base pair in the proposed 5′-terminal helix that was flipped to a C:G base pair to avoid a continuous run of 5 G’s in the sequence which might lower transcription yields. We tested two constructs for crystallization. One features a terminal G:C (G1:C68) base pair, whereas in the other, C68 of the terminal G:C pair is followed by two formally single-stranded uridine residues (U69, U70). Both constructs contain a 5′-triphosphate group at the 5′-end and a 2′-3′ cyclic phosphate group at the 3′-end due to auto-cleavage after transcription as a fusion with a HDV ribozyme. The former construct binds GTP in the presence of 2 mM Mg2+ with a KD of 5.5 nM in an exothermic reaction according to ITC experiments (Fig. 1C). This KD is even lower than the originally reported KD (17 nM) based on equilibrium centrifugation experiments [25]. It also binds GTP in the presence of 2 mM Mn2+, albeit with a lower affinity as reflected in a KD of 68 nM (Fig. 1C).

Both constructs crystallized under a variety of conditions as described above. Importantly, the construct with the G1:C68 closing base pair also crystallized in the absence of Mg2+ ions but in the presence of only Mn2+ ions in space group P21. These crystals diffracted to 2.5 Å and allowed SAD phasing based on the Mn2+ anomalous scattering. Four individual RNA chains were modelled into the asymmetric unit. Clear electron density was observed for all 68 nucleotides and GTP in all four chains as well as for three Mn2+ ions and two potassium ions per chain. The construct with the 3′-UU overhang yielded crystals belonging to space group P41212. Phases were obtained by molecular replacement with chain A from the Mn2+-containing crystal. The structure was refined to a final resolution of 1.6 Å with Rwork/Rfree values of 18.06%/21.73% and bond length and angle RMS deviations from ideality of 0.017 Å and 1.994°, respectively (Table 1). Here, the asymmetric unit contained one RNA chain and clear density was observed for all 70 nucleotides, GTP, one sodium, two potassium and three magnesium ions (Supplementary Fig. S1). Full structural statistics for both crystal forms are given in Table 1. The individual chains of the Mn2+-containing crystal superimpose with each other with root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values between 0.10 and 0.67 Å (Supplementary Table S3). When comparing those chains to the structure obtained from the Mg2+-containing crystal, RMSD values between 1.18 and 1.82 Å are observed (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Fig. S2) indicating overall very similar structures in both crystals. Local structural differences and differences in crystal packing will be discussed in detail later below.

Overall structure

The predicted secondary structure of the class V aptamer contained three canonical A-form helical elements with 6, 8, and 7 base pairs, respectively (Fig. 1B). The third helical element is capped by an apical UUCG tetraloop. The three helical elements are separated by two highly asymmetric internal bulges. The “lower” internal bulge which is close to the terminal G1:C68 base pair formally contains seven unpaired nucleotides on its 5′-side and two on its 3′-side. The “upper” internal bulge close to the apical loop is predicted to contain no nucleotides on its 5′-side and 13 nucleotides on its 3′-side (Fig. 1B). These predicted secondary structure elements are also found in the X-ray structure. In the 3D structure, the central and the apical helical element stack directly on top of each other (Supplementary Fig. S3), thereby extruding the upper internal bulge (nucleotides G40–G52). The capping UUCG tetraloop adopts the classical UNCG tetraloop fold, as expected and as previously observed in many other RNA structures. The extrusion of the bulge nucleotides G40–G52 together with the direct stacking interactions between the apical and the central helix brings the phosphate groups of G40, G52, and C53 in very close spatial proximity thereby creating a binding site for two Mg2+- or Mn2+-ions, which enable the close approach of the negatively charged backbone phosphate groups. There, the phosphodiester groups of G40 and G52 simultaneously bind to both Mg2+-ions, whereas the phosphodiester group of C53 only interacts with one of the two ions (Supplementary Fig. S4). According to a recent structure-based classification of Mg2+-binding sites in a high-resolution cryo-EM structure of the bacterial ribosome this Mg2+-binding site corresponds to a so-called MgAμc2-type Mg2+-microcluster [65].

The 5′-terminal helix is extended by a noncanonical Watson–Crick-like A:G base pair (A7:G62), which widens the helical diameter at this position. Stacking between the 5′-terminal and the central helix is mediated by the unpaired G61 (Supplementary Fig. S3). This extrudes six internal bulge nucleotides (U8 to G13) from the helical stack. Importantly, the 8 bp central helix positions the extruded nucleotides from the lower and the upper internal bulge on the same side of the helical stack in close spatial proximity to each other (Fig. 1D).

Structural organization of the G-quadruplex

The spatial proximity of the extruded nucleotides from both bulges allows for the formation of two G-tetrads involving nucleotides G12 and G13 from the lower bulge as well as G40, G42, G43, G46, and G47 from the upper bulge and the ligand GTP (Fig. 2). One G-tetrad consists of the GTP-ligand, G12, G42, and G46 and the other of G13, G40, G43, and G47 (Fig. 2C). Both G-tetrads have the same polarity (Supplementary Fig. S5). A potassium ion binding site is located in between the two G-tetrads (Figs. 1 and 2). On top of the GTP-containing tetrad U9 and U10 from the lower bulge and G41 and A45 from the upper bulge are arranged into a noncanonical base quartet (Fig. 2C). In this quartet U9 and G41 form an unusual trans-G:U wobble base pair whereas U10 and A45 pair in the standard Watson–Crick geometry. No direct hydrogen bonds are present between these two base pairs. However, the N7 atom of G41 and the amino group of A45 cooperate to coordinate a water molecule, which is well defined in terms of its electron density in the structure of the RNA with the 3′-UU overhang (Supplementary Fig. S6A). The oxygen atom of the water molecule is located in the same plane as the nucleobases of the noncanonical tetrad (Supplementary Fig. S6B). In the Mn2+-containing crystals A45 is observed to adopt both the syn and the anti conformation, most likely due to different intermolecular packing interactions (see below), and no electron density for the water molecule can be observed. The O2 of U9, the O4 of U10, the O6 of G41, and the oxygen atom of the coordinated water molecule point toward the center of this quartet and together with the four O6 atoms of the GTP-containing G-quartet below form the coordination shell for another potassium ion. The intramolecular G-quartet consisting of G13, G40, G43 and G47 stacks on an intra-bulge Watson–Crick G:C base pair consisting of C48 and G52 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S5) from the upper bulge which itself is stacked on top of the unpaired A49 from the same bulge. A49 and G52 are separated by two linker residues (U50 and U51) which are not involved in any base pairing or stacking interactions with their bases exposed to the solvent. In the Mn2+-containing crystals, the O2 of U51 is involved in a lone pair–π interaction with the base of A49 (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Residues G41 to G43, A45 to G47, and U10 to G13, respectively, represent the three continuously covalently connected strands of this quadruplex structure. They are all oriented in parallel with respect to each other (Fig. 2B and D). The strand from U10 to G13 contains a single-nucleotide bulge (U11) (Supplementary Fig. S8A). However, U11 can be deleted without any sizable consequences for the GTP-binding affinity (KD ∼ 11 nM), thus suggesting that it is not particularly important for structure formation (Supplementary Fig. S8B). The fourth “strand” of the quadruplex including U9 and G40 is completed by the noncovalently bound GTP, which is stacked between these two nucleotides. G40 adopts a syn conformation. The propeller loop connecting G43 with A45 consists of a single nucleotide (U44) (Supplementary Fig. S8C). Deletion of U44 is detrimental to high affinity GTP-binding since the corresponding mutant has a KD for the ligand of ∼ 550 nM (Supplementary Fig. S8D). The propeller loop connecting G40 with G41 consists only of the 5′-phosphate group of G41. This forces G41 into a highly unusual conformation with a χ angle close to the “high-anti” range (O4’-C1’-N9-C4 torsion angle of −63°) and a ribose conformation best described as O4’-endo (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S9). Formally, the strands containing G13 and G40, respectively, are connected by a lateral loop. However, with 26 nucleotides this loop is exceptionally long and contains the 5′-half of the central helix as well as the entire apical stem loop. A schematic depiction of the G-quartet, which also gives an overview of the observed ribose conformations for the other nucleotides in the G-quadruplex can be found in Fig. 2D.

RNA–ligand interactions

Previous binding studies with GTP analogs indicated that only the nucleobase is important for achieving high-affinity binding to the class V aptamer. In agreement with these results, in our structure the Watson–Crick and the Hoogsteen face of the guanine are involved in the expected hydrogen-bonding interactions foundational for G-quartet formation (Fig. 2C). In addition, the nucleobase of the ligand is involved in extensive stacking interactions with the canonical and the noncanonical tetrad layers above and beyond the intermolecular G-quartet (Supplementary Fig. S5). Furthermore, the O6 oxygen of GTP is part of the coordination shells of both potassium ions bound in the center of the quadruplex (Fig. 2C). The previously published analog binding experiments [28] also suggested that the 5′-triphosphate and the ribose moiety of GTP were not important for binding affinity. While we observed clear electron density for all three GTP phosphate groups which coordinate a Mg2+ ion, they do not form any contacts with the aptamer RNA. This is also evident from 31P-NMR experiments. These experiments showed that both the chemical shifts and the line-widths of the three NMR signals of the bound GTP are very similar to the ones of the 5′-terminal triphosphate group of the RNA, which is also not involved in any interactions (Supplementary Fig. S10). Furthermore, when we compared the affinity of GTP for the class V aptamer with the one of guanosine (KD = 3.7 nM), we found them to be very similar (Fig. 3A and B). The ribose moiety of GTP, however, is involved in intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions between its O4’ oxygen and the amino group of G41 as well as between its 2′-hydroxyl group and the U54 phosphate group (Fig. 3C). The 3′-hydroxyl group of GTP points toward the G55 phosphate group; however, both the distance and the relative orientation of the involved atoms are far from ideal for hydrogen bond formation (Fig. 3C). This is supported by ITC titration experiments with 2′-deoxy-GTP and 2′, 3′-dideoxy-GTP, respectively. 2′-deoxy-GTP binds the RNA with a KD of 44.3 nM and thus with an 8-fold lower affinity compared to GTP (Fig. 3B). On the other hand, the additional removal of the 3′-hydroxyl group in 2′, 3′-dideoxy-GTP does not lead to a further loss of ligand affinity (KD = 36.8 nM) (Fig. 3B). Thus, the intermolecular hydrogen bond between the 2′-hydroxyl group of GTP and the U54 phosphate group contributes favorably to the binding affinity.

Importance of helical elements and their stacking interactions for GTP binding and RNA folding

To gain additional insights into the importance of selected structural elements for RNA folding and ligand affinity, we performed a number of truncation and mutation experiments and measured the ligand affinity of the resulting RNA variants for GTP in ITC experiments. Stacking interactions between the different A-form helical elements in the structure are important for extruding nucleotides from the upper and lower bulges and their relative positioning in close spatial proximity to enable quadruplex formation. Furthermore, previous NMR experiments showed very broad signals for G imino protons with chemical shifts corresponding to those of G’s in G-quadruplex structures already in the absence of ligands [29, 66], thereby suggesting a certain degree of structural pre-organization in the free RNA (Supplementary Fig. S11). The helical elements and their stacking interactions might contribute to such a structural pre-organization. For the central helix, it was already shown that this helix must have a minimal length of 7 and a maximal length of 9 base pairs to enable ligand binding [29]. To test the influence of the terminal helix we chemically synthesized an RNA that started with U9 and ended in A60, thereby lacking the entire terminal helix and the 3′-side of the lower bulge (Supplementary Fig. S11). Imino proton NMR spectra showed that this RNA still yields signals in a chemical shift range typical for G imino protons in G-quadruplexes. However, these signals are very broad. Upon ligand addition in moderate molar excess (1.5-fold) these signals become sharp and additional signals appear resulting in a spectrum that is now very similar to that of the WT–RNA–GTP complex. No additional changes are observed when more ligand is added showing that binding is saturated and that the complex is in slow exchange on the NMR time scale, in agreement with a KD below ∼ 1 μM. This is borne out in an ITC titration experiment with this RNA where a KD for GTP of ∼ 70 nM was measured, which is only ∼ 13-fold higher than the one for the WT RNA (Supplementary Fig. S11). Thus, given the rather radical nature of this RNA variation compared to the WT the presence of the terminal helix is apparently not critically important for RNA folding and high-affinity ligand binding. Furthermore, the stacking interface between the terminal and the central helix, which consists of the A7:G62 Watson–Crick-like base pair and the intrahelical unpaired G61 is tolerant to mutations. G61 can be exchanged to A61 without significantly influencing both the KD for GTP (4.6 nM) and the structure (Supplementary Fig. S12), since the crystal structure of the G61A mutant resolved to 2.05 Å is virtually identical to the one of the WT RNA (Supplementary Fig. S12). An A7C-mutant which changes the A7:G62 Watson–Crick base pair to a narrower and more stable canonical C7:G62 base pair leads to a ∼ 7-fold drop in the affinity for GTP (KD ∼ 40 nM) (Supplementary Fig. S12).

To investigate the importance of the apical helix for GTP binding and structure formation, we designed a circularly permutated construct where now C68 and U1 are connected by a UUCG tetraloop and nucleotides U22 to A39 are deleted. This RNA has completely lost its ability to bind GTP as judged from imino proton NMR spectra. These spectra showed no changes upon addition of even a high excess of ligand and mostly very broad signals in agreement with the absence of a stable conformation (Supplementary Fig. S13). However, the circularly permutated RNA itself seems to be correctly folded on the secondary structure level, as the resonance characteristic for a correctly folded UUCG tetraloop at ∼9.8 ppm is present in the 1H spectrum [67]. Thus, the upper helix and presumably its direct stacking interaction with the central helix are important for restricting the mobility of the upper bulge residues to enable efficient GTP-binding.

Mutations in the noncanonical quartet impair ligand binding

While the truncated RNA-variant U9–A60 is still able to bind GTP with a rather high affinity, a further truncation of this RNA (G12–A60) that also lacks the three 5′ uridine residues U9–U11 shows only a very weak residual ligand affinity. The affinity cannot be quantified in ITC titrations but manifests itself as line broadening in NMR-titration experiments (Supplementary Fig. S13). Due to the absence of U9 and U10 in this construct, the noncanonical quartet consisting of the trans U9:G41 wobble and the U10:A45 Watson–Crick base pair can no longer form. Thus, the noncanonical quartet consisting of these four nucleotides is apparently essential for high-affinity GTP-binding. To gain more nuanced insights into the conformational requirements for the formation of this quartet and for high-affinity GTP binding we tested the effect of point mutations of nucleotides in the non-canonical quartet on ligand affinity (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S14). In the A45G mutant, the U10:A45 Watson–Crick base pair is likely to be replaced by a U10:G45 wobble base pair of similar stability. However, one coordination site for the water molecule stabilizing the quartet and binding one of the potassium ions would be lost. The mutation leads to a ∼50-fold drop in ligand affinity with a KD for GTP of ∼310 nM. A replacement of the U10:A45 Watson–Crick base pair with an isosteric but more stable G:C base pair in the U10C/A45G double mutant, which also disturbs the potassium coordination site has a similar effect leading to a KD for GTP of ∼360 nM. A G41A mutant where the U9:G41 trans wobble base pair could be replaced by a U9:A41 trans Watson–Crick base pair drastically lowers the ligand affinity by a factor of ∼800 (KD = 4.5 μM). Here, the potentially different positioning of the purine base would probably interfere both with water and potassium ion coordination. The U9C/G41A double mutant that would allow for the formation of a trans C:A wobble base pair isosteric to the trans G:U wobble base pair binds GTP with a KD of 2.2 μM, which corresponds to a ∼400 fold drop in affinity compared to the WT. Overall, these data show that the noncanonical quartet is not tolerant to mutations since even isosteric replacements apparently disturb the water and/or potassium ion coordination and thereby lead to a significant decrease in its affinity for GTP.

Effects of changes in other accessory structural elements

On the side opposite to the noncanonical quartet, the G-quadruplex is extended by the C48:G52 base pair (Fig. 5A). Both of the nucleotides stem from the upper internal bulge. The replacement by an isosteric U48:A52 base pair only leads to a slight loss of ligand affinity since the KD for GTP of this variant is ∼28 nM. In contrast, its replacement with a non-isosteric U:G wobble base pair in the C48U point mutant has a more severe effect and leads to a KD for GTP of ∼ 350 nM most likely due to less perfect stacking interactions with the adjacent G-quartet (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S14). The C48:G52 base pair itself stacks on the unpaired nucleotide A49 (Fig. 5B). This stacking interaction, however, is apparently not critical for the structural integrity since a deletion of A49 is well tolerated and has virtually no consequence on the KD for GTP (∼ 9 nM) (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S14). Together, A49, U50, and U51 can be considered as a tri-loop bridging C48 and G52 from the cross-bulge C48:G52 base pair. The bases of U50 and U51 are solvent-exposed and are not involved in hydrogen-bonding interactions. Interestingly, in all chains of the Mn2+-containing crystals the O2 from U51 is involved in a lone pair–π interaction with the base of A49 (Supplementary Fig. S7). However, this loop can be shortened by deleting one of the nucleotides U50 or U51 without a significant effect on the GTP binding affinity (KD ∼ 11 nM) (Fig. 5C).

Overall, the structural data and the ligand affinities for the different truncation variants and point mutants presented here and in our previous work [29] can be consolidated into a model describing the minimal sequence and structural requirements for folding and high-affinity ligand binding of the class V aptamer (Fig. 6). This model might serve as a basis for searching naturally occurring RNA sequences for class V aptamer-like RNA motifs to analyze if similar topologically complex G-vacancy quadruplex motifs occur in nature and might have functional roles as potential GTP sensors in biological systems.

Crystal packing and solution behavior

In both the Mg2+ as well as in the Mn2+-containing crystal form, the class V aptamer–GTP complex dimerizes via extensive stacking interactions between the noncanonical quartets of each monomer (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. S15). In both cases, the two monomers are arranged in an antiparallel orientation and the two stacking quartets are oriented in opposite directions to each other. However, the angle between the helical axis and the G-quadruplex unit differs between the two crystal forms (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. S15). In the Mg2+-containing crystal form the stacking interaction is dominated by the six membered rings of the A45 base. In the Mn2+-containing crystal form the stacking interactions between the noncanonical tetrads from the two monomers are more extensive. Here, A45 from one monomer adopts the anti conformation while A45 from the other monomer is in the syn conformation. This also disrupts the U9:A45 Watson–Crick base pair and the water binding site of the noncanonical quartet. The bases of the two A45 residues then stack on top of each other. Furthermore, the U9 and G41 residues from each monomer form reciprocal stacking interactions with each other, while U10, the fourth residue of the quartet, remains unstacked. In addition, the 2′-hydroxyl groups of the U10 residues are hydrogen bonded to the 5′-phosphate groups of the G42 residues from the opposite monomer.

The rather extensive stacking interactions and the intermolecular hydrogen bonds between the two monomers raise the possibility that the class V aptamer might also dimerize in solution. To address this question we carried out SEC-MALS experiments with the aptamer–GTP complex and compared its elution behavior at two different concentrations (20 and 100 μM). These experiments clearly demonstrated that the elution time of the complex is not concentration dependent and the determined molecular weights of 25.5 and 26.6 kDa, respectively, correspond to the one expected for a monomer (22.5 kDa) (Fig. 7B). In addition, SAXS experiments with the complex demonstrated that the crystal structure of the monomer predicted the scattering behavior in solution and yielded a SAXS envelope that corresponds well to the X-ray structure (Fig. 7C and D). Furthermore, SAXS experiments with the free form of the RNA showed that, in the absence of ligand, the RNA is less compact than in the GTP-bound state (Supplementary Fig. S16).

Performance of AlphaFold 3 in predicting the structure of the class V aptamer GTP complex

Recently, AlphaFold 3 was introduced as a tool for predicting the three-dimensional structures not only of proteins but of all kinds of biomolecular complexes including those of RNAs with a number of small molecule ligands and commonly occurring metal ions [48]. Notably, GTP, potassium, and magnesium ions are among the molecules offered as standard ligands on the AlphaFold 3 web server. With its complex topology and well-defined ligand-binding mode the class V aptamer GTP complex thus presents an ideal but potentially challenging test case for the structure prediction abilities of AlphaFold 3 with regard to RNA–ligand complexes. Notably, for proteins AlphaFold was successful in predicting highly complex structural topologies as e.g. found in deeply knotted protein structures and entirely new fold classes [68] which were experimentally verified only later [69].

We thus submitted the 68 nt wild-type sequence of the class V aptamer for structure prediction and specified GTP, two potassium and three magnesium ions as ligands. We then analyzed the five structures that were returned from the AlphaFold 3 web server. In all five generated models, the length and the A-form geometry of the lower and upper helices were predicted correctly. In addition, the structurally well-characterized UUCG-tetraloop exhibited its typical fold in all structures and showed the commonly observed hydrogen-bonding pattern (Supplementary Fig. S17). The prediction for the central helix, however, only partially corresponds to the experimentally determined secondary structure. Most importantly, none of the predicted structures featured any structural element that even remotely resembled a G-quadruplex structure. Instead, the bases of the internal loops are mostly splayed out and are not involved in any kind of inter- or intramolecular base pairing interactions. No consistent placement of the ligand is observed in the predicted structures, and rather surprisingly, the ligand is not predicted to be involved in any kind of base pairing interactions with the aptamer. Severe steric clashes are observed between GTP and RNA atoms in all predicted structures. Moreover, the magnesium and potassium ions are randomly placed throughout the RNA models with the exception of one magnesium ion, which is consistently located between the β- and γ-phosphate groups of the ligand GTP. Prediction attempts without the inclusion of cations or with different combinations of cations (e.g. only K+, only Mg2+, 2 K+ and more or less than 3 Mg2+ ions) lead to structural models with similar non-native features as those discussed above where the number and identity of cations corresponded to those observed with high occupancy in the X-ray structures. Thus, AlphaFold 3 completely failed in predicting the structure of the class V aptamer GTP complex underscoring the continuing need for experimentally driven structure determinations for complex RNA structures.

Discussion

Here, we present the structure of the class V GTP-binding RNA aptamer bound to its cognate ligand GTP. The structure rationalizes the very high affinity of this RNA for GTP (KD ∼ 5 nM under our assay conditions) since GTP is bound to the RNA as an integral part of a two-layered intermolecular G-quadruplex, extended by a non-canonical tetrad. Thus, both the Watson–Crick and the Hoogsteen side of the bound nucleotide form two intermolecular hydrogen bonds each to two other bases from the same G-tetrad. Both faces of the guanine base from the bound GTP are involved in extensive stacking interactions. One face stacks on top of the bases from the intramolecular G-tetrad of the quadruplex while the bases of the noncanonical tetrad stack on the other face. Thus, the guanine base of the ligand is almost completely buried in the structure, very similar to what has been found for instance for the guanine binding site in the guanine riboswitch which binds its ligand with a similarly high affinity [70, 71]. However, in the case of the class V aptamer the intermolecular hydrogen bonds and stacking interactions of the ligands base are augmented with a polar coordinative interaction between the O6 of the bound GTP and the two potassium ions incorporated into the G-quadruplex structure. Furthermore, the ribose moiety of the ligand contributes to the overall affinity by providing the O4’ oxygen as an additional hydrogen bond acceptor and its 2′-OH group as a hydrogen bond donor group in intermolecular interactions. In this regard, the structure of the class V aptamer GTP complex differs from the previously determined structures of the class I and II aptamers where the ribose moiety of the ligand was not involved in intermolecular interactions [26, 27]. Overall, the structure is in agreement with the results of the previously reported binding experiments with ligand analogs, which identified the functional groups at both the Hoogsteen- and the Watson–Crick face of GTP as important for high-affinity binding [28, 29]. These experiments also suggested an important role of the N3 nitrogen of GTP for RNA binding, which is not involved in any intermolecular interactions in our structure. However, in these experiments N3-methyl-GTP was used to block the accessibility of the N3 nitrogen for e.g. hydrogen bonding. This modification not only introduces a bulky substituent at the N3 but also changes the protonation state at the N1 nitrogen which is no longer a hydrogen bond donor in N3-methyl-GTP. Thus, this modification would also interfere with intermolecular G-quartet formation which requires the H1N1 imino group of the ligand and, therefore, the previously reported strong decrease in binding affinity for this ligand [28] is also in agreement with our structure.

The most important difference between the structure of the class V aptamer GTP complex and previously characterized RNA G-quadruplex structures is the overall topology of the RNA. In contrast to all previously characterized RNA G-quadruplex structures, the guanine residues forming the G-tetrads are located in two different internal bulges separated by an A-form helical element of a length that is tailored to place these two bulges in close spatial proximity on the same side of the helix. This is in agreement with previous mutational data, which showed that the central 8 bp helix can be reduced to 7 or extended to 9 base pairs but not to shorter or longer helices without losing ligand affinity [29]. In this regard, the structure presented here does not only differ from topologically simpler RNA G-quadruplexes conforming to consensus sequence motifs of the form G≥2–NX–G≥2–NX–G≥2–NX–G≥2 where the individual G-containing strands are connected by linear and short lateral, propeller or diagonal loops but also from the structures of the fluorescent dye binding RNAs. While these structures all feature intricate and structurally complex backbone folds, all the G-quadruplex forming guanine nucleotides are located in the same internal bulge or apical loop (Supplementary Fig. S18), and there is a gradual transition from a double helix to the quadruplex. This is either mediated by noncanonical base pairs followed by noncanonical tetrads as in the Spinach aptamer [72] or directly by noncanonical tetrads as in the Corn [73] and the sc1 aptamer which binds a peptide with an RGG motif from FMRP (fragile X mental retardation protein) [74]. In contrast, in the class V aptamer all the G-quadruplex forming elements are completely extruded from the remainder of the structure (Fig. 1), whose helical elements form an uninterrupted helical stack. Our structure, therefore, also suggests that G-quadruplex structures might be more widespread in naturally occurring RNA motifs than currently assumed since they might consist of G-nucleotides widely separated in sequence and secondary structure. Therefore, more complex and bioinformatically more demanding search models might be required to find such quadruplexes. Such search models would have to allow at least for much longer loop-lengths than currently considered and/or could no longer rely on sequence motifs or general features such as G-rich stretches as is currently the case in most G-quadruplex prediction programs [75]. Instead, they should be able to account for spatial proximity of G-containing elements as mediated by secondary or even tertiary structure. In the case of deriving rules for the prediction of topologically complex G-quadruplexes from secondary structure features, it might be possible to use structures such as the one presented here as starting point to define simple rules like the spacing of G-rich single-stranded elements by helices of defined lengths. However, the feasibility of such predictions will be easier to judge once additional structures of topologically more complex G-quadruplexes become available. In the meantime, the class V aptamer might be an experimental testing ground for characterizing the influence of secondary structure element variations on the binding and folding of the inter-bulge quadruplex structure. Possibilities to explore are for instance the insertion of kinks and bends into the central helix or testing the relative orientation of the two bulges along the helix. Specifically, it might be interesting to investigate if e.g. the lower bulge could be switched to the 3′-side of the helix and the upper bulge to its 5′-side (see Fig. 1) or if it is possible to obtain related structures where both bulges are located on the same side of the helix. Furthermore, other known RNA tertiary structural elements such as the kink-turn [76] might be suitable to bring G-rich single-stranded elements into close spatial proximity thereby potentially enabling G-quadruplex formation.

However, these experiments are clearly beyond the scope of the current study and will be subject of future investigations. Helical elements of appropriate length or kink-turns are among the simplest variants to force G-rich sequence elements into close spatial proximity and structurally more complex possibilities can be envisioned on the tertiary structure level. Predicting these structures from sequences approaches the problem of generally predicting accurate RNA tertiary structures in complexity which is apparently not yet generally solved [77, 78].

Furthermore, our structure features a noncanonical tetrad with a composition and base pairing geometry not previously observed in other G-quadruplex structures. A particularly interesting structural feature of the noncanonical tetrad is that it contains a so-called dinucleotide platform consisting of U9 and U10. In a dinucleotide platform, two nucleotides that follow each other directly in sequence are oriented coplanar side-by-side with respect to one another. They are often stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonding between the 2′-OH group of the 5′-nucleotide and a phosphodiester oxygen of the 3′-nucleotide. Dinucleotide platforms were originally discovered in the structure of the P4–P6 domain of the group I intron where two neighboring adenines form a so-called ApA platform at the base of the tetraloop receptor motif [79]. This platform is stabilized by a direct hydrogen bond of less than ideal geometry between the N3 of the 5′-A and the 6-amino group of the 3′-A. Much more common and thermodynamically more stable are GpU dinucleotide platforms where a hydrogen bond is observed between the 2-amino group of the G and the O2 of the U [80, 81]. The GpU platform is prevalent in complex RNA-structures and for instance occurs in such classical structures as the sarcin/ricin loop [82, 83]. It is often associated with E-loop/bulged-G motifs [84] and plays a role in mediating interactions with proteins as e.g. in the recognition of the anticodon loop of the threonyl-tRNA by its cognate aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase [85]. Here, protein residues stabilize this platform by forming additional base-specific hydrogen bonds. The additional stabilization of dinucleotide platforms by interactions with proteins or other ligands or by incorporating them into larger RNA structural motifs such as base triples or quartets is apparently a common theme. An example in this regard is the structure of the NAD+-II riboswitch where a GpU platform is part of a base quartet consisting of a cis Watson–Crick G:C and a cis Watson–Crick:Hoogsteen U:A base pair and is further stabilized by stacking with the nicotinamide moiety [86, 87]. A less common CpA platform in a base quartet where both platform nucleotides are involved in standard Watson–Crick base pairs is found in the structure of the SAMURI methyl transferase ribozyme [88]. Since incorporation of a dinucleotide platform into base quartets is apparently an efficient way for their additional stabilization, it is not surprising that they also have been found in noncanonical tetrads of G-quadruplex containing RNAs. Examples for this are the spinach aptamer where a noncanonical tetrad with an UpG platform is the foundation on which two canonical G-tetrads are stacked [72] and the sc1 aptamer that binds an RGG-motif peptide [74]. In the latter structure, a water-mediated UpG platform is present in a noncanonical tetrad that mediates the smooth transition between a double helix and a three-layered G-quadruplex. The UpU platform formed by U9 and U10 as present in the class V aptamer has been previously observed only once in the structure of the Haloarcula marismortui 23S rRNA [89] where it is part of a base triple with a third U residue. This scarcity of UpU platforms is very likely due to their thermodynamic instability [81] caused by the absence of a direct hydrogen bonding interaction between the two nucleobases (see Fig. 2C). The side-by-side arrangement of the two U residues will at most allow for the formation of a very weak hydrogen bond between the O2 of the 5′-U and the H5C5 group of the 3′-U. However, in the class V aptamer the respective C5–O2 distance of ∼ 3.3 Å argues against the presence of even such a weak hydrogen bond. Here, the two U residues are very likely held in place by the coordinative interactions of their oxygen atoms with the bound potassium ion in the center of the quadruplex. Such a stabilization by coordinative interactions to metal ions should also be possible for other thermodynamically unstable dinucleotide platform motives in the context of noncanonical tetrads in G-quadruplexes or other metal binding motifs. Thus, it might be interesting to explore the potential contribution of metal ion coordination to the stabilization of different dinucleotide platform motives in different structural contexts by a global analysis of known high-resolution RNA structures.

Another noteworthy feature of the noncanonical tetrad of the class V aptamer is the involvement of a stably bound water molecule in the stabilization of the tetrad and in potassium ion coordination. While water-mediated noncanonical tetrads have been previously observed in G-quadruplex forming RNAs in a few cases such as in the Corn aptamer [73] and in the aforementioned RGG-peptide binding sc1 aptamer [74], the geometry of water coordination is very different in all three cases (Supplementary Fig. S19). Overall, this suggests that there might be many additional possibilities to diversify G-quadruplex structures by combining and stabilizing them with structurally unique noncanonical tetrads that have not yet been observed.

Our structure also identifies the class V aptamer as belonging to the class of G-vacancy quadruplexes, where one of the guanine nucleotides is derived from a GTP-like ligand molecule. However, compared to the previously described examples for such systems the class V aptamer features a much higher affinity and structural complexity. This suggests that additional examples for these systems might be found if more complex topologies are considered in bioinformatics searches.

Finally, we would like to note that the complex structure of the class V aptamer by far exceeded the predictive power of AlphaFold 3. It was particularly surprising for us to note that AlphaFold 3 showed a strong tendency to place the nucleotides from the two bulges in the class V aptamer secondary structure (Fig. 1) in rather loosely packed structures dominated by limited stacking interactions (Supplementary Fig. S17). Virtually no hydrogen bonding interactions between bulge nucleotides or to the ligand were present in the predicted structures. One possible explanation might be that the training data sets for AlphaFold 3 were so strongly dominated by Watson–Crick base pairing interactions that in sequence contexts where Watson–Crick base pairing options are very limited as in the bulges of the class V aptamer AlphaFold 3 “prefers” stacking interactions over noncanonical hydrogen bonding interactions. Apparently, the abundant examples for noncanonical base pairings as present in e.g. many ribosomal RNA and riboswitch structures in general and for guanine nucleotide Watson–Crick:Hoogsteen pairings as present in many G-quadruplex structures in particular are not numerous enough to tip the scale toward structurally more reasonable prediction outcomes.

In general, the rather unsatisfying prediction results underscore on one hand the ongoing need for experimental structure determination of RNAs and on the other hand the usefulness of artificial but structurally diverse RNA aptamers as challenging training systems for structure prediction programs.
