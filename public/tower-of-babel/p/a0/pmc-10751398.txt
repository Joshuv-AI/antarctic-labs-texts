Prognostic value of systemic inflammation and for patients with colorectal cancer cachexia
Authors: Guo‐Tian Ruan, Hai‐Lun Xie, Kai‐Tao Yuan, Shi‐Qi Lin, He‐Yang Zhang, Chen‐An Liu, Jin‐Yu Shi, Yi‐Zhong Ge, Meng‐Meng Song, Chun‐Lei Hu, Xiao‐Wei Zhang, Xiao‐Yue Liu, Ming Yang, Kun‐Hua Wang, Xin Zheng, Yue Chen, Wen Hu, Ming‐Hua Cong, Li‐Chen Zhu, Li Deng, Han‐Ping Shi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development and progression of cancer cachexia are connected to systemic inflammation and physical performance. However, few relevant studies have reported the survival outcomes prediction of systemic inflammation and physical performance in patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) cachexia. This study investigated the prognostic prediction value of systemic inflammation and performance status in patients with CRC cachexia.MethodsThis multicentre cohort study prospectively collected 905 patients with CRC (58.3% males, 59.3 ± 11.5 years old). Cancer cachexia was diagnosed according to the 2011 Fearon Cachexia Diagnostic Consensus. The prognostic value of systematic inflammatory indicators was determined using the area under the curve, concordance index, and multivariate survival analysis. Performance status was evaluated with Eastern Coopertive Oncology Group performance score (ECOG‐PS). Survival data were analysed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses.ResultsThe area under the curve, concordance index and survival analysis showed that C‐reactive protein (CRP), lymphocyte to CRP ratio (LCR) and CRP to albumin ratio (CAR) were more stable and consistent with the survival of patients with CRC, both in non‐cachexia and cachexia populations. Among patients with CRC cachexia, high inflammation [low LCR, hazard ratio (HR) 95% confidence interval (95% CI) = 3.33 (2.08–5.32); high CAR, HR (95% CI) = 2.92 (1.88–4.55); high CRP, HR (95% CI) = 3.12 (2.08–4.67)] indicated a worse prognosis, compared with non‐cachexia patients [low LCR, HR (95% CI) = 2.28 (1.65–3.16); high CAR, HR (95% CI) = 2.36 (1.71–3.25); high CRP, HR (95% CI) = 2.58 (1.85–3.60)]. Similarly, among patients with CRC cachexia, high PS [ECOG‐PS 2, HR (95% CI) = 1.61 (1.04–2.50); ECOG‐PS 3/4, HR (95% CI) = 2.91 (1.69–5.00]) indicated a worse prognosis, compared with patients with CRC without cachexia [ECOG‐PS 2, HR (95% CI) = 1.28 (0.90–1.81); ECOG‐PS 3/4, HR (95% CI) = 2.41 (1.32–4.39]). Patients with CRC cachexia with an ECOG‐PS score of 2 or 3–4 and a high inflammation had a shorter median survival time, compared with patients with an ECOG‐PS score of 0/1 and a low inflammation.ConclusionsThe systemic inflammatory markers LCR, CAR and CRP have stable prognostic values in patients with CRC. The ECOG‐PS may be an independent risk factor for CRC. Combined evaluation of systemic inflammation and ECOG‐PS in patients with CRC cachexia could provide a simple survival prediction.Keywords: Cachexia, CRC, ECOG‐PS, Systemic inflammation

Introduction

According to global cancer data from 2022, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third in terms of morbidity and mortality among all malignant tumours, in both men and women. 1 Depending on the type of cancer, the prevalence of cancer cachexia can reach 80%, with gastrointestinal cancer and lung cancer having the highest incidence. 2 Up to 60% of patients with advanced CRC develop cachexia. 3

Cancer‐associated cachexia is accompanied by weight loss, specific loss of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, 4 , 5 leading to lower muscle strength, resulting in decreased overall function in body and chemotherapy tolerance in patients. 6 In addition, cachexia is the direct cause of 20% of cancer‐related deaths and may be connected to 25–30% weight loss, often progressing to heart and respiratory failure. 7 , 8 Cancer cachexia is always caused by the mixture of reduced food intake and metabolic changes, including increased energy consumption, excess catabolism and inflammation. 4 , 5 The decrease in food intake in patients with cancer is caused by primary anorexia and can be aggravated by secondary nutritional symptoms. Concurrent hypermetabolism, hypercatabolism and hypoanabolism aggravate related weight loss and are caused by systemic inflammation and catabolic factors, which work in part through the central nervous system. In addition, cancer treatment, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, can also lead to cachexia syndrome. 9 Systemic inflammation is a key driver of cancer‐associated cachexia by disrupting the balance between protein synthesis and protein degradation. 10 Systemic inflammation of cancer is caused by a variety of factors, such as tumour cells release cytokines and other inflammatory mediators, and activated immune cells release cytokines and chemokines.

The C‐reactive protein (CRP) is one of the most widely accepted systemic inflammation indicator. 4 CRP is an acute‐phase serum protein, which produced in hepatocytes in response to inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐1, tumour necrosis factor‐α, and especially interleukin‐6. 11 Elevated CRP levels are connected to poor prognosis in localized and metastatic CRC, 12 , 13 which can be seen in previously reported prognosis predictive scores such as lymphocyte‐to‐CRP ratio (LCR), CRP/albumin ratio (CAR), lymphocyte CRP score (LCS), modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS), and modified nutritional geriatric risk index (mGNRI) were all composed of CRP. Preoperative LCS is associated with predicting the short‐ and long‐term prognoses of patients with gastric cancer. 14 The mGNRI is a useful predictor of long‐term survival outcomes in patients with cancer. 15 Notably, mGPS, LCR, CAR and CRP levels were showed to correlate with the prognosis of patients with CRC. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

Previous studies have reported clear and consistent relationships between worsening quality of life parameters, such as global health, role, physical and social functioning, fatigue, pain, appetite symptoms, and increased systemic inflammation, independent of good performance status. 21 The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) is a composite rating of the actual level of functioning and self‐care ability of patients with cancer. 22 ECOG PS (PS score ≥2) is strongly connected to poor prognosis in patients with CRC. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Patients with refractory cachexia often have low physical performance and are unresponsive to anti‐cancer treatment. 4 In previous studies, the combination of systemic inflammation and ECOG PS was able to differentiate the prognosis of progressive cancers 16 , 21 ; however, there have been no reports on the survival outcomes prediction of systemic inflammation and ECOG PS in patients with CRC cachexia. This study was to investigate the prognostic prediction value of systemic inflammation and ECOG PS in differentiating between non‐cachectic and cachectic patients with CRC and the predictive value of the mixture of systemic inflammation and ECOG PS in patients with cachectic CRC.

Methods

Patient

A database of studies investigating the connection between nutritional status and clinical outcomes of common malignant tumours in China was analysed. These multiple medical centres cohort study with prospectively collected data of patients with cancer in China over 8 years (2013–2021). Eligible patients complied the following criteria: ≥18 years old, hospital stay of >48 h, pathological diagnosis of cancer, and ability to complete the questionnaire independently. This study included 905 patients with CRC (Figure S1 ). The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of all participating hospitals and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent has been obtained from all patients.

Data collection and definition

In this study, body measurements, questionnaires and blood sample were collected within 48 h of hospital admission. Blood samples were collected before radiotherapy, chemotherapy and surgery. Data collection included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status (yes vs. no), co‐morbidities [diabetes, yes vs. no; hypertension, yes vs. no; and coronary heart disease (CHD), yes vs. no], clinical data (receiving surgery therapy, yes vs. no; receiving chemotherapy therapy, yes vs. no; receiving radiotherapy therapy, yes vs. no; and tumour stage), nutritional intervention therapy (yes vs. no), laboratory indicators (albumin, g/dL; lymphocyte, ×109/L; CRP, mg/L), Karnofsky Performance Status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire‐Core 30 (EORTC QLQ‐C30), length of stay and hospital costs.

BMI was confirmed by the weight per height in meters squared (kg/m2). TNM staging was performed in accordance with the 8th edition of the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) Cancer Staging Manual. Biomarkers: LCR = lymphocyte/CRP 14 ; CAR = CRP/albumin 29 ; mGNRI = 1.489 × CRP + 41.7 × present body weight/ideal body weight. 30 The ideal body weight was confirmed performing the Lorentz equation: for men = height (cm) − 100 − [(height (cm) − 150)/4]; for women = height (cm) − 100 − [(height (cm) − 150)/2.5] 31 ; mGPS = CPR score + albumin score 16 ; LCS = lymphocyte score + CRP score. 31 The diagnosis of cancer cachexia was determined according to the 2011 Fearon Consensus, 4 and cancer cachexia can be diagnosed if one of the following criteria is met: (1) unintentional weight loss of >5% in the past 6 months; (2) BMI < 20 kg/m2 and any level of unintentional weight loss of >2% in the past 6 months; (3) sarcopenia and any level of unintentional weight loss of >2% in the past 6 months.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variable data satisfying the normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were compared using the Student's t‐test. For continuous variables that did not dissatisfy the normal distribution, the median (interquartile range) was used, and the differences between groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages), and the comparisons between groups were performed using Pearson's χ 2 test. The maximum selected rank statistic division was performed using the ‘maxstat’ R package for cutoff values for LCR and CAR. The cutoff values for LCR and CAR were 3443.71 and 0.14, respectively (Figure S2 , Table S1 ). Patients were followed‐up until the date of censoring or until death from any cause (if present). The overall survival (OS) of the patients was calculated from the date of enrolment to the date of death or censoring. Survival data taken to implement using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Multivariate Cox survival analysis models were evaluated utilizing hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.1), and two‐tailed P‐values <0.05 were considered significantly.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The detailed clinicopathological characteristics are shown in Table 1 . A total of 905 patients with CRC (mean age, 59.30 ± 11.50 years, 528 males and 377 females) met the inclusion criteria. According to the 2011 Fearon Consensus on Diagnosis of Cachexia, a total of 344 patients with CRC cachexia were identified in patients with CRC. Patients with CRC cachexia were more likely to be younger, to have advanced tumours, have lower Karnofsky Performance Status, higher ECOG PS score and EORTCQLQ‐C30, and had lower albumin levels, higher lymphocyte levels, higher CRP levels, higher CAR levels, higher LCS scores, lower mGNRI levels and lower LCR levels. In addition, the average length of hospital stay in this group of patients was longer, and hospitalization costs were higher.

Survival analysis based on systemic inflammatory indictors and performance status in patients with colorectal cancer

Survival curves showed that patients with low LCR, high CAR, or high CRP level predicted poor survival outcomes in patients with CRC, respectively (Figure 3 ), and restriction spline curves showed consistent results in patients with CRC (Figure S3 ). We also performed univariate and multivariate survival analysis in different subgroups, and the multivariate survival analysis showed that in all CRC patients, patients with CRC without cachexia, and patients with CRC cachexia, patients with low LCR, high CAR, and high CRP predicted worse prognosis (Table 2 ). On multivariate cox regression analysis, ECOG PS remained independently associated with OS of all patients with CRC, patients with CRC without cachexia, and patients with CRC cachexia (Table S3 ). We also analysed the survival prediction values of LCR, CAR, and CRP in different ECOG PS subgroups, patients with low LCR, high CAR, and high CRP predicted poor prognosis in the ECOG PS 0/1 subgroup, ECOG PS 2 subgroup, and ECOG PS 3/4 subgroup, respectively (Table S4 ).

Discussion

The present study found that the levels of systemic inflammatory markers (LCR, CAR and CRP) in patients with CRC cachexia were higher than those in patients without cachexia. We also found that LCR, CAR and CRP have a stable prognostic value in patients with CRC, both in patients with cachexia and those without cachexia. Okugawa et al. 17 found that LCR can be a prognostic indicator for perioperative and postoperative management of patients with CRC. Yasui et al. 18 found that the LCR and CAR inflammatory markers could accurately predict OS and recurrence‐free survival in patients with stage III patients with CRC. Zhou et al. 19 suggested that CRP and CAR are useful indicators in stage I–III patients with CRC. Similarly, Hua et al. 20 showed consistent results of CRP in prognosis prediction in patients with stage I‐III CRC. Multivariate survival analysis showed that high systemic inflammation predicted worse prognosis in patients with CRC cachexia than those without cachexia. Taken together, this large prospective cohort study suggest that high systemic inflammation is often connected to worse survival outcomes in patients with CRC, especially in patients with CRC cachexia.

Cachexia is still a challenging clinical syndrome. The current consensus definition of cachexia focuses on reducing the complex interaction between food intake and metabolic abnormalities and determining that skeletal muscle loss is the key to patient dysfunction. 4 It is often characterized by weight loss, low BMI and sarcopenia, 4 and systemic inflammation is of central importance in the development of cachexia. CRP and CRP‐related systemic inflammatory indicators included in this study showed that patients with CRC cachexia with higher levels of inflammation had a worse predicted prognosis. Non‐tumour cells in the tumour microenvironment and surrounding environment, including immune cells, endothelial cells and fibroblasts, play an key role in managing the cachexia‐inducing mediators secretion by tumours [S1] (Data S1). Mediators produced by tumours and their hosts induce intracellular changes directly related to persistent inflammation. 32 There are many sources of inflammatory factors in cachexia, including tumour cells, peripheral tissue parenchyma cells, tumour‐infiltrating cells and related infiltrating cells. 33 As a result, a complex tumour–host interaction has been built, promoting an imbalance that favours pro‐inflammatory rather than anti‐inflammatory states. The hypermetabolic environment produced by the body's pro‐inflammatory environment may induce cachexia, anorexia, fat loss and sarcopenia [S2] (Data S1).

Notably, our study also found that the ECOG PS has a consistent prognosis prediction value in patients with CRC. In particular, high ECOG PS scores predicted worse prognosis in patients with CRC cachexia than in those without cachexia CRC. The importance of systemic inflammatory response is not only a prognostic related factor but also a message of decreased physical function in patients with CRC cachexia. Poor physical function in cancer cachexia may be related to many factors, including weight loss, reduced volitional effort (fatigue or depression) or substrate availability (food intake), all of which are associated, at least in part, with systemic inflammation [S3, S4] (Data S1). This statement was confirmed in our mediation analysis, where physical activity accounted for a higher proportion of patients with CRC cachexia. That is, physical activity plays an intermediary role in the poor prognosis of patients with cachexia, promoting the development of patients with a worse prognosis. The results of this study suggest that the existence of a systemic inflammatory response is associated with decreased function and may be related to the deterioration of the quality of life in patients with CRC cachexia. Inflammation‐mediated metabolic changes are the cause of muscle mass loss in patients with cachexia. There are abnormalities in protein synthesis, protein degradation and amino acid metabolism (including transport and branched chain amino acid oxidation) in cachexia muscle of cancer patients. 34 In patients with cancer cachexia, the presence of tumours can lead to systemic inflammation characterized by many circulating factors. Signal peptides including interleukin (IL)‐6, IL‐1, tumour necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) and myostatin mediate muscle cachexia by acting on cell surface receptors in muscle cells, and ubiquitin leads to increased proteolysis by activating inflammatory pathways. 5 For example, TNF‐α alone can inhibit skeletal muscle cell differentiation, 35 while combined with cytokines such as IFN‐γ can reduce the muscle protein content of differentiated muscle cells and increase myofibril degradation. 36 TWEAK, another member of the TNF‐α superfamily, also mediates skeletal muscle atrophy by binding to 12A (TNFRSF12A), a member of the TNF receptor superfamily. The overexpression of TNFRSF12A is known to be associated with muscle cachexia [S5] (Data S1). One hypothesis is that some patients with cachexia may have decreased muscle strength and impaired mobility owing to muscle atrophy, which in turn limits their quality of life [S6, S7] (Data S1). In addition, anorexia and lack of physical activity may lead to reduced nutritional intake, which promotes the degradation of muscle proteins and causes fatigue. 6 , 37 This vicious circle could be exacerbated by the inflammatory state of the body, further limiting quality of life and increasing mortality. Our short‐term survival analysis of patients with CRC cachexia showed that a decrease in physical activity was affected by inflammation. The existence of systemic inflammatory reactions is related to a decline in function, which may be connected to quality of life deterioration in patients with cachexia. Our findings are similar to previous studies 16 , 38 , 39 and have significant implications. First, ECOG PS could act as an intermediate mediator of systemic inflammation for survival outcomes, and the combination of ECOG PS and systemic inflammatory indices could effectively differentiate the short‐term survival of patients with CRC cachexia. Second, both ECOG PS and systemic inflammatory indices were predictive of long‐term survival in patients with CRC both with and without cachexia. The combination of physical activity and systemic inflammation could provide a simple and effective framework for the impact on survival of patients with CRC cachexia.

The main highlight of this study is the analysis of the role of systemic inflammation and physical activity on survival among patients with CRC in a multicentre prospective cohort, and the integrated selection of systemic inflammatory indicators with stable predictive value to provide a framework for short‐term and long‐term survival in patients with CRC cachexia. Dolan et al. found that the composite ratio and cumulative score have prognostic value in patients with colon cancer, but a simple cumulative score of the normal reference range is recommended based on clinical practice [S8] (Data S1). Indeed, cumulative scores can provide clinicians with a simple and easy reference. In this study, firstly, we used the clinical reference cutoff value of CRP to be 10, and then we determined that the cutoff values of CAR and LCR were 0.14 and 3443.71, respectively, based on the population. Survival analysis showed that CAR and LCR had significant prognostic value. It provides a feasible reference cutoff value for clinical practice. In previous studies, the cutoff values of LCR and CAR established in CRC have not been clearly determined. The meta‐analysis of Fan et al. included nine CRC‐related studies. The results showed that the cutoff value of CAR in patients with CRC was lower than that in patients with metastatic CRC, and it could also indicate that the cutoff value in patients with advanced CRC was relatively high [S9] (Data S1). We speculate that the level of inflammation in these patients is relatively high, and similar findings may be found in patients with cancer cachexia. Okugawa et al. [S10] (Data S1) found that the cutoff value of LCR in CRC was 6000, while our cutoff value in patients with CRC cachexia was 3443.71, which similarly indicates that the level of inflammation in patients with CRC cachexia is relatively high. However, the current study still has many limitations. First, this was a cross‐sectional study, and further longitudinal and interventional studies are required to determine the nature of these independent relationships. Second, this study only observed the OS of patients with CRC and did not collect other observations, such as cancer‐related and non‐cancer‐related deaths. It is difficult to investigate the relationship between independent prognostic indicators and each cause of death, so the causes of death in patients still require further investigation. Finally, in addition to CRP, we used the maximum selection rank statistic to determine the optimal threshold values for inflammatory indicators. Identifying uniform threshold values for inflammatory biomarkers may significantly advance the final consensus.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that the systemic inflammatory markers LCR, CAR and CRP have stable prognostic values in patients with CRC. The ECOG PS may be an independent risk factor for CRC. Evaluation of systemic inflammation and ECOG‐PS in patients with CRC cachexia could provide a simple prognostic prediction framework.
