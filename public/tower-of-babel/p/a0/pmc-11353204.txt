Invited Perspective: Leveraging Research and Resources to Mitigate Health Impacts of Environmental Disasters—Insights from a South Korean Tire Factory Fire
Authors: Richard K Kwok, Aubrey K Miller
Published: 2024

The study by Han et al. of a tire factory fire in Daejeon, South Korea, published in this issue of Environmental Health Perspectives,1 provides valuable insights into the public health impacts of industrial fires. The incident led to significant increases in air pollution and disease incidence among nearby residents despite the factory having a warning system to alert the public about how to protect themselves from smoke exposure.1 This experience highlights the urgent need for timely data collection and comprehensive research following disasters to better inform response, recovery, and future preparedness efforts to protect public health. This is of growing concern because the frequency and severity of wildfires, hurricanes, and other climate-related disasters are expected to pose increasing health impacts to communities across the globe, especially among marginalized populations.2,3

For example, as the study by Han et al.1 shows, timelier efforts to identify and communicate the risks associated with the toxic air pollutants stemming from the tire factory fire may have reduced the apparent increased development of respiratory, skin, and neurological disorders. Furthermore, ongoing research could elucidate any prolonged sequelae as a result of these exposures—which would inform treatments to help speed recovery for those affected—as well as promote future preparedness strategies to reduce community exposures after such events.

Data collection and health studies following environmental disasters is crucial in helping the health research community and other stakeholders learn from these tragic situations and improve disaster response and recovery capabilities. Timely health research not only captures the acute and long-term health effects of such events (including mental health impacts) but also furthers our capability to develop, implement, and communicate valid and effective protective measures.

Establishing a baseline health status before a disaster occurs is ideal given that it provides a clear point of comparison to assess the true multidimensional health impacts of these events. Countries with universal health care systems, such as South Korea, are particularly well positioned to conduct this type of research owing to the availability of comprehensive electronic health records (EHRs). These records offer detailed longitudinal data on individuals’ health and are invaluable for assessing acute health effects, as well as the longer-term—and often more profound—health consequences. Such impacts may include increased risk of mental and physical health disorders, such as those seen after the 9/11 World Trade Center attack,4 the Deepwater Horizon oil spill,5,6 Hurricanes Katrina7 and Harvey,8 and other disasters.

However, using EHR data has its limitations. Symptoms recorded in EHRs can often be nonspecific, making it challenging to establish clear associations between exposures and health outcomes. In addition, general symptoms, such as headache, nausea, or respiratory discomfort, can have multiple causes, complicating the analysis. Despite these challenges, certain methodological techniques, such as generalized synthetic control methods, can help minimize confounding factors and provide more accurate estimates of the health impacts of an acute event.9 Although generalized synthetic controls have been used in other settings, the paper by Han et al.1 presents one of the first uses of this approach for exploring the health effects of disasters and hopefully will stimulate more use of this powerful technique.

Several programs and resources are available to support researchers and public health authorities in studying the impacts of disasters. For example, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Disaster Research Response (DR2) Program (https://www.niehs.nih.gov/research/programs/disaster) provides surveys, training materials, and other resources, including the Rapid Acquisition of Pre- and Post-Incident Disaster Data Protocol Designer (https://www.niehs.nih.gov/research/programs/disaster/human-studies), to enable researchers to field time-critical health investigations and studies. These resources employ validated field methods to facilitate timely data collection and can be used to improve the design, implementation, and coordination of health studies in post-disaster situations globally.

DR2 resources have been used for numerous disaster situations, including hurricanes, wildfires, train derailments, chemical releases, and earthquakes.8,10–13 For example, after Hurricane Harvey hit Houston, Texas, in 2017, DR2 strategies and resources were used to expedite data collection, implement research, and facilitate coordination across academic, government, and community stakeholders to inform the response and improve recovery and future preparedness.14

DR2 also provides resources to help minimize participant risks, obtain informed consent, protect confidentiality, and address other ethical considerations. Addressing community concerns about the health effects of environmental exposures is a perpetual challenge that must be proactively addressed through inclusivity, transparency, and ongoing communication between researchers and communities. Partnerships are crucial in designing, implementing, evaluating, and sustaining research efforts to support recovery, enhance resilience, and protect communities in the future—especially in low-resource settings and vulnerable subpopulations around the world.

To further support these efforts, the NIH Climate Change and Health Initiative (https://www.nih.gov/climateandhealth) has partnered with the US National Science Foundation (NSF) to provide funding to two NSF disaster-focused centers housed at the University of Colorado and the University of Washington. The University of Colorado Natural Hazards Center provides rapid awards to eligible grantees to get into the field quickly after extreme climate-related events, working with communities to start health-related research. The University of Washington Natural Hazards Reconnaissance Facility (https://www.washington.edu/research/shared-research-facilities-resources/nheri-natural-hazards-reconnaissance-facility/) offers technical instrumentation, training, and resources to field researchers focused on collecting perishable exposure and health data. Scientists from these two centers are coordinating their efforts to ensure that research team safety, human participant protections, and the efficient and effective use of limited resources are prioritized to support responses to climate-related disasters in the United States and globally.

As the frequency and intensity of environmental disasters increase, comprehensive data collection is essential to inform public health response and recovery efforts. Research efforts such as those by Han et al.1 demonstrate the application of innovative statistical methods and the use of available data resources to aid disaster response efforts that will better protect public health in the future. Investing in global disaster preparedness through processes and resources that improve data collection and the implementation of vital research is crucial to safeguarding public health and enhancing resilience in the face of increasing environmental challenges.
