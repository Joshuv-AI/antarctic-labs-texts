Preclinical dental students’ awareness and integration of pulp biology knowledge: a cross-sectional study assessing the understanding of pulpal functions and implications for clinical endodontic competency and practice
Authors: Aarna Sadotra, Ahishat Saniya, Sushanth S Shetty, Neil Shannen Dsouza, Zein M Jafar, Neva Rasheed, Preethesh Shetty, Raksha Bhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dental pulp is a vital tissue integral to tooth vitality and endodontic health. However, awareness and comprehension of pulpal functions among preclinical dental students remain inadequately characterized. Understanding baseline knowledge and attitudes in early dental education can inform pedagogical improvements in oral histology and foundational endodontic instruction. The study evaluates the awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and learning practices related to dental pulp and its functions among preclinical undergraduate dental students.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 225 preclinical (first and second-year) undergraduate dental students from multiple dental institutions. A validated 20-item structured questionnaire comprising four sections; demographics, knowledge assessment with 6 multiple-choice items, attitude evaluation using 5-point Likert scale with 6 items, and learning practice assessment with 5 items was administered via Google Forms. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, frequency distributions, percentages, and comparative analysis between academic years using chi-square and independent t-tests.ResultsSecond-year students demonstrated significantly higher knowledge accuracy (61.8%) compared to first-year students (38.2%, p < 0.05). The majority of students (92.4%) correctly identified mastication as a non-function of pulp, and 67.6% recognized A-delta fibers as responsible for sharp, localized pain. However, knowledge gaps were identified: 29.3% of students incorrectly attributed odontoblast function to enamel formation, and only 61.9% understood the physiological constraints limiting pulpal healing capacity. Attitude assessment revealed strong positive perception (mean Likert scores 3.16–4.37), with highest endorsement for understanding pulp structure (4.37) and learning pulp for endodontics (4.25). Exam-driven learning behavior was predominant (57.3%), while visual learning modalities were preferred (33.3% favored videos/3D models and 32.9% preferred textbook reading).ConclusionsWhile preclinical students demonstrate commendable foundational understanding of pulpal anatomy and core functions, specific knowledge gaps and exam-centric learning patterns point the need for enhanced pedagogical approaches. Integration of visual aids, clinically relevant case-based learning, active learning modalities, and structured early clinical correlation could strengthen conceptual understanding and foster deeper engagement with pulp biology, ultimately enhancing preparedness for clinical endodontic practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09437-8.

Introduction

The dental pulp, encased within the rigid confines of enamel and dentin, constitutes the central living component of the tooth and forms the dentin-pulp complex [1]. As a highly specialized connective tissue, the pulp maintains tooth vitality and integrity through its formative, nutritive, sensory, and defensive functions, serving as an early warning system against injurious stimuli and orchestrating reparative responses throughout the lifespan of the tooth [2, 3]. Its histological organization into distinct zones, innervation by myelinated Aδ fibers and unmyelinated C fibers, and immunological defense mediated by resident immune cells collectively underpin the biological basis of pulpal pathology and its clinical management [1–9]. A thorough understanding of pulpal structure, physiology, and pathology is therefore fundamental to clinical decision-making in endodontics, vital pulp therapy, and restorative procedures [2, 5, 10]. Notably, dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) residing within the pulp tissue possess the capacity to differentiate into multiple cell lineages, forming the biological basis of regenerative endodontic approaches [11, 12]. Their potential to restore pulp vitality through biologically based therapies further underscores the clinical significance of pulp biology knowledge in contemporary dental practice [13–16].

The importance of foundational science knowledge in shaping clinical competence has been well established in health professions education. Students who develop a strong conceptual understanding of anatomical and physiological principles during preclinical years demonstrate better clinical reasoning, more accurate diagnosis, and improved treatment decision-making [3, 4]. Within dentistry specifically, bridging basic science and clinical reasoning has been shown to enhance endodontic competency; likewise, early preclinical exposure to clinically relevant biological concepts is associated with improved preparedness for patient care [1, 2, 5]. Despite this, the transition from preclinical theoretical learning to clinical application of pulp biology concepts remains a persistent challenge, with many students failing to meaningfully integrate foundational knowledge into clinical problem-solving contexts [4, 5].

Preclinical education represents the critical stage at which students first encounter pulp biology through oral histology and basic sciences. However, awareness levels and conceptual clarity regarding pulpal functions may vary considerably among students with limited clinical exposure [17]. Dental education research has emphasized the importance of early integration between basic and clinical sciences, yet few studies have specifically characterized baseline knowledge, attitudes, and learning practices regarding pulp biology among preclinical students [18, 19]. Identifying these gaps is of particular importance, as misconceptions formed at the preclinical stage may persist into clinical practice, compromising diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning. A targeted assessment of preclinical students’ understanding can therefore provide valuable insights to inform curricular refinement and evidence-based pedagogical strategies.

The present cross-sectional study was designed with the following objectives: (1) to assess the level of awareness and knowledge of dental pulp anatomy, histology, and physiology among preclinical undergraduate dental students, and to evaluate students’ attitudes toward pulp biology and its perceived clinical relevance, (2) to examine learning practices and preferred modalities for engaging with pulp-related content, and (3) to compare knowledge and attitude outcomes between first-year and second-year students. This study aimed to generate evidence-based insights to strengthen oral histology and endodontic education.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines. A descriptive cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted to evaluate awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to dental pulp and its functions among preclinical dental students. The study was developed based on established concepts from standardized oral histology textbooks and contemporary pulp biology literature. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional research ethics committee of AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences, Nitte DU prior to data collection, ensuring compliance with research ethics standards and informed consent protocols (ETHICS/ABSMIDS/181/2025). This cross-sectional design was selected as it allows simultaneous assessment of multiple variables (knowledge, perception, and practice) within a defined population at a single time point, providing valuable baseline data for future longitudinal investigations. This approach is particularly valuable for identifying barriers to clinical integration of pulp biology knowledge and understanding how students currently translate theoretical understanding into practical application. It is important to note that this study assesses educational outcomes and knowledge levels at the preclinical stage and does not directly evaluate clinical endodontic performance or treatment competency.

Sample size calculation

The sample size of 225 was calculated using the single proportion formula [n = Z²P(1 − P)/d²] computed using OpenEpi (Version 3.01, www.openepi.com), assuming a 50% expected prevalence of adequate pulp biology knowledge (P = 0.50), a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and an absolute precision of 7% (d = 0.07), yielding a minimum required sample of 196. Accounting for a projected non-response rate of approximately 15%, the final target sample size was set at 225 students.

Study population

The study population comprised 225 preclinical undergraduate dental students from first and second academic years across multiple dental colleges. Students in clinical years (third year and beyond) were deliberately excluded to maintain focus on preclinical awareness and ensure responses reflected theoretical comprehension rather than clinical experience. Participants were recruited from various dental institutions to minimize institutional bias and enhance generalizability of findings. All participating institutions are affiliated with the same university and follow a uniform curriculum prescribed by the respective regulatory body, ensuring consistency in academic exposure and comparability of knowledge levels across the study population. The sample size of 225 was determined to provide adequate statistical power for detecting meaningful differences between subgroups (academic year, gender). Participation was voluntary, and informed written consent was obtained digitally before survey administration, adhering to all applicable ethical research guidelines.

Survey instrument development

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was designed specifically for this study, based on established content from standard oral histology textbooks (Orban’s Oral Histology, Ten Cate’s Oral Histology) and contemporary pulp biology literature, which is available as supplementary material. The instrument comprised 20 items organized into four sections: (1) Demographics, captured year of study, gender, and institutional affiliation; (2) Knowledge Assessment, comprised 6 multiple-choice questions evaluating understanding of pulp anatomy, histological zones, cellular composition, innervation patterns, and functional principles; (3) Attitude Evaluation, consisted of 6 Likert-scale statements assessing students’ perceptions regarding importance of understanding pulp structure, clinical relevance, and learning confidence; (4) Learning Practice, included 5 questions evaluating learning habits, laboratory exposure, and preferred learning modalities. The questionnaire was reviewed for content validity by faculty experts and piloted with 20 students before full-scale administration. No major modifications were made to the questionnaire following piloting. Internal validity of the questionnaire was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the knowledge and attitude subscales, with values above 0.70 considered acceptable for internal consistency. Content validity was established through expert review by faculty with specialization in oral histology and endodontics, who evaluated each item for relevance, clarity, and representativeness of the construct being measured. External validity was strengthened by recruiting participants from multiple dental institutions affiliated with the same university and following a uniform curriculum, enhancing the generalizability of findings across similar preclinical dental student populations.

Data collection

The survey was administered as a web-based, self-administered online questionnaire using Google Forms. The survey link was disseminated through class coordinators and faculty members via institutional communication platforms and class announcements, ensuring that the research team had no access to individual student email addresses at any stage of data collection. Participation was completely anonymous and voluntary, with no personal identifiers collected. A two-week data collection window was employed, during which two reminder messages were sent to maximize response rates. A convenience sampling strategy was employed, wherein all available preclinical students at participating institutions during the data collection period were invited to participate. Responses were automatically recorded through the Google Forms platform and exported into spreadsheet software for systematic analysis. No incentives were provided for participation. The final response rate was 78.5% (225 of 287 invited students). Attitude-based responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), and item-wise mean scores ± SD were computed.

Data analysis

Knowledge-based questions were scored as correct or incorrect. Attitude-based questions were evaluated on the 5-point Likert scale. Descriptive statistics were employed to determine frequency distributions, percentages, mean scores (± standard deviation), and ranges. Comparative analysis between first-year and second-year students was conducted using independent samples t-test for continuous variables and chi-square test (χ²) for categorical variables. The chi-square test was applied to compare item-by-item correct response rates between academic years, while the independent samples t-test was used to compare overall mean knowledge accuracy scores. Normality of continuous variables was assessed prior to parametric testing. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For proportions, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson score method. For mean Likert scores, 95% confidence intervals were calculated as mean ± 1.96 × (SD/√n). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0, Armonk, NY, USA. Missing data were minimal and handled by item-wise exclusion, with no imputation performed.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 225 responses were obtained from preclinical students across multiple dental colleges, yielding a response rate of 78.5% (225 of 287 invited students). Participants consisted of 52.9% first-year students (n = 119) and 47.1% second-year students (n = 106). Gender distribution showed 64.9% female (n = 146), 33.8% male (n = 76), and 1.3% (n = 3) who preferred not to disclose. Mean age was 20.2 ± 1.4 years (range: 18–24 years).

Knowledge assessment results

Knowledge assessment revealed variable performance across different pulp-related concepts. Overall mean knowledge accuracy was 59.8% ± 18.3% across all six items. The highest correct response rate was obtained for Question 1 regarding non-functions of pulp (92.4% correctly identified mastication), demonstrating strong understanding of basic pulp physiology. Similarly, 90.2% correctly identified the pulp core as the innermost zone, and 81.4% recognized fibroblasts as a principal pulp component. However, Question 3 revealed concerning findings: 29.3% of all students incorrectly associated odontoblasts with enamel formation. This misconception was significantly higher in first-year students (42.1%) compared to second-year students (15.1%, χ² = 18.47, p < 0.001), suggesting that progression through the curriculum gradually corrects this conceptual error. The moderate performance on Question 6 regarding pulp healing capacity (61.9% correct) reflects incomplete understanding of a clinically important concept. Comprehending that the pulp’s enclosed location within rigid dentinal walls and limited collateral circulation constrains regenerative capacity is essential for clinical reasoning about pulpal inflammation, necrosis progression, and treatment necessity.

Mean Likert scores across all attitudinal statements ranged from 3.16 to 4.37 on the 5-point scale, indicating strong agreement with statements regarding pulp importance and clinical relevance. The highest mean score (4.37 ± 0.82) was obtained for Statement 2 regarding importance of understanding pulp structure in preclinical years, with 87.5% strong agreement. Statement 6 yielded the second-highest mean score (4.25 ± 0.93), with 84.0% agreement, underscoring students’ appreciation for integration between preclinical and clinical disciplines. Learning practice findings

Analysis of learning practices revealed important patterns regarding student engagement with pulp-related content. A substantial majority (57.3%) of students reported studying pulp topics primarily during examination preparation, indicating exam-driven rather than concept-driven learning. Despite this predominantly exam-focused learning pattern, 82.7% of students reported having observed pulp histology slides during laboratory sessions, indicating substantial laboratory exposure. However, the discrepancy between laboratory exposure and reported confidence (only 65.4% confident in identifying structures) suggests that passive observation alone is insufficient without active engagement, guided analysis, and interactive discussion. When asked about preferred learning methods, students demonstrated clear preferences for visual and interactive modalities: video/3D models (33.3%), traditional textbook reading (32.9%), and interactive demonstrations (20.9%). Notably, only 12.9% primarily relied on lecture notes alone. This preference aligns with learning science research emphasizing the effectiveness of visual learning and multimedia-based instruction, particularly for complex histological concepts (Figure 3).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study systematically evaluated awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and learning practices related to dental pulp among 225 preclinical dental students across multiple institutions. The findings provide important insights into baseline understanding that precedes clinical endodontic training, identify specific barriers to knowledge integration, and reveal opportunities for enhanced dental education that bridges theory and clinical practice.

Overall knowledge performance demonstrated that while foundational understanding of basic pulp anatomy and physiology is generally strong, significant conceptual gaps persist in cellular biology and pulpal physiology. The excellent performance on basic functional knowledge items (92.4% correctly excluded mastication as a pulp function; 90.2% correctly identified pulp core anatomy) indicates that current curricula effectively communicate primary textbook-level concepts [1, 20]. However, the 29.3% misconception rate regarding odontoblast origin is concerning, revealing a fundamental misunderstanding of embryological origins and cellular differentiation specifically, odontoblasts are mesenchymal-derived cells responsible for dentin formation, whereas ameloblasts are epithelial-derived cells responsible for enamel formation. The significantly higher error rate in first-year students (42.1%) compared to second-year students (15.1%, χ² = 18.47, p < 0.001) suggests progressive curricular correction, yet its persistence in second-year students indicates the need for more explicit integrated teaching [21]. Similarly, the moderate performance on pulp healing capacity (61.9% correct) reflects incomplete understanding of a clinically important concept, highlighting the need for explicit integration of anatomical constraints with pathophysiology and clinical management rationale.

The significantly higher knowledge accuracy in second-year students (61.8% vs. 38.2%, p < 0.001) affirms the value of progressive curriculum design, wherein cumulative exposure through oral pathology, prosthodontics, and restorative concepts reinforces foundational histology and builds sound conceptual understanding through spaced repetition and contextual reinforcement [21]. The predominantly positive attitudes toward pulp biology (mean Likert scores 3.16–4.37) reflect strong student receptiveness to pedagogical improvements, with high endorsement for understanding pulp structure preclinically (87.5%) and its relevance to endodontic learning (84.0%) indicating perceived vertical integration between basic science and clinical disciplines [22]. However, the discrepancy between this high theoretical endorsement and moderate histological identification confidence (65.4%) reveals a theory-practice gap common in preclinical education, which requires active engagement with specimens, guided microscopy, and explicit linking of microscopic features to functional concepts [22].

The predominance of exam-driven learning (57.3%) is concerning as it results in surface-level learning and limited transfer to clinical problem-solving [23]. Students’ strong preference for visual learning modalities (33.3% videos, 32.9% textbooks, 20.9% demonstrations) and substantial laboratory exposure (82.7%) coupled with moderate identification confidence suggest that passive observation alone is insufficient, and that active multimodal instruction is needed to improve outcomes [24, 25]. Several evidence-based strategies are therefore recommended: incorporation of virtual microscopy, 3D reconstructions, and augmented reality tools aligned with visual-spatial learning preferences [26]; transition to active learning approaches including problem-based learning, flipped classrooms, and case-based discussions addressing both knowledge gaps and the theory-practice divide [26]; early clinical correlation connecting pulp concepts to vital pulp therapy, pulpal pathology, and regenerative endodontics to enhance perceived relevance and long-term retention [22, 27]; integrated curriculum design reinforcing pulp biology across multiple courses using spaced repetition [21]; targeted remediation of the odontoblast-ameloblast misconception through explicit teaching and visual demonstrations [28]; and formative assessment strategies including low-stakes quizzing and concept mapping to promote active learning and early identification of misconceptions [29].

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents assessment of longitudinal learning trajectories or causal relationships between teaching methods and knowledge and attitude outcomes [21]. Although all participating institutions follow a uniform curriculum under the same university, the self-reported nature of learning habits and preferences remains subject to response bias, and the multiple-choice format may not fully capture deeper conceptual understanding [23]. The use of Google Forms, while facilitating efficient anonymous data collection, carries limitations including lack of interviewer guidance, potential for satisficing behaviour, and response order bias due to absence of item randomisation. The attitude subscale includes factually grounded items which may limit construct validity; future studies should employ distinct subscales to independently measure attitude, perceived relevance, and self-efficacy. Although a response rate of 78.5% was achieved, reasons for non-participation were not formally recorded, which may introduce non-response bias. Future studies should compare first-year students across institutions with varying curricula and include clinical-year students and practicing clinicians to better understand the longitudinal evolution of pulp biology knowledge and its translation into clinical competency.

Conclusion

Preclinical dental students demonstrate a satisfactory foundational grasp of dental pulp anatomy and physiology, with knowledge deepening progressively across academic years. However, targeted gaps in cellular biology, particularly regarding odontoblast function and the physiological basis of limited pulpal healing, alongside a persistent disconnect between theoretical understanding and practical histological application, point to the need for curricular refinement. Exam-centric learning patterns further limit meaningful engagement with pulp biology concepts. Embedding active learning strategies, visual instructional tools, and early clinical correlation within the dental curriculum will strengthen conceptual integration and better prepare students for competent, evidence-based endodontic practice.
