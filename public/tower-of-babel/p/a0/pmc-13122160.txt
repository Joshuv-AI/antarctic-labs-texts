Digital Psychiatry with Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality: Recent Advances and Limitations
Authors: June Ho Chang, Yoo Jin Jang, Se Chang Yoon, Ji Hyun An, Jung-Seok Choi, Hong Jin Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digital psychiatry has rapidly expanded with the integration of immersive technologies such as virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR). These modalities allow for controlled, engaging, and ecologically valid interventions across a wide range of psychiatric disorders. In this review, a narrative synthesis was conducted based on systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials identified through PubMed, Google Scholar, complemented by seminal earlier works. VR-based therapies demonstrated robust efficacy for anxiety disorders and phobias, with meta-analytic evidence supporting large effect sizes. For post-traumatic stress disorder virtual reality exposure therapy was superior to waitlist control and comparable to established psychotherapies, although AR exposure therapy remains underexplored. In autism spectrum disorder, VR and AR interventions significantly enhanced cognitive, social, and communication skills. AR applications have additionally been validated for cognitive assessment in Alzheimer’s disease, and VR/AR-supported rehabilitation showed promise in attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and neurodevelopmental disorders. Despite encouraging findings, challenges remain, including small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, heterogeneity of protocols, and risks such as cybersickness or fatigue. VR and AR represent innovative tools with growing empirical support across psychiatric practice, extending from diagnostic assessment to therapeutic and rehabilitative interventions. Standardized protocols, large-scale trials, and long-term outcome studies are needed to integrate these technologies into routine clinical care.

INTRODUCTION

The rapid digitalization of psychiatric practice has fostered the emergence of digital psychiatry, a field integrating information technologies, mobile health platforms, and immersive tools into clinical care [1]. Our previous review [2] discussed how conversational agents can facilitate patient engagement, provide psychoeducation, and deliver cognitive behavioral interventions through accessible and scalable interfaces. Building upon that foundation, the present review focuses on another transformative dimension of digital psychiatry—virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR)—which offer fundamentally different modes of clinical interaction through immersive and interactive experiences.

VR and AR both aim to deliver simulations that feel realistic while remaining experimentally controllable. VR builds a complete artificial scene and replaces external sensory input so that users perceive only the simulated environment. Head and hand movements are tracked in six degrees of freedom and the viewpoint is updated with very low latency, which supports a strong sense of presence [3-5]. AR follows a different principle by adding registered digital content to the ongoing real world rather than replacing it. Sensor fusion with cameras and inertial units, together with visual inertial odometry and simultaneous localization and mapping, anchors virtual objects to physical surfaces and helps them persist as the user moves. Since AR preserves the natural context, it is well suited for guidance and assessment in everyday settings, including rehabilitation, training for instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), and just-in-time prompts [6].

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of these technologies across a wide spectrum of psychiatric disorders. VR-based exposure and cognitive training have shown efficacy in anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and psychosis [7-11], while AR applications have been explored for cognitive rehabilitation, mindfulness training, and social interaction enhancement [12-15]. Moreover, the integration of VR/AR systems with biometric sensors and artificial intelligence algorithms has allowed for real-time monitoring of physiological and emotional responses, leading to adaptive, personalized therapeutic environments [16]. Despite these promising outcomes, most studies remain limited in sample size and duration, and the long-term clinical effectiveness of immersive technologies requires further validation through large-scale controlled trials [17]. Nonetheless, challenges remain before VR and AR can be fully embedded into everyday psychiatric care. Technical barriers such as cybersickness, high device costs, disparities, and ethical concerns constrain their clinical adoption [18,19].

Considering these opportunities and challenges, the present review aims to provide an updated synthesis of recent advances and limitations of VR and AR in psychiatry. By contextualizing these findings within broader developments in digital mental health, we seek to inform clinical integration strategies and guide directions for future research. To achieve this, we conducted a literature search covering the past decade (2015–2025) using databases including PubMed and Google Scholar. Search terms such as “VR psychiatry,” “AR psychiatry,” “VR exposure therapy,” “AR exposure therapy,” and “digital psychiatry” were employed to identify relevant empirical studies, reviews, and meta-analyses. Based on the selected literature, this review summarizes current evidence regarding clinical applications, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic outcomes of VR and AR interventions.

To enhance transparency, we adopted a structured approach to literature selection. Studies were prioritized for inclusion based on the following criteria: (1) relevance to core psychiatric mechanisms or clinical outcomes; (2) use of immersive VR or AR technologies rather than non-immersive simulations; and (3) empirical designs including randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses published within the past decade. Earlier seminal studies were included selectively when they informed foundational mechanisms or clinical paradigms. Studies summarized in tables were chosen to represent areas with the strongest or most illustrative evidence within each diagnostic category.

MAIN SUBJECTS

Immersive System Characteristics and Presence in Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality

VR and AR are increasingly recognized as important components of digital mental health infrastructures that support assessment, treatment, and rehabilitation. Contemporary reviews in digital psychiatry consistently highlight VR and AR as key modalities alongside smartphone apps, social media, and chatbots, with growing evidence that immersive technologies can match or complement conventional interventions when embedded in evidence-based care [1].

VR typically involves computer-generated three-dimensional environments presented through head-mounted displays and motion tracking systems that largely occlude external sensory input. The system updates the virtual scene in real time based on head and body movements, so patients experience the virtual world from a first-person perspective and interact with it as if it were a real place. This combination of immersive display and sensorimotor contingency produces a strong sense of presence, the subjective feeling of being in the virtual environment, which is repeatedly identified as a key mediator of therapeutic and assessment effects [3].

AR follows a different route. Instead of replacing reality, AR overlays digital information or virtual objects onto the real world as viewed through smartphones, tablets, or optical and video see-through headsets. Patients continue to see real rooms, people, and objects, but these are augmented with explanatory labels, virtual stimuli, or interactive elements that are anchored in physical space. This allows fine control over stimuli while preserving the ecological richness and contextual relevance of the patient’s actual environment [6].

Both technologies share several affordances that are particularly relevant for psychiatry. First, they combine high experimental control with ecological validity. In VR, social cues, threat intensity, crowd size, or environmental complexity can be manipulated in a graded and reproducible way while patients still experience a naturalistic narrative world [17]. In AR, therapeutic elements are added onto everyday contexts such as homes, public transport, clinics, or workplaces, which supports generalization of learning. Second, VR and AR systems provide rich interactivity. They respond continuously to gaze, posture, movement, and sometimes speech, which supports engagement and allows therapists to design tasks that feel more like lived experiences than tests or instructions. Third, they support dense behavioral data capture. Movement trajectories, body sway, interpersonal distance, reaction times, and choice patterns can be logged unobtrusively and later analyzed as candidate digital biomarkers and components of digital phenotyping pipelines [20].

Psychological and Therapeutic Mechanisms of Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality in Psychiatry

The best validated application of VR in psychiatry is exposure-based treatment for anxiety disorders. Mechanistically, VR exposure implements core principles of modern learning theory [21]. Fear structures are activated by presenting feared cues in a controlled yet convincing manner. Patients are encouraged to remain in the situation and reduce safety behaviors, which allows violation of catastrophic expectations and formation of new inhibitory associations. VR adds precision to this process. Therapists can manipulate crowd density, height, proximity of virtual others, or trauma related cues in small increments, repeat identical sequences across sessions, and pause or replay moments that are clinically important. This level of control is rarely achievable in real-life environments [22].

AR based exposure uses the same learning mechanisms but anchors them in the patient’s real surroundings. AR spiders can be placed on the floor or wall of the patient’s actual room during treatment for specific phobia. Social phobia interventions can add virtual avatars to real social scenes to create graded challenges. Emerging systematic reviews suggest that AR exposure produces clinically meaningful symptom reduction and may be particularly useful for bridging clinic-based therapy and real-world contexts [23].

VR and AR also function as delivery systems for cognitive interventions. Virtual environments can be designed to systematically evoke dysfunctional beliefs and attentional biases, allow patients to test these beliefs through in scenario behavioral experiments, and support immediate cognitive restructuring with therapist or virtual coach guidance. In social anxiety, for example, patients can repeatedly practice public speaking to virtual audiences whose reactions range from supportive to mildly critical while therapists help them reevaluate automatic interpretations such as assumptions that everyone is hostile or bored. In psychosis spectrum conditions, VR can present ambiguous social situations and guide patients to consider alternative explanations for others’ behavior. Such tasks make abstract cognitive techniques more concrete and emotionally engaging [24].

VR is also used to train attentional control. Tasks embedded in realistic scenes require patients to shift attention flexibly away from threat cues, maintain focus on goal relevant information, or resist distraction. Because eye tracking and head orientation can be recorded, therapists can provide feedback on implicit gaze patterns. AR extends these mechanisms into everyday life by delivering just in time prompts that nudge attention toward adaptive cues during real activities, for instance reminders to seek social contact or to apply reappraisal strategies in stressful places [25].

Another distinctive mechanism is embodiment. VR uniquely allows manipulation of body ownership and perspective. Through first person avatars that are spatially aligned with the user’s real body, patients can experience a virtual body as their own. The properties of that body such as gender, age, size, or ethnicity can be systematically altered. Experimental work shows that these manipulations influence self-concept, implicit attitudes, and empathic responses [3]. In psychiatry, embodiment protocols have been explored to reduce self-criticism, enhance empathy in offenders, and modify disturbed body image in eating disorders. AR cannot yet reproduce full avatar-based embodiment, but it can augment the perception of one’s own body. Overlaying visualizations of posture, tension, or physiological arousal onto the real body may enhance interoceptive awareness and support metacognitive insight into how emotions manifest physically [26].

Immersive environments are well suited to support emotion regulation. VR can reliably induce targeted affective states through context rich scenes, such as crowded trains, emergency rooms, or natural landscapes. It can then scaffold the practice of regulation strategies ranging from diaphragmatic breathing and progressive muscle relaxation to mindfulness and self-compassion exercises. Patients often report that such training feels less abstract and more engaging than audio-based instructions alone [27].

When VR is combined with physiological sensing, it becomes a biofeedback platform. Heart rate variability, respiratory rate, electrodermal activity, or muscle tension can be visualized within the world. The color of the sky or the speed of ambient sounds, for example, can change as arousal decreases, giving patients immediate, intuitive feedback about the success of their regulation efforts. Meta analytic work on heart rate variability biofeedback suggests that acquiring control over autonomic indices can meaningfully reduce anxiety and stress, and VR may further enhance engagement and contextual richness for such training [28]. AR enables similar mechanisms in naturalistic settings. Wearable sensors can trigger AR delivered prompts when stress exceeds a threshold during daily activities. Short breathing exercises, coping statements, or grounding tasks can appear on a smartphone or glasses display at the moment of need, transforming biofeedback from a laboratory procedure into an ambient, continuous support system [29].

Although VR has received more research attention, AR has distinct advantages in community and rehabilitation settings. The first advantage is context anchored cueing. Therapeutic content can be linked to specific locations or situations, for example homework prompts that appear when a patient enters a previously avoided place. This supports the generalization of therapeutic learning from clinic-based sessions to daily life. The second advantage is cognitive and functional support. For people living with severe mental illness or neurocognitive disorders, AR overlays can highlight relevant objects, show step by step instructions, and provide real-time feedback to reduce cognitive load. Such systems can improve task performance and confidence, particularly when combined with game-like elements that maintain engagement. The third advantage is natural integration of gamification. Scoring systems, levels, and avatar-based rewards can be embedded in AR tasks in ways that interact with the real world. This is attractive for young people and for long term rehabilitation, where motivation is a major challenge, and early trials suggest beneficial effects on mood, stress, and social connectedness. From a behavioral perspective, AR thus functions as an in vivo skills trainer and behavioral activation tool, providing context-sensitive prompts and contingent feedback that support the acquisition and maintenance of adaptive daily routines and coping behaviors [6].

A central theme in recent digital psychiatry literature is the evolution from static content delivery to adaptive, data driven systems. VR and AR are increasingly integrated with artificial intelligence, machine learning, and multimodal sensing in order to create closed loop interventions. In VR, algorithms can adjust exposure intensity based on real time physiology or in world behavior, modify scenario difficulty in cognitive training tasks, and personalize social scenarios to match individual triggers and goals. In AR, context aware models can combine geolocation, time of day, sensor data, and recent app usage to deliver adaptive interventions in the flow of daily life. Over time, these systems may learn patient specific signatures of relapse risk and proactively deliver support before full symptom exacerbation occurs [30]. Clinical Applications of Virtual Reality in Psychiatry

Virtual reality exposure therapy (VRET) represents one of the most extensively studied applications in psychiatry. By immersing patients in controlled, repeatable, and emotionally evocative virtual environments, VRET facilitates graded exposure while minimizing avoidance behavior and logistical constraints associated with in-vivo exposure. Recent meta-analyses have reported large effect sizes for VR interventions in anxiety and phobic disorders, demonstrating parity with standard cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and superiority to wait-list controls [31,32]. These effects extend to social anxiety, agoraphobia, acrophobia, and performance anxiety, where the sense of presence and controllable intensity promote habituation and extinction learning [33,34]. Furthermore, advances in head mounted display (HMD) technology and portable smartphone-based VR have enhanced accessibility, allowing delivery in outpatient and tele psychiatric settings [29,35].

Across anxiety-related disorders, VRET has emerged as a robust and empirically supported intervention. In PTSD, VRET has outperformed wait-list controls and achieve comparable outcomes to trauma-focused CBT and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing therapy, with sustained reductions in Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale scores across trials [36]. Immersive VR enables safe, titrated re-exposure to trauma cues that would be impractical or distressing to reproduce in vivo. Beyond trauma-related conditions, VR exposure paradigms have also been successfully applied in obsessive-compulsive disorder, provoking symptom-relevant emotional responses while supporting habituation and distress tolerance [37].

Beyond traditional anxiety focused applications, VR technology has increasingly been explored as a tool for emotional regulation and mental well-being across both clinical and general populations. VR-enhanced mindfulness, behavioral activation, and self-compassion training have demonstrated reductions in depressive symptoms and rumination in pilot randomized controlled trials [27]. By offering immersive environments that visually reinforce positive affect or adaptive coping, VR facilitates emotional engagement and attentional refocusing [38]. Recent preventive applications, such as VR-based mental health promotion programs in pregnant women, have demonstrated significant improvements in mood and perceived stress, suggesting the potential of VR as a scalable tool for population-level well-being [39].

Moreover, VR technology can be effectively integrated with biofeedback systems, and innovative attempts to combine multiple modalities are continuously emerging. Accumulating evidence indicates that VR biofeedback not only reduces physiological arousal and anxiety but also enhances interoceptive awareness and perceived control, translating physiological training into improved emotional resilience and stress tolerance [40,41]. The recently developed VR-biofeedback motion chair (MindchairⓇ, Meditrix) is an innovative and automated digital medical device that integrates VR with biofeedback mechanisms to enhance the regulation of autonomic and emotional responses. The Mindchair enhances the sense of presence by integrating the movements of the VR video viewed through the user’s HMD with the movements of the motion chair they sit in. Simultaneously, it provides feedback in the form of icons at the bottom of the VR video, allowing users to easily monitor changes in their biosignals (electrocardiogram, heart rate variability, brain waves, and electromyogram) every second [42]. Higher presence was associated with greater reductions in cybersickness, anxiety, stress, and depressive symptom, and with greater treatment satisfaction [43]. Experimental findings further indicate that coupling deep-breathing biofeedback with VR gaming mechanics significantly enhances user engagement and accelerates physiological trainability, offering a scalable approach to stress regulation [44].

VR-based interventions have demonstrated growing potential in the management of psychotic disorders, addressing both symptom reduction and psychosocial rehabilitation [45]. Recent systematic reviews indicate that VR-assisted cognitive and social training can improve negative symptoms, social cognition, and functional outcomes in schizophrenia. Simulated social scenarios allow safe rehearsal of interpersonal skills and the reappraisal of persecutory beliefs within controlled contexts. VR-based cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis has shown moderate effects in reducing paranoia and enhancing social engagement, while vocational simulations support work readiness and executive functioning [45,46].

In autism spectrum disorder (ASD), VR has been utilized to model complex social cues, teach adaptive behaviors, and reinforce imitation skills. A randomized study demonstrated that VR-integrated rehabilitation significantly improved cognition and social communication compared with conventional therapy [47,48]. VR interventions yield medium-to-large effect sizes in improving social interaction, imitation, and communication skills in ASD populations. VR environments enable repetitive, predictable interactions that reduce social anxiety while providing real-time feedback [49].

Clinical Applications of Augmented Reality in Psychiatry

AR complements VR by superimposing virtual stimuli onto real environments, thereby combining immersion with situational relevance. Unlike VR, which fully isolates users in virtual scenes, AR retains real-world grounding, allowing therapeutic engagement in everyday contexts [16,23].

Augmented reality exposure therapy (ARET) is increasingly being integrated into the treatment of anxiety disorders as a hybrid approach that embeds therapeutic stimuli within the patient’s real environment. ARET enables patients to confront feared stimuli such as spiders, heights, or public spaces, projected within their actual surroundings. Multiple randomized trials have demonstrated outcomes equivalent to or superior to in-vivo exposure therapy, with lower dropout and refusal rates, suggesting higher patient acceptability. Because AR preserves real-world cues, it minimizes dissociation and facilitates direct generalization of therapeutic learning to daily life [12,23].

AR systems have also been introduced for the assessment of cognition and IADL in early Alzheimer’s disease. Compared to standard paper-and-pencil tests, AR-based evaluations demonstrate greater ecological validity and sensitivity to functional change [50]. By blending physical interaction with digital prompts, these systems enable dynamic assessments of real-world performance, potentially improving early detection and personalized rehabilitation [51].

Beyond clinical pathology, AR technologies have proven effective in psychoeducation, health promotion, and procedural guidance. Gamified AR programs have been shown to enhance motivation and engagement during therapeutic exercises, while AR-based preparation protocols significantly reduce anxiety and perceived pain in pediatric medical settings [52]. VR, AR, and mixed-reality platforms have been incorporated into both diagnostic and therapeutic frameworks for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [53]. VR-based continuous performance tests demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy compared with standard cognitive processing therapies. Interventions employing gamified VR classrooms or AR-robot systems improved attention span, working memory, and academic engagement, while minimizing medication reliance [54,55]. AR interventions for children with ASD and ADHD have improved attention, joint gaze, and emotion recognition [9,47]. In adults with neurodevelopmental disorders, systematic reviews suggest that both VR and AR facilitate psychosocial rehabilitation by enhancing communication, vocational readiness, and adaptive functioning [15]. Future Directions and Research Challenges

Despite the accumulating evidence supporting VR and AR interventions, most studies continue to suffer from methodological and practical limitations. Small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and heterogeneous intervention protocols constrain the generalizability of findings and impede meta-analytic synthesis [56,57]. Furthermore, there is limited standardization in device types, exposure parameters, and therapist involvement, resulting in inconsistent outcome measures across trials [58]. Importantly, these methodological limitations directly influence how findings should be interpreted across diagnostic categories. Evidence for VR-based interventions is most robust in anxiety disorders and phobias, where multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses support consistent therapeutic effects. In contrast, studies involving psychotic disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions, and neurocognitive disorders often rely on smaller samples, heterogeneous protocols, and exploratory outcomes, which limit the strength of causal inference and generalizability. Similarly, although AR-based interventions show promising feasibility and acceptability, the current evidence base remains comparatively sparse and methodologically diverse. As a result, conclusions regarding efficacy should be considered disorder-specific rather than universally applicable, underscoring the need for diagnosis-tailored trials with standardized designs and clinically meaningful outcome measures. Common adverse effects such as cybersickness, eye strain, or transient disorientation remain insufficiently addressed through systematic safety monitoring [18]. Ethical concerns, including data privacy, user consent, and digital inequality, also warrant attention as immersive systems increasingly collect sensitive behavioral and physiological data [19].

With respect to AR, several specific evidence gaps remain. Compared with VR-based interventions, AR applications are supported by a smaller number of randomized controlled trials, with most studies focusing on feasibility, short-term outcomes, or narrowly defined tasks rather than sustained clinical efficacy. In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists across AR protocols in terms of hardware platforms, software design, levels of therapist involvement, and outcome measures, which limit cross-study comparison and generalizability. Consequently, current evidence for AR-based psychiatric interventions should be interpreted cautiously and in a diagnosis-specific manner. In parallel, the recent commercialization and increasing public adoption of lightweight AR glasses may substantially lower barriers to access and familiarity with AR technology. As such devices become integrated into everyday life, their availability and user acceptance are likely to facilitate both large-scale research and real-world clinical implementation of AR-based psychiatric interventions, particularly those designed for continuous, ecologically valid support.

Therefore, future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols and long-term follow-up to establish reproducible evidence for clinical efficacy [16,59]. A particularly promising avenue lies in multimodal integration, combining VR and AR with biofeedback, wearable sensors, and artificial intelligence to enable adaptive, closed-loop interventions that dynamically respond to users’ physiological and emotional states [41,44,60]. Building on this trajectory, mixed reality combines the full experimental control of VR with the contextual relevance of AR. By using pass-through VR, occlusion-aware registration, and shared spatial maps, mixed reality can address current limitations, reduce cybersickness, improve real-world transfer, and manage attentional load while preserving immersion. In parallel, research should move beyond symptom reduction to encompass diagnostic assessment, functional rehabilitation, and mental well-being promotion [61]. These broader applications can support early detection, enhance treatment personalization, and foster resilience in both clinical and general populations.

From a clinical perspective, several cautious recommendations can be drawn from the existing evidence. VR-based interventions may be most appropriate for patients with anxiety-related disorders who can tolerate immersive environments and benefit from high levels of experimental control, whereas AR may be preferable in settings that require in situ support, behavioral activation, or skills training embedded in daily life [6,12]. Careful patient selection is essential, particularly for individuals with severe motion sensitivity, vestibular disorders, psychosis with poor reality testing, or high susceptibility to cybersickness. Across modalities, clinicians should monitor adverse effects such as nausea, disorientation, visual fatigue, and emotional overload, and adopt mitigation strategies including gradual exposure, session time limits, and regular symptom check-ins [17,18].

For researchers, greater methodological consistency is needed to advance the field. Priority areas include standardizing key intervention parameters such as exposure duration, intensity progression, and therapist involvement; implementing systematic safety and adverse-event reporting; and selecting appropriate comparator conditions, including active controls rather than waitlist designs where feasible. In addition, the use of core outcome sets that capture not only symptom reduction but also functional change, engagement, and real-world transfer would facilitate cross-study comparison and meta-analytic synthesis [58,59].

Finally, accessibility and scalability must remain central goals. Simplified hardware, mobile-based systems, and equitable digital infrastructure can facilitate real-world deployment in low-resource settings. Establishing international standards for usability, safety, and therapist training will ensure sustainable integration of immersive technologies into psychiatric care [62,63].

This review adopted a broad scope encompassing multiple psychiatric conditions, ranging from anxiety and trauma-related disorders to psychotic and neurocognitive conditions. While this transdiagnostic perspective allowed us to highlight shared therapeutic mechanisms and emerging clinical patterns of VR- and AR-based interventions, it limited the depth of disorder-specific critical synthesis. In particular, the heterogeneity in study designs, intervention protocols, and outcome measures across diagnostic categories constrained a clear stratification of established evidence versus preliminary or uncertain findings within each domain.

CONCLUSION

Digital psychiatry has entered a transformative era with the integration of immersive technologies such as VR and AR. Over the past decade, these tools have evolved from experimental interventions to clinically validated modalities across multiple psychiatric domains. VR-based therapies now hold robust empirical support for anxiety disorders, phobias, and PTSD, demonstrating efficacy comparable to conventional psychotherapies while offering unique advantages in engagement, controllability, and ecological validity. AR technologies, though less extensively studied, have demonstrated versatility from exposure therapy and psychoeducation to cognitive assessment in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease. Taken together, current evidence supports VR as an established adjunctive intervention for anxiety-related disorders, while applications in other psychiatric domains and AR-based interventions should be regarded as promising but not yet definitive. Still, these innovations illustrate how immersion, presence, and interactivity can enhance both therapeutic precision and diagnostic validity while promoting positive mental health, resilience, and subjective well-being in clinical and community populations.

Despite these advances, limitations temper the generalization of current findings. Most clinical trials remain small, short-term, and heterogeneous in design, often lacking standardized protocols or long-term follow-up. Adverse effects such as cybersickness and fatigue highlight the need for systematic monitoring and individualized exposure strategies. Ethical considerations regarding privacy, data security, and equitable access must be addressed before large-scale implementation. The convergence of AR with other digital modalities including biofeedback, wearable sensors, and artificial intelligence, highlights an important opportunity to further develop adaptive and data-informed digital psychiatric interventions. With rigorous methodology, ethical practice, and equitable access, these immersive tools can evolve from innovative adjuncts to integral components of digital mental healthcare, transforming how clinicians assess, engage, and empower patients in the years to come.
