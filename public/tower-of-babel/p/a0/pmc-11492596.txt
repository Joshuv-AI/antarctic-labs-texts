Bacterial and fungal aerosols in poultry houses: PM2.5 metagenomics via single-molecule real-time sequencing
Authors: Xilong Wang, Lei Chen, Guiwen Yang, Yumei Cai, Guanliu Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial aerosol contamination is a common problem in poultry farms, posing potential health risks to poultry and their caretakers. Exploring the distribution and diversity of the microbial community in poultry farm aerosols is crucial for effective mitigation strategies. The composition of bacterial and fungal aerosols is poorly understood, particularly the prevalence of potential pathogenic microorganisms in fine particulate matter (PM2.5) in various types of poultry houses. In this study, 27 PM2.5 samples were collected from 5 chicken houses and 4 duck houses in Shandong Province, China. Species-level diversity of bacterial and fungal components in PM2.5 samples was determined using advanced single-molecule real-time sequencing (SMRT) technology, based on the 16S and internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS) ribosomal genes. Microbial diversity and community composition varied significantly across the different poultry house. Notably, duck houses had higher concentrations (p < 0.01) of PM2.5 (92.8–143.1 μg/m3) than chicken houses (42.0–56.4 μg/m3). Furthermore, microbial variation in PM2.5 samples was associated with the type of poultry facility. The predominant pathogenic microorganisms included Aspergillus sydowii, Penicillium sp., Aspergillus insolitus, Cladosporium sp., Aspergillus sp., Aspergillus pseudoglaucus, Cladosporium sp. C4092-2-PD1, and Colletotrichum sp., all of which were classified as second category of pathogens. Aspergillus sydowii and Penicillium sp. were the dominant species in chicken houses, while Cladosporium sp., Aspergillus sp., and Aspergillus pseudoglaucus were the dominant species identified in duck houses. To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate bacterial and fungal diversity in PM2.5 samples collected from various types of poultry houses. These findings advance our understanding of poultry environmental microbiology and have important implications for safeguarding the health of both poultry and their caretakers.Key words: poultry house, PM2.5, bacteria; fungi, single-molecule real-time sequencing

INTRODUCTION

Microbial aerosols, which are suspended solids or liquids containing microorganisms and their metabolites, are crucial indicators of environmental air quality (Yu et al., 2016a; Lou et al., 2022). In recent years, the rapid expansion of intensive and large-scale poultry farming has created crowded and poorly ventilated environments that favor conditions for microbial aerosol pollution in poultry houses (Yu et al., 2016a; Yu et al., 2016b; Wang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2022; Qu et al., 2024). Microorganisms, such as bacteria and fungi, interact with fine particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter, generating PM2.5 microbial aerosols (Samake et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2024). These aerosols can enter the alveoli deep within the respiratory tract and infect multiple organs via blood–oxygen exchange, posing health risks to both animals and humans (Weichenthal et al., 2013; Fan et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020; Song et al., 2024). Understanding the biodiversity of PM2.5 microbial aerosols in poultry farming environments is therefore crucial for the prevention and control of animal diseases.

Second-generation high-throughput sequencing technology developed from PCR techniques has significantly advanced microbial diversity research. However, intrinsic limitations, such as exogenous gene mismatches, severely limit sequencing depth and genus-level analyses (Athanasopoulou et al., 2021). However, the advent of third-generation sequencing technology, such as single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing (Hestand and Ameur, 2019; Meslier et al., 2022), transcends the limitations of traditional microbial diversity analysis methods and enables direct species-level analysis without requiring PCR amplification (Song et al., 2023). Furthermore, combined with advanced bioinformatics methods, third-generation sequencing technology facilitates a comprehensive analysis of the entire genome structure of microbial communities in environmental contexts, maximizing the potential of biological resource exploration (Chen et al., 2020). Currently, this innovative technology is widely used in applications such as food safety assessment (Dong et al., 2022) and analysis of digestive tract microbial flora (Pereira et al., 2020). However, systematic studies on the biodiversity of PM2.5 microbial aerosols in poultry breeding facilities are limited.

This study used SMRT technology to analyze the diversity in PM2.5 microbial aerosol samples collected from different poultry farming environments. First, we evaluated the aerosol pollution levels in indoor environments in 5 chicken houses and 4 duck houses. Second, we compared PM2.5 samples from the different poultry houses to identify at the species level the major pathogenic bacteria and fungi found in the bioaerosols. Third, we investigated the factors influencing the distribution characteristics of the dominant microbial species in the different poultry houses. The results of this study will provide a fundamental framework for the precise control and prevention of airborne pathogen transmission in poultry breeding facilities.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample Collection

We objectively evaluated microbial aerosol pollution and diversity in nine representative poultry houses located in Shandong Province, China, a major poultry farming region. These included 5 chicken houses (Chic-a, Chic-b, Chic-c, Chic-d, and Chic-e) (35.38°N–36.28°N, 116.20°E–117.59°E), which employed the cage-rearing system, and 4 duck houses (Duck-A, Duck-B, Duck-C, and Duck-D) (35.41°N–37.26°N, 108.10°E–120.01°E), which adopted the free-range rearing method on nets. During the experiment, chickens and ducks had ad libitum access to feed and drink. Electric exhaust fans were used for ventilation, chickens and ducks moved freely in their respective breeding houses (Supplementary Figure S1).

Samples were collected mainly during autumn and winter, from October 2023 to March 2024 (Supplementary Table S1). During this period, the air environment in the poultry houses was relatively enclosed, ensuring the high reproducibility of the collected samples.

The collection of indoor PM2.5 samples required special experimental materials and instruments, including 0.45-µm glass microfiber filters (GF/B 1821-047/090, Whatman) and atmospheric particle collectors (ZR-3922, Qingdao Junrui Intelligent Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The PM2.5 inlets fitted in the aforementioned collectors meet the EN 12341 standard (Patel and Aggarwal, 2022). Furthermore, a portable T3 weather station (ZDZ-T3, Wuhan Hanqin Xintong Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to record critical indoor environmental factors in real time, such as PM2.5 concentration and temperature, with a 0.5-h monitoring interval.

Prior to sampling, PM2.5 fiberglass filters, forceps, sample storage containers, and associated experimental materials were sterilized in a high-temperature, high-pressure steam autoclave at 121°C for 25 min. Other nonpressure-resistant consumables or instruments were sterilized by wiping with 75% ethanol or exposure to ultraviolet light. Filters that had not been exposed to PM2.5 bioaerosols served as negative controls at each sampling location. The samplers were operated using default settings, with the inlet positioned at 1.2 m above ground level. After each sampling session, PM2.5 filters were promptly transferred into sterile sample containers, transported at 4°C, and stored at −80°C.

DNA Extraction

Indoor PM2.5 samples collected on glass microfiber filter membranes from different poultry houses were divided into 2 equal portions using sterile scissors. Half of the samples were subjected to DNA extraction, while the other half were stored at −80°C for further analysis. DNA extraction of PM2.5 samples was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol using an Omega DNA Isolation Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, D5625-01). The quality of the extracted DNA was verified by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop NC-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). DNA samples that passed the quality checks were stored at −80°C in an ultra-low temperature freezer until use for library construction.

PCR Amplification and PacBio Sequel II Sequencing

To analyze bacterial community diversity, the entire 16S rRNA gene (approximately 1500 bp) was amplified by PCR using the forward primer 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and the reverse primer 1492R (5′-ACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The amplification protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 98°C for 5 min, followed by 28–32 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 54°C for 35 s, extension at 72°C for 40 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 6 min. To analyze the fungal diversity, the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region (approximately 1300 bp) of the rRNA was amplified using the primer pair ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and LR3 (5′-CCGTGTTTCAAGACGGG-3′). The ITS regionamplification protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 98°C for 5 min, followed by 28–32 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 54°C for 35 s, extension at 72°C for 38 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 6 min. After purification using Agencourt AMPure Beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA), the PCR products were quantified using the PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing of both the 16S rRNA and ITS PCR products was performed on the PacBio Sequel II platform using Personalbio (http://www.personalbio.cn) according to standard procedures. Raw sequence data were submitted to the NCBI Short Read Archive database (BioProject accession number PRJNA1117626).

Bioinformatics Analysis

SMRT raw sequences were analyzed using the PacBio SMRT Link portal (version 5.0.1.9585). The Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2) software (version 2019.4) was used to analyze the bacterial and fungal community in accordance with the guidelines contained in the online tutorials (https://docs.qiime2.org/2019.4/tutorials/). Initial sequence denoising was performed using the DADA2 plugin (Callahan et al., 2016). Unmatched sequences were first filtered out using QIIME2, then subsequently processed using DADA2 for quality control, denoising, splicing, and chimera detection. The high-quality reads obtained were then aligned and grouped into nonsingleton amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) via MAFFT (Katoh et al., 2002). Taxonomic classification was performed by aligning the representative sequences of each ASV with the Silva (for bacterial sequences) and UNITE (Release 8.0, https://unite.ut.ee; for fungal sequences) databases. ASV rarefaction was performed to determine the relative position of each sample across taxonomic ranks, from genus to species.

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), a widely used method for assessing the influence of environmental factors on microbial diversity and composition, was performed using the Bray–Curtis distance matrix. The microbiome dataset was analyzed using the vegan package in R and QIIME2. PCoA was performed to determine the common and unique species in PM2.5 samples collected from various poultry houses. Alpha diversity indices, such as the Simpson, Pielou's evenness (Pielou_e), and Shannon indices, were calculated using ASV table data within the QIIME2 framework to evaluate microbial diversity. Furthermore, a linear discriminant analysis effect size was performed using default parameters to identify potential biomarkers among the different PM2.5 sample groups (Segata et al., 2011).

Pathogenic Microorganism and Functional Analyses

To accurately identify the microorganisms potentially pathogenic to both humans and poultry present in various poultry houses, we compared the 10 most relative abundances of bacterial and fungal species in this study with the species listed in the Catalogue of Pathogenic Microorganisms Infecting Humans (Vision 2021, Ministry of Health, P.R. China) (Song et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024).

Furthermore, to determine the biological functions of the airborne bacterial and fungal communities in various poultry environments, their pathogenicity, as well as the characteristics of microbial consortia, were evaluated. The metabolic functional profiles of bacteria and fungi from different sampling sites were analyzed using PICRUSt2 (Douglas et al., 2020). Bacterial functional attributes were determined using 16S rRNA sequencing data from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (https://www.kegg.jp/), while fungal functional predictions were made based on ITS sequencing data from the MetaCyc database (https://metacyc.org/) using default system parameters (https://github.com/picrust/picrust2/wiki) (Langille et al., 2013).

Statistical Analysis

PM2.5 concentrations, temperatures, and relative abundance were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA and multiple comparison tests were performed using SAS 9.1 software (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) to identify differences and determine statistical significance between different groups. Spearman's rank-difference coefficient of correlation analysis (Spearman's rho), also performed using SAS 9.1, was performed to determine the associations among PM2.5 concentrations, temperatures, and the relative abundance of prominent bacterial and fungal species. Statistical significance was defined at P-value < 0.05 or < 0.01.

RESULTS

PM2.5 Concentrations and Temperatures in Various Chicken and Duck Houses

During the sampling period, the average PM2.5 concentrations within duck housing environments were as follows: 143.1 μg/m3 (Duck-A); 126.9 μg/m3 (Duck-B); 92.8 μg/m3 (Duck-C); and 92.9 μg/m3 (Duck-D). Notably, these concentrations exceeded those recorded from chicken housing environments, which were on average 52.5 μg/m3 (Chic-a), 44.7 μg/m3 (Chic-b), 56.4 μg/m3 (Chic-c), 48.6 μg/m3 (Chic-d), and 42.0 μg/m3 (Chic-e) (P < 0.01) (Figure 1A). Similarly, the temperatures in the duck houses were 27.0°C (Duck-A), 22.8°C (Duck-B), 21.6°C (Duck-C), and 22.1°C (Duck-D). These values were also higher than those in the chicken houses: 16.8°C (Chic-a), 17.7°C (Chic-b), 17.4 °C (Chic-c), 15.2°C (Chic-d), and 16.8°C (Chic-e) (P < 0.01) (Figure 1B).

Bacterial and Fungal Diversity in PM2.5 Samples

The average count of ASVs detected from 27 samples were 48,148 for bacteria and 42,249 for fungi (Tables S2 and S3). Bacterial ASVs differed among houses; the ASVs in the Chic-a, Chic-b, Chic-c, Chic-d, Chic-e, Duck-A, Duck-B, Duck-C, and Duck-D groups were 3,862, 5,308, 3,728, 4,490, 6,502, 1,013, 1,504, 2,403, and 4,671, respectively, with a common ASV of 0 (Figure 2B). The number of fungal ASVs in the above houses were 1,700, 1,211, 188, 447, 3,008, 1,005, 1,665, 2,006, and 2,365, respectively, with only 31 common ASVs (Figure 2C).

Venn diagram of amplicon sequence variants (A, C); principal coordinate analysis plots (B, D); and Simpson, Pielou's evenness, and Shannon indices (E, F) for bacterial and fungal communities.

PCoA was performed to evaluate the similarities in beta diversity among the PM2.5 samples collected. Notably, the samples collected from similar types of poultry houses exhibited clustering (Figures 2B and 2D), indicating robust experimental reproducibility.

The Simpson, Pielou_e, and Shannon indices are standard metrics frequently employed as alpha diversity indices to characterize both the richness and evenness of microbial communities. These indices of PM2.5 samples collected from the duck houses differed significantly from those of samples collected from the chicken houses (Ρ < 0.05) (Figures 2E and 2F).

Bacterial and Fungal Taxonomy and Relative Abundance in PM2.5 Samples

In this study, we focused on 3 hierarchical taxonomic levels: phylum, family, and species. The predominant bacterial phyla observed were Bacillota (70.8%), Bacteroidota (9.5%), and Pseudomonadota (9.2%), collectively representing over 89.0% of all reads (Figure 3A). The dominant fungal phyla were Ascomycota (91.1%), Basidiomycota (8.3%), and Mucoromycota (0.5%), comprising more than 99.0% of all reads (Figure 3B).

Top 10 relative abundance of amplicon sequence variants of bacteria and fungi detected at the phylum (A, B), family (C, D), and species (E, F) levels.

The most abundant bacterial families exhibiting a relative abundance of more than 10.0% across all samples (Figure 3C) were Lactobacillaceae (22.4%) and Lachnospiraceae (12.7%). Notably, the predominant bacterial families varied across different types of poultry houses. Clostridiaceae (9.5%), Bacteroidaceae (10.4%), and Veillonellaceae (9.1%) were the predominant families in chicken houses, whereas Bacillaceae (4.6%), Aerococcaceae (7.3%), and Corynebacteriaceae (5.5%) were the predominant families in duck houses.

Within the top 10 fungal families with a relative abundance exceeding 10.0% across all samples, the most abundant family was Aspergillaceae (39.6%). Similar to bacterial results, the primary classification of fungal families varied across different poultry house types. In chicken houses, the predominant family was Clavicipitaceae (12.2%), whereas, in duck houses, the most dominant families were Debaryomycetaceae (32.7%) and Cladosporiaceae (14.9%) (Figure 3D).

Variations at the species level in the distribution of predominant bacterial and fungal species were also observed across different types of poultry houses. Five bacterial species were identified (≥5.0%) in chicken house samples, with Blautia wexlerae (18.2%), Lucifera butyrica (9.1%), Clostridium phoceensis (6.3%), Ligilactobacillus salivarius (7.5%), and Phocaeicola vulgatus (6.2%) being the most abundant. Notably, only 1 bacterial species, Pediococcus pentosaceus (5.7%), was detected (≥5.0%) in duck house PM2.5 samples (Figure 3E).

Three fungal species were identified (≥5.0%) in chicken house samples, with Aspergillus sydowii (28.2%), Aschersonia sp. A-8 (11.9%), and Penicillium sp. (10.0%) being the most abundant. The predominant fungal species identified In duck house samples were Suhomyces sp. (32.4%), Cladosporium sp. (8.0%), Aspergillus sp. (5.8%), and Aspergillus pseudoglaucus (7.4%) (Figure 3F).

Relationship Among Microbial Abundance Species, PM2.5 Concentration, and Temperature

A significant positive association (Ρ > 0.5) was observed between the relative abundance of the top 10 bacterial species and both PM2.5 concentrations and environment temperatures (Ρ < 0.05 or Ρ < 0.01) (Figure 4). Significant positive correlations were also observed between PM2.5 concentration and both temperature and 5 other fungal species (e.g., Aspergillus sydowii, Aschersonia sp. A-8, Penicillium sp., Cladosporium sp., and Aspergillus pseudoglaucus) (Ρ < 0.05 or Ρ < 0.01).

Second Category of Pathogens

Of the top 10 most abundant bacterial and fungal species identified, 8 fungal species (Aspergillus sydowii, Penicillium sp., Aspergillus insolitus, Cladosporium sp., Aspergillus sp., Aspergillus pseudoglaucus, Cladosporium sp. C4092-2-PD1, and Colletotrichum sp.) were retrieved from the Catalogue of Pathogens Infecting Humans and classified as second category pathogens (SCPs).

Significantly, the relative abundances of Aspergillus sydowii and Penicillium sp. were higher in duck houses than in chicken houses (Figure 5A and 5B). The relative abundance of Aspergillus pseudoglaucus was higher in duck houses than in chicken houses (Figure 5F).

Microbial Functions and Pathways

The most abundant metabolic pathway identified in bacteria was carbohydrate metabolism (Figure 6A), which is essential for energy production and the maintenance of cellular structures. Bacteria use carbohydrates as their main energy source. Other significant metabolic pathways identified were amino acid metabolism, as well as cofactor and vitamin metabolism. These results indicate that airborne bacteria in poultry breeding environments are actively involved in both biosynthetic and catabolic processes.

Metabolic functional profiles of bacteria (A) and fungi (B) in different poultry houses.

The most abundant pathway among fungi was nucleoside and nucleotide biosynthesis, which is critical for DNA and RNA syntheses for fungal growth and replication (Figure 6B). Metabolic pathways related to cofactors, prosthetic groups, and electron carrier biosynthesis also support fungal enzymatic functions and cellular respiration. Furthermore, pathways involved in respiration and electron transfer indicate fungal contributions to oxidative processes and energy production. Altogether, these results demonstrate the multifaceted metabolic activities of airborne fungi in poultry houses, underscoring their role in nutrient cycling and environmental dynamics.

Biomarker Prediction

Thirty bacterial and 25 fungal taxa exhibited significantly different abundances across the 27 PM2.5 samples (p < 0.05).

As shown in Figure 7A, g_Caryophanon, c_Negativicutes, c_Clostridia, o_Micrococcales, g_Bacillus, g_Streptococcus, and o_Sphingobacteriales were identified as potential biomarkers of airborne bacteria at the Chic-a, Chic-d, Chic-e, Duck-A, Duck-B, Duck-C, and Duck-D sampling sites, respectively. Similarly, c_Eurotiomycetes, o_Penicillium, o_Coronophorales, f_Sporormiaceae, g_Simplicillium, c_Saccharomyctes, and g_Rhizomucor were identified as airborne fungi biomarkers at the Chic-a, Chic-b, Chic-c, Chic-e, Duck-A, Duck-C, and Duck-D sampling sites, respectively (Figure 7B). The identification of this biomarkers provides essential preliminary information for future studies aimed at understanding the poultry ecosystem and developing strategies for managing the spread of airborne microbes in poultry breeding environments.

DISCUSSION

Several studies over the last 20 years have described the severe and widespread microbial aerosol contamination in poultry breeding environments (Banhazia et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2016b; Zhang et al., 2019a; Zhang et al., 2019b; Lou et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023), with bacteria and fungi being vital and prevalent microbial PM2.5 components (Yang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019). The concentration and microbial composition of these aerosols may indicate the sanitary conditions of the poultry environment. However, systematic studies on the biodiversity of PM2.5 microbial aerosols in different poultry breeding environments remain relatively limited.

Short- or long-term exposure to environments with high PM2.5 concentrations can cause several adverse health effects, including cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and increase mortality rates (Kioumourtzoglou et al., 2016; Erqou et al., 2018). Thus, accurately monitoring changes in PM2.5 concentrations is essential for reducing the risk of microbial aerosol infection in poultry farming environments.

This study found that PM2.5 concentrations ranged from 92.8 to 143.1 μg/m3 in duck houses, which are significantly higher (p < 0.01) than those in chicken houses (42.0 to 56.4 μg/m3). This difference may be attributed to the higher temperature typically observed in duck houses (23.4°C vs. 16.8°C), which promotes increased microbial activity and aerosolization. Previous studies have underscored the significant influence of temperature on total PM concentrations (Cambra-López et al., 2010).

Although no national or industry-safe levels have been established for PM2.5 concentrations in poultry breeding environments, the data obtained exceed the concentration limit set by the Ambient Air Quality Standard, which mandates a maximum daily mean PM2.5 concentration of 35.0 μg/m3 (Ling and Hui, 2019). Moreover, studies indicate that when the 24-h PM2.5 concentration surpasses 37.5 μg/m3, short-term mortality rates increase by 1.2% (Krzyzanowski and Cohen, 2008). These findings emphasize the potential health risks posed by elevated contaminant levels in poultry houses, both to humans and livestock.

The application of SMRT technology has revolutionized the analysis of microbial diversity at the species level, significantly improving both the efficiency of analysis and accuracy of results (Hestand and Ameur, 2019; Meslier et al., 2022; Song et al., 2024). In this study, SMRT technology was applied to investigate bacterial and fungal diversity in PM2.5 samples collected across different poultry houses.

The most abundant bacterial phyla in the PM2.5 samples collected in this study were Bacillota (70.8%), Bacteroidota (9.5%), and Pseudomonadota (9.2%) (Figure 3A), differing from other livestock houses where Firmicutes and Proteobacteria dominate (Zhang et al., 2019b; Yang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019). This difference may be caused by variations in environmental conditions, husbandry practices, and animal species, as well as seasonal and methodological differences.

Notably, the most abundant fungal phyla were Ascomycota (91.1%) and Basidiomycota (8.3%), consistent with those observed in other animal houses. In chicken houses, Ascomycota (39.5%–68.2%) and Basidiomycota (3.5%–37.5%) were the top 2 fungal phyla (Yang et al., 2018), whereas Ascomycota (65.9 %) and Basidiomycota (32.4%) were predominant in duck houses (Wu et al., 2019). Therefore, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota are common phyla in aerosols in poultry houses.

Furthermore, different types of poultry houses exhibited distinct distributions of predominant bacterial and fungal species. In chicken houses, the most abundant bacterial species were Blautia wexlerae (18.2%), Lucifera butyrica (9.1%), Ligilactobacillus salivarius (7.5%), and Phocaeicola vulgatus (6.2%). The most abundant fungal species were Aspergillus sydowii (28.2%), Aschersonia sp. A-8 (11.9%), and Penicillium sp. (10.0%). In contrast, the most abundant bacterial species in duck houses was Pediococcus pentosaceus (5.7%), while the predominant fungal species were Suhomyces sp. (32.4%), Cladosporium sp. (8.0%), Aspergillus sp. (5.8%), and Aspergillus pseudoglaucus (7.4%).

Thus, these pathogens may be ubiquitous in poultry breeding environments. However, they have not been described at the species level by researchers in related fields to date. This may be because these studies only performed analyses up to the genus level via next-generation sequencing technology. These findings highlight the specific microbial compositions associated with different poultry environments, reflecting both the unique microbial reservoirs inherent to different poultry species and the environmental conditions within their respective housing facilities.

SCPs pose a significant risk of severe diseases in both humans and animals, with high potential for nonzoonotic and zoonotic transmission (Song et al., 2023). In this study, 8 fungal species (Aspergillus sydowii, Penicillium sp., Aspergillus insolitus, Cladosporium sp., Aspergillus sp., Aspergillus pseudoglaucus, Cladosporium sp. C4092-2-PD1, and Colletotrichum sp.) were classified as SCPs.

Among the identified SCPs, the Aspergillus fungi are particularly noteworthy for their association with human respiratory infections. For instance, Aspergillus sydowii has been implicated in cases of pulmonary aspergillosis (Nagashima et al., 2023). Other Aspergillus species can trigger various allergic reactions and severe systemic infections, particularly in individuals with severe immunodeficiency (Paulussen et al., 2017). Penicillium, a diverse fungal genus found worldwide, produce a wide range of mycotoxins (Visagie et al., 2014) and can cause various invasive diseases (Lyratzopoulos et al., 2002). Colletotrichum sp., which is typically associated with economically significant plant diseases, can cause keratitis and subcutaneous and systemic infections in humans (Guarro et al., 1998; Fernandez et al., 2002). Cladosporium sp., is a common environmental saprobic fungus that has been documented as an etiologic agent for subcutaneous and deep infections in both humans and animals (Sandoval-Denis et al., 2016).

Notably, the relative abundances of Aspergillus sydowii, and Penicillium sp. species were higher in duck houses than in chicken houses (Figures 5A and 5B). The relative abundance of Aspergillus pseudoglaucus was higher in duck houses than in chicken houses (Figure 5F). This finding indicates that duck housing conditions provide more favorable ecological niches for these pathogens, potentially influenced by factors such as temperature and PM2.5 concentration.

These findings contribute to our understanding of pathogen ecology in poultry breeding environments and emphasize the need for further research to elucidate the complex interactions between pathogens and their host environments. Studies on comprehensive surveillance and management strategies will help reduce the impact of pathogens on both poultry and human health.

In this study, we explored the potential relationships among bacterial and fungal species abundance, PM2.5 concentration, and temperature in poultry houses. Our study found a significant positive association (ρ > 0.5) between the relative abundance of the top 10 bacterial species and both PM2.5 concentration and environmental temperature. Similarly, significant positive correlations were also found among PM2.5 concentration, temperature, and 5 fungal species. Thus, PM2.5 concentration and temperature may significantly impact the bacterial and fungal communities in poultry environments. Understanding these dynamics is critical for improving indoor air quality and implementing effective biosecurity measures.

CONCLUSION

Duck houses had significantly higher PM2.5 concentrations than chicken houses. Eight fungal species (Aspergillus sydowii, Penicillium sp., Aspergillus insolitus, Cladosporium sp., Aspergillus sp., Aspergillus pseudoglaucus, Cladosporium sp. C4092-2-PD1, and Colletotrichum sp.) were the SCPs identified in PM2.5 samples collected from 5 chicken and 4 duck houses in Shandong Province, China. Blautia wexlerae, Lucifera butyrica, Clostridium phoceensis, Ligilactobacillus salivarius, Phocaeicola vulgatus, Aspergillus sydowii, Aschersonia sp. A-8, and Penicillium sp. were the dominant bacterial and fungal species in chicken houses. In duck houses, the dominant species were Pediococcus pentosaceus, Suhomyces sp., Cladosporium sp., Aspergillus sp., and Aspergillus pseudoglaucus. These findings provide crucial insights into the microbial composition of PM2.5 in chicken and duck houses, laying a solid foundation for the development of strategies for preventing and controlling airborne infections in poultry environments. Elucidating the mechanisms governing the generation and transmission of these pathogens is essential for effectively managing potential poultry diseases.
