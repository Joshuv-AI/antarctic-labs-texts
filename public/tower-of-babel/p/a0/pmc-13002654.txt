Nerve dissection technique to optimize functional outcome: resection of cystic vestibular schwannoma: how I do it
Authors: Mohammed Hasen, Jihad Abdelgadir, Jeffrey Nadel, William T. Couldwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreserving facial nerve function during the gross total resection of a cystic vestibular schwannoma (CVS) is a significant surgical challenge. In this paper we describe our technical strategy to achieve this objective.MethodA 59-year-old man with progressive left-sided hearing loss had a 2.9-cm partially cystic lesion in the left internal auditory canal (IAC) and cerebellopontine angle (CPA). A translabyrinthine approach was used with early facial nerve identification, sharp dissection, delayed cyst deflation, and continuous neuromonitoring.ResultsGross total resection was achieved without facial nerve injury or brainstem violation.ConclusionWith deliberate planning and technical execution, gross total resection of CVS is possible while preserving neurological function, even in anatomically distorted and adherent lesions. Relevant surgical anatomy

Cystic vestibular schwannomas (CVSs) pose a surgical challenge because of their intimacy with the facial nerve (FN). Although gross total resection (GTR) is a primary objective, preserving FN function is key, given its impact on patient quality of life. A hallmark challenge is obliteration of the arachnoid plane, particularly near the brainstem and FN [7]. Occasionally, structural instability and adhesion-induced plane loss leave no safe corridor for gross total resection (GTR). Natural dissection boundaries may be obliterated by cystic degeneration, repeated microhemorrhages, and chronic inflammation promoting peritumoral fibrosis [7]. We describe the fundamental principles and core strategies of FN identification and dissection consistent across retrosigmoid, translabyrinthine, and middle fossa approaches, with emphasis on the commonly employed translabyrinthine approach.

Although studies comparing translabyrinthine and retrosigmoid approaches in CVS treatment have demonstrated no significant difference between approaches in major complication rates or FN outcomes, analyses rarely adjust for tumor morphology (including cystic versus solid subtypes) or report resection extent and FN outcomes by approach [3]. Consequently, although mixed cohorts have reported approach-specific differences in GTR, findings cannot be extrapolated to cystic tumors. CVS studies primarily emphasize surgical complexity related to tumor size and adhesiveness rather than approach-dependent outcomes, supporting individualized surgical decision-making based on hearing status, anatomy, and surgeon experience rather than presumed superiority of a single corridor [8].

The translabyrinthine approach carries distinct demands compared with retrosigmoid approaches, necessitating substantial temporal bony work that may prolong operative time, particularly in poorly pneumatized bones. Additionally, translabyrinthine surgery routinely necessitates abdominal fat harvesting and multilayer closure to minimize cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak risk, adding operative steps and donor-site morbidity. Nevertheless, studies have demonstrated that with meticulous closure techniques after translabyrinthine surgery, CSF leak rates rival those of other skull base approaches, supporting continued use when appropriate [4]. The senior author believes that the real value of the translab approach is to identify the facial nerve early in the dissection in the distal canal and also that the approach obviates the need for cerebellar retraction.

Understanding the FN's anatomy within the posterior fossa and internal auditory canal (IAC) is foundational. The FN originates from the brainstem anterior and superior to the vestibulocochlear nerve and traverses the cerebellopontine angle (CPA) cistern before entering the IAC. CVS growth often displaces the FN anteriorly or anterosuperiorly over its capsule, stretching it into a thin, ribbon-like structure. Alternatively, the nerve becomes displaced posteriorly or inferiorly or even splays through the tumor. The FN maintains its own arachnoid sheath, providing a crucial dissection plane. The nerve’s vascular supply must be preserved. The translabrynthine approach provides excellent CPA, IAC, and brainstem interface exposure, allowing early cranial nerve identification. However, the translabyrinthine approach is significantly constrained anatomically, limiting its application. Small or inadequately pneumatized mastoids, high-riding or anteriorly located jugular bulbs, anteriorly positioned sigmoid sinuses, or prior mastoid surgery can restrict the surgical corridor, increasing difficulty and compromising safe exposure of the internal auditory canal and cerebellopontine angle, necessitating alternative approaches. Thorough preoperative imaging assessment and case-specific approach selection are important.

Description of the technique

Video 1 illustrates the technique in a 59-year-old man who presented with progressive, severe left-sided hearing loss. MRI demonstrated a 2.9-cm partially cystic lesion in the left IAC and CPA and mild brainstem compression, consistent with CVS (Fig. 1). The patient was positioned supine, head turned to the right (Fig. 2). After mastoidectomy and labyrinthectomy, the IAC was skeletonized with 270-degree decompression, and dura over the posterior fossa and IAC was opened (Fig. 3). Cyst deflation was deliberately delayed until tumor architecture, dissection planes, and adjacent neurovascular structures were clearly defined. Dense adhesions between the superior pole of the tumor capsule and the trigeminal nerve, particularly near collapsed cyst walls, were sharply dissected with microscissors and suction. Ultrasonic aspiration internally debulked the solid component and reestablished safe corridors, altering internal resistance dynamics and displacing surrounding structures, requiring frequent spatial reassessment. In regions lacking clear planes, the dissection angle was adjusted strategically to minimize fascicular injury risk, favoring sharp dissection. A thin rim of capsule was left on the nerve, and the tumor rim was dissected using a fine hook and countertraction with soft cottonoids (Fig. 4). GTR was achieved without brainstem violation or bleeding. The FN was stimulated briskly at 0.02 mA post-resection, confirming preservation. A multilayered closure was performed to prevent postoperative CSF leak. Indications

Resection is indicated for patients with progressive symptoms, tumor growth, or brainstem compression. Key determinants in approach planning are morphology, cyst location, and hearing status. In patients with non-serviceable hearing, the translabyrinthine approach is preferred for early brainstem and meatal FN segment identification after anatomical distortion [5].

Anteriorly placed or multiloculated cysts are more likely to distort the brainstem and nerves, necessitating earlier intervention to avoid irreversible deficits [9]. Recent hemorrhage or instability are associated with greater peritumoral adhesion and surgical complexity. For symptomatic CSF pathway obstruction by the cystic mass, decompression to relieve intracranial pressure and prevent neurological decline may be urgent [10].

Limitations

Thin-walled CVSs can collapse or rupture, eliminating tactile feedback and causing distortion that obscures critical landmarks and increases the risk of traction, stretch, or thermal injury to nearby nerves [1, 6]. Anteriorly or medially located cysts often displace the FN, increasing vulnerability and identification difficulty [7]. Multiloculated CVSs can interface unpredictably with surrounding neurovascular structures, obscuring FN trajectory, particularly when cyst membranes rupture or collapse, eliminating planes of separation.

Dense adhesions to FN or brainstem further limit resectability. Sharp dissection using microscissors is preferred [1, 2], which may risk nerve injury. When no discernible dissection plane exists, anatomy is substantially distorted, or capsule is inseparable from FN or brainstem, dissection trajectory modification or leaving a thin tumor layer may preserve function and avoid iatrogenic injury [6, 8, 9]. These adaptations prioritize functionality and safety with maximal safe resection (MSR). MSR introduces theoretical tumor regrowth risk, requiring long-term surveillance [2]. Scar tissue from previous surgery or radiation can obscure dissection planes, increasing nerve injury risk and frequently necessitating MSR. Often, tumor capsule is inseparable from adjacent structures, creating unacceptable risk of neurological deficit with GTR [6, 9]. Rarely, tumor nerve infiltration renders complete anatomical separation impossible without sacrificing function.

How to avoid complications

Preventing complications requires anticipation, vigilance, and adaptability. Surgeons must be willing to change strategy and prioritize MSR based on tumor behavior using real-time feedback from monitoring and anatomy.

Plane obliteration in CVSs makes blunt dissection hazardous, particularly with densely adherent tumor capsule. Sharp, deliberate dissection techniques are often preferred [1, 2], particularly after cyst rupture, because internal resistance loss makes the surrounding tissues more vulnerable to displacement and injury.

The translabyrinthine approach offers early visualization and consistent access to the FN’s cisternal and meatal segments that retrosigmoid approaches may not allow, especially for anterior cysts or extensive brainstem adherence. Although early cyst decompression improves exposure, tumor collapse and distortion are more likely [1, 8]. Because key landmarks shift after decompression, dissection should pause until orientation reestablishment.

Severe adhesions may require altering dissection trajectory mid-surgery to avoid endangering functionality [6, 9]. Approaching the tumor from a different quadrant can significantly reduce risk. GTR may carry unacceptable neurological risk in cases with inseparable adhesion, making MSR with close postoperative imaging follow-up preferred to prioritize safety and functionality [6, 9]. The senior author often dissects tumors along the nerve plane medial-to-lateral by elevating tumor capsules while stabilizing nerves with a suction tip placed on a soft microcottonoid. For very adherent tumor at the porous acousticus lip, a lateral-to-medial trajectory may be employed starting in the IAC, minimizing residual tumor left to preserve functionality.

Electromyographic feedback and intraoperative neuromonitoring provide real-time dissection data, allowing early FN identification, especially after cyst collapse [5]. Higher-voltage stimulation may aid detection of displaced FN, especially after cyst decompression alters spatial relationships [5].

Imaging features are not always reliable. Extent of adhesions and loss of dissection plane discovered intraoperatively necessitate on-the-fly adjustments [8].

CVSs are associated with higher postoperative bleeding rates, possibly because of fragile tumor vasculature and the tendency to reaccumulate blood or fluid [10]. Monitoring for CSF pathway obstruction and hydrocephalus postoperatively [10] and using extra vigilance with hemostasis and early imaging can detect complications before clinical deterioration.

Specific information for the patient

House-Brackmann grade I-II FN function is achieved in 80–90% of solid vestibular schwannoma and 60–70% of CVS cases, particularly those requiring MSR or involving large, adherent cysts [9, 10]. Even with expert monitoring and careful technique, temporary or permanent facial weakness is possible.

CVSs require tailored strategies, and, to preserve function, intraoperative decisions may deviate from plans. MSR may be preferred over GTR to maximize safety [6, 9]. Tumor recurrence risk after MSR is low with regular MRI monitoring of small residual tumor [2].

CVSs have higher risk of postoperative complications than solid tumors because of the friable thin walls of CVSs and their proximity to CSF pathways [10]. Patients should be aware of warning signs, including headache, nausea, or confusion.
