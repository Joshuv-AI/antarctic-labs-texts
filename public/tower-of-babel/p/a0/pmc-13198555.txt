Cis-variation–trans-variation interactions between Hsp90 promoters shape G × E effects and underlie divergent phenotypic thermal adaptation plasticity in two congeneric oyster species
Authors: Zhuxiang Jiang, Chaogang Wang, Mingyang Du, Rihao Cong, Ao Li, Wei Wang, Guofan Zhang, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal warming may drive adaptive evolution by influencing natural selection and utilizing temperature-related phenotypic plasticity. However, predicting the evolutionary patterns of phenotypic plasticity under climate change remains a challenge, underscoring the need to elaborate on the underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms. In this study, we focus on the expression plasticity divergence of heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90), which is temperature responsive and exhibits a strong selective sweep in the upstream noncoding region of two allopatric congeneric oyster species: cold-adapted Crassostrea gigas and warm-adapted Crassostrea angulata. Functional characterization confirmed Hsp90 expression as an ideal proxy for thermotolerance. The evolutionary divergence in constitutive and plastic expression patterns represents adaptation to the mean and variance in habitat temperature, respectively. By combining forward and reverse genetic approaches, four causative loci with G + G × E effects were identified in the Hsp90 promoter regions of C. gigas and C. angulata, indicating cis-variations. Moreover, the g.-2291G allele of the causative locus in C. angulata is specifically bound to by the positive transcription factor purine-rich element binding protein B (PURB), explaining the constitutive expression of Hsp90. Meanwhile, the response of PURB to thermal stress determines the magnitude of plastic Hsp90 expression in C. angulata. This integrative study revealed that cis-variations interact with trans-variations and underlie the G × E effect under environmental changes, thereby mediating the divergence in plastic gene expression. Furthermore, we established a paradigm for studying genetic variants and their G × E impacts at a finer resolution, i.e., single-nucleotide level, in nonmodel organisms. The findings may deepen our understanding of the significant role of phenotypic plasticity in modulating adaptive responses and promote predictions of adaptive potential in marine organisms under climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s42995-026-00373-6.Keywords: Phenotypic plasticity, Temperature, G × E, Hsp90, Cis-variation, Oyster

Introduction

Phenotypic plasticity is defined as the capacity of a single genotype to produce multiple phenotypes in response to varying environmental conditions (Pfennig et al. 2010, 2021). It is a widely observed phenomenon in which the environment interacts closely with genetic variation within natural populations (Pigliucci et al. 2006). Under the threat of global warming, adaptive phenotypic plasticity may allow organisms to cope with rapid changes in environmental temperature and thereby maintain population resilience or persistence (Ghalambor et al. 2007). To measure such abilities, thermal adaptive traits are commonly quantified along temperature gradients and derive reaction norms (Chevin et al. 2010; de Villemereuil et al. 2018; Kelly 2019; Li et al. 2018). Such norms are determined by constitutive expression at an optimal temperature and plastic expression in response to thermal stress; the magnitude of the latter is indicated by the steepness of the slope within the linear part (Li et al. 2019; Schaum et al. 2022). They are linked to the mean and variance in the environmental temperatures, respectively (Sandoval-Castillo et al. 2020). The effects of climate change are expected to vary across spatial scales (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 2014), with temperature-associated environmental shifts mainly occurring along the mean and variance (Alley 2000; Logan and Cox 2020). This hypothesis signifies a comprehensive understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying thermal tolerance, including the constitutive and plastic expression of temperature-associated traits, which remain poorly studied in marine organisms.

Organisms respond to temperature changes via adaptive traits occurring at multiple levels, including morphology (Simola et al. 2016), physiology (Metallo and Vander Heiden 2013), and gene expression (Gibert et al. 2016). The latter is often used as a miniphenotype for investigating plastic responses (Bedulina et al. 2013; Gibert et al. 2016; Grishkevich and Yanai 2013). Divergence in gene expression plasticity is widely observed among naturally differentiated species (Aubin-Horth and Renn 2009; Gibert et al. 2016; Monteiro et al. 2015), with the regulatory regions of environment-response genes playing a pronounced role in adaptation to spatial climatic gradients (Kelly 2019). Variations in gene transcription are mediated by interactions between cis- and trans-regulatory elements within gene regulatory networks (Bao et al. 2019; Signor and Nuzhdin 2018; Wittkopp et al. 2004). Cis-regulatory elements are short, typically noncoding sequences adjacent to coding regions, which comprise specific binding sites for trans-acting factors (Bao et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023; Wittkopp and Kalay 2012). Candidate genetic variations associated with gene–environment interaction (G × E) in plastic or environmentally responsive traits have been identified (Grishkevich and Yanai 2013; Lovell et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2023a). However, pinpointing precise gene variants and investigating their functional mechanisms remains a challenge. In most studies, variants putatively involved in G × E interactions have been identified employing forward genetic approaches such as genome-wide association studies and quantitative trait locus mapping to detect signals associated with plasticity responses and calculate their statistical (Chen et al. 2023), combined with reciprocal transplantation experiments to investigate genetic variations in naturally divergent populations adapted to heterogeneous environments significance (Lafuente and Beldade 2019). The causative variants underlying G × E interactions in yeast were identified at the single-nucleotide level using high-throughput forward and reverse genetic approaches (Chen et al. 2023). However, achieving this in nonmodel organisms remains a challenge due to the lack of molecular tools such as genome editing.

In studying adaptive evolution, related species that adapt to different environments can be used to examine the role of plasticity in evolution (Grant and Grant 2002; Kingsolver and Buckley 2017). Owing to the extreme and highly variable daily and annual temperatures experienced by intertidal organisms, they have evolved pronounced and complex heat-tolerance plasticity (Brahim and Marshall 2020; McCoy and Widdicombe 2019). Prominent genotype–environment interactions in oysters play a significant role in environmental adaptation (Hughes et al. 2017; Li et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023a, b). Crassostrea gigas and C. angulata are two allopatric congeneric oyster species adapted to cold (Northern China) and warm (Southern China) habitats, respectively (Ren et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2008, 2010). They exhibited elevated constitutive and low plastic expression of crucial heat (Du et al. 2024a, b) and cold adaptation-related genes (Wang et al. 2023). The elevated thermal limits (LT50) of C. angulata under acute heat shock conditions suggest an enhanced potential for thermotolerance (Ghaffari et al. 2019). Additionally, integrating resequencing and transcriptomic data revealed that variations in gene noncoding regions and transcriptional plasticity play critical roles in shaping thermal tolerance in oysters (Li et al. 2021). Among the selected C. angulata HSPs, Hsp90 demonstrated the most notable plasticity in response to heat stress along with a selective sweep in its upstream noncoding region (Li et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2021). This finding aligns with those of extensive studies confirming the critical role of Hsp90 in thermal adaptation (Gibson et al. 2016; Sreedhar et al. 2004). Therefore, Hsp90 expression levels across different temperature ranges can be quantified to investigate the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying phenotypic plasticity.

In this study, we characterized the functional role of Hsp90 in thermal adaptation and investigated the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying its divergent expression patterns between two congenic oyster species adapted to relatively warm and cold habitats. It serves as a case study and establishes a paradigm for recognizing gene variants underlying phenotypic plasticity and the associated G × E effects at a finer resolution. It helps develop gene-manipulation tools to breed novel C. gigas varieties with enhanced thermal tolerance to resist summer-season mortality, affecting the global oyster cultivation industry. Moreover, it enhances our understanding of the remarkable role of phenotypic plasticity in adaptive responses and aids in predicting the adaptive potential of marine organisms under climate change conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

For the RNAi experiments, 20 C. gigas were prepared for siRNA-based selection, and 300 individuals were identified for post-RNAi heat shock experiments. For mutation screening, wild individuals of C. gigas and C. angulata (n = 30) were sampled from their natural habitats. Moreover, wild C. gigas (n = 96) and C. angulata (n = 98) individuals were collected from different populations to verify the presence of the mutations. F2 progeny of a hybridization between two congeneric oyster species, conducted by a previous study (Du et al. 2024a), were included for further validation. Spawning and experiment details are described in Supplementary Text S1.

RNAi experiments

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were synthesized by GenePharma; their sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. The interference duration for a selected siRNA was optimized during the preliminary experimentation phase. Based on the outcomes of pilot experiments, we identified the most efficacious siRNA (Hsp90-742) and subsequently administered identical injections to these three groups, each consisting of 100 individuals (Details in Supplementary Text S2).

Short-term heat stress experiments

C. gigas and C. angulata were acclimated for a week in 500 L tanks containing seawater maintained at room temperature. For sublethal experiments, they were exposed to 37 °C for varying durations. Gills were sampled, flash-frozen, and stored for further analysis. This protocol was also followed for the F2 populations, with 400 oysters subjected to the same conditions of heat stress (Details in Supplementary Text S3). For semilethal experiments, they were exposed to 42 °C, and the dead individuals were counted each hour.

Overexpression experiments

Total RNA from C. gigas was extracted, reverse-transcribed to first-strand cDNA, and the Hsp90 open reading frames (ORFs) were identified and cloned into a pCMV-N-mCherry vector. The forward and reverse primers are listed in Supplementary Table S2. HEK293 cells were transfected with the pCMV-Hsp90 plasmid and subjected to heat stress at 42 °C for 1 h to assess apoptosis and necrosis by flow cytometry and laser confocal microscopy. For the subcellular localization, HeLa cells were transfected with the same construct and subjected to heat stress at 42 °C for 1 h. Then, cell membranes were stained with DiO solution, and nuclei with Hoechst 33,342. Confocal images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope, and images were processed with the LSM Software ZEN 2012 (Details in Supplementary Text S4).

qRT-PCR experiments

Total RNA from the gill tissues of C. gigas and C. angulata (n = 15 each) was extracted and used for first-strand cDNA synthesis. Primers were designed utilizing Primer 5 and synthesized by RuiBiotech (Supplementary Table S3). qPCR was performed in three biological replicates, each consisting of an equimolar mixture of cDNA synthesized using RNA from five oysters as a template; Ef-1α served as the internal control, employing the 2−ΔΔCT method to calculate relative expression levels (Details in Supplementary Text S5).

Western blotting

HEK293T transformants were harvested, lysed in M-PER™ Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent with a protease inhibitor cocktail, and the protein-containing supernatant was collected post-centrifugation. Proteins were denatured with 4X protein loading buffer, separated by 12% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked, incubated with primary (anti-Flag) and secondary antibodies. Proteins were detected with an ECL Western blotting substrate and a Gel Doc™ XR System (Details in Supplementary Text S6).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The Hsp90 cis-regulatory regions from C. gigas and C. angulata were cloned into the pGL3-basic vector. Luciferase was assayed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System and quantified on a Varioskan Flash multimode reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample (Details in Supplementary Text S7).

Screening of causative promoter variations

To investigate variations in the Hsp90 promoters between C. gigas and C. angulata, 30 samples from each species were collected for genomic DNA extraction. After DNA quality assessment and PCR amplification of the Hsp90 promoter, sample-specific pools were created, each containing 30 samples of the same species. These pools were subjected to NGS sequencing on an Illumina platform. Subsequently, potential SNPs and Indels were identified using HaplotypeCaller, and the statistical significance of the variations between the two species was determined using a χ2 test based on site sequencing depth data. To cross-validate the identified genotype variations, additional samples from both species were collected, their DNA was sequenced, and the data were analyzed accordingly. LD and heritability analysis (R2 value) were calculated employing SHEsis (Shi and He 2005) (Details in Supplementary Text S8).

Functional analysis of mutations

Mutations were introduced at each variant site within the pGL3-Hsp90 plasmid. Primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Supplementary Table S4), and the luciferase reporter assay procedure was the same as that described above (Details in Supplementary Text S9).

DNA pulldown assay

The DNA pulldown assay involved preparing nuclear extracts from C. gigas gill tissue, and nuclear protein extraction was validated by Western blotting. Biotin-labeled probes (Supplementary Table S5) were designed—based on selected SNPs—and immobilized onto streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. These probes were mixed with nuclear protein extracts, incubated, washed, and eluted to collect the bound proteins. MS analysis of the eluted proteins was performed, and regulatory proteins that potentially interact with the mutated sites were identified through a comparative analysis between the probe and control groups (Details in Supplementary Text S10).

EMSA

The ORFs of Purbs were identified by screening the oyster genome and subsequently amplified with PCR. The amplicons were ligated with the pCMV-N-Flag vector. After transfection of cells with plasmids, the supernatant protein-containing fraction was extracted, and Western blotting was used to detect PURB protein contents. The supernatant protein was then used to functionally characterize a selected SNP and its potential impact on DNA–protein interactions via EMSA using biotinylated DNA probes (Details in Supplementary Text S11).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed on gill tissue homogenates prepared with a lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor. Protein G Plus Magnetic Beads were incubated with either anti-HSP90 or mouse IgG antibodies (control), followed by incubation with the supernatants. After washing, the samples were denatured and subjected to SDS-PAGE for MS-based protein identification (Details in Supplementary Text S12).

Data collection and statistics analysis

Monthly mean sea surface temperature (SST) data were obtained from the Oceanographic Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The dataset covered the nearshore areas of China, including the Bohai, Yellow, East China, and South China Seas. The datasets are available at https://datapid.cn/CSTR:33685.11.IOCAS.20231223.016. The figure explaining the cell mechanism was created using BioRender (https://www.biorender.com). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted utilizing Omicshare CloudT001s (http://www.omicshare.com/tools/?l=en-us).

In the analysis of the effect of each locus, significant changes in constitutive expression (in the absence of environmental factors) were considered as G effects. One-way and two-way ANOVA were employed in the luciferase reporter assay to investigate the significance of both genotype and environment, and to examine whether certain loci exhibited genotype–environment interaction (G × E effects). These tests were used to assess the G and G × E effects of genetic variations through GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2). In the hybrid F2 population, a linear mixed model was applied to analyze the statistical significance of G, G × E, and G + G × E effects employing SPSS (version 27). Statistically significant inter-group variations were denoted at *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01. (Details in Supplementary Text S13).

Results

Functional characterization of oyster Hsp90

To elucidate the role of oyster Hsp90 in thermal adaptation, we conducted a series of experiments at the cellular and organismal levels. Mcherry-Hsp90 was used to assess the effects of heat stress on HSP90 protein levels in HeLa cells by confocal laser scanning microscopy. The results demonstrated a stable cytoplasmic expression of red fluorescence emitted by the Mcherry-Hsp90 molecule under control conditions, whereas under heat stress, the red fluorescence signal increased substantially, indicating the inducibility of CgHsp90 in response to heat stress (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, flow cytometry revealed that heat stress induced significant cell apoptosis (P < 0.01). In contrast, the HSP90 overexpression group exhibited a significantly lower proportion of apoptotic cells than the control group (Fig. 1B, P < 0.01). To further functionally validate C. gigas Hsp90, RNAi experiments were conducted to evaluate alterations in thermal tolerance. In a preliminary experiment, siRNA-742 markedly reduced CgHsp90 expression after 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S1); consequently, it was selected for further experiments as the most effective siRNA, and 24 h was determined as the optimal time point. In the preliminary experiment, we evaluated the significance of Hsp90 for thermotolerance in oysters through heat shock (42 °C) treatment post-siRNA injection. In this heat shock experiment, following RNAi knockdown, the survival rate of the CgHsp90-RNAi group was significantly lower than that of the nonsense strand (NC) (P < 0.05) and H2O groups (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1C). The mortality rates of these three groups after 15 h of heat exposure were 33, 27, and 9%, respectively (Fig. 1D).

Moreover, we performed label-free quantification (LFQ) to identify HSP90-interacting proteins and to study the underlying network of HSP90-regulatory proteins. Western blots confirmed the HSP90-capturing efficiency of the anti-HSP90 antibody, allowing us to putatively recognize 491 HSP90-associated proteins from oyster gill lysates (Fig. 2A). Although potential epitope-masking or nonmodel species cross-reactivity issues caused by limited antibody-specificity may contribute to false-negative results, a more than theefold enrichment in these interacting proteins endows high confidence in their specificity to HSP90. Among these 491 proteins, 295 specifically bound with HSP90 (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S6). These included HSP70, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK1), NADH dehydrogenase alpha 1 (NDUFA1), 26S proteasome non-ATPase regulatory subunit 1 (PSMD1), and Tripartite Motif Containing 9 (TRIM9). GO classification primarily associated these 295 proteins with cellular processes, single-organism processes, metabolic processes, cells, cell parts, organelles, binding, and catalytic activity (Fig. 2B). KEGG enrichment demonstrated that these proteins were significantly associated with 35 pathways (P < 0.05). The most representative proteins were mainly enriched in metabolic pathways (valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation; oxidative phosphorylation; glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism; O-antigen nucleotide sugar biosynthesis; the TCA cycle and pyruvate metabolism), protein translation pathways (ribosome and mRNA surveillance pathway), and cell-structure-related pathways (regulation of actin cytoskeleton, tight junction, and gap junction) (Fig. 2C).

Screening for genetic variations with a potential causative role

To investigate phenotypic plasticity in response to heat stress, we analyzed the expression patterns of Hsp90 as a molecular indicator. A comparative analysis revealed significantly enhanced basal transcription of Hsp90 in C. angulata than in C. gigas (6.3-fold, P < 0.01). The two species exhibited upregulated gene expression at 6 and 12 h under heat stress, with greater magnitudes in C. gigas at 6.1-fold and 16.2-fold (P < 0.05) compared with 0 h. Moreover, the expression peaked at 12 h in C. gigas and then sharply declined until the 24 h mark. In contrast, with C. angulata, the upregulation was slow at 6 h (1.2-fold) and 12 h (1.4-fold), reaching a peak (2.6-fold), only after after 24 h (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A). The more prominently constitutive expression of Hsp90 in C. angulata corresponded to higher mean temperatures, while the more sensitive expression pattern observed in C. gigas was consistent with the pronounced temperature variance seen in their habitats; detailed in Supplementary Text S14 (Fig. 3B). The transcription capacity of these cloned Hsp90 promoters was assessed by employing the luciferase reporter assay. The activity of the Hsp90 promoter was upregulated in response to heat stress (42 °C for 1 h), the magnitude of such a response was consistently greater with C. gigas (8.5-fold), compared with C. angulata (7.5-fold) (Fig. 3C). Therefore, we investigated the species-specific variations in the CgHsp90 promoter; quality analysis confirmed the efficacy of library construction and reliability of our sequencing data (Supplementary Table S7). Chi-square tests identified six SNPs and three InDels to vary significantly between the two species (Supplementary Table S8, P < 0.05). Further genotype-based identification using expanded populations—94 and 100 wild C. gigas and C. angulata, respectively—verified the accuracy of these variations (Supplementary Table S9, P < 0.05, χ2 test).

Identification of G × E effects on the genetic variations detected

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the effects of the identified genetic variations, we assessed changes in transcriptional activity and used an F2 self-sewing population of C. angulata ♀ × C. gigas ♂ (AG × AG F2) to evaluate G × E effects at each site under heat stress. The three SNPs (g.-2291 T > G, g.-1736A > T, and g.-821C > T) and one indel (g.-1511ATT > A) caused a significant decline in transcriptional activity compared to the unmutated plasmid under non-stress conditions (pGL3-CgHsp90, Fig. 3D). Additionally, two of these SNPs (g.-2291 T > G and g.-1736A > T) significantly increased transcription magnitude under heat stress. Moreover, these four variations were identified with G and G × E effects via a two-way ANOVA.

To further verify the G × E effect of these four variations in vivo (within oysters), we utilized the AG × AG F2 population—which shared a homogenized genetic background with C. angulata and C. gigas—and quantified Hsp90 transcription in each genotype at different time points of heat stress. Linear mixed-model analysis also showed that these four variations were associated with G + G × E effects. The most strongly linked loci between the two species showed lower linkage disequilibrium (LD) in the F2 population, confirming a partial disruption of LD association (Fig. 4A). Moreover, each locus distinctly influenced Hsp90 expression, with varying magnitudes across environments (Supplementary Table S10). Hsp90 expression with different genotypes of each locus demonstrated that the C. angulata homozygous allele maintained high expression under 24 h of thermal stress, whereas the C. gigas homozygous allele showed a more sensitive expression pattern, reaching a peak at 6 or 12 h. Notably, Hsp90 expression patterns were consistent between homozygous genotypes with the g.-2291 SNP and their respective wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. 4B).

Functional analysis of the G × E effect in cis-variations

The four variants were screened for upstream regulatory transcription factors (TFs) via DNA pulldown experiments. The nuclear protein marker—histone H3—confirmed the quality of the extracted proteins (Supplementary Fig. S2), whereas silver staining consistently detected most proteins to exist in a bound state, possibly due to their low concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S3). Mass spectrometry (MS)-based detection revealed a pulldown of 22, 30, 16, 14, 21, 22, 14, 40, and 26 proteins by g.-2291 T, g.-2291G, g.-1736A, g.-1736 T, g.-1511ATT, g.-1511A, g.-821C, g.-821 T, and empty magnetic bead groups, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4A). After excluding the empty magnetic bead-bound proteins, we identified the proteins pulled by the WT and mutant probes, along with their specific binding partners, as potential binding partners. Among the binding proteins that were identified based on a functional annotation of the oyster genome—we screened candidate TFs—considering their common or specific interactions with WT and mutant probes. The process recognized Pur-beta (PURB) as a binding protein pulled down by the g.-2291 probe. We then performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to verify the binding specificity of PURB to the Hsp90 promoters from C. angulata and C. gigas. The overexpression of PURB-Flag (Supplementary Fig. S4B) impeded the electrophoretic mobility of the probe containing the C. angulata allele region (g.-2291G), whereas it did not interact with the C. gigas allele region (g.-2291 T) probe (Supplementary Fig. S4B, lanes 2 and 6). Unlabeled probe-based competition assays effectively competed with specific interactions, eliminating shifted bands and confirming specific binding between PURB-Flag and the C. angulata Hsp90 promoter (Supplementary Fig. S4B, lane 7). Furthermore, FLAG tag-overexpression did not alter the electrophoretic mobility patterns (Supplementary Fig. S4B, lane 8), indicating non-interactability between the HEK293T cell line protein and the C. angulata allele region (g.-2291G) probe. Notably, a previous HSP90 functional characterization also reported the absence of direct PURB–HSP90 binding, suggesting that PURB may be specific to the HSP90 promoter. Subsequently, we conducted a luciferase reporter assay to investigate the regulatory relationship between PURB and the Hsp90 promoter in C. angulata. PURB-overexpression significantly increased the relative luciferase activity of pGL3-CgHsp90; g.-2291G, C. angulata allele (Supplementary Fig. S4B, P < 0.01). However, luciferase activity did not vary remarkably when PURB was cotransfected with pGL3-CgHsp90; g.-2291 T, C. gigas allele (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Western blotting confirmed the protein overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Further investigation of PURB expression trends in the two species revealed divergent patterns during heat shock. PURB expression significantly decreased at 6 h and 12 h (P < 0.01) but significantly increased at 24 h (P < 0.01) in C. angulata, but exhibited the opposite pattern in C. gigas (Supplementary Fig. S4D).

Discussion

In our study, the sharp increase in CgHsp90 expression observed confirmed the ability of Hsp90 to respond to heat stress, which is consistent with surveys of aquatic organisms, indicating that frequent activation of the heat shock response (HSR) is a biochemical strategy for adaptation to variable thermal environments (Tomanek 2010). Several studies on S. cerevisiae (Duina et al. 1998), Ostrinia furnacalis (Quan et al. 2022), and Homo sapiens (Horowitz 2014) confirmed the critical role of HSP (Samali et al. 2001), consistent with the decrease in thermal tolerance observed after CgHsp90 Knockdown. The inhibition of apoptosis following CgHsp90 overexpression further indicates the role of HSP90 in enhancing thermotolerance by protecting against cell damage. The interaction of proteins involved in heat shock responses—such as HSP70—and those associated with ubiquitination—such as TRIM9 and PSMD1—indicate the role of HSP90 in protecting misfolded proteins and facilitating the degradation of excessively damaged proteins, which are crucial for cell proteostasis and promoting recovery during thermal stress (Maxwell et al. 2021; Mayer and Bukau 2005). Our study observed a significant enrichment of the HSP90-interacting proteins involved in energy metabolism pathways. Moreover, given the energy costs of HSR and ubiquitination (Maxwell et al. 2021), these results suggest that HSP90 may enhance energy metabolism to support HSRs and ubiquitination, thereby increasing thermotolerance in oysters. Considering its central role, the expression patterns of Hsp90 provide an excellent case study of environmentally responsive genes for investigating phenotypic plasticity that helps organisms adapt to changing temperatures.

Adaptation of oysters to thermal stress is driven by genes involved in energy metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and stress response pathways, with HSPs playing a central role in mediating thermal tolerance (Chen et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). Higher constitutive expression of Hsp90 is beneficial for the warm-adapted species—C. angulata—to maintain protein homeostasis in warm habitats (southern), representing a thermal adaptation strategy observed in organisms inhabiting warm environments (Oksala et al. 2014; Woodruff et al. 2022). Conversely, the pronounced plasticity of Hsp90 transcription enables C. gigas to adapt to environmental temperature fluctuations, thereby widening the temperature adaptation range of the organism. Collectively, these findings imply that the molecular norm of thermal adaptation, characterized by Hsp90 expression, is broader in C. angulata and higher in C. gigas, determined by plastic and constitutive expression, respectively, suggesting that both patterns contribute to adaptation with a specific nature of temperature change—mean and variance, respectively. Similar responses to stable or fluctuating environments have been detected in ectotherms across spatial environmental gradients at various latitudes (Gunderson and Stillman 2015), geographic sites (Kenkel and Matz 2016), and tidal zones (Tomanek 2002), with a much wider range of acute thermal conditions shaping elevated HSP plasticity. Moreover, under thermal stress, a higher constitutive and lower plastic expression of Hsp90 in C. angulata than in C. gigas represents the trade-offs seen in the transcription of environment-responsive genes in oysters (Wang et al. 2023), as well as in other organisms adapted to divergent environments (Bedulina et al. 2013; Fangue et al. 2006; Li et al. 2019; Rago et al. 2019). Meanwhile, the recognition of the same pattern in cold-adapted genes, such as Scd (stearoyl-CoA desaturase) in C. gigas, suggests that plasticity is trait (gene-type)- and environment-dependent (Pigliucci et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2023).

Several studies have compared locally adapted populations, indicating that adaptive evolution in the expression of environment-responsive genes is often mediated by variations in their regulatory regions, particularly the promoters (Kelly 2019; Lasky et al. 2014). Here, we focused on an identified core region—the promoter—where genetic polymorphisms are correlated significantly with Hsp90 expression in oysters. Specifically, low constitutive activity and high plastic upregulation indicate its role as a selection hotspot and its contribution to divergence in gene expression (Grishkevich et al. 2012; Tangwancharoen et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2023). To gain a broader understanding of the genetic basis of these patterns, we validated the gene variants identified between the two species using cell-level experiments and an F2 hybrid population. Gene expression divergence is regulated by four causative cis-variations within the Hsp90 promoter, with G and G × E effects under heat stress, consistent with findings in other environmentally responsive genes (Lovell et al. 2016; Wittkopp and Kalay 2012). These observations support the common occurrence of G × E effects from cis-variations in such genes. Unlike in model organisms, where precise parallel editing is available, this study applied a single-nucleotide mutation at the cellular level, along with associative gene expression analysis, to explore the G × E effect at the single-nucleotide level. More generally, we established a paradigm for studying gene variants and their G × E effects at a finer resolution in nonmodel organisms by integrating forward and reverse genetic approaches.

Gene expression is regulated by TF binding to promoters (Zhang and Zhou 2014). Variations in the g.-2291 locus can disrupt the TF (PURB)-binding site and interact with thermal stress to collaboratively control constitutive and plastic expression of the associated genes (Hsp90) through promoter associations rather than direct binding, as was demonstrated with the regulatory regions of environment-responsive genes (Grishkevich et al. 2012; Sato et al. 2016; Wittkopp and Kalay 2012). The variation at the g.-2291 T > G locus (C. gigas allele > C. angulata allele) observed in our study is consistent with previous findings, suggesting that Pur proteins bind to G-rich elements in nucleic acids (Johnson et al. 2013). Intersite cooperativity in PURB contributed to a ~ 12-fold increase in nucleoprotein complex stability in mice (Ramsey and Kelm 2009), which may account for the pronounced constitutive expression of Hsp90 in C. angulata. Additionally, the G × E effects of the g.-2291 locus suggest that cis-variations within the Hsp90 promoter can potentially influence constitutive expression (G) and modulate plastic expression in response to environmental changes (G × E) in C. angulata. Our study indicated that PURB expression showed an opposite trend—upregulated in C. angulata but downregulated in C. gigas under thermal stress—which may explain the low plasticity in Hsp90 transcription in C. angulata. Such a divergent pattern, mediated by coordination between cis- and trans-elements, has also been reported for Scd (Wang et al. 2023). Therefore, we propose that the cis-variation (g.-2291) with G + G × E effects regulates variation in phenotypic plasticity patterns—constitutive expression and heat sensitivity—in a key heat-responsive gene, Hsp90, by influencing its interaction with the environment-responsive gene PURB, which encodes a TF. Our study indicates that cis-variation, a hotspot of environmental selection, interacts with trans-variation to shape G and G × E effects in response to climate change. However, the plastic expression patterns of C. gigas genes remain unclear, suggesting the involvement of other molecular mechanisms.

Conclusion

Here, we found that the constitutive expression and heat plasticity of Hsp90 are associated with adaptations to the mean and variance of temperature. C. angulata (warm adapted) demonstrated greater constitutive and lower plastic expression compared to C. gigas (cold adapted), indicating that controlling Hsp90 expression intensity at a particular time—to mitigate energy costs—is an optimal strategy for adaptation to environmental changes. By combining forward and reverse genetic approaches, we identified tightly clustered causative cis-variations with G + G × E effects at the single-nucleotide level within the promoter region. Constitutive expression is regulated through cis-variation to create (g.-2291G, C. angulata) or delete (g.-2291 T, C. gigas) TF (PURB)-binding motifs, and plastic expression is influenced by trans-variation (PURB transcription), responsive to thermal stress. In summary, the divergent patterns in plastic expression were regulated via an interaction between cis- and trans-variations induced by thermal stress. Moreover, the identified genetic variations provide valuable targets for enhancing heat resistance in oysters, offering potential solutions to mitigate summer mass mortalities in the oyster industry. Our study serves as a case study demonstrating how temperature changes—mean and variance—shape alterations in plastic (G × E) responses and provides new insights into predicting the adaptive potential of marine organisms as the climate continues to change.
