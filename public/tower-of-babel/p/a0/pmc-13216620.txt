GACNet: A graph attention-based neural network for electroencephalography localization via contrastive learning
Authors: Thi-Oanh Ha, Huong-Giang Doan, Hieyong Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Electroencephalography (EEG) based eye tracking methodology has encountered numerous obstacles due to the noisy, high-dimensional, and non-stationary characteristics inherent in brain data. In this study, we proposed a novel regression framework, named GACNet, which synergistically integrates a Graph Attention Network (GAT) for dynamic spatial modeling and supervised contrastive learning to enhance regression accuracy. For the encoder representation, we implement a temporal module that utilizes a 1D convolutional layer, channel-wise attention, and a bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) network. This configuration effectively captures both local and long-range temporal dependencies. Concurrently, the GAT-based spatial module treats the 129 EEG channels as a dynamic graph. It autonomously learns the importance of inter-channel relationships (edges) and node significance, effectively filtering irrelevant nodes and focusing on the most salient neural pathways for eye tracking. To further bolster the quality of representation, we employ a clustering-guided supervised contrastive (SupCon) loss, which ensures compactness among positive clusters while maximizing the separation from negative samples within the learned feature space. This is coupled with a joint optimization strategy that integrates regression loss, enabling the model to cultivate discriminative and task-relevant representations concurrently. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed framework achieves root mean square errors (RMSE) of 17.21 mm, 20.18 mm, and 25.14 mm, substantially outperforming the previous state-of-the-art results of 24.37 mm, 27.91 mm, and 33.03 mm on the EEGEyeNet, EEGEyeTrack-Level 1, and EEGEyeTrack-Level 2 datasets, respectively. This remarkable improvement demonstrates the superiority of our dynamic graph-based attention model, combined with contrastive learning, over static or transformer-based approaches for robust EEG regression. The source code is publicly available at: https://github.com/timeseriessignal/GACNet.

Introduction

Eye tracking technology plays a significant role in understanding non-verbal communication, enhancing human-computer interaction (HCI), and analyzing cognitive responses1. Its applications span various domains, including healthcare, assistive technologies, marketing, and gaming2,3. These diverse applications underline the importance of eye tracking systems as a fundamental component in both cognitive and behavioral studies. Since 2018, research on eye tracking has accelerated, particularly with a growing focus on AI-driven applications. Notable trends include the integration of deep learning algorithms to improve eye movement prediction and advancements in eye-tracking-based user experience (UX) design. This convergence of AI and eye tracking not only enhances predictive accuracy, but also broadens the applicability of such systems to real-world scenarios, where adaptability and personalization are increasingly essential. These developments contribute to a deeper understanding of visual attention and engagement, facilitating the creation of more intuitive and adaptive interfaces that respond effectively to user behaviour.

Among the various eye-tracking technologies, electroencephalography (EEG)-based eye tracking (EEG-ET) has garnered significant attention as a highly promising approach within the field of Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI). Unlike video-based approaches that rely heavily on visual cues, EEG-ET captures the neural correlates of eye movement, thus providing a deeper and more direct representation of user intention. EEG-ET4 has emerged as a promising research direction within brain-computer interface (BCI) studies. It offers distinct advantages over conventional camera-based methods, greater robustness under suboptimal lighting conditions and to operate effectively even with closed eyes. EEG-ET simplifies data collection5 by enabling the simultaneous acquisition of EEG and eye-tracking data without requiring additional hardware. This shift aims to address limitations associated with traditional camera and electro-oculography (EOG) techniques6, potentially enhancing BCI integration and operational reliability in various environments. Current video-based eye tracking systems (VOG)7 confront challenges such as high computational demands, dependence on ambient lighting, variability due to individual differences in pupil/iris contrast, and complex setup and calibration requirements. Therefore, EEG-ET emerges as an attractive alternative that complements existing methods while enabling new possibilities for non-visual or constrained environments. EEG-ET presents a potential solution to these issues.

Many traditional methods have utilized EEG cues, such as K-Nearest Neighbor approach8, Linear Regression method8,9, neural networks8,10 or other nearly state-of-the-art methods, including the Vision Transformer methods11 and the Graph Convolutional Neural Networks frameworks12. These algorithms form the foundation of EEG-based gaze estimation research, demonstrating that both traditional and modern machine learning techniques can extract meaningful correlations between brain activity and eye movement. These methods primarily aim to capture spatial features or interactions across brain regions by modeling either spatial, temporal, or functional dependencies assuming a uniform contribution from each EEG channel within short time windows. However, this assumption oversimplifies the inherently dynamic nature of brain signals, which vary across individuals, tasks, and contexts.

Building upon these foundations, the adoption of deep learning techniques for EEG signal decoding has gained momentum, with the potential to address ongoing challenges in this field. These techniques can identify complex patterns within EEG data, which is essential given the challenges of intrinsic noise, multiple independent artefact sources, and the weak nature of neural signals. The ability of deep networks to hierarchically learn non-linear representations makes them particularly well suited for EEG analysis, where conventional models often struggle to disentangle overlapping neural and artifactual information. Deep learning methodologies are key to effectively extracting multidimensional features across the spatial, temporal, and global dimensions of EEG signals. Recent advancements include the development of interpretable attention-based convolutional neural network (Attention-CNN)11 designed explicitly for gaze estimation from EEG data. This framework assigns dynamic weights to individual electrodes based on their relevance, improving focus on meaningful signals while reducing the influence of problematic channels. Electrodes located near the eyes capture electrical activity from the orbicularis oculi muscle, and the prefrontal cortex plays a crucial role in precise gaze estimation. Additionally, Moreno Alcayde et al.13 proposed LSTM and Transformer-based models for predicting gaze location and fixations, demonstrating effectiveness even under limited signal conditions. Similarly, Key et al.14 introduced an improved EEG-Vision Transformer (EEGViT) model incorporating depthwise separable convolution and improved preprocessing, further increasing gaze estimation performance. However, previous studies share a critical limitation. Most models either assume that all EEG channels (electrodes) contribute equally to the eye-tracking task or model spatial relationships between channels in a static manner15,16. This overlooks the complex nature of the brain, where functionally connected regions form networks to perform specific tasks, and the contribution of each area changes dynamically. In other words, these approaches fail to reflect the brain’s actual network topology and the dynamic importance of each node (channel), leading to the fundamental problem of incorporating noise from less informative channels into the learning process, thereby hindering the model’s robustness and accuracy.

To address this limitation, we propose a novel spatio-temporal regression framework based on a GAT combined with Contrastive Learning. The core innovation of GACNet lies in its ability to dynamically learn the inter-channel correlations by representing EEG channels as a graph, thereby mimicking the brain’s functional connectivity. In this process, the model strengthens the edges between channels highly relevant to the eye-tracking task while weakening the connections of noisy, irrelevant ones, allowing it to autonomously identify the critical flow of information.

Furthermore, our model integrates a channel-wise attention mechanism to achieve a deeper level of learning. Among the significantly connected channels within the graph structure, it is designed to assign higher weights to the channels containing the most decisive information for prediction at a given moment. This dual-attention mechanism enables a level of precise feature extraction unattainable by previous models by effectively filtering out unnecessary information and emphasizing core neural signal patterns. This approach not only enhances the signal’s interpretability but also dramatically improves robustness against noise and individual variability, opening a new horizon for more accurate and generalizable EEG-based eye tracking.

Literature review

Temporal dimension

Most EEG studies have predominantly focused on classification tasks17, including emotion recognition18, mental workload assessment19, and seizure prediction20. Unlike classification, regression enables fine-grained, continuous estimation of cognitive and physiological states, thereby offering deeper insights into neural mechanisms21.

The transition from classification to regression, however, introduces significant challenges. EEG signals are inherently noisy22 and high-dimensional23, and subject to non-linear dynamics across both temporal and spatial domains24. Thus, models must preserve temporal resolution and generalise effectively in continuous domains, even with scarce data. Initial research efforts predominantly employed handcrafted features25, such as band power26, entropy27, and connectivity metrics28, in conjunction with traditional regression techniques, particularly linear regression8,9 and support vector regression (SVR)29. Although these models offer some interpretability, they often fail to fully capture the complex dynamics present in raw EEG signals.

Recent investigations have explored deep learning architectures for the regression of EEG data30,31. EEG signals are characterised by their rich temporal dynamics, with cognitive and sensory events often represented by transient waveforms occurring over millisecond intervals32,33. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been adapted to capture local spatial patterns across EEG channels17, while recurrent neural networks (RNNs) such as long short-term memory networks (LSTMs)34 have effectively modeled temporal dependencies. However, traditional unidirectional LSTMs may overlook essential future context–which is particularly important in applications like gaze prediction, where anticipatory neural activity carries predictive value. BiLSTMs35 address this limitation by processing sequences in both forward and backward directions, providing a more complete temporal representation. Several studies have demonstrated the advantages of BiLSTM for EEG-based regression tasks, such as predicting (x, y) gaze coordinates from multichannel EEG signals, where they have outperformed both CNNs and conventional LSTMs36,37. Nevertheless, models that focused exclusively on temporal dynamics often neglect spatial inter-channel relationships, treating EEG channels as independent or sequentially arranged inputs, an assumption that contradicts the true anatomical and functional topology of the brain, potentially leading to suboptimal feature representations.

Spatial dimension

EEG electrodes are positioned according to standardised topographies, resulting in adjacent electrodes frequently exhibiting correlated activity due to either spatial proximity or functional coupling38. Graph-based neural networks, particularly Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs)39, offer a robust framework for modelling this spatial structure. To construct such graphs, EEG electrodes are modelled as nodes, with edges representing spatial or functional dependencies (e.g. distance, correlation, coherence).

GCNs operate on these graphs by aggregating features from neighbouring nodes, thereby facilitating the learning of local spatial patterns40. When integrated with temporal models such as LSTM networks or BiLSTM networks, GCNs have demonstrated strong performance in tasks such as emotion regression41, specifically in predicting arousal and valence scores, as well as in attention tracking42. However, these GCN-based models typically rely on a pre-defined, static graph structure, which fails to capture dynamic changes in functional connectivity that occur during cognitive tasks. This limitation highlights the need for a more adaptive approach, such as attention mechanisms, that can learn the importance of node relationships on-the-fly.

Recently, transformer-based architectures have also been explored for EEG regression tasks. For instance, Yang et al. proposed ViT2EEG11, which leverages vision transformer modules for gaze prediction. More recently, Key et al.14 introduced EEGViT, integrating depthwise separable convolutions with ViT blocks to improve spatio-temporal learning. While these models offer powerful global representation capabilities, they typically require large datasets and extensive training, which may not be feasible in low-resource EEG settings. Furthermore, their application in contrastive learning frameworks remains limited.

Spatial and global dimension

A fundamental challenge in EEG regression is the extraction of discriminative and compact representations from noisy and high-variance data. Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon)43 has recently emerged as a robust framework for learning embeddings; within this framework, samples that share the same label (or, in the context of regression, those that exhibit similar value ranges) are pulled closer together in latent space, while dissimilar samples are pushed apart. This approach significantly enhances generalisation, particularly in scenarios characterised by data scarcity or label imbalance.

While SupCon has primarily been employed in classification tasks, its application in EEG regression remains underexplored. Recent studies43 have sought to adapt contrastive objectives for regression tasks by discretising continuous labels into similarity classes or by defining similarity thresholds. Nevertheless, these approaches have not yet been integrated with spatio-temporal EEG encoders, such as hybrid BiLSTM-GCN models, thereby presenting a valuable opportunity for further research and development within this domain. A key challenge in EEG regression is the scarcity of labelled data, particularly in real-world applications where subject-specific calibration is costly. Few-shot EEG regression remains an open problem, as most existing models struggle to generalise to unseen conditions or participants. In this context, contrastive pretraining offers a promising strategy for improving generalisation by learning robust feature embeddings that transfer well across recording sessions and across different subjects, thereby addressing the variability inherent in EEG data.

GAT-based spatial module:

In the GAT block, node representations are refined by integrating information from their neighbors through attention-weighted message passing. It is important to note that the clustering process is performed exclusively on the training set. In this research, we employ two benchmark datasets to evaluate the proposed method, such as

The EEGEyeNet dataset8: this dataset segmented the Electroencephalography (EEG) signals into three distinct ocular event classes composing fixations, saccades, and blinks. During the data acquisition phase, participants were instructed to fixate on dots presented sequentially at 25 predetermined screen locations. Each dot was displayed for a duration of 1.5 to 1.8 seconds, covering the four corners of the screen as well as the centre, which appeared three times per experimental block. Following the EEGEyeNet benchmark, we use minimally preprocessed EEG signals that retain ocular artifacts (EOG), enabling evaluation under realistic conditions where both neural and artifactual components are present.

The EEGEyeTrack dataset55 was also employed to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method. This dataset comprises recordings from 116 sessions involving 113 participants. Each session lasted approximately six minutes, resulting in a total of 11 hours and 45 minutes of EEG data. Each experimental session consisted of four stages. The first two stages, referred to as level-1 recordings, involved target movements restricted to the horizontal and vertical axes of the screen. In contrast, the subsequent two stages, termed level-2 recordings, featured target movements in multiple directions, offering additional degrees of freedom. Within each level, one stage contained abrupt target displacements, while the other included continuous target motion across the screen. The former are designated as saccade recordings, and the latter as smooth recordings. For this study, we used the saccade recordings from both level 1 and level 2.

Metrics

In this work, two complementary evaluation measures are utilized to quantify the spatial deviation between the model’s predictions and the ground-truth gaze positions, such as: RMSE is employed as the evaluation metric for the EEGEyeNet dataset8, and the Mean Euclidean Distance (MED)55 that is used for the EEGEyeTrack dataset55. These metrics are adopted to ensure consistency and facilitate a fair comparison with the state-of-the-art (SOTA) results reported on these corresponding benchmark datasets.

A selection of 40 channels was manually chosen based on biological relevance and expert insight, making them particularly informative for gaze localization.

This work introduces an attention mechanism to analyze the impact of varying the number of EEG channels on the output of the model. The input channel count is systematically modulated, selecting those associated with the highest attention weights before the feature extraction phase, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

For the EEGEyeTrack dataset55, the K-Means, Agglomerative, Birch, and Gaussian Mixture Model methods yield identical prediction errors, indicating comparable clustering quality across these centroid and model-based approaches. This effectiveness may be attributed to the alignment between K-Means clustering and the geometric assumptions inherent in the SupCon loss as well as the regression objectives.

Conclusion

This study introduces GACNet, a spatio-temporal EEG regression framework that significantly advances gaze position estimation. At its core, a GAT models dynamic inter-channel dependencies by representing EEG sensors as a unified graph, enabling attention-guided channel pruning to eliminate low-utility nodes and thereby improving robustness and computational efficiency. By jointly integrating Bi-LSTM for temporal modeling, GAT for spatial reasoning, and a clustering-guided supervised contrastive objective for discriminative feature learning, GACNet departs from previous methods that treat these modules independently. On EEGEyeNet and EEGEyeTrack (Levels 1–2), the proposed framework achieves 17.21 mm, 20.18 mm, and 25.14 mm RMSE, respectively which represent a substantial improvement over prior state-of-the-art models, while maintaining high efficiency (11.05M parameters, 0.55 GMac) suitable for real-world deployment.

The proposed GACNet offers several notable contributions. First, its attention-guided channel selection improves accuracy of the prediction with fewer sensors, allowing lightweight EEG acquisition. Second, the graph-attentive spatial modeling effectively captures fine-grained inter-channel dependencies, surpassing GCN/CNN-based baselines. Third, the clustering-informed supervised contrastive loss reinforces intra-cluster consistency and inter-cluster separability, producing highly discriminative embeddings for regression tasks. Furthermore, the framework achieves an optimal trade-off between performance and efficiency, providing superior accuracy at a lower computational cost, thereby making it well suited for portable and real-time applications. Finally, GACNet exhibits strong cross-dataset generalization, maintaining stable performance on additional EEG datasets beyond EEGEyeNet and EEGEyeTrack, which demonstrates its adaptability to different electrode configurations, signal distributions, and recording environments.
