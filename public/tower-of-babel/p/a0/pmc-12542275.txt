Function and evolution of plant annexins in response to abiotic stresses
Authors: Tao Tong, Yanli Zhang, Wei Jiang, Guang Chen, Fanrong Zeng, Dawei Xue, Yuan Qin, Fenglin Deng, Zhong-Hua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Calcium (Ca²⁺) serve as pivotal signaling molecules in plants, engaging in a diverse array of biochemical and physiological processes. Annexins (ANNs) constitute a significant family of Ca²⁺-dependent phospholipid-binding proteins in plants. These ANN proteins are involved in a variety of critical physiological and biochemical processes through their distinct functions, which include membrane trafficking, cytoskeletal organization, cellular homeostasis, and ion transport. This review summarizes the fundamental properties and functional diversity of ANNs, as well as their interactions with abiotic stress conditions. We further elucidate their evolutionary origins, highlighting the role of plant ANNs in response to abiotic stresses, with an emphasis on their expression patterns across key plant lineages. Our analysis reveals that ANNs are an evolutionarily conserved family, ubiquitously present in nearly all examined plant species, likely originating from Rhodophyta. Furthermore, this review examines the expression profiles of the ANN gene family across several evolutionarily significant plant lineages, focusing on the regulatory mechanisms of ANNs in response to salinity, drought, and temperature extremes. Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the intricate evolutionary history and diverse biological functions of plant ANNs will enhance the potential to improve abiotic stress tolerance in plants.

Introduction

Calcium (Ca2+) is a universal signaling molecule in eukaryotic cells, playing a crucial role in numerous biochemical reactions and physiological processes in plants [1–4]. The majority of Ca²⁺-binding target proteins are conserved across eukaryotic organisms, facilitating the generation and decoding of Ca2+ signals [5–7]. Among these protein families, annexins (ANNs) represent an evolutionarily conserved, multigene family prevalent in plant species. These proteins typically interact with membrane phospholipids in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner, utilizing a highly conserved tertiary structure [8]. Annexins function as dynamic signaling molecules interacting with Ca²⁺, and other cellular components [9], which strongly indicates that these small soluble proteins exhibit functional diversity [10]. Numerous studies have proposed that annexins are implicated in a broad spectrum of essential biological processes including membrane trafficking, membrane-cytoskeletal organization, cytoskeletal organization, cellular homeostasis, apoptosis, and ion transport [11–13].

Since the initial isolation of plant annexins from tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [14], nearly four decades of research have progressively elucidated their functional complexity. From a macro-omics perspective, numerous annexin family members across various plant species have been functionally characterized, facilitated by the increasing availability of genome sequences [15]. Furthermore, extensive proteomic and transcriptomic studies have revealed the ubiquitous presence and dynamic expression patterns of annexins in diverse plant tissues [9]. Multi-omics analyses and studies on molecular evolution have demonstrated that ANNs actively participate in the establishment of plant stress resistance mechanisms, thereby, mitigating the impact of adverse environmental conditions throughout evolution and speciation [12, 16]. At the physiological level, subcellular localization analyses have confirmed the cytosolic abundance and membrane association of annexins in most plant tissues and organs throughout the plant’s life cycle [17, 18]. The aggregation of annexins on cell membranes indicates their potential functional roles in ion channel regulation and membrane protection [19, 20]. Additionally, the subcellular localization of annexins exhibits considerable variability contingent upon cell type and prevailing stimuli [11]. In terms of molecular functions, in vitro characterization of annexins, along with their recognized dynamic distribution patterns, underscore their potential role as significant regulatory or effector proteins that respond to environmental stresses, particularly those associated with Ca2+ and reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling pathways [8]. In conclusion, annexins, as multifunctional proteins, are integral to plant-environment interactions.

The conservation of the C-terminal domain and the variability of the N-terminal domain in annexins enable them to fulfill diverse functional requirements [8, 13]. Nonetheless, there is a paucity of specific details and experimental evidence concerning the structure of annexins, especially regarding the dynamics of their binding to plant cell membranes to form ion channels in a Ca²⁺- dependent manner. Additionally, the evolutionary trajectory of plant membrane-associated proteins remains inadequately addressed, and the functional mechanisms and expression patterns pertinent to evolutionarily significant plant lineages require further elucidation.

In this review, we have comprehensively summarized the functional diversity of annexins in plant development and stress signaling. Our study includes a bioinformatic analysis of their evolutionary origins, multi-gene family identification, protein structure, and expression patterns. Furthermore, we have examined the signal transduction pathways of membrane-associated proteins in response to abiotic stress. Consequently, this paper offers valuable insights for future research on the role of annexins in the genetic enhancement of plants for improved resistance to abiotic stresses.

Annexins for ion transport, membrane trafficking, and cytoskeletal organization in plants

Ion transport

The Ca²⁺ transport activity of annexins is identified as a critical component of their involvement in signal transduction processes within plant cells [12, 21]. It has been proposed that annexins have the ability to integrate with membranes to form Ca²⁺-permeable channels [22]. In plants, recombinant annexin CaANN24 from bell pepper (Capsicum annuum) was first reported to regulate of Ca²⁺-permeable channels and transporters in vesicles [23]. Additionally, investigations into plant annexins have revealed that conserved motifs within these proteins can form a conserved salt bridge in the central pore of selective gated ion channels, thereby establishing the structural basis for annexins as potential ion transporters [24]. It has been demonstrated that ZmANN33/35 annexins in maize (Zea mays) sense increases in cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentration ([Ca²⁺]cyt) by facilitating Ca²⁺ permeability conductance across the plasma membrane [25]. Similarly, in Arabidopsis thaliana, the membrane-localized AtANN1 is involved in Ca²⁺- and K⁺-permeable conductance across the plasma membrane and serves as a molecular intermediary between ROS and [Ca²⁺]cyt in root cells [19]. Concurrently, the soluble intracellular AtANN1 contributes to the modulation of the H2O2-induced Ca²⁺ signature in Arabidopsis roots [26]. Thus, annexins display distinct subcellular localizations and diverse functional roles; however, the extent to which calcium transport via annexin-formed Ca²⁺-permeable channels or transporters constitutes their primary physiological function remains a subject of ongoing debate.

Membrane trafficking

Annexins demonstrate distinct subcellular localizations, frequently partitioning between cytosolic and membrane-associated pools in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner. The specific target membranes for various annexins are generally the plasma membrane and those involved in the biosynthetic or endocytic pathways, suggesting the involvement of annexins in membrane trafficking processes [27]. During [Ca²⁺]cyt-driven processes, annexins may function as Ca²⁺ sensors or effectors to facilitate membrane repositioning [28]. Presently, the roles of annexins in stromule formation, membrane repair, exocytosis, and endocytosis have been extensively investigated, with substantial evidence supporting the involvement of plant annexins in membrane trafficking [12, 17]. Immunofluorescence analysis has demonstrated that AtANN1 and AtANN2 are involved in Golgi-mediated vesicle secretion in Arabidopsis [29]. Additionally, observations using light-sheet fluorescence microscopy have indicated a significant accumulation of AtANN1-GFP at the plasma membrane and within vesicles in non-dividing cells [30]. In conclusion, plant annexin proteins are predominantly expressed in vesicle- associated membrane structures such as the tonoplast, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus. This expression pattern suggests a potential role in mediating Ca²⁺ signaling through membrane trafficking and secretion in response to stress conditions [12]. However, Current evidence reveals that direct molecular interactions between annexins and SNARE proteins, including Syntaxin of Plants (SYPs), have not been established in most plant species. SYPs are recognized as key facilitators of vesicle fusion across various cellular compartments, such as the plasma membrane and trans-Golgi network. Although annexins are implicated in membrane dynamics, there is a lack of experimental validation for their physical or functional association with SNARE proteins in plants, in contrast to the well-documented annexin-SNARE interactions observed in animal systems [31–33].

Cytoskeletal organization

The dynamic rearrangement of actin filaments is crucial for various cellular processes, including the regulation of cytoskeletal reorganization and cytokinesis in plants [34]. Certain plant annexins possess a conserved F-actin binding motif that facilitates membrane-actin interactions [35]. Annexins from species such as tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), shy plant (Mimosa pudica), and zucchini (Cucurbita pepo) have been shown to bind F-actin in vitro in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner [36–38]. Initial research on shy plant annexins demonstrated that plant annexins mediate Ca²⁺-induced actin bundling in vitro and contribute to nyctinastic movements [38]. Semi-quantitative RT-PCR analysis has indicated that GhFAnnx from cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) regulates fiber polar expansion during elongation stages by facilitating F-actin-directed vesicle trafficking to growth sites [39]. In alignment with the involvement of the cytoskeleton, ectopic expression of AnxCb6 in Gallini cotton (Gossypium barbadense) enhances fiber elongation rates without increasing cell number, indicating its reliance on cytoskeletal reorganization for anisotropic growth [40]. The heterologous overexpression of cotton GhAnn3 in Arabidopsis has been shown to enhance leaf length, germination rate, and trichome density, which is associated with annexin-mediated actin bundling [41]. Mechanistically, the interaction between GhFAnnxA and GhCDPK1 regulates a ROS-Ca²⁺ feedback loop that modulates F-actin dynamics at the plasma membrane, thereby facilitating fiber elongation [42]. Fundamentally, the organization of the cytoskeleton underpins these elongation phenotypes through annexin-mediated F-actin remodeling. Notably, AtANN3 integrates extracellular ATP signals with auxin transport by modulating cortical actin arrays in the roots and hypocotyls of Arabidopsis [43]. Collectively, these findings establish annexins as molecular bridges coordinating Ca²⁺ signaling with F-actin-membrane interactions to orchestrate cytoskeleton-dependent cellular morphogenesis.

Structural and compositional analysis of plant annexins

Annexin proteins, which exhibit less than 45% amino acid sequence homology with their animal counterparts, have preserved unique structural characteristics throughout plant evolution [13, 44]. A typical plant annexin protein is composed of two main domains, with molecular weights ranging from 32 to 42 kDa: a variable short N-terminus and a conserved core C-terminus [11]. In contrast to the conserved N-terminal domain of animal annexins, the plant counterparts display significant variability across numerous species [45, 46]. This N-terminal domain generally consists of approximately 10 amino acid residues with high sequence variability. These residues are anchored to the conserved C-terminal core via two hydrogen bonds, a structural configuration that enhances the conformational plasticity of annexins across different functional states [8, 45, 47]. This variability may have enabled annexins to perform efficient regulatory functions in plants [48], such as mediating cellular responses to abiotic stresses. Biochemical experiments have suggested that the N-terminal domain modulates distinct properties of annexins through an allosteric mechanism [49]. The super-helical annexin core comprises four repeated domains located at the C-terminus, which exhibit significant similarity across various plant annexin subfamilies [17]. Each repeated C-terminal domain consists of approximately 70 amino acid residues that fold into five α-helices (a-d) [44] (Fig. 1A). These helices, along with the connecting short loops form a helix-loop-helix bundle, a configuration essential for Ca2+-mediated interactions with membranes. A predominant characteristic of most plant annexins is their Ca2+-dependent binding to acidic membrane phospholipids through canonical Ca2+-binding sites. Typically, plant annexins contain Ca2+-binding sites of the conserved endonexin fold (K-G-X-G-T-{38–40 variable residues}-D/E) within repeat Domains 1 and 4, with some variability observed in Domain 4 [27, 50] (Fig. 1B, C). The tertiary structure of canonical monomeric annexins generally resembles a slightly curved disc with grooves and clefts [44, 45, 49] and annexins bind to negatively charged phospholipid bilayers on the convex molecular surface via a Ca2+-bridging mechanism [44]. The amino acid composition of Ca2+-binding sites in annexins varies considerably among different subfamilies, indicating their differential affinities and abilities for membrane interaction [13]. Moreover, it has been reported that the presence of Ca2+ can reduce the thermal stability of plant annexins [45].

In summary, while plant annexin proteins exhibit evolutionary conservation in their overall structure, they display considerable diversity in their N-terminal domains and calcium-binding sites. These variations contribute to the unique properties and functions of plant annexins in different biological contexts, highlighting the need for further investigation into the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Comparative evolutionary analysis of the annexin family in green plants

The origin of annexins in green plants

Among the eight annexins reported in Arabidopsis thaliana to date, Annexin1 (AtANN1, AT1G35720) has been the subject of the most extensive research [51]. The putative orthologous protein sequences of AtANN1 were retrieved from the 1000 plant transcriptome databases (OneKP) available on the China National GeneBank (CNGB) platform (https://db.cngb.org/blast/blast/blastp/?project=onekp) using AtANN1 protein sequence as the reference [52, 53]. Within the OneKP dataset, which includes 1,322 terrestrial plants and algae, 70% of the species were found to possess orthologous proteins of AtANN1 [54]. Our evolutionary analysis indicates that homologous proteins of AtANN1 are present in both chlorophytes and rhodophytes, implicating that the ancestral gene of AtANN1 underwent early evolutionary events prior to the emergence of green plants, while retaining a relatively conserved sequence throughout the evolutionary process [55] (Fig. 1D). This is further corroborated by the widespread presence of annexins across most eukaryotic species including both plants and animals [56].

Remarkably, the interspecies sequence similarities of ANN1 in seed plants exceed 60%, whereas both the sequence similarity and the protein tertiary structure of ANN1 in algae exhibit considerable divergence. For instance, CsAnn1 in the chlorophyte algae, Chlamydomonas sp, demonstrated only 21% protein sequence similarity to Arabidopsis thaliana. These findings suggested that ANN1 may have played a pivotal and distinctive role in the evolutionary transition from algae to land plants, potentially influencing early plant evolutionary radiation [57] (Fig. 1B). Sequence alignment analysis revealed the absence of the endonexin sequence in ANN domain 1 in streptophyte algae, while vascular plants lack the endonexin sequence in ANN domains 2 and 3. These variations in ANN1s may have also influenced the transition of green plants from aquatic to terrestrial environments [27] (Fig. 1C). From an evolutionary standpoint, the structure and sequence of ANN1s have maintained a high degree of conservation throughout the evolutionary trajectory from algae to land plants and subsequently to seed plants. Nonetheless, there remains approximately 30% to 80% variation in the protein sequences, which may be advantageous for green plants of different species in adapting to their specific environmental conditions [23, 58, 59] (Fig. 1B, C).

Expansion and diversification of annexins in plants

Additionally, a total of 521 annexin genes were identified after searching the gene superfamily across 37 representative species from 11 evolutionarily significant plant lineages. Throughout the evolutionary history of green plants, there has been a discernible increase in the average number of annexin proteins, ranging from a single protein in chlorophyte algae to nine in both mosses and gymnosperms and reaching 20 in angiosperms (Fig. 2). Notably, the number of annexin proteins in streptophyte algae experienced a significant increase, with Chara braunii exhibiting 17 annexin proteins. This suggests that the ANN gene family may have undergone multiple gene duplication events during algal evolution, with the earliest occurrences likely taking place in streptophyte algae. As a critical transitional group from aquatic to terrestrial environments, streptophyte algae represent the sister group to terrestrial plants and harbor the earliest forms of numerous key protein families associated with abiotic stress tolerance [60, 61] (Fig. 2). The number of ANN genes exhibited considerable variation among seed plant species, suggesting that the substantial expansion of the ANN gene family in monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants was realized through gene duplication, recombination and exchange. However, the establishment of the ANN gene family in terrestrial plants prior to the emergence of seed plants may have relied on vertical interspecific inheritance [8, 13]. In summary, the ANN proteins and annexin domains originated from chlorophyte algae and initially expanded in bryophytes, laying the groundwork for functional adaptation and large-scale expansion in terrestrial plants. This evolutionary trajectory unfolded alongside profound environmental shifts, thereby establishing a complex relationship between annexins and plant adaptive evolution, as well as stress response mechanisms [13, 57, 62, 63].

To further investigate the origin and evolutionary history of the plant ANN gene family, Hidden Markov Model (HMM) strategy was employed to identify ANN protein sequences from the Superfamily database (https://supfam.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/SUPERFAMILY/index.html). A comprehensive analysis identified 309 putative ANN orthologs across 24 species, encompassing 21 distinct algal and land plant lineages (Fig. 3). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that ANN proteins are categorized into seven clades (A-G). Specifically, clades A and F contained ANN proteins from gymnosperms Pinus taeda, while clade E comprised ANN proteins of lycophyte Selaginella moellendorffii and moss Physcomitrella patens, and clades B, C, and D exclusively clustered the ANN proteins from angiosperms (Fig. 3). Previous studies have speculated that the annexin subfamilies in angiosperms primarily originated from one to three ancestral genes present in mosses, ferns, and gymnosperms approximately 500 million years ago [13]. Thus, these imply that the evolution of plant annexins likely involved multiple gene duplication events. The genetic characteristics of ANN proteins in angiosperms are further elucidated by the presence of protein pairs in the phylogenetic tree with eight direct orthologous protein pairs identified between eudicots (AtANN1/SlANN1, AtANN5/CpANN6, AtANN3/CpANN5, MdANN2/CusANN2, GrANN6/CpANN2, GrANN2/CpANN11, MeANN8/PtANN9, and MeANN9/PtANN1) and three pairs in monocots (HvANN2/TaANN2, HvANN6/TaANN6, and ZmANN4/OsANN1). The remaining ANN proteins were observed as intraspecific paralogs (Fig. 3), which indicates that some ancestral ANN genes may have predated the divergence of monocots and eudicots. Therefore, the expansion of the ANN gene family in angiosperms is likely to depend more on intraspecific genome segment replication and gene tandem repeat events, rather than intraspecific inheritance in plants [64, 65].

Taken together, integrating the phylogenetic analysis of ANN1 proteins and gene families suggests that ANN proteins likely originated from chlorophyte algae. These proteins subsequently underwent structural and functional modifications in streptophyte algae and bryophytes (especially in the tertiary structure), facilitating the transition from aquatic to terrestrial environments. Over time, ANN proteins underwent expansion and duplication in ferns, lycophytes, and seed plants. Consequently, the evolution of ANN proteins in plants appears to be influenced by both intrinsic genetic mechanisms and extrinsic environmental pressures. The widespread presence of annexins across diverse plant lineages and their involvement in environmental stress responses underscore their adaptive significance in plant biology and evolution [13, 57, 59].

Tissue specific gene expression and subcellular localization of plant annexins

Tissue specific expression of ANNs

To better understand the basis of functional divergence, we conducted a gene expression analysis of ANNs from 11 representative species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Gossypium raimondi, Glycine max, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Amborella trichopoda, Picea abies, Selaginella moellendorffii, Physcomitrella patens, Marchantia polymorpha, Cyanophora paradpxa) using publicly available transcriptome datasets. The monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous species contain gene expression profiles across various tissues and under abiotic stresses, but algae datasets are limited to abiotic stress conditions. Gene expression profiles across different tissues were observed in the remaining species (Fig. 4). In the green alga, Cyanophora paradoxa, CypANN1 demonstrates the ability to positively respond (upregulation) to external light levels, nutrient deficiencies, and temperature variations. This undoubtedly sets the stage for the subsequent functional evolution in abiotic stress responses of annexins in plants. Conversely, in the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha, the expression data for MpANN1 in response to abiotic stresses is unavailable, although it is notably expressed in aseptic conditions. Unlike in liverwort, ANN genes in the model moss, Physcomitrella patens, are expressed ubiquitously across all tissues. This distinct tissue expression pattern may reflect the origin of the ancestral functions of ANN genes in tissue and developmental processes. Furthermore, the increase in the expansion of ANN genes is likely associated with tissue-specific expression and functional differentiation among individual gene members [8, 13] (Figs. 2 and 4). In lycophytes, gymnosperms, and basal angiosperms, ANN genes exhibit high and widespread expression levels across various tissues (Fig. 4).

Linking the subcellular localizations to the function of ANNs

The subcellular localization of annexins is essential for their functional roles within plant cells. Advanced methodologies, including multi-omics assays, cellular immunology techniques, cell imaging, and protein fractionation, have demonstrated that annexins are not only abundantly present in the cytoplasm and on the plasma membrane [9], but are also localized in the tonoplast [69], Golgi apparatus, and Golgi-derived vesicles [70], as well as in chloroplasts [71, 72], the nucleus [73], mitochondria [74], thylakoids [75], and matrix [72]. Currently, the AtANN1 protein has been isolated and recovered from the plasma membrane, vacuole, peroxisomes, mitochondria, stroma, and thylakoids [76]. The extensive subcellular distribution of plant annexins underscores their functional diversity [9]. In particular, AtANN1 exhibits a Ca²⁺-dependent reversible (membrane ↔ cytoplasm) localization pattern in response to salt stress in Arabidopsis thaliana [77]. Under conditions of salt or drought stress, AtANN1 and AtANN4 co-localize in the endoplasmic reticulum to perform their functions [78]. Additionally, cold stress in wheat leads to the accumulation of the annexin p39 on the plasma membrane of stem cells and the aggregation of p22.5 in microsomes [17, 79]. However, the signaling cascades mediated by annexins and the regulatory mechanisms involving cellular components still require further investigation.

Functions of plant annexins in response to abiotic stresses

Interestingly, in monocots and eudicots, ANN genes that are highly expressed across various tissues demonstrate a pronounced responsiveness to external stresses. This observation indicates that the ANN gene family is involved in intricate regulatory pathways that enable adaptation to environmental changes through modifications in the morphology and structure of annexin proteins [15]. Notably, ANN genes from monocots and eudicots exhibit high expression levels under cold, heat, salinity, and drought stresses (Fig. 4), aligning with findings from previous studies [9, 80]. Moreover, the specific roles of ANN gene family members in these abiotic stress responses are increasingly being clarified, with all functions linked to the regulation of Ca2+ signaling [21, 34].

Drought and salinity stress

Among various abiotic stresses, salinity is particularly prevalent [81]. In Arabidopsis, salt stress triggered the translocation of AtANN1 from the cytosol to the membrane, and the independent mutations in AtANN1 and AtANN4 resulted in heightened sensitivity to osmotic stress and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling in a Ca2+-dependent manner [77]. This adaptive mechanism involves annexins and non-selective cation channels (NSCCs) mediating alterations in Ca²⁺ flux within cells. With the assistance of downstream signaling events including the binding of Ca²⁺ sensors, this process facilitates the accumulation of Salt Overly Sensitive 1 (SOS1) and other ion channels in the cell, thereby mitigating the damage induced by salt stress [9]. The increase in cytosolic Ca²⁺ due to salt stress triggers the activation of downstream NADPH oxidases, leading to an intracellular accumulation of ROS. This accumulation of ROS subsequently activates AtANN1-mediated Ca²⁺ flux in Arabidopsis thaliana, which in turn promotes the transcription of downstream SOS1 [21]. In the case of △Atann1 mutants responding to salt stress, the absence of AtANN1 expression led to the increasement of ROS-dependent [Ca²⁺]cyt, subsequently resulting in decreased transcript levels of downstream AtSOS1 [82]. For instance, the constitutive overexpression of AnnBj2 in Brassica juncea and its heterologous overexpression in tobacco both demonstrated enhanced salt-tolerant phenotypes through ABA-dependent mechanisms [83, 84]. In Capsicum annuum, CaANN9 gene silenced lines exhibit heightened sensitivity to salt stress, including chlorophyll degradation and ROS accumulation [85]. Integrating the upstream and downstream elements of annexins enables a more thorough examination of the signaling pathways and response mechanisms mediated by annexins under salt stress [15]. Recent studies also showed that 14-3-3 proteins regulate the SOS pathway by inhibiting SOS2 (AtCIPK24), thereby affecting plant salt tolerance [86]. Similarly, the interaction between GhANN8b and the phosphatase GhDsPTP3a in cotton regulated Ca²⁺ influx and enhanced salt tolerance [87]. As an upstream regulator of AtANN1, AtPP2-B11 can upregulate the expression of AtANN1 in response to salt stress. Additionally, AtPP2-B11 modulates the expression of Na+ homeostasis genes to mitigate salt stress, serving as a molecular bridge between salt stress and ROS accumulation [88]. Ectopic overexpression of cassava (Manihot esculenta) MeANN2 in Arabidopsis significantly increased the expression of stress-responsive genes AtRD29B and auxin signaling pathway-related genes AtIAA4 and AtLBD18, thereby enhancing salt tolerance [89].

With the global escalation of drought stress, there is a consequent reduction in both crop yield and quality [90]. Proteomic analyses have revealed that annexins are expressed and accumulated in the shoots of Brassica rape and the barley crown of Hordeum vulgare in response to external drought stress [91, 92]. AtANN1, the homolog of GhANN1, was identified as playing a significant role in the ABA-induced responses of plants to drought stress [28]. Furthermore, OsANN3 has been shown to activate the ABA signaling pathway by binding to phospholipids in rice, thereby facilitating Ca²⁺ influx to enhance drought resistance [80]. SnRK2s (Sucrose Non-Fermenting 1-related protein kinases 2) are pivotal kinases in the ABA signaling pathway, targeting various ion channels, including SLAC1 (Slow Anion Channel-Associated 1) and GORK (Guard Cell Outward Rectifying K⁺ channel). Ca2+-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs), calcineurin B-like proteins (CBLs) and CBL-interacting protein kinases (CIPKs) are capable of decoding Ca2+ signals and operate independently of ABA in response to drought stress [93–95]. Consequently, overexpression lines of OsANN3 demonstrate enhanced growth morphology under drought stress compared to wild-type plants by mitigating water loss in rice [80]. Furthermore, OsANN9 may play a positive regulatory role in rice under drought stress by modulating the ROS accumulation [96]. In drought conditions, the overexpression of StANN1 in potato (Solanum tuberosum) plants facilitated greater water retention in green tissues and maintained robust chloroplast function and photosynthetic rates, thereby exhibiting enhanced drought tolerance [97]. One study showed that heterologous expression of EpAnn from the fungus Endocarpon pusillum in Arabidopsis plants resulted in transgenic plants with increased germination rates under oxidative stress and enhanced tolerance to drought [98].

It has been observed that drought and salt stress share overlapping effects at the gene expression level in plants. Analysis of annexin expression in various plant species under saline and drought stress conditions revealed similar expression patterns [9]. In a study of Arabidopsis annexin transcripts, it was found that the transcript abundance of AtANN1, AtANN6, and AtANN8 significantly increased under salt and drought stress conditions [99]. Moreover, the overexpression of AtANN8 led to altered expression of stress-regulated genes in transgenic plants, thereby enhancing their tolerance to salt and dehydration stress [16]. A detailed study reported that AtANN1 and AtANN4 operate synergistically to regulate responses to salt and drought stress in Arabidopsis [78]. Similarly, the overexpression of the cotton GhANN1 and tomato SpANN2 genes has been shown to significantly enhance drought and salt tolerance in transgenic plants [100, 101]. In Poplar (Populus trichocarpa), trees overexpressing PtANN1 displayed increased chlorophyll content, elevated antioxidative enzyme activities, and a notable growth advantage under drought and salt stress conditions [102]. Recently, transgenic tobacco plants overexpressing TdANN12 from durum wheat (Triticum durum) exhibited reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and higher ROS scavenging capacity, resulting in significantly improved tolerance to both drought and salt stress [103]. Given that the majority of these studies have focused on angiosperms, future research should prioritize investigating the spatial and temporal expression of ANNs and their functional roles in specific cell types, particularly in response to drought and salinity tolerance, within evolutionarily significant yet understudied plant lineages.

Thermal stress

In recent years, the frequency of extreme weather events, including both low and high temperatures, has increased due to climate change, exerting a significant impact on crop production and ecosystem health [104]. Cold stress is detected by Ca2+-responsive proteins located on the plasma membrane, which initiate downstream signaling responses [9]. It has been reported that AtANN1-induced Ca²⁺ signaling is pivotal in establishing cold tolerance in plants. As a downstream component of stomatal opening factors 1 (OST1), AtANN1 undergoes phosphorylated by OST1/SnRK2.6. This phosphorylation enhances the Ca²⁺ transport activity of AtANN1, indirectly activating the expression of cold-responsive genes such as C-repeat binding factors (CBFs) and cold-regulated proteins (CORs). Ultimately, this signaling cascade positively regulates the plant’s cold tolerance mechanisms [105]. Notably, experimental results in crops have demonstrated that cold conditions significantly upregulate OsANN8 expression in rice and lead to the accumulation of two intrinsic annexins in wheat membranes [79, 106]. Furthermore, studies on maize have confirmed that ZmANN33 and ZmANN35 contribute positively to the chilling recovery process of plants in a Ca²⁺-independent manner [107]. In a manner similar to other plant stress responses, a rapid increase in [Ca²⁺]cyt is essential for triggering the heat stress response in plants. This process is regulated by plasma membrane-localized cyclic nucleotide-gated channels (CNGCs) localized on the plasma membrane [9, 108]. Initial evidence for the role of annexins in the heat stress response was observed in lotus (Nelumbo nucifera), where the expression of NnANN1 is upregulated following heat treatment. Furthermore, ectopic expression of NnANN1 in Arabidopsis thaliana has been shown to enhance resistance to heat stress during seed germination [109, 110]. It has been demonstrated that AtANN1 is rapidly upregulated in response to heat stress by mediating the heat-induced increase in [Ca²⁺]cyt and the expression of heat stress response genes, including heat shock factors (HSFs) and heat shock proteins (HSPs) [108]. Moreover, loss-of-function mutants of AtANN1 and AtANN2 exhibit increased sensitivity to heat shock treatments, suggesting that annexins may contribute to heat tolerance in plants through the regulation of ROS [108]. Additionally, the sensitivity of the MYB30 mutant to heat stress and oxidative stress is found to be contingent upon the functions of AtANN1 and AtANN4 [111]. In rice, the annexin OsANN1 enhances plant thermotolerance by upregulating the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), thereby reducing hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation [18].

Heavy metal stress

Heavy metal pollution adversely affects plant physiological processes, induces nutritional imbalances, and can ultimately result in wilting and mortality [112]. An examination of the expression profiles of 12 maize annexin genes has demonstrated that these genes are responsive to heavy metal [nickel (Ni), zinc (Zn), and cadmium (Cd)] stress and are regulated by jasmonic acid [113]. Given the similarity in ionic radii between Cd2+ and Ca2+, certain annexins exhibit Ca2+-binding properties. Studies have confirmed that ectopic expression of PeANN1 from Populus euphratica in Arabidopsis leads to Cd2+ accumulation in transgenic Arabidopsis [114]. Additionally, upon Cd exposure, the annexin gene AND4 was highly expressed in tomato roots; however, this expression was independent of the Cd2+ concentration [115]. The precise mechanisms by which annexins function under heavy metal stress are yet to be fully understood [8]. Consequently, further research is necessary to elucidate the role of annexins in conferring heavy metal tolerance in plants.

In conclusion, annexins are ubiquitously distributed across diverse plant tissues and exhibit specific functions at various developmental stages. This distribution and functional specificity influence the expression levels and patterns of annexin family members in distinct cell types or under particular environmental conditions. As crucial mediators of plant responses to the environmental stimuli, annexins play significant regulatory and functional roles in the establishment of plant stress tolerance.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Plant annexins constitute a family of highly conserved proteins characterized by their ability to bind both Ca²⁺ and phospholipid properties, thereby coordinating various cellular components involved in physiological and biochemical responses to environmental cues. Despite retaining their primary functional domains throughout evolution, annexins have demonstrated considerable diversity in biological functions. Recent research has further highlighted their potential to form ion channels upon membrane binding [8, 13]. However, the precise structural basis for this property in plant annexins remains to be elucidated.

With the availability of genome sequences from diverse species and accompanying functional studies, it has become evident that environmental stresses such as salinity, drought, and extreme temperature, significantly affect the expression of annexin genes in numerous plant species [9]. Our evolutionary bioinformatics analyses further corroborate the strong association of annexins with early plant adaptation and stress response mechanisms, indicating their potential role in facilitating the transition of plants to terrestrial environments and enhancing tolerance to abiotic stresses [56]. Nevertheless, the relationship between the biological functions of annexins across various plant lineages and the genetic pressures exerted by internal and external factors remains underexplored. Moreover, it remains unclear whether all members of the ANN gene family within specific plant species exhibit functional relevance and redundancy, and whether these characteristics are a consequence of phylogenetic relationships among different species [58]. Additionally, it is uncertain whether distinct annexins possess specific functions that contribute to the regulation of cellular physiological and biochemical processes through unique signaling pathways. The dynamic interactions of plant annexins across various subcellular compartments remain experimentally unvalidated. In conclusion, a more comprehensive understanding of the intricate relationships among annexins, environmental adaptation, and plant evolutionary history in the context of stress regulation is essential. Such insights will not only enhance our knowledge of this intriguing gene family but also lay the groundwork for future research aimed at improving plant species’ adaptation to abiotic stresses in the face of a changing climate.
