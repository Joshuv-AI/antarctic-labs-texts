Spin-lock based fast whole-brain 3D macromolecular proton fraction mapping of relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis
Authors: Jian Hou, Zongyou Cai, Weitian Chen, Tiffany Y So
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA sensitive and efficient imaging technique is required to assess the subtle abnormalities occurring in the normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) and normal-appearing grey matter (NAGM) in patients with relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS). In this study, a fast 3D macromolecular proton fraction (MPF) quantification based on spin-lock (fast MPF-SL) sequence was proposed for brain MPF mapping. Thirty-four participants, including 17 healthy controls and 17 RRMS patients were prospectively recruited. We conducted group comparison and correlation between conventional MPF-SL, fast MPF-SL, and DWI, and compared differences in quantified parameters within MS lesions and the regional NAWM, NAGM, and normal-appearing deep grey matter (NADGN). MPF of MS lesions was significantly reduced (7.17% ± 1.15%, P < 0.01) compared to all corresponding normal-appearing regions. MS patients also showed significantly reduced mean MPF values compared with controls in NAGM (4.87% ± 0.38% vs 5.21% ± 0.32%, P = 0.01), NAWM (9.49% ± 0.69% vs 10.32% ± 0.59%, P < 0.01) and NADGM (thalamus 5.59% ± 0.67% vs 6.00% ± 0.41%, P = 0.04; caudate 5.10% ± 0.55% vs 5.53% ± 0.58%, P = 0.03). MPF and ADC showed abnormalities in otherwise normal appearing close to lesion areas (P < 0.01). In conclusion, time-efficient MPF mapping of the whole brain can be acquired efficiently (< 3 min) using fast MPF-SL. It offers a promising alternative way to detect white matter abnormalities in MS.Keywords: Spin-lock, Multiple sclerosis, Magnetic resonance imaging, Myelin, Magnetization transferSubject terms: Multiple sclerosis, Imaging techniques, Diagnostic markers

Introduction

MRI has been adopted as a tool for the diagnosis and monitoring of multiple sclerosis (MS) in both clinical and research settings1,2. However, conventional T1- or T2-weighted MRI sequences have limited sensitivity to pathological changes and extensive alterations that occur outside visible MS lesions3–5. Effective methods are thus required to assess the physiopathological processes associated with MS in both lesions and the normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) and normal-appearing grey matter (NAGM).

Magnetization transfer (MT) imaging is an MRI technique that characterizes the brain microstructure by measuring magnetization exchange between free water protons and protons bound to macromolecules6,7. In quantitative MT (qMT), macromolecular proton fraction (MPF) represents the relative amount of macromolecular protons involved in MT effect, which reflects the tissue macromolecular level in a quantitative manner8. MPF has exhibited a strong correlation with myelin histology9,10, and has demonstrated its stability and reliability for tissue characterization11. However, the time-consuming data acquisition and complicated analysis associated with qMT make MPF mapping challenging in routine clinical MS imaging12,13. Fast methods have been developed to quantify MPF with an assumption of constant relaxation time ratio (T1/T2) and an extra T1 map as prior knowledge14,15. Recently, a novel MPF quantification model, termed MPF-SL, was reported16. Based on the off-resonance spin-lock MRI technique, it can remove tissue parameters associated with the free water pool in the quantitative MT model. As a result, it enables MPF characterization in an isolated fashion without the assumption of free water relaxation or extra T1 mapping acquisition. However, its applications in MS have not yet been explored.

Diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) is another common MRI method used to characterize oedema and demyelination. Previous studies have shown a significant correlation between the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) and tissue damage in MS17,18. However, it is unclear whether diffusion MRI and MPF mapping could offer complementary information about brain damage in MS.

In view of these considerations, the aims of this study were threefold: (1) to propose a fast three-dimensional (3D) time-efficient sequence for whole-brain MPF quantification, based on the previously described MPF-SL model; (2) to investigate the feasibility of using this technique for the assessment of microstructural changes in patients with relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS); and (3) to compare MPF with established DWI for the assessment of RRMS.

The typical T1w, T2w, FLAIR, MPF maps, and the corresponding segmentation masks of grey matter, white matter, deep grey matter and lesions from a patient and a healthy control were shown in Fig. 3. Discussion

We proposed a time-efficient MPF mapping method based on spin-lock. The total scan time for achieving 3D whole-brain coverage, including imaging data acquisition and 3D B1 mapping, is less than three minutes. We investigated this approach in simulation, phantom, and in vivo experiments, and prospectively examined its clinical feasibility to assess microstructural changes in patients with RRMS. Our results show significantly lower MPF within lesions, NAWM, NAGM, and NADGM (thalamus and caudate) in patients compared to controls. Furthermore, both MPF and ADC characterized abnormalities in close to lesion areas (P < 0.01).

In addition to our proposed rapid MPF imaging approach with spin-lock preparation, there are several other related works of MT imaging on MS using MTR19. It was found that MTR in lesions was lower than that in NAWM, and MTR also decreased in NAWM among patients compared with normal subjects, indicating a loss in microstructural integrity, which is consistent with our findings in this study. However, MTR is semi-quantitative, which could be confounded by sequence parameters. In contrast, MPF is an intrinsic property of the tissue, which can reflect the relative amount of bound pool involved in MT compared with free water protons in a quantitative way. Compared to the other existing qMT or MPF imaging approaches14–16,20,21, the proposed technique further simplifies the acquisition scheme. To obtain MPF quantification, four spin-lock prepared acquisitions are needed using the conventional MPF-SL method16. In contrast, with further sequence optimization and physical modeling, only two spin-lock prepared acquisitions are needed for MPF quantification using our proposed rapid MPF-SL method, which significantly reduces the scan time and the motion artifact. To summarize, under the same conditions of fixed kba and T2b as used in other fast MPF mapping methods, only two contrast-prepared volumes and B1 mapping are required for MPF estimation in our proposed approach, resulting in high scan time efficiency. The proposed method does not require T1 maps as prior knowledge or fitting output for MPF quantification, which leads to significantly reduced scan time. The proposed approach needs a B1 map for MPF calculation, which can be obtained rapidly using established fast B1 map methods such as Ref.22.

A decrease in MPF in brain tissues is generally consistent with demyelination or neurodegeneration in MS23. We found reduced MPF values within the NAWM and NAGM in patients compared with controls, which shows good agreement with previous studies12,24–26, suggesting that microstructural changes of NAWM and NAGM may be quantifiable with MPF. For NADGM, a significant decrease in MPF was found within the thalamus and caudate in this work. In a previous study, Yarnykh et al.27 reported a significant decrease of MPF within the thalamus, pallidum, and putamen, but no significant difference within caudate between patients and controls. The possible reasons to cause the discrepant findings might be a different study population and methodological distinctions in acquisition protocols. Further investigation is needed to compare our proposed fast MPF-SL approach and the existing MPF mapping methods.

Furthermore, we compared the regional ROIs of four categories in MPF and ADC. Based on the results of Table 2, significantly reduced MPF and increased ADC are shown within the NAWM-Close compared to the NAWM-Far (P < 0.01), NAWM-Contra (P < 0.05) and NWM (P < 0.01) of controls. The MPF values for NAWM-Far (P = 0.11) and NAWM-Contra (P = 0.08) were not significantly different from the MPF values for the control NWM. The ADC values in the NAWM-Far were similar to the NAWM-Contra (P = 0.64), but the NAWM-Contra had statistically significantly higher ADC values than the controls (P < 0.01).

Previous literature suggests that potential changes in the NAWM-Contra in patients with MS may occur due to potential damage to transhemispheric projection fibres and subsequent Wallerian degeneration28, which may be related to lesion load. This hypothesis has been supported by previously demonstrated reductions in N-acetyl aspartate in regions contralateral to demyelinating lesions, suggesting a reduction in neuron integrity29,30. In this study, patients had relatively low disease severity, and low disability (mean EDSS 3.26). This may partially account for the relative lack of white matter changes seen in the NAWM-Far and NAWM-Contra, with MPF values in these regions being similar to controls. On the other hand, alterations in ADC values typically reflect parenchymal inflammation or alterations in diffusibility. These may occur in projectional fibres in multiple sclerosis, but theoretically, they may even occur in the absence of demyelination31. It has also been shown that ADC alterations can be transient in the course of MS and may revert to normal within 1 to 2 weeks32,33. In contrast, MPF measurements predominantly evaluate macromolecular content. Therefore, MPF and ADC may detect complementary findings occurring in the NAWM. MPF may more truly reflect changes in cellular damage and tissue destruction.

There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, this work serves as a preliminary feasibility study using relatively lower resolution MPF maps for quantification. MPF imaging with higher resolution15 may be helpful to further analysis by reducing the partial volume effect. Secondly, following previous studies14,15,20,25, kba and T2b were also considered constants during brain MPF quantification in this work. However, there is a possibility that such an assumption may be violated in MS. For example, a recent study has reported that the MT exchange rate may correlate with cognitive impairment in multiple sclerosis34. Future work is needed to investigate the influence of kba and T2b on MPF estimation and on MS assessment using our proposed MPF imaging approach. Thirdly, a larger subject group would be helpful to fully assess the clinical utility of the proposed method.

In conclusion, our proposed fast MPF-SL imaging is a time-efficient MPF mapping technique with a promise to evaluate microstructural changes in multiple sclerosis for clinical use.

Like the methods reported in Refs.14,15, we assume exchange rate kba is a constant. In general, we can use Bloch-McConnell equation to calculate MPF from Rmtsl16. A B1 map is needed to calculate MPF, which can be obtained using the existing fast B1 mapping approaches.

The proposed fast MPF-SL sequence is designed based on 3D turbo spin echo (TSE) acquisitions with MPF signal encoded using the spin-lock preparation. At the beginning of the sequence, an RF pulse was used to set the magnetization to zero for both free water and MT pool. After magnetization reset module, the recovery time T1rec is chosen with T1rec>T1 which is a condition required for Eq. (3), as shown in the derivation details in Supplementary Information S1. T1rec was set to 1850 ms in our proposed sequence to satisfy this condition for white matter (WM) and grey matter (GM). The spin-lock pulse was performed with the following parameters: ω11/2 π = 100 Hz, ω12/2 π = 500 Hz, Δω1/2 π = 800 Hz, and Δω2/2 π = 4000 Hz, and TSL = 50 ms.

Simulations

We conducted two-pool full Bloch-McConnell equation simulations using the previously reported sequence parameters. Both WM and GM were modeled in the simulation: for WM, T1/T2 = 1084 ms/70 ms and 1084 ms/10 μs for free water pool and MT pool, respectively, MPF 13%, kba 23 s-1; for GM, T1/T2 1820 ms/99 ms and 1820 ms/10 μs for free water pool and MT pool, respectively, MPF 6.5%, kba 40s-1. To examine the accuracy of MPF estimation with various T1 and T2 values, we performed simulations with T1 ranging from 500 to 2000 ms and T2 ranging from 50 to 150 ms. We also performed simulations with B1 RF inhomogeneity in the range of 0.7 to 1.3 and B0 field inhomogeneity in the range of – 100 to 100 Hz to investigate the robustness of MPF quantification in the presence of B1 RF and B0 field inhomogeneity. The relative percentage error of measured MPF was calculated by 100% ×|measured MPF – true MPF|/true MPF.

Phantom study

Eight agarose phantoms were prepared and divided into two groups: one group containing 3 phantoms with 0.4 mM MnCl2 and agarose concentrations of 1\%, 2\%, and 3\%, respectively; and the other group containing 5 phantoms with no MnCl2 and agar concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 \% with 1% increment. Note agar concentration reflects the MPF content. Like the previous studies12,16, MnCl2 was used to tune T1 and T2 relaxation time. Besides the proposed fast MPF-SL approach, the conventional MPF-SL imaging16 was also performed. The imaging parameters of phantom experiments are provided in Table 3.

MRI acquisition

The MRI scans were carried out using a 3.0 T Achieva MRI scanner equipped with dual transmit (Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). An eight-channel head coil (Invivo Corp, Gainesville, USA) was used for signal reception, while a body coil was employed for signal transmission. The study protocol was approved by our local institutional review board of the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong-New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (N.T., Hong Kong SAR) and was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed written consent was obtained from all human participants. MRI examinations consisted of the following sequences in the transverse plane of the brain: 3D T1-weighted MP-RAGE (T1w), 2D multi-slice T2-weighted turbo spin echo (TSE), 3D T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion-recovery (FLAIR), 2D multi-slice DWI with 3 b-values of 0, 500, and 1000, proposed 3D fast MPF-SL acquisition, and vendor-provided 2D multi-slice B1 mapping22. The detailed imaging parameters are listed in Table 3.

Subjects

Thirty-four participants (mean age, 38 years ± 9 [standard deviation]; 13 male), including 17 MS patients and 17 age and sex matched controls, were recruited at our institution between 1 August 2022 and 31 October 2022. Inclusion criteria consisted of: above 18 years; MS diagnosis according to the revised McDonald criteria1 and RRMS disease course for MS patients; voluntary participation in the study for controls. Exclusion criteria consisted of: contraindication to MR imaging such as the presence of metallic implants, claustrophobia, and pregnancy; presence of other neurological conditions, brain injury, substance abuse or major psychiatric illness; and datasets degraded by artefacts. Expanded disability status scale (EDSS)35 score and disease duration in years were collected from patients half an hour before the MRI scan. The use of disease modifying therapies (DMT) was also recorded in patients within 1 year prior to the MRI scan. Demographical information of participants is summarized in Table 4.

Image analysis

ADC maps were automatically reconstructed by the scanner manufacturer's software, which performs linear regression over the DWI signal logarithms with all b-value based on the mono-exponential model. Following the processing pipeline shown in Fig. 5, MPF maps were reconstructed using custom MATLAB scripts based on fast MPF-SL model mentioned above, with fixed T2b=10μs, and kba=23s-1 and 40 s-1 for white matter and grey matter, respectively.

The processing pipeline from raw MR images to obtain the MPF map. In step 1, the Rmtsl map can be calculated using two spin-lock-prepared imaging maps via Eq. (3); In step 2, the MPF map can be estimated using Rmtsl map and acquired B1 map following the previously described work16.

To investigate the feasibility of the MPF technique in detecting MS related microstructural changes, global and regional changes were both evaluated:

To assess global abnormalities, whole brain MPF maps were reconstructed, followed by segmentation of the NAWM, NAGM, normal-appearing deep grey matter (NADGM) and MS lesions. MPF values of these regions were compared between patients and controls. The segmentation details are described in Supplementary Information S2.

To examine subtle abnormalities in regional NAWM in patients, we manually defined four WM regions of interest (ROIs) in MS patients: (1) within MS lesions; (2) NAWM-Close, ≤ 5 mm from the lesion but excluding the margin of the lesion; (3) NAWM-Far, > 5 mm from the lesion; and (4) NAWM-Contra, in the corresponding mirror site of the NAWM-Far in the cerebral hemisphere. Further details are described in Supplementary Information S2. The same shape and size of ROIs were drawn on the MPF maps and ADC maps. All ROIs and segmentations were verified by an experienced neuroradiologist (T.Y.S) with more than 10 years of experience.

Statistical analyses

Phantom MPF measurements were compared between the conventional and proposed fast MPF-SL methods by bivariate linear regression and Bland–Altman analysis. MPF values in the NAWM, NAGM, and NADGN of patients were compared to the corresponding normal tissues in controls using Mann–Whitney U and t-tests. Alterations in MPF between controls and patients were evaluated through percentage differences as well as effect sizes (Cohen’s d), defined as the ratio of the mean difference between the two groups to the pooled standard deviation. MPF and ADC values of the regional NAWM were compared using t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. Pearson’s correlation was evaluated between MPF and ADC in NAWM. P < 0.05 indicated a significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc (Medcalc software V20.211, https://www.medcalc.org/).
