Tension gastrothorax in late diagnosis of congenital diaphragmatic hernia
Authors: Yasmine Houas, Hela Oueslati, Nada Sghairoun, Asma Guitouni, Riadh Jouini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTension gastrothorax is a rare but potentially fatal complication of congenital diaphragmatic hernia, frequently mistaken for tension pneumothorax. Early recognition is essential to avoid delays in definitive management. We describe the case of a 14-month-old boy who presented with sudden respiratory distress and non-productive vomiting. Imaging revealed a tension gastrothorax caused by herniation of the stomach, spleen, and colon through a diaphragmatic defect. The patient underwent emergency surgery with successful reduction of the herniated organs and repair of the defect. This case highlights the importance of considering tension gastrothorax in the differential diagnosis of acute pediatric respiratory distress with mediastinal shift. CT imaging plays a crucial role in accurate diagnosis, and timely surgical intervention is lifesaving.Keywords: Tension gastrothorax, Diaphragmatic hernia, CT scan, Case report

Introduction

Tension gastrothorax is a rare but life-threatening condition that occurs when abdominal organs, most commonly the stomach, herniate into the thoracic cavity through a diaphragmatic defect and become acutely distended with air or fluid. This leads to significant intrathoracic pressure, causing lung compression, mediastinal shift, and compromised venous return, which can rapidly progress to respiratory and circulatory failure if not promptly recognized and treated. The clinical presentation often mimics more common conditions such as tension pneumothorax or pulmonary infections, making diagnosis challenging, especially in previously healthy children [1,2].

We report the case of a 14-month-old boy presenting with acute respiratory distress in whom imaging revealed a tension gastrothorax due to a previously undiagnosed congenital diaphragmatic hernia. This case underscores the importance of early recognition and the role of imaging, particularly CT scan, in guiding appropriate and life-saving management. We will also discuss the key clinical and radiological features that support the diagnosis of tension gastrothorax and help distinguish it from other thoracic emergencies in children.

Case report

A previously healthy 14-month-old boy was brought to the emergency department for acute respiratory distress and non-productive vomiting that had started less than 24 hours prior. There was no history of trauma, fever, foreign body inhalation, or similar episodes in the past. No prenatal diagnosis of diaphragmatic hernia was made.

Upon arrival, the child appeared cyanotic with peripheral bluish discoloration, tachycardic at 165 bpm, polyenoic with a respiratory rate of 52 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 88% on room air. He was alert and responsive but showed moderate subcostal and intercostal retractions, with decreased air entry on the left hemithorax and slight abdominal distension. Heart sounds were shifted to the right, raising suspicion of intrathoracic mass effect. Capillary refill was less than 3 seconds, and blood pressure was within normal limits for age.

A thoracoabdominal X-ray revealed a large, well-defined radiolucent area occupying the majority of the left hemithorax, with a mediastinal shift to the right and absence of normal bowel gas pattern in the abdomen (Fig. 1). Differential diagnoses at this point included giant lobar emphysema, bullous emphysematous pneumonia, and congenital pulmonary airway malformation.

Thoracoabdominal radiograph showing a large radiolucent area (red arrows) occupying the left hemithorax, associated with absence of diaphragmatic contour on the left side, suggestive of intrathoracic herniation of abdominal organs.

A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest and abdomen provided a definitive diagnosis: a large left posterolateral diaphragmatic hernia, with herniation of the stomach, spleen, and splenic flexure of the colon into the thoracic cavity (Fig. 2). The herniated organs caused significant compression and collapse of the left lung, explaining the respiratory compromise.

A CT scan showing intrathoracic herniation of the stomach (Star) into the left hemithorax, with significant mediastinal shift to the right (yellow arrows), consistent with tension gastrothorax. (A) transverse section. (B) coronal section.

After initial stabilization with oxygen therapy and nasogastric decompression, the patient was taken to the operating room. A transabdominal surgical approach confirmed the CT findings. The herniated viscera were carefully reduced into the abdominal cavity, and the diaphragmatic defect was closed primarily with non-absorbable interrupted sutures. No hernia sac was identified.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. The child was extubated on postoperative day 1, resumed feeding on day 2, and was discharged home on day 5. At one-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with normal respiratory function.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but serious cause of acute respiratory distress in infants: tension gastrothorax, a life-threatening complication of late-presenting congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH). While CDH is typically diagnosed antenatally or in the neonatal period, approximately 10%-20% of cases present later in life, often with nonspecific respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms. In such cases, diagnosis is frequently delayed due to misinterpretation of imaging or clinical overlap with more common thoracic pathologies such as pneumonia, pneumothorax, or emphysema [1].

Tension gastrothorax occurs when the stomach herniates through a diaphragmatic defect into the thoracic cavity, where it becomes massively distended with air or fluid, creating significant intrathoracic pressure. This leads to compression of the ipsilateral lung, shifting of the mediastinum, and impairment of venous return, resulting in severe respiratory distress and potential cardiovascular collapse. Unlike a simple diaphragmatic hernia, which may be asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic, tension gastrothorax is a surgical emergency due to its rapid hemodynamic effects [2,3].

Clinically and radiologically, tension gastrothorax may closely mimic tension pneumothorax, but distinguishing between them is critical, as their management differs significantly. In tension pneumothorax, chest X-ray typically shows a collapsed lung with absent vascular markings, a large radiolucent area, and contralateral mediastinal shift. There is often a visible pleural line, and the diaphragm may appear flattened [4]. In contrast, tension gastrothorax presents as a large air-filled structure in the hemithorax, but with air-fluid levels, sometimes accompanied by soft tissue densities representing herniated abdominal organs (e.g., stomach, colon, or spleen). The stomach can sometimes be traced by a nasogastric tube coiled in the thoracic cavity, which is a key diagnostic clue. The mediastinal shift is often more dramatic in tension gastrothorax due to the mass effect of distended viscera [5,6].

Other differential diagnoses such as giant lobar emphysema or congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) can also mimic this radiologic appearance. Giant lobar emphysema typically shows hyperinflation of a lung lobe, often the left upper lobe, with displacement of adjacent lung tissue, but the lucency is homogeneous without fluid levels or bowel-like patterns [7]. CPAM may appear as a multicystic or solid mass with varying degrees of aeration but rarely causes the severe mediastinal shift seen in tension gastrothorax. Bullous emphysema may show thin-walled air-filled spaces, but again lacks the complexity of air-fluid levels or signs of herniated abdominal content [8]. Given the overlapping clinical presentations, failure to recognize these radiographic distinctions can lead to misdiagnosis. In particular, placing a chest tube in a patient with tension gastrothorax, under the false assumption of pneumothorax, can cause iatrogenic perforation of intrathoracic abdominal viscera, worsening the clinical condition [9].

Therefore, early and accurate radiologic interpretation is essential for differentiating tension gastrothorax from other causes of thoracic radiolucency in children, ensuring the correct and life-saving surgical management: In our case, the child presented with acute respiratory distress, cyanosis, and polypnea, along with radiographic findings showing a large air-filled structure occupying the left hemithorax. Initial differential diagnoses included giant lobar emphysema or bullous lung disease. However, the presence of abdominal gas paucity and mediastinal shift raised suspicion for a diaphragmatic origin. CT scan confirmed the diagnosis of tension gastrothorax, with herniation of the stomach, spleen, and colon compressing the left lung.

Immediate decompression via nasogastric tube placement and surgical intervention are the cornerstones of management. Unlike tension pneumothorax, chest tube insertion is contraindicated, as it may cause visceral injury and worsen the patient’s condition. In our case, early diagnosis, stabilization, and surgical reduction of herniated organs followed by primary diaphragmatic repair led to a favorable outcome [10].

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the importance of considering tension gastrothorax in any child presenting with sudden respiratory distress and mediastinal shift on imaging. Unlike pneumothorax, it involves herniation and distension of abdominal organs into the thoracic cavity, requiring a different treatment approach. A thoracoabdominal CT scan is crucial for confirming the diagnosis and avoiding mismanagement. Prompt recognition and surgical intervention are essential to prevent life-threatening cardiopulmonary collapse.
