Going the distance: a cross-sectional geospatial analysis quantifying province-wide inequities in travel-based access, and fragility of access to French-language primary care provided by family physicians in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Christopher Belanger, Patrick E Timony, Arlynn Bélizaire, Antoine Desilets, Jonathan Peter Fitzsimon, Alain P Gauthier, Leanda Godfrey, Sathya Karunananthan, Krystal Kehoe MacLeod, Mwali-Nachishali Muray, Cayden Peixoto, Lise M Bjerre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesLanguage-concordant care, or healthcare in one’s preferred language, is important both for health equity and for improving health outcomes. Linguistic minorities, like Francophones in Ontario, Canada, are at risk of poorer clinical outcomes if they receive non-language-concordant primary care. However, common ratio-based access measures can provide misleading views of minorities’ actual access levels. This cross-sectional geospatial study demonstrates a new way to measure primary care access using average travel time to the nearest five English- and French-speaking family physicians. We also introduce the concept of primary care access fragility, where a region’s primary care access may depend on one or a few local family physicians. Our research question is: are there differences in travel burden and access fragility for census subdivisions (CSDs) across language (English/French), rurality (urban/rural) and region (north/south) in the province of Ontario, Canada?DesignWe conducted a cross-sectional geospatial analysis to estimate English-language and French-language primary care travel burdens and access fragility in Ontario, Canada. We used population and boundary data from Statistics Canada’s 2021 census, road-network data from OpenStreetMaps, and family physician practice locations and language abilities from the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario. We measured travel burden using Valhalla, an open-source road-network analysis platform.SettingWe conducted our analysis for Ontario, Canada’s 577 CSDs, which correspond roughly to municipalities and with populations ranging from 5 inhabitants in Rainy Lake 17B to a high of 2 794 356 in Toronto.ParticipantsUsing public data from January 2026, we identified 15 762 family physicians practising in Ontario, of whom 11.0% reported speaking French. Patient data were obtained from the most recent 2021 census.Primary and secondary outcome measuresOur first primary outcome measures were CSD-level mean travel time to the nearest five English-speaking family physicians, and CSD-level mean travel time to the nearest five French-speaking family physicians, which we compared to explore regional inequities in travel burden. Our secondary outcome measures were based on a novel notion of the travel burden component of ‘primary care access fragility’. This metric indicates how dependent a region’s access is on a small number of local physicians and is defined as the difference between the CSD-level mean travel time to the nearest one physician and to the nearest five physicians. As the difference in travel times grows, so too does access fragility.ResultsMedian differences in French-language and English-language travel burdens were strongly significant across rurality, regions and overall (median difference 13.4 min, p<0.001, IQR 4.9–29.3 min), as was primary care access fragility (median difference 5.2 min, p<0.001, IQR 0.8–18.4), with larger travel burdens and fragilities in northern and rural communities.ConclusionsCompared with the general public, Ontario’s French-speakers face higher travel burdens to language-concordant family physicians and higher access fragility, especially in rural and northern regions. Our results are of interest to policymakers and health-system planners, and our methods are applicable to other populations and regions.Keywords: Primary Health Care, Health Equity, Health policy

Introduction

Minority-language patients often face barriers to accessing healthcare in their preferred language, or access to ‘language-concordant care’. Far from being a mere preference, empirical studies have also found that patients from minority language groups are at risk of poorer clinical outcomes if they receive non-language-concordant care, even when they also speak the majority language.1,6 As such, language-concordant care is increasingly recognised as a core element of health equity frameworks. In Canada’s most-populated province of Ontario, which is majority English-speaking but with vibrant Francophone minority communities, language is embedded in official health equity frameworks at both the national7 and provincial8 levels, and in some cases language-concordant care is a legal right protected under Ontario’s French Language Services Act.9 In order to ensure that healthcare access is equitably distributed, both to improve patient outcomes and to meet official policy goals, we need to compare the Francophone minority populations’ access to French-language healthcare with that of the general/anglophone population to care in English.

Although common approaches to measuring healthcare access rely on simple population-to-provider ratios,10 11 we have previously argued that this method is unsuitable for measuring access to language concordant care for minority language populations.12 In our previous work,12 we proposed an alternative method for calculating access to French-speaking physicians in Ontario by calculating competition-adjusted ratio (ie, since physicians accept patients on a first come first served basis, access ratios were adjusted to account for the fact that Francophones must compete with the entire population to access the French-speaking physicians). We also measured the probability of accessing language concordant care and found that, although French-speaking physicians may be more equitably distributed than was originally thought and Francophones may have better access to language concordant care than was originally estimated, they continue to have far worse access to care in French than the general population has to care in English. However, the regional ratio approach used in our previous work does not account for distance to care as a facilitator/barrier to access.

Thus, in this follow-up study, we propose and demonstrate a new method that avoids this issue, based on average travel time to the nearest five English-speaking and French-speaking family physicians. Travel burden is clear and intuitive, allows straightforward comparisons between English- and French-language access levels, is recognised as an important element in rural health modelling of health equity,7 and has been used in prior work exploring local access levels.13 Furthermore, recent studies using patient-level data have found that greater travel distances to access primary care are associated with negative outcomes including higher emergency department use and reduced uptake of recommended cancer screenings.14

We also introduce the novel concept of primary care access fragility, the notion that a region’s primary care access may depend on one or a few local providers, such that the loss of one physician would drastically reduce access. Primary care might be especially fragile in rural and remote regions, where a few physicians may serve entire communities, or where several physicians are nearing retirement age. Such fragility is discussed in news media15 16 and recognised in government policies,17 18 but to our knowledge, it is understudied in the research literature: prior research into ‘fragile’ healthcare has focused on conflict-affected states19 20 or on system-level concerns.21 We expect access fragility to be a multi-factorial concept, and in this paper, we explore the travel burden component by studying the difference in regional distance to the nearest five physicians compared with the nearest one.

Methods

Study setting and context

Our study focused on the province of Ontario, Canada. Canada is an officially bilingual nation with two official languages, English and French. Ontario is Canada’s most populous province (14,223,942 pop., 2021 census) and second-largest province in terms of surface area with a total land area of just over 892 000 km2s.22 While many Ontarians live in the south of the province in large urban centres, there are also many smaller towns and rural communities, and in the north of the province some remote communities are only accessible via seasonal ice roads or small aircraft. While Ontario is an English-majority province, it borders the French-majority province of Québec and has the largest French-speaking population outside of Québec. Ontario is a major destination for French-speaking immigrants who seek post-secondary educational opportunities in French. Ontario also has policies and laws, including the French Language Services Act, requiring it to provide equitable access to French-language services at certain publicly funded institutions.9 In the most recent census data from 2021, Ontario had 14 099 790 residents outside of institutional settings of whom approximately 11.1% spoke French and 3.4% identified French as their ‘first official language spoken’.22 In this study, we focused on disparities in access to English- and French-speaking family physicians between northern and southern Ontario, and urban and rural Ontario, because prior research has found disparities in access to language-concordant primary care between these regions.10

Data sources and collection

All data used in the present study was collected from publicly available sources. Physician data, including self-reported language ability, were collected from the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario’s (CPSO) website in January 2026.23 Regional geographic boundaries, regional classifications, road networks and census data were obtained from Statistics Canada’s public website.24,27 Road networks were also collected from OpenStreetMaps via the Geofabrik download server.28

Data preparation

Region definitions and population counts

In this study, we considered two types of administrative regions defined by Statistics Canada. For trip origins and population counts, we used dissemination blocks (DBs, n=1 37 867 in Ontario), which are the smallest type of administrative region and correspond roughly to city blocks. For our presentation and statistical analysis, we used census subdivisions (CSDs, n=577 in Ontario), which correspond roughly to municipalities (figure 1). DB boundaries and CSD boundaries align, such that each DB belongs to only one CSD and each CSD comprises many DBs. DB populations were read from Statistics Canada’s 2021 Geographic Attribute File counts for ‘Total population’.27 All Statistics Canada data was from the 2021 census, the most recent as of writing. Because our focus here is on travel times across the province, we have not included information about patients’ language abilities in the present analysis. However, as we discuss briefly in the closing section, regional data concerning language abilities is available and could be used in future studies.

Distribution and population of Ontario’s census subdivisions (CSDs).

Regional classification

We classified CSDs as northern or southern according to the administrative health regions they fell within, as defined by Ontario’s Ministry of Health. We labelled CSDs as ‘northern’ if they were in the ‘North East’ or ‘North West’ health regions, and labelled all other CSDs as ‘southern’ (figure 2). This is consistent with Ontario’s policy definitions of the North for provincial programmes like the Northern Health Travel Grant.17

Classification of Ontario’s CSDs according to whether they are urban or rural, and in Ontario’s north or south regions. CSDs, census subdivisions.

We classified CSDs as urban or rural using Statistical Area Classifications (SAC) definitions provided by Statistics Canada26 (figure 2), which specifies whether CSDs are part of a Census Metropolitan Area (CMA, minimum population of 100,000) or a Census Agglomeration (CA, minimum population of 10,000), defined as ‘large, densely populated centres made up of adjacent municipalities that are economically and socially integrated’, and differentiates these from Metropolitan Influenced Zones (MIZ), which are CSDs that lie outside economically and socially integrated regions.29 Consistent with prior studies of Ontario’s physicians,10 we labelled CSDs ‘urban’ if they fell within a CMA or a CA, and ‘rural’ if they fell within an MIZ.

Physician classification

We used an algorithm shown in figure 3 to identify physicians providing comprehensive family medicine care to the community (‘family physicians’), as opposed to all other physicians (‘other physicians’). In Ontario, there is no perfect way to identify community-based family physicians; for example, physicians with specialties in Family Medicine may be practising in other areas (eg, sports medicine) or working in other capacities (eg, for government agencies). We developed our selection criteria iteratively through exploratory data analysis, consultation with the authors of previous studies10 13 and with expert input from our research team. Readers are referred to the paper where we introduced this method for more details.12

Cohort creation chart for the population of family physicians providing comprehensive family medicine services to the community. CPSO, College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario.

Physicians were identified as ‘French-speaking’ if they self-reported the ability to practise in French. Primary practice locations were converted from addresses to latitude/longitude coordinates, or ‘geocoded,’ using Google’s geocoding service and the R package tidygeocoder.30 31

Geospatial travel analysis

We calculated travel burden from DBs to family physician locations using the Valhalla routing engine, an open-source road-network analysis platform that computes travel distances and times along road networks obeying traffic laws and speed limits. To ensure that trips began from points where people might plausibly live, and not from unrepresentative and uninhabited points like freeway on-ramps, swamps, or off-road trails, each DB was assigned a trip origin that was the point on the road network nearest to its centroid that was a named road, was not a major high-speed freeway/motorway, and that had a Statistics Canada rank and class consistent with residential zones. Trips ended at the latitude/longitude coordinates of physicians’ primary practice locations.

We used a two-step heuristic to calculate each DB’s average travel burden to access primary care, in which we first used a fast method to find candidate locations that could be expected to include the closest locations via road network, and then used a slower but more accurate method to find which of those candidate locations were closest via road travel. The rationale for using a heuristic is that an exhaustive search would require approximately one billion detailed road travel calculations from each DB to each physician location, which could take weeks of processing time for little extra benefit.

Describing our heuristic in more detail, for each DB we first used a fast straight-line algorithm to find a pool of the nearest physician locations that included at least 100 locations and at least five French-speaking family physicians. Then we used Valhalla to calculate the road-network travel burden from each DB to each of these locations. We included at least 100 locations to ensure we found the locations closest via road network, since nearest physician location by a straight line may not be the nearest via road. We computed 13 789 080 unique trips from each DB (n=137 867) to its nearest road-accessible family physician locations (n=6336). For each DB, we found the average distance to the nearest one and five family physicians, for any physician and for French-speaking physicians. Results were aggregated to CSDs using population-weighted averaging. Our analysis did not include traffic, weather conditions or time-of-day information.

Statistical analysis

We compared within-CSD differences in travel burden and primary care access fragility using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a non-parametric test of whether differences in observations have a distribution centred around 0. We examined the effects of and interactions between rurality (ie, urban/rural), region (ie, north/south) and physician language (English/French) on mean CSD-level travel burden and primary care access fragility using a three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. Before running statistical tests, we removed outliers defined as any CSD with mean travel times that were undefined—meaning that they are inaccessible by road—or more than 24 hours, which is unrealistically long for road travel. Consequently, remote, fly-in communities in Northern Ontario were excluded from these analyses.

Software and hardware

The analysis was carried out using the R Language for Statistical Computing32 on a Dell R720 server.

Data analysis

Analysis 1: travel burden to five nearest family physicians

We aggregated fine-grained DB-level travel data to the larger CSD level, and calculated CSD-level mean travel times to the five nearest family physicians of any language ability, and then to the five nearest French-speaking family physicians. This metric has been used in prior studies of primary care accessibility13 and is intuitively meant to capture a proxy measure of the average amount of time a patient would need to travel to access care. With population-level data, we cannot calculate actual distances from patients to their actual physician, and there is no public data in Ontario about which physicians are accepting new patients. The choice of calculating the distance to the five nearest family physicians was based on two factors: first, while recent data is sparse, historically approximately one in five Canadian family physicians has been accepting new patients33; and second, an earlier sensitivity analysis did not identify a significant difference between using the nearest five and the nearest ten physicians.13

Analysis 2: primary care access fragility

We calculated the travel burden component of primary care access fragility as the difference between the travel time to the nearest one family physician and the travel time to the five nearest family physicians, first for any physician and then again for French-speaking physicians. For example, if a CSD’s average travel time to the nearest physician is 5 min and the average travel time to the nearest five physicians is 15 min, its travel fragility index would be 15 min–5 min =10 min. Intuitively, if the nearest physician retired, residents would need to travel an average of 10 extra minutes to access care.

Patient and public involvement

There was no formal patient and public involvement with this study.

Results

Family physician population

We collected data for a total of 45 179 physicians licensed to practise in the Province of Ontario, which represents all physicians listed on the CPSO’s website with active registration statuses at the time of data collection. We found 42 535 physicians practising in Ontario, of whom 4678 (11.6%) self-reported the ability to practise in French. We identified 15 762 as family physicians likely to be providing comprehensive family medicine services to the community in Ontario, 1738 (11%) of whom were able to practise in French (figure 3).

Most physicians were located in southern and urban areas, and northern and rural physicians were more likely to speak French than their southern and urban counterparts (table 1).

Discussion

Summary of the main results

We identified and located n=15 762 family physicians likely to be providing comprehensive primary care in Ontario, of whom n=1738 (11.0%) self-report being able to provide care in French. Across municipalities, we found that French-speakers must travel significantly farther to access language-concordant care than the general population (mean difference 26.5 min, median difference 13.4 min), and for both linguistic groups, we found that travel was significantly farther in rural and northern areas than urban and southern areas, respectively. We introduced the concept of ‘primary care access fragility,’ an empirical measure of how dependent a region’s primary care system is on one or a few local physicians. We found that French-language primary care is more fragile than English-language primary care, and that fragility is higher in rural and northern areas than urban and southern areas.

Explanation of the findings

Francophones face consistently higher travel burdens to access language-concordant care than the general population. These inequities are worse in rural and northern regions, and French-speaking physicians are not evenly distributed throughout the province, creating pockets where patients could experience significantly worse access to physician care. For example, while areas in Ontario’s east and south had similar access levels between linguistic groups, French-language access was clearly worse in the south-west and in many remote northern communities. Furthermore, these burdens likely represent a best-case scenario because French-speaking physicians also accept non-Francophone patients, meaning that Francophone Ontarians must compete with all Ontarians for access to these French-speaking physicians.12

Similarly, while access fragility is similar for English and French speakers across much of the province, figure 6 shows pockets where French-language fragility is much higher, indicating regions that are vulnerable to losing access if even a small number of physicians retire or relocate. In these regions, the loss of a single physician could drastically reduce regional access levels.

Our study also complements recent findings using the ‘competition-adjusted ratio’ approach to measuring health access, which suggested that French-speaking physicians may be more equitably distributed than previously thought, particularly in the north east and eastern regions of Ontario with larger concentrations of Francophones.12 In particular, our study suggests that when we consider travel burden and access fragility as dimensions of access, even regions with favourable French-speaking physician-to-francophone population ratios may continue to experience unmet needs for language concordant care. These findings suggest that a travel-based approach to studying health access in Ontario can help to identify areas with less equitable access to language-concordant services, and to inform efforts to meet policy and legislative health equity goals.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations, including its focus on primary care delivered by physicians excluding other provider types (eg, nurse practitioners) due to lack of data, the inherent uncertainty in its method of estimating which physicians are providing primary care, its reliance on OpenStreetMaps data, and that it does not consider the distribution of Ontario’s French-speaking population. Future research could extend this study in several ways, for example incorporating data about Ontario’s French-speaking population to match supply with demand. Our algorithm for identifying primary care physicians could be improved with additional data, such as billing data or qualitative data drawn from local knowledge, physician full-time equivalent levels, and which physicians are accepting new patients. We could also consider access to other primary care providers such as Nurse Practitioners, Physician Assistants and Community Paramedics.34 The novel notion of primary care access fragility could be refined and developed theoretically and empirically, and factors beyond travel could be explored. Future collection of patient-level language data in administrative data—for example, by including preferred language on health cards—could enable detailed individual-level analyses cross-referenced with French-speaking physician billing data. Finally, our analysis could support health-system planning through detailed studies of other regions or more specialties, assuming the requisite data are available.

Conclusions

Identifying and locating Ontario’s French-speaking family physicians is an important step towards measuring and understanding gaps in access to language-concordant healthcare for Ontario’s Francophone residents. This paper has demonstrated a repeatable methodology and confirmed results that could only be previously inferred from existing papers. Taken together, these findings suggest that a travel-based approach to studying health access in Ontario can help to identify areas with less equitable access to language-concordant services, and to inform efforts to meet policy and legislative health equity goals.
