A machine learning-based method to optimize the immunogenicity of human leukocyte antigen class I-restricted neoantigens
Authors: Jinhong Zhang, Weijun Zhou, Jinyi Yu, Chaoyang Yan, Jian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoantigens, arising from somatic mutations, have the potential to induce immune responses against tumor cells. As natural neoantigens that drive immune responses are uncommon, some methods to optimize neoantigens have been devised, leading to “heteroclitic” neoantigens capable of triggering cross-immunization. Existing methods, however, concentrate exclusively on the affinity of neoantigens for human leukocyte antigen (HLA), while neglecting the enhancement of their immunogenicity. Here, we developed a machine learning-based method, Naso, which integrates simulated annealing search and multi-objective training to optimize the immunogenicity of neoantigens. We also designed a search space optimization strategy to improve search efficiency. Experimental evaluation demonstrated that Naso outperforms existing methods in enhancing the immunogenicity of neoantigens. Specifically, Naso can transform nonimmunoreactive neoantigens into immunoreactive ones with no more than three mutation amino acids. Moreover, Naso can obtain competitive results in other immunological features, such as binding affinity and presentation. Consequently, Naso-optimized neoantigens can elicit an immune response and facilitate cross-immunization, thereby promoting the development of heteroclitic neoantigen-based vaccines. The source code of Naso is available at https://github.com/lyotvincent/Naso.

Introduction

Neoantigens are tumor-specific antigens generated by somatic mutations in tumors and can be presented by major histocompatibility (MHC) molecules on tumor cells [1]. These peptide–MHC (pMHC) complexes can be recognized by T cells, which then induce a specific immune response against the cancer. As neoantigens are expressed in tumor cells but not in normal cells and neoantigen-specific T cells can be found in most cancers, the neoantigen vaccine represents a safe and effective approach for inducing antitumor T cell responses [2, 3]. However, not all neoantigens can elicit T cell responses for various reasons. For example, to stably bind to MHC molecules, neoantigens must have the proper length and the correct anchor residues at specific positions to fit into pockets of the MHC molecules [4, 5]. Notably, only 10% of tumor mutations generate high-affinity MHC binders, with merely 1% eliciting T cell recognition [1]. This scarcity of immunogenic neoantigens poses major challenges for vaccine development. While some neoantigens cannot elicit an immune response, they can be optimized by modifying a few residues to enhance their immunological characteristics, including MHC binding affinity, presentation, and even immunogenicity. TCR cross-reactivity enables recognition of similar epitopes, with single TCRs capable of interacting with 106 MHC-bound peptides [6]. Therefore, these optimized neoantigens, also known as “heteroclitic” neoantigens, can activate naïve T cells and are sufficiently similar to the original neoantigens to elicit a cross-reactive response from the activated T cells to the original neoantigen, provided that the original neoantigen exhibits an intermediate binding affinity [5, 7]. For example, CALRMUT represents a neoantigen in myeloproliferative neoplasms [8, 9]. However, its immunogenicity is limited by insufficient MHC molecules with high-affinity binding capacity in patients [5]. In contrast, the CD8+ T cell response, which is cross-reactive to the CALRMUT neoantigens, can be efficiently induced by heteroclitic CALRMUT neoantigens. Consequently, the heteroclitic neoantigen-based vaccine is regarded as a promising therapeutic approach.

The challenge lies in the considerable individual variability of neoantigens and the vastness of their sequence space. Manually identifying heteroclitic neoantigens that trigger an immune response requires specialized expertise and extensive experimental validation, making the process highly resource- and time-intensive [7]. To address this problem, some in silico computational methods have been proposed to design heteroclitic neoantigens. Given the rapid development of machine learning, many approaches employ techniques such as transformer models [10] and reinforcement learning to optimize neoantigens [11, 12]. This trend aligns with the broader success of computational intelligence in predictive tasks within computational biology [13–16]. In the more specific field of neoantigen prediction research, such methods have been applied to predict the binding affinity between neoantigens and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) (the human form of MHC) molecules [17, 18], the likelihood of neoantigen presentation on the cell surface [19, 20], and immunogenic potential [21, 22]. These predictive models facilitate the initial screening of candidate neoantigens. However, despite these advancements, existing neoantigen design methods primarily focus on enhancing HLA binding affinity while neglecting immunogenicity, the critical capacity of neoantigens to elicit actual immune responses. This limitation is particularly consequential, as T cells frequently fail to recognize high-affinity neoantigens with insufficient immunogenicity.

Therefore, we developed a method called Naso to optimize neoantigens automatically, enhancing their immunogenicity and other immunological features, including binding affinity and presentation. Specifically, the method carries out a Simulated Annealing (SA) search [23] in sequence space, starting from a raw neoantigen sequence. Additionally, we designed a sequence-based strategy to limit the search space, thus improving search efficiency. Furthermore, we employed a multi-objective loss function, considering neoantigens’ structure confidence and immunological features, to direct the search toward optimizing neoantigens with high immunogenicity. In brief, the optimization process employs an iterative procedure to mutate and update the sequence, guided by the Metropolis acceptance criterion. At each step, a new sequence is generated by mutating the current one within a dynamically constrained peptide set. The proposed mutation is then accepted or rejected for update based on the multi-objective loss function. HLAs are typically categorized into two types: HLA class I (HLA-I) and HLA class II (HLA-II). As all nucleated cells can have HLA-I molecules on their surface, while HLA-II molecules are generally expressed by professional antigen-presenting cells [24], this study focuses on HLA-I molecules, considering that there are far more HLA-I molecules than HLA-II molecules. By broadly exploring the sequence space, Naso can search for an optimized neoantigen that binds with the target HLA-I allele with higher affinity and is more likely to be presented than raw neoantigens. Moreover, compared to three state-of-the-art peptide optimization methods, Naso-optimized neoantigens demonstrate superior immunogenicity, suggesting a greater probability of eliciting an immune response. Our method can efficiently generate an optimized neoantigen with no more than three mutation positions, ensuring the homology between the optimized neoantigen and the raw neoantigen to trigger cross-immunization. This saves resources and time when designing heteroclitic neoantigen-based vaccines, a promising therapeutic approach for cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Overview of Naso

Fig. 1 shows the workflow of the Naso method. The method accepts two input parameters: a raw neoantigen and a target HLA-I allele (Fig. 1a), with compatibility across all HLA-I alleles including novel variants. Initial processing involves masking HLA anchor residues followed by selection of mutation positions (Fig. 1b). To identify the optimal peptide, Naso attempts to iteratively mutate and update in limited steps (Fig. 1c, e). Specifically, Naso searches for a new sequence for mutation within the defined peptide set. Rather than including all potential sequences in the peptide set, we employed a strategy whereby the set is constructed dynamically with each step. Subsequently, Naso selects a peptide randomly from the peptide set mentioned above and proceeds with the Metropolis acceptance criterion for updating. To identify peptides exhibiting an acceptable structure and a higher probability of eliciting cross-immunization, we designed a loss function that introduces a weighted sum of structure quality terms and immunological feature terms (Fig. 1e). After some iterations, Naso outputs the optimized neoantigens. It’s worth noting that Naso does not alter the length of the sequences, while allowing users to specify the number of mutations introduced during the optimization. In the validation part, we compared the immunological features of Naso-optimized neoantigens with those of other methods in terms of binding affinity, presentation, immunogenicity, and so forth (Fig. 1d). Additionally, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on distinct pairs of neoantigens to ascertain whether the structural alterations in the mutants can effectively interact with the target HLA-I alleles and are more likely to interact with TCRs.

Dataset

This study obtains the binding data between peptides and HLA-I alleles from TSNAdb [25], dbPepNeo [26], and NEPdb [27]. It is worth noting that the data used for validation are all experimentally validated, making the experiment more convincing. Specifically, the binding affinity of data from TSNAdb, the presentation and immunogenicity of data from dbPepNeo, and the immunogenicity of data from NEPdb, are experimentally validated as positive. Following removal of duplicate and inconsistent entries, the dataset comprised 2536 peptides (length 8–11, predominantly 9-mers) categorized by HLA type: HLA-A (1996), HLA-B (473), and HLA-C (67). As the trigger for cross-immunization requires that raw neoantigens bind to HLA molecules, we attempted to remove the raw neoantigens with no affinity. However, for the TSNAdb database, the binding affinity is only validated for the wild peptides, so we removed data in which the raw neoantigens are identified as nonbinders by NetMHCpan-4.1 EL [19] and finally obtained 2420 combinations of different HLAs and neoantigens. We used these peptides as seed sequences and performed mutation in the Naso method.

Naso program

Starting from an original neoantigen, we used the SA method to find a new but similar sequence with desired immunological features inspired by deep network hallucination [28], which generates symmetric protein homo-oligomers by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) optimization. Closely related to MCMC, the SA algorithm applies the Metropolis acceptance criterion to find the global minimum of a loss function that may have multiple local minima. It simulates the physical process by slowly cooling a solid until it is “frozen” at a minimum energy configuration. In our method, the loss function focuses on peptides’ structural modeling and immunological properties, which will be described in more detail in the next section. The SA method can be applied to a variety of sets, including continuous sets [29] and finite sets [30], thereby enabling Naso to conduct searches in sequence space, which is finite.

Loss function

To optimize for neoantigens, we defined a composite loss function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $L$\end{document} composed of structure quality terms and immunological feature terms, which jointly consider peptides’ structures, presentation, and immunogenicity. Specifically, the loss is computed on two types of terms after each search: The first is the structure prediction confidence metrics, which include the predicted local-distance difference test (pLDDT) score [32] and predicted TM-score (pTM) [33]; The second is a measure of immunological features, comprising of the presentation and immunogenicity scores of searched sequences measured by the BigMHC method [20]. We used AlphaFold [34] for structure prediction. Following the default configuration of AlphaFold, we set the number of recycles to three and the number of ensembles to 1. These values are widely adopted due to their demonstrated robustness and effectiveness across a variety of prediction tasks. To reduce the computational cost, we also employed simplifications during AlphaFold predictions. Moreover, all these predicted scores are scaled between 0 and 1, and higher values are better. Results

The immunological features of Naso mutant peptides are more robust than those of raw neoantigens

Immunogenicity reflects the capacity of neoepitopes to elicit an adaptive immune response. This process encompasses the binding of neoepitopes to HLA molecules, their presentation to T cells, and their recognition by TCRs. Therefore, immunogenicity is a comprehensive feature that can be partially reflected by other immunological features, including binding affinity, presentation, etc. For example, immunogenic peptide–MHC complexes demonstrate significantly stronger binding affinity than nonimmunogenic counterparts, as evidenced by the TESLA consortium [35]. We thus employed the following tools for the evaluation of binding affinity and presentation: two IEDB-recommended [36] methods (NetMHCpan-4.1 BA [19] and NetMHCpan-4.1 EL [19]), three commonly utilized methods (MixMHCpred-3.0 [17], MHCflurry-2.0 BA [18], and MHCflurry-2.0 EL [18]), and the state-of-the-art method (BigMHC EL [20]). The binding affinity is evaluated using NetMHCpan-4.1 BA, MixMHCpred-3.0, and MHCflurry-2.0 BA, whereas NetMHCpan-4.1 EL, MHCflurry-2.0 EL, and BigMHC EL address the presentation of the peptide. Notably, while BigMHC EL (state-of-the-art) is specifically employed for loss function calculation in Naso’s framework, its superior predictive performance makes it particularly valuable for presentation likelihood evaluation. We also predicted other features, including immunogenicity and binding stability, using IEDB Immunogenicity tool [21] and NetMHCstabpan [37], respectively. More details about evaluation metrics are provided in Supplementary data.

As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the optimization of sequences by Naso results in enhanced binding affinity, higher presentation probability, and increased immunogenicity with each successive optimization step. This evidence supports the efficacy of Naso and suggests that it can generate mutant peptides with more substantial immunological features with additional optimization steps. Moreover, 91% of Naso-optimized neoantigens are identified as strong binders (rank <0.5 by NetMHCpan-4.1 BA), while 94% and 76% are verified to have high presentation probability (rank <0.5 by NetMHCpan-4.1 EL) and be positive in immunogenicity (score > 0 by IEDB Immunogenicity tool), respectively (Fig. 2b). These results confirm the usability of our method. Compared to raw neoantigens, Naso-optimized neoantigens demonstrate superior binding and presentation evaluation performance (Fig. 2c and d). Binding stability is also essential, as the peptide–MHC complex must stay bound until it can be recognized by T cells [38]. Compared to raw neoantigens, the peptides mutated by Naso exhibit more robust binding stability (Fig. 2e). Hydrophobicity is another feature associated with effective antigen presentation [35]. A more hydrophobic neoantigen is thought to be more likely to bind to the HLA binding cleft and TCR, given that the binding cleft of the HLA class I molecule and the TCR contact residues are both hydrophobic [39]. The distribution of hydrophobicity is similar between raw neoantigens and Naso-optimized neoantigens (Fig. 2f), which suggests that Naso-optimized neoantigens may have the potential to elicit immune responses, given that all raw neoantigens have been validated to bind to HLAs. We further analyzed the key residues mediating interactions with HLA-I molecules. Neoantigen recognition by HLA-I molecules depends on two primary anchor residues: P2 (position 2 of the peptide) and PΩ (the C-terminal residue of the peptide) [40]. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1a, e, Naso-optimized neoantigens exhibit high frequencies of hydrophobic residues, particularly LEU (L) at P2, and LEU (L) or VAL (V) at PΩ, which are known to facilitate peptide-HLA interactions. Notably, Naso rarely alters these critical anchor residues, particularly P2, thereby preserving the validated binding affinity of the raw neoantigens. For secondary anchor residues, including P3, P5, and P6, Naso may introduce optimizations (Supplementary Figs S1b–d). Specifically, the optimized sequences show increased frequencies of certain hydrophobic amino acids, such as TRP (W) at P3 and P5, and ILE (I) at P6, which become the most prevalent residues at these positions. Although Naso enhances hydrophobicity at these positions to improve binding affinity, its specific preference for TRP and ILE (amino acids significantly associated with immunogenicity [21]) over other hydrophobic amino acids aligns with Naso’s primary goal of optimizing toward immunogenic peptides.

Naso-mutated neoantigens generate higher immunogenicity than other methods

An immunogenic neoantigen can trigger the immune response. However, as mentioned above, only a tiny fraction of the mutations in an individual tumor are reflected by neoepitopes recognized by spontaneously occurring T cells. So, we were motivated to optimize neoantigens to improve immunogenicity. We compared Naso with raw neoantigens and three state-of-the-art peptide mutation methods: Aomp [11], Pepppo [12], and Mobius [41] (details about compared methods in Supplementary data). Immunogenicity is evaluated using IEDB Immunogenicity tool [21] and NeoaPred model [22]. Fig. 3a illustrates the IEDB and NeoaPred immunogenicity scores distributions across different methods. A higher score indicates a greater probability of eliciting an immune response, and the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test is conducted to determine if Naso generates peptides with higher scores. The resulting P-values show Naso performs almost better than other methods, proving that Naso has a greater probability of triggering cross-immunization. Fig. 3b illustrates the stratification by peptide length. Naso still performs better on the IEDB and NeoaPred evaluation metrics. Moreover, Naso is most effective for peptides of length eight and nine, while nine is the most common length of peptides presented by HLA-I [42, 43]. Although the mean score decreases as peptide length increases, Naso still works better than other methods for all peptide lengths. Naso also exhibits better performance across three major HLA-I genes (HLA-A, B, and C), with significant differences from the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Fig. 3c). However, Naso’s superiority over Aomp and Mobius on HLA-C did not reach statistical significance based on the NeoaPred metric, potentially attributable to limited power from the small HLA-C subset (N < 40) or inherent HLA-C immunogenicity prediction challenges. The results, illustrated in Fig. 3d, show that Naso enhances neoantigens’ immunogenicity, obtaining the highest mean scores for almost every allele predicted by the IEDB and NeoaPred tool. To further validate the robustness of Naso’s immunogenicity optimization capabilities, we extended our comparative analysis to include two other evaluation methods: BigMHC IM [20] and DeepImmuno [44]. Supplementary Fig. S2 demonstrates Naso’s statistically significant superiority over all benchmarked methods in both overall and length-stratified analyses. Overall, Naso achieves more robust immunogenicity than existing methods across diverse stratification strategies, confirming the method’s effectiveness.

Naso-mutated peptides enhance the possibility of interacting with TCRs

To investigate how neoantigen mutations enhance immunogenicity, we conducted MD simulations on HLA-A*02:01 complexes with raw neoantigens and their Naso mutants (details about MD see Supplementary data). Three peptide pairs are selected through sequential filtering: (i) exclusion of peptides lacking predicted HLA-A*02:01 affinity (NetMHCpan-4.1 EL), followed by (ii) selection of top three 9-mer mutants based on BigMHC IM scores. The final set includes raw neoantigens (YLLEFNTTV, TLAGDIHIV, and ELQDEVYTL) and their corresponding Naso mutants (YLWEWITTV, TLADNIWIV, and ELIDFVWTL). Analysis reveals comparable hydrogen bonding patterns between raw neoantigens and mutants with HLA-A*02:01 (Supplementary Fig. S3), with both forming bonds at P1, P2, and PΩ positions. The structures also show that all raw neoantigens and mutant peptides are bound in the conventional orientation, with the peptide backbones accommodated in the binding pockets of HLA-A*02:01 (Fig. 4). However, a reduced number of hydrogen bonds are observed between the HLA receptor and mutant peptides at other positions, which increases the likelihood of interaction with TCR. Moreover, some mutant peptides exhibit exposed side chains that project away from the HLA-A*02:01 binding pockets, forming a highly featured surface with the potential to interact with TCR (Supplementary Fig. S4). In particular, the side chains of P4 GLU in YLWEWITTV and P4 ASP and P5 ASN in TLADNIWIV face outward of the HLA pocket. In contrast, such exposed side chains are not observed in raw neoantigens. We also employed the high-precision deep learning model DeepNeo-tcr [45] to quantitatively assess T cell reactivity. The results demonstrate that Naso-optimized neoantigens have a statistically significantly higher probability of being recognized by TCRs compared to raw neoantigens (Supplementary Fig. S7). This computational evidence aligns with the structural observations and confirms that Naso mutant peptides exhibit enhanced immunogenicity through improved TCR engagement compared to raw neoantigens, enabling cross-immunization.

Naso reveals better performance on different types of peptides

Depending on whether raw neoantigens or wild-type peptides bind to HLA, there are different situations that can trigger cross-immunization. To see if Naso is more likely to elicit an immune response in these situations, we classified the data into four categories based on the binding level of these peptides for evaluation. The four categories are defined as follows: (i) wild-type peptides as binders paired with raw neoantigens as weak binders; (ii) wild-type peptides as binders paired with raw neoantigens as strong binders; (iii) wild-type peptides as nonbinders paired with raw neoantigens as weak binders; (iv) wild-type peptides as nonbinders paired with raw neoantigens as strong binders. NetMHCpan-4.1 EL is employed for classification (details about classification can be found in Supplementary data). We removed the raw neoantigens identified as nonbinders from the data to make the results more realistic since the trigger for cross-immunization assumes raw neoantigens bind to HLA molecules. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5a, when wild-type peptides are binders and raw neoantigens are weak binders, Naso-optimized neoantigens exhibit significantly superior binding affinity (predicted by NetMHCpan-4.1 BA, MixMHCpred-3.0, and MHCflurry-2.0 BA) and presentation (predicted by NetMHCpan-4.1 EL, MHCflurry-2.0 EL, and BigMHC EL) compared to raw neoantigens. Furthermore, Naso achieves better immunogenicity performance (predicted by IEDB Immunogenicity tool and NeoaPred model) than other optimization methods. A similar trend is observed when wild-type peptides are binders and raw neoantigens are strong binders (Supplementary Fig. S5b), indicating the robust performance of our model in cases where both wild-type peptides and raw neoantigens show HLA binding. When wild-type peptides are nonbinders, Naso-optimized neoantigens still show significantly improved binding affinity and presentation relative to raw neoantigens (Supplementary Fig. S5c and d). In terms of immunogenicity, Naso performs comparably or better than other methods. The slightly weaker immunogenicity performance may be attributed to the limited amount of data (92 instances, representing ~4% of the total dataset), where evaluation results are more susceptible to randomness. Additionally, Naso mutants yields improved binding stability (predicted by NetMHCstabpan) compared to raw neoantigens across all four data categories (Supplementary Fig. S6). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Naso robustly enhances the capacity to elicit immune responses across diverse and challenging immunological contexts.

Discussion

Neoepitope-based immunotherapy has been seen as a very promising cancer treatment, in which the neoantigens bind with HLA and then are presented to and recognized by T cells to trigger effective immune responses [11]. As a result, neoantigen vaccine design is a crucial area in the treatment of patients with cancers. However, only 1%–2% of neoepitopes can be recognized by naturally occurring T cells [46, 47], making it challenging to select a set of neoantigen candidates for the production of vaccines. To find more appropriate neoantigens, there are some methods for peptide mutation, such as Aomp, Pepppo, and Mobius. However, these methods mainly concern the affinity to HLA alleles, which is only one step of the immune response. Here, we proposed a Naso method for neoantigen optimization based on simulated annealing to search for mutant neoantigens with a higher probability of triggering cross-immunization. This task has not yet been achieved. Our method is not restricted by HLA-I alleles or peptide length. The mutant peptides by Naso have a higher probability of being recognized by T cells and high homology with the source peptides, helping to trigger cross-immunization. We compared Naso with three state-of-the-art methods on the data collected from three datasets, and Naso demonstrates stronger immunogenicity than other methods. Moreover, it performs well in critical immunological features including binding affinity and presentation. Molecular dynamics further reveals its therapeutic potential for cancer immunotherapy.

This algorithm tailored for neoantigen mutation will have several critical applications. As the mutated peptides by Naso have a higher probability of triggering cross-immunization, they can be used as neoantigen candidates with higher confidence, reducing the number of peptides that need to be synthesized and tested, thus accelerating the process of cancer vaccine design. Moreover, Naso can optimize peptides for desired features, such as the stability of peptide-HLA complexes, combined with other prediction algorithms.

However, Naso has some limitations. For example, some neoantigen vaccines may not be effective in patients lacking the corresponding HLA-I alleles [5]. Currently, Naso is only evaluated using the same HLA-I allele for each peptide, and future efforts could focus on identifying mutant neoantigens with higher affinity to a broader set of HLA alleles. Additionally, Naso is designed for HLA-I alleles and does not yet support HLA-II alleles, so expanding its coverage to include HLA-II could significantly enhance its applicability. It is also important to note that the current evaluation relies primarily on in silico predictions. While this approach provides valuable insights, future work will involve real-world experimental case studies to further validate and substantiate the biomedical relevance of the results. In summary, this study represents an attempt to introduce a machine learning-based Naso in the field of automated neoantigen mutation. It has the potential to be applied in heteroclitic neoantigen–based vaccine design for eliciting cross-immunization against cancer cells while sparing normal cells, thereby benefiting clinical research.
