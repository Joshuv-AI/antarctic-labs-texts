Design and development of a 1-bit dual-mode metasurface for sub-6 GHz wireless communication systems
Authors: Inam Ullah, Fahad Ahmed, Ahsaan Gul Hassan, Irfan Mehmood, Haq Nawaz, Nosherwan Shoaib, Sultan Shoaib, Sitan Xie, Humayun Shahid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents design and development of 1-bit dual-mode metasurface for sub-6 GHz wireless communication systems. The design consists of four distinct configurations: two for transmission and two for reflection mode. Diode-based phase switching capability was initially considered in simulations, however the final prototype was fabricated with fixed stripline phase configuration. These striplines replicate diode ON/OFF states to validate the phase-switching logic to minimize the cost and design complexity. The proposed unit cell works effectively in both transmissive and reflective modes while maintaining a phase shift of 180° ± 20° throughout its operational bandwidth. Despite its multi-layer structure and dual-mode operation, the proposed unit cell has low insertion loss of around 0.4 dB and 0.21 dB in transmissive and reflective mode, respectively at the central frequency. The unit cell achieves 3 dB fractional bandwidth of 16% in transmissive mode and 12% in reflective mode. A 10 × 10 elements array simulation has been carried out to demonstrate pre-defined beam steering capabilities with ± 60° in both transmissive and reflective modes providing coverage of 240° out of complete 360°. The gain of the source antenna is enhanced by more than 6 dB across the operational bandwidth, with a fluctuation of 3 dB. The performance of the proposed metasurface is successfully validated through both numerical simulations and experimental results, with a good agreement observed in the overall radiation pattern and characteristics.Subject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

In recent years, dual-mode metasurfaces have attracted significant attention due to their ability to manipulate EM wave propagation and control beam direction. As a result, these surfaces have been considered as an essential component of the next generation wireless communication systems1–3. A metasurface structure consists of a 2-D array of subwavelength meta-atoms, or unit cells, which are integrated with tunable components such as diodes or switches. By adjusting the bias states of these tuning elements, the amplitude, phase, and polarization of incident EM waves can be dynamically adjusted without conventional phase arrays4,5. Most conventional metasurfaces are unidirectional, operating in either transmissive or reflective mode. However, some designs achieve dual-mode operation, supporting both transmission and reflection, which enables full-space resource utilization6,7. It consumes low power and has minimal hardware complexity which makes it a cost-effective and efficient solution for smart wireless environment8. With this programmable nature, a type of metasurface called nearly passive metasurface (NPM), offer a flexible and scalable solution for future wireless communication9.

NPM alters wireless communication by offering flexibility to real time EM environment. It enhances the coverage area and channel capacity in dense urban environments and indoor communication environment, where EM waves experience interference due to obstacles which causes poor signal performance10,11. These surfaces are ideal for dynamic beam scanning and directional control, which help to reduce signal loss and optimize coverage area over dense environment, such as smart cities12,13. It can also be used in advanced multi-path communications by allowing precise control over both transmission and reflection, which enable high-efficiency beamforming and multi-user access support14. For future 5G and 6G networks, these metasurfaces helps to manage interference and improve link reliability in highly dense environments, while supporting multi-beam configurations and enhanced spectral efficiency15. Figure 1 illustrates metasurface applications with fixed phase configuration (striplines) and potential dynamic configuration (with diodes). Additionally, these surfaces enable seamless integration with emerging technologies like augmented reality and internet of things (IoT), ensuring reliable and efficient connections even in complex environments16,17.

Previous designs in dual-mode metasurfaces include an intelligent programmable Omni-metasurface which integrates both transmission and reflection modes, but this design has multiple stacked layers and open-air gap between two layers which make it complex in nature18,19. In20,21 five diodes per unit cell were used which face issues of complex control circuitry, large aperture and limited efficiency. Two notable designs, a compact mmWave reflective NPM22 and a varactor-based K-band dual-polarized surface23, have been proposed. However, these designs exhibit limitations, notably insufficient scanning characterization and a narrow phase-stability range, respectively. In24 a dynamic reflection–transmission surface is presented, which constrains aperture efficiency, bandwidth, and gain enhancement. Better performance is reported in25 and26, albeit with unidirectional operation. In contrast, the works in27–29 demonstrate dual-mode operation at the expense of performance parameter, and structural compactness.

To address these limitations, a passive dual-mode 1-bit metasurface for sub-6 GHz applications is proposed. Specifically, the proposed design addresses the dual-mode coverage limitation of unidirectional designs25,26, the structural complexity of existing full-space designs18–21, and the limited scanning range of previous dual-mode works27–29. The proposed unit cell has the capability of four distinct design configurations: two for transmission and two for reflection mode. This design achieves ±60° pre-defined beam direction in both modes (transmission and reflection). The unit cell Fractional bandwidth of 16% and 12% (transmission and reflection mode respectively), and gain enhancement of more than 6 dB to the incident signal is achieved. The proposed design presents a novel metasurface that successfully integrates dual-mode operation, compact geometry, and low structural complexity. This integration advances the state of the art and establishes a versatile platform for realizing future intelligent wireless environments.

A detailed research methodology was employed to develop the proposed metasurface. Initial design simulations were carried out using diode models (Skyworks SMP 1340-079LF) ON/OFF circuitry to explore dynamic switching capabilities, however the final prototype was fabricated using striplines instead of diodes to implement a fixed-phase configuration. These striplines replicate actual diode circuitry for ON/OFF states to validate the phase control with limited resources. Thus, the configurations states described in this paper are determined at the time of fabrication and cannot be changed during operation. However, while using diodes and its biasing circuitry, it would transform this design from fixed reflecting surface to bidirectional nearly passive metasurface (Figs. 2, 3).

Unit cell design and geometric configuration

In this section, the geometric configuration and operating principle of the proposed unit cell is presented. It consists of a modified patch on top of the upper substrate and a patch on the bottom of the lower substrate, which is slightly shifted from the center. These substrates are separated by a ground layer with a standard thickness of 0.035 mm. This design employs a diode-based phase control mechanism to realize 1-bit operation in both transmission and reflection modes. These diodes circuitry reverse the current distribution between the top and bottom patches, thereby generating binary phase states of 0° and 180°. Both the patches are fabricated on the same F4BM substrates (εr = 2.55, tan δ = 0.0015). The thickness of each substrate is 2 mm and it is bonded using adhesive material Rogers 4450F (εr = 3.52, tan δ = 0.004) with a thickness of 0.1 mm. The bottom patch of lower substrate is connected with ground plane through metallic via of diameter 1 mm. The unit cell footprint is 30 × 30 mm2, which corresponds to 0.5λ × 0.5λ at 5 GHz. The unit cell operates under linear polarization. Throughout all simulations, a linearly polarized plane wave with normal incidence is assumed, corresponding to transverse magnetic (TM) mode excitation via Floquet port setup. Initial design simulations were carried out using ON/OFF circuitry from the datasheet of Skyworks SMP 1340-079LF diode. The ON states were represented by series RL circuitry (R=0.85 Ω, L=0.7nH) while the OFF state used series LC circuitry (L=0.7nH, C=0.18 pF). The magnitude and phase responses presented in Figs. 4 and 5 were obtained using these diode equivalent circuit models. Thus, changing surface impedance Z_s using diodes RL circuitry or stripline placement, the unit cell acts as a reflective surface while generating a phase shift of 180° ± 20°. The desired phase shift is achieved by ON/OFF states of diodes circuitry or adjusting the striplines which alter the effective electrical length of the reflective patch, that control the current flow between the patches as shown in Fig. 3. As a result, a precise control over the reflection phase while maintaining minimal insertion loss has been achieved. The simulated magnitude and phase response of the unit cell in reflective mode is shown in Fig. 4. The shaded area, starting from 4.7 to 5.25 GHz is clearly representing that the phase difference of 180°±20° is achieved and the reflection loss is less than 0.21 dB, resulting a fractional bandwidth of 12% at 5 GHz central frequency. In conventional 1-bit metasurface configurations, the unit cell typically provides a fixed 180° phase shift at the center frequency. In contrast, the proposed design exhibits a non-monotonic phase response: when both diodes are ON or OFF/ striplines are present or removed, effectively leaving the surface unloaded, the phase difference initially increases and subsequently decreases. The geometry is optimized to achieve a 200° phase shift at the center frequency. Additionally, two distinct 180° phase-shift points are realized at 4.8 GHz and 5.2 GHz. It is worth noting that the fractional bandwidth in the reflective mode is comparatively narrow, as the 0°/180° phase states are achieved primarily through perturbation. It can be enhanced by using thicker substrate or multi-stacked substrate or optimizing space between elements in sub-wavelength31. The angular stability of the unit cell in reflective mode has been analyzed for incidence angles up to ±45° from normal incidence, and the results are presented in Fig. 4b. The phase difference remains within 180° ± 20° across all analyzed angles; However, the reflection magnitude shows a gradual increase in loss of approximately 1.5 dB at ±45° compared to normal incidence. This behavior is physically expected: as the incidence angle increases, the electrical path length through the dielectric substrate increases, raising the effective permittivity seen by the wave and consequently increasing ohmic and mismatch losses. Despite this, the reflection performance at oblique angles remains within acceptable limits for practical deployment, where extreme incidence angles are rarely encountered. The response at negative incidence angles is symmetric, so only positive angles (15°, 30°, and 45°) are plotted in Fig. 4b to keep the figures clean and readable32.

Results and discussion

For 10x10 element array simulations, the feed was modeled as a point source with cosⁿ(θ) directivity, which creates a spherical wavefront at the aperture plane. The amplitude and phase distribution of this wavefront at each element position were incorporated into the array factor calculations. Experimental measurements were conducted in the far-field region. For the 300 × 300 mm2 array, the Fraunhofer distance is calculated as 2D2/λ ≈ 3.0 m. The receiving antenna was positioned at 3.2 m from the array, ensuring far-field conditions34.

The far-field results for both transmissive and reflective modes are shown in Figs. 7, 8, respectively. It is clear from the results that precise pre-defined phase distribution beam steering is achieved in the pre-defined scanning range of −60° to +60° in steps of 10° in both transmissive and reflective modes, providing an overall angular coverage of nearly 240° out of the full 360°. The simulated gain at 5 GHz is 20.2 dBi and 21.5 dBi, and the aperture efficiency is 45% and 33% in transmissive and reflective modes, respectively. Frequency sweep analysis shown in Fig. 9 reveals stable gain and aperture efficiency across the operational bandwidth, and the sidelobe level is less than -10 dB in both transmissive and reflective modes. As the scanning angle increases, the sidelobe level increases and the gain decreases. The transmissive mode gain is less than reflective mode gain because of array insertion losses. Furthermore, the main lobe follows the proposed pre-defined steering directions consistently, which confirms the effectiveness of the designed pre-defined phase distribution and uniform performance of the array. Beam distortions of 1-2° are observed at larger scan angles, mainly due to aperture truncation and edge effects. It is important to note that the beam steering results shown in Figs. 7, 8 represent simulated performance for different phase configurations. Each configuration is a fixed design determined at fabrication; dynamic beam scanning is not possible with the stripline-based passive approach. However, multiple metasurface units with different phase configurations could be fabricated for various fixed beam directions (Fig. 10).

Three separate measurements were taken: one in open air, one with a simple metallic plate, and one with the proposed reflective metasurface positioned in front of the horn antennas, as shown in Fig. 11. The measured values were used in Eqs. (15–17), and the reflection coefficient was calibrated. Metallic plate was purely used for calibration purposes. The measured results show scanning angle deviation of approximately 3–4° and gain reduction of ≈ 3 dB. The observed deviation in scanning angle and gain reduction is instead attributed to two practical factors, first one is Feed Illumination Mismatch: In the array simulations, the feed was modeled using an ideal symmetric cosⁿ(θ) pattern, which provides a half-power beamwidth (HPBW) of approximately 36° in both the E-plane and H-plane. This near-symmetric illumination results in a relatively uniform amplitude taper across the 300 × 300 mm2 aperture with an estimated edge taper of approximately − 3 dB. In the actual measurements, a standard gain wideband horn antenna was used. The horn exhibits an E-plane HPBW of approximately 30° and an H-plane HPBW of approximately 40°, which introduces asymmetric and non-uniform aperture illumination compared to the idealized simulation model. This difference reduces the effective aperture efficiency and introduces additional spillover losses, with an estimated combined feed-related gain reduction of approximately 1.5 to 2.0 dB. The second factor is Fabrication and Assembly Tolerances: The fabricated prototype consists of multi-layer F4BM substrates bonded with Rogers 4450F adhesive. Practical fabrication tolerances introduce the following deviations from the simulated ideal structure, Copper etching tolerance of ± 0.1 mm in both dimensions, which introduces patch dimension variations of approximately ± 1.3% and shifts the effective resonant frequency of individual elements by roughly ± 25 MHz, Substrate dielectric constant variation of ± 0.05 for the F4BM material (nominal εr = 2.55), introducing per-element phase perturbations of approximately ± 3° and Inter-layer bonding alignment tolerance of ± 0.15 mm using Rogers 4450F, which introduces additional path length variations. When combined across all 100 elements, these random phase perturbations produce a statistical gain reduction of approximately 0.5 to 1.5 dB, estimated using the standard random phase error formula for phased arrays: ΔG ≈ − 4.34 × σ2 (dB), where σ2 is the mean-square phase error in radians. Together, these two factors account for approximately 2.5 to 3.5 dB of the observed gain reduction, which is consistent with the measured difference of approximately 3 dB. However, the measured results remain consistent and are validated by the results shown in Fig. 12. And the proposed design while using diodes has more flexible wave manipulation and better performance in the term of scanning angle, 3-dB fractional bandwidth, and peak gain and aperture efficiencies in both transmissive and reflective modes. It is worth noting that in Fig. 12, the simulated pattern shows a wider main lobe than the measured pattern despite having higher gain, which may appear contradictory. This behavior arises from the fundamental difference in aperture illumination between the two cases. In simulations, the ideal cosⁿ(θ) feed model provides near-uniform amplitude illumination across the full 300 × 300 mm2 aperture, maximizing gain but producing a wider main lobe because the uniform amplitude weighting maximizes the Fourier spread. In contrast, the actual horn antenna used during measurements applies a strongly tapered and asymmetric amplitude distribution across the aperture due to its non-uniform beam pattern (E-plane HPBW ≈ 30°, H-plane HPBW ≈ 40°). This amplitude tapering reduces the effective aperture area and therefore reduces the measured gain, while simultaneously concentrating the high-amplitude contributions toward the aperture center an effect equivalent to amplitude tapering in phased array design, which narrows the apparent main lobe at the cost of reduced gain. Consequently, both the lower gain and the narrower beamwidth of the measured pattern are physically consistent and expected.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the design and development of a dual-mode metasurface operating at sub-6 GHz frequencies. The proposed design employs a compact sandwiched patch-ground-patch structure with diodes-based phase control for dynamic configuration and stripline-based phase control for fixed configuration, providing binary phase distribution (0°/180°) in both transmissive and reflective modes. The unit cell achieves 3-dB fractional bandwidths of 16% and 12% in transmissive and reflective modes, respectively. The transmission and reflection losses are measured as 0.45 dB and 0.21 dB, respectively, ensuring low-power operation and efficient signal manipulation. Array simulations of 10×10 element confirm pre-defined beam direction spanning ±60° from broadside in both modes providing an angular coverage of approximately 240° out of a full 360°. The system achieves a gain enhancement of more than 6 dB in both modes. The fabricated prototype implements fixed stripline configuration for passive operation. However, the design framework naturally accommodates PIN diodes for future dynamic reconfiguration and advanced beam steering applications.
