Revision Hip Arthroplasty with a Single-Taper Cementless Conical Revision Stem: Clinical and Radiographic Assessment of 57 Implants
Authors: Stefano Lucchini, Andrea Burla, Andrea Poletto, Francesco Castagnini, Simone Gerardi, Francesco Traina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal stem design for revision hip arthroplasties is still controversial. A retrospective cohort study of revision hip arthroplasties performed using a single-taper cementless conical stem was designed, aiming to assess clinical outcomes, complications and survival rates, and radiological osseointegration, at a minimum follow-up of 2 years.MethodsA retrospective observational cohort study was conducted on 57 femoral revision arthroplasties performed between 2004 and 2018 using a cementless monoblock conical tapered stem, with a minimum 2-year follow-up. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Harris Hip Score (HHS). Radiographic evaluations included stem alignment, bone stock remodeling across Gruen zones, and osseointegration graded using 2 systems: Global Radiographic Score (a 5-scale index combining bone quality and osseointegration) and the Engh criteria (fixation classified as bone ingrowth, stable fibrous fixation, or unstable fixation). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was also performed.ResultsAt a mean follow-up of 6.0 ± 3.9 years, the mean HHS was 86.02 ± 9.73. The cumulative stem survival rate was 96.6% at 60 months and 120 months (implant survival: 96.6% and 89.7%, respectively). Bone stock improved across all Gruen zones, with significant increases in zones 1, 3, and 5. No radiolucent lines were found in 59.6% of proximal femurs and 93.0% of distal femurs. More than half of the stems (54.4%) achieved the maximum Global Radiographic Score (20 / 20), and 84.2% demonstrated bone ingrowth according to the Engh criteria.ConclusionsThe single-taper conical revision stem provided good clinical outcomes and reliable survival rates (96.6%) at 6 and 10 years, with 93% of cases exhibiting very good stem stability.

Femoral component revision after failed total hip arthroplasty (THA) aims to exchange the stem while providing adequate reconstruction of the proximal biomechanics, valid primary stability, secondary stabilization, and bone stock regeneration.12) In femoral component revisions, different strategies with different implant choices may be adopted, according to the residual bone stock, the femoral morphology, and the possible modifying factors (plugs, remodeling, stenosis, discontinuity, osteolysis, etc.).2) Cemented stems were adopted in cases of minimal metaphyseal bone loss or for the cement-in-cement technique.1) Cementless long stems, with cylindrical and conical tapered designs, were generally favored due to the distal bony anchorage bypassing the proximal bone defect, with modular options available to restore hip biomechanics more closely.23) To date, conical tapered stems are generally preferred even in complex revision settings when a viable isthmus is present, due to long-term survival rates, good signs of osseointegration, and bone regeneration at midterm.123) The main drawbacks of conical tapered stems are stem subsidence, perioperative fractures, and anterior cortex perforation.45) However, there is a substantial lack of radiological evaluation of conical tapered revision stems in terms of secondary stability, osseointegration, bone regeneration, and global radiographic performances at mid- to long-term follow-up.45)

Thus, a retrospective cohort study of revision hip arthroplasties performed with single-taper cementless conical stems at a minimum follow-up of 2 years was designed. We aimed to assess (1) clinical outcomes, (2) complications and survival rates, and (3) radiological osseointegration features of this stem for femoral component revisions in THAs, using a detailed assessment system. We hypothesized that a single-taper conical tapered stem for femoral component revisions could provide reliable and stable mid- to long-term outcomes (survival rate > 95% at 6–10 years), with no concerns regarding thigh pain, achieving a stable and effective osseointegration with bone stock regeneration in at least 95% of the cases.45)

METHODS

All the procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of IRCCS Istituto Ortopedico Rizzoli (IRB No. CE-AVEC 349/2021/Oss/IOR). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

A retrospective cohort of patients treated in our department for revision of failed THA between 2004 and 2018 using a specific monoblock (single taper) conical stem was selected. The inclusion criteria were as follows: femoral component revisions with a single-taper cementless conical stem (Wagner SL Revision stem, Zimmer: the index stem), adherence to annual clinical and radiographic follow-ups, and a minimum follow-up of 2 years. For every case, a series of standard pelvis radiographs soon after revision (postoperative baseline control) and at a minimum follow-up of 2 years was required. Exclusion criteria were as follows: primary THA with the index stem, femoral component revisions with stems other than the index one, and inadequate follow-up or absence of a complete medical chart.

Patient Cohort

Out of 368 femoral component revisions, 172 were performed using the index stem (46.7%). One hundred fifteen cases were excluded due to inadequate follow-up (38 cases, 10.3%) or incomplete chart records (77 cases, 20.9%). Fifty-seven patients (57 hips) were finally enrolled (15.5%). Demographics are described in Table 1. Out of 57 revisions, 22 hips (38.6%) were femoral and acetabular revisions, whereas 35 hips (61.4%) were only femoral stem revisions. Femoral revisions were performed for the following reasons: 38 cases (66.7%), aseptic loosening of the previous implant; 3 cases (5.3%), second-stage procedure after spacer implantation in periprosthetic infection; 14 cases (24.6%), periprosthetic fracture; 1 case (1.8%), mechanical failure (breakage) of the femoral stem; and 1 case (1.8%), metallosis.

Implant Features and Surgical Technique

Wagner SL Revision stem is a single-taper cementless conical stem with a grit-blasted surface covered in titanium alloy and 8 longitudinal ribs, ranging in size.6) Four stem lengths are available (190 mm, 225 mm, 265 mm, and 305 mm, all with a caput-collum-diaphyseal angle of 135°).6) Femoral component revisions which could not be addressed with primary stems (cemented or cementless) and with a supportive isthmus (up to Paprosky IIIB included) were treated with this stem.456) Dual-taper modular implants were avoided due to specific concerns regarding junctional failure in relatively younger (69 years) and heavier (77 kg) patients: the implant selection also reflected the surgeon’s long-standing familiarity with this stem design.456) A preoperative radiological planning with radiographs and computed tomography was performed. All the surgeries were performed with the patients in the supine decubitus position, using a lateral approach (Table 1).

Clinical Evaluations

Patients were clinically assessed every year after the revision, using Harris Hip Score (HHS), until the last follow-up.4)

Complications and Survival Rates

Perioperative and postoperative complications were recorded. Implant failure occurred when a single component was re-revised for any reason. Reasons for re-revisions were tracked.

Radiographic Assessment

Standard anteroposterior pelvis x-rays were performed in the preoperative setting, soon after surgery and at the latest follow-up. All the radiographic measurements were taken by a single surgeon specialized in hip surgery (SL) after an interrater reliability test. The intraobserver reliability study was conducted on the radiographic parameters of 40 x-rays from the group of 57 patients by carrying out 2 evaluations 4 weeks apart.

Femoral component positioning was assessed on the immediate postoperative radiographs, after an appropriate magnification, using conventional measurement techniques: femoral offset, varus-valgus configuration, and leg length discrepancy were assessed.7) The preoperative bone loss was assessed using Paprosky classification.2) A dedicated radiographic analysis was adopted, which focused on (1) the bone stock remodeling, (2) osseointegration and secondary implant stability, (3) the Global Radiographic Score, based on the previous 2 scoring systems, and (4) the Engh Score evaluation of femoral stem osseointegration and stability.89) Stem subsidence was also assessed using the “Engh modified method” described by Zhang et al.,3) by comparing an immediate postoperative x-ray and an x-ray obtained at the last follow-up.

Immediate Postoperative and Secondary Bone Stock Remodeling and Cortical Lesions

For each case, an initial study of postoperative bone stock was conducted dividing the femur into Gruen zones (zone 7, often absent in revision surgery, was assessed together with zone 6).10) Cortical thickness modifications, bone density changes, and defects of the cortical bone were evaluated for each zone and calculated as a comparison between immediate postoperative and last available follow-up.8) A 4-stage classification was adopted, +4, +2, 0, and –2, from no lesions (no decrease in bone density) to major bone loss, and was performed for every Gruen zone.810)

Osseointegration and Secondary Stability of the Implants

Any radiolucent line at the stem—bone interface was considered and quantified (apart from external and superior profiles of the prosthetic shoulder).810) An anteroposterior plain x-ray was evaluated at the last available follow-up, on standard and negative views. Periprosthetic femoral bone was divided into 2 main zones: proximal femur (Gruen zones 1, 2, and 6) and distal femur (Gruen zones 3 and 5).810) Bone remodeling was quantified using a 5-stage classification (Table 2).8)

The Global Radiographic Score was calculated (Table 3).8) The Global Radiographic Score is a composite numerical system designed to evaluate femoral cementless revision stems in THA. It integrates 2 validated radiological indices: the Secondary Bone Stock score, which assesses cortical bone quality across all Gruen zones, and the Osseointegration–Secondary Stability score, which quantifies the extent and location of radiolucent lines. Both components are scored on a 10-point scale and equally weighted, yielding a final score out of 20. The Global Radiographic Score demonstrated high intra- and interobserver reliability and showed strong correlation with clinical outcomes, supporting its utility for objective radiographic assessment in revisions.8)

Finally, Engh criteria of osseointegration and stability were evaluated.9) The Engh classification was used to evaluate femoral stem fixation based on radiographic criteria. It assigns a numerical score ranging from –10 to +24, with points attributed to features such as reactive lines, calcar atrophy, pedestal formation, and spot welds. A score of ≥ 10 indicates bone ingrowth, 0 to 9 suggests stable fibrous fixation and < 0 denotes unstable fixation. This system enables standardized assessment of osseointegration and mechanical stability in cementless femoral components.9)

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2 (IBM Corp.). Quantitative variables are presented as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was performed to assess the intra- and interobserver reliability. Kaplan-Meier survivorship analysis was used to estimate the surviving femoral stems, using different endpoints (any component revisions and stem revisions). As the Shapiro-Wilk test proved a non-normal distribution of the data regarding bone stock and radiographic scores, the analysis of the increase in bone stock was carried out using a one-way Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the correlation between postoperative stem subsidence and radiographic scores was assessed by Spearman’s test for non-parametric data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which confirmed non-normal distributions for Engh Score (p = 0.019), Global Score (p = 0.007), Weighted Score (p = 0.031), and Paprosky Score (p = 0.044). The correlation between clinical outcomes (HHS) and radiographic scores (Global Radiographic Score, Engh) was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (r). Significance level was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

A mean follow-up time of 6.0 ± 3.9 years (range, 2–16 years) was obtained. Intraobserver reliability proved good, obtaining an ICC of 0.72 (95% CI, 0.59–0.85). Interobserver reliability was also found to be acceptable, with an ICC of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.55–0.83).

Clinical Outcomes and Implant Survival Rates

At the last follow-up, the mean HHS was 86.02 ± 9.73 points (range, 30–95). The preoperative HHS could not be collected due to several catastrophic reasons for revision in 26.31%. No intra- or postoperative fractures occurred. Two cases of postoperative dislocation (3.5%) occurred within 2 months: 1 was successfully treated with closed reduction followed by a 30-day immobilization using a hip spica cast (no dislocation recurrence), and the other suffered multiple chronic dislocations that required multiple closed reductions. The patient was unwilling to undergo a further re-revision procedure.

Two cases of early superficial infection (3.5%) occurred (persistent wound drainage and dehiscence in the first 30 days after revision), requiring surgical debridement with implant retention and subsequent antibiotic therapy. Both the patients were obese with a Charlson score graded 3 (1 periprosthetic fracture and 1 aseptic loosening). One late deep infection (1.8%) occurred at 60 months and led to implant removal. The pre-revision diagnosis leading to stem reimplantation was periprosthetic fracture. The diagnosis was based on draining fistula, positive total-body bone scintigraphy, serum C-reactive protein persistent elevation, and synovial fluid aspiration (> 90% of polymorphonucleates), with intraoperative cultures yielding Staphylococcus epidermidis. Postoperative antibiotic therapy included ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and gentamicin.

Two stem failures requiring re-revisions occurred at 60 and 182 months after surgery, respectively, for periprosthetic hip joint infection and aseptic loosening. The patients underwent prosthesis explantation followed by targeted antibiotic therapy with teicoplanin and rifampicin. The cumulative survival of the stem was 96.6% (95% CI, 93.2%–99.9%) at 60 months and at 120 months (last follow-up with at least 20% of the implants at risk) (Fig. 1). Re-revision due to any causes (endpoint: any single component removal/exchange) was necessary for 4 patients, providing a cumulative survival of the implant of 96.6% (95% CI, 93.2%–99.9%) at 60 months and 89.7% (95% CI, 82.3%–97.1%) at 120 months.

Post-revision Component Positioning

The mean postoperative offset was 47.8 ± 7.1 mm (range, 35–71 mm). Coronal stem configuration was neutral in 91.2% (52 stems): outliers showed a varus deviation always < 5°. The leg length discrepancy was 9.1 ± 8.6 mm (range, 0–25 mm).

Bone Stock Remodeling

The preoperative bone loss was severe in a small number of cases: 4 hips (6.8%) with Paprosky grade 3A and 3 hips (5.1%) with Paprosky grade 3B. Most of the cases (31 hips [52.5%] with Paprosky grade 1 and 21 patients [35.6%] with Paprosky grade 2) had a low-grade bone loss. At the last follow-up, the highest rank of bone stock remodeling (+4) was achieved in 49.1% (28 hips) in zone 1, 49.1% (28 hips) in zone 2, 77.2% (44 hips) in zone 3, 78.9% (45 hips) in zone 6, and 78.9% (45 hips) in zone 5. Apart from 2 cases in zone 3 (3.5%), no revision implants had a major bone loss in any area. The analysis of postoperative bone stock remodeling revealed bone stock increase in each Gruen zone, from the immediate postoperative to the last follow-up. The increase was statistically significant only in zones 1, 3, and 5 (Table 4).

Osseointegration and Stability

No radiolucent lines were found in 34 hips (59.6%) in the proximal femur and in 53 hips (93.0%) in the distal femur. Radiolucent lines with less than 50% of stem extension were noted in 18 hips (31.6%) in the proximal femur and in 3 hips (5.3%) in the distal femur. Radiolucent lines with more than 50% of stem extension were noted in 5 hips (8.8%) in the proximal femur and in 1 hip (1.8%) in the distal femur. Regarding bone remodeling, 33 hips (57.9%) were classified as stage 0, 10 hips (17.5%) stage 1, 6 hips (10.5%) stage 2, 4 hips (7.0%) stage 3, and 4 hips (7.0%) stage 4 (Table 2) (Fig. 2). Osseointegration and stability of the implants were very good: 31 cases (54.4%) scored 20 / 20 points at Global Score.

The secondary stability was valid in most of the cases: 34 femurs (59.6%) achieved class 1A, 16 femurs (28.0%) class 1B, 1 femur (1.8%) class 2A, 2 femurs (3.4%) class 2B, 1 femur (1.8%) class 3A, 2 femurs (3.4%) class 3B, and 1 femur (1.8%) class 3C (Table 3) (Fig. 3). Engh Score also proved to be good, with a median of 21.5 points (IQR, 18.7–24) (Table 5). Forty-eight stems (84.2%) achieved a very good outcome (> 10 points). The functional outcome (HHS) showed a moderate correlation with both the Global Radiographic Score (r = 0.56) and the Engh Score (r = 0.54), whereas both radiographic scores were strongly correlated (r = 0.82).

Only 5 cases (8.8%) showed no subsidence at the last follow-up. The mean subsidence was 2.22 mm (95% CI, 0.97–3.47 mm; range, 0–4.9 mm). No statistically significant correlation was found between stem subsidence and Engh Score (p = 0.37) and Global Score (p = 0.35). Stem subsidence was not correlated to preoperative bone loss (p = 0.15).

DISCUSSION

The single-taper conical revision stem for femoral component revision provided reliable and stable midterm outcomes. More than 96% of the implants survived at 5 years and only 1 aseptic loosening occurred (1.8%). The mean clinical score was good (HHS, 86.02 ± 9.73). The radiographic scores were optimal, even if more than 10% of the cases had a high-grade preoperative bone loss. The hypothesis was confirmed.

This article has many limitations. Most of them are related to the retrospective design of the study, the lack of control groups, a limited population, and a midterm evaluation. Only a single type of stem was included. Moreover, there is a lack of preoperative clinical assessment, due to the inclusion of catastrophic and unexpected failures (femoral neck breakage and periprosthetic fractures, impacting 26.31% of the cases). Many types of failures and bone loss were also included, resulting in an inhomogeneous cohort. On the other hand, a capillary postoperative radiographic assessment was conducted, evaluating bone remodeling, bone regeneration (remodeling), and osseointegration.

The clinical outcomes of this case series achieved similar results in comparison to the available literature.411) Regis et al.11) achieved an HHS of 75 points at 13 years, whereas the clinical outcomes of Baktır et al.12) were as high as 88 points at 14.4 years: the present case series achieved an HHS of 86 points at 6 years, similar to Zhang et al.3) In a systematic review of the same tapered stem, 87% of the patients achieved good to excellent outcomes, a rate that favorably compares to the present case series.4) In the literature and in our experience, the index stem was not correlated to relevant percentages of thigh pain.4)

The 5-year survival rate of this case series was 96.6% (Fig. 1). This percentage was similar to the pooled survival rate of 92%–98.5% at more than 8 years described in a dedicated meta-analysis.45) Moreover, this percentage was far higher than the general survival rates of aseptic and septic revisions regardless of the stem usage (86.3% and 85.9% at 7 years).13) Among complication rates and reasons for re-revisions, no periprosthetic fracture occurred in this case series, compared to 1.5% of the systematic review and meta-analysis.5) Similarly, a lower dislocation rate (3.5% vs. 5.7%) was detected.5) On the contrary, there was a higher incidence of infection (3.8% vs. 2.4%).5) A full comparison with the available literature is not possible due to heterogeneous data collection across studies. However, the incidence reported in our study likely reflects the complexity of our population: all 3 infections occurred in patients with recognized risk factors (Charlson score ≥ 3, obesity, or revision for periprosthetic fracture), indicating that infectious events clustered in clinically high-risk individuals.

Radiographic results showed that the tapered revision stem increased the postoperative bone stock at midterms in Gruen zones 1, 3, and 5 in the trochanteric region and in the diaphyseal zone. More than 75% of the cases had an optimal bone regeneration in the diaphyseal area. The bone stock increase in the diaphyseal region was quite predictable for a diaphyseal-engaging stem: on the other hand, bone stock increase in the trochanteric region was less likely, mostly considering that single-taper conical tapered stem led to mild stress shielding in 20% of the cases.1415) The findings about bone stock evaluation were superior to most of the other case series: however, the different reasons for revision and the different preoperative bone loss may impair any proper comparison among case series.4) Using a quantitative analysis (ratio of bone width to the outside diameter), Baktır et al.12) and Gutiérrez Del Alamo et al.16) found that more than 65% of the cases had substantial bone regeneration at the final follow-up, especially in cases of minor bone loss. Regis et al.11) noticed a little lower outcome, 63.9%, whereas Böhm and Bischel17) found that 88% of the cases had at least a minimal bone regeneration at a qualitative evaluation. The moderate correlation observed between functional and radiographic outcomes suggests that these parameters capture different yet complementary aspects of revision success. As the single-taper conical stem achieves stable fixation through diaphyseal anchorage, proximal remodeling is less determinant for clinical recovery. Accordingly, functional scores (HHS) are also influenced by patient-related factors (soft-tissue competence and general health condition), not fully reflected in radiographic indices.

An almost ubiquitous stem subsidence was noted in the present case series, with a mean value of 2 mm: no correlation with preoperative bone loss and postoperative radiographic osseointegration was recorded. This finding was consistent with Sandiford et al.,18) but was in contrast to most of the case series, reporting larger stem subsidence, sporadically leading to re-revisions or complications.417)

In the distal femur, where the stem achieves fixation, no radiolucent lines were found in 93% of the cases. In 5.3% of the cases, radiolucent lines involved less than 50% of the stem extension, and only in 1 case did the line extend for more than 50% of the stem. In the other case series, radiolucent lines were detected in 0%–8.3% of the cases, comparable to our results.111218) Gutiérrez Del Alamo et al.16) noticed radiolucent lines only in Gruen zones 1 and 2 in 13.9% of the cases, not dissimilar to Hernández-Mateo et al.,19) reporting radiolucency in zones 1,7,10, and 14.15)

In the present case series, 93% of the cases showed a very good stability, with optimal long-term fixation in 59.6%. While long-term fixation and osseointegration were rarely evaluated, the percentage of stable implants was in line with the case series by Zhang et al.3) (97%), Regis et al.11) (91%), Baktır et al.12) (90.6%), whereas it was lower in the case series by Gutiérrez Del Alamo et al.16) (83.5%).

More than half of the cases achieved optimal osseointegration with no radiolucency even in the proximal femurs, and in 85% of the cases, a very good bony ingrowth was reported. Friebert et al.20) compared revisions performed for aseptic loosening and for periprosthetic fracture: both groups achieved good bone integration at a minimum follow-up of 2 years using the Global Radiographic Score, with periprosthetic fractures being more rapid. On the contrary, Hellman et al.21) noticed that 94.1% of the cases had a stable fixation by bone ingrowth at a minimum follow-up of 2 years, but without using a properly designed score.

In conclusion, the single-taper conical revision stem demonstrated encouraging midterm clinical and radiographic outcomes, with a 96.6% survival rate at 6 and 10 years and a low dislocation rate (3.5%). Radiographic assessment indicated satisfactory bone regeneration and osseointegration, with only 1 case of aseptic loosening. However, given the limited sample size, single-center design, and absence of a control group, these results should be interpreted with caution. The stem may represent a valuable option in selected cases with circumferential diaphyseal support and compromised proximal bone stock, but larger comparative studies are warranted to better define its role relative to modular or cemented designs.
