Association of a novel triglyceride-glucose and non-HDL-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (TyG-NHHR) index with cardiometabolic multimorbidity: a dual-cohort prospective study in Chinese and European adults
Authors: Yilin Pan, Jingru Bi, Long Feng, Xiaonan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of two or more cardiometabolic diseases, represents a growing challenge for population health and health systems worldwide. Insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia are key metabolic disturbances underlying CMM, yet their joint contribution to CMM development remains incompletely understood. A composite index integrating the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and the non–HDL-cholesterol–to–HDL-cholesterol ratio (NHHR), termed TyG-NHHR, was developed to examine its association with incident CMM in two population-based cohorts.MethodsProspective data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2018) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (2008–2014) were analysed. After excluding participants with prevalent CMM or missing data, 9,297 adults aged 45 years and older were included. TyG-NHHR was calculated as TyG multiplied by NHHR. Incident CMM was defined as the new onset of at least two cardiometabolic conditions (heart disease, stroke, hypertension, or diabetes) during follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, body mass index, and medication use. Baseline, cumulative, and trajectory patterns of TyG-NHHR were examined, with dose–response relationships assessed using restricted cubic splines. Joint effect and bidirectional mediation analyses were conducted to explore interrelationships between TyG and NHHR.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 7 years, 1,851 participants (19.9%) developed CMM. Higher TyG-NHHR levels were consistently associated with increased CMM risk. Each standard deviation increase in baseline TyG-NHHR was associated with a 13% higher risk of CMM (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.09–1.17). Participants in the highest quartile had a substantially elevated risk compared with those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.33–1.83), with stronger associations observed for cumulative exposure and persistently high trajectories. A linear dose–response relationship was identified. Joint-effect analysis indicated overlapping metabolic pathways between TyG and NHHR. Mediation analysis indicated that insulin resistance accounted for a substantial proportion of the association between atherogenic dyslipidemia and CMM, whereas the reverse pathway was limited. Findings were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsTyG-NHHR is a simple, routinely derived metabolic index associated with the risk of developing cardiometabolic multimorbidity in middle-aged and older adults across diverse populations. Integrated metabolic indicators such as TyG-NHHR may support early identification of individuals at elevated risk of CMM and inform population-level prevention strategies, particularly in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01307-9.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) are the leading cause of premature mortality and disability worldwide, placing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and economies [1]. The prevalence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM)—the coexistence of two or more CMDs—is rapidly rising globally. CMM is associated with higher mortality, reduced quality of life, and greater clinical management complexity [2]. Consequently, simple and cost-effective tools are urgently needed to identify individuals at high risk of CMM, enabling targeted preventive strategies.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a core mechanism in the pathogenesis of CMM [3]. Although the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is the gold standard for assessing IR, it is complex, costly, and invasive, limiting its use in large-scale epidemiological studies and routine clinical practice [4]. Consequently, a need exists for simple, reliable surrogate markers. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting triglycerides and glucose, has emerged as a promising alternative. Numerous studies have confirmed its strong association with the risk of various cardiometabolic diseases [5].

However, IR alone does not explain the full risk of CMM, as atherogenic dyslipidemia is also a key contributor to its progression [6]. The non-HDL-c/HDL-c ratio (NHHR) is a comprehensive lipid marker that captures the balance between pro-atherogenic (e.g., LDL-C, VLDL-C) and anti-atherogenic (HDL-C) lipoproteins. Notably, evidence suggests that NHHR is a better predictor of cardiovascular events than conventional low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [7, 8].

While TyG-obesity combinations exist, obesity is an upstream factor often mediated by downstream metabolic disturbances [9]. Atherogenic dyslipidemia represents a distinct, proximal pathological process. To simultaneously capture insulin resistance and lipid dysregulation, we constructed the TyG-NHHR index as the product of TyG and NHHR. This multiplicative approach creates a single composite metric without requiring population-specific standardization. Using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), we investigated the association between TyG-NHHR and incident CMM.

Methods

Study population

Data were obtained from two nationally representative prospective cohorts: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2018) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA, 2008–2014). Both studies received ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards, and all participants provided written informed consent. Variables were harmonised across cohorts using comparable definitions and measurement units. Participants with prevalent cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) at baseline, or with missing data on exposures, outcomes, or key covariates, were excluded. Data assessment

Exposure assessment

The primary exposure was the TyG-NHHR index, defined as the product of the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and the non–HDL-cholesterol–to–HDL-cholesterol ratio (NHHR). TyG was calculated as ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2], and NHHR as (total cholesterol − HDL cholesterol)/HDL cholesterol.

Outcome ascertainment

The outcome was incident CMM, defined as the new onset of at least two cardiometabolic conditions (heart disease, stroke, hypertension, or diabetes) based on self-reported physician diagnoses. Participants were followed from baseline until CMM onset, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Covariate assessment

Baseline covariates included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, body mass index (BMI), medication use, and biochemical markers. Anthropometric and biochemical measurements were performed by trained staff using standardised protocols. Detailed variable definitions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

TyG-NHHR was examined in three forms: (1) baseline TyG-NHHR (continuous and quartiles); (2) cumulative TyG-NHHR, calculated as a time-weighted average of the first two measurements; and (3) TyG-NHHR trajectories, identified using K-means clustering to capture longitudinal exposure patterns.

Baseline characteristics were summarised by TyG-NHHR quartiles using means (±SD), medians (IQR), or frequencies (%), and compared using ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, or chi-square tests, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare the cumulative incidence of CMM.

Associations between TyG-NHHR and incident CMM were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, reporting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, education, and marital status), and Model 3 (additionally adjusted for smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, and medication use). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5), and proportional hazards assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

Restricted cubic splines were applied to assess potential non-linear dose–response relationships between baseline TyG-NHHR and CMM risk. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age (<60 vs. ≥60 years), sex, cohort, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Effect modification was evaluated using multiplicative interaction terms, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to interaction tests.

The joint effects of TyG and NHHR were examined by categorising participants according to the median values of each index. Multiplicative interaction was assessed using product terms, and additive interaction was evaluated using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI).

Exploratory mediation analyses were performed to examine potential pathways linking TyG and NHHR to CMM, with NHHR or TyG at follow-up considered as mediators of baseline exposure–outcome associations. Mediation effects were estimated using bootstrapping and interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Sensitivity analyses included a landmark analysis excluding CMM events occurring within the first two years of follow-up and analyses using discrete-time Cox models. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2). Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

In exploratory model comparisons, while all indices improved model discrimination over the base model, the TyG index alone showed slightly better performance metrics than the composite TyG-NHHR index (Supplementary Table 4). We conducted two sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Table 5). First, a landmark analysis was performed, excluding events within the first two years of follow-up to account for potential reverse causality. Second, we employed a discrete-time Cox model to align the analysis with the periodic nature of the follow-up assessments. The association between the TyG-NHHR index and CMM risk remained robustly significant in both scenarios, reinforcing the stability of our primary conclusion.

Discussion

In two large, prospective cohorts from Asia and Europe, we found that the novel composite index TyG-NHHR was an independent, dose-dependent predictor of incident CMM. These findings introduce TyG-NHHR as a novel tool for integrated metabolic assessment and provide new insights into the pathological synergy between IR and atherogenic dyslipidemia.

The conceptual basis of the TyG-NHHR index is its integration of two core pathophysiological pillars of CMM: IR and atherogenic dyslipidemia. The TyG index is a validated surrogate for IR [10]. However, previous indices combining TyG with obesity metrics (eg, BMI) reflect a more upstream, diffuse metabolic state. In contrast, our index incorporates NHHR, a marker shown to be superior to traditional LDL-C in capturing the total burden of atherogenic lipoproteins [7]. This combination allows TyG-NHHR to specifically target the intersection of IR and atherogenesis.Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analysis showed a robust, near-linear dose-response relationship between TyG-NHHR and CMM risk, with no threshold effect. The association was consistent across subgroups defined by age, sex, baseline BMI, and blood pressure, supporting the generalisability of the index. Finally, the stronger association observed with cumulative exposure and high-level trajectories supports a cumulative-burden hypothesis. This finding highlights the importance of assessing long-term metabolic trajectories, rather than single measurements, for accurate risk prediction.

Beyond the observed risk association, this study clarifies CMM pathogenesis by dissecting the interaction between IR and dyslipidemia. Our joint effect analysis revealed a statistically significant sub-additive interaction, a finding that challenges the hypothesis of two independent causal pathways. Instead, it suggests substantial mechanistic overlap, likely through shared downstream pathways such as chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and sympathetic nervous system overactivation. These pathways are established drivers of cardiometabolic diseases [11].

To investigate the directionality of this overlap, we performed bidirectional mediation analysis. Our analysis revealed a potential directional pathway: TyG (IR) mediated a substantial portion of the association between NHHR (dyslipidemia) and CMM, whereas the reverse mediation was not significant. This asymmetry provides strong, population-based evidence consistent with the lipotoxicity hypothesis in CMM pathogenesis. This hypothesis posits that excess circulating lipids deposit ectopically in non-adipose tissues (eg, liver, muscle, and β-cells), thereby impairing insulin signalling, inducing organelle stress, and ultimately driving systemic IR [12]. Notably, TyG alone exhibited slightly better predictive performance than the composite index. This aligns with our mediation findings, suggesting TyG acts as a dominant downstream effector. Thus, the composite index serves primarily to characterize the joint metabolic phenotype rather than to enhance statistical prediction.

Synthesizing these findings, we propose a two-stage model for CMM pathogenesis. Atherogenic dyslipidemia (high NHHR) may act as both an initiator and a potentiator. As a potential upstream factor, it is hypothesized to directly promote atherosclerosis. It may then potentiate the disease by inducing or exacerbating IR (high TyG). These two components then synergistically contribute to CMM onset and progression through shared downstream pathways.This integrated model has significant clinical implications. It positions atherogenic dyslipidemia (represented by NHHR) as a prime therapeutic target for CMM prevention. Targeting NHHR may confer a dual benefit: directly mitigating atherosclerotic risk while simultaneously alleviating a key upstream contributor to IR (lipotoxicity).

The study’s primary strengths are its large-scale, bi-racial, prospective cohort design, which supports the robustness of the observed associations and enhances the external validity of our findings. Furthermore, our multi-dimensional analytical approach (including cumulative exposure, trajectory models, interaction, and mediation analyses) enabled a robust and nuanced dissection of these complex relationships.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, our reliance on self-reported CMM, while common in large epidemiological studies, is a potential source of information bias. Second, potential selection bias may arise from the exclusion of participants with missing baseline data. Third, the observational design precludes definitive causal claims. Although we adjusted for many covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as detailed dietary intake or genetic susceptibility, remains possible. Furthermore, the TyG index is a surrogate marker and not a direct measure of insulin resistance. Fourth, the lack of statistical significance in the UK subgroup likely reflects limited statistical power due to the smaller sample size. Fifth, given the extensive exploratory analyses, the possibility of Type I error cannot be excluded. Finally, our cohort consisted primarily of middle-aged and older adults, so our findings may not apply to younger populations.

Our findings are poised for clinical and public health translation. The TyG-NHHR index, derived from four low-cost, routine biochemical markers, provides a practical tool for integrated metabolic assessment—particularly in resource-limited settings—and promotes a paradigm shift from single-disease assessment toward proactive, integrated multimorbidity management. To realize this potential, future research must prioritize external validation in diverse populations, evaluating its clinical utility against established models (e.g., FRS), and elucidating underlying molecular mechanisms via multi-omics. Ultimately, these efforts should culminate in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to determine if interventions targeting the TyG-NHHR index can effectively reduce CMM incidence.

Conclusions

In summary, this study suggests that the TyG-NHHR index is an independent and simple predictor of incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults. The index integrates the core drivers of insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia, providing insights into their interaction and highlighting potential targets for CMM prevention. Therefore, TyG-NHHR may serve as a useful tool for the early identification and risk stratification of CMM, particularly in primary care settings.
