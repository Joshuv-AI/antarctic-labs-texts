Cognitive function and eye movement patterns during reading tasks in older adults
Authors: Haiwei Zuo, King Hann Lim, Heming Huang, Sivaraman Eswaran, Jonathan Then Sien Phang, Luping Song, Chenhui Zhang, Zhenjun Li, Hongming Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONProgressive cognitive decline associated with aging can be measured by observing alterations in eye movement behavior during reading. This study explores the association between eye movement profiles and cognitive performance in older adults during reading‐based tasks in virtual reality.METHODSSixty older adults with Alzheimer's disease (AD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and healthy controls (HC) wore a virtual reality (VR) headset during a reading‐based task. Recorded eye movements yielded four gaze features (F2–F5): fixation duration, region of interest (ROI) entry count, regression count, and saccade amplitude. With the reading‐task total score (F1), these formed a five‐feature set (F1–F5) for logistic regression under leakage‐safe stratified 10‐fold cross‐validation. Concurrent validity was assessed against paper‐based Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE).RESULTSThe reading‐task total score was strongly correlated with MMSE (r = 0.92, p < 0.001). Eye‐tracking features showed significant group differences, with longer fixation duration, more ROI entries, more reading‐order regressions, and larger saccade amplitude in cognitively impaired groups. The three‐class combined model reached macro area under the curve (AUC) = 1.000, accuracy = 0.997, and macro F1 = 0.997; for MCI versus HC, eye‐tracking‐only and combined models reached AUC = 1.000 compared with AUC = 0.895 for the reading‐task score alone.DISCUSSIONIntegrating gaze features with the reading‐task total score can improve cognitive assessment by capturing process‐level information. Participants with AD and MCI exhibited longer fixation durations, more ROI entries, and more reading regressions than controls, with the largest effects in AD. The combined model showed its clearest value in the three‐class setting.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cognitive assessment, eye movement, mild cognitive impairment, virtual reality

INTRODUCTION

The growing population of older adults is at high risk of suffering cognitive decline involving memory loss, language difficulties, and impaired reasoning due to progressive neurodegenerative disease such as Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 This disease currently affects approximately 57 million people worldwide and is projected to affect 153 million by 2050. 2 According to Petersen et al. 3 , approximately 6.7% of adults aged 60–64 have mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and this proportion exceeds 25% among individuals aged 80 years and older. An early detection of MCI and AD 4 is therefore critical for timely clinical management to slow down the disease progression. Common tools used in clinical practice for cognitive screening include the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) 5 , 6 . The MMSE detects MCI with modest accuracy at 62.7% sensitivity and 63.3% specificity 7 , while the MoCA can reach higher MCI sensitivity (e.g., 80.5% in a large Chinese community sample) but shows variable specificity across populations 8 , 9 . These tools provide limited process‐level information and have constrained ecological validity 10 , 11 . Hence, a better assessment of cognitive decline is needed to capture objective behavioral features beyond summary performance scores 12 .

Digital cognitive assessments can improve MCI detection sensitivity to 80%–86% 13 , 14 . Immersive virtual reality (VR) further provides ecological validity, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.765–0.96 for MCI identification 15 , 16 , 17 . Eye‐movement features also provide additional diagnostic value, with meta‐analytic sensitivity of 75%–84% and specificity of 73%–75% 18 , 19 . Combining task performance with gaze features can improve classification accuracy, with AUC increasing from 0.78 to 0.95 when gaze metrics were added 20 . However, current VR assessments 21 , 22 often introduce new tasks rather than adapting established screening protocols such as the MMSE and MoCA. In addition, there is no study employing a dedicated reading paradigm in VR with eye tracking for cognitive assessment 23 . The integration of cognitive assessment with eye tracking has not yet been established within a common evaluation framework 24 , 25 . Table S1 summarizes cognitive assessment approaches for MCI/AD detection and their limitations.

Reading‐based tasks are particularly informative because they simultaneously engage multiple cognitive domains including attention and working memory 26 , 27 , 28 . Patients with AD exhibited longer fixation durations, increased regressions, and reduced saccade efficiency during reading compared to healthy controls (HC). 29 , 30 These gaze disruptions were consistent with compensatory executive control theory 31 . This framework posits that early cognitive decline is masked by the recruitment of additional executive resources to maintain task performance 32 . Increased visual checking behavior in MCI was proposed to reflect such compensatory effort before overt performance declines 20 , 33 . Fraser et al. 34 applied machine learning to reading eye‐movement features and achieved 86% accuracy for MCI classification. Groznik et al. 35 validated these measures on 99 participants and reported AUC of approximately 0.75 for distinguishing healthy aging from MCI and AD. Because many screening items are presented as written stimuli, reading‐based tasks provide a natural paradigm for integrating cognitive assessment with gaze measurement 12 , 36 . All existing reading‐based studies used desktop displays rather than immersive VR environments.

The present study adapts key elements of conventional paper‐based screening into a reading‐based assessment in VR. Five clinically established screening domains are implemented as text‐based VR items: orientation to time, orientation to place, registration, attention and calculation, and delayed recall. Domain‐aligned scoring is retained to preserve clinical familiarity and enable concurrent validity assessment against a conventional reference measure. Integrated eye tracking records four eye‐movement features: fixation duration, region of interest (ROI) entry count, regression count, and saccade amplitude. Together with the total task score, these features are evaluated using logistic regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation to quantify the incremental value of eye‐movement information.

Cognitive domain in reading task implementation

The cognitive domain can be associated with reading because reading is fundamentally a mental process that involves multiple levels of thinking and information processing 37 , 38 , 39 . When a person reads, they do not only recognize written symbols, but they actively engage in decoding words, understanding vocabulary, interpreting sentence structure and constructing meaning from text 27 , 28 , 37 . These processes correspond directly to cognitive functions such as attention, language comprehension, memory, and executive control. In the context of cognitive assessment, reading‐based tasks are therefore useful because participants must maintain instructions, interpret response options, and retrieve relevant information while their eye movements are being monitored 12 . The conceptual linkage between reading operations, cognitive domains, and the eye‐movement features analyzed in this study is shown in Figure S1.

A text‐based reading panel is presented in virtual reality to capture the reading process. Participants read task instructions and response options before providing either a verbal or controller‐based response. Orientation, registration, and recall tasks use verbal responses to maintain consistency with conventional administration. The attention‐and‐calculation task uses controller‐based selection to enable automated scoring. Table S2 summarizes the task implementation, response modality, scoring rule, and reading processes for each domain. The total score ranges from 0 to 21 across the five cognitive domains. Verbal instructions are artificial intelligence (AI) ‐generated and delivered automatically to ensure standardized administration across participants. Language and visuospatial subtests are not included in this version.

Eye movement metrics for cognitive assessment

Eye movements are governed by a distributed network of cortical and subcortical brain regions that substantially overlap with the neural substrates affected by Alzheimer's disease pathology. The oculomotor control system comprises two complementary descending pathways for saccade generation 40 , 41 . The cortical pathway originates in the frontal eye fields (FEF) and projects through the superior colliculus (SC) to the brainstem saccade generator (BSG), mediating voluntary saccades under top‐down executive control from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). During reading, the oculomotor circuit extends beyond saccade generation: the angular gyrus (AG) and temporal cortex (TC) support lexical and semantic processing that modulates fixation duration on individual words, while the hippocampus (HPC) and entorhinal cortex (EC) influence memory‐guided gaze behavior by supporting the encoding and retrieval of previously viewed information 42 . These cognitive structures are not typically included in classical saccade circuit diagrams 43 but are essential for understanding how cognitive decline alters reading‐related eye movements. Figure S2 illustrates both the classical oculomotor pathways and the cognitive structures relevant to reading‐based eye tracking, mapped onto the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Revision 5 (DSM‐5) neurocognitive framework.

The DSM‐5 defines six neurocognitive domains for the classification of neurocognitive disorders: complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual‐motor function, and social cognition 44 . Each domain is subserved by identifiable brain regions that are progressively affected by AD neuropathology following the Braak staging of neurofibrillary tangle propagation 45 . Multiple pathways converge on the SC and FEF, indicating that individual gaze metrics are sensitive to multiple cognitive domains simultaneously. Table S3 summarizes the correspondence among these domains, their neural substrates, the approximate Braak stage of involvement, and the features that capture the resulting oculomotor effects.

The progressive nature of AD pathology creates a temporal sequence in which different oculomotor signatures emerge at different disease stages 46 . In the MCI stage, medial temporal lobe degeneration (Braak stages I–II) primarily impairs memory function while frontal executive resources remain relatively preserved. Compensatory recruitment of these executive resources produces a characteristic gaze pattern: task performance is maintained at a near‐normal level, but the underlying gaze behavior shows elevated ROI entry counts and increased regression counts as the reader repeatedly checks and re‐reads information to compensate for weakened memory encoding 47 . As the disease progresses to the dementia stage, pathology extends to the parietal and frontal cortices (Braak stages III–VI), compromising both the attentional and executive networks. At this stage, compensatory mechanisms become insufficient, and gaze behavior shifts toward longer fixation durations, larger saccade amplitudes, and increased viewing of task‐irrelevant regions. The integration of task performance scores with process‐level gaze metrics therefore captures complementary information: the reading‐task total score (F 1) reflects the outcome of cognitive processing, whereas the gaze features (F 2–F 5) reveal the efficiency and strategy of the underlying processing.

Based on evidence from prior reading‐based eye‐tracking studies in cognitive aging 19 , 33 , four primary gaze features were extracted in the present reading‐based assessment. These metrics are fixation duration, ROI entry count, regression count, and saccade amplitude. Fixations and saccades are detected using the velocity‐threshold identification (I‐VT) algorithm 48 . The velocity threshold is set to 40 deg/s to accommodate head‐mounted VR viewing conditions 49 . EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

A cross‐sectional study was conducted to evaluate the proposed VR assessment across the cognitive spectrum from healthy aging to dementia. Sixty older adults were recruited from three tertiary hospitals and surrounding communities in southern China and classified into three groups (n=20 per group): AD, MCI, and HC. The AD group met NIA‐AA criteria for probable dementia 50 , and the MCI group met Petersen's criteria for amnestic MCI 51 . Healthy controls were defined as participants who reported no subjective cognitive complaints. Inclusion criteria required age ≥60 years, ≥9 years of education, and normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision (acuity ≥0.8). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; a legally authorized representative was accepted for the AD group. Table 1 summarizes demographic characteristics and baseline MMSE scores. Ethical approval was obtained from the Curtin University Human Research Ethics Committee (HRE2024‐0718; approved on December 16, 2024).

All experiments were conducted in a quiet, well‐lit room (4 m × 4 m) with controlled lighting for stable eye tracking, and each participant completed both the paper‐based MMSE and the VR assessment in a single session. As summarized in Figure 1, the procedure comprised four phases: preparation and headset fitting (approximately 10 minutes), nine‐point calibration with circular tracking confirmation (approximately 3 minutes, repeated if mean gaze accuracy exceeded 0.5), the five cognitive tasks in fixed order (orientation to time, orientation to place, registration, attention and calculation, delayed recall; approximately 5 minutes), and a wrap‐up comfort check. Verbal responses were recorded by the examiner and controller‐based responses captured automatically.

Five participant‐level features were used for analysis: the outcome‐level reading‐task total score (F1, range 0–21) and the four process‐level gaze features F2–F5 defined above, computed as participant‐level means across all task‐by‐ROI observations (Table S6). These features were not stratified by task or ROI type to avoid high‐dimensional feature spaces in this small sample. The classification models built on these features were evaluated using stratified 10‐fold cross‐validation, which preserves each class's proportion within every fold and uses nine folds for training and the held‐out fold for testing in turn. To prevent data leakage, all data‐dependent steps, here the z‐standardization of the features, were fitted on the training folds only and then applied to the held‐out fold; we refer to this train‐only preprocessing as leakage‐safe 25 , 52 . Because fold‐level estimates are variable at this sample size, they were interpreted alongside the bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) and permutation test following Varoquaux et al. 53 . Specifically, this cross‐validation was repeated 20 times with different random partitions; reported point estimates are the mean across repetitions, and 95% CIs were obtained by participant‐level percentile bootstrap (2,000 resamples) of the out‐of‐fold predictions.

Psychometric properties of the reading‐task score were examined in the full sample (N=60): internal consistency (Cronbach's α with a Feldt 95% CI and McDonald's ω, estimated both overall and within each diagnostic group), internal structure (inter‐domain and corrected domain‐total correlations, and a single‐factor exploratory factor analysis with the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett's test), and concurrent validity against the paper‐based MMSE total score. Given the sample size and the HC–MCI–AD composition, the factor analysis is exploratory and the coefficients are interpreted as instrument‐level properties.

RESULTS

Demographic variables were comparable across groups (Table 1): no significant differences in age (F(2,57)=1.42, p=0.251), education (F(2,57)=0.78, p=0.463), or gender (χ2=0.54, p=0.762). The reading‐task total score (F1) differed significantly across groups (F(2,57)=38.24, p<0.001, η2=0.57; controls > MCI and AD, Tukey all p<0.001) and correlated strongly with the paper‐based MMSE total score (r=0.92, p<0.001; 95% CI 0.87–0.95); because the reading task adapts MMSE content, this represents convergent rather than independent validation. Across the full sample the five‐domain score showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α=0.76, 95% CI [0.65, 0.84]; McDonald's ω=0.78) and a single dominant factor (eigenvalue 2.64, 52.8% of variance), with the supporting coefficients reported in Table S7. These pooled coefficients were largely driven by between‐group differences: once between‐group variance was removed, within‐group internal consistency was negligible (Cronbach's α<0 in all three groups; Table S7), the expected behavior of a multidomain instrument under a restricted within‐group range (notably the control ceiling). Per‐domain scores by group and within‐group covariance matrices are provided in Tables S8 and S9.

Experiment 1: Eye movement profile analysis

Between‐group differences in the four eye‐tracking features (F2–F5) were tested using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey‐adjusted pairwise comparisons. Omnibus p values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, and effect sizes were reported as eta squared. Table 2 tabulates the descriptive statistics and post‐hoc comparison of diagnostic groups. At the participant level, mean fixation duration (F2) showed a large group effect (F(2,57) = 36.27, eta squared = 0.56). AD and MCI participants fixated longer than controls (Tukey p < 0.001 for both), whereas the AD‐MCI contrast was not significant after adjustment (p = 0.073). ROI entry count (F3) showed a very large group effect (F(2,57) = 132.08, eta squared = 0.82), and reading‐order regression count (F4) showed a large group effect (F(2,57) = 29.04, eta squared = 0.51). Both features followed a monotonic AD > MCI > controls pattern, with all pairwise comparisons significant (Tukey p < 0.001). Saccade amplitude (F5, a.u.) showed a moderate group effect (F(2,57) = 17.75, eta squared = 0.38), with larger amplitudes in AD and MCI than in controls, but no significant AD–MCI difference.

Sensitivity analyzes based on mixed‐effects models yielded convergent results. Adding group significantly improved model fit for fixation duration (χ2(2)=49.26, p=2.0×10−11), log‐transformed ROI entry count (χ2(2)=134.48, p=6.3×10−30), and log‐transformed regression count (χ2(2)=138.56, p=8.2×10−31). At the task level, a mixed model of mean saccade amplitude also showed a significant group effect (χ2(2)=25.20, p=3.4×10−6). The group × task interaction was not significant (χ2(8)=3.38, p=0.908), indicating that the overall group differences were broadly consistent across the five task domains.

Experiment 2: Latent structure of the five‐feature space

Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the participant‐level five‐feature matrix after z‐standardization of F1‐F5 across all 60 participants. The first principal component (PC1) explained 70.4% of the total variance, PC2 explained 16.2%, and PC3 explained 6.7%, so that the first two components accounted for 86.6% cumulatively and the first three accounted for 93.3%. Under the displayed orientation, higher PC1 values reflected a joint pattern of longer fixation duration, more ROI entries, more regressions, and lower F1 performance. Thus, the dominant variance structure was largely driven by eye‐movement features, while behavioral performance contributed an additional orthogonal dimension rather than collapsing completely into the same component.

In the reduced PCA space, healthy controls clustered predominantly at lower PC1 values, MCI participants occupied intermediate positions, and AD participants shifted toward higher PC1 values (Figure S4C), indicating that most between‐group separation followed a single severity‐related axis. Partial overlap remained along PC2, consistent with secondary variation in saccade amplitude that did not map perfectly onto diagnosis. Together, the PCA results support the view that F1 and F2−−F5 capture related but non‐identical aspects of cognitive impairment.

Experiment 3: Classification performance across feature sets

Classification performance was evaluated using the leakage‐safe stratified 10‐fold cross‐validation described in the Experimental Setup section. Three prespecified logistic‐regression model classes were compared. Model A used the reading‐task total score alone (F1), Model B used the four eye‐tracking features alone (F2−F5), and Model C used the combined feature set (F1−F5). For the binary MCI‐versus‐HC task, performance was summarized using AUC, accuracy, F1, sensitivity, and specificity.

For the MCI‐versus‐HC task, Model A (F1 only) achieved an AUC of 0.895 (95% CI 0.760–0.990), accuracy of 0.875 (95% CI 0.775–0.975), and F1 of 0.875 (95% CI 0.768–0.974) (Table 3). In contrast, both Model B (F2–F5 only) and Model C (F1–F5) achieved AUC, accuracy, F1, sensitivity, and specificity values of 1.000 (95% CI 1.000‐1.000). Beyond the fold‐averaged model comparison, the corrected binary robustness suite showed AUC = 1.000 for both MCI‐versus‐HC and AD‐versus‐HC for the combined model (label‐permutation p = 0.001 for the eye‐tracking models on both contrasts). AD and HC were near‐completely separable in this cohort, including non‐overlapping F1 ranges (AD 7‐13 vs HC 16‐21), so AD‐versus‐HC is best read as a sanity check rather than an informative incremental‐value contrast. A demographics‐only baseline model using age, sex, and education achieved an AUC of 0.44, whereas the combined model remained at AUC = 1.000, indicating that the observed discrimination was not explained by baseline demographic imbalance.

To assess whether combining the reading‐task total score with eye‐tracking features provided additional value when all three diagnostic groups were considered simultaneously, the same three model classes were compared using a three‐class one‐vs‐rest analysis. In this setting, the combined model (Model C) showed the strongest overall performance among the three models (Table 3; Figure 3), with macro AUC of 1.000 (95% CI 1.000–1.000), accuracy of 0.997 (95% CI 0.983–1.000), and macro F1 of 0.997 (95% CI 0.983–1.000). The corresponding values for Model A were 0.961 (95% CI 0.906–0.997), 0.917 (95% CI 0.833–0.983), and 0.917 (95% CI 0.836–0.981), respectively, whereas Model B achieved 0.961 (95% CI 0.921–0.990), 0.877 (95% CI 0.800–0.950), and 0.876 (95% CI 0.795–0.954), respectively.

DISCUSSION

A reading‐based VR cognitive pre‐screening tool with integrated eye tracking underwent early internal validation in this study. Reading‐task total score (F1) was strongly correlated with paper‐based MMSE (r = 0.92, p < 0.001), supporting convergent validity because the VR task adapts established cognitive screening content. Beyond overall task score, the process‐level gaze features (F2–F5) differentiated HC, MCI, and AD participants, indicating that reading‐related eye movements captured clinically relevant variation in cognitive efficiency and visual‐cognitive control.

Classification performance should be read against the composition of this small, clinically separated cohort. The informative contrast is MCI‐versus‐HC, where scores overlapped (MCI 14–20 vs HC 16‐21): here the reading‐task score alone reached AUC 0.895, whereas eye‐tracking‐only and combined models reached 1.000. These ceiling values must be interpreted cautiously. With only 20 participants per group and highly separated case–control groups, perfect or near‐perfect internal discrimination is not evidence of clinical‐grade screening accuracy. Small clinical machine‐learning studies are known to yield optimistic, ceiling‐prone estimates. These results therefore should be framed as internal validation only. External validation in independent, community‐based, and multi‐site cohorts with a broader severity spectrum and larger per‐group samples is required before any claim about real‐world screening performance can be made.

Gaze behavior during reading‐based tasks revealed distinct group patterns. MCI participants showed elevated ROI entry counts and task‐dependent increases in checking behavior, despite less severe impairment than the AD group. AD participants showed longer fixation durations, more regressions, and greater background viewing, suggesting less efficient information extraction and weaker control over task‐relevant gaze allocation.

The proposed assessment is not intended to replace brief paper–based screens such as the MMSE or MoCA. A complete session is more demanding in equipment, space, and operator time (approximately 18 minutes; the Experimental Setup section). Its more natural comparator is a comprehensive neuropsychological battery, which provides comparably rich process‐level information yet requires considerably longer administration and trained examiners 54 , 55 . The additional cost yields objective gaze information that brief screens cannot capture in a single, substantially shorter session; whether this trade‐off is justified warrants formal cost‐effectiveness and acceptability evaluation.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross‐sectional design does not establish temporal ordering between gaze changes and cognitive decline, and test‐retest reliability was not assessed. Calibration requirements, data‐quality exclusions, and susceptibility to cybersickness further highlight practical barriers for head‐mounted displays in older adults 56 . The domain‐aligned design preserved clinical familiarity but may underuse the broader ecological potential of VR. Future work should prioritize multi‐site external validation, longitudinal follow‐up, and robustness testing across devices, languages, and clinical settings.

CONCLUSION

A domain‐aligned reading‐based VR assessment with integrated eye tracking was developed and internally evaluated for older adults across HC, MCI, and AD groups. The reading‐task total score was strongly correlated with paper‐based MMSE (r = 0.92, p < 0.001; 95% CI 0.87–0.95), while four eye‐tracking features (F2–F5: fixation duration, ROI entry count, regression count, and saccade amplitude) captured clinically relevant process‐level differences during reading‐based tasks. Under leakage‐safe cross‐validation, AD‐versus‐HC and MCI‐versus‐HC reached AUC = 1.000 in this internally validated case‐control cohort; because AD‐versus‐HC was near‐completely separable, the key incremental signal was MCI‐versus‐HC, where the F1‐only AUC was 0.895 and eye‐tracking–only and combined AUCs were 1.000. In the three‐class analysis, the combined model reached macro AUC 1.000, accuracy 0.997, and macro F1 0.997. PCA further suggested that behavioral performance and gaze features contributed partly distinct information. These findings support VR‐based reading assessment with integrated eye tracking as a promising approach for early cognitive screening, pending external validation in larger and more heterogeneous cohorts.
