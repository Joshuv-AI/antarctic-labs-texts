Cellulose saccharification to alleviate CCR of xylose for enhanced 1, 2, 4-butanetriol production
Authors: Shengji Li, Lisha Qin, Mengyue Ge, Yao Zuo, Mengyang Li, Sheng Xu, Xin Wang, Kequan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract1,2,4-Butanetriol (BT) is a valuable polyol with applications in pharmaceuticals, materials, and military sectors. BT biosynthesis primarily uses xylose as substrate, with glucose supporting cell growth. However, glucose-mediated carbon catabolite repression (CCR) suppresses xylose assimilation, limiting BT production. This study aimed to investigate the impact of glucose on xylose-driven BT production in dual-carbon-source utilization and to develop a cellulose-based simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) strategy for enhanced glucose management, thereby alleviating CCR and improving BT yields. Heterologous BT biosynthesis pathways were introduced into Escherichia coli, with catabolic genes xylAB and yiaE/ycdW knocked out and xylose transporter xylE overexpressed, the BT synthesis pathway was constructed. Optimized fermentation conditions yielded 2.85 g/L BT. Next, the glucose-xylose ratio was optimized by adding xylose at early exponential phase, while low initial glucose with fed-batch feeding significantly reduced CCR, resulting in a BT titer of 3.59 g/L. To further alleviate CCR, an SSF strategy with continuous low-concentration glucose supply from cellulose was implemented, achieving 4.48 g/L BT. Finally, cellulose-xylan co-utilization strategy yielded 1.35 g/L BT. This study provides a reference for alleviating the CCR effect caused by the utilization of dual-carbon-source in biosynthesizing BT, and offers insights for producing BT from lignocellulose.Keywords: 1,2,4-Butanetriol; Dual-carbon-source utilization; Carbon catabolite repression; Simultaneous saccharification and fermentation

Introduction

Due to the ever-growing energy demand and the continuous depletion of unsustainable fossil feedstocks, the exploration of renewable resource utilization has become a global hotspot [1]. Lignocellulose, as a renewable resource, is widely distributed and abundantly reserved in nature, and can be directly obtained from agricultural waste by-products [2]. Glucose and xylose derived from lignocellulose hydrolysates can serve as carbon sources for synthesizing various high-value-added products, including BT [3,4].

1,2,4-Butanetriol (BT) is a polyol containing three hydroxyl groups, a characteristic that endows it with unique reactivity, enabling its extensive applications in the fields of pharmaceuticals, materials, and military [5,6]. The hydroxyl groups of BT can react with nitric acid to produce the energetic compound BT trinitrate (BTTN), which serves as a propellant and energetic plasticizer in the military field [7]. In the pharmaceutical industry, BT is a key intermediate for the synthesis of anti-HIV drugs (e.g., Amprenavir) and cholesterol-lowering drugs [8]. Additionally, BT can be used as a blocking agent for controlled drug release [9]. Moreover, the addition reaction of BT with isocyanates is employed in the synthesis of polyurethane foams, whose properties are comparable to those of natural rubber [10].

Currently, chemical synthesis remains the predominant method for BT production [11]. The most widely employed approach involves the reduction of malic acid using NaBH4 as a reducing agent [12]. However, this catalyst is non-recoverable, and the synthesis generates substantial quantities of boric acid as a by-product [11]. Moreover, the process is energy-intensive and operates under harsh conditions, resulting in elevated production costs and significant environmental concerns [13]. In contrast, biotechnological synthesis offers mild reaction conditions and reduced manufacturing expenses, emerging as a promising sustainable alternative [14]. Due to the absence of a natural biosynthetic pathway for BT, the development of engineered microbial strains has emerged as a crucial approach for its biotechnological production. The first reported biosynthesis of BT was achieved through a two-step fermentation process involving Pseudomonas fragi and Escherichia coli [5]. However, this two-step process was complex. Valdehuesa et al. (2014) [11] succeeded in producing BT in a single E. coli strain using xylose as the substrate. Since then, various single-host microorganisms, including Arabidopsis [15], Saccharomyces cerevisiae [16], and Gluconobacter oxydans [17], have been explored as chassis for BT production. The substrates for BT biosynthesis include xylose, glycerol, arabinose, and C1 compounds [18].

Compared to other carbon sources used for BT biosynthesis, xylose as a substrate is particularly advantageous, as it is the second most abundant sugar in nature and requires only a single carbon removal step, thereby reducing the number of reaction steps and lowering the reaction complexity [11], In contrast, alternative substrates such as arabinose are prohibitively expensive, while glucose-based routes are constrained by enzyme and cofactor limitations [19]. The conversion of xylose to BT proceeds via a four-types enzymatic cascade, including xylose dehydrogenase, xylonate dehydratase, 2-keto-3-deoxy-xylonate (KDX) decarboxylase, and alcohol dehydrogenase enables the bioconversion, meanwhile, glucose serves as the growth carbon source, thereby promoting BT synthesis [20]. However, when using mixed carbon sources for BT biosynthesis, glucose inhibits xylose uptake through CCR, thereby limiting BT production [21]. To alleviate CCR and promote xylose assimilation, various strategies have been explored (Fig. 1), including the employment of the low-CCR chassis strains [22] and the genetic deletion of PTS to derepress carbon catabolite control [1,23]. Furthermore, the overexpression of xylose-responsive transcriptional activator XylR and xylose transporter XylE has demonstrated partial relief of CCR [24]. Nevertheless, the trade-off between glucose-dependent biomass accumulation and xylose-directed BT biosynthesis continues to constrain product yields. The low BT yield resulting from CCR-mediated repression of xylose utilization remains to be further optimized.

Glucose–xylose co-utilization strategy for BT production. (A) When glucose and xylose coexist, severe CCR markedly impedes xylose utilization, resulting in low BT titers. (B) By knocking out the PTS system, the CCR effect was eliminated; however, this reduced glucose uptake, impaired NADPH supply and biomass formation, ultimately leading to a decrease in BT production. (C) SSF of cellulose provides low-limit glucose concentrations, alleviating CCR while maintaining adequate NADPH availability and biomass for enhanced BT synthesis.

In this study, we first constructed a xylose-based BT biosynthetic pathway in E. Coli, eliminated competing branches and overexpressed xylose transporter protein XylE, the BT synthesis pathway was constructed in E. Coli (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, the effects of glucose-to-xylose ratios on BT production during mixed-carbon-source fermentation were investigated. To alleviate glucose-induced CCR, we developed a simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) strategy, which effectively relieved CCR and achieved a maximum BT titer of 4.48 g/L. Finally, xylan was employed to replace xylose, achieving BT production via cellulose-xylan co-SSF.

Materials and methods

Microorganisms and medium

The strains used and constructed in this study are list in Table S1. The LB medium consisted of 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 10 g/L NaCl. The seed culture medium contained 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L tryptone, and 5 g/L NaCl. The fermentation medium was composed of 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L NaCl, 2.5 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 0.12 g/L MgSO4, 0.11 g/L CaCl2, 10 g/L CaCO3, 0.33 mg/L (NH4)6Mo7O24, 2.25 mg/L H3BO3, 0.639 mg/L CoCl2·6H2O, 0.225 mg/L CuSO4·5H2O, 1.44 mg/L MnCl2·4H2O, and 0.261 mg/L ZnSO4·7H2O, 5 g/L glucose and 20 g/L xylose initally, and followed by variable additions of glucose, xylose, cellulose, and xylan tailored to specific experimental conditions. The cellulase (CTec2) and xylanase (Htec3) used in this study were obtained from Novozymes A/S (Denmark).

Construction of plasmids

The plasmids constructed in this study are listed in Table S2, and the primers used are shown in Table S3. The xylE fragment was amplified by PCR using primers P11/P12 with the genome of MG1655 as the template. The plasmid pTrc99a-kdcA-xylB was linearized with BamHI/SalI restriction enzymes, and the recombinant plasmid pTrc99a-kdcA-xylB-xylE was obtained through homologous recombination. The pTarget series plasmids were amplified using primers P1/P2, P3/P4, P5/P6, P7/P8, and P9/P10, respectively, yielding the plasmids pTarget-ΔyiaE, pTarget-ΔyjhH, pTarget-ΔyagE, pTarget-ΔxylA, and pTarget-ΔyjhH.

Construction of the mutant E. coli strains

Electrocompetent E. coli Trans1-T1 cells carrying pCas9-λRed (encoding CRISPR-Cas9 and arabinose-inducible λ-Red recombinase) were prepared as described [25]. The primers used for amplifying donor DNA are listed in Table S3. The electroporation program was set at a voltage of 2500 V with a pulse duration of 5–6 m s. The plasmid pTarget-ΔxlyA and its corresponding donor DNA were co-transformed into Trans1-T1 to generate the recombinant strain T1-ΔxlyA (M01). Subsequently, the plasmid pTarget-ΔyagE and its corresponding donor DNA were co-transformed into M01 to obtain the mutant strain T1-ΔxlyAΔyagE (M02). The plasmid pTarget-ΔyjhH and its corresponding donor DNA were co-transformed into M02 to generate the mutant strain T1-ΔxlyAΔyagEΔyjhH (M03). The plasmid pTarget-ΔycdW and its corresponding donor DNA were co-transformed into M02 to obtain the mutant strain T1-ΔxlyAΔyiaEΔycdW (M04). Finally, the plasmid pTarget-ΔyagE and its corresponding donor DNA were co-transformed into M04 to yield the mutant strain T1-ΔxlyAΔyiaEΔycdWΔyagE (M05). The method for curing the pCas and pTarget plasmids from the knockout strains was performed as described by Datsenko and Wanner [26]. Each knockout strain was then transformed with the plasmids pTrc99a-kdcA-xylB and pCWJ-yjhG-adhP to generate the recombinant strains M01, M02, M03, M04, and M05, respectively. The mutant strain T1-ΔxlyAΔyiaEΔycdW was transformed with the dual plasmids pTrc99a-kdcA-xylB-xylE and pCWJ-yjhG-adhP using the heat-shock method to obtain the recombinant strain M06.

Culture conditions

Individual colonies were cultured on LB solid medium containing 0.05 g/L ampicillin and 0.068 g/L chloramphenicol, incubated upside down at 37°C for 12–14 h. A single colony was then inoculated into 5 mL of LB medium containing 0.05 g/L ampicillin and 0.068 g/L chloramphenicol and cultured at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 8–10 h. The primary seed culture was subsequently inoculated into 50 mL of secondary seed medium containing 0.05 g/L ampicillin and 0.068 g/L chloramphenicol, and cultured at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 6–10 h, until the OD600 reached approximately 2.5–3. The secondary seed culture was then inoculated into 50 mL of fermentation medium containing 0.05 g/L ampicillin and 0.068 g/L chloramphenicol at an inoculation volume of 4% (initial OD600 of approximately 0.1). The culture was incubated at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Unless otherwise specified, when the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 1 mmol/L for induction, and the culture temperature was reduced to 30°C for continued incubation for 72 h.

Analytic methods

Cell growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). Determination of xylose using the DNS (3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid) reduction method [27]: A blank control was prepared by mixing 320 μL of DNS solution with 320 μL of distilled water, boiling for 10 min, cooling, and then diluting to a final volume of 4 mL with water. For the sample determination, 320 μL of the diluted sample was mixed with 320 μL of DNS solution, boiled for 10 min, cooled, and then diluted to a final volume of 4 mL with water. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using the blank control as a reference, and the concentration of reducing sugars was calculated based on the standard curve of reducing sugars.

Acetic acid and BT were detected using an Agilent Technologies 1290 high-performance liquid chromatograph under the following conditions: Bio-Rad Aminex® HPX-87H Ion Exclusion Column (300 mm × 7.8 mm) for organic acid analysis; mobile phase of 0.008 M H2SO4; flow rate of 0.6 mL/min; column temperature of 55°C; injection volume of 20 μL; and a refractive index detector. The cellulase activity was measured using the filter paper assay method [28].

Results and discussion

Engineering glucose and xylose co-utilization for BT biosynthesis in E. Coli

The BT biosynthetic pathway in E. coli utilizing xylose as the substrate primarily consists of xylose dehydrogenase, xylonate dehydratase, KDX decarboxylase, and alcohol dehydrogenase. However, endogenous enzymes in E. coli form by-pathways that consume carbon flux, thereby reducing the efficiency of xylose conversion to BT [29]. To enhance carbon flux, Jing et al. (2018) [9] deleted the genes yiaE and ycdW, which encode KDX reductase, resulting in respective increases of 19% and 41% in BT titer and yield. Additionally, to block the xylose by-pathway, antisense RNA was employed to inhibit the expression of xylose isomerase (XylA), which achieved an 214% increase in BT titer. The deletion of branch pathways ultimately enhanced BT yield.

To realize the biosynthesis of BT, we constructed an engineered E. coli strain M00 by transforming it with plasmids pTrc99a-kdcA-xylB and pCWJ-yjhG-adhP, resulting engineered strain achieved a BT yield of 0.66 g/L in shake-flask fermentation. Next, we evaluated the impact of knocking out branch pathway genes, including Xylose isomerase and xylulose kinase (encoded by xylAB), KDX aldolase (encoded by yagE/yjhH), and KDX reductase (encoded by yiaE/gcdW), on BT production. The strain M01, with the xylAB gene knocked out, achieved a BT yield of 1.4 g/L, which is 2.1-fold higher than that of M00. Following the knockout of the fructose-1,6-bisphosphate aldolase gene yagE, the resulting strain M02 produced a BT yield of 0.82 g/L, a 41% decrease compared to M01. When both yagE and yjhH genes were knocked out in M03, the BT yield dropped to 0.55 g/L, a 60% decrease compared to the initial strain M00. These results suggest that knockout of the genes yagE and yjhH restricts the glycolytic pathway, thereby affecting cell growth and ultimately leading to decreased BT production. On the basis of M01, complete knockout of the genes yiaE and gcdW resulted in strain M04, which achieved a BT yield of 2.1 g/L, a 50% increase compared to M01. Further attempts to knock out yagE in M04 led to strain M05, which exhibited reduced cell growth and a BT yield of 0.78 g/L, a 44% decrease compared to M01 (Fig. 2B). Knockout of the yagE eliminated the alternative carbon utilization pathway for xylose, thereby compromising cell growth [30] and ultimately resulting in decreased BT production. Finally, Strain M04, which knocked out the genes xylAB, yiaE, and ycdW was selected for subsequent studies.

To promote the utilization of xylose, the xylose transporter (encoded by xylE) was overexpressed, resulting in strain M06. When cultivated in a fermentation medium containing 5 g/L glucose and 20 g/L xylose, strain M04 consumed negligible amounts of xylose within the first 6 h of fermentation, whereas strain M06 had already consumed a small amount of xylose. After 20 h, xylose was nearly undetectable in the fermentation broth of M06, while 2.4 g/L of xylose remained in the fermentation broth of M04 (Supplementary Fig. 1). This indicates that the overexpression of xylE enhanced the utilization of xylose as xylE is a member of the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) of transporters [31]. Ultimately, strain M06 achieved a BT yield of 2.54 g/L, representing a 20.9% increase compared to M04, while also exhibiting faster cell growth.

In order to optimize BT production by the recombinant E. coli, the fermentation conditions were optimized. The original fermentation conditions were as follows: an IPTG concentration of 1 mM, induction temperature of 37°C, induction OD600 of 0.6–0.8, with an initial addition of 5 g/L glucose and 20 g/L xylose. Firstly, the concentration of the inducer (IPTG) was optimized. Fermentation was carried out over a range of inducer concentrations from 0.25 mM to 2 mM (Fig. 3a). The highest BT yield of 2.63 g/L was achieved at an inducer concentration of 0.25 mM. There was no difference in the specific BT production capability per unit biomass (data not shown). The primary reason for the decreased yield at higher IPTG concentrations was the slower cell growth observed under these conditions. Induction was performed over a temperature range of 20–37°C, and the highest BT yield of 2.82 g/L was obtained at an induction temperature of 33°C (Fig. 3b). This may be attributed to the enhanced stability of intracellular enzymes at lower temperatures, which is conducive to efficient catalysis for BT production. When induction was initiated at an OD600 of 1.5, the highest BT yield of 2.85 g/L was achieved (Fig. 3c), corresponding to a specific BT yield of 120 mg/DCW (dry cell weight). Optimizing culture conditions significantly enhanced BT synthesis via recombinant pathways in E. coli [32], yielding 2.85 g/L of BT, which represents a 12.2% improvement compared to strain M04.

Effects of the glucose-xylose ratio on BT production

In the biosynthesis of BT from glucose-xylose dual carbon sources, glucose plays a dual role: supporting cell growth and supplying reducing equivalents for BT synthesis. The availability of glucose and the cellular NADH/NADPH ratio are therefore critical for the efficient operation of the xylose conversion pathway. [20]. To maximize xylose utilization for BT production and glucose-driven biomass formation in the dual-carbon-source system, optimizing the glucose-xylose ratio is essential. We first to optimized the fermentation process control of xylose. Initially, the timing of xylose addition was investigated within a range of 0 to 40 h. It was found that during the logarithmic growth phase at 3 h, the yield of BT synthesis reached its maximum of 3.08 g/L (Fig. 4a), which was coupled with the growth of the strain (Supplementary Fig. 2). This may be attributed to the requirement of NADH/NAD+ as cofactors in the BT synthesis process. As demonstrated in the study by Gao et al. (2019), the supplementation of additional NADH further enhanced BT production. During the logarithmic growth phase, the intracellular concentration of NADH may be more abundant compared to other periods, which likely facilitates the conversion of xylose to BT. Prolonging the logarithmic growth phase of the strain may potentially promote BT synthesis. Next, the impact of glucose concentration on strain growth and BT yield was explored by setting five concentration gradients of glucose ranging from 0 g/L to 15 g/L. A glucose concentration of 5 g/L was found to be optimal for both cell growth and BT production (Fig. 4b-c). Higher glucose concentrations suppressed xylose uptake due to robust CCR (Supplementary Fig. 3) and inhibited cell growth resulting from acetic acid accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 4). Besides, although xylose was completely consumed within 30 h at an initial glucose concentration of 15 g/L, no BT was produced, while acetic acid accumulated to nearly 8 g/L. When acetate concentration exceeds 5 g/L, it has a negative impact on cell growth and leads to a decrease in recombinant protein production [33], which in turn results in reduced BT yield. Since BT synthesis is coupled with cell growth, adding xylose too early will result in its utilization for cell growth, leading to a low product yield. Conversely, adding xylose too late, after the exponential growth phase, will also cause a decrease in BT production [14]. While glucose supplementation provides a carbon source for cell growth, and an appropriate concentration of glucose can prevent xylose from being consumed for cellular growth, excessively high glucose concentrations not only induce CCR that inhibits xylose uptake but also promote acetate accumulation, resulting in multiple adverse effects [34].

(A) The effect of xylose feeding time on BT concentration; Under different Gluose concentration: (B) Growth curve of M06, (C) BT synthesis curve of M06; The impact of varying fed-batch initiation times on the strain: (D) Growth curve of M06 (E) BT synthesis curve of M06.

To further mitigate the CCR effect caused by high glucose concentrations and the excessive production of acetate, the impact of fed-batch glucose addition on strain growth and BT synthesis was investigated: based on an initial glucose concentration of 5 g/L, glucose was supplemented to 2 g/L every 5 h starting at 10 h, 20 h, and 30 h, respectively, for a total of three additions. The addition of low-concentration glucose at 20 h and 30 h both significantly promoted strain growth and BT synthesis. Since BT synthesis is coupled with cell growth, the addition of glucose at 20 h, compared to 30 h, resulted in the greatest increase in BT yield. The BT titer reached 3.59 g/L, with a molar yield of 25%. Compared to the control group with only an initial addition of 5 g/L glucose, the BT yield increased by 31.02%, the molar yield increased by 29.1%, and the biomass increased by 36.3% (Fig. 4d-e). Compared to batch fermentation, fed-batch fermentation can maintain a lower glucose concentration, which helps alleviate the carbon catabolite repression (CCR) effect [35]. Moreover, as demonstrated in the above experiments, a lower glucose concentration can effectively reduce acetate formation, thereby avoiding various adverse effects. Regarding the feeding timing, the BT yield was optimal when glucose was supplemented at 20 h. Glucose supplementation at 10 h, when the xylose concentration had declined to approximately 7 g/L, triggered CCR, which consequently inhibited the residual xylose uptake. This suppression occurred during the mid-exponential phase and resulted in a more pronounced decline in BT production. Conversely, excessively delayed glucose addition failed to supply the necessary reducing equivalents in time to support BT biosynthesis [21].

Simultaneous saccharification of cellulose alleviates glucose-mediated CCR on xylose

Cellulose is the polymers of glucose, and these polysaccharides represent extremely abundant and renewable biomass resources. Utilizing glucose derived from cellulose hydrolysis for the production of BT offers significant potential for the effective utilization of agricultural waste as an alternative to non-renewable resources. Employing polymers as the carbohydrate source rather than monomers is advantageous for reducing catabolite repression [36]. Introducing the enzymatic hydrolysis of cellulose into the fermentation process provides a continuous supply of glucose to the microbial strain, enabling the SSF of cellulose. Currently, there are few studies on the concurrent application of cellulose for BT synthesis and existing research predominantly focuses on utilizing only the xylose fraction from lignocellulosic biomass as the substrate for BT production.

To further alleviate CCR in the dual-carbon-source utilization system for BT production, we investigated the effect of glucose supply via simultaneous saccharification of cellulose on BT synthesis. In this process, the hydrolysis of cellulose continuously provides glucose for the biosynthesis of BT, and the adjustment of the cellulose hydrolysis rate is crucial for effectively improving the efficiency of BT production. It has been reported [37] that the optimal temperature for cellulase activity ranges between 40°C and 50°C, while the optimal pH is between 4 and 5.5. To reconcile the mismatch between the optimal fermentation conditions and those required for cellulose hydrolysis, the pH and temperature of cellulose enzymatic hydrolysis were investigated. Cellulase (30 FPU/g cellulose) was added to a fermentation medium containing 20 g/L cellulose powder and 20 g/L xylose to hydrolyze the cellulose. The results indicated that under the optimal fermentation conditions of 33°C and pH 6, the glucose yield from cellulose hydrolysis was 0.49 g/g. Although this is lower than the glucose yield of 0.8 g/g obtained under the optimal enzymatic hydrolysis pH for cellulose (Fig. 5a), the hydrolysis of cellulose for 24 h still resulted in an accumulation of 9.74 g/L glucose (data not shown) under fermentation conditions. Experiments exploring glucose concentrations as described above demonstrated that the glucose produced was sufficient to meet the demands of the fermentation process. Within the first 10 h, 6.9 g/L of glucose was accumulated from cellulose hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, in the fed-batch experiments with glucose feeding, BT synthesis was inhibited when feeding was initiated at 10 h (Fig. 4e). Fermentation conditions of 33°C and pH 6.0 were selected for subsequent experiments.

To adjusting the hydrolysis rate of cellulose is evidently necessary. Given that cellulose is insoluble in water, the solid-to-liquid ratio significantly impacts mass transfer and enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency during fermentation. Under an enzyme loading of 30 FPU/g cellulose and with xylose as the substrate at a concentration of 20 g/L, the effects of different cellulose solid-to-liquid ratios (10%–60%) on BT synthesis by the strain were investigated. The highest BT yield was achieved at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 20% (corresponding to a cellulose concentration of 20 g/L), with a BT yield of 1.33 g/L after 66 h of fermentation (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, to avoid excessive hydrolysis of cellulose that could lead to an overabundance of glucose and inhibit BT production, the impact of varying cellulase loading levels (15–35 FPU/g cellulose) on BT synthesis was examined. The results indicated that a cellulase loading of 25 FPU/g cellulose resulted in the highest BT yield of 3.93 g/L (Fig. 5C). In the process of SSF of cellulose, to prevent prolonged lag phases post-inoculation that could lead to glucose accumulation, the effects of different initial inoculation OD600 values (0.1–2) on BT synthesis were explored. An initial OD600 of 0.5 resulted in the highest final BT yield of 4.48 g/L, with a molar yield of BT of 31.7% (Fig. 5D). When comparing fed-batch glucose fermentation with SSF of cellulose (Fig. 5E–F), the former resulted in glucose depletion at 10 h, causing xylose to be utilized for growth and rapid consumption, thereby reducing BT yield. In contrast, during SSF of cellulose, continuous hydrolysis of cellulose provided a sustained supply of glucose at low concentrations, with xylose primarily being utilized for BT synthesis (Fig. 5F). Moreover, SSF strategy enhanced the strain's growth rate and extended its growth phase (Fig. 5E) by continuously providing low-concentration glucose, which is consistent with our previous conclusion that BT production is coupled with cell growth. In summary, the SSF strategy continuously supplying low concentrations of glucose achieved a lower CCR effect while preventing xylose from being consumed for cellular growth. Moreover, this low-concentration continuous glucose feeding promoted cell growth, ultimately enhancing the yield of BT [38].

Biosynthesis of BT via a cellulose-xylan co-utilization strategy

Xylan is widely present in plant tissues and constitutes approximately 35% of the dry weight of plants, representing the main component of hemicellulose [36,39]. Utilizing xylan hydrolysis to provide xylose offers significant advantages in terms of sustainability, cost control, and process innovation, especially in the utilization of agricultural waste, where it holds great potential. Given that the optimal temperature for xylanase activity is 55°C, the hydrolysis of xylan at different temperatures for 24 h was investigated under conditions of 30 g/L xylan concentration and an enzyme loading of 750 U/g. At the fermentation temperature of 33°C, the enzyme activity was relatively poor, with a yield of only 8.4 g/L with 0.28 g/g xylan, which is 66% lower than that at 55°C (24.3 g/L 0.81 g/g) (Fig. 6A). Since the synthesis of BT is coupled with cell growth and mainly occurs in the exponential phase of fermentation, xylose was pre-hydrolyzed at 55°C to ensure an adequate supply of xylose for BT synthesis. Further investigation into the impact of enzyme loading on xylan hydrolysis revealed that, at an enzyme loading of 750 U/g, the xylose yield reached its highest value of 0.81 g/g (Fig. 6B). For hydrolysis time, although 16 and 24 h resulted in satisfactory xylose levels, an extended hydrolysis process would prolong the fermentation cycle. Thus hydrolysis 6 h which 12.84 g/L of xylose had already been produced (Fig. 6C) but also during the BT fermentation process, xylan could still be slowly hydrolyzed to provide xylose at 33°C. Therefore, a pre-hydrolysis time of 6 h for xylan was adopted.

Simultaneous fermentation of cellulose-xylan hydrolysis. (A) Yield of xylose from xylan hydrolysis at different temperatures; (B) The influence of xylanase loading on the yield of xylose from xylan hydrolysis; (C) Xylose concentration resulting from xylan hydrolysis at different time intervals; (D) Different concentrations of xylan.

After pre-hydrolyzing xylan for 6 h, a secondary seed culture and cellulase were inoculated into media containing different concentrations of xylan to initiate the simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation of cellulose and xylan. Following 66 h of fermentation, the highest yield of BT was observed in the medium with a xylan concentration of 30 g/L, reaching 1.35 g/L (Fig. 6D). However, based on the SSF experiments, the theoretical yield should be greater than 2.7 g/L. We speculate that, in the cellulose-xylan co-SSF system, the lower titer may be attributed to multiple factors beyond the potential CCR effect [40]. These may include: asynchronous release kinetics of glucose and xylose within the multi-enzyme system; the increased proportion of water-insoluble substrates following xylan addition; and potential inhibition of cells or enzymatic activity [41]. Consequently, the strategy for BT synthesis from cellulose-xylan mixtures requires more in-depth investigation.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced a heterologous BT synthesis pathway, knocked out xylA, yiaE, and ycdW, and overexpressed the xylose transporter xylE to construct recombinant strain M06 for BT production, achieving a BT yield of 2.54 g/L. Next, the fermentation conditions for shake flask cultivation were optimized to efficiently produce BT. consequently, the fermentation conditions were: inducer concentration of 0.25 mmol/L, induction OD600 of 1.5, and induction temperature of 33°C, yielding a BT production of 2.63 g/L. For glucose-xylose ratio impact BT synthesis. The timing of xylose addition was also optimized, finding that the maximum molar yield of BT (23%) was achieved when glucose was added during the logarithmic growth phase (3 h), and the highest BT yield was obtained with an initial glucose concentration of 5 g/L. To avoid acetic acid accumulation and CCR cause by high glucose concentration, a fed-batch strategy was employed, with a feeding operation after 20 h, resulting in the greatest increase in BT yield, with a titer of 3.59 g/L. By regulating glucose supply through SSF of cellulose to further relive CCR, the BT yield was increased to 4.48 g/L, with a molar yield of BT at 31.7%. Finally, the SSF of cellulose and xylan for 66 h produced 1.35 g/L of BT.

The highest BT titer achieved in this study was 4.48 g/L using the SSF strategy. Compared to the approach reported by Cao et al. (2015), which employed xylose as the substrate and glycerol as a co-carbon source to circumvent CCR (yielding 3.92 g/L BT with a molar yield of 27.7%), our cellulose SSF-based CCR mitigation strategy increased the BT titer by 14.38% and raised the molar yield to 31.7%, representing a 4-percentage-point improvement. This result directly validates the effectiveness of our strategy in alleviating CCR by controlling the glucose release rate via SSF. Furthermore, compared to the direct fermentation using a glucose-xylose mixed carbon source described by Zhang et al. (2024), our SSF process achieved a 13.7% higher molar yield under similar carbon source conditions, further highlighting the advantage of the SSF strategy for synergistic co-utilization of mixed sugars. It is important to note that all results in this work were obtained at the shake-flask scale. Transitioning to industrial production requires systematic validation at different scales [42]. The intense agitation and aeration in fermenters can significantly accelerate cellulose hydrolysis, potentially leading to excessively rapid glucose release at the initial stage. This may re-trigger CCR or cause challenges related to uneven mass transfer. Therefore, during scale-up, it may be necessary to regulate the hydrolysis rate by optimizing process parameters, such as agitation speed and enzyme feeding strategies [43].

More importantly, a preliminary economic analysis indicates that cellulase costs account for over 50% of the total production cost in this process, which points to a critical direction for future optimization to enhance economic feasibility. To improve commercial viability, future research could focus on: 1) Reducing enzyme cost: by screening or engineering cheaper enzyme cocktails with higher activity and stability, or employing enzyme immobilization techniques to increase their reusability, thereby significantly reducing the enzyme consumption cost per fermentation batch [43]; 2) Process integration and optimization: exploring more efficient coupling of enzymatic hydrolysis, saccharification, and fermentation at the bioreactor scale to increase product titers while reducing energy and material costs [44].

This study demonstrates that a cellulose SSF strategy can contribute to alleviating the CCR effect. Building on this, the feasibility of BT production via cellulose-xylan co-utilization was investigated. Although the yield achieved with lignocellulosic polymers indicates a need for further strain and process optimization, the results demonstrate the potential for producing BT from lignocellulose, and may provide insights for future optimization.
