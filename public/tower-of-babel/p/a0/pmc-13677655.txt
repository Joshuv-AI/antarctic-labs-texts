Validation of the Cultural Values Scale (CV Scale) in Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Isaac Wasswa Katono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost cross-cultural research in Africa often relies on tools developed in Western contexts, yet these instruments are not universally valid. The Cultural Values Scale (CVS), though widely used, has not been validated in Uganda or the broader East African region. The objective of this study was to psychometrically evaluate the Cultural Values Scale (CVS) as a first step toward its potential application in the East African context.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 324 final-year students from business and non-business programs at a university in Uganda. The validation process involved exploratory factor analysis (EFA), reliability testing, convergent and discriminant validity assessment, split-sample cross-validation through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), measurement invariance testing, and hierarchical regression to establish predictive validity.Results• Exploratory factor analysis: Produced a five-factor solution consistent with the theoretical model.• Reliability: Internal consistency was high across dimensions (Cronbach’s α = 0.81–0.92).• Convergent and discriminant validity: Convergent validity was achieved (Average Variance Extracted (AVE) > 0.50), and discriminant validity was confirmed, as the square root of each construct’s AVE exceeded its correlations with all other constructs.• Split-sample cross-validation (CFA): Dividing the sample into two equal groups demonstrated model stability across subsamples, with acceptable fit indices (Group 1: CFI = 0.90, RMSEA = 0.080; Group 2: CFI = 0.903, RMSEA = 0.042).• Measurement invariance: Configural invariance was established across gender groups (CFI = 0.901; RMSEA = 0.049), followed by partial metric invariance after freeing two masculinity items, and partial scalar invariance after intercept adjustments, enabling meaningful latent mean comparisons.• Predictive validity: Hierarchical regression revealed that collectivism (β = .62, p <.001), uncertainty avoidance (β = .57, p <.001), and long-term orientation (β = .51, p <.001) significantly predicted entrepreneurial intentions within a multivariate model, jointly explaining 66.1% of the variance. Notably, uncertainty avoidance showed a positive association consistent with adaptive resilience in collectivist contexts characterized by uncertainty.ConclusionThe Cultural Values Scale demonstrated robust psychometric properties using Ugandan data. This validation provides a reliable tool for studying cultural influences on entrepreneurial intentions and lays a solid foundation for future scale validations, localized cultural research, and entrepreneurial training initiatives across East Africa.

Introduction

The influence of culture in the lives of individuals as well as communities is a widely recognized fact (Zouari & Shtudiner, 2024). Due to the importance of culture, Hagen (1962) criticizes economists for neglecting the role of culture in the economic development process, though some economists are even proud of this inattention (Tshikuku, 2001). Recently, there has been an increased interest in cross-cultural research, though most of these studies have been conducted in the industrialized world and some emerging economies like China (Miao & Zhang, 2024). However, there has been a surge of scholarly interest in African cultural values, as evidenced by the work of organizations like Afrobarometer, which conducts pan-African surveys on social, political, and economic issues (Afrobarometer, 2024). At the societal level, culture refers to the shared norms, values, beliefs, and practices that characterize a group of people within a specific geographic region (Dickson et al., 2003). In the present study, culture is conceptualized as “the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one human group from another” (Hofstede, 1980, p. 25). It is understood as a dynamic and evolving process situated within a given society, shaping a shared cognitive framework of beliefs, values, meanings, and attitudes. This framework informs and directs individuals’ perceptions, thought patterns, reasoning, behaviors, responses, and social interactions.

When a study is designed toward an individual level of analysis, scales developed to measure cultural orientation should be used (Konig et al., 2009). Personal cultural orientation refers to individual-level variations in cultural values, beliefs, and behaviors that distinguish one person from another within the same cultural context. Unlike broader, national-level cultural dimensions, personal cultural orientation provides a more granular understanding of how cultural values manifest in personal attitudes and actions. These orientations are influenced by a variety of factors, including personal experiences, education, social environment, and individual personality traits (Sharma, 2010). Unfortunately, while many instruments have been developed to measure cultural orientation, most were designed in a developed-country context and have not been validated in sub-Saharan Africa. Hofstede’s original framework was developed to describe national-level cultural tendencies, whereas instruments such as the Cultural Values Scale operationalize these dimensions at the individual level. This distinction is critical for avoiding ecological fallacy and for examining how cultural orientations influence personal attitudes and behaviors within heterogeneous populations.

Veloutsou et al. (2005), assert that multi-item measures developed from a given context should not be considered universally reliable and valid, hence measures developed for a particular population may have to be redesigned for other populations (Churchill & Peter, 1984). In order to understand and measure the influence of culture in sub-Saharan Africa—an area often neglected by cross-cultural management scholars (Jackson, 2013), there is a need for cross-validation of some of these instruments. Specifically, many African management scholars (e.g., Kamoche, 2011; Zoogah, 2013) emphasize that Western theories often fail to capture the communal, relational, and context-specific realities of African organizational environments. In Uganda, where Ubuntu—“I am because we are, and since we are, therefore I am” (Mbiti, 1969)—anchors identity in communal solidarity and collective survival, entrepreneurship is often shaped by collective responsibility and adaptive responses to uncertainty. This interpretation is offered as a contextual implication rather than a formal hypothesis (Bigos & Mika, 2014; Sau, 2025; Oppong, 2021). In this Ubuntu-driven, type of entrepreneurship group welfare takes precedence over individual goals, measurement instruments must be empirically validated within local contexts to ensure that culturally embedded meanings and behavioral patterns are accurately captured., This issue has significant implications in fields where understanding cultural orientations is vital for crafting effective, culturally grounded strategies and policies to mitigate society challenges.

Yoo et al. (2011) developed a 26 item CV scale, tool to assess individual cultural orientations based on Hofstede's five dimensions of culture: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, collectivism, long-term orientation, and masculinity. As stated above, measuring cultural dimensions at the individual level, the CVSCALE reduces the risk of ecological fallacy that arises when national averages are applied to individuals. The Cultural Values Scale was selected over alternative frameworks such as the GLOBE model because it provides a concise, psychometrically validated instrument specifically designed for individual-level measurement in survey-based research. The instrument measures cultural orientations in their experienced or perceived form ("As Is") rather than normative expectations ("As Should Be"), ensuring that responses reflect current behavioral tendencies. This tool has been validated in Thailand (Prasongsukarn, 2009). In this case the tool has exhibited strong psychometric properties. More recently, to address the scarcity of African-specific cultural measurement tools, another cultural scale was developed in Cameroon (Ngueutsa et al., 2021b). While prior validation in Thailand has shown strong psychometric properties, comparable validation evidence remains limited in East African contexts. Cultural values are expected to influence entrepreneurial intentions by shaping risk perception, goal orientation, and decision-making behavior. Understanding these relationships requires reliable measurement instruments capable of capturing individual-level cultural orientations.

Building on the preceding discussion, the present study conducted in Uganda pursued four primary objectives. The first is to validate the CV scale through configural invariance, while the second one is to examine whether different genders perceive the items of this tool similarly through a test of equal factor loadings, or metric invariance (Brown, 2006). The third is to compare latent mean scores of cultural variables across genders through the establishment of scalar invariance, while the fourth is to examine the predictive validity of this tool.

More broadly, the study seeks to contribute to the theoretical framework of cross-cultural research by reducing dependence on unvalidated Western instruments, which may lead to biases in results. The validation of the CVS using data from Uganda provides the East African region with a psychometrically valid cultural instrument, which should support more accurate theorizing in disciplines such as social sciences, business, and development studies. The rest of the paper is structured thus: The next section presents the literature review and theoretical framework, while section three presents the methodology used to conduct the study. Section four presents the results, while section five sums up the study with a conclusion, implications and suggestions for future research.

Literature review

Dimensions of culture: a global and african perspective

A systematic approach to the analysis of societal and individual values, beliefs, norms, and practices is required, in order to understand what culture is. Hofstede’s conceptualization of culture stands out as one of the most powerful articulations of the cultural phenomenon, explaining the rationale underlying both social and economic human interactions (Feldman, 2019). Recent studies have extended Hofstede’s work to better represent African cultural diversity, emphasizing the role of sociocultural systems in shaping leadership, entrepreneurship, and decision-making (Shayo, 2023). Hofstede’s cultural dimensions are discussed briefly below:

Hofstede conceptualizes culture in terms of five dimensions: Power distance is the extent to which power is distributed across members of a culture (Bond, 1986). Power distance implies that inequalities exist and are accepted (Saffu, 2003). Individualism and its counterpart, collectivism, describe the extent to which people are integrated into social groups. In societies characterized by individualism, personal objectives take priority over collective aims, and self-interest is regarded as paramount (Triandis, 1995). Masculinity refers to overall toughness and competitiveness of a society (Saffu, 2003). It is the extent to which achievement and aggression are valued (Hofstede, 1980). Masculinity is characterized by assertiveness, which is not encouraged in feminine cultures due to its dysfunctional effect (Hofstede, 1991). Uncertainty avoidance is an expression of a society's tolerance for uncertainty and ambiguity, indicating the extent to which a culture programs its members to feel either uncomfortable or comfortable in unstructured situations i.e., different from the usual situation (Inkeles & Levison, 1969). Tolerance for ambiguity is the extent to which a society is open to change and innovation (Kedia & Bhagat, 1988), which has strong implications for entrepreneurship. According to Żemojtel-Piotrowska and Piotrowski (2023), long-term orientation emphasizes thrift and perseverance, while short-term orientation highlights respect for tradition, social obligations, and protecting one’s “face.”

Hofstede’s model continues to be criticised methodologically and theoretically (McSweeney, 2002; Ouyang et al., 2025). Key areas of concern at policy level are the framework’s generalizability, level of analysis, and the validity of its constructs (Bolzonella 2024). More recently, the model has been critiqued for its inability to address gender perspectives, noting that the masculinity–femininity dimension inadequately captures contemporary understandings of gender and cultural differences (Vollmann et al., 2023; Żemojtel-Piotrowska & Piotrowski, 2023). Yet other scholars argue that Hofstede’s framework fails to provide strategic direction in complex, multi-layered, and multicultural contexts (McSweeney, 2002). Nevertheless, as Jackson (2011) observes, much of the critique has not yet produced a fundamentally new paradigm to replace Hofstede’s influential contribution. While Hofstede’s framework was originally developed to describe national-level cultural patterns, subsequent research has adapted these dimensions for individual-level measurement using psychometrically validated survey instruments. This transition allows researchers to examine within-country variation in cultural orientations and reduces the risk of ecological fallacy.

Alternative cultural frameworks: the role of self-construal theory

The Markus and Kitayama (1991) Independent vs. Interdependent Self-Construal Theory explains how individuals define themselves within social contexts through their relationships with others. Their model proposes two forms of self-construal: independent and interdependent. In the former, people see themselves as distinct entities, prioritizing personal autonomy, achievements, and self-expression. This stance is the case in individualist cultures, where people emphasize their own thoughts, feelings, and goals (Krammer& Bessen, 2007). The latter is an interdependent self-construal in which people view themselves as fundamentally connected to others, giving precedence to group harmony and social roles. This stance is found in collectivist cultures, where people emphasize social cohesion and collective welfare (Aaker & Schmidt, 2001). The Markus and Kitayama model supports the notion that individuals within the same national culture can exhibit varying degrees of independence or interdependence (Kim & Drolet, 2003). Such variations highlight the importance of measuring individual-level cultural orientations alongside societal-level analyses (Oyserman et al., 2002).

"As Is" and "As Should Be" in cultural measurement

The GLOBE study (House et al., 2004) study advanced the notion of "As Is" (practices) and "As Should Be" (values) to mitigate some of the limitations in Hofstede’s approach (Frese, 2015). This approach should make it possible for scholars to examine both the current behaviors and norms people follow in daily life (As Is) and the aspirational cultural values they look forward to (As Should Be). In the present study, the Cultural Values Scale adopts the "As Is" orientation, focusing on respondents’ experienced cultural behaviors rather than normative expectations. This approach ensures that the measurement reflects observable attitudes and decision-making tendencies.

As is and As should be

Distinguishing "As Is" from "As Should Be"

The 'As Is' stance hinges on understanding the descriptive dimension of social norms, examining how individuals behave and what their beliefs are within their daily societal environment. This orientation prioritises the observation of behaviors and beliefs as they take place in daily life, influenced by real-world interactions, institutional frameworks, and immediate surroundings, rather than focusing on idealized or aspired conduct (Bicchieri, 2016). On the other hand, "As Should Be” refers to the desired position in terms of values and norms i.e. those that a society believes should be ideally followed. This notion underscores the understanding of societal aspirations rather than lived experiences, thereby distinguishing between current cultural states and long-term ideological intentions (Stephenson, 2023). Through the separation of practices from values, GLOBE seeks to examine both the present realities and future aspirations of a society, thus providing a more nuanced understanding of the drivers of behavioral changes in society. This distinction is particularly relevant for studies examining behavioral outcomes, such as entrepreneurial intentions, where actual practices are more predictive than aspirational values.

Comparing Hofstede and GLOBE

Despite the merits of the GLOBE approach, scholars remain divided on the issue of values vs. practices. In particular, scholars who compare Hofstede’s and GLOBE’s cultural theories often arrive at contradictory conclusions (Venaik & Brewer, 2008). The lack of consensus in their findings further affirms the complexity of measuring culture across diverse societies, historical contexts, and economic conditions (Adamovic, 2023). Despite the conceptual richness of the GLOBE framework, its extensive dimensional structure often requires large datasets and complex modeling procedures. For survey-based validation studies with moderate sample sizes, concise instruments such as the Cultural Values Scale provide a more practical and psychometrically efficient alternative (Dhingra et al., 2015).

The debate on cultural models

Despite criticisms and a lack of a unified position on a number of issues, researchers concur that cross-cultural scholars now have multiple measurement options i.e., they can continue using Hofstede’s model, which remains widely validated (Gerlach &Eriksson, 2021) or adopt the more complex nine-dimensional framework from the GLOBE study (House et al., 2004). The other option is to employ hybrid models that integrate elements of both (Tung & Verbeke, 2010). Hence researchers call for more empirical research in the bid to understand why values and practices exhibit complex relationships given that cultural practices and values remain highly context-dependent, making it difficult to apply rigid judgments universally (Davidov et al., 2018).

Refining cultural theories

The aim of scholarly critiques is not to disprove or dismiss cultural models but rather to enhance their applicability to transitional societies (Nicholas et al., 2024). The purpose is to improve the accuracy, predictive power, and relevance of cultural theories, thereby ensuring their continued usefulness for academic, business, and policy applications (Inglehart, 2020). Despite long-standing disagreements regarding Hofstede’s work, recent research continues to confirm the construct validity and relevance of his cultural dimensions in business and management studies (Minkov & Kaasa, 2022). Many new studies have been anchored in this conceptualization, such as the Cultural Autonomy and Cultural Dimensions in Africa project, which investigates Afrobarometer-derived scores on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (focusing on individualism/collectivism, power distance, indulgence, and flexibility) across 36 African countries and more than 200 ethnolinguistic groups (van Pinxteren, 2020). More recently, Żemojtel-Piotrowska and Piotrowski (2023) analyzed Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, emphasizing their continued relevance in cross-cultural research, while Minkov and Kaasa (2021) introduced methodological refinements that reinforce the enduring applicability of Hofstede’s model. Furthermore, Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck’s (1961) “relationship orientations” are reflected in Hofstede’s dimensions of individualism and power distance (Adamovic, 2023). As Minkov and Hofstede (2011, p. 17) note, “The enormous popularity of Hofstede’s model is not due to the fact that it is the absolutely right one or the true one. It stems from the model’s coherence and predictive ability.”

Building on this theoretical foundation, the present study examines whether individual-level cultural dimensions measured using the Cultural Values Scale demonstrate reliable psychometric properties and meaningful predictive relationships with entrepreneurial intentions in the Ugandan context.

Cultural dimensions such as collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term orientation are expected to influence entrepreneurial intentions by shaping risk tolerance, planning behavior, and goal orientation. However, the validity of these relationships depends on the availability of reliable measurement instruments capable of capturing individual-level cultural orientations. Therefore, validating the Cultural Values Scale in the Ugandan context is a necessary step before examining its predictive utility.

Study conceptual model

Based on the theoretical insights enumerated above, the present study hypothesises that individual-level cultural orientations shape entrepreneurial intentions through cognitive and motivational pathways consistent with cross-cultural entrepreneurship theory, such as the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1985). Cultural values such as collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term orientation have been linked to entrepreneurial behavior through mechanisms including social support, structured planning, and future-oriented decision-making (Bachkirov et al., 2018; Triandis, 1995). Accordingly, this study conceptualizes entrepreneurial intentions as a function of individual cultural dimensions measured using the Cultural Values Scale (CVS). Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The study utilized a cross-sectional survey research design, and was conducted in a higher-education institution located in the central region of Uganda.

Sample size determination

Since the study was to employ CFA and SEM, guidelines for sample size using these methodologies were followed (Brown, 2006). Hence based on these guidelines, the study determined a minimum required sample size for CFA/SEM, meaning any sample larger than this threshold would be considered acceptable. The non-centrality parameter (NCP) measures how much a statistical model deviates from the null hypothesis (Brown, 2006). In structural equation modeling (SEM), the NCP concept helps assess model fit and statistical power. Achieving sufficient power requires an adequate sample size, and a minimum of 125 participants is recommended to reach the commonly accepted 0.8 power level. A higher NCP improves the ability to detect significant effects, while a smaller sample size may lead to a lower NCP, reducing the model’s effectiveness in identifying meaningful relationships.

Study Population and sampling

Study population

The study population comprised all students enrolled at a private university in Uganda. Approximately 16,000 students were registered at the institution in 2024. Final-year students, numbering around 4,000, were eligible to participate in the study. A sample of 324 students was included, determined by sample size requirements for the planned analyses.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited from various courses after lecturers introduced the study during class sessions.

Sample characteristics

The number of participants included in the invariance tests varied. While the overall sample size was 324, the number included in specific invariance tests was lower in some cases due to missing gender data. Additionally, the higher proportion of male students in certain programs influenced the group sizes in the invariance testing.

Sampling strategy: The study employed a convenience sampling approach.

Measures to reduce bias

Common method bias occurs when variations in item or construct correlations stem from the measurement approach rather than the latent factors (Brown, 2006). To mitigate this, the study design incorporated several procedural remedies, including assuring respondents of confidentiality and anonymity, and emphasizing that there are no right or wrong answers. Common method bias was further assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. The first unrotated factor accounted for less than 50% of the total variance, indicating that common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern in this study.

Data collection and measures

Data collection

Questionnaires containing the CV scale and EI measures (Linan & Chen, 2009) were administered to these students by their professors during class.

The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional research.

Undergraduate students from various disciplines were requested to participate in the study. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was implied through completion of the questionnaire following an explanation of the study purpose. Students had to be in their final year in order to be eligible to participate in the study. Hence data was collected from them, between July and December 2024 using the CV Scale which consists of 26 items measuring the five dimensions discussed above, on a scale of 1 strongly disagree to 5 strongly agree. To examine the predictive ability of this scale, data was collected on the entrepreneurial intentions of the respondents using four validated items, also measured on a scale of 1 strongly disagree to 5 strongly agree (Linan & Chen, 2009). The questionnaire was administered following the validation guidelines of Crunchinho et al. (2024). Missing data were handled using the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm, which provides unbiased parameter estimates under the assumption of Missing at Random (MAR) (Enders, 2010; Rubin, 1976). To establish robust psychometric properties, the study followed a six-step validation process using SPSS and AMOS 28.

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 for exploratory factor analysis and reliability testing, and AMOS version 28 for confirmatory factor analysis, measurement invariance testing, and structural modeling.

Since the study aimed at validating the psychometric properties of the Cultural Values Scale rather than estimate national cultural parameters, statistical weighting was not applied. Instead, regional benchmarking was used to assess contextual alignment with established cultural patterns.

Results and discussion

Descriptive statistics

The study collected data from a total sample of 324 students, comprising business students (N = 178) enrolled in programs such as Bachelor of Business Administration (BBA) and Bachelor of Science in Accounting and Finance, and non-business students (N = 146) drawn from Law and Social Science disciplines. The mean age of respondents was 24.9 years (SD = 5.3). The sample included 150 male students and 119 female students, while 55 respondents did not indicate their gender.

Cross-cultural comparison with the cameroon validation study

This divergence highlights the methodological distinction between applying an established cross-cultural instrument (CVSCALE) and constructing a localized measure tailored to specific cultural practices. In Uganda, Uncertainty Avoidance and Long-Term Orientation exhibited particularly high factor loadings (ranging from.781 to.862 and.691 to.874, respectively), underscoring a strong emphasis on structured decision-making and future-oriented planning. By contrast, the Cameroonian factors emphasized ritual practices and cultural identity markers, reflecting the salience of traditional ceremonies and language use in everyday life.

The factor structure obtained in the present Ugandan study demonstrates both convergence and divergence when compared with the cultural values validation conducted in Cameroon by Ngueutsa, Wassouo, and Kouabenan (2021b). Whereas the Ugandan sample yielded five distinct dimensions consistent with Hofstede’s framework—Collectivism, Masculinity, Uncertainty Avoidance, Power Distance, and Long-Term Orientation—the Cameroonian study identified three broader cultural factors: (1) practice of cultural activities related to protection and celebration of life (e.g., rites dedicated to ancestors, purification ceremonies, traditional healing), (2) practice of activities expressing cultural identity and belonging (e.g., learning and using the mother tongue), and (3) practice of cultural activities related to the manifestation of joy (e.g., dowry, traditional marriage, engagement rituals). Measurement invariance and cross-validation

Measurement invariance (MI) refers to the degree to which a measurement instrument assesses the same construct across different groups or conditions (Horn & McArdle, 1992). Establishing measurement invariance is essential for ensuring that observed differences between groups reflect true differences in the latent constructs rather than variations in measurement properties.

Four hierarchical forms of measurement invariance are commonly examined in structural equation modeling (Brown, 2006):

To evaluate the stability and generalizability of the measurement model, the sample was divided into subgroups and the model was fitted across these groups. This cross-validation procedure is widely recommended in structural equation modeling to assess model robustness and replicability (Hair et al., 2019; Bolton & Long, 1993).

Conclusion and contributions

This study set out to cross-validate the Cultural Values Scale (CVS; Yoo et al., 2011) using Ugandan data, given East Africa’s distinct socio-cultural landscape characterized by a fusion of customary norms, mixed institutional legacies, and relationship-centred cultural values which make Western-constructed instruments amenable to construct bias and ecological fallacy when applied without validation (Hofstede, 2001; Ngueutsa et al., 2021a).

The findings demonstrate that the CVS is a robust psychometrically valid instrument within this context. Evidence of strong convergent and discriminant validity, along with satisfactory reliability, confirms the scale’s structural integrity and measurement consistency among Ugandan respondents.

The study achieved configural invariance, confirming the theoretical stability of the five-factor structure across gender groups. Furthermore, both partial metric and partial scalar invariance were established, enabling meaningful comparison of latent cultural constructs between male and female students. Achieving partial invariance is consistent with cross-cultural psychometric research, where certain culturally sensitive items may function differently across groups without undermining the overall validity of the measurement model (Byrne, 2016).

Importantly, the need to free two masculinity items during invariance testing suggests that gender-role expectations embedded within these items may be interpreted differently in the Ugandan socio-cultural context. Rather than indicating measurement failure, this finding highlights the contextual sensitivity of gender-related constructs and underscores the importance of validating cultural instruments within local settings. In this context, masculinity may reflect achievement orientation, assertiveness, and goal-directed behavior rather than traditional gender dominance, thereby explaining why female respondents demonstrated comparable or higher scores on certain masculinity indicators. This interpretation aligns with contemporary perspectives that conceptualize masculinity as a behavioral orientation rather than a strictly gendered attribute.

The predictive validity analysis further strengthens confidence in the CVS by demonstrating that individual-level cultural orientations significantly predict entrepreneurial intentions. The hierarchical regression model showed that cultural dimensions jointly explained a substantial proportion of variance in entrepreneurial intentions (Adjusted R2 =.661). While this level of explained variance appears high, it reflects the combined predictive power of multiple theoretically related cultural constructs rather than the effect of a single variable. Such results are consistent with multivariate behavioral models in which cognitive, motivational, and social influences operate simultaneously to shape entrepreneurial decision-making.

Among the cultural dimensions, collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term orientation emerged as significant predictors of entrepreneurial intentions, suggesting that entrepreneurship in this context is strongly embedded within social networks, structured planning, and future-oriented decision-making. These findings support the growing body of research indicating that uncertainty avoidance does not necessarily suppress entrepreneurial behavior; rather, individuals in high-uncertainty environments may develop adaptive planning strategies to manage risk and navigate volatile economic conditions (Oppong, 2021). This interpretation should therefore be understood as a contextual implication of the findings rather than a formal hypothesis tested in this study. Hence the cross-validation of this instrument beyond WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (Henrich et al., 2010) and emerging Asian contexts re-positions Hofstede’s framework within Sub-Saharan Africa, enabling a deep understanding of cultural artifacts that shape behavior, cross-cultural research and policy in this region.

Study limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study utilized a convenience sample drawn from a single public university, which may limit the cultural and socioeconomic generalizability of the results. Although the sample provided an appropriate context for initial validation of the Cultural Values Scale (CVS) in Uganda, it does not fully capture the diversity of cultural orientations across the broader Ugandan population or across different institutional, regional, and socioeconomic contexts. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary validation evidence within a specific institutional setting rather than population-level estimates of cultural values.

Second, while the possibility of weighting or benchmarking against national population data was considered in order to enhance representativeness, the available demographic benchmarks were not sufficiently aligned with the cultural constructs measured in this study to permit reliable weighting procedures. In particular, national datasets provide demographic distributions (e.g., age, gender, region), but do not include population-level measures of cultural value dimensions comparable to those assessed using the CVS. As a result, statistical weighting would not have meaningfully improved construct-level generalizability and could have introduced additional bias. This limitation has been acknowledged to ensure transparency regarding the scope of inference.

Third, the study employed a cross-sectional research design, which limits the ability to examine changes in cultural orientations over time or to establish causal relationships between cultural values and behavioral outcomes such as entrepreneurial orientation. Cultural values are dynamic and may evolve in response to social, economic, and institutional transformations. Longitudinal research designs would therefore provide stronger evidence regarding the stability and predictive validity of the CVS across time.

Fourth, although the study demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties for the CVS in the Ugandan context, further validation is required across multiple institutions, regions, and cultural settings to establish broader measurement equivalence and generalizability. Replication studies using probability-based sampling methods would strengthen confidence in the robustness of the scale and enhance its applicability across diverse African populations.

Suggestions for future research

Future research should expand validation of the Cultural Values Scale across diverse African regions to establish broader cross-cultural measurement stability and enhance the generalizability of cultural research within the continent. Researchers should also examine how cultural dimensions interact with socio-economic and institutional factors such as access to capital, labor market conditions, and educational systems to shape entrepreneurial intentions and business outcomes.

Longitudinal research designs are particularly recommended to track shifts in cultural orientations over time and to assess how these changes influence entrepreneurship, innovation, and sustainable development in emerging economies. Such designs would provide stronger evidence regarding the temporal stability of cultural constructs and their predictive relevance for behavioral outcomes.

Methodologically, future studies should refine measurement approaches by employing advanced statistical techniques (Lai et al., 2022) such as Bayesian estimation, multilevel modeling, and cross-national invariance testing to strengthen the psychometric robustness of cultural instruments. Additionally, sector-specific research may provide valuable insights by comparing how cultural values influence entrepreneurial success across industries such as technology, agriculture, manufacturing, and services, thereby informing targeted policy and training.
