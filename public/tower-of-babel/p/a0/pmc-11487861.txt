Emergency treatment of tongue bite after rat poison poisoning in a patient with recurrent depressive disorder: a case report
Authors: Meiyu Shen, Yingchao Huo, Lijuan Zhang, Ying Yang, Yu Fang, Xiaolin Tan, Zhiying Wan, Xiaofen Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent depressive disorder (RDD) is mainly manifested as a low mood, negative and pessimistic mood, and often presents suicidal tendencies when severe. This study reports on a patient with RDD who experienced rat poison poisoning occurring tongue biting. The patient’s psychiatric symptoms improved after receiving emergency management and subsequent combined medical care.Case presentationThe patient was a 51-year-old female with RDD complicated with rat poison poisoning. Tongue bite occurred on the third day of hospitalization. She received emergency treatment. Depression, anxiety and suicide risk assessment, safety management and caregiver-health education were conducted on the day of admission and before discharge. At the follow-up after discharge, the risk of suicide was reduced.ConclusionsSuicide in patients with RDD is characterized by repetition and complexity. The occurrence of clinical cases involving tongue bite after gastric lavage is rare. Suicide assessment and emergency management play an auxiliary role in the treatment of patients with recurrent depressive disorder.

Background

Recurrent depressive disorder (RDD) refers to a history of depressive episodes marked by recurrent depressive episodes, without independent episodes of elevated mood and hyperactivity that meet the criteria for mania. It is mainly manifested as a low mood, negative and pessimistic mood, and often presents suicidal tendencies when it becomes severe [1]. There have been a few cases of relapsing depression patients taking rat poison “smell death” and tongue biting. The clinical diagnosis and treatment of tongue bite after rat poison poisoning in a patient with recurrent depressive disorder in our hospital is reported as follows, combined with literature analysis.

Case presentation

The patient was a 51-year-old female. She had a history of postpartum depression in 2000 and had not received systematical treatment. She had three episodes of episodic depression without obvious inducement in April 2000, September 2018, and June 2020, respectively. And she had a previous history of several attempted suicide. In January 2022, due to the diagnosis of hyperthyroidism, she began to suffer from anxiety and poor sleep. On the morning of April 12, 2022, she felt uncomfortable and wanted to die, and took a packet of rat poison “smelling death” orally. After taking the medicine, she regretted it and told her family within 1 h. So family members sent her to a nearby hospital for gastric lavage treatment. The clinical laboratory of the hospital did not carry out residue detection technology, and the family members refused to send the residue samples to other units for inspection. Observation for 6 h after emergency gastric lavage showed that the patient was conscious and had no symptoms such as convulsions, dizziness, headache, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea. That night, she had symptoms such as shaking hands, stiff neck, and faster speaking speed, and had no auditory or visual hallucinations. On April 13, 2022, she was hospitalized for further diagnosis and treatment due to nausea, retching and paroxemic stiffness of the entire upper limb.

After admission, relevant auxiliary examinations were completed, and no obvious abnormalities were found in general medical and neurological examinations. Given that the hospital had no residue monitoring operation and the patient had undergone gastric lavage, family members refused to be examined for rat poison and body residue. The patient did not express hallucinations or delusions; no abnormality was observed on the thinking logic and intelligence; memory had declined; there was self-awareness and the ability to concentrate. She was depressed and anxious, and worried about her thyroid problem. She complaint that it was better to die sooner than suffer. Her interest declined, and she complained that she liked to play mahjong and had not played with his neighbors recently. Deny any history of emotional hyperactivity. Her emotional response was coordinated with the external environment. She had an increased pathological will and attempted suicide repeatedly. The patient was diagnosed by an experienced psychiatrist using the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview, and met the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD) recommended by the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [2]. On April 14, the Nurses’ Global Assessment of Suicide Risk Scale (NGASR) was used to assess the severity of suicide risk, in which the item of hopelessness was 3 points, depression, loss of interest or lack of happiness were 3 points, suicidal intention in speech was 1 point, mental illness history was 1 point, and the history of suicide attempts was 3 points. A total of 11 points indicated a high risk of suicide. Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) score of 25 points indicated severe depression; the Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS) score of 73 indicated major depression. The total score of Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) was 39 points, indicating the possible existence of mental illness, among which the dimension of anxiety and depression was 19 points, the lack of vitality 7 points, thinking disorder 4 points, the activation factor 6 points, and the hostility factor 3 points. On April 15, 2022, thyroid function test + sex hormone test was performed. Results indicated a low thyrotropin of 0.099uIU/ml (normal value 0.55–4.78).Blood routine + hsCRP + blood biochemistry + urine routine + coagulation function + pretransfusion pathogen showed no obvious abnormalities, and the nucleic acid test results of novel coronavirus were negative. Head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed left basal ganglia ischemia, cerebral atrophy, and vacuolar sella. Thyroid ultrasound indicated diffuse heterogeneous changes in the thyroid gland. On April 16, 2022, blood routine examination showed that white blood cells were 14.96*109/L and neutrophils were 94.7% (normal value 40-75%). Myocardial infarction 3 items suggested myoglobin 158.11ug/L (normal value 0-110 ug/L). Biochemical examination indicated that creatine kinase was 759U/L (normal value 40–200 U/L), lactate dehydrogenase 258U/L (normal value 120U/L-250U/L), and aminotransferase 226U/L (within the normal range). After admission for complete examination, lorazepam tablets (1 mg, once a night) were given to improve sleep; compound hyperthyroidism tablets were prepared orally (3 tablets, twice a day, fasting in the morning and evening) to treat thyroid diseases, and energy mixture supported treatment. On April 16, 2022, the patient had sudden tongue biting behavior, and the front end of the bitten tongue was vomited on the bed by the patient with excessive oral congestion. After emergency pressure and hemostasis, the patient was sent to the operating room for debridement and suture under general anesthesia plus tongue stump repair. After operation, recombinant bovine basic fibroblast growth factor topical gel (befusine) was applied locally. After taking out the gauze in the patient’s mouth, the patient still committed suicide by biting his tongue. According to the doctor’s advice, diazepam injection was injected 10 mg intramuscularly, and olanzapine oral avalanche tablet was 5 mg orally for sedation. Wrap the base of the tongue with gauze. The mouth opener protected the tongue, and both sides were tied behind the ear to prevent the patient from pushing it out with the residual base of the tongue. Restrain hands to prevent the mouth opener from being pulled out by the patient. On April 17, during the ward round, the patient reported that her memory of suicide by biting tongue was somewhat vague. After further questioned, she denied the existence of verbal auditory hallucinations or a sense of being controlled at that time. However, the symptoms of the patient were suggested to be episodic, and the doctor advised to add olanzapine to improve thinking, magnesium valproate to stabilize mood, and befusine to promote wound healing. The wound was repeatedly rubbed with normal saline to prevent infection. During treatment, the patient was closely observed for any change in her condition. Caregivers were advised to strengthen care to prevent the recurrence of self-injury and suicide. On April 19, routine blood and biochemical function tests returned to normal values. The NGASR score of 7 points indicated a moderate risk of suicide, in which the patient scored 3 points for depression, loss of interest or lack of happiness, 1 point for mental illness history, and 3 points for the history of suicide attempts. HAMD score of 17 points indicated mild to moderate depression; SDS score was 62 points, indicating mild depression. The BPRS score was 27 points, including 11 points for the dimension of anxiety and depression, 5 points for lack of vitality, 4 points for thinking disorder, 4 points for the activation factor, and 3 points for the hostility factor. On April 20, the patient was in a good general condition. There was still low mood, less activity, no obvious abnormality in volitional behavior, and no self-injury and suicide behavior. Self-awareness partially recovered. At the family’s request, the patient was discharged. After discharge, the doctor advised to continue to take olanzapine oral avalanche tablet 5 mg (once a night), escitalopram oxalate tablet 10 mg (once a day), magnesium valproate sustained-release tablet 250 mg (twice a day). Outside the hospital, antibiotics continued to be administered orally and recombinant bovine basic fibroblast growth factor gel was applied externally to the tongue suture. It was recommended to go to the stomatology department for reexamination after 1 week and psychiatric department at any time if uncomfortable.

On May 30, August 26, and November 28, 2022, respectively, the charge doctor and nurse followed up the patient by phone, and she reported occasional negative thoughts, but no specific implementation plan or behavior. Family members reported that the patient could cooperate with medicine, and children also visited her regularly. However, she still had less interest, no sense of pleasure, and occasionally suicidal intentions in speech. On December 19, the patient went to the psychiatric clinic for drug adjustment due to symptoms of sleeping difficulty and early waking up. The total NGASR score was 4 points, including 1 point for history of mental illness and 3 points for history of suicide attempt, indicating a low risk of suicide. Doctors again stressed that family members should strengthen suicide risk prevention at home. If the symptoms worsened, she should be admitted to the hospital in time. On March 6, 2023, the patient was followed up by phone, and complained that it was inconvenient to answer the phone while playing mahjong with friends. Then communicate with family members over the phone to understand the patient’s situation. They reported that she had been interested in mahjong activities for nearly a year. She could dance square with friends at night, take medicine on time, sleep well, and had no suicide speech and plan. Ask for regular review.

Discussion and conclusions

Relationship between rat poison poisoning and self-injury behavior

In this case, the patient diagnosed with recurrent depressive disorder had a significant background of suicide attempts, including several prior instances, and most recently attempted suicide through the ingestion of rat poison. Following this incident, only gastric lavage was administered, and there was no subsequent testing for residual body substances. She subsequently presented with symptoms of hand tremors, stiff neck, and increased speech rate. The findings of the head MRI revealed mild cerebral atrophy. Given the patient’s overall health status and medical history, the possibility of tongue biting behavior as a result of this condition can be dismissed. Studies have shown that exposure to rat poison can result in detrimental effects on the nervous system and various muscle groups, including skeletal and cardiac muscles [3, 4]. This can manifest in symptoms such as impaired speech and swallowing, as well as heightened mental agitation and manic behavior, which may elevate the likelihood of self-harm [5, 6]. Tongue biting is a frequently reported adverse outcome of rat poison ingestion, with the patient’s pathological volition potentially contributing to this behavior. In addition, the literature review found that cases of tongue biting as a suicide attempt were uncommon. Hence, it is imperative for healthcare professionals to diligently observe the mental and behavioral status of individuals with depressive disorder who have attempted suicide by ingesting rat poison, particularly in the initial phases post-ingestion. Regular monitoring of the patient’s neurological and muscular functions is advised, with prompt intervention as deemed necessary. For populations at heightened risk, securing the individual’s head during regaining consciousness or ongoing observation is recommended to mitigate the potential for tongue biting.

Concealment of tongue bite and coping strategies

Tongue bite is caused by damage to the tongue when the teeth close, and can occur during daily chewing, falls, convulsions, and even suicidal behavior. The symptoms experienced by tongue bite patients are related to the severity of the bite. Mild cases may result in small wounds and minimal bleeding on the tongue. In more severe cases, patients may have larger wounds, continuous bleeding, and in extreme cases, the tongue may be partially or completely severed. The most significant danger of “tongue biting” is the risk of suffocation and shock due to excessive bleeding and blood secretions [7]. The cognitive impairment of patients with depression is related to the severity of their clinical symptoms, and many patients still have cognitive impairment even after other depressive symptoms have improved [8, 9].

In this case report, the patient with recurrent depressive disorder had the behavior of “biting her tongue to kill herself”, which was hidden and difficult to be prevented by ordinary protective restraints. During the first aid treatment of the patient after a tongue bite, the tongue depressor was forcibly used to pry open the teeth, and a mouth opener was given to support the upper and lower teeth, protecting the tongue and preventing further tongue bites. Meanwhile, attention should be paid to the pressure on the broken end of the tongue to stop bleeding. The patient’s head should be turned to one side to timely clean up any clots in the mouth, as well as oral and nasal secretions, ensuring a clear respiratory tract to prevent asphyxia. It is also important to rapidly establish venous access to replenish blood volume. Examine the wound carefully and contact the operating room for emergency debridement and suturing. After the patient returned to the ward following surgery, she was placed in the emergency room to monitor changes in her condition and vital signs. Due to the patient’s restlessness and repeated tongue biting behavior, protective restraint was administered as advised by the doctor, including oral fixation to prevent further tongue biting and physical protective restraint.

In this case, the patient and caregivers were instructed to maintain oral hygiene and follow a light diet to avoid infection after surgery. They were advised to use normal saline daily to wipe the wound and remove oral secretions. Additionally, they were instructed to gargle after eating to prevent bacterial growth and infection caused by food residues. The patient should be advised to speak less. It is important to provide proper patient personal safety care and ward safety management, and to strictly adhere to shift change protocols. Impulsive behavior intervention treatment should be implemented, and protective restraint nursing should be carried out effectively. In the early stages after receiving a tongue prosthesis, it was difficult to eat. The liquid diet gradually transitioned to a normal diet, with the avoidance of spicy, irritating, and hard foods. It is important to provide care and emotional support to the patient, while also monitoring their progress closely. Family members should be informed to take care of the patient round the clock. If the patient expresses any negative remarks, medical staff should be notified immediately. Additionally, medical care should extend to providing psychological support for family members, encouraging them to offer social and familial support and companionship.

Intervention of suicide attempts in patients with recurrent depressive disorder

The incidence of suicidal ideation in depressed patients in China is high, so early screening and targeted intervention should be given attention to prevent suicide in depressed patients [10]. Patients with depression are at risk of self-injury or even suicide, which is related to their depressive mood, low self-evaluation, pessimism, and despair. Suicide is a complex process that involves many factors, from suicidal ideation to actual suicide. The difference in the incidence of suicidal ideation in different countries may be related to life background, lifestyle, and economic level. Patients hospitalized generally indicate a more serious condition. Being unable to work after hospitalization leads to reduced economic resources, lack of interpersonal communication, and other factors that easily lead to sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, and ultimately induce suicidal ideation. It suggests that medical staff should not only focus on the physical symptoms and drug treatment of patients with depression but also pay timely attention to the mental health problems of patients and the burden of their family caregivers to control the occurrence of adverse events. Therefore, the risk factors of suicide should be comprehensively investigated in the clinical assessment of patients’ suicide risk. A variety of methods, such as self-reporting and clinical interviews, can be used to assess suicide risk in patients [11, 12].

This patient was worried about the treatment effects of hyperthyroidism and gradually began experiencing poor sleep and a pessimistic mood. They then engaged in negative behaviors, such as ingesting rat poison “smell death” and biting their tongue. Following ingestion of poison, it is imperative to promptly empty the gastric contents and perform gastric lavage to minimize absorption. Additionally, immediate monitoring of vital signs and thorough physical examination are essential to maintain the stability of the patient. Rat poison exposure can result in various adverse effects on the nervous, cardiovascular, and respiratory systems, such as tremors, muscle stiffness, difficulty swallowing, irregular heartbeats, and breathing difficulties, necessitating prompt recognition and appropriate treatment. Furthermore, in cases where circumstances allow, blood and urine samples may be analyzed for toxins in order to exclude the presence of other substances or drug-induced toxicity, facilitating a definitive diagnosis and the formulation of a more precise treatment regimen. Additionally, prompt evaluation of patients’ psychological well-being and suicide risk is imperative to guarantee their safety. Providing emotional support to patients is essential in fostering feelings of care and support, ultimately aiding in the enhancement of their self-awareness, self-regulation, and coping skills.
