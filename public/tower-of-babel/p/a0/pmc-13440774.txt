Twenty years of frontal lobe surgery: Clinical outcomes and lessons from the frontier
Authors: Zainal Muttaqin, Jacob Bunyamin, Novanda Rizky Radityatama, Muhammad Kamil, Muhamad Thohar Arifin, Aris Catur Bintoro, Vega Karlowee, Happy Kurnia Brotoarianto, Ajid Risdianto, Krisna Tsaniadi Prihastomo, Dody Priambada, Fariz Eka Setiawan, Rafi Ilmansyah, Yuriz Bakhtiar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Frontal lobe epilepsy (FLE) surgery often requires complex presurgical evaluations and intraoperative monitoring, which may not be readily available in resource-limited settings. We report our experience establishing FLE surgical services in Indonesia to develop strategies for a simple but reliable service in low- and middle-income countries.Methods: We included patients who underwent FLE surgery between 2003 and 2023 in Semarang, Indonesia. These patients underwent surgery with at least 12 months of follow-up. Univariate and multivariate Firth penalized logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with surgical outcome, stratified by follow-up duration. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were performed to assess time-to-seizure recurrence across subgroups.Results: 87 FLE surgeries were performed over a period of 20 years. Approximately 60% of the patients were pediatric. Most patients (63.2%) showed visible focal cortical dysplasia on imaging. At the ≥5-year follow-up group, 56.8% of the patients had Engel Class I disease, with 79.3% have favorable (productive) functional outcomes. Among factors associated with postoperative outcomes, the number of anti-seizure medicines ≥3 was a significant seizure-free predictor at ≥5-year follow-up (odds ratio 0.18, 95% confidence interval 0.04–0.87, p = 0.033). The median seizure-free survival was 10.0 years for the overall cohort.Conclusion: Despite several limitations, this research suggests that establishing routine FLE services in countries with limited resources is feasible. To maintain a simple but reliable service, a streamlined surgery pathway should be introduced, together with strengthening national capacity and adopting cost-efficient technology.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a common neurological condition with an unequal global distribution between high-income countries and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Despite advances in antiseizure medications, drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) affects approximately one-third of all epilepsy cases,[13] and 80% of patients with epilepsy reside in LMICs.[6,17] Uncontrolled seizures are associated with reduced quality of life, a higher risk of injury, neuropsychological comorbidities such as anxiety or depression, and an increased risk of sudden death from epilepsy.[3,11,15,22,23] Reportedly, epilepsy surgery is a cost-effective way to reduce the seizure burden in carefully selected patients with DRE;[35] however, in reality, surgery remains underutilized owing to many factors, including the need for complex presurgical evaluations.[16,37,44] Unlike in high-income countries, advanced presurgical facilities such as long-term video electroencephalography (LT-VEEG), positron emission tomography (PET), single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), magnetoencephalography (MEG), functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and invasive intracranial monitoring (subdural EEG/electrocorticography [ECoG]) may be limited in LMICs.[37]

Frontal lobe epilepsy (FLE) is the second most common type of DRE, after temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). Compared with TLE, FLE has a diverse semiology and is often more challenging to treat, with a lower rate of postoperative seizure freedom.[27] Owing to its complexity, FLE frequently requires specialized invasive intracranial monitoring before and during surgery to confirm the extent of the epileptogenic zone (EZ).[14] This intracranial monitoring is an additional challenge in establishing surgical services for FLE in LMICs, as it requires capital investment and specialized human expertise.[18,45]

In this study, we describe the experience of establishing a simple but reliable FLE surgery pathway in Indonesia, one of the largest LMICs, with a total population of 280 million.[2] We report the treatment and clinical outcomes of our 20-year experience with FLE surgery at two hospitals in Semarang, Central Java, Indonesia. As the capital of the third-most populous province in Indonesia, Semarang is home to the first Level 4 epilepsy surgery center.[42] The epilepsy surgery service was initiated by an epilepsy neurosurgeon (Z. M.), who established it at two hospitals (Kariadi Hospital and Telogorejo Hospital) in Semarang. The service has been operational since 1999, initially for simple TLE cases but has now expanded to more complex cases, including FLEs.[31,32] We also explored factors associated with seizure-free outcomes to better understand those influencing the results of FLE surgery in resource-limited settings and steps required to reduce the “treatment gap.” We hope that this approach can be adopted by other LMICs facing similar challenges.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patient’s data collection

This study is a retrospective, observational study conducted in two epilepsy surgery centers (Dr. Kariadi Hospital and Telogorejo Hospital) located in Semarang, Indonesia. The prospectively maintained epilepsy patients’ database from January 2003 until December 2023 was reviewed. Institutional Review Board, Dr. Kariadi Hospital, has approved this research protocol (No.16639/EC/KEPKRSDK/2025), and patients’ identities were anonymized to ensure confidentiality. Given the retrospective nature of this study and the use of de-identified data from a prospectively maintained registry, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional policy. All consecutive patients who underwent frontal lobe resective surgery and fulfilled the inclusion criteria during the study period were enrolled; no cases were excluded based on outcome availability or completeness of follow-up data.

A total of 87 patients diagnosed with refractory FLE, defined based on International League Against Epilepsy classification, were included. Patients were evaluated by our multidisciplinary team.

The inclusion criteria for this study were (1) diagnosis of refractory FLE – defined as patients who failed two antiepileptic drugs (AED) to achieve sustained seizure control; (2) patients who underwent epilepsy surgery; (3) availability of complete clinical, pathological, and surgical records; and (4) patients should be followed up at 12 months minimum. Patients with seizures that did not originate in the frontal lobe are excluded. Based on these inclusion criteria, all patients had a minimum follow-up of 12 months; no patients were lost to follow-up. Continuity of follow-up was ensured through the prospectively maintained institutional database, supplemented by direct clinic attendance records.

Data were extracted from a prospectively maintained epilepsy database including sociodemographic data such as age, sex, age at surgery, hospital location, patients’ domicile, and handedness. Clinical data such as seizure onset, duration of epilepsy, seizure frequency, duration of seizure-free intervals, number of AEDs at the time of surgery, aura, and intelligence quotient (IQ) results.

The imaging findings from preoperative brain MRI with and without contrast, performed as part of the epilepsy protocol, were categorized into normal MRI, focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), low-grade epilepsy-associated tumor (LEAT), vascular malformations, and other lesions.

Surgical planning was determined through the epilepsy multidisciplinary conference, incorporating semiology, scalp EEG, Video-EEG (VEEG) findings, and imaging results. Standard resection was defined as a lobar or sublobar resection confined to the frontal lobe, specifically targeting the EZ and adhering to anatomical boundaries (e.g., dorsolateral frontal lobectomy, premotor and supplementary motor area resections, orbitofrontal resections, and focal lesionectomies). Extended procedures, such as callosotomy or hemispherotomy, were reserved for patients with rapid bilateral propagation.

Outcomes were classified using the Engel Epilepsy Surgery Outcome Scale, with surgical success defined as Engel class I. Engel classification was assigned by the treating epilepsy surgeon (Z.M.) based on structured clinic assessment at the last available follow-up visit. Outcome data were extracted from the prospectively maintained database by a co-investigator (R.I and N.R.R) and verified against clinic records. To account for variability in follow-up duration, outcomes were stratified into two groups: <5 years and ≥5 years based on the median last follow-up data in our cohort. Postoperative complications were documented and categorized into infectious events, new-onset neurological deficits, and other surgical complications.

Functional outcome was assessed by a single assessor (Z.M) at the last available follow-up visit and categorized based on psychosocial and vocational milestones. No standardized validated instrument was used; outcomes were classified pragmatically into three categories such as favorable (productive), unfavorable (nonproductive), and developmental (pediatric). Favorable is defined as patients engaged in age-appropriate full-time or part-time employment or successfully continuing formal education. Unfavorable is defined as patients who are unemployed or have ceased education primarily due to seizure frequency or neurological/cognitive deficits, whereas developmental is defined as patients too young for the workforce/formal higher education who are meeting age-appropriate developmental milestones.

Diagnostic procedure

All patients with focal DRE underwent a detailed analysis of seizure semiology, either by themselves or by witnesses, including recordings, if available. Patients exhibiting focal extra-TLE semiology were referred for LT-VEEG monitoring. A conclusive (unilateral) LT-VEEG result was used to guide the MRI examination, which was performed using a specific protocol for malformations of cortical development (the harmonized neuroimaging of epilepsy structural sequences protocol).[5] Those with concordant results across semiology, LT-VEEG, and MRI could proceed directly to ECoG-guided FLE surgery. Patients with doubtful or multilobar LT-VEEG results, normal MRI, or discordance among noninvasive presurgical studies were referred to undergo invasive monitoring with subdural grid electrodes for 1–3 weeks. All data were reviewed by a multidisciplinary epilepsy team for clinical decision-making. This team consisted of neurologists, pediatric neurologists, neuroradiologists, and epilepsy surgeons who met regularly to obtain a clinical consensus. All resections were lesionectomies guided by ECoG. Seizure frequency was calculated based on the following criteria: very frequent (>10 seizures daily), frequent (1–10 seizures daily), average (1–6 seizures weekly), rare (>1 seizure monthly), and very rare (<1 seizure monthly).

Statistical analysis

Factors affecting seizure outcomes (Engel Class I vs. Engel Class II–IV) were classified into three groups: (i) demographics, (ii) clinical history, and (iii) investigation procedures. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between each variable and seizure freedom (Engel Class I). Variables were selected based on prior literature reporting associations with seizure-free outcomes following FLE surgery.[7,8,24,25,38] Continuous variables were dichotomized at the cohort median to preserve statistical power, given the modest sample size: age at surgery (<14 vs. ≥14 years; median 14 years) and age at onset (<6 vs. ≥6 years; median 6 years). The number of anti-seizure medicines (ASMs) was dichotomized at <3 versus ≥3, consistent with the thresholds reported in prior FLE surgical outcome studies.[24,25,38]

Univariate logistic regression was performed for all candidate variables. Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. Within each follow-up group, univariate screening was performed to identify candidate predictors (p < 0.25) for inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression model. Within each follow-up group, the <5-year group included age at surgery and age at onset, while the ≥ 5-year group included age at surgery, age at onset, number of ASMs, and MRI lesion type.

The number of events per variable (EPV) was calculated for each model: the <5-year group had 26 seizure-free events across final predictors (EPV = 12.5, adequate), while the ≥ 5-year group had 24 events across 4 predictors (EPV = 6.25, below the recommended threshold of 10). Given the suboptimal EPV in the ≥5 years group, Firth penalized logistic regression was applied to reduce small-sample bias and overfitting. Conventional and penalized estimates are reported alongside each other for transparency. Model estimates for the ≥5-year group should be considered exploratory.

To evaluate potential selection bias introduced by subdural EEG monitoring, a sensitivity analysis was performed by repeating the multivariable logistic regression after excluding patients who underwent subdural EEG monitoring (n = 16), retaining only those who proceeded directly to surgery (n = 71). In addition, subdural EEG status was included as a covariate in a secondary multivariable model to assess its independent association with seizure freedom after adjustment.

To assess the potential impact of missing histopathological data, best-case (all missing patients assigned Engel Class I) and worst-case (all missing patients assigned Engel Class II–IV) sensitivity analyses were performed for the ≥5-year multivariable model. Missing data for variables other than histopathology were handled using listwise deletion.

Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate time-to-seizure recurrence (event = Engel Class II–IV), with seizure-freedom probability reported at 1, 5, 10, and 20 years. Subgroup comparisons were performed using the log-rank test for subdural EEG status and MRI lesion status. Cox proportional hazards regression was additionally performed to estimate hazard ratios for key predictors, with the proportional hazards assumption verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

All consecutive patients who underwent frontal lobe resective surgery and fulfilled the inclusion criteria during the study period were enrolled; no cases were excluded based on outcome availability or completeness of follow-up data. Continuity of follow-up was maintained through the prospectively maintained institutional database, supplemented by clinic records and telephone contact where required; no patients were lost to follow-up.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 2026.04.0+526; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Logistic regression was performed using the base stats package; Firth penalized regression using the logistf package; and survival analyses using the survival and survminer packages.

RESULTS

Between 2003 and 2023, 863 patients underwent epilepsy surgery at our center, and 788 (91.3%) patients had focal epilepsy. Overall, 679 (86.2%) patients had TLE, while 109 (13.8%) had extra-TLE. Among extra-TLE cases, 87 (79.8%) were FLE [Figure 1]. Of the 87 patients, 44 had a latest follow-up <5 years (<5 years group) and 43 had a follow-up of 5 years or more (≥5 years group). We observed an increase in the number of FLE surgeries since 2015, which reflects an increase in referrals and local capacity to perform complex surgeries [Figure 2]. Preoperative characteristics

Of the 87 patients, 43 had a latest follow-up of <5 years (<5-year group) and 44 had a follow-up of 5 years or more (≥5 years group). Of the 87 patients, 63.2% were male with an age range of 1–49 years (median, 14 years). Most patients in this case series were in the pediatric (1–18 years) age group, accounting for approximately 60% of all cases. The most common seizure presentation was focal awareness seizures (34.5%), followed by focal seizures with impaired awareness. Thirty-five patients (40.2%) had frequent seizures (1–10 daily), while 16.1% had very frequent seizures (>10 days). Almost one-third of patients reported the presence of auras. Twenty-six (29.8%) patients were aged <10 years old and may have had difficulty describing auras [Table 1].

Imaging studies showed that 55 (63.2%) patients had MRI-visible FCD, followed by low-grade epilepsy-associated tumors (LEATs, 12.6%) and normal MRI (negative) findings (12.6%). Approximately 90% of the patients had a localized EZ in the frontal lobe after VEEG monitoring, and the majority (62%) were located in the right hemisphere. Sixteen patients (18.4 %) underwent invasive subdural grid EEG monitoring for 7–14 days, while four (4.6%) were referred for PET/computed tomography [Table 1].

All patients with doubtful EZ localization underwent subdural EEG. Only a few patients were referred for PET imaging due to limited availability and insurance coverage.

To assess potential selection bias introduced by the nonrandom allocation to subdural EEG monitoring, baseline characteristics and postoperative outcomes were compared between patients who underwent subdural EEG implantation and those who did not [Table 2]. The two groups were well-matched across all baseline demographics, clinical history, and MRI lesion characteristics (all p > 0.05). The only statistically significant differences were a higher proportion of multilobar VEEG localization in the subdural EEG group (43.8% vs. 2.8%, p < 0.001) and a lower proportion achieving Engel Class I (25.0% vs. 64.8%, p = 0.005), consistent with referral of more clinically complex cases for invasive monitoring [Table 2].

Postoperative outcomes

All surgeries were performed using ECoG-guided lesionectomies, with a follow-up period ranging from 12 months to 20 years. In the ECoG, the premotor area, comprising 47.1% of all cases, was the most common location for the EZ. We categorized the Engel Class based on follow-up durations (<5 years and ≥5 years) which showed that among the <5-year follow-up group, there were 58.1% patients categorized as class I and 39.5% categorized as class II. While in ≥5-year follow-up group, there were 56.8% patients categorized as class I and 29.6% patients categorized as class II. Among the 16 patients who underwent subdural EEG evaluation, 7 (43.8%) had Engel Class II and five (31.2%) had Engel Class I. While most patients were complication-free, two (2.3%) patients had surgical morbidity that required craniotomy (one with an acute epidural hematoma after subdural EEG grid placement and one with epidural empyema 1 month after surgery). Seven (8.1%) patients experienced temporary neurological complications: two with drop foot, one with right leg paresis, two with finger paresis, and one with mild hemiparesis [Table 3].

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a median seizure-free survival of 10.0 years for the overall cohort [Figure 5]. Seizure-freedom probability was 94.3% (95% confidence interval [CI] 86.7–97.6%) at 1 year, 51.3% (95% CI 38.2–62.9%) at 5 years, and 41.9% (95% CI 26.4–56.7%) at 10 years. Log-rank analysis demonstrated a significantly lower seizure-freedom probability in patients who underwent subdural EEG monitoring compared with those who did not (log-rank χ2 = 7.799, p = 0.005), consistent with the selection of more clinically complex cases for invasive monitoring. Factors associated with postoperative outcomes

Multivariable logistic regression was performed separately for the <5-year group (n = 43, Engel Class I=25, EPV = 12.5) and the ≥5-year group (n = 44, Engel Class I=25, EPV = 6.25) [Supplementary file Table S4]. In the ≥5-year group, where EPV fell below the recommended threshold of 10, Firth penalized regression was applied to reduce small-sample bias. The variables that were included in multivariate analysis were sex, age at surgery, age at onset, epilepsy duration, seizure frequency, number of ASMs, postoperative IQ level, and MRI lesion type. These variables were previously reported to correlate with seizure freedom in epilepsy patients.[2,4,7,25,32] In the ≥5-year group, Firth penalized logistic regression identified ≥3 ASMs as the only significant predictor of seizure freedom (odds ratio [OR] 0.8, 95% CI 0.04–0.87, p = 0.033) [Supplementary File Table S5]. No statistically significant predictors were identified in the <5-year group. Other variables, including age at surgery, age at onset, and MRI lesion type, did not reach statistical significance in either model [Table 4]. In the sensitivity analysis excluding patients who underwent subdural EEG monitoring (n = 71), the direction of association between ASMs ≥3 and seizure freedom remained consistent (OR 0.273, 95% CI 0.04–1.67, p = 0.160), though statistical significance was not retained, likely reflecting reduced statistical power in the smaller sample. When subdural EEG status was included as a covariate in a secondary multivariable model, it was not independently associated with seizure freedom after adjustment (OR 0.200, 95% CI 0.03–1.58, p = 0.127), supporting the interpretation that its effect reflects case complexity rather than an independent predictor of outcome.

Sensitivity analyses for missing histopathological data (n = 76, 87.4% missing) demonstrated that the significant association of ASMs ≥3 with seizure freedom in the primary analysis (OR 0.181, p = 0.033) was not robust under best-case (all missing patients assumed seizure-free: OR 0.667, p = 0.764) or worst-case (all missing patients assumed seizure recurrence: OR 1.110, p = 0.925) scenarios. These findings highlight the exploratory nature of these associations and the need for caution in their interpretation [Supplementary File Tables S6 and 7].

DISCUSSION

This study highlights advances in FLE surgery and the challenges of developing and establishing surgical services in Indonesia, an LMIC. While we previously discussed the barriers to creating an epilepsy surgery center in Indonesia and strategies to overcome them,[41] this study offers insights into the adoption of more advanced presurgical evaluation and intraoperative monitoring techniques, such as subdural grid and ECoG, in resource-limited settings. Similar to other centers in LMICs, the diverse expertise of our team – including epilepsy surgeons, epileptologists, pediatric neurologists, neuroradiologists, neuropathologists, and EEG technicians trained both locally and abroad (Japan) – was of great benefit. Based on our experience, LMICs centers should establish strong collaborations with more advanced centers to facilitate the transfer of knowledge and expertise.

From a logistical perspective, surgery for drug-resistant FLE is not as straightforward as that for TLE, given the required presurgical evaluation and intracranial monitoring, which may not be readily available in most epilepsy centers in Indonesia. Currently, as the only Level 4 epilepsy surgery center in Indonesia, surgeries requiring advanced intracranial monitoring (ECoG) are conducted only in Semarang. Nevertheless, our seizure-freedom rate was comparable to those reported in other centers in high-income and LMICs across various age groups (adults and children).[8,24,25,38,46] While complications cannot always be avoided, our complication rate was acceptable, with no mortality,[4] and was even lower than the reported rate in a high-income country.[26] This finding indicates that ECoG is essential during surgery, as its use can help delineate the EZ and eloquent cortices. Similar to other types of epilepsy, seizure freedom after FLE was associated with improved psychiatric and socioeconomic outcomes.[25]

We performed subdural EEG device implantation only in patients with selective indications (MRI-negative, doubtful semiology, or unclear/multilobar foci on VEEG monitoring). Hence, EZ localization was more challenging in patients undergoing subdural monitoring than in those who could proceed directly to surgery. This likely reflects selection bias – whereby more complex, MRI-negative, or multifocal cases were preferentially referred for invasive monitoring – rather than a direct causal effect of the procedure itself. This interpretation was further supported by sensitivity analyses in which the exclusion of subdural EEG patients did not materially alter the primary outcome associations, and in which subdural EEG status was not independently associated with seizure freedom after adjustment for baseline covariates (OR 0.200, 95% CI 0.03–1.58, p = 0.127). In addition, as Engel outcomes were recorded at variable follow-up timepoints rather than standardized intervals, the potential for survival bias cannot be excluded.

Strategies to reduce FLE treatment gaps: Lessons from 20 years

To overcome this treatment gap, particularly for FLE, three strategies were developed based on our experience: (i) simplifying the FLE surgery protocol, particularly in places with constrained resources, (ii) expanding the epilepsy surgery network to reach people with physical/knowledge barriers to accessing surgery, and (iii) implementing cost-effective technology to assist subtle FCD detection.

Simplifying FLE surgery protocol for implementation in resource-limited settings

A shorter duration from epilepsy diagnosis to surgery significantly affects good seizure outcomes, highlighting the importance of a streamlined presurgical evaluation pathway to ensure early access to surgery.[7,38] As previously discussed, presurgical evaluation in LMICs should be tailored to available resources. For example, we did not routinely recommend PET imaging because our center in Semarang, a regional city, does not have a PET scanner available. Patients would have had to travel to Jakarta, the national capital, and the procedure was not covered by insurance. At present, our institution also lacks access to SPECT, MEG, or other advanced modalities that could assist in locating the EZ in complex cases; therefore, we relied on the available diagnostic modalities (MRI and EEG/intracranial EEG).

Given the unique challenges faced by LMICs in establishing an epilepsy surgery center,[12,39,40] we previously advocated the need for a streamlined surgical pathway to allocate resources efficiently for implementation in resource-limited settings.[21,32,33] Previously, we reported that, in Indonesia, the waiting time from epilepsy diagnosis to surgery can take up to 15 years.[34] With this pathway, we aim to reduce the waiting time at least from the presurgical evaluation point of care. All patients with ETLE or suspected semiology were directly referred for LT-VEEG monitoring. The decision to perform LT-VVEG before imaging was to obtain EZ lateralization to aid lesion detection, particularly if FCD was suspected. LTVEEG results can provide additional information to help neuroradiologists identify epileptogenic lesions [Figure 6].

Strengthening the epilepsy surgical network across the nation and reducing barriers to surgery

Considering the large population of Indonesia, a single center cannot fully address the national burden of epilepsy. In addition to the sociocultural barriers previously discussed,[30,42] Indonesia faces unique geographical challenges in terms of access to surgical services. As the world’s largest archipelagic nation, the country consists of approximately 17,000 islands, requiring patients from different islands to travel to Central Java by air or sea for surgery. This travel imposes additional time and financial burdens, further limiting access to surgical services. At present, epilepsy surgery services are expanding across Indonesia, with other neurosurgical centers (three on the island of Java and one each in the islands of Sumatra and Sulawesi) providing these services. However, these centers are still developing; only simple TLE surgeries (level 3 epilepsy centers) are performed in these centers, whereas complex cases requiring ECoG are referred to Semarang. We are actively supervising newly established centers until routine epilepsy surgery can be performed independently at each center.

To reduce knowledge barriers, we collaborated with the Indonesian Epilepsy Society (Yayasan Epilepsi Indonesia), Indonesian Neurosurgeons Association (Perhimpunan Dokter Bedah Saraf Indonesia), and Indonesian Neurological Association (Perhimpunan Dokter Neurologi Indonesia) to increase awareness of epilepsy surgery through social media campaigns. More importantly, we included patients who had undergone epilepsy surgery to share their positive experiences through video platforms. Despite prolonged waiting times, patients often expressed gratitude for having the information that enabled them to access surgical services. Epilepsy generates an increasing interest as an online search term compared with other neurological conditions; nonetheless, epilepsy surgery remains underrepresented on social media platforms.[28] Our previous data showed that >90% of patients in our center were self-referred and learned about epilepsy surgery services from social media platforms.[34] A study from the Middle East showed that, despite the substantial information accessible to the public, content supervision by accredited professionals is important to ensure the credibility of information regarding epilepsy.[1]

Implementation of cost-effective technology to aid subtle FCD detection

Given that FCD accounts for the most common imaging pathology in our series, identifying it on imaging is important, particularly for FCD type I, which is more common in MRI-negative epilepsy.[43] It should be noted, however, that definitive histopathological confirmation was unavailable in 87.4% of cases, limiting the precision of lesion-specific outcome conclusions. Radiological classification was used as a surrogate, which may not fully capture pathological heterogeneity, particularly for FCD subtypes. Furthermore, patients with missing histopathology differed significantly from those with available data in terms of epilepsy duration (3.0 vs. 6.0 years, p = 0.032) and number of ASMs (2.0 vs. 3.0, P = 0.014), suggesting that missingness may be informative and should be considered when interpreting lesion-outcome associations. Previous studies have also suggested that FCD detection on MRI is associated with good postoperative seizure outcomes after FLE surgery.[8,24,29] We previously reported that the rate of MRI-negative cases reached 21.3% of the operated cases in our center,[2] whereas in this study, a 12.6% prevalence was observed. While the rate may seem lower than the reported prevalence in the literature (30– 40%),[10,19] these numbers only accounted for all patients who underwent surgery, excluding those who were not eligible. We also expect this rate to increase over time as we develop capacity and experience in managing challenging cases, including MRI-negative epilepsy. One way to increase efficiency is to adopt an epilepsy-specific imaging protocol and enhance MRI capabilities (e.g., upgrading from a 1.5 T machine to a 3T machine). In addition to improving the infrastructure, MRI-negative cases often require additional imaging modalities to detect epileptogenic lesions or zones. Recently, several MRI-based automated FCD detectors have been introduced that can be used to streamline the FLE surgery pathway by providing more information for detecting subtle FCD based on cortical morphometry.[9,20,36] Some open-source programs, such as the multilevel epilepsy lesion detection program, offer an interpretable report that could help clinicians detect subtle cortical abnormalities, and this technology can be employed in centers with limited resources.[36]

Limitations

The main limitation of this study is the incomplete availability of histopathological data, which was missing for over half of the total data. While radiological findings provided a surrogate for lesion identification, the lack of definitive tissue diagnosis in these cases prevents a granular correlation between specific pathological entities (e.g., subtle FCD subtypes) and clinical outcomes. This missing data may introduce a bias in lesion–outcome analysis, as patients without histopathology may represent a distinct clinical subgroup. We were handling the missing data using listwise deletion, which may reduce the effective sample size and could introduce bias if the missingness is related to outcome or predictor variables.

Second, we acknowledge that the Engel outcomes were based on the last follow-up, resulting in variable follow-up durations across patients. Although stratification and inclusion of follow-up duration as a covariate partially mitigate the issue, the absence of a standardized time-point outcome or time-to-event analysis (e.g., Kaplan-Meier) may introduce survival bias.

Third, multivariate regression was stratified by follow-up and included only predictors identified through univariate screening. The ≥5-year group included four predictors for 25 seizure-free events, which may increase the risk of overfitting. Confidence intervals for age at onset and MRI lesion type were particularly wide, and these results should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies with standardized follow-up intervals, larger sample sizes, and more complete histopathological data are warranted to validate these findings.

Fourth, patient selection for subdural EEG monitoring was not random but driven by clinical complexity – specifically, MRI-negative lesions, indeterminate seizure localization, or multilobar epileptogenic foci. This constitutes a systematic selection bias, as the monitored cohort represents an inherently more refractory subgroup compared to those managed without invasive evaluation. Consequently, outcome differences observed between the two groups likely reflect underlying case complexity rather than the effect of monitoring per se, and cross-group comparisons should therefore be interpreted with considerable caution.

CONCLUSION

We suggested the feasibility of establishing a simple but reliable FLE surgery service in LMICs, despite the challenges posed by more complex presurgical evaluation and intraoperative monitoring compared with those of simple TLE cases. This experience underscores the importance of establishing a strong epilepsy care network and incorporating cost-effective technology in LMICs. Finally, we showed that a streamlined pathway for FLE surgery can achieve favorable outcomes with acceptable complication rates even when access to advanced presurgical modalities is lacking, and that this approach can be adopted by centers facing similar challenges.
