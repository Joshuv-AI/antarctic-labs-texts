The 30-15 intermittent fitness test in male futsal players: reliability and validity properties
Authors: Jun Li, Yan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 30-15 Intermittent Fitness Test (30-15IFT) has become a common on-field method to assess the level of cardiorespiratory performance in sports. Although literature has suggested excellent reliability properties, the validity against objective measures has been mainly conducted in individual sports, while findings in team sport athletes like futsal are lacking. Therefore, the main purpose of the study was to examine the reliability and validity of the 30-15IFT. Fifty-eight U21 male futsal players (age = 20.10 ± 0.90 years; height = 177.14 ± 5.72 cm; weight = 67.31 ± 7.33 kg) were recruited. A within-subject test-retest with seven days apart between trials was conducted to examine the reliability of the 30-15IFT, while the criterion validity included estimated VO₂max derived from the 30–15IFT and laboratory measured VO₂max obtained from the continuous running test in laboratory conditions performed seven days prior the 30-15IFT.Results indicated very high reliability of the 30-15IFT for Vmax (ICC = 0.95; CV = 1.00%, p < 0.001), peak heart rate (HRpeak; ICC = 0.94; CV = 1.12%, p < 0.001) and maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max; ICC = 0.95; CV = 1.10%, p < 0.001). Correlations between the 30-15IFT outcomes and a graded incremental treadmill test protocol were large for Vmax (r = 0.75), HRpeak (r = 0.73) and VO2max (r = 0.78). This study shows that the 30-15IFT is a reliable and valid intermittent field-based test to assess the level of cardiorespiratory fitness in male futsal players.Keywords: Futsal, 30-15 Intermittent fitness test, Maximal velocity, Maximal oxygen uptake, Peak heart rateSubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Futsal is considered un upcoming and popular sport worldwide regarding the number of professional and recreative participants and spectators1, resulting in the high level of skills and physical demands required for being successful2. Talent identification and recruitment are constantly improving because of the critical physical, technical and tactical components for success3. As an intermittent high-intensity sport, futsal players often cover distances with sprints in short bursts4 and maintaining intensity of effort at 85% of the maximum heart rate5. Interestingly, physiological demands of futsal have been shown to be higher than soccer, explaining this phenomenon by the unlimited substitution rule during futsal match6. It has been well-documented that professional futsal players cover approximately 14% of the total distance at high-intensity running and sprinting, interspersed by low- and medium- intensity activities every 14 s and 37 s, and change locomotor activities every 3.3 s7.

Over the years, measuring cardiorespiratory performance through objective methods has been a cornerstone for planning and programming training interventions8,9. Although this approach has merit in terms of generating ‘true data’, shortcomings often include high costs, time consumption and the need for an experienced practitioner to monitor the process10. Moreover, evidence highlights that there is still a considerable lack of understanding of the information obtained from valid intermittent aerobic fitness tests11. Due to different physiological demands between futsal and soccer6, where futsal players engage in more strenuous activities during the match, opposed to soccer players, who rely more on other players and have more frequent activities of lower intensity3, one can expect that field-based diagnostics between these two is not equal. Thus, there is a need for reliable, valid, cost-effective, field-based tests to predict aerobic capacity. Also, the complexity between the physical, technical and tactical elements of futsal has led to developing a multifaceted, easy-to-perform tool for talent identification and the creation of personalized training protocols12–17.

To overcome this problem, the 30 − 15 Intermittent Fitness Test (30-15IFT) was developed.18 The test includes two lines that are 40 m apart. At the signal, the athletes attempt 30-second shuttle runs, interspersed with 15 s of passive recovery, as directed by audio beeps. The velocity starts at 8 km/h and increases by 0.5 km/h after each stage18. The test stops if an individual cannot continue due to accumulated fatigue, or if they fail to reach the next 3-metre zone at the signal on three occasions. Outcome measures include maximal running velocity (Vmax) achieved at the last completed stage and peak heart rate (HRpeak). An estimated maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) is calculated from Vmax, gender, age and body mass18. Along with estimating maximal aerobic capacity, the 30 − 15 IFT is able to capture anaerobic capacity, neuromuscular capacity and changes in direction, as well as allowing adequate time for recovery19,20.

Previous studies have investigated the reliability and validity of the 30-15IFT in team sports, including handball13,18, ice hockey21,22, basketball13,18,23,24, rugby25,26, and soccer27–29, while studies conducted among futsal players are lacking11. Although evidence indicates that the 30-15IFT is a reliable tool to assess the level of cardiorespiratory performance, less data is available regarding its validity against objective methods3,11. A study by Čović et al.2 showed moderate to large correlations between the 30-15IFT and continuous running tests among female footballers. However, eligible data from Valladares-Rodríguez et al.11 only investigated the reliability properties of the 30-15IFT in male and female futsal players and confirmed very large test-retest correlations (ICC > 0.90); but the validity has yet to be investigated.

Therefore, the main purpose of the present study was to examine the test-retest reliability of the 30-15IFT and its’ validity against laboratory measured treadmill test in male futsal players. According to previous findings, we hypothesized that the 30-15IFT outcomes (Vmax, HRpeak and VO2max) would exhibit excellent test-retest reliability (ICC > 0.69)18,20 and acceptable validity (r > 0.50)11.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Fifty-eight U21 male futsal players (age = 20.10 ± 0.90 years; height = 177.14 ± 5.72 cm; weight = 67.31 ± 7.33 kg) were recruited. The inclusion criteria included fully active futsal players playing First Division Professional Futsal League (playing experience: 6.22 ± 2.34 years) with no history of injury or musculoskeletal pain. The exclusion criteria included players with an injury-related problem and those who failed to attend more than 85% of training sessions during the competitive season. All data relating to the inclusion and exclusion criteria were collected via a questionnaire. The study participants trained on average 5.1 ± 1.4 times per week (9.7 ± 2.2 h per week) and were classified as the national club level players. Each player trained in the existing team environment with other players for 5 years at minimum. The G*Power analysis calculator showed that, with a two-sided P value of 0.05, an effect size of at least 0.5011 and a statistical power of 0.80, the appropriate sample size should be n = 26. Due to a potential drop-out rate, we doubled the number of participants. This study was approved by the Guangdong Provincial Football Sports Center (ethical code: 25443–27182) and was carried out in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. All athletes signed a statement expressing their willingness to participate in all testing for this study. To protect participant confidentiality, all data were anonymized and stored securely. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any repercussions.

30-15IFT

The 30-15IFT was used to assess aerobic capacity18. We followed the methodology by Valladares-Rodríguez et al.11. In brief, participants sprinted repeatedly between two markers placed 40 m apart, following a rhythm dictated by a previously recorded beep. This pacing method enabled participants to run at suitable intervals and assisted them in modifying their speed: upon entering the 3 m zones at each end and when reaching the middle zone (20 m line), a brief beep was sounded. In the 15 s recovery phase, every player walked toward the nearest of the 3 lines, while getting ready for the next stage. Participants were directed to finish as many levels as they could, and the assessment concluded when a participant could no longer uphold the required running pace or when they failed to arrive in a 3 m area around each line at the time of the audio signal on 3 successive instances.

The test ended if athletes were unable to complete the distance due to exhaustion or fatigue, or if they were unable to reach the next 3-metre zone after three successive beeps. The final recorded speed was used to calculate the Vmax, from which an estimated VO2max, gender (G; males = 1; females = 2), age (A; in years) and body mass (BM; in kg) was derived using the following formula:

VO2max30−15IFT (mL*kg− 1*min− 1) = 28.3 – (2.15*G) – (0.741*A) – (0.0357*BW) + (0.0586*A*Vmax30−15IFT) + (1.03*Vmax30−15IFT))18.

Graded incremental treadmill test

VO2max was assessed using a ramp protocol on a motorized treadmill and determined by a breath-by-breath pulmonary gas exchange system (Quark B2, COSMED, Italy). The starting speed was set at 7 km/h and increased by 1 km/h every minute until volitional exhaustion. Exhaustion was defined as meeting one or more of the following criteria: (i) a VO2 of < 1.0 ml*kg⁻¹*min⁻¹ after an increase in speed; (ii) an exchange ratio of > 1.1; and (iii) a peak lactate level of > 6 mmol*L⁻¹ after exercise If a participant reported any bodily pain or discomfort, the test was stopped. After exercise, the recovery period included walking at 4.5 km/h for 5 min. Aerobic data regarding VO₂ max were averaged on a 5-second basis and included as a proxy for cardiorespiratory capacity in further analyses. Along with VO₂ max, HR_(peak) at VO₂ peak was monitored at a frequency of 1 Hz (Polar Electro Oy, Finland), and Vmax was presented as the final running speed30.

Data analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q–Q plots, while the homogeneity of variance was assessed using the Levene test. Basic descriptive statistics are presented as means and standard deviations (SD), with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Test-retest reliability was examined by the standardized differences in mean calculated to examine the magnitude of the change between trials. The magnitude of the difference was examined using effect size (ES), which was classified as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.5), moderate (0.5–0.8), large (0.8–1.6), or very large (> 1.6)31. Moreover, agreements between trials 1 and 2 were determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), where values of 0.10, 0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.90, and 1.0 indicated low, moderate, high, very high, nearly perfect, and perfect correlation, respectively18. According to Bucheit et al.18, good reliability is considered to be present when the coefficient of variation (CV) is < 5% and the ICC is > 0.69.

The validity of the 30-15IFT outcomes (Vmax, HRpeak and VO2max) was examined against objective methods using Pearson’s product-moment correlation (r), indicating small (r = 0.10–0.30), moderate (r = 0.30–0.50), large (r = 0.50–0.70), very large (r = 0.70–0.90), and almost perfect (r = 0.90-1.00) associations. Limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated as the mean difference between two methods (d) and 1.96*SD of the difference. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v27.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with an alpha level of p < 0.05 set a priori to denote statistical significance.

Validity of the 30 − 15 IFT

The 30-15IFT outcomes showed significant differences from the ‘true’ values for Vmax (2.04%, t – value = -5.113, p < 0.001), HRpeak (5.99%, t – value = -10.643, p < 0.001) and VO2max (5.01%, t – value = -3.996, p < 0.001). Discussion

The main purpose of the study was to examine the reliability and validity properties of the 30-15IFT in young male futsal players. Findings suggest that the 30-15IFT is highly reliable for Vmax, HRpeak and estimated VO2max (CV < 2.0% and ICC ≥ 0.90) and is correlated with objective data (r > 0.70).

Very high reliability of the 30 − 15 IFT in this study is in line with previous findings13,18,21–29 Evidence suggests that the 30-15IFT is highly reliable for male and female team sport athletes from handball13,18, ice hockey21,22, basketball13,18,23,24, rugby25,26, and soccer27–29. A recent systematic review by Grgić et al.20 showed that the reliability properties of the 30-15IFT were adequate for male elite ice hockey players (ICC for Vmax = 0.96, HRpeak = 0.97female elite soccer players (Vmax = 0.91, HRpeak = 0.94), female basketball players (Vmax = 0.85, HRpeak = 0.96), male elite wheelchair rugby players (Vmax = 0.99, HRpeak = 0.95), male rugby players (Vmax = 0.83–0.94, HRpeak = 0.96), male professional soccer players (Vmax = 0.80), and male and female futsal players (Vmax = 0.92–0.96, HRpeak = 0.90–0.91).

Although we observed slightly higher values for Vmax, HRpeak and VO2max in trial 2, indicating a potential learning effect, small ES and non-significant test-retest differences indicated that soccer players had a lack of experience in performing test3,32. Similar reliability findings have been obtained for other intermittent field-based tests, including the Yo-Yo IR133,34 and Yo-Yo IR233,34. Data from these tests showed very low CV (< 2.0%)33,34. On the other hand, the Yo-Yo IR1 and Yo-Yo IR2 have been used as indicators of individual’s ability to repeatedly perform intense exercise (Yo-Yo IR1) and the ability to recover from repeated exercise with a high contribution from the anaerobic system (Yo-Yo IR2).33 Because of these discrepancies of measuring different physical capacities in athletes, studies have shown that the 30-15IFT and Yo-Yo IR are highly correlated in sub-elite female netball players (r = 0.71)34, and young soccer players (r = 0.75)27–29.

Regarding the validity of the 30-15IFT, we found very large correlations with outcome measures of objective data derived from laboratory methods. Unfortunately, the validity of the 30-15IFT has yet to be investigated, due to a relatively small number of studies with small sample sizes and different sporting activities.28,35 A study by Scott et al.36 showed that the 30-15IFT was largely correlated with other field-based tests (r = 0.63–0.79) and was a useful tool to predict performance in several anaerobic tasks. However, the relationship was based on estimated, rather than laboratory-based data. The most similar study in soccer by Čović et al.3 exhibited strong positive correlations between the 30-15IFT and continuous running for VO2max (R = 0.67), HRpeak (r = 0.77) and Vmax (r = 0.57), which is somewhat in line to our study.

Our data indicated a slightly higher coefficient of correlation explained by characteristics of a sample. Most recently, a study by Paulsen et al.28 found a strong relationship between the 30-15IFT and Yo-Yo IR (r = 0.68–0.82) for performance with and without the ball, respectively. When observing correlations between Yo-Yo IR 1 and 2 and aerobic capacity, studies found strong correlations among elite female soccer players (r = 0.70)33. According to previous evidence, mean values of Vmax, HRpeak and VO2max were larger for the 30-15IFT in comparison to continuous running, which agrees with our findings3. From a physiological perspective, the importance of high aerobic power levels in futsal players is important, due to faster recovery between high-intense efforts or after exhaustion37. Since futsal is predominantly characterized by intermittent actions, where aerobic system plays an important role, the 30-15IFT may serve as a good field-based alternative to monitor and track cardiorespiratory fitness. Although we observed very large correlations between the 30-15IFT and laboratory-based outcomes, the LoA showed somewhat wide ranges, especially for HRpeak and VO2max, indicating that the interpretation and practical applicability of the test should be done with caution. As we failed to provide typical error and the smallest worthwhile change at a group level3, the usefulness of the 30-15IFT has yet to be investigated. Nevertheless, up-to-date studies from various individual and team sports clearly state that the utility of the 30-15IFT is justified and that the test is specifically designed to accurately assess small intermittent running intensity differences, such as VIFT changes of 0.5 km/h, providing individualized aerobic training velocities and distances based on the assessment results3.

Study limitations

This study is not without limitations. Although we conducted a test-retest reliability and construct validity of the 30-15IFT in male futsal players, we failed to perform test sensitivity to detect meaningful changes in performance. Previous evidence in female soccer players has indicated that the typical error (0.31 km*h− 1) was slightly larger than the smallest worthwhile change (0.20 km*h− 1) of the 30-15IFT, but both values were lower than 0.5 km*h− 1, indicating that an individual performance change of 0.5 km*h− 1 (one running stage) can be classified as ‘real and meaningul’3. Thus, it is possible that a single stage of the 30-15IFT can be a good indicator of a test usefulness in practical settings. Second, we failed to conduct analyses according to playing positions or player’s professionalism, since studies have confirmed such differences3. Establishing position- and competitive level-related differences in the 30-15IFT would be a good starting point for detecting players with lower 30-15IFT outcomes and applying different training protocols for their enhancement. Also, we did not assess the level of fatigue, as continuous training and match loads might have influenced the findings. Finally, more objective methods to examine accumulated exhaustion after completing the tests, such as blood lactate, should be performed in future research.

Practical applications

Based on our findings, the 30-15IFT is confirmed as a reliable and valid field-based tool to estimate aerobic capacity in team sports, such as futsal. Although the reliability properties have been widely established, validity studies are still lacking, making it relatively impossible to predict Vmax, HRpeak and VO2max from less objective methods. Because of very large correlations between laboratory- and field-based data, the 30-15IFT may serve as an adequate stimuli for monitoring physical capacities and giving an insight into athlete’s physical capabilities and achieving maximal output11. Since the test is easy-to-administrate, simple, cost-effective, and can be conducted in larger groups of athletes12, it serves as a good alternative to measure important factors of sports performance.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that the test-retest reliability of the 30-15IFT was at almost perfect level, and the validity was supported by very large correlations against objective methods. Although differences between the 30-15IFT and continuous running were significant and ESs seemed to be moderate-to-large, the tendency of linear relationship was present. Thus, the 30-15IFT seems to be a reliable and valid field-based method to assess cardiorespiratory fitness in male futsal players.
