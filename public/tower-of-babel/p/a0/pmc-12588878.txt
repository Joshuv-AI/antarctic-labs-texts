The Effect of Emotional Intelligence Skills Training on Work–Family Conflict Based on Self‐Regulation Theory in Iranian Nurses: A Mixed Method Study
Authors: Fatemeh Abbasi, Zakieh Khoramaki, Mohammad Hossein Kaveh, Mahin Nazari, Mohammad Fararouei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo determine the effect of emotional intelligence skills training on work–family conflict based on self‐regulation theory in nurses.DesignMixed‐method study with sequential‐explanatory design.MethodsThe quasi‐experimental part was done on 102 nurses in control and intervention groups. In the qualitative section, in‐depth individual interviews were conducted with 8 participants, and data were extracted using the content analysis method.ResultsThe average total score of emotional intelligence in the experimental group was significantly higher compared to the control group. But the findings did not show a significant difference in the average scores of work–family conflicts before and after the intervention in groups. The analysis of the findings from the interview showed that several factors explain the lack of effectiveness of emotional intelligence training in reducing work–family conflict.ConclusionThe findings of the quantitative and qualitative parts of the study showed that the training of emotional intelligence was useful for individuals, but the fact that it could reduce the work–family conflict might be due to some other factors, e.g., individuals, family and workplace as well.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or member of the public contributed to the study findings, and the study was conducted only on the population of nurses.Keywords: emotional intelligence, nursing, self‐regulation theory, work–family conflict

Background

Work and family constitute two important aspects of our lives, and individual health is entirely affected by the coordination and proportion between these two fields; the presence of conflict between these two factors is often followed by adverse consequences for individuals, families, organisations and society (Rassas et al. 2022). Baraty quoted from Greenhouse and Beutell: work–family conflict as a form of conflict in a role in which the pressure of roles in various fields of work and life leads to bilateral conflicts (Baraty 2017). Some factors that create work–family conflict could be great work volume, an increase in commercial competition and an unsafe economy. The organisations expect their staff to do further work to tackle such conditions, and this may lead to an increase in working hours and work preferences against familial life (Galletta et al. 2019). On the other hand, changes in society have led to increased participation by males and females in the work market. These changes have been especially noticeable in females (Panda 2018).

Despite the fact that women have undertaken the occupational tasks, the volume of their responsibility has not been reduced in the family, and this causes further stress and consequently more conflicts among them. The research conducted in this field confirms this statement (Panda 2018; Zhou et al. 2018).

Nursing is one of the jobs that has been approximately dominated by women. It is important to examine work–family conflict in the nursing field for the practical and theoretical concepts given (Yildiz et al. 2021). Nursing is often assumed to be a hard occupation, so the occupational conditions to which the nurses are exposed, such as irregular work shifts (Pien et al. 2021), high working volume (Labrague et al. 2021), night work, the lack of permission to exit the workplace (Ogunlade and Fwan 2014), a background for high occupational stress (Mosadeghrad 2013), and occupational burnout (Galletta et al. 2019), increase work–family conflict in them.

Emotional intelligence is one of the foremost skills that serve as a characteristic of individuals' capability to solve conflict, and today it is highly addressed (Alavi 2014). The concept of emotional intelligence that was initially proposed by Salovey and Mayer (1990) clarified personal differences in methods of reasoning about one's emotions. Supporters of emotional intelligence claim that better understanding and proper emotional regulation help a person effectively deal with stressful factors in the work environment (Garcia Zea et al. 2023). People with high emotional intelligence can gain more knowledge about the conflict, identify it faster and more correctly, and resolve it. Also, it enables them to control and balance their emotions in situations of emotional arousal (Valente and Lourenço 2020). Thongjeen in his study entitled ‘Investigating the Effect of Conflict Management, Work‐Life Balance and Emotional Intelligence on Employee Performance in Private Companies’ in Yangon, Myanmar, found that conflict management, work‐life balance and emotional intelligence have an effect on employee performance (Thongjeen 2023). By examining work‐life balance as a moderator of the effect of work–family conflicts and emotional intelligence on employee performance in a business, Vernia showed that emotional intelligence has a positive effect on work‐life balance (Vernia and Senen 2022).

The five dimensions of emotional intelligence are self‐awareness, self‐regulation, self‐motivation, empathy and social skills (Dhieni et al. 2022). Many study results indicate that, unlike cognitive intelligence, emotional intelligence is not a fixed and unchangeable capability but can be changed and grown, and one can increase and improve it through special training at quantitative and qualitative levels (Jung et al. 2019; Schutte et al. 2013). Emotion regulation has been defined in Barkley's model as a capacity‐building process that allows individuals to react with delay when they are exposed to situations that lead to an emotional response. Such a delay may help to acquire essential information to perceive what caused the emotional event (Grover et al. 2020). Emotion regulation enables individuals to direct, control, balance and even change their emotions (especially the negative ones, e.g., anger). Thus, self‐regulation deficiency is related to emotional‐behavioural complications in individuals, e.g., anger (Almalki 2016). The main idea that lies behind regulation is that you can recognise and even feel the changing of emotions (Ford and Gross 2019). Putting the above‐said information together creates the hypothesis that one might control the problem of work–family conflict and regulate the negative emotions through emotional intelligence skills training.

As mentioned, in the ever‐evolving health care landscape, the well‐being of nurses emerges as a major concern, and work–family conflict emerges as an important dimension. This study examines the complex interaction between emotional intelligence, self‐regulation theory, and work–family conflict, focusing on the effect of targeted skill training on Iranian nurses. In the initial stages, we carried out a quantitative methodological approach, a widely used paradigm in understanding the impact of educational interventions. However, as the results of the quantitative phase unfolded, a surprising revelation emerged: the lack of significant change in the study variables. This important moment prompted a critical re‐evaluation of our research framework and objectives. Recognising the need for a deeper understanding of the subtle factors influencing the observed results, we moved towards a qualitative approach.

This methodological transfer aligns with the dynamic nature of the investigated phenomena (Kumatongo and Muzata 2021; Santos et al. 2020) and allows a deeper exploration of the complexities surrounding the impact of emotional intelligence skills training on work–family conflict among Iranian nurses. In this regard, we try to provide valuable insights that go beyond the limitations of a methodological approach and provide a comprehensive understanding of the complex interaction between emotional intelligence skills training and work–family conflict among Iranian nurses.

Methods

This was a mixed‐methods study with a sequential‐explanatory design.

Phase 1 Quantitative Part

In this quasi‐experimental design method, the research population included the employed nurses in two Grand hospitals of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Marital status and (2) having 1 year or more working background. The hospital was allocated to testing and control groups at the cluster level in order to prevent dissemination and information disclosure. To this end, two hospitals were allocated to the testing group (Faqhihi hospital) and control group (Namazi hospital) by a simple randomised method (coin toss). The required number of samples was calculated based on a similar study (Rasooli et al. 2009). Based on this and using the following formula, the standard deviation of occupational conflict was 15, the effect value of the intervention was 10, the correlation of the before and after scores was 0.5, and considering the 10% dropout rate, 57 people were obtained in each group.

Among nurses, 111 respondents were recognised as qualified subjects. (Intervention group: 54 subjects; control group: 57 subjects) Research data were collected by using three questionnaires, described as follows:

Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (Bar‐Owen 1997)

The EQ‐i is a 133‐item self‐report instrument. Items are first‐person singular statements that are summed to obtain a total score that represents overall emotional intelligence. Scores on 5 higher‐order composite dimensions (intrapersonal intelligence, interpersonal intelligence, adaptability, stress management and general mood) and scores on 15 lower‐order component scales (see Table 1). The answer to each item is set on a 5‐point Likert scale (completely agree, agree, somewhat disagree and completely disagree). The initial form of this questionnaire was 133 questions, and in this research, the 90‐question form that was implemented by Samoui in Iran on 500 students was used (Samoui 2000). The reliability of the instrument was 0.93 in the audio‐visual study, 0.86 in the Islamian study (Islamian et al. 2014), 0.97 in the Khodadady and Tabriz (2012) study and 0.94 in this study.

Work–Family Conflict Questionnaire (Carlson et al. 2000)

This inventory consists of two parts: work–family conflict and family–work conflict, each with 18 questions. Carlson et al. (2000) declared the reliability of this questionnaire to be 0.78 or higher (Carlson et al. 2000). This inventory is rated by a Likert 5‐scale criterion (strongly disagree; choice 1) through strongly agree (choice 5). The score range was from 18 through 90 in this questionnaire; the items have been drawn up in such a way that a higher score denotes lesser conflict.

Self‐Regulation Questionnaire of Brown et al. (1999)

The self‐regulation scale was created by Brown et al. (1999) to measure the components of self‐regulation and consists of 63 items and has 7 subscales (acceptance, evaluation, initiation, review, planning, implementation and measurement). Each subscale has nine items, and a 5‐point Likert scale (totally agree = 5 and totally disagree = 1) was used to score each item. Razavi et al. (2012) in Iran announced the reliability of the self‐regulation questionnaire as 0.91. Aydin et al. (2014) studied 591 students; in addition to the psychometrics of this questionnaire, they also declared its reliability to be 0.87, and Cronbach's alpha in this study was also 0.74.

Intervention in Phase 1 Quantitative Part

The training intervention included 7 sessions (30–60 min), and it was implemented in small groups (3–6 members) using interactive lectures, discussion, question and answer and sending key messages daily via the What's Up social network for the intervention group.

The outlines of training content were the work–family conflict, the related causes and consequences, the role of emotions in the reduction or increase of conflicts, emotional self‐awareness, self‐regulation, empathy or recognition of emotions in others, management of emotions in others and their behavioural skills, characteristics of individuals with high emotional intelligence, emotional self‐regulation strategies, e.g., anger control skills and ways of emotion self‐regulation (Schutte et al. 2013; Saadi et al. 2012). The above‐mentioned training intervention was not implemented in the control group. Only a pamphlet was presented in this group on the irrelevant subject of emotional intelligence. We employed strategies derived from self‐regulatory learning to improve the training of emotional intelligence and make emotional intelligence feasible since, firstly, the common point of emotional intelligence and emotion self‐regulation lies in the recognition and changing of emotions you feel (Stoyanova‐Bozhkova et al. 2022). On the other hand, there is an important and strong relationship between emotional intelligence and emotion self‐regulation. The goal of emotion self‐regulation is that the individuals assume the task of their own learning (Jeong 2020). The self‐regulatory person possesses exclusive objectives: they are inclined to learn, they are aware of how to use learning strategies, and they are sure to achieve the given goals (self‐efficacy). They analyse this point throughout the learning process to determine whether such a strategy can provide the given goals or not (White and Grason 2019) and, in fact, is the same as self‐regulation in emotional intelligence (Maharishi and Ganesh 2020).

The research data were completed in two phases, including 2 months before and after the end of the intervention, through questionnaires in both groups. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 11.5. An independent t‐test was used to compare the baseline and post‐intervention measures between groups. A paired t‐test was used to compare the within‐group baseline and post‐intervention measures.

Phase 2 (Qualitative Section)

In the qualitative approach, researchers want to look at the subject from the perspective of the research participants. They focus on the opinions and experiences of the participants and look for new findings from the topics and concepts that people express (Corbin and Strauss 2014). In the qualitative part of the current research, the main question was what other factors (besides knowledge and attitude that are usually effective in the educational program) influence nurses to have a suitable coping behaviour regarding work–family conflict.

Among the participants in the quantitative part of the study, using a purpose‐based sampling method, nurses who, in addition to their willingness and ability to participate in the study, were able to clearly express their communication experiences were selected using an in‐depth individual and semi‐structured interview. Sampling was done until data saturation was reached, and finally, eight nurses participated in the qualitative part of the study. The reason for using the in‐depth interview technique individually was the busyness of the nurses during the work shift and the impossibility of gathering people and conducting a group interview. All the above items were used in order to improve the transferability of the study results.

The face‐to‐face interview by two of the authors (health education and promotion of Doctoral and Master's students) started with the main questions of this research, including ‘Was the training good and useful? In your opinion, for what reasons did the problem of work‐family conflict not change compared to before the training?’. Then, in order to clarify the concept and deepen the interview process, follow‐up and exploratory questions were gradually asked, and the interview ended until the purpose was fully and clearly stated. The interviews were conducted in a quiet place and time with prior coordination and using notes and a tape recorder for 30–40 min. Immediately, recorded interviews, verbatim notes and notes during the interview were also added to it. This procedure led to maintaining the dependability and conformability of extracted words and codes. In cases where follow‐up questions were raised, another interview appointment was arranged with the interviewee, and questions were asked. All interviews were recorded with the permission of the interviewers and were completely transcribed and analysed after the end of the interview. Interview transcripts were returned to participants for comments and/or corrections. The criterion for stopping sampling was data duplication or information saturation, and no new data was obtained. Content analysis was done by the research team according to the proposed stages of the Graneheim and Landman model (Graneheim and Lundman 2004). To enhance trustworthiness, A. F. and K. MH coded the verbatim transcripts independently. The first round of coding by two of the authors resulted in 650 coded text segments. Then, these open codes were discussed among all authors to organise and group them into meaningful clusters. Words and sentences or paragraphs, were considered meaning units. Then meaning units, according to their latent content, reached the level of abstraction and conceptualisation and were named using codes. In the following, the codes were compared with each other in terms of their similarities and differences and were categorised under more abstract classes with specific labels. In the end, by comparing the categories with each other and engaging in deep and detailed reflection, the latent content in the data was introduced under the title Study Theme.

Intrapersonal Factors

It includes personal factors that hindered the application of training, which includes three subcategories.

Will

The findings showed that the lack of adequate individual will and planning for change has been one of the reasons for the impracticality of training. One of the participants expresses: In my opinion, if I use your statements in life, it can be very effective. I may not use them personally or frequently, but the trainings were practical and helpful (Nurse No. 5).

Fatigue

The high occupational volume and multiple numbers of work shifts make the personnel so tired and demotivated that no motive may remain for thinking about the subject under the conditions other than the time of break. One of the participants believes as follows: Someone with 7–8 work shifts in a week, as she goes home, lacks any motive or enthusiasm. As one is tired and bored and cannot implement the given plans, it means that if you implemented this plan among female householders with more free time, you would achieve a much better result. (Nurse no. 4).

Stress

Due to a lot of businesses and working and familial conditions, the married nurses are under more stress. For this reason, a person tries to preserve the existing conditions and not introduce new ones. Nurse No. 3 declared: Someone who is stressed with a lot of business tries to repeat the previous work, and s/he does not take a new method. You know that anyone should be in a tranquil condition to try a new business, but when you are too busy with a great volume of work, you try to repeat your previous behaviors to do your tasks.

Interpersonal Factors

There are some barriers created by the others, and they hinder the individual from internalising training in his/her mind. They consist of two subclasses:

Family and Relatives

Coordination of family and the surrounding people with individuals' wishes that is deemed as reducing pressure to decrease the problem of work–family conflict can be a difficult and time‐consuming task: For example, I try at home to bring my spouse to a counselor, but I have not yet succeeded, e.g., one training session… I was a person with a character that I'd never say no to, but I've recently moved away a little from others. However, the behavior of these surrounding people is causing difficulties for those who want to change their lifestyles. (Nurse no‐1).

Colleague in Workplace

They (officials) only think for themselves. New duties are adding to our responsibilities every day. They never say you're doing things well, and it results in our being better and brings public satisfaction to our services and the people who are working right here. They do anything to indicate that we (doctors) are much better in any case than you, the nurses, for example, they frequently ask the patients’ attendants to tell them the name of the nurse who has treated badly with the given patient. (Nurse no‐2)

Environmental Factors in Family and Hospital

The environmental factors that should have been provided for better function and performance are never paid attention to; thereby, the nurses would be able to deal with the job and family with more comfort.

The related subcategories include welfare services, e.g., housing, transportation, kindergarten and counselling centres, as well as lots of work shifts against low salaries and benefits, discrimination between personnel and the ignorance of the officials.

The problem of night work, for which the nurses have problems bringing their children to the workplace, may exert pressure on these nurses. It is not an easy job to solve these problems with 5‐ or 10‐hour trainings. In my opinion, if the conditions were prepared for the nurses to take care of their children in a kindergarten near the hospital, then their stress would be reduced for their children. (Nurse no‐2)

Structural Factors

The barriers existing in the process of implementation of the work have not caused any changes in the results. It could be a result of the numerous questions asked and the inadequate time and focus to give answers to the questions.

You know, perhaps it was because the number of questions was too many. You know, we are highly busy, and the questions were in such a way that you should have concentrated on them. If a negative and positive verb were not considered, the question might have completely changed. Namely, you should have highly concentrated on them to notice what they meant. (Nurse no‐6)

Discussion

Quantitative Section

Based on the findings of the present study, the average score of emotional intelligence in the intervention group increased after the training, and there is a significant difference between before and after the intervention. This finding is in agreement with the results of the study of Nadler et al. Based on the findings of the present study, the average score of emotional intelligence in the intervention group increased after the training, and there is a significant difference between before and after the intervention. This finding is in agreement with the results of the studies of Nadler et al. (2020) and Kozlowski et al. (2018) which are aligned. Emotional intelligence is one of the types of social skills that can control stress and give a person the ability to better face environmental problems and limitations. Teaching emotional intelligence to nurses is very important considering the nature of their profession so that they can use this skill in communication with patients in addition to managing emotions in their personal lives (Raeissi et al. 2023; White and Grason 2019).

The results of the present study showed that the average score of work–family conflict in the intervention and control groups after the intervention did not show a significant difference from before the intervention. This means that the findings of this research could not support the initial hypothesis that ‘with emotional intelligence training, work‐family conflict can be reduced’. ‘Reduced’ to confirm. The result of the present study is not consistent with the studies of Chakravorty and Singh (2020) and Nadler et al. (2020). Looking for an answer to the question of why the training could not reduce or find meaning in the work–family conflict, a qualitative part was also added to the study. The system of rotating work shifts in the nursing profession is one of the reasons why nurses have more problems with family functioning and work stress compared to teachers (Liu et al. 2020).

Among the other reasons for this finding, we can mention the multifactorial nature of the work–family conflict phenomenon. In the occurrence of this phenomenon, not only the individual himself but also the work environment, managers and colleagues, as well as his wife, family and children and economic and social conditions are effective (the issue of support or lack of support for behaviour change) (Labrague et al. 2021). Rassas et al. (2020) and Rhéaume (2022) confirm this claim. Support from the wife, relatives, friends and colleagues was related to family interference in work. While none of these social supports were related to the interference of work in the family, this issue has also been discussed in the qualitative part of this study.

The results of the present study show that the average score of self‐regulation in the experimental and control groups before and after the intervention showed no significant difference. This finding is in line with the study of Tavakoli Zadeh (2011) entitled ‘Investigation of the Effectiveness of Self‐Regulated Learning Strategies on the Mental Health of Male Students’. In this study, 30 middle school students (15 in each group) were trained in 18 one‐hour sessions. Despite this finding, Sabzi et al. (2022), in their study with the aim of determining the effectiveness of executive function training on emotional self‐regulation and test anxiety in 30 female students with writing disorders, showed that executive function training is effective on their emotional self‐regulation and test anxiety.

Qualitative Section

In this part, the analysis and interpretation of the findings from the in‐depth interview with a group of participants are given to complete the quantitative section.

In the results section, four categories—intrapersonal, interpersonal, environmental and structural factors—were mentioned for the lack of effect of emotional intelligence training to reduce work–family conflict.

One of the factors within the intrapersonal is the lack of will to change the title. The study of Ding et al. (2018) also confirms this finding. In a survey they conducted on 25 nurses, they concluded that the ability of nurses in family and work matters under the conditions of the lack of a supportive work and family environment and higher expectations. He suffers from work and family limits.

Another factor within the intrapersonal that was mentioned in the results section of the study is the presence of stress and psychological pressures caused by the pressure of family roles and job roles. This psychological barrier reduces the desire of a person to change and wants to maintain the existing conditions more stress. Don't impose. Polat et al. (2018) conducted a study titled ‘Source of Conflict Between Work and Family Roles’ and acknowledged that the same pressure caused by performing the duties of one role creates conflict in fulfilling the requirements of another role.

The second part of the results is related to interpersonal factors and includes relationships with family, friends and colleagues. People around them certainly play a big role in helping to reduce work–family conflict or aggravating this problem. In the study of Polat et al. (2018), the effect of 329 nurses' organisational silence on work–family conflict and perceived social support was determined. Results show that it is possible to reduce nurses' sense of social support by taking measures to reduce the causes of work–family conflict. Hasan Seif et al.'s (2014) study entitled ‘Investigation of organizational and psychological factors affecting work‐family conflict’ was conducted on 214 married female nurses of private hospitals in Shiraz, and the results of path analysis showed a reduction in conflict and role ambiguity, and the reduction of work–family conflict in nurses was due to the support of head nurses.

The third part of the results states that some environmental factors can also help create a change that reduces conflict. Its subcategories include welfare services such as housing, commuting, kindergarten and a counselling centre in the hospital. Also, increasing personnel, preventing discrimination in the workplace, and choosing appropriate people to be responsible can play a big role in increasing morale and job satisfaction. As far as we know, no study has been found that could match this finding.

The last part is related to the structural factors, which are related to the factors that caused problems in the implementation of the work and affected the results, such as the many questions in the emotional intelligence questionnaire and a lack of sufficient concentration to answer the questions.

According to reliable sources and texts, it is concluded that work–family conflict is an important concern for nurses, especially female nurses, which requires more extensive and operational investigations to properly deal with this challenge.

In the present study, the collection of information through questionnaires had limitations due to the fact that some of the participants may not have given correct and real information. Another limitation of the present study was the short intervention and follow‐up time of the samples, and it is better that the samples are followed up to 6 months after the educational intervention, and the intervention time should be increased to at least 1 year. Also, one of the implementation problems of the study was the fact that the nurses were married and had shifts, which made the intervention work, i.e., the implementation of the training programme, difficult, as well as the high volume of work in the hospitals under study, which are considered to be the main referral centres for patients in Shiraz. Despite all the existing challenges, this study was able to present new dimensions in the reasons related to work–family conflict in nurses, which we hope will attract the attention of nursing managers and hospital officials.

Conclusion

Quantitative Section

The average score of emotional intelligence in the intervention group increased after training, indicating a significant difference before and after the intervention. Teaching emotional intelligence to nurses is crucial for their profession, as it helps manage stress and improve communication. However, the study did not support the initial hypothesis that emotional intelligence training can reduce work–family conflict. Work–family conflict is influenced by various factors, including the nature of the nursing profession and the multifactorial nature of the phenomenon. The study suggests that support from various sources and environmental factors can contribute to reducing work–family conflict.

Qualitative Section

Intrapersonal factors, such as the lack of will to change and stress from family and job roles, contribute to work–family conflict. Interpersonal factors, such as relationships with family, friends and colleagues, can either alleviate or aggravate work–family conflict. Environmental factors, including welfare services and adequate personnel, can help reduce work–family conflict. Structural factors, such as questionnaire limitations and implementation challenges, affect the results and implementation of interventions. Further research and attention from nursing managers and hospital officials are needed to address work–family conflict concerns among nurses.

Implications for Nursing Management

Nursing managers can use the emotional intelligence training program based on self‐regulation theory to reduce work–family conflict among nurses.
