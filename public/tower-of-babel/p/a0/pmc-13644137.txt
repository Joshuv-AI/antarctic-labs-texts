Lipoprotein(a) and atherosclerosis risk across estimated glucose disposal rate strata: evaluating synergistic predictive utility
Authors: Yihui Li, Nahua Xu, Lei Yan, Pingting Yang, Jiangang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] levels and insulin resistance (IR) are both established risk factors for atherosclerosis. Nevertheless, the interrelationship between these two factors and their combined effects on atherosclerosis remain poorly understood. This research aimed to explore the distribution characteristics of Lp(a) across individuals at varying IR degrees and to evaluate the combined ability of Lp(a) levels and the IR surrogate marker, estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), in predicting atherosclerosis.MethodsThis retrospective study involved data from 10,753 participants who received health examinations at the Third Xiangya Hospital (2017–2025). Carotid ultrasound was employed to diagnose atherosclerotic plaques. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were used across eGDR quartiles to examine the relationship between Lp(a) and plaque risk. The incremental benefits of incorporating both Lp(a) and eGDR in predicting atherosclerosis were further assessed via machine learning–based analyses.ResultsIn this study, the median Lp(a) concentration of the participants was 12.4 mg/dL, with 14.4% exhibiting values greater than 30 mg/dL. RCS analysis identified a significant nonlinear (U-shaped) relationship of Lp(a) with eGDR, whereas peak Lp(a) levels were observed within the uppermost eGDR quartile (Q4). Lp(a) levels demonstrated an independent positive link to atherosclerotic plaque risk; notably, this association remained significant in both the lowest and highest eGDR quartiles. Furthermore, machine learning analyses indicated that the incorporation of Lp(a) and eGDR conferred a modest gain in the model’s discriminative capacity when forecasting atherosclerotic plaque, with the AUROC value increasing slightly from 0.7810 to 0.7820.ConclusionsLp(a) levels exhibited a positive linear association with atherosclerotic plaques in the overall population, which varied across eGDR strata. Furthermore, the interaction between Lp(a) and eGDR was significantly relevant for the prediction of this condition. The integration of Lp(a) levels and the eGDR into machine learning models modestly improved the identification of high-risk individuals. This strategy supports more precise clinical interventions, ultimately contributing to improved cardiovascular health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03014-0.

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a distinctive particle comprising low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) that is bound to apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] via disulfide bonds [1]. Its plasma concentration is strongly influenced by genetic factors, accounting for the wide variation observed among various populations [2]. Notably, the Lp(a) concentration remains remarkably stable throughout life and is not greatly impacted by dietary or environmental modifications. Additionally, it serves as an independent predictor for atherosclerosis [3]. Levels exceeding 30 mg/dL are particularly concerning, as Lp(a) at these concentrations can actively drive the progression of atherosclerosis [4]. A wealth of epidemiological and observational evidence indicates that increased levels of Lp(a) are correlated with increased rates of various atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVDs), such as myocardial infarction and stroke [5–7]. Furthermore, the genetic basis of high Lp(a) as a cause of ASCVD is robustly established [8].

As a cardinal pathophysiological hallmark of type 2 diabetes (T2D), insulin resistance (IR) represents a major mediator of atherosclerosis. The eGDR, which reflects IR clinically [9, 10], is obtained from the equation below: 21.158 – [0.09 × waist circumference (WC)] − (3.407 × hypertension) – [0.551 × glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c)], where hypertension is coded 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no” [11]. Accumulating evidence has proven that reduced eGDR values are closely linked to an increased risk of ASCVD [12, 13].

Although Lp(a) and IR are each established as independent predictors of atherosclerosis, the interplay between them remains a subject of debate. The literature predominantly reports a negative association of Lp(a) with IR among middle-aged as well as older populations [14, 15]. In support of these observations, meta-analyses have indicated that T2D risk is paradoxically elevated in individuals with very low Lp(a) concentrations [16]. This inverse association has also been documented in patients with hypertension and dyslipidaemia [17]. Conversely, conflicting evidence suggests that Lp(a) does not modulate IR during pregnancy [18]. Given these discrepancies, it was proposed that IR status may modify the relationship of Lp(a) concentrations with atherosclerotic plaque risk.

Moreover, traditional statistical methods often struggle to elucidate complex, multifactorial interactions. In contrast, machine learning algorithms excel at capturing nonlinear patterns and high-order interaction effects. This gives them a distinct advantage in predicting cardiovascular disease risk [19]. Despite this potential, the application of machine learning to joint risk stratification incorporating both Lp(a) and eGDR remains limited. Consequently, the study intends to (1) characterize the Lp(a) distribution across eGDR-defined quartiles of IR; (2) elucidate the influence of Lp(a) on atherosclerosis progression within different levels of IR; and (3) using machine-learning models to quantitatively estimate the additional prognostic ability of Lp(a) levels and eGDR, singly and in tandem, for atherosclerosis.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

All subjects were obtained from Third Xiangya Hospital’s Department of Health Management (Central South University), a leading medical facility serving China’s central region. The hospital’s database comprises approximately 7.3 million outpatient records, encompassing detailed demographic information, laboratory test results, and diagnostic data systematically coded according to the ICD-10-CM classification system [20]. Eligible participants, who were recruited between 2017 and 2025, were adults (≥ 18 years) with complete baseline physical examinations (covering blood pressure, hepatic and renal function, blood lipids, abdominal ultrasound, and electrocardiogram) and documented Lp(a) levels. The exclusion criteria included (1) a lack of carotid ultrasound results; (2) the absence of the necessary data for eGDR, such as WC, hypertension history, and HbA1c levels; and (3) the presence of severe physical or mental comorbidities, including severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, malignant tumours, or acute diseases. Ultimately, the final analysis comprised 10,753 participants (Figure S1). The informed consent documentation as well as the study protocol underwent review and received approval from the Institutional Review Board and the Independent Ethical Committee of Third Xiangya Hospital (approval No. 24005). Each individual signed a written consent form ahead of participation.

Laboratory and clinical measurements

Demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, self-reported medical history, and family history were collected via a standardized online questionnaire (https://new.selfhealth.com.cn/#/login). Additionally, clinical measurements were performed by trained and experienced clinicians following standard protocols. Specifically, seated blood pressure readings were obtained using the mercury sphygmomanometer following a 15-minute rest, in accordance with American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines [21]. WC was determined using a non-elastic tape placed halfway between the lowest rib and the iliac crest after full exhalation [22]. All laboratory assays were conducted by certified personnel in Third Xiangya Hospital’s central laboratory, who followed standard operating procedures. Blood draws were performed on fasting participants, with specimens processed at the lab within 2 h for biochemical assessment. The panel comprised triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-C, Lp(a), serum creatinine (Scr), uric acid (UA), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and HbA1c. Among them, Lp(a) concentrations have been quantified via an immunoturbidimetric assay (Beijing Leideman Co., Ltd.), which measures turbidity changes resulting from antigen‒antibody complex formation relative to standards.

Carotid ultrasonographic assessment

Carotid ultrasound examinations were performed by experienced sonographers. The probe was aligned parallel to the vessel’s long axis to ensure that the ultrasound beam was perpendicular to the vessel wall. Carotid intima–media thickness (cIMT) tests were carried out on the distal common carotid artery and the carotid bulb, with the former site located 1.0–1.5 mm before the bifurcation, and areas containing plaque were carefully excluded. cIMT was defined as the vertical separation on a grayscale (two-dimensional) image from the intimal leading edge to the adventitial outer border. A focal outpouching into the arterial lumen was defined as carotid plaque if it met the cIMT criteria of either ≥ 1.5 mm or a thickness at least 1.5 times that of the adjacent vessel wall [23].

Data analysis

Participants were grouped into eGDR quartile categories for comparisons of baseline variables. As appropriate, results are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]), median (interquartile range [IQR]), or frequency (percentage). For continuous variables, group comparisons were conducted using either one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, and for categorical variables, the chi‑square test was used. Four-knot restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was conducted to evaluate the potential nonlinear association of Lp(a) levels with carotid plaque risk, adopting the median Lp(a) level serving as the reference point. Multivariable logistic regression adjusted for confounders examined the independent correlation of Lp(a) with carotid plaque risk, within the entire population and across eGDR quartiles. Age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) were included in Model 1, while additional factors considered in Model 2 were systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Scr, UA, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and FPG levels. After stratification by age, sex, BMI, hypertension, and abnormal glucose regulation (AGR) status, subgroup and interaction analyses were performed. These analyses aimed to evaluate the consistency of the Lp(a)-plaque relationship. Finally, RCS modelling was further conducted to assess how Lp(a) concentrations varied with eGDR within the whole population and among those without diabetes.

Machine learning techniques were employed to mitigate multicollinearity and enhance model robustness. The proportion of missing data was less than 5% for all the variables, as shown in Figure S2. Multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) was employed to manage incomplete data. Specifically, numerical variables were imputed via predictive mean matching (PMM), whereas categorical variables were imputed using logistic regression models. To determine the strongest predictors, a comprehensive variable‑screening approach was adopted. Initially, candidates were screened according to baseline statistical significance and a variance threshold of ≥ 5%. The variable set was then refined using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) and the Boruta algorithm. The variable-selection procedures were conducted with the R packages glmnet, Boruta, and caret.

Subsequently, seven machine learning algorithms were employed to construct predictive models: Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (K-NN), Naive Bayes, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests, and Neural Networks. All the models were trained and evaluated on the consolidated dataset. To optimize the hyperparameters and ensure robust performance estimation, a repeated fivefold cross-validation strategy (5 folds × 5 repeats) was adopted. The average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) calculated across the validation folds was used to quantify model discrimination. The algorithm that yielded the highest AUROC value was selected as the optimal solution. This entire workflow was executed using the caret package in R. Extended models were developed by stepwise augmentation of the baseline model with Lp(a), eGDR, and both jointly, a process executed via the most effective machine learning algorithm. Model discrimination was compared using the C statistic. The statistical significance of differences between C-statistics was assessed via bootstrap resampling, with the Bonferroni method controlling for multiple comparisons at a corrected P of 0.0167. Furthermore, the accuracy of the model predictions was evaluated using calibration curves.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical features

A total of 10,753 subjects were recruited for the study. The mean age of the participants was 51.14 ± 10.39 years, and 59.6% were female. Table 1 summarizes the baseline features by eGDR quartile (Q1: ≤8.59; Q2: 8.59–10.04; Q3: 10.04–10.98; Q4: >10.98). Higher eGDR levels correlated significantly with a stepwise reduction in age, BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, and serum levels of FPG, TG, TC, HbA1c, Scr, UA, as well as BUN (all P < 0.001). A similar downward trend in hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis was observed as eGDR quartiles rose.

Nonlinear relationships between Lp(a) and eGDR with atherosclerosis risk

Figure 1 displays the Lp(a) level distributions for both the whole population and across eGDR quartiles. Overall, Lp(a) levels presented with a right-skewed pattern. A significantly higher median Lp(a) and a higher frequency of Lp(a) ≥ 30 mg/dL were observed within the upper eGDR quartiles (Q3 and Q4) than within the lower quartiles (Q1 and Q2). However, the increase did not follow a consistent monotonic pattern. Notably, the second-lowest eGDR quartile (Q2) displayed the lowest median Lp(a) alongside the lowest proportion of high levels (11.1 mg/dL and 12.3%), even falling below those observed in the lowest eGDR quartile (Q1).

Red lines represent the Lp(a) distribution, and orange lines represent the RCS regression curves

Consistent with these findings, the Lp(a) exhibited a significant nonlinear U‑shaped dependence on eGDR in the RCS analysis (P for nonlinearity < 0.001; Fig. 2). The solid line denotes the effect estimate, along with the shaded region indicating 95% confidence bounds. Specifically, as the eGDR increased from low levels, Lp(a) concentrations gradually decreased, reaching a nadir at an eGDR of approximately 8.0 mg/kg/min. Beyond this inflection point, further increases in the eGDR (indicating improved insulin sensitivity) were related to a rebound rise in Lp(a) levels. Moreover, this nonlinear relationship remained significant in the nondiabetic subgroup (P for nonlinearity = 0.010; Figure S3), with inflection points identified at eGDR values of 9.0 and 11.5 mg/kg/min.

Furthermore, the RCS modeling in Fig. 1 revealed a dose-dependent elevation in atherosclerotic risk with rising Lp(a) levels, and this relationship was characterized by absence of a saturation threshold. This correlation was most pronounced in the lowest eGDR quartile (Q1), where the odds ratio (OR) reached 4.2. Consistent with these results, a significant positive correlation linking Lp(a) levels to plaque incidence was confirmed by logistic regression analyses in both the lowest (Q1) and highest (Q4) eGDR groups (Table 2). With stratification for gender, age, BMI, and hypertension status in the analyses, this positive association remained robust (Table S1). However, the analysis stratified by AGR status indicated that the relationship of Lp(a) with atherosclerotic plaque was nonsignificant among the prediabetes cohort. Notably, significant interactions were observed among AGR status, Lp(a), as well as plaque burden (P for interaction = 0.004). RCS analyses of Lp(a) and atherosclerotic plaque risk, stratified by AGR status, revealed no statistically significant nonlinear associations across the normal glucose, prediabetes, and diabetes subgroups. Furthermore, the OR relating to elevated Lp(a) levels was most pronounced within the diabetes group (Figure S4).

Multivariate model performance and the incremental predictive contribution of Lp(a) and eGDR

Boruta‑based variable selection revealed Lp(a) levels and eGDR as significant contributors to atherosclerosis prediction (Figure S5A), and the specifics of the LASSO regression procedure are depicted as Figure S5B–C. Variables consistently selected by both algorithms were incorporated into a baseline model alongside standard covariates: age, sex, BMI, SBP, DBP, BUN, Scr, UA, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, FPG, AGR status, and hypertension history. Table S2 presents the detailed results of the feature selection process. The performance metrics for the seven machine learning models trained on baseline variables are presented in Table 3. The AUROC values ranged from 0.7213 (K-NN) to 0.7810 (Naive Bayes), with the other models performing as follows: logistic regression (0.7484), SVM (0.7809), XGBoost (0.7761), random forest (0.7731), and neural network (0.7661). Notably, the naive Bayes model emerged as the top-performing model, achieving the highest discriminative ability. The added predictive value of incorporating Lp(a), eGDR, and their combination into the optimal naive Bayes baseline model is presented in Table 4. Including these markers improved the model’s ability to discriminate (C statistic). However, following Bonferroni correction, the improvement observed in the combined Lp(a) and eGDR model (P = 0.018) was not statistically significant (P threshold > 0.0167). The specific performance variations are shown in Fig. 3A. The model calibration curves following the inclusion of the eGDR or Lp(a) levels are shown in Fig. 3B, indicating that the model fits the data well, with no significant deviations observed.

Discussion

Drawing on large-scale data from Chinese health screening centres (2017–2025), the present analysis yielded a population median Lp(a) of 12.4 mg/dL, and 14.4% of subjects had concentrations of ≥ 30 mg/dL. These observations closely correspond to those reported previously on East Asian and Chinese cohorts [15, 24], confirming the representativeness of this study population. Crucially, a significant nonlinear U-shaped correlation of Lp(a) levels with the eGDR was identified. Specifically, Q4 (the highest eGDR quartile) showed the maximum median Lp(a) level along with the greatest frequency of elevated Lp(a) (≥ 30 mg/dL), while Q2 exhibited the lowest values for both parameters. While Lp(a) levels demonstrated a continuous positive association with atherosclerotic plaque risk overall, this risk was significantly pronounced in both the lowest and highest eGDR quartiles. Furthermore, stratified analysis indicated that the association linking Lp(a) to atherosclerotic plaque burden varied across different glucose regulation conditions. Notably, incorporating both Lp(a) and eGDR into the machine learning model yielded a modest improvement in predictive accuracy for atherosclerotic plaques (AUROC: 0.7810 vs. 0.7820).

Both Lp(a) level and IR are widely recognised as independent risk factors for ASCVD [24–27]. Lp(a) drives ASCVD progression through several pathogenic mechanisms, involving the transport of oxidized phospholipids and other atherogenic components, as well as the promotion of inflammation and thrombosis. These mechanisms are consistent with a causal role based on prior genetic evidence [8, 28]. Similarly, IR, defined by impaired insulin sensitivity in the periphery, is closely linked to inflammatory responses, metabolic dysregulation, and endothelial impairment. These factors collectively facilitate the development and advancement of atherosclerotic lesions [29, 30]. The eGDR provides a cost-effective and convenient alternative to measuring IR, and has been validated to predict cardiovascular disease incidence across diverse populations [31, 32].

However, accumulating evidence points to a negative correlation of Lp(a) concentrations with IR [15, 33]. Findings of the China Health and Nutrition Survey specifically indicate an inverse correlation of Lp(a) levels with IR among Chinese adult males [15]. This trend is further corroborated in hypertensive patients, where Lp(a) concentrations significantly and progressively decrease as the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA) index increases. Indeed, Lp(a) concentrations have been shown to be negatively proportional to fasting blood glucose, C-peptide, insulin, and HOMA index [17]. Similar inverse patterns have been observed in cohorts with dyslipidaemia and obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome [33, 34]. Reinforcing these findings, a meta‑analysis suggested that those in the bottom Lp(a) quintile (thresholds < 3–5 mg/dL) presented with a 38% elevated probability of T2D (95% CI 29–48%, P < 0.001) than those in the highest quintile did (thresholds > 27–55 mg/dL) [35]. The underlying mechanism may be attributed to exceptionally high insulin sensitivity, which is often accompanied by markedly reduced circulating insulin levels. This reduction may attenuate or even abolish the negative regulatory effect of insulin on the transcription of hepatic apo(a), thereby triggering a compensatory increase in Lp(a) synthesis [36, 37]. A proposed explanation for the negative correlation of Lp(a) levels with diabetes susceptibility involves elevated oxidative stress, which promotes the oxidation of Lp(a)-bound phospholipids, ultimately triggering mild inflammatory responses and autoimmune reactions [16, 38]. Notably, however, a negligible relationship was reported in one study linking log-transformed Lp(a) to the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, yielding a Spearman’s correlation coefficient of − 0.047 [39]. The ODYSSEY OUTCOMES study reported that prediabetic individuals had greater concentrations of Lp(a) than people with T2D, with pharmacological lowering linked to increased new-onset diabetes. This suggests a complex, nonlinear correlation of Lp(a) levels with IR, where extremes have distinct metabolic implications [40]. In this study, IR was assessed using the eGDR, revealing a U-shaped relationship with Lp(a) levels rather than a simple linear correlation. Specifically, an inflection point in eGDR was detected at the value of 8.0 mg/kg/min. Above this threshold (indicating higher insulin sensitivity), Lp(a) levels tended to increase as the eGDR increased. Conversely, below this threshold (indicating greater IR), Lp(a) levels increased progressively as the eGDR declined. Consistent with this bimodal pattern, both the median Lp(a) concentration as well as the percentage of cases with increased Lp(a) concentration (≥ 30 mg/dL) reached their nadir among the second-lowest eGDR quartile.

This study revealed a linear positive relation of Lp(a) levels with atherosclerotic plaques, a finding corroborated by robust evidence from large-scale cohorts. Notably, the UK Biobank, comprising 460,506 middle‑aged individuals, suggested that Lp(a) was linearly correlated with ASCVD risk, which was continuous and lacked any threshold effect (HR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.10–1.12) [6]. Consistently, the long-term cohort of healthy women reported a steady increase in major adverse cardiovascular events that corresponded to elevated Lp(a) concentrations, with no observable plateau [41]. A meta-analysis demonstrated an independent, near-linear correlation of baseline Lp(a) levels with cardiovascular outcomes, even among patients treated with statins [42]. Furthermore, data from this study revealed a significant positive linear relationship linking Lp(a) to carotid plaque, but this association was observed exclusively in the extreme quartiles of eGDR (Q1 and Q4). Corral et al. also emphasized that extremes of the Lp(a) spectrum—both markedly elevated and suppressed levels—are associated with significant metabolic consequences [43]. These findings indicate that future research into the influence of Lp(a) on atherosclerosis and ASCVD risk should consider IR levels.

Beyond the global assessment, further subgroup and interaction analyses were also carried out. No significant interactions were detected regarding Lp(a) and traditional risk factors, e.g., gender, age, BMI, or hypertension. This consistency aligns with the view that high Lp(a) concentrations, independent of these conventional factors, confer residual cardiovascular risk [44]. In contrast, stratification by glucose metabolism status revealed striking heterogeneity. While a positive correlation of Lp(a) with atherosclerosis risk was evident in both normoglycaemic individuals and patients with diagnosed diabetes, this association was notably attenuated and statistically nonsignificant in the prediabetic population. Furthermore, the AGR and Lp(a) demonstrated a significant interplay (P = 0.004). These outcomes underscore the pivotal effect of glucose metabolic dynamics on modulating the correlation of Lp(a) levels with atherosclerosis risk, although the exact mechanisms are still incompletely defined. During the prediabetic stage, hyperinsulinaemia may persistently suppress hepatic Lp(a) production by inhibiting apo(a) transcription in hepatocytes, thereby attenuating the correlation of Lp(a) with carotid plaque risk [37]. Furthermore, the occurrence of elevated Lp(a) levels alongside IR often occurs alongside cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, obesity, and kidney dysfunction. This clustering of risk factors may reflect differences in unmeasured confounders, including baseline risk and pharmacotherapy, which could obscure the true correlation of Lp(a) levels with plaque risk [39, 45].

Given the significant nonlinear interplay between Lp(a) levels and IR, and their independent contributions to cardiovascular risk, integrating these markers is hypothesized to substantially enhance the performance of prediction models for atherosclerosis. In support of this, a UK Biobank study demonstrated that stratifying populations by Lp(a) level (cut-off: 125 nmol/L) and IR status significantly refined ASCVD risk stratification [39]. Moreover, machine learning models offer distinct advantages over traditional statistical methods in capturing complex nonlinear patterns and higher-order interactions [46]. In the present study, the naive Bayes algorithm effectively optimized atherosclerosis risk prediction. Although the simultaneous inclusion of both Lp(a) levels and eGDR yielded a greater improvement in model performance than either indicator alone did (P = 0.018), this effect was not statistically significant following correction for multiple comparisons. Given the significant nonlinear interplay of Lp(a) and eGDR demonstrated in the present study, the observed modest increase in AUROC value underscores the importance of incorporating IR when assessing Lp(a)-related risk. Consequently, the value of this combination model lies not only in the slight enhancement of predictive accuracy but also in providing a novel biological perspective for personalized risk stratification.

Strengths and limitations

This work capitalized on a broad‑scale dataset from Chinese health screening centres (2017–2025), ensuring robust statistical power. It systematically characterizes the significant nonlinear (U-shaped) association of Lp(a) levels with the eGDR, providing novel perspectives on the metabolic interplay between these two markers and emphasising the necessity of considering IR levels when assessing the Lp(a)-atherosclerosis association. Furthermore, machine learning approaches were applied to capture complex nonlinear patterns as well as interactions, offering a methodological advantage over traditional linear regression models in risk prediction. Finally, by focusing on the combined predictive utility of Lp(a) and eGDR, this study highlights a potential strategy for personalized risk stratification.

It needs to be noted that this study’s observational design prevents definitive conclusions regarding causality, even though Lp(a) and eGDR were identified as independent contributors to atherosclerosis. Secondly, single baseline measurements were used for both Lp(a) and the eGDR. While Lp(a) concentrations are known to exhibit relatively low biological variability [3], a single assessment may not fully capture long-term exposure. Similarly, given that IR is a dynamic state, its temporal variability may serve as an additional potent predictor of cardiovascular disease [47]. Consequently, whether the observed nonlinear relationship of Lp(a) with eGDR evolves over time requires further investigation. Additionally, it is notable that the presence of carotid plaques was utilized as a surrogate endpoint for atherosclerosis rather than hard ASCVD events. While carotid plaque detection is widely recognized as an indicator of subclinical atherosclerosis, prospective studies recording clinical endpoints are needed to confirm its direct impact on cardiovascular outcomes.

In addition, regarding the applicability of the eGDR, its formula (including the constant 21.158) was originally derived from a type 1 diabetes cohort. Slight deviations in absolute values may be introduced within the healthy population. However, the eGDR has been validated as a robust surrogate for the hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp in the general population [48–51]. Therefore, it remains effective for ranking insulin sensitivity. The preference for the eGDR stems from its ability to integrate WC, hypertension, and HbA1c levels, providing a holistic view of metabolic risk. The TyG index and TG/HDL ratio, though frequently applied, exhibit variable diagnostic performance in terms of sensitivity and specificity across diverse patient populations [10, 52]. Furthermore, the eGDR was favoured over the diagnosis of metabolic syndrome because the analysis of IR as a continuous variable tends to preserve greater statistical power than the categorical classification of metabolic syndrome does. Additionally, the reliance on a health examination cohort introduces potential healthy volunteer bias, which could undermine the applicability of these findings to the wider community. Moreover, the study population was predominantly recruited from southern China, resulting in limited ethnic diversity. Since Lp(a) levels are known to vary significantly across ethnic groups [53], caution needs to be exercised when these results are extrapolated to different groups. Finally, cross-validation with five folds enhanced model robustness and mitigated overfitting, yet external validation using different groups remains necessary to verify the model’s generalizability and clinical utility.

Conclusions

This study offers novel insights demonstrating that the strength of the relationship of Lp(a) levels with atherosclerosis risk varies according to IR status. Specifically, a linearly positive association was found of Lp(a) concentrations with atherosclerotic plaque burden across populations at the extremes of the eGDR spectrum, encompassing both optimal insulin sensitivity (highest eGDR) and severe IR (lowest eGDR). Consequently, clinical interpretation of Lp(a) levels must be contextualized within the patient’s broader metabolic profile rather than evaluated in isolation. Integrating Lp(a) levels with eGDR yields a modest predictive performance for atherosclerosis, thereby facilitating personalized cardiovascular prevention. Importantly, this strategy enhances risk detection across heterogeneous metabolic states, promoting more effective cardiovascular care.
