Family‐Centred Telehealth‐Enhanced Dietary Counselling for the Treatment of Protein‐Energy Malnutrition in Rural‐Living Older Adults Transitioning From Rehabilitation to Home: A Pilot Controlled Trial
Authors: Skye Marshall, Molly M. Fahey, Barbara S. van der Meij, Marian A.E. de van der Schueren, Lucy Leigh, Elizabeth Johnston, Charlene Wright, Robina Rogan, Megan Crichton, Helena Rodi, Anna Chapman, Xueying Tang, Anna Ugalde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionFamily carers have a key role in supporting malnourished older adults; yet, intervention evidence is lacking in the rehabilitation setting. This study aimed to explore the preliminary effects and acceptability of a family‐centred, telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention for treating protein‐energy malnutrition (PEM) in rural‐living older adults transitioning from rehabilitation to home, compared with usual care, in matched patient‐carer dyads.MethodsA pragmatic, historically controlled, prospective, two‐arm non‐randomised controlled trial was conducted as a pilot study. Fifteen malnourished older adults admitted to rural rehabilitation units in New South Wales, Australia, and their family carers, were recruited and matched to 15 historical controls. The Patient‐Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG‐SGA) was the primary outcome for nutrition status assessed at rehabilitation admission, discharge, and 3 months post‐discharge. Quality of life, physical function, length of stay, discharge location, institutionalisation, mortality, carer burden, and service satisfaction were secondary outcomes. The historical control group received usual care. The intervention group received a telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention developed with co‐design principles, which engaged the family carer as a partner in the nutrition care team, delivered during the rehabilitation admission and for 3 months post‐discharge.ResultsThe difference in PG‐SGA score from baseline to 3 months post‐discharge between the two groups was 3.46 (95%CI: −2.07, 9.01; p = 0.238). The intervention group had a higher proportion of patients who were well‐nourished at 3 months post‐discharge compared to controls (60% vs 13%). At 3 months post‐discharge, the intervention group had lower odds of being rated malnourished or with more severe malnutrition (i.e., moderate vs. well‐nourished, severe vs. moderate) on the PG‐SGA (OR 0.01, 95%CI: 0.00, 0.27; p = 0.005). There was a trend towards the intervention group having reduced odds of being discharged to a location other than home (OR 0.18, 95%CI: 0.03, 1.07; p = 0.06). The intervention was perceived as acceptable to patients and family carers. There were no differences between groups in other outcomes.ConclusionCompared with usual care, the family‐centred telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention for the treatment of PEM in rural‐living older adults transitioning from rehabilitation to home demonstrated preliminary efficacy and was acceptable to both patients and family carers. Future research aiming to improve PEM should meaningfully engage family carers as partners in the nutrition care team.

Introduction

Family members, friends, and neighbours who support older adults alongside the traditional health and aged care systems, often referred to as family carers, play an important role in supporting older patients diagnosed with protein‐energy malnutrition (PEM) [1]. PEM is the unintentional and preventable loss of lean tissues, such as muscle, blood and immune cells, and viscera, with or without fat loss, due to prolonged inadequate dietary intake or uptake of protein and energy [2, 3]. Inpatient rehabilitation settings have one of the highest prevalences of PEM (30%–65%) [4, 5, 6, 7] due to overlapping risk factors, such as multimorbidity and polypharmacy, hospital acquired PEM, and the physiological and social changes that occur during the ageing process [8]. Living in a rural and remote area has also been associated with increased risk of PEM [9].

Although PEM is preventable and treatable, many malnourished patients find it difficult to adhere to dietary recommendations due to diverse physiological, socio‐economic, cultural, and environmental barriers [8, 10, 11]. Limited opportunity for treatment during the rehabilitation admission, failure to engage family carers, and limited post‐discharge support, particularly in the rural setting, create further treatment barriers over which patients and family carers have limited agency to address [8, 12]. Failure to treat PEM in the rural rehabilitation setting has been associated with increased risk of worsening nutritional status, longer and/or re‐hospitalisation, institutionalisation, physical dysfunction, reduced quality of life, and mortality [6, 13, 14]. Overall, these negative consequences directly undermine the goal of rehabilitation by decreasing independence and increasing health system burden.

A study conducted in rural and remote Australia found that family carers of malnourished rehabilitation patients were often not aware their care‐recipient was malnourished, yet saw themselves as primarily responsible for the care‐recipient's nutritional wellbeing [10]. A direct causal link between the poor nutrition knowledge of family carers and increased risk of PEM in older adults has been established [15]. Several reviews have found that empowering family carers to play a key role as part of the care team for malnourished older adults living at home can improve the care‐recipient's nutritional status, quality of life, and physical function, without increasing carer strain [16, 17]. With the increased risk of PEM in older adults in rural settings, combined with fewer community‐based healthcare resources for support, a sub‐acute rehabilitation admission offers the opportunity to engage with family carers to improve the nutrition‐related family‐centred care that will follow patients through the transition from rehabilitation to home [9]. However, a family‐centred model of care for the treatment of PEM in rural and remote areas has not been developed or evaluated.

As populations age globally, and with rural and remote areas consistently having a higher proportion of older adults [18, 19], family‐centred models of care are imperative. Reflecting this, the United Nations has identified integrating formal and informal (i.e., family) care systems as a priority research area [20]. Additionally, consumer engagement in the development and delivery of healthcare has become widely recognised as essential, with co‐design principles and methods increasingly applied to enhance the design, acceptability, and feasibility of interventions [21, 22].

Research Aim

To explore the preliminary effects of a family‐centred, telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention for treating PEM in rural‐living older adults transitioning from rehabilitation to home on nutrition status, patient‐centred outcomes, and healthcare utilisation in matched carer‐patient dyads, compared with usual care, and to describe its acceptability to patients and family carers.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

A pragmatic, historically controlled, prospective, two‐arm non‐randomised controlled trial was conducted as a pilot study. A historical control was chosen due to the high risk of intervention contamination within the small rural rehabilitation units with shared rooms and a cluster design was avoided due to unknown differences between sites prior to commencement. The protocol, including all intervention and data collection materials, has been published as open access elsewhere [3]. This study has been reported according to the CONSORT 2010 statement: extension to randomised pilot and feasibility trials [23] and the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) Checklist [24]. The study was prospectively registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry Number (ACTRN12618000338268) and received approval by the Northern NSW Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/18/NCC/47). All participants (patients and family carers) provided written informed consent.

Eligibility

Participants were older adults (patients) and their family carers (Table 1). The scored PG‐SGA assessment, validated in geriatric rehabilitation [25], was conducted within 1‐week of being admitted to rehabilitation to determine study eligibility.

Setting and Recruitment

The historical control group was recruited from August 2013 to February 2014 from two sub‐acute inpatient government‐funded rehabilitation units (herein referred to as ‘units’) within the same rural local health district in New South Wales, Australia. Unit A (34 beds) was classified as being in a regional centre (Modified Monash [MM] category 2) with a catchment area spanning MM2 to MM5 (small rural town). Unit B (24 beds) was classified as being in a large rural town (MM3) with catchment area spanning MM3 to MM5. Both units were co‐located with an acute care hospital with the purpose of supporting people to achieve sufficient independence with activities of daily living via medical and allied health therapies. Medical therapies included geriatrician care with or without other specialities, as needed. Admissions were accepted for all indications except dementia, which was managed in specialist facilities. The intervention group was recruited from June 2018 to April 2019 from Unit A only (MM3). Unit B chose not to participate in the intervention phase. The medical and allied health team, food service, and admission criteria for the units was consistent for both control and intervention phases.

Potentially eligible patients were identified by the rehabilitation clinical team using routine screening for PEM risk with the validated Malnutrition Screening Tool [26, 27] and clinical judgement upon admission and were referred to the study Accredited Practising Dietitian (herein referred to as the study dietitian [SM or MF]) for full eligibility assessment. The sample size for the intervention group was n = 15 patients and n = 15 family carers [n = 30 individual participants making n = 15 dyads] based on resource and data availability. Patients in the intervention group were matched to patients in the historical control group to create a 1:1 ratio of intervention versus control, according to three hierarchical criteria: baseline PG‐SGA rating, age, then sex.

Control Conditions

The control group received usual care, which included being placed on a standard high‐protein high‐energy hospital diet code [28] during their rehabilitation admission unless contraindicated. Patients received assessment and additional intervention from the health service dietitian, if referred by the usual clinical care pathways. Referrals could be initiated by another healthcare professional if nutritional risk was detected, or upon request by the patient or their family carer. For patients referred to a dietitian during admission, support may have involved face‐to‐face dietary counselling, modification of the hospital diet code, or provision of oral nutritional supplements. Post‐discharge dietetics support may have involved referral to a community dietitian for follow‐up with or without ongoing prescription of oral nutritional supplements. The model of care delivered as usual care is represented in Figure 1, with the role of the patient and family carer informed by qualitative interviews conducted at the study sites [10] and role of the dietitian informed by the Nutrition Care Process [29]. The availability of dietetic services to each of the units were approximately 0.15 full time equivalents for both control and intervention periods. The nutrition care policies, scope of practice, nutrition screening and assessment procedures, and dietary menu options also did not change.

Intervention Condition

Development of the Intervention

Literature, consumers (patients, family carers), and stakeholders (health care professionals, consumer advocates, researchers) contributed to the design of the family‐centred telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention for the treatment of PEM in rehabilitation and during their transition home. The development of the intervention was based on the co‐design principles of being purpose‐driven, utilising inclusive collaboration, sharing power, and adaptive learning [30]. The intervention was iteratively developed by research dietitians (SM supported by EI, MF) based on the activities described in Table 2. Decisions were made by the research team, with priority given to consumer‐expressed preferences and needs, where feasible and evidence‐based.

Intervention Model of Care

The intervention delivered a model of care which integrated formal and informal systems for rehabilitation patients with PEM, facilitated by telehealth, to empower family carers in providing effective individualised nutrition support (Figure 2). The model of care involved four individualised and needs‐based strategies and was delivered by the study dietitian who was not otherwise involved in the care of patients at the rehabilitation units. These strategies were: low‐bandwidth telehealth (i.e., telephone calls) enhanced nutrition counselling during rehabilitation admission, discharge planning, low‐bandwidth telehealth follow‐up, and the provision of individualised educational resources. During follow‐up, a minimum of four telehealth consultations were provided at intervals of 3‐weeks or less; however, additional consultations were provided as needed. The strategies applied the Theory of Planned Behaviour and Reasoned Action [33] to increase an individual's ability to make recommended changes. All engagements between the study dietitian, family carer, and patient included education and shared goal setting, problem‐solving, and contingency planning. The strategies aimed to build the family carers' independence in providing nutrition care to support the care‐recipient during and beyond the post‐discharge phase.

Intervention model of care: A family‐centred telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention delivered as inpatient and post‐discharge care in rural New South Wales, Australia, to support patient nourishment and independence.

The model of care was designed to position the family carer and patient as equal partners in care, with the study dietitian aiming to engage both parties together, or equally, in every instance of care provision. However, in practice, family carers often assumed higher responsibility for the patient's nutrition care plan and dietary intake than the patient, consistent with previous findings and the control model of care [10].

The intervention had institutional (jurisdiction and hospital leadership, research and governance), departmental (rehabilitation nurse unit manager, jurisdictional head of nutrition and dietetics, food services department), and the rehabilitation multidisciplinary team support and buy‐in which was established during the processes outlined in Table 2. The intervention model of care was integrated within the health service systems and aligned with existing protocols and software. Intervention patients were not seen by health service usual care dietitians during the intervention period. Health service‐related intervention costs such as oral nutrition support supplements or changes to hospital diet codes were absorbed by the health service during patient admission. Food and supplements were not provided by the study post‐discharge. The cost of providing telehealth, salary for the interventionist, printing resources, and travel for home visits were provided by study funding (AUD$13,000) with all other costs provided in‐kind. No specialist training was required for the interventionist (MF) as the model of care was within existing skillsets for Accredited Practising Dietitians.

Outcomes at baseline (admission) and discharge were measured at bedside, and 3 month post‐discharge assessments were conducted via home visits. All outcome data were collected via patient and carer interview and/or the medical record by the study dietitian, further described in detail elsewhere [3].

Implementation of the intervention was evaluated and reported according to the preliminary Consensus Statements for Dietary Counselling Interventions for the Management of Malnutrition in Adults in a Clinical Research Context [39]. For each participant engagement, the study dietitian completed an intervention log, detailing engagement duration, persons present, type and purpose of contact, timepoint, nutrition care plan outcome and details, comments (e.g., receptivity, health behaviour models used, problems solved, participant concerns, other), communication with other healthcare professionals (including handovers), and agreed monitoring plans. A 24‐h dietary recall was also implemented to measure impact of the dietary counselling on energy and macronutrient intake, with intake analysed using FoodWorks Professional nutrient analysis software (Xyris, QLD, Australia, Version 10), supported by the AUSNUT 2017 food composition database.

Statistical Approach

For longitudinal continuous outcomes, linear mixed models, with a random effect for participant, and fixed effects for group (control vs intervention), timepoint (baseline, discharge, and 3 months post‐discharge), and the interaction between group and time, were utilised. Effect estimates were reported as the mean difference between intervention and control at each timepoint (95% CI), as well as marginal mean estimates (and 95% CI) for each group at each timepoint. For the longitudinal ordinal outcome (PG‐SGA rating), a generalised linear mixed model was utilised, using the cumulative log link function, a random effect for participant, and fixed effects for group (control vs intervention), time, and the interaction between group and time. Effect estimates were reported as Odds Ratios (OR) for scoring higher on the PG‐SGA rating at each timepoint (for control vs intervention), and crude proportions for the response levels are reported for each group at each timepoint.

Dichotomous cross‐sectional outcomes were modelled using logistic regression, with a fixed effect for group. Effect estimates were reported as OR (control vs intervention), and predicted probabilities (95% CI) of the event were also reported for each group. The length of stay (LOS) outcome was modelled using a negative binomial regression model, with a fixed effect for group. Effect estimates were reported as Rate Ratios (RR) and predicted mean LOS (and 95% CI) was also reported for each group.

For the longitudinal outcomes (PG‐SGA score, PG‐SGA rating, weight, BMI), missing data were assumed to be Missing at Random and handled via the Maximum‐Likelihood estimation method. For the longitudinal outcomes of Modified Barthel Index and AQoL‐6D, two participants had completely missing data on the response, and were excluded from the model under the Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) assumption. For the cross‐sectional outcomes, participants with missing responses were excluded from the analysis, and the missing data were assumed to be MCAR. Analyses were primarily conducted within the R Statistical Programming Language [R statistical environment, version 4.4.1]. The Zarit Burden Interview score and nutrient intakes were compared between timepoints using the Friedman Test on IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0.0.0). As this was a pilot study, statistical significance was only one indicator used to evaluate differences between groups in outcomes, alongside measures of precision and effect sizes. Service satisfaction results were quality checked and reported by an independent investigator. Clinically meaningful effect sizes, whilst relevant, were also interpreted with caution because, despite patient matching, there remained some clinically relevant differences in baseline values, such as the PG‐SGA score. For longitudinal outcomes, change scores were not directly calculated (due to substantial missing follow‐up data); however, differential change estimates were also obtained from the models via the interaction term.

Protocol Deviations

The planned qualitative interviews were not performed due to resource constraints; and measurement of mid‐arm circumference was not pursued due to common contraindications leading to a high frequency of missing data. Rehospitalisation length of stay could not be analysed due to a high level of missing data and data inaccuracy from the primary source data.

Results

Sample Characteristics

Between July 2018 and April 2019, 17 patient‐carer dyads were recruited to the intervention group; however, two withdrew prior to commencement. The reason for withdrawal in both cases was that the family carer was overwhelmed with general caring responsibilities. After matching intervention participants against historical controls, 30 patient participants were included for data analysis (n = 15 per group), and 15 family carers were included (n = 15 in the intervention group). Only two family carers were recruited during the control period and therefore their data were not presented. Patient participants were mostly older Caucasian women, born in Australia, with primary or secondary school education, and were married or widowed/divorced (Table 4). Patients were primarily admitted to rehabilitation for the management of an acute condition (e.g., stroke, myocardial event) rather than chronic conditions (e.g., cancer, Parkinson's disease) from acute care. In both groups, two thirds were moderately malnourished upon admission, and one third was severely malnourished. Receipt of domiciliary services and community nursing at home, prior to admission, was poorly stated by participants and therefore data not presented.

Implementation of the Intervention

The implementation of intervention conditions, informed by the intervention log, have been reported in Table S1. Overall, there were 45 dietary counselling engagements delivered face‐to‐face during rehabilitation (100% involved the patient; 47% involved the family carer), 13 telehealth engagements during rehabilitation (15% involved the patient; 100% involved the family carer), 65 telehealth engagements in the 3 months post‐rehabilitation discharge (65% involved the patient; 77% involved the family carer). Of the telehealth engagements, most were dietary counselling, although several were brief monitoring or logistic support planning. Only nine of the 15 dyads received the discharge planning session integrated with the rehabilitation discharge planning as many patients were discharged from the unit early; when this occurred, asynchronous discharge planning was attempted. Face‐to‐face engagements during the rehabilitation admission had a median duration of 15 min (range 10–15 min) if delivered at bedside, or 27.5 min (range 10–90 min) if delivered in a private room in the unit. Telehealth engagements during rehabilitation had a median duration of 11 min (range 1–30 min), and 16 min (range 2–70 min) post‐discharge. A total of 100 engagements with patients and/or family carers involved monitoring tolerance and/or adherence to the nutrition care plan, which included dietary intake and behaviour change. Across the three timepoints, the care‐recipients substantially increased their intake of energy and macronutrients, with the most significant improvement observed in protein intake (47.0 g vs 67.7 g vs 76.1 g, p = 0.045; Table S2).

Nutrition Status and Patient‐Centred Outcomes

The marginal mean PG‐SGA score was lower in the control group, with a mean difference between cohorts of −5.4 (95%CI: −9.9, −0.9) at baseline (p = 0.020), −1.6 (95%CI: −6.2, 3.0) at discharge (p = 0.493), and −1.9 (95%CI: −7.51, 3.64) at 3 months post‐discharge (p = 0.491). Although PG‐SGA ratings did not differ significantly between groups at baseline (Table 4), the intervention group had a higher mean PG‐SGA score, indicating greater symptom burden or more indicators of PEM than the control group. The difference in the change from baseline to 3 months post‐discharge between the two groups was 3.46 (95% CI: −2.07, 9.01; p = 0.238), meaning the improvement in the intervention group was 3.46 units greater than in the control group. The intervention group had a higher proportion of patients that were well‐nourished at the 3 months post‐discharge timepoint than the control group (60% vs 13%). There was no difference in the odds of scoring a worse PG‐SGA rating at discharge between the groups (OR 0.38; 95%CI: 0.07, 2.22; p = 0.284), but at 3 months post‐discharge the intervention group had statistically significantly lower odds of being rated malnourished or with more severe malnutrition (i.e., rating B vs. A, C vs. B) on the PG‐SGA (OR 0.01, 95%CI: 0.00, 0.27; p = 0.005).

There were no meaningful differences between or within groups at baseline or at any follow‐up timepoint for total body weight, BMI, and quality of life (Table S3). Although not statistically significant between groups, there was an overall trend for higher point estimates at discharge and 3 months post‐discharge in the intervention compared to controls for physical function as indicated by the Modified Barthel Index. There was no difference in the odds of death between groups (OR 0.55; 95%CI: 0.32, 39.14; p = 0.30).

Healthcare Utilisation Outcomes

The rate ratio for rehabilitation length of stay was 1.0006 (95%CI: 0.655, 1.646); p = 0.982). There was no difference in the odds between groups of being institutionalised (OR 0.16 (95%CI: 0.02, 1.66); p = 0.12) or being re‐hospitalised in the 3 months following discharge (OR 3.21 (95%CI: 0.63, 16.38); p = 0.16). There was a trend towards the intervention group having reduced odds of being discharged to a location other than home (OR 0.18 (95%CI: 0.03, 1.07); p = 0.06).

Intervention Acceptability

On the Zarit Carer Burden Interview, family carers predominantly reported little to no burden at the time of the patient's discharge from rehabilitation (scores of 0–21 on a scale of 0‐88). Although data attrition was higher at 3 months post‐discharge, the overall pattern persisted over time with most family carers continuing to report little to no burden (Table 4). The median Zarit Burden Interview score at discharge was 11.0 (IQR: 5, 16.5) and at 3 months post‐discharge was 9.5 (IQR: 2.5, 23.5) (p = 0.317 between timepoints).

Of the 10 family carers who completed the carer nutrition service satisfaction survey, responses indicated that the intervention was acceptable (Table 4), with no carers selecting ‘disagree’ or ‘strongly disagree’ on any of the 15‐items. Eight carers ‘agreed’ or ‘strongly agreed’ that the care the patient received ‘helped their body to heal’ and ‘improved their general health’, and seven carers ‘agreed’ or ‘strongly agreed’ the nutrition care plan ‘improved the results of the patient's medical treatment’. Eight carers also ‘agreed’ or ‘strongly agreed’ that after the patient was discharged, they ‘found the telephone calls from the dietitian were useful’ and nine ‘agreed’ or ‘strongly agreed’ that they ‘valued having continued support from the dietitian via telephone calls’. Qualitative statements (n = 5) on the surveys were all supportive of the intervention, “It's nice to be kept informed. I wasn't aware that a dietitian's support was available before”, and “Very helpful and educational for me as well”.

Patient satisfaction with the nutrition intervention (n = 10 completed) was higher than that of carers, with responses to all 15‐items indicating ‘agree’ or ‘strongly agree’, except for one neutral response to ‘the care I received from the dietitian improved my general health’ and ‘the care I received from the dietitian helped me to recover faster’. The four qualitative responses were supportive of the intervention, for example, “I am pleased with everything… It has helped me a lot”.

Discussion

This study described the piloting of a novel, family‐centred telehealth‐enhanced model of care for the treatment of PEM in older adults transitioning from sub‐acute rehabilitation to home in rural Australia. Developed using co‐design principles, the model aimed to empower family carers as partners in the nutrition care team and to extend dietetic support beyond hospital discharge using low‐bandwidth telehealth. The intervention was found to be acceptable to both patients and carers, with carer burden remaining low.

Preliminary findings suggest that the co‐designed model of care demonstrated potential efficacy for improving nutritional status, a key indicator of PEM, with the intervention group showing markedly lower odds of worse PG‐SGA ratings at 3 months post‐discharge compared with controls. Although improvements in the PG‐SGA score were not statistically significant, baseline differences between groups, where intervention participants were more malnourished at admission, may have reduced observable change. The direction of effect and consistency across indicators, including trends towards better physical function and higher rates of discharge to home, support the interventions' promise for promoting nutritional recovery, independence, and ageing in place, all outcomes preferred by consumers and associated with economic benefits [40, 41, 42].

The mechanisms underlying these outcomes likely relate to several interlinked factors. The intervention model of care applied the Theory of Planned Behaviour [33] to strengthen family carers' confidence, perceived control, and motivation to implement nutrition recommendations, which may have increased adherence to care plans. Regular telehealth contact enabled continuity of care, early problem‐solving, and reinforcement of behaviour change, while shared goal setting fostered collaboration between patients, carers, and the dietitian. Co‐design principles further enhanced contextual relevance and trust, ensuring that the approach reflected the realities of rural life and the caregiving relationship. Further, the intervention included both patient‐level and health service‐level factors with institutional buy‐in, factors known to improve the implementation of healthcare interventions in rural contexts [43]. Collectively, these mechanisms may explain the observed improvements in nutrition status and sustained engagement among participants.

These findings align with broader evidence showing that malnutrition‐focused telehealth interventions can improve dietary intake and quality of life among older adults, though few have yet demonstrated clear effects on nutritional status [32, 44]. The family‐centred framework used in this pilot is also novel. Few previous interventions for PEM have meaningfully engaged family carers, despite evidence that their involvement enhances adherence to nutrition care and patient outcomes [10, 31, 32]. Recent reviews confirm this gap, only one of 13 telehealth malnutrition interventions [33] and zero of six rehabilitation‐based interventions [45] involved family carers in a substantive role. This pilot therefore adds valuable evidence on how structured, theory‐informed engagement of carers can be integrated into formal nutrition services. Further exploration of implementation barriers and enablers is warranted, particularly as telehealth and co‐designed models can now be adapted to diverse settings, including metropolitan areas where carer participation may be constrained by other factors [46].

Implementation of the intervention was feasible within existing health service structures, requiring minimal additional staffing and using low‐bandwidth telehealth that was compatible with rural connectivity constraints. The model leveraged the existing expertise of Accredited Practising Dietitians, supported by flexible scheduling and brief, focused telehealth engagements. Key facilitators included high patient and carer engagement, simple technology requirements, and strong institutional support for multidisciplinary collaboration. However, some challenges were identified, such as early patient discharge disrupting planned sessions and variability in carer availability. Integration of the model into standard rehabilitation discharge workflows or community dietetic services could overcome these barriers, ensuring that family‐centred nutrition care becomes a routine component of transition planning. This would allow sustainable scale‐up without major system redesign, particularly in rural and resource‐limited settings.

Beyond individual‐level outcomes, this model of care has broader health system relevance. By bridging inpatient and community care, it addresses a well‐documented service gap interrupting continuity of care [47], aligning with national policy priorities on integrated, person‐centred care and equitable access for rural populations [48, 49]. The approach supports more efficient use of health resources by potentially reducing unplanned readmissions and institutionalisation whilst aiming to enhance patient autonomy and ageing in place. The model's low‐bandwidth telehealth design and carer‐led focus make it scalable within existing workforce and infrastructure constraints, offering a practical template for health services seeking sustainable nutrition interventions in both rural and urban contexts.

Limitations and Implications for Future Research

As a pragmatic pilot, this study had limitations impacting the ability to interpret causality. Key limitations included the small sample size, use of a historical control, low family carer participation in the control group, and potential bias arising from the study dietitian delivering and assessing the intervention. Regarding the use of a historical control; there was a substantial time‐gap between the control and intervention periods. Although clinical teams, the unit, and key setting factors were observed to remain consistent over this time; there is likely to be period effects of unknown influence on the outcomes of the study. While attrition bias in PG‐SGA data is possible, missing data were managed appropriately and were due to other study outcomes such as readmission or death. For future studies, it is recommended to reduce the number of outcome measures, and/or select shorter versions of validated tools to improve data collection feasibility, such as the EQ‐5D‐5L [50] or low burden technology to capture dietary intake rather than completing 24‐h dietary recalls [46, 51].

Whilst this study tested a specific delivery of dietetic counselling, future research should evaluate this model in a fully powered trial, as well as evaluate variations of the model to suit other settings and cultures. Adaptations should reflect local service models and consumer preferences using co‐design methodology and principles to ensure acceptability and uptake [21], and continuity of care should always be considered for improved patient experience and long‐term outcomes. Evaluation of whether the discharge planning session is more effective if coordinated and conducted synchronistical with the unit's discharge planning would be of value given the feasibility constraints of this intervention delivery method. Finally, all dietary counselling interventions should be fully reported to support translation to practice, such as using the Consensus Statements for Dietary Counselling Interventions for the Management of Malnutrition in Adults in a Clinical Research Context [39].

Conclusion

Compared with usual care, a family‐centred telehealth‐enhanced dietary counselling intervention for the treatment of PEM in older adults as they transition from rehabilitation to home in a rural setting of Australia may be acceptable to both patients and family carers and may lead to improved clinical and patient‐centred outcomes, such as nutrition status and institutionalisation. Future research is needed to confirm these findings. Studies aiming to address PEM in other settings should meaningfully engage family carers as partners in the nutrition care team, focus on continuity of care using telehealth and consumer‐co‐designed strategies, and fully report dietary counselling methods to promote translation to practice.
