Exploring barriers to help-seeking for sexual issues in Iranian postmenopausal women: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Maryam Taherpour, Shahideh Jahanian Sadatmahalleh, Zainab Alimoradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSexual health is integral to the well-being of postmenopausal women, yet in Iran, cultural taboos and systemic limitations create significant barriers to seeking help for sexual issues. This study explores the lived experiences and perspectives of Iranian postmenopausal women regarding barriers and facilitators to sexual help-seeking to inform culturally sensitive interventions.MethodsA qualitative study using conventional content analysis per Graneheim and Lundman’s methodology was conducted. Seventeen semi-structured interviews with sexually active, married postmenopausal women were held from December 2024 to April 2025 at health centers affiliated with Qazvin University of Medical Sciences. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed to identify themes.ResultsAnalysis yielded 366 primary codes, 248 main codes, 35 subcategories, 9 categories, and three themes: (1) Individual, Family, and Social Barriers to Seeking Sexual Help, (2) Communication and Structural Challenges of Healthcare Providers in Sexual Help-Seeking, and (3) Infrastructure Deficiencies in Sexual Health Services.ConclusionSexual help-seeking is hindered by psychological, cultural, and systemic factors. Interventions should include education campaigns, stigma reduction through media, and improved access to affordable, empathetic sexual health services to enhance well-being among Iranian postmenopausal women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13528-5.

Introduction

Sexual health is fundamental to the overall health and well-being of individuals, couples and families, and to the social and economic development of communities and countries [1]. The World Health Organization defines sexual health as a state of physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being in relation to sexuality, requiring respectful and positive approaches to ensure safe and pleasurable experiences [2]. In Iran, sexual health is heavily stigmatized, particularly for women, with cultural norms discouraging open discussion and limiting access to care [3, 4]. Postmenopausal women face unique challenges, including reduced libido, vaginal dryness, and relational discord, exacerbated by societal taboos and inadequate sexual health services [5–7]. Prevalence studies, primarily cross-sectional, report sexual dysfunction rates of 67% to 72% in Iranian postmenopausal women and 68% to 86% globally, though differences in study populations and methodologies limit direct comparisons [5, 7, 8]. Despite available treatments, many women, especially in Iran, avoid seeking help due to shame, embarrassment, and systemic barriers, with Asian studies showing 45% to 88% of affected women not pursuing professional care [8, 9]. Extant research in Iran focuses predominantly on biomedical aspects of menopause, with limited qualitative exploration of sociocultural influences on help-seeking behaviors. This study addresses this gap by examining the lived experiences and perspectives of Iranian postmenopausal women regarding barriers and facilitators to seeking help for sexual problems, offering insights to inform culturally sensitive interventions. This qualitative approach is essential to capture the nuanced interplay of cultural, social, and systemic factors shaping these behaviors [10].

Methods

Study design

This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to ensure methodological transparency and rigor. A qualitative approach using conventional content analysis per Graneheim and Lundman was employed [11, 12]. This methodology was selected as the most appropriate to explore the complex, multifaceted, and culturally-sensitive perceptions of Iranian postmenopausal women regarding barriers to sexual help-seeking. Given the paucity of prior research in this specific context and the need to generate deep, nuanced understanding rather than quantify phenomena, a qualitative design was essential to capture the lived experiences and embedded sociocultural values of participants [13]. Conventional content analysis was particularly suitable as it allows for the inductive derivation of categories and themes directly from the data without predetermined theoretical constraints, which was crucial for exploring an under-researched area and ensuring the findings are firmly grounded in the participants’ own perspectives and the unique Iranian context [11, 14].

Participants

This qualitative study involved 17 married Iranian postmenopausal women aged 45 years and older who were experiencing menopause-related symptoms. Participants were recruited from comprehensive health centers affiliated with Qazvin University of Medical Sciences between December 2024 and April 2025. The inclusion criteria were: Iranian nationality, literacy, absence of diagnosed chronic medical (e.g., cardiovascular disease, diabetes) or psychiatric conditions (e.g., clinical depression, anxiety disorders), and being sexually active within the preceding year. The rationale for excluding women with these comorbidities was to minimize their potential confounding effects on sexual function and help-seeking behaviors, thereby allowing for a more focused investigation of the primary sociocultural and interpersonal barriers within the general population of postmenopausal women. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was employed to ensure diversity across key demographic characteristics (age, education, socioeconomic status), menopause-related factors (duration of menopause), and marital characteristics (duration of marriage, perceived quality of the marital relationship). Sampling continued until data saturation was achieved, which was confirmed when no new codes emerged from subsequent interviews. Participants’ demographic information was systematically recorded using a researcher-developed checklist.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted by a female researcher to foster trust and facilitate candid communication. The data collection period spanned from December 2024 to April 2025. Interviews were held in a private, pre-arranged setting. Prior to each interview, the study’s objectives and procedures were thoroughly explained, and written informed consent was obtained, emphasizing participants’ right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. Interviews commenced with broad, open-ended questions (e.g., “Have you ever faced a sexual concern or problem?” and “What did you do?”). Follow-up probes were used to explore experiences and barriers in depth (e.g., “Why don’t you consult a therapist for sexual problems?”). Each interview lasted between 45 and 90 min (mean: 60 min). Data collection ceased once thematic saturation was confirmed. All interviews were audio-recorded with permission and transcribed verbatim to ensure accuracy for analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis as delineated by Graneheim and Lundman [11]. Transcripts were generated within 24 h of each interview, meticulously reviewed multiple times to identify meaningful content, and organized into semantic units. Line-by-line analysis assigned codes to phrases or sentences bearing semantic significance. The coding process aimed to capture the maximum number of codes to ensure comprehensive data representation. To enhance the consistency of coding and confirmability of the findings, the emerging codes and categories were routinely discussed and refined through peer review and consensus within the research team. Codes were systematically grouped into subcategories and categories based on conceptual similarities, culminating in the derivation of overarching themes reflective of the data’s core meanings. A manual approach was employed for data storage, retrieval, and management to maintain methodological integrity.

Rigor of the data

The rigor of the data collection process was supported through the implementation of a systematic and meticulous approach. The Graneheim and Lundman method for conventional content analysis was employed to ensure a structured framework for data analysis [15]. Each interview transcript was subjected to multiple readings to cultivate a deep understanding of the data, thereby enhancing the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings. The iterative process of coding and categorization facilitated a rigorous examination of the data, enhancing the reliability of the study results. Furthermore, the collaborative nature of refining subcategories and categories until a consensus was reached among researchers further strengthened the rigor of the data analysis process. Overall, the appropriate methodology and thorough approach to data collection and analysis underscored the robustness and rigor of the study’s findings.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University (approval code: IR.MODARES.REC.1402.187). All participants were provided with comprehensive verbal and written information regarding the objectives and methodology of the study, and were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Written informed consent and permission for audio recording were obtained from all participants. Data were anonymized, access was restricted solely to the research team, and results were reported in aggregate to maintain confidentiality. Interview settings were arranged to ensure participants’ comfort and privacy. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Subcategories, main categories, and extracted themes

Theme 1: Individual, family, and social barriers to seeking sexual help

This theme highlights barriers at individual, familial, and societal levels, comprising four categories.

Category 1: Individual strategies of women

Participants often relied on informal sources (e.g., online resources, prayers): “One of my friends turned to prayers and charms, saying she got better after that” (Participant 11, 52 years old). Others hoped for spontaneous resolution: “I haven’t gone anywhere for help; I just deal with it” (Participant 16, 51 years old). Self-management included supplements: “I use supplements and herbal teas” (Participant 2, 49 years old). Some sought extramarital satisfaction: “Due to my husband’s sexual issues, I sometimes have sexual relations with my husband’s friend” (Participant 17, 49 years old).

Category 2: Lack of motivation in women to seek specialized help

Participants prioritized other demands: “There are so many life responsibilities; sexual issues are the lowest priority” (Participant 3, 50 years old). Many normalized issues: “A woman who reaches menopause gets old and can’t have sexual relations” (Participant 16, 51 years old). Lack of treatment desire stemmed from marital issues: “I’m cold toward my husband; his behavior makes me uninterested” (Participant 4, 49 years old).

Category 3: Ineffective communication with spouse

Silence preserved harmony: “My husband wouldn’t like me talking about our sexual issues” (Participant 6, 55 years old). Fear of judgment hindered communication: “I can’t tell my husband I’m in pain; it’ll hurt his pride” (Participant 12, 50 years old).

Category 4: Social stigma and concealment

Stigma led to concealment: “In Iran, if someone raises sexual issues, they become ostracized” (Participant 14, 48 years old). Media perpetuated misconceptions: “They promote salons, but salons aren’t important” (Participant 15, 50 years old).

Theme 2: Communication and structural challenges of healthcare providers in sexual help-seeking

This theme emphasizes communication and structural barriers in healthcare, comprising two categories.

Category 1: Communication and trust issues in providing sexual health services

Embarrassment deterred consultation: “It’s uncomfortable with a male doctor” (Participant 8, 49 years old). Physician neglect was common: “They don’t care; they brush it off” (Participant 10, 49 years old).

Category 2: Organizational barriers in providing sexual health services

Physician inefficacy was noted: “The doctor said I had an infection, but the issue persists” (Participant 12, 50 years old). High costs were prohibitive: “Psychologists’ fees are high; 45 minutes costs 400,000 tomans” (Participant 5, 52 years old) [1].

Theme 3: Infrastructure deficiencies in sexual health services

This theme addresses systemic deficiencies, comprising three categories.

Category 1: Lack of skills and knowledge among sexual health service providers

Vulnerable groups needed attention: “The government should educate lower-income families” (Participant 8, 49 years old). Trust was critical: “A place where you can talk like sisters” (Participant 1, 49 years old).

Category 2: Need for continuous sexual education

Participants sought knowledge: “People lack awareness; they only know what they saw on Instagram” (Participant 9, 51 years old). Continuous education was needed: “Sexual issues aren’t taught anywhere” (Participant 12, 50 years old). Couple-centered education was critical: “The husband needs to come with the wife” (Participant 13, 49 years old).

Category 3: High costs and limited access to affordable services

Government intervention was needed: “In smaller cities, there are no services” (Participant 13, 49 years old). Access was a priority: “There should be centers people can easily go to” (Participant 3, 50 years old). The high cost of professional services was a prominent barrier for many participants. This was exemplified by statements such as: “Many have financial issues. Psychologists’ fees are high; 45 minutes costs 400,000 tomans [approximately 8 USD at the time of data collection] (Participant 5, 52 years old). While not a universal experience, the affordability of care was a frequently cited concern that limited access for a significant portion of the sample.

Discussion

This study identifies three key themes influencing help-seeking behaviors for sexual issues among Iranian menopausal women: individual, familial, and social barriers to seeking sexual help; communicational and structural challenges with healthcare providers in help-seeking; and infrastructural deficiencies in sexual health services. These findings reveal the complex interplay of psychological, cultural, and systemic factors that hinder help-seeking and provide important insights into the socio-cultural dynamics of sexual health in Iran.

Individual, familial, and social barriers reflect deep cultural stigma and personal reluctance as significant obstacles to help-seeking. Participants’ reliance on informal sources, such as prayer or online resources, aligns with Chen et al.’s findings on the impact of stigma on help-seeking in Asian contexts [16]. Similarly, Bahri et al.’s observations regarding cultural acceptance of self-sacrifice among Iranian women support normalization of sexual issues during menopause [3]. A notable finding of this study is the extent to which some participants sought sexual satisfaction outside familial frameworks, challenging cultural norms of marital fidelity in Iran. This behavior may represent a pragmatic response to unmet needs within conservative marital dynamics, indicating tension between cultural expectations and individual agency that warrants further investigation. This contrasts with global studies by Nicolosi et al., which report lower prevalence of such behaviors in comparable populations, likely due to different cultural constraints [9]. Reliance on informal resources also raises concerns regarding the accuracy and safety of these strategies, as participants may be exposed to unverified information, highlighting gaps in accessible and reliable sexual health resources.

Communication barriers centered on couples, such as silence to maintain marital harmony, resonate with Bouchard et al.’s findings on the effectiveness of couple-based interventions for sexual issues [17]. However, resistance from conservative spouses, as reported by participants, complicates the feasibility of such interventions within Iran’s patriarchal context. This paradox indicates that while couple-based education is theoretically beneficial, its practical application may be limited by gender dynamics, underscoring the need for alternative approaches such as women-only support groups to facilitate open dialogue. Communicational and structural challenges emphasize deficiencies in healthcare interactions. Reports of physicians’ neglect of sexual issues align with Velasco et al.’s work on social stigma in mental health help-seeking, where dismissal by providers impedes care-seeking [18]. Support by Brotto and Basson for ongoing sexual education corresponds with participants’ calls for increased awareness; [19] however, limited health literacy in Iran, exacerbated by cultural taboos, poses a significant challenge to implementing such programs. Another key finding is participants’ heightened embarrassment when discussing issues with male physicians, possibly reflecting culturally specific gender sensitivities insufficiently explored in prior Iranian studies. This contrasts with findings by Ramlachan and Naidoo in less conservative settings, where provider gender had less impact [20]. This discrepancy suggests that culturally tailored training for providers, emphasizing gender sensitivity, could mitigate these barriers, although provider resistance due to cultural discomfort remains a concern. Media campaigns, as proposed by Markiewitz and et al., are essential to shifting attitudes [21], yet their effectiveness within Iran’s conservative media landscape, where public discourse on sexual health is heavily censored, remains uncertain.

Infrastructural deficiencies underscore systemic gaps such as inadequate provider training and limited access to services, corroborated by Moradi et al.’s calls for improvement in sexual health services in Iran [6]. Emphasis by Gopichandran and Chetlapalli on building trust in resource-limited settings supports participants’ demand for compassionate and trustworthy providers [22]. However, the high cost of services, particularly for low-income participants, was a more significant deterrent than anticipated, highlighting socio-economic disparities insufficiently addressed in prior studies. This raises a pivotal question: can subsidized services alone address access issues without simultaneous efforts to build trust and cultural competence among providers? Integration of sexual health into primary care, as suggested by Ramlachan and Naidoo,20 appears promising; yet, the lack of specialized training among Iranian healthcare providers may limit its effectiveness and underscores the need for targeted educational reforms. Participant conservatism due to cultural taboos may have constrained the depth of disclosures, especially regarding sensitive topics such as extramarital relationships, indicating that future studies should employ anonymous methods, such as online surveys, to collect more candid responses. The applicability of findings beyond Iran is limited by cultural specificity, although similarities with other conservative societies, such as Poland, indicate partial transferability.

Collectively, these findings necessitate a multifaceted policy and practice strategy to effectively address identified barriers to sexual help-seeking. The following recommendations are formulated to provide practical guidance for key stakeholders:

Implementation of national awareness and destigmatization campaigns: The Ministry of Health and Medical Education, in collaboration with national media, should initiate public health campaigns designed to normalize discussions about sexual health post-menopause. These campaigns should employ culturally resonant messaging to counter pervasive social stigma, redefine sexual health as a vital component of older women’s well-being, and publicly promote available and reliable resources.

Adoption of healthcare system reforms: Mandatory educational modules on menopause and sexual health—emphasizing empathy, cultural competence, and gender-sensitive communication—should be integrated into curricula and continuing education for gynecologists, general practitioners, and midwives. Concurrently, health insurance schemes should be expanded to cover consultations with specialists and sexual health counselors, thereby reducing the critical financial barrier identified by participants.

Strengthening of social and educational infrastructure: Investment is needed to develop community-based, women-led support networks that provide a safe and confidential initial point of contact. Furthermore, educational initiatives should be expanded to deliver accurate information on sexual health across the lifespan, ranging from revised premarital counseling curricula to public workshops and accessible online resources for older adults.

Adopting this coordinated strategy, which simultaneously addresses individual, clinical, and systemic levels, is essential to translate current findings into tangible improvements in the sexual health and overall quality of life of Iranian menopausal women. Future research should examine the long-term impact of these interventions and include diverse populations such as healthcare providers and male partners to develop comprehensive strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include its rigorous qualitative approach, using Graneheim and Lundman’s methodology, member checking, peer review, and external audits to ensure credibility [11]. Maximum variation sampling enhanced transferability within Iran’s sociocultural context. Limitations include potential participant reticence due to cultural taboos, though diverse sampling mitigated this. Applicability outside Iran may be limited due to cultural specificity.

Conclusion

This study illuminates barriers to sexual help-seeking among Iranian postmenopausal women, addressing the research aim by identifying cultural, psychological, and systemic factors. It contributes to the sparse qualitative literature in Iran by highlighting how stigma, ineffective communication, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure impede help-seeking. Key insights emphasize the need for culturally sensitive interventions, including sustained sexual education, media-driven stigma reduction, and enhanced service access, particularly for underserved groups. These strategies aim to improve sexual health outcomes and well-being. Future research should evaluate these interventions longitudinally and include provider and partner perspectives for inclusive solutions.
