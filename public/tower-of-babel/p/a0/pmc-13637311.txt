A multimodal ROS generation and bimodal ferroptosis and cuproptosis self-reinforced strategy for effective bone metastases therapy
Authors: Zhuo Ma, Qiao Ye, Xiaorui Song, Mengting Gao, Jinxuan Wang, Ximeng Zhang, Ziyi Wang, Ziliang Zheng, Ruiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone metastasis is a common complication in patients with advanced breast cancer, which can seriously damage the quality of life of patients and shorten their overall survival. About 70% of late patients will have bone metastasis, which often leads to serious bone-related events. In clinical practice, early and accurate diagnosis and treatment of bone metastasis are still major challenges. In this study, we have developed a new type of integrated nano diagnostic material (CuOx), which combines photoacoustic imaging (PAI) with multi-mode cancer treatment ability to accurately identify and effectively eliminate bone metastasis in the early stage. CuOx effectively induces oxidative stress by consuming antioxidants (GSH and Cys) and generating reactive oxygen substances through multiple pathways, including photodynamic therapy, peroxidase-like activity and Fenton-like reactions. This multifaceted method not only enhances the therapeutic effect by triggering self-reinforcing iron death and copper death mechanisms, but also provides accurate imaging through PAI. Experimental data from in vitro and in vivo studies show that CuOx can significantly inhibit the progress of bone metastasis and clearly show the location of the tumor, providing a new strategy for early diagnosis and treatment. Introduction

Bone metastasis is an extremely diverse and highly malignant lesion. Its overall incidence is gradually increasing, posing a serious threat to human health [1]. The complexity and diversity of this disease make its treatment extremely challenging. The current clinical treatment methods, including surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy [2], have significant limitations. Surgical resection may damage normal tissue and cannot accurately locate and thoroughly remove the tiny metastasis hidden in the dense bone cortex [3]. Although chemotherapy and radiotherapy have a certain effect on inhibiting tumor growth, they cannot completely remove heterogeneous tumor cells. They also often cause adverse side effects outside the bone, such as nausea, vomiting, immunosuppression, cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity [4, 5]. Additionally, the strong antioxidant defenses of tumor cells can weaken treatment efficacy, allowing tumor cells to evade oxidative stress damage induced by therapy, thereby leading to treatment resistance and tumor recurrence [6]. In recent years, with the deepening understanding of tumor biology and cell death mechanisms, new forms of non-apoptotic cell death, such as ferroptosis [7] and cuproptosis [8], have provided novel therapeutic strategies for the treatment of bone metastasis.

Cuproptosis refers to a type of cell death triggered by excessive intracellular copper accumulation, which induces the aggregation of mitochondrial lipoylated proteins and the degradation of Fe-S clusters, ultimately resulting in proteotoxicity [9]. Mitochondrial lipoylated protein aggregation disrupts normal mitochondrial function, causing energy metabolism disorders and increased cellular oxidative stress [10]. The depletion of Fe-S clusters impairs the catalytic activity of various Fe-S cluster-dependent enzymes, which in turn aggravates cellular dysfunction [11]. Given the limitations of single-mechanism therapies that may lead to suboptimal therapeutic outcomes [12], multi-mechanism synergistic treatment modalities are becoming the mainstream of cancer therapy. Synergistic therapy combining ferroptosis and cuproptosis has been verified as an efficient therapeutic approach for bone-metastatic tumors [13]. Ferroptosis is a type of cell death induced by iron metabolism disorders, with lipid peroxidation (LPO) serving as its critical upstream process [14]. LPO is initiated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxygen (O2), which oxidize unsaturated lipid molecules to form lipid hydroperoxides (LOOH) [15]. This process is particularly critical in tumor cells, which typically exhibit high metabolic activity and elevated ROS levels, thereby providing abundant substrates for LPO [16]. Consequently, increasing ROS production while ensuring adequate oxygen supply can expand the LPO and promote ferroptosis. However, efficient induction of LPO still faces two major challenges: the GSH/GPX4 axis is the core of the antioxidant defense in tumor cells [17]. High intracellular concentrations of glutathione (GSH) and cysteine (Cys) continuously clear ROS, converting toxic lipid hydroperoxides (LOOH) into non-toxic lipids, thereby forming an antioxidant barrier [18]. Tumor hypoxia and insufficient endogenous H2O2 supply limit the efficiency of metal ion-mediated Fenton/Fenton-like reactions, greatly restricting ROS production [19]. These dual constraints profoundly undermine the therapeutic efficacy of ferroptosis against tumor lesions.

To overcome these limitations, current studies generally adopt a multi-component coupling strategy: simultaneously loading photosensitizers, Fe2+/Cu2+ ions, GSH depletors, O2 carriers, or H2O2 prodrugs into the same nanoplatform, aiming to synchronously generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and deplete antioxidants through multiple pathways such as chemodynamic therapy (CDT), peroxidase (POD)-like catalysis, photodynamic therapy (PDT), and Fenton/Fenton-like reactions [20, 21]. However, the increase in the number of components not only complicates the synthetic route and amplifies the variation between batches but also leads to unpredictable superimposed pharmacokinetic behaviors: the release rates, spatial distribution, and synergy time windows of each component in vivo are often mismatched, resulting in insufficient LPO intensity or short duration of action [22, 23]. Moreover, the long-term accumulation of multiple metal ions, photosensitizers, and small-molecule depletors may cause potential side effects such as hepatorenal toxicity, immune activation, or metal overload, significantly increasing the difficulty and cost of preclinical safety evaluation [24, 25]. Therefore, developing a single-component platform that can integrate multi-pathway ROS generation, antioxidant depletion, self-reinforced ferroptosis and cuproptosis within the same spatiotemporal scale is considered an urgent need to improve therapeutic controllability and reduce clinical translational barriers.

Herein, in order to realize the concept of synergistic treatment of bone metastases with self-reinforced cuproptosis and ferroptosis caused by multimodal ROS in a single component, an innovative acid and NIR-II-light dual-responsive nanoplatform CuOx has been developed in this study. It aims to synergistically suppress bone metastasis growth by generating ROS through multiple pathways and depleting antioxidants GSH and Cys to trigger self-reinforced ferroptosis and cuproptosis. Specifically, CuOx generate ROS and induce lipid peroxidation-driven ferroptosis in tumor cells through photodynamic therapy, peroxidase activity, and Fenton-like reactions. Meanwhile, CuOx can deplete intracellular antioxidants Cys and GSH, weakening the tumor's antioxidant defenses and enhancing ROS-induced lipid peroxidation for the first self-reinforced. Under the acidic tumor microenvironment, CuOx controllably releases Cu2+, directly inducing cuproptosis via lipoylated protein aggregation. The released Cu2+ further participates in Fenton-like reactions to continuously generate ROS, reinforcing the ferroptosis process and creating a second self-reinforced. Additionally, CuOx enables deep tissue photoacoustic imaging and mild photothermal effects under NIR-II irradiation. This facilitates precise tumor localization and enhances enzymatic reaction kinetics to improve therapeutic effect. The multifunctional system is integrated into a single component, forming a closed-loop of "imaging–locating–triggering–amplifying–killing". This makes CuOx a promising strategy for the precise diagnosis and treatment of bone metastasis, with significant potential for clinical translation.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

The CuOx nanomaterials were synthesized through a one-step process. Tumor microenvironment (TME)-responsive CuOx nanomaterials were prepared via a chemical precipitation method. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations demonstrated that CuOx displayed a regular cubic morphology, with an approximate particle size of 80 nm. (Figure S1). X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed to examine the structural features of CuOx. (Fig. 1b). The two main characteristic diffraction peaks that appear at θ ≈ 35.4° and 38.62° respectively correspond to the (110) and (-111) crystal planes of the monoclinic phase CuO, indicating that the product is pure monoclinic CuO without the presence of Cu₂O or metallic Cu impurity phases [26, 27]. To further analyze the composition of the CuOx, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed (Figs. 1c, d). The Cu XPS spectrum displayed two distinct peaks at 934.9 eV and 955.25 eV, corresponding to the Cu 2p 3/2 and Cu 2p 1/2 orbitals, respectively. Additionally, two satellite peaks were observed at 943.9 eV and 962.65 eV, which could be ascribed to the strong spin–orbit coupling effect and multi-electron transition processes of Cu2+. The O 2p XPS spectrum exhibited two distinct peaks at 531.45 eV and 532.9 eV. The peak at 531 eV was attributed to lattice oxygen, while the peak at 533 eV was likely associated with defect states related to oxygen vacancies, providing compelling evidence for the formation of CuOx. UV–vis spectra of CuOx nanosuspensions (15.6—250 µg mL−1) show a systematic decrease in absorbance with decreasing concentration, indicating that the dispersions obey the Lambert–Beer law over the entire range and are free of aggregation or sedimentation-induced deviations (Fig. 1e). According to the measurement results of dynamic light scattering (DLS), the Zeta potential of CuOx nanoparticles is 27.02 ± 2.32 mV, and the diameter of CuOx is large. Compared with the results of TEM, this difference can be attributed to the presence of a hydration layer (Fig. 1f).

By monitoring the change of absorbance at a specific wavelength at different times in a solution with a pH of 6.5 (Fig. 1g), and the release behavior of Cu2+ in CuOx was studied as shown in Fig. 1h. Initially (0—30 min), the absorbance showed a relatively flat trend, indicating a low release rate of Cu2+. Subsequently, the absorbance gradually increased at 580 nm, indicating that the release of Cu2+ has entered an accelerated stage. This shows that under the acidic conditions of TME, the stability of the material gradually decreases, which promotes the dissolution of Cu2+. The whole release curve shows a continuous upward trend, indicating that the continuous release of Cu2+ is crucial to maintaining the long-term therapeutic effect. As shown in Fig. 1i, there is a good linear correlation between the signal strength of photoacoustic (PA) and the concentration of CuOx nanomaterials. In the concentration range of 0 to 1000 µg/mL, the PA signal has a linear increase relationship with the concentration of CuOx, indicating that the material has a stable photoacoustic response in a wider concentration range. Therefore, CuOx has significant potential to become an effective photoacoustic imaging and therapeutic agent.

Demonstration of antioxidant depletion and H2O2 generation ability of CuOx

Due to its high efficiency in antioxidant consumption, CuOx can specifically consume GSH and Cys in the tumor microenvironment and produce hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at the same time. This effect not only weakens the antioxidant defense mechanism of tumor cells, but also provides abundant substrates for the subsequent generation of ROS, thus laying the foundation for a self-reinforced anti-tumor mechanism based on ferroptosis. In order to evaluate the antioxidant consumption capacity of CuOx, the content changes of GSH and Cys after the addition of CuOx were measured using GSH test kits, Cys test kits and ultraviolet spectrophotometers respectively. As shown in Fig. 2a, b and Figure S2, the characteristic absorption peaks of 412 nm and 600 nm gradually decrease over time and are completely consumed in about 20 min, indicating that CuOx can effectively consume GSH and Cys. In addition, in order to confirm that CuOx produces H2O2 when consuming Cys, the H2O2 content in the reaction was quantitatively analyzed using the H2O2 test kit. The characteristic absorption peak of 415 nm gradually increases over time, reaching its peak in about 30 min. This shows that in the process of sulfide consumption, CuOx produces a large amount of H2O2, thus ensuring that there is sufficient substrate for the generation of multi-mode ROS to destroy tumor cells (Fig. 2c).

Enzymatic activity and photodynamic ability

As shown in Fig. 2d, CuOx shows significant Catalase (CAT)-like enzyme activity. Compared with the CuOx and H2O2 groups, the CuOx + H2O2 group produces significantly more oxygen, and the oxygen generation capacity is further enhanced after the addition of sulfides. This CAT-like enzyme activity reduces hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment, thus creating favorable conditions for subsequent damage to tumor cells. The photodynamic performance of CuOx was assessed using the singlet oxygen (1O2) probe DPBF (Fig. 2e, Figure S3). In comparison with the control group (CuOx + N2 + L), the characteristic peak absorption value at 425 nm for the CuOx + L group exhibited a significant decrease. This suggests that CuOx undergoes type II photodynamic therapy, which depends on oxygen to generate 1O2. The photodynamic performance was likewise enhanced following the addition of H2O2 and Cys. This indicates that the consumption of Cys by CuOx to generate H2O2 substrate indeed augments the ability of CuOx to produce 1O2, which is critical for tumor cell damage. Figure 2f employs the hydroxyl radical (·OH) probe TMB to demonstrate the capacity of CuOx to consume H2O2 and generate ·OH under varying conditions. When compared to the CuOx control group, the CuOx + pH 7.4 group generated a limited amount of ·OH. This ·OH generation was attributed to the peroxidase (POD)-like enzyme activity of CuOx. Under the TME at pH 6.5, CuOx releases substantial Cu2+, which facilitate ·OH generation via Fenton-like reactions. Moreover, upon Cys addition, CuOx consumes Cys to generate extra H2O2 substrate, thereby resulting in enhanced ·OH production (Figure S4). This synergistic ROS generation via photodynamic effects, POD-like enzyme activity, and Fenton-like reactions amplifies the overall antitumor efficacy.

Photothermal effect

The photothermal properties of CuOx were systematically examined under 980 nm laser irradiation (0.25 W/cm2) over a 5-min period, with a notable temperature elevation observed as the material concentration increased from 0 to 2000 μg/mL (0.2 mL).

The photothermal properties of CuOx were systematically examined under 980 nm laser irradiation (0.25 W/cm2) over a 5-min period. A elevation in temperature was observed as the concentration of CuOx increased from 0 to 2000 μg/mL (0.2 mL).. Remarkably, CuOx at 500 μg/mL achieved a maximum temperature of 45.6 ℃ within approximately 5 min. Moreover, the photothermal stability of CuOx was evaluated. Following five on/off cycles of 980 nm laser irradiation, the findings verified that CuOx exhibited excellent photothermal stability (Fig. 2h). In addition, the photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of CuOx was determined to be 41.2%. (Fig. 2i). The photothermal effect of CuOx achieves moderate photothermal effects, which effectively facilitate enzyme-like reactions and further exacerbate tumor cell damage, thereby contributing to the overall antitumor efficacy.

Motivated by the superior biosafety of CuOx, human umbilical vein endothelial (HUVEC) were utilized as a model to assess the biosafety of CuOx via the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 3a). The findings demonstrated that CuOx exerted minimal cytotoxicity on HUVEC at a concentration of 500 μg mL−1, leading to the conclusion that this concentration is therapeutically safe.

Moreover, given the robust ability of CuOx to deplete Cys and GSH, and to generate ROS in multiple modes to induce self-reinforced ferroptosis and cuproptosis, the tumor-killing capacity of CuOx was assessed using human 4T1 breast cancer cells and the CCK-8 assay. Under various conditions, CuOx demonstrated variable cytotoxicity toward 4T1 cells, particularly under additional 980 nm laser irradiation and a pH 6.5 acidic environment. Notably, its toxicity increased sharply in a concentration-dependent manner. Compared to the PBS group, CuOx significantly enhanced toxicity to 4T1 cells under TME pH 6.5 and 980 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 3b). To monitor cellular uptake, CuOx was co-incubated with 4T1 cells, followed by NIR-II PAI imaging (Fig. 3c). The photoacoustic signal gradually increased over time, indicating efficient intracellular delivery of CuOx.

Figure 3d illustrates the clonogenic capacity of 4T1 cells following different treatments. Within 14 days, the PBS control group formed numerous clones with regular morphology and clear boundaries. The clone area of the CuOx treatment group alone was comparable to that of the PBS group, indicating that long-term cell proliferation was not significantly inhibited at therapeutic concentration. In contrast, the cloning area of the CuOx + L group and CuOx + H+ group is significantly reduced, which means that a single stimulus can significantly damage the ability to form cloning. Most importantly, the CuOx + L + H+ group can hardly see the clone area, and its size is significantly smaller than that of any single processing group. This confirms that the acid-light dual synergistic reaction significantly inhibits the proliferation of tumor cells. Taipan blue dyeing also got consistent results (Fig. 3e). After 6 h of culture, the cell vitality of the PBS control group was close to 100%, while there was no significant difference in the CuOx treatment group alone, indicating that its intrinsic toxicity was extremely low. The CuOx + 980 nm laser and CuOx + H⁺ groups showed substantial viability reduction, with the acid–laser combination further decreasing viability below that of either single stimulus. AO/EB double staining was used to evaluate live/dead 4T1 cells. Extensive dead cells were observed when treated with CuOx + L or CuOx + acid, exhibiting obvious apoptosis similar to the CCK-8 results. This indicated that CuOx has a good acid-light dual intelligent response. Notably, acid-triggered apoptosis exceeded that of the laser group, likely due to acid-induced Cu2+ release driving cuproptosis and Fenton-like chemistry enhancing ferroptosis (Fig. 3f). Thus, CuOx demonstrates excellent biosafety toward normal cells and achieves highly efficient tumor-cell killing under acid-light dual intelligent response.

In vitro GSH/Cys depletion and dual self-reinforced ferroptosis–cuproptosis mechanisms of CuOx

To verify that CuOx kills tumor cells through self-reinforced ferroptosis and cuproptosis, Fig. 5 systematically evaluates its ability to deplete intracellular GSH/Cys and to trigger lipid peroxidation (LPO) and mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) collapse. As the CuOx concentration increased from 250 to 1000 μg mL−1, intracellular GSH and Cys levels declined in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 5a, c). GSH dropped from 100% (blank control) to 27% (Fig. 5b), and Cys fell from 100 to 28% (Fig. 5d), demonstrating that CuOx efficiently exhausts GSH and Cys within the TME and disrupts the antioxidant defense of tumor cells. In addition, C11-BODIPY and JC-1 staining were employed to assess CuOx -induced lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial apoptosis. Figure 5e shows that CuOx alone can elevate LPO levels (red/green fluorescence ratio decreases from 5.2 to 4.5), indicating that the POD-like activity of CuOx generates a basal amount of ROS. Simultaneous depletion of GSH and Cys weakens the TME antioxidant environment, thereby enhancing ROS-induced lipid peroxidation and achieving the first self-reinforcement mechanism. The CuOx + H⁺ group further significantly increases LPO (ratio drops from 4.5 to 0.7), as acid stimulation triggers sustained release of Cu2+ driving cuproptosis and generating large amounts of •OH through Fenton-like reactions, thereby achieving the second self-reinforcement mechanism. Acid-light synergy (H⁺ + L) further lowers the ratio to 0.25 (Fig. 5f), indicating massive ROS generation and severe membrane lipid peroxidation. JC-1 staining confirmed these results by demonstrating changes in mitochondrial potential. The JC-1 red/green fluorescence ratio revealed that CuOx alone induced only mild depolarization (ratio decreased from 1.87 to 1.54), laser or acid alone reduced the ratio to 0.94 and 0.55, respectively, whereas the CuOx + H⁺ + L group dropped to 0.26 (Fig. 5g), indicating severe ΔΨm collapse and full activation of the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway caused by ferroptosis oxidative stress. Immunofluorescence staining analysis of the GPX4 indicator (Fig. 5h) showed that in PBS controls, GPX4 exhibited high expression. The CuOx group significantly down-regulated GPX4 (P < 0.001), indicating that CuOx triggers ferroptosis by depleting intracellular GSH and Cys. Introduction of laser or acid further reduced GPX4 levels to 0.39 ± 0.03 a.u and 0.35 ± 0.03 a.u, respectively, under combined acid and light stimulation, GPX4 was almost completely exhausted (0.17 ± 0.03 a.u) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5i), confirming that CuOx can effectively induce ferroptosis. Similar results were observed in the Western blot analysis of the DLAT index (P < 0.001) (Figs. 5j, k, S5).

DLAT plays a core role in cuproptosis by mediating copper-dependent protein oligomerization and mitochondrial metabolic collapse. To obtain molecular evidence that CuOx triggers the cuproptosis pathway in bone-metastatic 4T1 cells, immunofluorescence staining for DLAT was performed (Fig. 6a). The results showed that CuOx activated the cuproptosis pathway. DLAT, a key regulator of cuproptosis, exhibited minimal fluorescence in the PBS control group (1 a.u.). The CuOx group showed a slight increase in DLAT to 1.50 ± 0.45 a.u, the laser or acid alone groups increased DLAT to 1.84 ± 0.95 a.u and 50.09 ± 1.38 a.u, respectively, while the H⁺ + L group displayed the highest DLAT signal (P < 0.001), demonstrating effective induction of cuproptosis (Fig. 6b). In vivo targeted NIR-II PA imaging and photothermal imaging

To systematically confirm the tumor-targeting ability and biological distribution characteristics of CuOx, an orthotopic 4T1 bone-metastasis model was established in BALB/c mice. Baseline NIR-II photoacoustic (PA) imaging performed prior to the intravenous injection of CuOx revealed negligible background signal within the tibial lesion (0 h, Fig. 7a). Following the tail-vein injection of CuOx (200 µL, 5 mg mL−1), PA signals were collected at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h under identical acquisition parameters (980 nm excitation). The photoacoustic imaging results demonstrated rapid selective accumulation in the tumor: the PA signal intensity within the metastatic focus began to rise at 1 h post-injection, peaked at 2 h, and then gradually decayed over the subsequent 22 h (Fig. 7b). Based on these pharmacokinetic findings, the time point of 2 h post-injection was identified as the optimal window for photothermal therapy and imaging procedures.

a) Time- dependent NIR-II PAI imaging of bone metastasis model mice following intravenous injection of CuOx. b) The corresponding changes of photoacoustic signal quantified by NIR-II PAI imaging. c) Thermal images of bone metastases tumor-bearing mice treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and CuOx. d) Corresponding local temperature curves recorded upon exposure to a 980 nm laser (0.25 W cm−2, 5 min)

The mild photothermal capability of CuOx in vivo was subsequently evaluated. Two hours after intravenous injection, tumors were irradiated with a 980 nm laser (0.25 W cm−2, 5 mm spot diameter), with surface temperatures monitored using high-resolution infrared thermography at 1 min intervals (Fig. 7c). CuOx -treated tumors exhibited a rapid and uniform temperature increase: ΔT reached 4.37 ± 0.25 °C within 5 min, achieving mild hyperthermia that accelerates catalytic Fenton-like reactions without inducing nonspecific thermal necrosis. In contrast, PBS-treated tumors showed only a moderate ΔT of 1.8 ± 0.26 °C under identical irradiation conditions (Fig. 7d). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CuOx is a precisely targeted, rapidly cleared, and mildly photothermally activated nanoplatform, making it highly suitable for ferroptosis-cuproptosis therapy in bone-metastatic tumors.

In vivo anticancer effects of CuOx in bone metastasis model

After rigorously validating the in vitro efficacy of CuOx, an orthotopic 4T1 bone metastasis model was employed to evaluate its in vivo therapeutic potential in BALB/c mice (7–8 weeks, 18 ± 2 g), which were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 3 per group): (i) PBS, (ii) CuOx, and (iii) CuOx + L. On day 0, 4T1 cells (4 × 105 cells in 20 μL PBS) were injected into the proximal tibia to induce bone metastasis.

When the tumor volume reaches about 80 cubic millimeters (which indicates that the implantation operation has been successfully completed), intravenous injection begins. CuOx (200 µl, 5 mg/ml) or PBS five times (0th, 3rd, 6th, 9th and 12th days) are administered via tail intravenous injection every 72 h. Two hours after injection, the CuOx + L group was irradiated with a laser irradiation of 980 nm NIR-II wavelength (0.25 W cm−2, 5 min, with a light spot diameter of 5 mm) to match the peak accumulation time of nanoparticles in the tumor previously determined by pharmacokinetic imaging (Fig. 8a). Use a digital caliper every two days to measure the tumor size (length × width × height), and use the formula V = 0.5 × L × W2 to calculate the tumor volume. Weigh the mice every day and monitor their overall health through eating behavior. After 15 days, uncontrolled tumor growth appeared in mice treated with PBS, and the use of CuOx alone could moderately inhibit tumor progression. In contrast, the CuOx + L group almost completely inhibited the development of the tumor (Fig. 8b). The quantitative analysis of tumor volume further confirms this trend (Fig. 8c), which is attributed to the consumption of glutathione/cysteine and the generation of multipath reactive oxygen, thus causing self-reinforced ferroptosis and cuproptosis. It is worth noting that the survival rate of the CuOx + L group at 50 days is 100% (Fig. 8e). In addition, there was no obvious weight loss in all groups (Fig. 8d), and the hemolysis test showed no obvious hemolysis phenomenon (Figure S6), which confirmed that CuOx had good biocompatibility. To further systematically evaluate the biosafety profile of CuOx treatments in vivo, histological and blood-related analyses were performed. No obvious histological damage or abnormal lesions were observed in major organs including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney by H&E staining (Figure S7). Meanwhile, routine blood tests and serum biochemistry assays showed no significant differences in key hematological parameters (RBC, WBC, PLT) and hepatic/renal function indicators (ALT, AST, SCr, BUN) among all groups (Figure S8). Together, these results demonstrated that the CuOx exhibited favorable biosafety and good biocompatibility in vivo, supporting its further potential in biomedical applications.

In order to quantitatively evaluate the therapeutic effect of CuOx on tumor-related osteolysis, high-resolution micro-CT and tissue staining analysis were carried out at the end of the study (day 15). Figure 9a shows the representative three-dimensional reconstruction image along the sagittal plane and the cross-section. The tibia treated with physiological saline (PBS) has extensive bone cortical perforation and deep dissolution damage, and its extension depth is more than 1 mm to the dry end, which is consistent with severe metastatic bone destruction. In contrast, the tibia treated only with copper oxide monotherapy retains part of the thickness of the bone cortex and maintains the continuity of the trabeculae, despite the presence of local pores. It is worth noting that the bone cortex of the CuOx + L group presents an almost complete shell and a dense trabeculae network that is no different from normal bones. Quantitative microcomputer tomography indicators confirm these qualitative observations. Bone volume (BV) increased from 0.27 ± 0.05 mm3 (PBS group) to 0.64 ± 0.04 mm3 (CuOx group), and further increased to 1.14 ± 0.05 mm3 (CuOx + L group) (P < 0.001, Fig. 9d). The bone surface (BS) also showed a similar trend (PBS group: 10.58 ± 0.96 mm2, CuOx + L group: 18.53 ± 0.87 mm2) (Fig. 9e). At the same time, the bone mineral density (BMD) increased from 0.75 ± 0.03 g/cm3 (PBS) to 1.04 ± 0.03 g/cm3 (CuOx + L) (Fig. 9f), which confirms that the bone microstructure has been well preserved. H&E and Masson staining verified these findings (Figs. 9b, c). H&E and Masson three-color staining on the tibia showed that there was severe loss of bone cortex, a significant decrease in the content of the trabeculae, and the complete destruction of normal bone tissue structure in the PBS treatment group. In contrast, the bones of the CuOx + L treatment group retain normal structure and collagen deposition, highlighting the anti-tumor protection potential of this nano-diagnosis platform.

For further clarify the potential mechanism of anti-tumor treatment effect, immunofluorescence staining of DLAT and GPX4 was performed on different groups of tissues. As shown in Fig. 9g, the expression levels of DLAT and GPX4 proteins are regulated by different treatment regimens. Compared with the PBS control group, the expression of DLAT protein in the CuOx + L treatment group increased significantly (through enhanced red fluorescence), while the expression of GPX4 protein decreased significantly (through weakened red fluorescence). These research results show that CuOx + L treatment can effectively trigger cuproptosis and ferroptosis. The synergy of these two processes promotes the death of tumor cells, thus achieving good therapeutic effects.

Conclusion

In this study, a new type of nano-diagnostic material CuOx has been successfully developed, which combines PAI with multimodal cancer treatment to effectively treat bone metastasis. CuOx can consume GSH and Cys and produce ROS through PDT, peroxidase-like enzyme activity and Fenton-like reactions, thus causing self-reinforced cuproptosis and ferroptosis. In vitro and in vivo experiments show that CuOx can achieve a significant tumor inhibition effect and can clearly locate tumors. The mild photothermal effect of this material under 980 nm laser irradiation further enhances the anti-tumor effect. In the in situ 4T1 bone metastasis model, CuOx was combined with laser irradiation to achieve almost complete tumor suppression and 100% survival rate in 50 days. Micro-CT and histological analyses confirmed robust bone preservation. CuOx provides a promising strategy for overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies and holds potential for clinical translation.
