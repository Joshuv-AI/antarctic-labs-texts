Lack of Zoonotic Coronavirus Species Detected Among Children Hospitalized With Pneumonia in the Philippines
Authors: Yusuke Sayama, Michiko Okamoto, Mayuko Saito, Raita Tamaki, Mariko Saito-Obata, Reynaldo Frederick Negosa Quicho, Christina Dahlia Joboco, Socorro Lupisan, Hitoshi Oshitani
Published: 2023

To the Editor—We read with interest the recently published study by Vlasova et al demonstrating a novel canine coronavirus (CoV) isolated from children hospitalized with pneumonia during 2017–2018 in East Malaysia [1]. The viruses of the family Coronaviridae infect avian species and various mammals [2]. Among the CoVs that infect humans (HCoVs), HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63, HCoV-HKU1, and HCoV-OC43 usually cause mild symptoms [3]. However, in the past 2 decades, we have experienced the emergence of 3 highly pathogenic CoV species, namely, severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS)-CoV, Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS)-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2; all of these species are considered to have a zoonotic origin [4]. Therefore, it is crucial to know whether zoonotic CoV species are circulating in humans, especially in areas with similar environments to East Malaysia. Thus, we examined specimens collected to detect the CoV species, including zoonotic CoV from children admitted to 2 hospitals covering rural areas in the Philippines.

We tested 454 nasopharyngeal swab samples from children in Biliran (n = 311, January–December 2018) and Palawan (n = 143, June 2015–June 2016). Two collection sites were main community hospitals providing both secondary and tertiary care, located in rural tropical islands. The participants were children aged 8 days to 59 months hospitalized with severe pneumonia based on the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness guidelines [5]. Viral RNA was extracted from the sample and amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase gene of CoVs in the same method used in a previous Malaysian study [6]. The PCR products were sequenced using the Sanger method. Our study was approved by the ethical committees of the Philippines (2008-05-10 and 2013-002) and Japan (2012-1-63, 2016-1-258, and 2018-1-70).

Of the 454 samples, 8 (1.8%) tested positive for CoV species (5/311 [1.6%] in Biliran and 3/143 [2.1%] in Palawan) (Table 1). Among the Biliran samples, HCoV-NL63 (n = 1), HCoV-HKU1 (n = 2), and HCoV-OC43 (n = 2) were detected. Among the Palawan samples, HCoV-NL63 (n = 1) and HCoV-OC43 (n = 2) were detected. Seven of the 8 HCoV-positive patients were aged <8 months. HCoV-229E and other CoV species were not detected in both areas.

No CoVs other than HCoVs were detected among the approximately 450 children with severe pneumonia during 2015–2018 in remote islands located in the eastern and western Philippines. The previous study in Malaysia detected canine CoV in 8 of 301 (2.5%) children hospitalized with pneumonia during 2017–2018 [1]. Our study did not find any canine and other zoonotic CoVs using the same method as the Malaysian study [1]. Our results suggest that zoonotic CoVs are at least not a common cause of pediatric pneumonia in the study areas. However, another research group reported canine CoV in the urine samples of adults who had traveled to Haiti [7]. Furthermore, they detected a porcine delta-CoV among children with acute undifferentiated febrile illness in Haiti [8]. There might be unrecognized transmission of zoonotic CoV in human population. Therefore, it is important to be aware of risk of spillover transmission of CoV from animals to humans.
