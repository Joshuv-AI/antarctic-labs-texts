Prevalence, trend and resistance patterns of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales in Bangladesh: a nationwide laboratory-based study
Authors: Aninda Rahman, Mohammad Julhas Sujan, S M Shahriar Rizvi, Zakir Hossain Habib, Abul Hasnat, Hridika Talukder Barua, Alina Shaw, William MacWright, Ahmed Taha Aboushady, John Stelling, Marianne Holm, Florian Marks, Nimesh Poudyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) pose a significant public health threat, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Bangladesh, where efforts to contain antimicrobial resistance remain inadequate. CRE infections are major contributors to healthcare-associated infections (HAI) and are associated with high mortality, limited treatment options, and increased healthcare costs. This study evaluates the prevalence, resistance patterns, and temporal trends of CRE in Bangladesh using data from standard public and private microbiology laboratories.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, cross-sectional analysis of antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) data from 2017 to 2020. Data were initially collected from 34 microbiology laboratories across Bangladesh, with 26 ultimately meeting quality criteria for inclusion. CRE was defined based on Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Data were standardized using WHONET and analyzed using STATA 17. A total of 123,933 Enterobacterales isolates with defined CRE status were included in the final analysis after excluding isolates lacking complete carbapenem susceptibility data.ResultsOf the 123,933 Enterobacterales isolates analyzed, 12.3% (n = 15,252) were identified as CRE. Males (16.5%; 8,274/50,142) were more frequently affected than females (9.5%; 6,978/73,791), with the highest prevalence among patients aged < 1 year (18.1%; 528/2,912) and ≥ 60 years (14.4%; 5,442/37,856). The annual prevalence of CRE remained stable over the study period: 12.3% (2,713/21,982) in 2017, 12.4% (4,709/37,896) in 2018, 12.3% (5,540/44,898) in 2019, and 12.0% (2,290/19,157) in 2020 (p = 0.427). CRE was most frequently detected in tracheal aspirates (57.7%; 1,481/2,567) and wound swabs (20.9%; 3,950/18,878) compared to urine (7.4%; 5,468/73,939). Indoor samples showed a significantly higher prevalence (18.4%; 6,402/34,727) compared to outdoor samples (8.8%; 6,876/78,005), suggesting a high burden likely associated with healthcare settings. Klebsiella spp. (24.9%; 7,995/32,067) had a higher proportion of CRE than Escherichia coli (7.9%; 5,846/74,060). Among CRE isolates, resistance to individual carbapenems was high: imipenem (75.3%–91.1%) and meropenem (93.9%–100.0%). Resistance was near-universal for third and fourth-generation cephalosporins, including cefotaxime (92.1%–99.3%) and ceftazidime (87.1%–97.6%), as well as the monobactam aztreonam (75.2%–97.5%). Resistance to aminoglycosides remained comparatively lower but still substantial, with amikacin and gentamicin resistance ranging from 61.5% to 86.4% and 68.1%–86.8%, respectively.ConclusionsCRE prevalence in Bangladesh remains high, with extensive resistance to carbapenems and β-lactams. However, the comparatively lower resistance to aminoglycosides suggests a narrow but valuable therapeutic window. Strengthened infection prevention, antimicrobial stewardship, and surveillance are urgently needed to contain further spread.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), Antimicrobial resistance, Healthcare-associated infections, AMR surveillance, Antimicrobial susceptibility testing, Infection prevention and control, Bangladesh

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most formidable global health challenges of the 21st century, threatening the efficacy of modern medicine and leading to millions of deaths annually [1, 2]. Among the most concerning pathogens are Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), which have emerged as a critical global public health threat due to their association with high mortality, limited treatment options, and rapid transmission in healthcare settings [3, 4]. These pathogens are increasingly implicated in healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where rapid urbanization, high population density, widespread antibiotic misuse, and often inadequate diagnostic capacities and fragmented surveillance systems create an ideal environment for the emergence and spread of highly resistant pathogens like CRE [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has classified CRE as priority pathogens for urgent research and development of new antimicrobials [6, 7].

In Bangladesh, the burden of antimicrobial resistance is rising, with prior studies reporting high resistance to broad-spectrum β-lactams and carbapenems among Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli and Klebsiella spp [8, 9]. A Lancet report from 2019 associated approximately 98,800 deaths with AMR in Bangladesh [10]. Studies have also identified a lack in implementing containment activities for AMR [11] and a significant gap in controlling hospital-acquired infections (HAI) through Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) measures [12–14]. Very few studies in Bangladesh have specifically assessed CRE Enterobacterales [15, 16]. A report from the national AMR surveillance (2016–2023) indicated an 11% resistance rate of Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales against carbapenems in Bangladesh [17]. However, a significant knowledge gap persists, as most available data on CRE in Bangladesh are derived from individual facilities or national sentinel surveillance, which often exclude crucial data from the private sector and lack comprehensive, longitudinal trends necessary for robust national policy formulation [18].

To address this critical knowledge gap and provide a more representative national overview, we conducted a nationwide retrospective analysis utilizing comprehensive antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) data collected from a diverse network of 34 public and private microbiology laboratories across Bangladesh. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalence and temporal trends of CRE in Bangladesh from 2017 to 2020, and (2) characterize the detailed resistance profiles of the most frequently isolated CRE organisms. These findings are intended to support national AMR containment strategies, strengthen clinical guidelines, and inform antimicrobial stewardship interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective, cross-sectional study using laboratory data collected from January 2017 to December 2020. The study was conducted as part of the CAPTURA (Capturing Data on Antimicrobial Resistance Patterns and Trends in Use in Regions of Asia) initiative [19], in collaboration with the Communicable Disease Control (CDC) division of the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR), and the International Vaccine Institute (IVI). Data were obtained from 34 public and private microbiology laboratories in eight administrative divisions of Bangladesh.

Laboratory selection and quality assessment

Facilities were selected based on their ability to perform routine AST and their willingness to share retrospective data. Laboratory quality was assessed using a laboratory assessment tool called the ‘Rapid Laboratory Quality Assessment (RLQA)’ tool developed by the CAPTURA consortium. This tool evaluates key components, including equipment, staff capacity, media quality, AST protocols, and internal and external quality control. Laboratories scoring below 60 in the assessment or lacking adequate data systems were excluded following experts’ discussion as described before in other publications [18, 19]. Ultimately, data from 26 laboratories met inclusion criteria and were used for analysis (Fig. 1), comprising 7 public facilities and 19 private microbiology laboratories.

Flowchart of case selection

Data collection and processing

Raw AST data were collected in various formats (manual registers, Excel sheets, or Laboratory Information Systems) and standardized using WHONET version 25.4.5 [20]. BacLink was used to convert datasets into WHONET format where needed. Laboratories also received training in data curation and quality control to ensure consistent data entry. Data cleaning included de-duplication of isolates, removal of contaminants and mixed flora, and exclusion of entries missing key demographic or microbiological variables (e.g., age, sex, specimen type, date, organism).

Only the first isolate per patient per infection episode was included, following CLSI M39 guidelines [21]. AST results were interpreted using CLSI breakpoints. CRE was defined as any Enterobacterales isolate showing resistance (R) to at least one carbapenem (imipenem, meropenem, or ertapenem) [22]. For Proteus spp., a species-aware definition was applied where CRE status was determined by resistance to meropenem and/or ertapenem, disregarding imipenem results to avoid misclassification due to intrinsic non-susceptibility. Additionally, while Salmonella spp. were included in overall prevalence estimates, they were excluded from detailed antimicrobial resistance profiling (Table 2) due to their distinct clinical and susceptibility characteristics compared to other Enterobacterales.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted using STATA version 17.0 [23]. Frequencies and proportions were calculated for categorical variables. CRE prevalence was estimated annually and stratified by organism, specimen type, sex, age group, and patient setting (inpatient vs. outpatient). Resistance rates were calculated for key antimicrobials among the five most common CRE organisms. Temporal trends were visualized to assess changes in CRE prevalence over the study period.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 146,960 Enterobacterales isolates were initially identified from 26 microbiology laboratories after initial data processing (Fig. 1). Of these, 23,027 isolates were subsequently excluded from CRE analysis due to the lack of carbapenem susceptibility data required to define their CRE status. Thus, a final total of 123,933 Enterobacterales isolates with defined CRE status were included in the analysis. Among these, 15,252 (12.3%) were identified as carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE).

Urine was the most common specimen type (68.4%; 73,939/108,076), followed by pus/wound swabs (17.5%; 18,878/108,076) and blood (10.3%; 11,170/108,076) (Table 1). The majority of samples were collected from outdoor settings (62.9%; 78,005/123,933), while a substantial proportion originated from indoor settings (28.0%; 34,727/123,933). Escherichia coli (59.8%; 74,060/123,933) and Klebsiella spp. (25.9%; 32,067/123,933) constituted the vast majority of Enterobacterales isolates.

The majority of the samples were from female patients (59.5%; 73,791/123,933) compared to males (40.5%; 50,142/123,933). When examining the prevalence of CRE within each demographic group, males (16.5%; 8,274/50,142) were significantly more frequently affected than females (9.5%; 6,978/73,791) (p < 0.001). The highest prevalence was observed among patients aged < 1 year (18.1%; 528/2,912) and those 60 + years (14.4%; 5,442/37,856) (p < 0.001).

The prevalence of CRE varied significantly by specimen type (p < 0.001), with tracheal aspirates showing the highest prevalence (57.7%; 1,481/2,567), followed by pus/wound swabs (20.9%; 3,950/18,878), blood (9.0%; 1,001/11,170), and urine (7.4%; 5,468/73,939) (Table 1). The prevalence of CRE was notably higher among samples collected from hospitalized patients (18.4%; 6,402/34,727) compared to outdoor samples (8.8%; 6,876/78,005) (p < 0.001), underscoring the strong association between CRE and healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).

Distribution of CRE by organism

Klebsiella spp. was the most commonly isolated CRE organism, and nearly one in four Klebsiella isolates (24.9%; 7,995/32,067) were carbapenem-resistant. In contrast, 7.9% (5,846/74,060) of E. coli isolates met CRE criteria. Following the application of a species-aware definition to account for intrinsic imipenem non-susceptibility, carbapenem resistance was identified in 10% (584/5,863) of Proteus spp. Citrobacter spp. also showed a significant proportion of CRE at 17.3% (296/1,716) and 11.7% (276/2,353) of Enterobacter spp were CRE.

Temporal trend of CRE prevalence

The prevalence of CRE among Enterobacterales remained stable over the four-year study period (Fig. 2). In 2017, the proportion of CRE was 12.3% (2,713/21,982), followed by 12.4% (4,709/37,896) in 2018, 12.3% (5,540/44,898) in 2019, and 12.0% (2,290/19,157) in 2020. A Cochran-Armitage test confirmed no significant temporal trend in overall CRE prevalence (p = 0.427). Similarly, logistic regression adjusted for covariates showed no significant annual change in the odds of resistance (OR: 0.987, 95% CI: 0.970–1.005; p = 0.153). Pathogen-specific analysis reveals a high, stable endemicity for Klebsiella spp., which consistently exhibited the highest CRE prevalence, increasing from 24.8% (1,414/5,693) in 2017 to 26.5% (1,270/4,800) by 2020. Conversely, E. coli maintained a relatively lower and stable prevalence throughout the study period, fluctuating minimally between 8.3% (2,210/26,485) and 7.2% (871/12,124). Other organisms showed more variable annual patterns, such as Citrobacter spp., which peaked at 20.5% (103/503) in 2019 before declining to 10.6% (29/273) in 2020.

Resistance patterns of Enterobacterales

The antimicrobial resistance patterns of major Enterobacterales isolates, excluding Salmonella, are detailed in Table 2. Overall, resistance to third-generation cephalosporins was notably high across most species, while carbapenem resistance, though varying by species, remained a significant concern throughout the study period. Aminoglycoside resistance was generally lower across the family, though high rates of ciprofloxacin and aztreonam resistance were frequently observed. Analysis of species-specific resistance patterns revealed distinct profiles, particularly for Klebsiella spp., which consistently demonstrated the highest resistance to carbapenems, specifically imipenem (20.2%; 4,621/22,892) and meropenem (23.6%; 5,586/23,672). This genus also exhibited the highest resistance to cefotaxime (72.9%; 5,739/7,872) among the third-generation cephalosporins.

In contrast, Escherichia coli showed lower resistance to carbapenems, with imipenem at 5.4% (2,873/53,386) and meropenem at 7.6% (4,444/58,489), but displayed high resistance to ciprofloxacin (65.6%; 43,910/66,931) and ceftriaxone (61.8%; 43,804/70,913). Proteus spp. exhibited substantial resistance to aminoglycosides, with amikacin resistance at 38.1% (2,017/5,288) and gentamicin at 46.5% (2,586/5,564), alongside high ciprofloxacin resistance (59.8%; 3,264/5,456). Citrobacter spp. displayed relatively high resistance to meropenem (18.7%; 199/1,063) and aztreonam (60.1%; 498/828), while Enterobacter spp. showed notable resistance to the beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combination amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (77.1%; 1,690/2,192).

Resistance patterns of CRE organisms

Among the identified CRE isolates, resistance to third-generation cephalosporins was nearly universal (Table 3). Cefotaxime resistance ranged from 93.5% (43/46) in Citrobacter to 99.3% (3,041/3,062) in Klebsiella spp. Similarly, ceftazidime resistance was identified in 88.1% (171/194) of Citrobacter and 97.6% (4,985/5,108) of Klebsiella spp. isolates. Ceftriaxone resistance was observed in 90.4% (5,046/5,581) of E. coli and 97.4% (7,422/7,617) of Klebsiella spp.

Resistance to individual carbapenems varied by drug and species (Table 3). Meropenem resistance was 98.3% (5,586/5,685) in Klebsiella spp., 95.7% (4444/4645) in E. coli, and 97.6% (165/169) in Enterobacter spp. Imipenem resistance was recorded at 91.1% (4,621/5,070) in Klebsiella spp. and 67.4% (989/1,468) in E. coli. For beta-lactam beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations, piperacillin-tazobactam resistance was 96.7% (6,788/7,022) in Klebsiella spp., 82.2% (3,019/3,671) in E. coli, and 39.4% (154/391) in Proteus spp. Regarding other classes, amikacin resistance ranged from 61.5% (3,184/5,176) in E. coli to 86.4% (6,514/7,541) in Klebsiella spp. Gentamicin resistance was 68.1% (3,625/5,325) in E. coli and 86.8% (6,779/7,808) in Klebsiella spp. Nitrofurantoin resistance was identified in 45.2% (1,856/4,104) of E. coli and 80.7% (1,594/1,975) of Klebsiella spp. isolates.

Discussion

This nationwide analysis of 123,933 Enterobacterales isolates from Bangladesh between 2017 and 2020 reveals a substantial and consistently high burden of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), with an overall prevalence of 12.3%. While the annual prevalence fluctuated slightly between 12.3% (2,713/21,982) in 2017 and 12.0% (2,290/19,157) in 2020, statistical analysis confirmed no significant temporal trend (p = 0.427), indicating a persistent and entrenched public health challenge rather than a decline.

The disproportionate prevalence among older adults (≥ 60 years, 14.4%), infants (< 1 year, 18.1%) and male patients (16.5%), alongside the strong association with hospitalized individuals (18.4% in indoor settings vs. 8.8% in outdoor), further underscores the well-established link between CRE and healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) [5]. This prevalence is comparable to that reported in several other South Asian countries and LMICs. For instance, studies from India, Pakistan, and Nepal have reported CRE prevalence rates ranging from 15% to over 40% in various clinical settings, reflecting a significant regional and global burden [24–28]. Klebsiella spp. accounted for over half of the CRE isolates (24.9% of all Klebsiella isolates were CRE), consistent with global trends identifying it as a major reservoir for carbapenemase production and nosocomial transmission [29, 30].

Our findings align with and expand upon prior reports of increasing antimicrobial resistance among Gram-negative organisms in Bangladesh, including data from the national AMR surveillance program [17] and previous publications [8, 9]. Specifically, our observed overall CRE prevalence of 12.3% is slightly higher than the 11% resistance rate of Enterobacterales against carbapenems reported by the national AMR surveillance (2016–2023) in Bangladesh [17], suggesting a potentially increasing trend or broader capture of resistant isolates in our study. This study significantly strengthens the evidence base by incorporating data from both public and private laboratories across multiple years, providing a more comprehensive and nationally representative picture of CRE epidemiology than previously available. The consistent prevalence of CRE over the study period, ranging from 12.0% to 12.4%, suggests endemic circulation rather than sporadic outbreaks, although annual fluctuations may reflect changes in diagnostic capacity, case mix, or infection prevention practices.

The alarmingly high resistance observed among CRE isolates to third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cefotaxime 93.5%–99.3%, ceftazidime 87.1%–97.6%, ceftriaxone 87.8%–97.4%) and carbapenems (imipenem/meropenem 75.3%–100.0%) is a critical finding, indicating severely limited treatment options for these infections. These resistance patterns are consistent with regional data from South Asia, where high CRE prevalence is often linked to widespread antibiotic misuse, suboptimal infection prevention and control (IPC) practices, and limited microbiological capacity [25, 26, 28]. The extensive resistance to piperacillin-tazobactam (39.4%–96.7%) further narrows the therapeutic arsenal. This broad-spectrum resistance profile mirrors the global challenge posed by CRE, particularly in regions with high antibiotic consumption and inadequate surveillance [5]. While molecular confirmation was beyond the scope of this study, these phenotypic profiles strongly suggest the widespread dissemination of potent carbapenemase enzymes, such as metallo-β-lactamases (e.g., NDM-type enzymes), which are known to be prevalent in the region [31]. Importantly, our data show that resistance to amikacin (61.5%–86.4%) and gentamicin (68.1%–86.8%) remained comparatively lower than to β-lactams, suggesting that aminoglycosides may offer some, albeit limited, therapeutic options for CRE infections. Similarly, nitrofurantoin retained considerable activity against urinary isolates (45.2%–80.7%), consistent with its localized effect and lower selection pressure, making it a potential option for uncomplicated urinary tract infections caused by susceptible CRE.

The disproportionate prevalence of CRE in tracheal aspirates (57.7%) and wound swabs (20.9%) compared to urine (7.4%) points to the severity of infections, HAI, and the need for enhanced IPC in critical care and surgical settings. It may also suggest a sampling bias toward critically ill patients in Intensive Care Units rather than the general population. The strong association with inpatient status (18.4% prevalence in indoor samples vs. 8.8% in outdoor samples) further supports the classification of CRE as a key driver of HAIs. This strong association with inpatient status and specific specimen types (tracheal aspirates, wound swabs) is consistent with global epidemiological patterns of CRE, where these pathogens are frequently identified as causative agents of healthcare-associated outbreaks and severe infections in critical care settings worldwide [5]. These findings reinforce the urgent need for robust hospital-level antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs), regular surveillance, and strict infection control measures, particularly in high-risk departments. This is particularly crucial, as studies have shown a gap between knowledge and practice regarding AMR among clinicians working in hospitals in Bangladesh [32].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this study relied on retrospective AST data, which, despite rigorous quality assessment, may be subject to inherent biases due to variations in laboratory practices and reporting standards across different facilities. Although all included laboratories met minimum quality criteria, not all used identical AST methods (e.g., disc diffusion vs. automated systems) or participated in external quality assessments consistently, potentially introducing some heterogeneity. Second, the absence of molecular data on carbapenemase genes (e.g., blaNDM, blaKPC, blaOXA-48) limits our understanding of the specific resistance mechanisms driving the observed phenotypic resistance patterns. Third, the lack of clinical outcome data prevented us from correlating CRE infections with patient morbidity, mortality, or treatment failures, which would provide crucial insights into the clinical impact of these resistant pathogens. Furthermore, isolates excluded due to missing carbapenem data were more likely to originate from blood cultures (26.4% vs. 10.3%) and outdoor settings (76.1% vs. 62.9%). As these groups typically exhibited lower resistance rates in our primary analysis (compared to tracheal aspirates or indoor settings) their exclusion may lead to a slight overestimation of the national burden. Consequently, our reported overall prevalence of 12.3% should be interpreted as a conservative upper estimate of the CRE burden among isolates undergoing antimicrobial susceptibility testing in Bangladesh. Finally, although this study represents a nationwide analysis, a key limitation is the lack of granular geographical data to assess intra-divisional variation or identify specific regional hotspots across Bangladesh.

Despite these limitations, the study represents the most extensive analysis of CRE burden and resistance trends in Bangladesh to date. It provides valuable insights for national AMR control efforts and contributes to regional and global surveillance data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales remain a serious and persistent public health threat in Bangladesh, with high prevalence and extensive resistance to critical antimicrobials. The association of CRE with hospitalized patients and severe infections highlights the urgent need for comprehensive, nationwide implementation of robust infection prevention, control measures, and effective antimicrobial stewardship programs. Encouragingly, Aminoglycosides showed comparatively lower resistance, suggesting a limited but notable therapeutic potential against CRE. Finally, continuous surveillance, improved laboratory capacity for detection and characterization, and evidence-informed treatment guidelines are paramount to mitigating CRE spread and preserving last-line antibiotic efficacy in Bangladesh.
