Enhanced B cell priming induces broadly neutralizing HIV-1 apex antibodies
Authors: Lorie Marchitto, Kshitij Wagh, Ryan S Roark, Severin Coleon, Hui Li, Ashwin N Skelly, Michael P Hogarty, Rumi Habib, Wenge Ding, Kasirajan Ayyanathan, Weimin Liu, Zizhang Sheng, Yicheng Guo, Joena Bal, Lena M Smith, Laura L Sutherland, Younghoon Park, Andrew J Connell, Frederic Bibollet-Ruche, Emily Lewis, Samantha J Plante, Macy J Akeley, Jinery Lora, Chengyan Zhao, John W Carey, Christian L Martella, Yingying Li, Mary S Campion, Melinda G Lituchy, Rebecca A Osbaldeston, Colette G Gordon, Amie Albertus, Justin Su, Chiaki Noguchi, Ying K Tam, Christopher Barbosa, Bo Liang, Khaled Amereh, Xuduo Li, Agnes A Walsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient priming of B cell precursors is a rate-limiting step in the induction of V2 apex broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs)1,2. Here we describe a germline-targeted HIV-1 Env (CAP256.OPT4) that increases the efficiency of V2 apex bnAb precursor priming by 30–400-fold compared with wild-type HIV-1 Envs and induces, in over 90% of macaques, neutralization breadth that includes Asn130-containing viruses. Using three different delivery platforms—persistently replicating simian human immunodeficiency viruses (SHIVs), protein nanoparticles and mRNA—we show bnAb priming as early as 4 weeks after infection or immunization, and neutralization breadth in the plasma by 12 weeks. In 14 SHIV-infected macaques, neutralization breadth reached as high as 90% on a 21-virus panel with potency as great as 1:20,000 (50% inhibitory dilution). Monoclonal bnAbs isolated from these animals were similarly broad and potent, with cryo-EM structures representing three distinct lineages revealing canonical needle-like HCDR3 binding. Env–Ab coevolution and structural analyses identified five key residues and loop features under positive selection and temporally associated with neutralization breadth. Importantly, prime–boost immunogens designed to capture these features induced broad and potent neutralization of globally diverse viruses including those containing Asn130 glycan. Furthermore, rhesus bnAbs were not restricted to IGHD3-15*01 heavy-chain alleles. These results expand the use of the rhesus model for HIV-1 vaccine design and provide a molecular blueprint for inducing V2 apex bnAbs in rhesus and humans.Subject terms: HIV infections, Protein vaccines

Main

Potent bnAbs are rarely elicited in HIV-1 infection3–5, yet these antibodies are the cornerstone of effective vaccination. When bnAbs are elicited in humans or SHIV-infected rhesus macaques (RMs), the V2 apex is a frequent target1,3,6. V2 apex bnAbs generally exhibit unusual features, including long (over 21 amino acids), tyrosine-sulfated, negatively charged heavy chain complementarity determining region 3 (HCDR3) segments that assume highly conserved needle, axe or composite shapes7,8. These structural motifs penetrate the dense apical glycan shield of Env to contact the underlying positively charged V2 apex C-strand. Antibodies with these properties are rare in naive human and rhesus B cell repertoires1,8–10 so, to prime them efficiently, Env immunogens must exhibit exceptional affinity or avidity. To this end, investigators have identified naturally occurring Envs with a propensity for binding V2 apex bnAb germline precursors1,6,11–14 or have used structure- or evolution-guided mutagenesis to select for Env variants with these properties8,9,15–17. Others have focused on multivalent Env presentation to increase avidity for germline B cell precursors18–20. Each strategy has shown some success, although the development of Env immunogens that consistently elicit potent bnAbs targeting diverse HIV-1 strains, including those with glycans at apex residue Asn130, in outbred animals has been largely unsuccessful. Here we describe a germline-targeted Env, developed using a neutralization-signature-informed strategy, that binds preferentially to rhesus and human V2 apex bnAb precursors in vitro and leads to rapid induction of potent bnAbs in the plasma of RMs after SHIV infection or vaccination.

CAP256.OPT4 Env design

We adapted a signature-based epitope targeted (SET) immunogen design strategy21 to address the specific problem of V2 apex bnAb precursor priming. This approach uses an iterative design scheme to optimize immunogens to carry HIV-1 Env signature patterns for bnAb precursor sensitivity (Methods) and led us to CAP256.OPT4 as a lead germline-targeting Env (Fig. 1a). A variant of this Env (CAP256.OPT4-S, also known as OPT4-shielded) was also generated by introducing a potential N-linked glycosylation site (PNGS) at residue Asn187 to shield an immunodominant off-target strain-specific epitope in the V2 carboxyterminus. Viruses bearing CAP256.OPT4 or CAP256.OPT4-S Envs were neutralized by as many as 6 out of 17 V2 apex-targeted inferred germline (iGL) or unmutated common ancestor (UCA) bnAb lineage antibodies with IC50 values ranging from 0.07 to 174 μg ml−1, whereas viruses expressing the parental CAP256SU wild-type Env were sensitive to none (Fig. 1b). Binding studies demonstrated affinity of CAP256.OPT4 and CAP256.OPT4-S Envs for additional human and rhesus iGL and UCA antibodies, and this could be further enhanced by avidity effects with nanoparticle presentation (immunogen design is described below).

bnAb induction in SHIV-infected macaques

SHIV infection of RMs was shown previously to recapitulate many of the immunobiological features of HIV-1 infection in humans, including bnAb induction1,22 (Supplementary Note). We infected eight RMs with SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 and eight others with SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S. SHIV replication kinetics were typical with peak viraemia at 2–4 weeks and semi-steady state setpoint viraemia by week 12 (Fig. 1c,d). SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S peak viraemia and setpoint viraemia were significantly higher than for SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 (Extended Data Fig. 1), with two animals showing rapid clinical deterioration necessitating euthanasia. Of 14 evaluable monkeys, all developed potent autologous neutralizing antibody (nAb) responses beginning as early as 4–8 weeks after infection. Autologous nAbs developed earlier and to higher titres in SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-infected RMs due to the absent glycan at residue 187. In all RMs, much of the early polyclonal autologous NAb response was directed to the C strand, as neutralization titres were reduced by 10-fold to 100-fold or more against isogenic R169E C-strand mutants (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 1). Early C-strand-targeted NAb responses were corroborated by rapid and complete escape at Env residues 166–177 in the evolving viral quasispecies (Fig. 1f; see the section on Env-antibody co-evolution below). Notably, in 13 out of 14 monkeys, we observed heterologous neutralization against a panel of 21 tier 1B/2 viruses as early as 12 weeks after infection, with breadth rapidly increasing to as high as 90% and inhibitory dilution (ID50) titres as high as 1:20,000 by 32–36 weeks after infection (Fig. 1e–h). Neutralization breadth was positively correlated with peak and setpoint plasma virus load (Extended Data Fig. 1), implicating higher antigen load as a driver of bnAb priming and expansion. The marked neutralization breadth and potency that we observed (Fig. 1e) was strictly C-strand dependent (Fig. 1i). Importantly, plasma from nearly all RMs neutralized multiple Asn130-containing viruses, with some RMs (CN81, A19N225, A19X045 and A19X066) neutralizing at least 4 out of 7 strains (Fig. 1e).

Efficiency of V2 apex bnAb priming

Rapid induction of bnAbs in over 90% of RMs with ID50 titres frequently exceeding 1:1,000 was a surprise and suggested that bnAb precursor priming efficiency in the SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S model might far exceed anything that we had observed previously in 115 RMs infected with SHIVs bearing wild-type HIV-1 Envs1. To test this hypothesis, we used antigen-specific fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to compare the priming of V2 apex bnAb-like B cells by SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 and SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S versus SHIVs expressing wild-type CAP256SU, CH505 or Q23.17 Envs. We chose these Envs because they had previously been shown to exhibit a natural predilection for inducing V2 apex bnAbs either in their natural human hosts or in SHIV-infected monkeys1,22,23 and because they had been found to bind preferentially to V2 apex bnAb precursors in vitro7,11–14. Using FACS, we isolated autologous, epitope-specific IgG+ B cells from peripheral blood at 12 weeks after SHIV infection (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1) and analysed their paired heavy and light immunoglobulin (Ig) chains to identify expanded B cells with immunogenetic features of typical rhesus V2 apex bnAbs, including a long HCDR3 over 21 amino acids and IGHD3-15*01 gene use1,7,22. To identify potential V2 apex bnAb precursors that used non-IGHD3-15*01 genes, we also looked for expanded B cell lineages with HCDR3s over 21 amino acids with any IGHD gene, including IGHD3-15*01. From 8 SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-infected animals, we found a total of 105 expanded lineages that used the IGHD3-15*01 gene (average of 13 lineages per RM, range 0–30) and a total of 116 expanded lineages that used any IGHD gene (average of 15 lineages per RM, range 2–34; Fig. 2b). From 6 SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S-infected animals, we found a total of 53 expanded lineages that used the IGHD3-15*01 gene (average of 9 lineages per RM, range 4–14) and a total of 62 expanded lineages that used any IGHD gene (average of 10 lineages per RM, range 5–17) (Fig. 2b). Combined, SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 and OPT4-S primed an average of 11 V2 apex-targeted B cell lineages per RM that used the IGHD3-15*01 gene and 13 that used any IGHD gene. These results were in stark contrast to RMs infected with SHIV.CAP256SU (n = 4), SHIV.CH505 (n = 4) and SHIV.Q23.17 (n = 4), in which plasma viral loads were comparable but we detected only 0, 2 and 2 expanded IGHD3-15*01 lineages and 0, 3 and 2 expanded all-IGHD lineages, respectively (Fig. 2c). As a positive control, we sampled two RMs from each of the latter three SHIV groups later in infection after they had developed V2 apex bnAbs in the plasma1 and identified in each monkey expanded bnAb lineage B cells (Extended Data Fig. 2). From these results, we could calculate directly the relative enhancement in priming efficiency in SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S-infected RMs versus wild-type SHIV.CAP256SU/CH505/Q23.17-infected RMs to be 11/0.33 (33-fold; P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) for IGHD3-15*01 lineages and 13/0.42 (31-fold; P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) for all IGHD lineages. We next extended this comparison to include 115 RMs infected by any one of 16 different SHIVs1 that were followed for an average of 2 years for evidence of V2 apex C-strand targeted antibodies (Methods). When normalized to the number of V2 apex targeted lineages identified per RM per week of infection, the results showed a highly significant 372-fold enhancement (P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) in the frequency of IGHD3-15*01 lineage priming and a 418-fold enhancement (P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) in the frequency of all IGHD lineage priming in SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S-infected RMs (Fig. 2d and Methods).

Among all of the SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S-infected RMs, we identified a total of 178 expanded lineages, 158 (89%) of which contained the IGHD3-15*01 gene. The remaining 20 (11%) contained other IGHD genes as listed in Fig. 2e. Rhesus non-IGHD3-15*01 gene mismatches compared with orthologous human genes ranged from 1 to 6 amino acids, indicating close homologies (Supplementary Fig. 2). The HCDR3 lengths of the rhesus antibodies ranged from 22 to 27 amino acids. When these non-IGHD3-15*01 rhesus antibodies were expressed with their respective paired light chains and tested for neutralization potency and epitope specificity, they were in many cases indistinguishable from IGHD3-15*01 antibodies, showing potent neutralization that was C-strand dependent (for example, see CN81-1161 (IGHD3-28*01) and A19X008-10 (IGHD3-34*01)), compared with all other IGHD3-15*01 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) in Fig. 2f. Overall, these findings support the conclusion that OPT4/OPT4-S Envs enhance V2 apex bnAb induction by increasing the efficiency of bnAb precursor priming and that a non-negligible proportion of these expanded precursors uses non-IGHD3-15*01 genes.

V2 apex bnAb expansion and maturation

To understand how enhanced priming led to rapid development of bnAbs in plasma, we sorted IgG+ B cells from peripheral blood at 28 weeks after infection when the plasma breadth and potency had expanded in 13 out of 14 animals (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3). For these analyses, we used sorting probes corresponding to OPT4-S, OPT4-S containing C-strand residue 169/171 epitope knockout (KO) mutations, and a heterologous Env V703.2117 from the AMP trial24,25. We identified a total of 101 expanded V2 apex targeted lineages with HCDR3 over 21 amino acids and any IGHD gene in animals infected with SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 compared with 116 lineages 16 weeks earlier. Similarly, in SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S-infected RMs, we identified 52 lineages at week 28 compared with 62 lineages 16 weeks earlier (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3). From this analysis, several themes emerged: (1) of the 153 expanded B cell lineages identified at week 28, all but 58 had been detected at week 12, indicating that they were primed early and persisted; (2) conversely, of the 178 lineages detected at week 12, 70 were no longer found at week 28, suggesting competition among lineages for expansion and affinity maturation; (3) even in instances in which multiple primed lineages did persist through week 28, their relative sizes could change substantially (for example, CE79 and CI93 (Fig. 3a,b), and A19N170, A19N185, A19X008, A19X045, A19X064, CI42 and CI71 (Extended Data Fig. 3)), again suggesting competition among lineages; and (4) the proportion of B cell lineages at week 28 that bound to both the autologous probe and the heterologous V703.2117 probe was very high, indicating that affinity maturation had led to the acquisition of substantial breadth in binding (for example, CE79 and CI93 (Fig. 3a,b) and CN81 and A19X045 (Extended Data Fig. 3)). This is especially notable because the V703.2117 Env contains a glycosylated Asn130 residue that can obstruct V2 apex bnAb binding. Overall, of the 153 lineages at week 28 that bound OPT4-S in a C-strand-dependent manner, 95 bound to V703.2117 and 60 of these had been detected at week 12. These findings help to explain the rapid development of neutralization breadth in the 13 RMs (Fig. 1e).

To assess affinity maturation towards improved breadth and potency, we analysed a total of 155 mAbs representing 60 expanded lineages from 13 RMs (Supplementary Data 2). Variable heavy chain (VH) gene alleles were inferred from the IMGT database26 (Supplementary Data 3) and somatic hypermutation (SHM) was estimated. For every B cell lineage except for one, we found substantial increases in SHM between week 12 and week 28 (Fig. 3c,d). When mAbs representing the 60 lineages were tested for neutralization against the autologous virus, all but four neutralized potently in a C-strand-dependent manner and with variable dependence on N160 (Supplementary Data 2). This was a key finding, as it indicated that the many expanded C-strand-targeted B cell lineages that we identified comprised antibodies with necessary features of V2 apex bnAbs, including structures and chemistries that enabled them to penetrate the apical glycan shield, bind to C-strand residues and neutralize virus potently. Most of these mAbs could also neutralize CAP256.OPT4-S virus when an Asn130 glycosylation site was added (Fig. 3e,f). When tested for heterologous neutralization, breadth and potency varied widely (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Data 2), as would be expected for antibody lineages needing to undergo further affinity maturation to optimize binding and neutralization. We isolated 13 mAbs, each from a different macaque, that exhibited broad and potent neutralization against a large panel of 21 heterologous viruses, including 7 that contained Asn130 (Fig. 3f). The breadth against Asn130-lacking viruses ranged from 29% to 86%, and breadth against Asn130-containing viruses ranged from 14% to 71%. Potency assessed by geometric mean IC50 titres was similar for both sets of viruses: 1.03 versus 0.54 µg ml−1, respectively. In each RM, neutralization by the mAb could largely account for the breadth and potency observed in the corresponding week 28 plasma.

Structural characterization of V2 bnAbs

We produced the antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) of three representative bnAbs: CE79-1571 with 38% heterologous breadth, CN81-2029 with 52% heterologous breadth and CI93-1365 with 48% heterologous breadth (Fig. 3f). We complexed each of these Fabs with the soluble Q23-SCT27 trimer2 stabilized in its prefusion-closed conformation at a 3:1 Fab-to-trimer ratio, flash-froze the complexes on cryo-grids and collected single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) data on the Titan Krios system (Supplementary Fig. 3). From over 100,000 particles of each Fab complex, we obtained reconstructions at a resolution of 2.9–3.3 Å (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Table 1). All three structures revealed extended tyrosine-sulfated HCDR3 needle-like conformations that penetrated the V2 apex, with each using a conserved aspartic acid to interact with the apex rim—a mode of recognition observed in the human V2 apex bnAbs PGT145 and PCT64-35S27,28 as well as the rhesus V2 apex bnAb RHA17,22 (Fig. 4b, Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Determination of the structures of Fabs CE79-1571, CN81-2029 and CI93-1365 with Env trimer enabled the specifics of their recognition to be delineated, including antibody class29,30, contact residues and SHM (Fig. 4c–e, Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5 and Supplementary Table 1). All three bnAbs belonged to the same reproducible RHA1-antibody class, for which we previously identified two other members, 6070-a.01 and T646-a.017 (Extended Data Fig. 5). This antibody class differs from other modes of V2 apex recognition in that it does not use C-strand hydrogen bonding and depends only on the particulars of the HCDR3 region7. Members of this class have HCDR3s with lengths over 22 residues and use the IGHD3-15 germline gene in a motif defined by CX[T|R|K]X[YY|FY|YF]XDDYsulfated[G|A]X[F|L]DXW (the residues in brackets indicate allowed alternatives and ‘X’ represents any amino acid).

Almost all light-chain paratope contacts made by these antibodies interacted with glycan, while the heavy chains made between 61–84% of their contacts with protein and the rest with glycan (Supplementary Table 1). For heavy chains, there were between 24 and 36 paratope contacts on each antibody, of which 12–15 involved glycan. Of these, 5–9 were altered by SHM (around two-thirds involving protein and about one-third glycan), with protein contacting SHM often focused to the site of V2 apex penetration (Fig. 4d,e). In general, we observed a higher proportion of SHM involving glycan than the relative surface area of glycan interaction. When compared to PCT64-35S, for which the total SHM was higher (Fig. 4f), we found glycan-contacting SHM to also be higher (Extended Data Fig. 4). We speculate that the higher glycan-related SHM may be because glycan (in all cases related to the highly conserved Asn160 glycan) is a conserved feature of the epitope, while the protein surface underwent escape31. For CE79-1571 and CN81-2029, the loss of Asn160 resulted in substantial reductions in neutralization while, for CI93-1365, neutralization appeared unchanged (Extended Data Fig. 4d), therefore highlighting differences in glycan contribution to bnAb binding by different members of the same class. Overall, we observed marked convergent evolution of these SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-induced bnAbs and note that many of the protein-contacting SHM changes were in close proximity, if not direct contact, with C-strand changes at residues 166 and 169 in the evolving virus quasispecies, suggesting their development in response to co-evolution of the infecting SHIV.

Env–antibody co-evolution leading to breadth

Previous studies of HIV-1-infected individuals have shown that viral diversification at bnAb epitopes precedes or coincides with the development of neutralization breadth23,32–35. Here we had the unique opportunity to analyse patterns of HIV-1 Env evolution leading to V2 apex bnAbs in multiple RMs infected by the same SHIV strain. We analysed longitudinal Env evolution in each host to identify sites and variable loop features under selective pressure and the temporal correlation between these mutations and breadth development (Fig. 4g,h and Extended Data Figs. 6–8). We used LASSIE36 to identify amino acid and glycan mutations under selection using a loss of transmitted-founder sequence criterion of over 80% for any timepoint for each macaque. As variable loops evolve through insertion–deletion mutations (indels) in addition to amino acid substitutions, leading to unreliable alignment, we analysed changes in alignment-free characteristics of length, number of glycans and net charge to characterize longitudinal evolution21. To identify mutations and loop features critical for Env–bnAb coevolution, we tested them for statistical correlations with the extent and kinetics of breadth development across macaques.

Selection was predominantly focused in the V2 apex with each macaque showing mutations in at least one of eight V2 apex sites, of which residues 169 and 130 were most common (Fig. 4g,h and Extended Data Figs. 6–8). The most frequent loop changes were evolution to more negatively charged V2 loops, and elongation and increased glycosylation of V1 loops. Three mutating sites and three loop characteristics were found to be significantly correlated with maximum breadth across macaques (Fig. 4h). Of the former, only residues 130 and 169 were repeatedly mutated in multiple RMs, while 160 was selected in only a single monkey. Higher mutation frequency at 130 and 169, and evolution to more negatively charged V2 loops as well as longer and more glycosylated V1 loops, were all individually positively correlated with breadth development (Extended Data Fig. 8b). The most consistent change in variable loops was a charge flip of the V2 hypervariable loop from that of +1 or +2 for CAP256.OPT4-S and CAP256.OPT4, respectively, to a negative charge as low as −3 (Fig. 4h). Negatively charged V2 loops evolved in all RMs except for A19N160, which along with 46010, developed the least breadth (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Figs. 6–8b). In all but two RMs with the lowest breadth, Arg169 showed high mutation frequency either before or concomitant with the onset of breadth. His130 mutations showed a similar trend for most RMs. Each of these mutations or loop changes also showed temporal correlation with the development of plasma breadth (Extended Data Fig. 8c–j). In all RMs, the V2 loop charge flip from positive to negative preceded or was contemporaneous with the onset of heterologous breadth. Increase in length and glycosylation of V1 was found in the middle stages of breadth development in some RMs (Extended Data Fig. 8c–j). These results indicate that the five identified Env sites and loop features not only correlate with maximum breadth but also show temporal correlations with breadth development.

To further investigate the mechanism by which these identified mutations and loop features could select for breadth development, we compared the sequence of neutralized heterologous viruses at these sites to the mutations that appeared in SHIV-infected macaques (Extended Data Fig. 8h–k). We found that each selected mutation and loop feature arose before or concomitant with the onset of neutralization of heterologous viruses with matching or similar amino acids and loop features. For example, Q23.17, which has a negatively charged V2 loop, could only be neutralized by plasma from timepoints after negatively charged V2 loops had already evolved in the virus quasispecies (Extended Data Fig. 8h). Similarly, CM244 and V703.2117 could only be neutralized by plasma from macaques of which the virus quasispecies had acquired R169K and H130N mutations, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 8i,j). A systematic analysis across all mutations and all heterologous viruses revealed that, in 97% of the instances of neutralization of heterologous Envs, the result could be explained by the accumulation of the same or similar mutations or loop features in the SHIV quasispecies (Extended Data Fig. 8k and Supplementary Note). For germline targeting, OPT4 was deliberately designed to include the short and positively charged V1/V2 loop, Arg169 and His130 that are rare in global HIV-1 viruses but were shown empirically to be necessary for binding bnAb lineage UCAs and intermediate precursors (Extended Data Fig. 8l,m). Together, these results suggest that longitudinal Env evolution that replaced these rare germline-targeting amino acids and loop features at the V2 apex of SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 and OPT4-S with more prevalent amino acids and features found in heterologous wild-type Envs was critical in maturing primed V2 apex bnAb precursors to acquire heterologous breadth.

Immunization elicits V2 apex bnAbs

To generate a particulate CAP256.OPT4 priming immunogen that resembles SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 virions, we used sortase A to site-specifically conjugate soluble OPT4 trimers to ferritin nanoparticles (OPT4–scNPs)19. Size-exclusion chromatography and negative-stain electron microscopy (Extended Data Fig. 9a,b) confirmed the presence of well-folded, native-like OPT4 Env trimers on the multimeric nanoparticle. OPT4–scNPs, but not OPT4 soluble trimer, induced intracellular Ca2+ flux in mouse B cells expressing the iGL of the human V2 apex bnAb CH01, indicating receptor cross-linking (Extended Data Fig. 9c). Soluble OPT4 Env trimers bound by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to human and rhesus V2 apex bnAb iGL or UCA receptors in an Asn160- and Arg169-dependent manner, and the breadth and magnitude of binding were greatly enhanced by nanoparticle presentation (Fig. 5a). Using bilayer interferometry (BLI), we found that OPT4–scNP bound to 4 out of 4 human and 11 of 11 rhesus V2 apex bnAb precursors, while soluble OPT4 trimers bound only a fraction of these and with lower magnitude (Fig. 5a). Thus, the avidity effects of multimeric OPT4–scNPs greatly improved V2 apex bnAb precursor binding.

To test OPT4 priming and boosting in vivo, we conducted a pilot immunogenicity trial in five RMs in which we vaccinated twice with OPT4–scNPs adjuvanted with empty lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)37,38 at weeks 0 and 10 and then boosted with LNP-encapsulated mRNA (mRNA-LNP) encoding a membrane-anchored OPT4-S trimer at week 27 (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 9d). Immunized monkeys were sampled at biweekly intervals and their binding and nAb responses were examined. All monkeys developed high-titre binding antibodies to OPT4 and OPT4-S trimers, which increased after the second and third immunizations, as well as cross-binding antibodies to heterologous Envs Q23.17 and CM244 (Extended Data Fig. 9e). A large fraction of this polyclonal response was directed to the V2 apex as plasma antibodies blocked the binding of three human V2 apex bnAbs but not that of a control non-V2 apex reactive 2G12 bnAb (Extended Data Fig. 9f). These results were confirmed by EM polyclonal epitope mapping (EMPEM) (Extended Data Fig. 9g).

To characterize the neutralization potential of vaccine-induced antibody responses, longitudinal plasma samples were examined. Consistent with the binding data, autologous neutralizing antibodies to OPT4 were detected as early as week 3 (Fig. 5c). These titres, which were largely R169 C-strand dependent, increased substantially after the second immunization with average ID50 values increasing by over 400-fold (Fig. 5c). As expected, no OPT4-S neutralization was detected after the first OPT4–scNP immunization, confirming the shielding effect of the Asn187 glycan. Moreover, removal of the Asn160 glycan did not enhance neutralization, indicating that 2909-like antibodies39 with short (less than 22 amino acids) HCDR3 segments had not been elicited. Notably, addition of a glycan at position 130 in both OPT4 and OPT4-S reduced but did not abrogate autologous neutralization, indicating that some of the induced polyclonal antibody response was able to tolerate the Asn130 glycan (Fig. 5c).

We next assayed week 31 plasma IgG against 15 heterologous viruses including five that contained Asn130 glycan. Normal rhesus plasma contains around 20 mg ml−1 IgG40, so our starting IgG concentration of 2,000 µg ml−1 corresponds to a 1:10 plasma dilution. At this threshold of detection, all five vaccinated RMs exhibited bnAb activity 4 weeks after the OPT4-S mRNA-LNP boost (Fig. 5d). Two RMs (192254 and 192291) neutralized more than 50% of viruses tested including 2 of 5 viruses containing Asn130. Neutralization by RM192254 was exceptional, showing over 70% breadth and a geometric mean IC50 = 93 µg ml−1. These bnAb responses were strictly C-strand dependent (Extended Data Fig. 9h). Notably, a mAb representing this bnAb lineage in RM192254 was found to contain an HCDR3 of 26 amino acid length encoding a rhesus IGHD3-20 (D3-SXKU*01) gene—not a IGHD3-15*01 gene found in many other rhesus V2 apex bnAbs41. Thus, priming with OPT4–scNPs, followed by immunofocused boosting with OPT4-S mRNA-LNPs, expanded multiple V2 apex bnAb precursors and elicited a heterologous plasma breadth that, in one animal, reached titres far exceeding those required for clinical protection24,42–44.

In the pilot trial, neutralization breadth and potency were variable (Fig. 5d). In that study, the OPT4 and OPT4-S prime and boost (Fig. 5b) did not contain Asn130 nor did they introduce heterogeneity at key epitope residues and loop features predicted by Env–Ab coevolution analyses (Fig. 4g,h) to be important for driving affinity maturation to breadth. Thus, we designed a new trial in which eight monkeys were vaccinated with either OPT4 (n = 4) or OPT4-S (n = 4) Env nanoparticles followed by an mRNA-LNP boost with OPT4-S containing Asn130 glycan (Fig. 5e). This was followed by two cocktail boosts comprised of four naturally evolved Envs from SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/S-infected RMs that contained H130N, R169K and V1/V2 loop features that were associated with the development of bnAbs in RMs CI42, CI93 and CN81, as well as mutations at 166 and 178 that were recurrent in multiple SHIV-infected RMs (Extended Data Figs. 6, 7 and 9i,j). Then, 2 weeks after the final boost, 8 out of 8 RMs exhibited neutralization breadth against 20–70% of a heterologous tier-2 virus panel weighted toward Asn130-containing viruses (Fig. 5f,g). The median breadth was 50% with a geometric mean IC50 against all sensitive viruses of 315 µg ml−1. The median breadth against Asn130-containing viruses was 43% with a geometric mean IC50 of 717 µg ml−1. Heterologous neutralization was strictly C-strand dependent as determined by CAM13 and 0921.v2.c14 epitope-KO viruses (Fig. 5f,g). Neutralization breadth and potency was equivalent in both study arms.

Discussion

Despite 40 years of concerted effort, the development of an effective HIV-1 vaccine remains a challenge. In outbred animal models, vaccination leading to bnAbs in serum at clinically protective titres has been particularly challenging with few successes, the exception being in ungulates with atypical immunogenetics and limited translatability to humans45,46. Immunization strategies targeting the fusion peptide47, membrane-proximal external region48 and V2 apex16 have had limited success in outbred animals or humans where immunogenicity has generally been weak and bnAb serum titres low. Other immunogens targeting the CD4bs, V3 glycan supersite and V2 apex have been shown to prime putative precursors but failed to elicit serum bnAbs15,49–58. In this context, a recent report18 was notable for inducing potent bnAbs in the serum of RMs after multivalent liposome-bound HIV-1 Env trimer immunization. Although providing proof-of-principle for breadth induction, the study required nearly 2 years of sequential vaccinations and was largely limited to heterologous viruses lacking a common glycosylation site at residue Asn130, therefore requiring further improvements for broader applicability.

Here we report a germline-targeted HIV-1 Env that enhances V2 apex bnAb precursor priming by 30–400 fold compared with wild-type Envs and rapidly induces bnAbs at high titres in SHIV-infected and vaccinated monkeys in less than 36 weeks. After SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S infection, 13 out of 14 RMs neutralized Asn130-glycan-containing viruses at a geometric mean ID50 titre of 1:143 (Fig. 1e), with cryo-EM analysis of bnAbs from three RMs showing reproducible structural and chemical features highly similar to human V2 apex bnAbs. In a pilot immunogenicity trial in five RMs, nanoparticle and mRNA LNP-delivered OPT4 and OPT4-S Envs—neither of which contain a glycan at residue 130—elicited antibodies with over 50% breadth in two monkeys, in each case neutralizing 2 of 5 Asn130-containing viruses (Fig. 5b,d). In one of these monkeys (RM192254), the breadth exceeded 70% and IC50 reached 1 µg ml−1, equivalent to an ID50 titre of 1:20,000. To improve on these results, we designed a rational lineage-based prime-boost regimen from Env–Ab coevolution analyses of the SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S-infected monkeys that developed breadth (Fig. 4g,h and Extended Data Figs. 6–8). In particular, we incorporated into our boost immunogens a cocktail of Envs homologous to the priming immunogen but containing additional substitutions at residues 130, 166 and 169, along with V1 and V2 loop length and charge changes, as critical drivers of bnAb maturation. In this second trial, bnAbs were elicited in 8 out of 8 RMs, in each case targeting one or more Asn130-containing viruses (Fig. 5e–j). Potency was as high as 3.18 µg ml−1 (IC50), equivalent to an ID50 of 1:6,300. Importantly, the total number of expanded V2 apex bnAb-like lineages in these 8 RMs was 63, of which 46 (73%) contained the IGHD3-15*01 gene and 17 (27%) contained other non-IGHD3-15*01 genes, a result comparable to SHIV.OPT4/OPT4-S-infected monkeys. These results—elicitation of bnAbs targeting Asn130-containing viruses in 100% of vaccinated monkeys after just 5 immunizations over 9 months—are to our knowledge unprecedented in an outbred animal model.

A key question remains of whether the frequent induction and maturation of V2 apex bnAbs in RMs can be translated to humans, given the differences in the IGHD gene repertoires and other distinguishing immunogenetic features between the two species1,7,9,10. The many expanded non-IGHD3-15*01 lineages primed by OPT4 and OPT4-S by SHIV infection and vaccination in this study, including the exceptionally broad and potent IGHD3-20 (D3-SXKU*01) lineage in RM192254 (Fig. 5d)41, suggest that the answer to this question will be yes. This may not be surprising, as the second-most-common bnAb specificity in HIV-1 infected humans is the V2 apex3 and because the OPT4/OPT4-S priming immunogen simply presents a shape, chemistry and electrostatic charge conducive to binding antibodies with a corresponding HCDR3, regardless of IGHD gene use. Key insights are likely to come soon as OPT4 and OPT4-S immunizations of humans are currently underway (HIV Vaccine Trials Network protocol HVTN322; NCT07390474).

Methods

Design of CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S Env immunogens and SHIV.CAP256.OPT4/OPT4-S

Using a two-step iterative process, we designed an HIV-1 Env to include features enabling binding to multiple rhesus and human V2 apex bnAb germline precursors1,7. V2 apex bnAb precursors, in contrast to most that target other epitopes, can bind to and neutralize a small subset of heterologous wild-type Env pseudoviruses11–14. This feature enabled us to define Env signature patterns for bnAb precursor sensitivity and resistance and adapt our SET immunogen design strategy21 to address the specific problem of V2 apex bnAb precursor targeting. We identified statistically significant amino acid and glycan neutralization signatures in diverse HIV-1 Envs that were associated with neutralization sensitivity to iGL antibodies corresponding to four human V2 apex bnAb lineages (CH01, PG9/16, VRC26, PCT64). Similar to our findings based on mature V2-bnAbs21, shorter and more positively charged V1 and V2 loops were significantly associated with sensitivity to multiple V2 apex bnAb precursors. Following the earlier V2-SET immunogen design strategy, we replaced V1and V2 loops in different wild-type Envs with loops from other naturally occurring Envs that exhibited optimal length and charge for sensitivity, in this case ZM233 for V1 and T250 for V2. We next iteratively tested a series of signature-optimized Envs as pseudoviruses for neutralization sensitivity to V2 apex bnAb precursors, which was again enabled by the unique heterologous neutralizing capacity of such precursors. In addition to the human precursors, we also included ten robustly inferred UCA sequences from rhesus V2 apex bnAbs1,7. This experimental testing enabled rapid iteration for assessing breadth and potency of V2 bnAb precursors for candidate Env immunogens and allowed us to examine sequence variation in the critically important C strand that could not otherwise be identified using signature analysis tools. After applying this combined development process to five different wild-type Envs, we identified the superinfecting transmitted/founder Env sequence (CAP256.SU) as well as a variant isolated at week 34 (CAP256.wk34) from the human individual CAP25623 as equivalent Env platforms in which to make substitutions in V1, V2 and the C-strand to create Env CAP256.OPT4 as our lead germline-targeting Env (Fig. 1a). A variant of these Envs (CAP256.OPT4-S) was also created to introduce a potential N-linked glycan sequon at residue Asn187 to shield an immunodominant strain-specific epitope in the V2 carboxy terminus. The design and construction of SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 and SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S were straightforward following previously described methods59. The nucleotide sequences of CAP256.OPT4 gp160 (PZ137521), CAP256.OPT4-S gp160 (PZ137522), SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 (PX677578) and SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S (PX677579) have been deposited in GenBank.

Non-human primates

Ten male Indian RMs (A19N225, A19X086, A19X008, A19X064, A19N185, A19X066, A19N160, A19X035, A19N170 and A19X045) aged 6 years were housed at the New Iberia Research Center (NIRC) at the University of Louisiana at Lafayette, LA, in accordance with guidelines from the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC). Nineteen Indian RMs, ten male (CN81, CI42, 46010, DL82, DO54, CY69, CY71, DL89, DL94 and DM47) and nine female (CI93, CI71, CE79, 192250, 192254, 192263, 192276, 192291 and DM87) aged 3–11 years were housed at Bioqual, Rockville, MD, in accordance with AAALAC guidelines. Macaques were randomly assigned irrespective of age or sex to different SHIV or immunization groups. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation. The study design and all experimental procedures were approved by the University of Pennsylvania, University of Louisiana (NIRC) and Bioqual Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees. Monkeys were naive with respect to previous SHIV infections and vaccinations with HIV with the exception of five (192250, 192254, 192263, 192276 and 192291), which were repurposed from a liposome HIV MPER immunization trial that failed to elicit HIV-1 nAbs. Before study entry, these monkeys were tested for anti-HIV-1 Env binding and neutralizing antibodies and were found to be negative for both (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 9d–f). RMs were sedated for procedures including blood draws and SHIV inoculations. Animals infected with SHIVs received subcutaneous infusions of 25 mg per kg of anti-CD8α mAb (MT807R1) 2 days before intravenous SHIV inoculations. These anti-CD8 mAbs transiently depleted CD8+ cells for a duration of approximately 2 months. Demographic information of the animals is listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Processing and storage of clinical specimens

Blood was collected in acid citrate dextrose containing vacutainers and sent by overnight delivery. It was then processed, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) viably frozen, and plasma was collected and frozen as previously described1. Lymph nodes were collected in RPMI1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum and shipped and processed on the same day. These tissues were subjected to manual disruption to create single-cell suspensions and then cryopreserved with or without Ficoll–Hypaque gradient purification. PBMCs typically showed over 90% viability, and lymph nodes showed over 70% viability.

Plasma viral RNA quantification

Plasma viral load measurements were performed by the NIH/NIAID-sponsored Nonhuman Primate Virology Core Laboratory at the Duke Human Vaccine Institute according to methods described previously1. The lower limit of quantification of this assay was 62 RNA copies per ml.

Cell line source

HEK293T/17 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-11268). TZM-bl cells were obtained from BEI Resources (ARP-8129). Expi293F were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (A14527). All cell lines were authenticated by the original source. Cells were regularly monitored for unusual morphology or growth kinetics. All cell lines were certified mycoplasma free. No commonly misidentified cell lines were used.

Virus stock generation

SHIV and Env-pseudotyped virus stocks were generated by transfection of HEK293T/17 cells, as previously described1. Infectious virus stocks typically contained over 103 ng ml−1 p27Ag and over 106 infectious units per ml.

Neutralization assay

nAb assays were performed as previously described1,60. This assay was originally developed by our laboratory (G.M.S.)61 and has been cross-validated by the Central Reference Laboratory for the Collaboration for AIDS Vaccine Discovery funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation62. The starting dilution for rhesus plasma was 1:20, which is equivalent to 1,000 µg rhesus IgG per ml. Lower dilutions of plasma (<1:20) frequently result in non-specific inhibition of virus entry or Tat-mediated expression of the luciferase by the TZM cells, a problem that can be avoided by using purified plasma IgG. For neutralization assays using purified plasma IgG, the starting concentration was 2,000 µg ml−1. bnAb mAbs were assessed for neutralization using the same TZM-bl assay in laboratories at the University of Pennsylvania (G.M.S.) and at Beth Israel New England Deaconess Hospital (M.S. Seaman). Statistical analysis based on neutralization results was performed using GraphPad Prism (v.11.0).

Generation of soluble trimers for flow cytometry

Stabilized HIV Env trimers with C-terminal AviTags were expressed in Expi293F cells and were purified as previously described17. A V2 epitope KO (V2-KO) construct was generated by introducing R169E and K171E mutations into the OPT4 backbone. Site-specific biotinylation of the AviTag was achieved by co-transfection with Escherichia coli biotin ligase (BirA). Transfected cells were cultured for 4 days, the supernatant was collected and Env trimers were purified by affinity chromatography using either agarose-bound Galanthus nivalis lectin or Toyopearl AF-Tresyl-650M resin conjugated to the bnAb PGT145. Eluted proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using the Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column equilibrated in PBS or Tris-buffered saline. The biotinylation efficiency was assessed by BLI using streptavidin biosensors on an Octet RH96 instrument (Sartorius). Statistical comparisons of priming efficiency

We used three different approaches to compare the priming efficiency of SHIV.OPT4 and SHIV.OPT4-S with SHIVs bearing wild-type HIV-1 Envs. The first was a simple and direct comparison of the total number of B cell lineages with features of V2 apex bnAbs (HCDR3 length > 21) that were expanded (>1 member) in the initial 12 weeks of infection by SHIV.OPT4 and SHIV.OPT4-S compared with the same interval in SHIV.CAP256SU-, SHIV.CH505- and SHIV.Q23.17-infected RMs. Normalized on a per macaque basis, the result was a marked 31-fold difference favouring OPT4/OPT4S (P < 0.0001; Wilcoxon rank-sum test). We next expanded this comparison to include 115 RMs infected by SHIVs expressing any one of 16 different primary HIV-1 Envs described elsewhere1,59. In that study1, we followed 115 RMs prospectively for an average follow-up period of 2 years looking for the development of neutralization breadth targeting any one of six canonical bnAb epitope supersites: V2 apex, V3 glycan, CD4bs, fusion peptide/interdomain, membrane proximal external region and silent face. We used a combination of neutralization assay, mapping by epitope-specific phenotypic mutants, negative-stain EMPEM, bnAb mAb isolation and cryo-EM to identify bnAb specificities. We also subjected serial plasma viral RNA samples to single-genome sequencing to identify genetic signatures of epitope targeting in the absence of bnAbs, which was rare. Using these combined methods, we could determine the frequency with which the V2 apex C-strand was targeted by bnAbs or expanded strain-specific bnAb-like lineages. Over the 2 year period of follow-up, we identified 25 lineage expansions among the 115 RMs. This enabled us to make a direct comparison of the number of C-strand-directed lineage expansions per monkey per week in the 14 SHIV.OPT4/OPT4-S monkeys (~0.95) with that in the 115 RMs (~0.0025), showing a 372-fold enhancement (P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank test). This difference was even more pronounced if we expanded our definition of V2 apex bnAbs to include all D genes. In this case, we observed an average of 1.1 lineages per week in the SHIV.OPT4/OPT4-S group with no change in the other 115 RMs, a 418-fold enhancement (P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank test).

Cryo-EM data collection and processing

The high-resolution structures of representative SHIV.OPT4/S-induced lineage members CE79-1571, CN81-2029 and CI93-1365 in a complex with HIV-1 envelope trimer were determined using single-particle cryo-EM. The samples were prepared by diluting purified Q23-SCT27 envelope trimer to 2.5 mg ml−1 with a threefold molar excess of Fab-to-trimer and incubated on ice for 45 min to allow complex formation, after which the samples were supplemented with a final concentration of 0.005% (w/v) n-dodecyl β-d-maltoside to shield protein from the air–water interface. Copper R1.2/1.3 Holey carbon-coated grids (Quantifoil R 1.2/1.3 300 Mesh, Cu; EMS) were glow-discharged and then applied with 3 μl of sample for 30 s in the Vitrobot Mark IV at room temperature under 100% humidity. The sample grids were blotted for 3 s and then immediately vitrified using liquid ethane. Cryo-EM data were collected on the FEI Titan Krios 300 kV cryo-transmission electron microscope equipped with a Gatan K3 detector operating in counting mode using EPU (v.2.9)68. A total dose of 58 e− Å−2 was fractionated over 50 raw frames with defocus values set to cycle between −0.80 and −1.8 μm. All data processing was performed in cryoSPARC (v.4.5)69, including motion correction, CTF estimation, particle picking, 2D classification, ab initio modelling and iterative 3D refinements. Initial particle picking was performed using the non-templated blob picker, followed by a second round of template picking using sideview 2D classes to increase the number of these particles. All homogenous and non-uniform 3D refinements were performed using C1 symmetry. Data acquisition and processing details for all structures are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3 and Extended Data Table 1.

Atomic model building and refinement

Each atomic model was solved by iterative manual rebuilding in Coot70, ISOLDE71 and real-space refinement in Phenix72. The initial model used for the envelope trimer in each complex was the apo structure of Q23-APEX-GT2 (PDB 9NVV), which has just two point mutations relative to the Q23-SCT27 trimer used in this study16. The initial model of each Fab variable region (Fv) was obtained using the AlphaFold3 server73. To prepare the initial model of each complex, the apo envelope trimer and Fv models were fit into each respective cryo-EM reconstruction density using UCSF ChimeraX (v.1.11)74. The overall structure quality for each model was periodically assessed using MolProbity (v.4.0)75 and EMRinger (v.1.0)37 during refinement until satisfactory validation was achieved. Protein interface calculations were performed using PDBePISA (v.1.52)76. Final model validations and statistics are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3 and Extended Data Table 1.

Longitudinal Env evolution

We identified sites under selection using our previously developed algorithm LASSIE36 with sites with more than 80% loss of transmitted/founder sequence in any single longitudinal timepoint deemed significant. Visualization of evolution at these sites of interest were done using sequence logos using Logomaker package for Python77. For longitudinal hypervariable loop evolution, we tracked medians and interquartile ranges at each timepoint from each macaque of three alignment-free characteristics—length, number of glycans and net charge—each of which we have shown to significantly impact neutralization of some bnAbs21. All visualizations including sequence logos were made using the Python package Matplotlib (v.3.8.4).

Env–Ab coevolution analysis

Several statistical and correlation tests were performed to analyse Env–bnAb coevolution. First, the correlation between the maximum longitudinal mutation frequency at each selected site and maximum plasma breadth developed in each macaque was analysed using the one-sided Kendall tau rank test, with the rationale that, at specific critical Env sites, more Ab selection pressure leading to high mutation frequency could be indicative of broader and more potent coevolving bnAb development. We also similarly compared variable loop characteristics for statistical correlation with maximum breadth development. We previously showed that V1 and V2 loop length, charge and number of glycans could have an important impact on V2 apex bnAb neutralization and showed that shorter and more positively loops are favoured21. Thus, for per-macaque correlations, we used the maximum longitudinal loop length and minimum longitudinal net charge (that is, more negative charge), with the rationale that viral escape from V2 apex bnAbs could develop through these pathways. Second, for the mutations and loop features that were significantly associated with breadth development, we analysed temporal correlations using longitudinal mutational frequency and plasma breadth from each macaque and each timepoint to test whether mutations could potentially have a causal relationship with breadth development and, if so, at what stage of breadth development. Third, to further test a potential of causal relationship, we analysed whether there was any correlation between onset of heterologous neutralization of a specific virus and the mutations of interest. For this, we compared per-macaque maximum mutation frequency at each site between the groups of macaques that can neutralize the virus versus those that cannot, and statistically tested the difference in mutation frequency using the one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. If a certain mutation is found to be important for development of neutralization of a given virus, such mutations must be present recurrently in most macaques who develop neutralization of such virus. Thus, we identified the maximum mutation frequency before or at the time that macaques developed neutralization and statistically compared whether this is a higher frequency than at transmission using the one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Fourth, we analysed which of the potential sites of interest showed such profiles and then tested how many cases were concordant with our hypothesis that heterologous neutralization of a virus with different sequences at critical Env sites in the V2 apex required either that mutation to be sampled in the longitudinal Envs or, if not, diversity had accumulated at that site. Our rationale was that diverse amino acids could select for bnAb maturation that is less reliant on the exact residue at the Env site of interest. To adapt this analysis to variable loop length, charge and number of glycans, we assumed based on our previous work on mature human V2 apex bnAbs21 that shorter more positively charged or less glycosylated variable loops as compared to the transmitted/founder Env or longitudinal Envs would be well tolerated, while longer more negatively charged or more glycosylated variable loops would be less-well tolerated. While the maximum V1 length and number of glycans were significantly associated with the maximum breadth in macaques, this was true only for a few macaques, and across all macaques these features were not predictive (Supplementary Data 4). On the other hand, while number of V2 glycans was not significantly associated with breadth overall, they were predictive of heterologous neutralization in SHIV.CAP256.OPT4 infected macaques (Supplementary Data 4). This suggested that the lack of V2 glycan in SHIV.CAP256.OPT4, which lasts for only a short time during infection as glycan is acquired by 4 or 8 weeks, might have induced V2 apex bnAb precursors that are reliant on glycan-free V2 loops, reminiscent of original antigenic sin. Thus, we included V2 loop glycans as an important predictive feature in our analyses (Extended Data Fig. 8k). All statistical tests were performed using the stats package in SciPy (v.1.13.1).

Negative-stain EMPEM analysis

For polyclonal serum Fabs, around 1 mg of polyclonal Fabs at around 10 mg ml−1 was mixed with 20 µg of CAP256.OPT4-S Env trimer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. To remove excess unbound Fab/IgG, the mixture was separated by SEC on the Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column. Fractions eluting at the expected volume for the complex were combined and concentrated with a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off spin concentrator to a nominal trimer concentration of around 1 mg ml−1. The concentrated samples were then diluted to 0.4 mg ml−1 with HEPES-buffered saline (HBS), containing 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4 augmented with 8 mM glutaraldehyde for cross-linking and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Unreacted glutaraldehyde was quenched by addition of 1 M Tris, pH 7.4 to a final Tris concentration of 80 mM. As needed, quenched samples were diluted to 0.1–0.2 mg ml−1 with HBSS or applied without dilution to glow-discharged carbon films on 300 mesh copper EM grids for negative staining. After blotting, the samples were stained for 1 min with 2% uranyl formate, blotted and allowed to air dry. Negatively stained grids were examined on the Philips EM420 electron microscope operating at 120 kV, ×49,000 nominal magnification and ~0.5 µm defocus. Images were acquired on a 76-megapixel CCD camera, corresponding to a nominal calibration of 2.4 Å per pixel. Datasets were typically around 100 or 500 images for monoclonal or polyclonal samples, respectively. Image analysis was performed with standard protocols in Relion (v.3.0)78.

BLI

BLI analysis was performed as previously described19,43. BLI binding assays were performed on the Octet Red96e system (Sartorius) at 30 °C with an orbital shake speed of 1,000 rpm. Assays were performed in flat bottom 96-well assays plates (Greiner) using 0.22-µm-filtered PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20 and 0.1% BSA (PBS-T-BSA). Antibodies (100 µg ml−1) were immobilized on hydrated anti-human Fc capture (AHC) biosensors for 120 s. Antibody-coated sensor tips were washed for 60 s. The baseline signal was measured for 180 s by dipping antibody-coated sensor tips in PBS-T-BSA. Next, the antibody-coated sensor tips were incubated with antigens at 50 μg ml−1 for 200 s to allow for association of the antigen. The binding response (nm) was determined at the end of the 200 s of association for each antigen using Data Analysis HT 12.0 software (Forte Bio).

Indirect ELISA using Env trimers

Corning 384-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 15 µl of streptavidin diluted to 2 µg ml−1 in 0.1 M NaCO3. The plates were washed with PBS-T (PBS + 0.05% Tween-20) and the wells blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 40 µl blocking buffer (SuperBlock comprising PBS, 15% goat serum, 4% whey protein and 0.05% Tween-20). After blocking, the plates were washed, then incubated with 10 µl per well of biotinylated Env diluted to 2 µg ml−1 in blocking buffer for 1 h. Beginning at a concentration of 100 µg ml−1 monoclonal antibodies were serially diluted in SuperBlock. Similarly, serum was diluted 1:30 in SuperBlock and serially diluted. The plates were washed, then incubated with 10 µl of a serial dilution of monoclonal antibody or RM serum for 1.5 h. The wells were washed twice with PBS-T, then incubated for 1 h with 10 μl of goat anti-human IgG-HRP (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 109-035-098) or mouse anti-rhesus IgG-HRP (Southern Biotech, 4700-05) diluted 1:8,000 or 1:30,000 in blocking buffer, respectively. The plates were washed twice, then developed with 20 µl per well of tetramethylbenzidine peroxidase substrate for 15 min, then quenched with equal volume 1% HCl. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a SpectraMax 340 PC. Binding titres were analysed as area-under-curve of the log-transformed concentrations.

ELISA blocking assay

Corning 384-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 15 µl of streptavidin diluted to 2 µg ml−1 in 0.1 M NaCO3. Plates were washed with PBS-T (PBS + 0.05% Tween-20), then blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 40 µl blocking buffer (SuperBlock: PBS + 15% goat serum, 4% whey protein and 0.05% Tween-20). Blocking buffer was removed and the plates were washed two times. Then, 10 µl of biotinylated HIV-1 Env at a concentration of 2 µg ml−1 was incubated in each well for 1 h. The plates were washed, then incubated in triplicate with or without 10 µl of a 1:50 dilution of RM plasma in blocking buffer for 1.5 h. To determine the relative binding, no plasma was added to a group of wells. Human bnAbs PGT145, VRC26.25 or PG9 were conjugated to HRP using HRP conjugation kit (Lightning-Link, Abcam, ab102890). Optimal PGT145-HRP, VRC26.25-HRP and PG9-HRP concentrations were determined by binding of titrated antibody to Env that resulted in binding signal reaching an A450nm between 1 and 2. Plates were washed and incubated with 10 μl per well of HRP-labelled PGT145, VRC26.25 or PG9. Plates were washed and developed with 20 µl per well of a tetramethylbenzidine peroxidase substrate for 15 min and quenched with equal volume 1% HCl. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a SpectraMax 340 PC. Binding of PGT145-HRP, VRC26.25-HRP and PG9-HRP to HIV-1 envelope in the absence of plasma was compared with in the presence of plasma using the formula: 100 − [(mean A450 with plasma/mean A450 without plasma) × 100], where the A450 is the absorbance at 450 nm. Based on historical negative controls, assays were considered to be valid if the positive control antibodies blocked greater than 20% of the HRP-labelled antibody binding.

HIV Env protein production

HIV-1 envelope was expressed and purified as previously described58,79. In brief, Freestyle HEK293F cells were diluted at the time of transfection to 1.25 × 106 cells per ml with fresh Freestyle293 media in 500 ml batches. Co-transfection was performed with plasmid DNA (650 µg SOSIP trimer plasmid and 150 µg furin expressing plasmid per 1 l of culture volume) complexed with 293fectin in Opti-MEM I. After 6 days, cell cultures were collected by centrifugation of the cells for 60 min at 4,000 rpm in a Sorvall table top centrifuge. The supernatant was filtered through 0.8 µm filter and concentrated to approximately 100 ml with Vivaflow 200 cassettes (Sartorius) with a 30 kDa MWCO. The concentrated cell-free supernatant was filtered again with a 0.8 µm cellulose filter system. The clarified concentrate was applied to a 10 ml affinity column containing PGT145 or 2G12 conjugated CnBr-activated Sepharose resin, using an AKTA Pure set to a flow rate of 2 ml per min. After washing the column in PBS with 0.05% sodium azide, Env trimers were eluted using 3 M MgCl2, and immediately diluted with 5 volumes of 10 mM tris, pH 8. The eluate was filtered with a 0.2 µm filter bottle system and concentrated to 2 ml with a Centricon-70 10 kDa MWCO. To biotinylate avi-tagged Envs, 25 µM of avi-tagged Env was dialysed into Tris pH 8 for 2–16 h. Dialysed Env was biotinylated in a BirA biotin-protein ligase reaction (Avidity) for 5 h with mild agitation at 30 °C. Biotinylated envelope was concentrated to 2.4 ml before SEC. Approximately, 2 ml of concentrated protein was purified on the HiLoad 16/60 Superose 6 pg column (Cytvia) in 500 mM NaCl buffer with 10 mM Tris, pH 8 to isolate trimeric protein. All chromatography steps were conducted on an AKTA Pure (Cytvia). The trimeric Env fractions were pooled, filtered with a 0.2 µm spin filter column, and snap-frozen for long-term storage at −80 °C.

Sortase A conjugation of HIV-1 envelope trimers to ferritin nanoparticles

Env trimer conjugate nanoparticle production was performed as described previously19. Sortase-A-tagged CAP256.wk34.c80.OPT4 envelope was expressed with amino acids LPSTGG encoded at its C terminus in Freestyle293 cells. The envelope was purified by affinity chromatography with Env trimer-specific antibody PGT145 and Superose6 16/60 column chromatography as stated above. Helicobacter pylori ferritin subunits were expressed with a GGGGG repeat sequence encoded at their N terminus. Ferritin was expressed in Freestyle HEK293F cells by transiently transfecting 650 μg of expression plasmid with 293fectin. Then, 5 days after transfection, ferritin nanoparticles were purified by nickel chromatography. His tags were cleaved off of the ferritin by HRV-3C cleavage overnight. CAP256.OPT4 Env with a C-terminal sortase A tag and ferritin particles with a sortase A N-terminal tag were buffer-exchanged into 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.5. 120 μM SOSIP gp140 was mixed with 120 μM ferritin subunits and incubated with 100 μM sortase A overnight at room temperature. After incubation, conjugated particles were isolated from free ferritin or free SOSIP gp140 using the Superose6 16/60 SEC column followed by sephacryl S-500 10/300 SEC. The final protein was snap frozen and stored in 10 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl pH 8.0 at −80 °C.

B-cell-receptor-dependent calcium flux in mouse B cells

Calcium flux in murine B cells was evaluated using the Fluo-4 direct calcium assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, F10471) as described previously19. Spleen cells from homozygous CH01 RUA3 (CH01 RUA3 VLJL+/+, VHDJH+/+) site-directed knock-in mice were used for ex vivo calcium flux assays. Mice were generated on the C57BL/6 background with IgH-locus-directed gene-targeting techniques80. For each condition, 107 splenocytes were stained for 30 min at room temperature with B cell surface marker anti-B220-BV711 and anti-CD19 PE in HBSS with 3% FBS. Prestained splenocytes were washed with HBSS and then incubated with an equal volume of 2× Fluo-4 direct calcium reagent loading solution for 30 min at 37 °C and 30 min at room temperature. Cells were washed and incubated with LIVE/DEAD Near IR_APC-Cy7 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) staining buffer for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, cells were washed and resuspended in calcium-containing HBSS. The baseline for Fluo-4 fluorescence was acquired for 20 s before potential ligation of the B cell receptor by 25 μg ml−1 of goat F(ab′)2 anti-mouse IgM (Southern Biotech) or 25 pM of OPT4 trimer or OPT4–scNP. Data were acquired on the BD LSR II flow cytometer and analysed using FlowJo software (v.10.10.1).

Ethics statement

This investigation was conducted with a focus and prioritization on the ethical treatment of animals and the four Rs of animal research: reduction, refinement, replacement and responsibility. Aside from great apes, the adaptive immune system of Indian RMs most closely approximates that of humans. This is especially the case for the naive germline B cell repertoire, which cannot be replicated or replaced by genetically engineered mice or other small animals or by biological or synthetic in vitro model systems. In total, 29 RMs divided among one SHIV infection and two Env immunization protocols were used, corresponding to the minimum number subjects required to obtain robust and statistically significant study end points. Animals were housed at Bioqual or at the New Iberia Research Center at the University of Louisiana at Lafayette, LA, in accordance with guidelines of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) standards. Care and attention were given to animal husbandry and the physical and psychological well-being of the monkeys, and housing and environmental conditions were maintained as specified by the AALAC. Where possible, animals were co-housed to maintain previous bonding relationships. All procedures involving monkeys followed written standard operating procedures developed in accordance with guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), including anaesthesia and euthanasia. Throughout the study, attention was given to the minimization of pain and distress, including appropriate administration of analgesics. Predetermined clinical end-point indications for euthanasia were followed, including prespecified weight loss, inappetence, weakness, inability to obtain food or water, infections unresponsive to antibiotics, and signs of severe organ dysfunction. Euthanasia was performed using methods consistent with AVMA guidelines. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) of the University of Pennsylvania, Bioqual and the University of Louisiana at Lafayette. Mice were cared for in a facility accredited by AAALAC. All study protocols and all veterinary procedures were approved by the Duke University IACUC. Rooms were on a 12 h–12 h light–dark cycle. Heat and humidity were maintained within the parameters outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and animals were fed a standard rodent diet. Rodents were housed in individually ventilated micro-isolator caging on corn cob bedding, which was changed regularly as needed. Group housing of up to 5 mice per cage was encouraged. Environmental enrichment for singly housed animals was provided.

Plasma neutralization is shown against an autologous V2 apex mapping panel as well as 21 heterologous viruses, expressed as reciprocal ID50 titres. RM ID, rhesus macaque identification; WPI, weeks post-infection.

Neutralization breadth and potency of mAbs isolated from SHIV.CAP256.OPT4- and SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S-infected rhesus macaques. Neutralization potency (IC50, µg ml−1) for monoclonal antibody tested against autologous V2 apex mapping panel and 21 heterologous viruses. All mAbs were isolated at week 28 with the probes used for isolation and the corresponding HCDR3 length indicated.

Immunogenetic characteristics of expanded B cell lineages from SHIV.CAP256.OPT4- and SHIV.CAP256.OPT4-S-infected animals. Each animal is identified with its infecting virus and name at the top of its respective table.

Env features associated with the development of heterologous breadth.
