Prevalence and prognostic implications of chronic kidney disease in patients with atrial functional mitral regurgitation
Authors: Yusuke Morita, Takahiro Sakamoto, Taiji Okada, Tomohiro Kaneko, Masashi Amano, Yukio Sato, Yohei Ohno, Masaru Obokata, Kimi Sato, Tomoko Machino-Ohtsuka, Yukio Abe, Kazuaki Tanabe, Nobuyuki Kagiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) is increasingly recognized as a distinct mitral regurgitation subtype; the prognostic impact of coexisting chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains unclear.MethodsData from 989 patients enrolled in the multicenter REVEAL-AFMR registry were analyzed. Coexisting CKD was defined as reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Patients were further classified by CKD severity as stage 1 + 2 (eGFR ≥60), stage 3a (eGFR ≥45 and < 60), stage 3b (eGFR ≥30 and < 45), and stage 4 + 5 (eGFR <30). The primary endpoint was a composite of cardiovascular death and heart failure hospitalization. Kaplan-Meier and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to assess the primary endpoint.ResultsCoexisting CKD was present in 429 of 989 patients (43.4%). Lower eGFR was associated with more advanced mitral regurgitation (MR), higher BNP levels, and more severe heart failure symptoms. Over a median follow-up of 1002 days, 181 patients (18.3%) experienced the primary endpoint. The cumulative incidence of the primary endpoint increased stepwise across CKD stages (log-rank P < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, CKD stage remained independently associated with adverse outcomes (HR 1.26; 95% CI 1.09–1.46; P = 0.002). This association was consistent across MR severity strata, with no significant interaction between MR severity and CKD stage.ConclusionAmong conservatively managed patients with AFMR, CKD is common in AFMR, and CKD stage is independently associated with worse outcomes in a graded manner, irrespective of MR severity. Renal function assessment may improve AFMR risk stratification.Keywords: Atrial functional mitral regurgitation, Chronic kidney disease, Heart failure

Introduction

Atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) is a distinct subtype of mitral regurgitation (MR), characterized by left atrial dilation and dysfunction in the absence of substantial left ventricular remodeling. Unlike primary MR, which is caused by structural abnormalities of the mitral valve, or secondary MR due to left ventricular dysfunction, AFMR is mainly attributable to atrial enlargement and impaired atrial function, which can result in malcoaptation of the mitral valve leaflets [1], [2], [3], [4].

Cardiorenal syndrome (CRS) is generally defined as a bidirectional interaction in which dysfunction of either the heart or kidney contributes to progressive impairment of the other organ [5]. Renal impairment can exacerbate cardiac dysfunction through volume overload, neurohormonal activation, inflammation, and structural remodeling. Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a major component of cardiorenal pathophysiology, is a known risk factor for cardiovascular disease and is associated with structural and functional cardiac changes, including left atrial enlargement, myocardial fibrosis, and increased ventricular filling pressures [6]. Although AFMR has recently been recognized as a distinct clinical entity, evidence regarding its natural history remains limited, particularly in relation to CKD. A previous study suggested that CKD may contribute to worse outcomes in patients with AFMR. However, this report was largely based on a single-center analysis with a relatively small sample size [7]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence and prognostic impact of coexisting CKD in patients with AFMR and to evaluate whether outcomes worsen in a graded manner across CKD stages using data from the multicenter REVEAL-AFMR (Real-World Observational Study for Investigating the Prevalence and Therapeutic Options for Atrial Functional Mitral Regurgitation) registry.

Methods

Study design

The REVEAL-AFMR study was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study designed to investigate the prevalence, clinical characteristics, treatment strategies, and outcomes of patients with AFMR [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The study design was previously published and was approved by the institutional review board of Juntendo University, Japan, and each participating center approved the conduct of the study [8]. In brief, patients who underwent transthoracic echocardiography between January 1 and December 31, 2019, at 26 participating centers in Japan were retrospectively screened. Adults (aged ≥20 years) diagnosed with AFMR were included, as determined by cardiologists specializing in echocardiography. AFMR was defined as significant MR with preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF ≥50%) and left atrial enlargement in accordance with contemporary concepts of AFMR [13], [14]. Left atrial enlargement was defined as a left atrial volume index (LAVI) ≥ 38 mL/m2 in men or ≥ 41 mL/m2 in women. If LAVI was unavailable, left atrial enlargement was defined as a left atrial diameter ≥ 40 mm in men or ≥ 37 mm in women, based on normal ranges in the Japanese population [15]. Exclusion criteria included degenerative mitral valve changes, systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve, MR primarily attributable to left ventricular remodeling, prior mitral valve intervention (including mitral valve surgery, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, and transcatheter mitral valve replacement), or acute decompensated heart failure (HF). Patients without estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) data were also excluded from this analysis. CKD was defined using eGFR calculated with the CKD-EPI Eq. [16]. Coexisting CKD was defined as eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Patients were categorized into CKD stages according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [17].

The cohort study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines and was approved by the institutional review board of Juntendo University, Japan, and each participating center, including the Shimane University Faculty of Medicine (approval number: K20220511–1). All enrolled individuals were informed about the study details and were reassured that they could withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was waived, given the observational and retrospective design, in accordance with the ethical guidelines for medical and biological research involving human participants in Japan. Study information was provided in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry public database before enrollment began (registration number: UMIN000046146).

Echocardiographic assessments

All transthoracic echocardiographic studies were reviewed by cardiologists specializing in echocardiography at each participating site. MR severity was graded using an integrated approach incorporating qualitative and quantitative metrics [18], [19]. Quantitative parameters, including effective regurgitant orifice area, regurgitant volume, and regurgitant fraction, were calculated using the proximal isovelocity surface area method and the continuity equation. For consistency across sites, shared example cases and anonymized echocardiographic videos were reviewed, and 100 randomly selected studies were reanalyzed by a core laboratory for data validation, as previously described in the REVEAL-AFMR registry report [8].

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was the time to first occurrence of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization. Cardiovascular death was defined as death due to sudden death, HF, or myocardial infarction. HF hospitalization was defined as an unplanned admission with a primary diagnosis of HF that required intensification of HF treatment. All-cause mortality was also evaluated as a sensitivity endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Associations between CKD groups and relevant clinical variables were assessed with one-way analysis of variance and Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. The cumulative probability of the primary endpoint was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group differences were compared with the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models were used to assess the primary outcomes. Models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, prior congestive HF, CKD stage, MR severity, and hemoglobin, consistent with previous analyses of this cohort [8]. Missing data for each variable were summarized, and no imputation was performed unless otherwise specified; complete-case analysis was used for the primary multivariable models. To assess the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed. These included a Cox proportional hazards model stratified by participating center to account for potential inter-center variability, an additional Cox regression analysis using all-cause mortality as the outcome, and a Fine–Gray competing-risk regression model evaluating cardiovascular death with non-cardiovascular death treated as a competing event. To evaluate the combined and interactive effects of CKD and MR, additional Cox models were included to examine each component and their interactions. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR, version 1.54 (Saitama Medical Centre, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R, version 4.0.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [20]. A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 989 patients with AFMR, coexisting CKD (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) was identified in 429 (43.4%). Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics by CKD stage. Patients with lower eGFR were generally older and tended to have a prevalence of NYHA class III/IV, AF, and prior HF hospitalizations. Use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and loop diuretics became more common with advancing CKD stage. In laboratory data, hemoglobin and albumin levels tended to decrease, whereas B-type natriuretic peptide levels increased as the CKD stage increased. On echocardiography, tricuspid regurgitation velocity and the proportion of patients with moderate-to-severe or severe MR increased with higher CKD stage. No patients had undergone transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair or transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement.

Kaplan-Meier analysis by CKD stage

Over a median follow-up of 1002 days (IQR: 546–1146 days), 181 patients (18.3%) experienced the primary outcome. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a stepwise increase in the cumulative probability of the primary endpoint with worsening renal function (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 1). Throughout follow-up, patients with CKD stages 4 + 5 had the highest cumulative incidence of the primary endpoint, whereas those with CKD stages 1 + 2 had the lowest cumulative incidence. The number at risk declined in parallel with CKD severity over time. For example, at 3 years, the numbers at risk were 218 in stage 1 + 2, 59 in stage 3a, 30 in stage 3b, and 26 in stage 4 + 5, indicating greater attrition among patients with more advanced CKD due to adverse events. During follow-up, 105 patients underwent mitral valve surgery and 26 underwent transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. In addition, 89 patients underwent tricuspid valve surgery (87 concomitant with mitral valve surgery), and 63 underwent catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation. No patients underwent transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair or transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement.

Kaplan–Meier curves for the cumulative probability of the primary endpoint (cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure) according to CKD stage. The cumulative probability of the primary endpoint increased progressively with worsening CKD stage.

Association between CKD stage and adverse outcomes

The Cox regression analysis of the association between CKD stage and clinical outcomes is shown in Table 2. In univariable analysis, CKD stage was significantly associated with a higher risk of the primary endpoint (HR: 1.50; 95% CI: 1.33 to 1.70; P < 0.001). After adjustment for clinically relevant covariates, including age, sex, BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), NYHA functional class, hemoglobin level, and previous congestive HF, CKD stage remained an independent predictor of adverse outcomes (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.09 to 1.46; P = 0.002). Other independent predictors included older age, NYHA class III/IV, lower hemoglobin level, and previous congestive HF. To account for potential inter-center variability, an additional multivariable Cox proportional hazards model stratified by participating center was performed. The association between CKD stage and adverse outcomes remained materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S1). As a sensitivity analysis, we evaluated all-cause mortality as the outcome. Over a median follow-up of 1005 days (IQR: 568–1146 days), 189 all-cause deaths were observed. CKD stage remained independently associated with all-cause mortality in the multivariable Cox regression model (HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.42; P = 0.006) (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, a Fine–Gray competing-risk regression analysis treating non-cardiovascular death as a competing event was performed. CKD stage remained significantly associated with cardiovascular death after accounting for competing risks (subdistribution HR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.16 to 1.89; P = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S3).

Impact of CKD stage on prognosis stratified by MR severity

The prognostic impact of CKD stage was evaluated by MR severity. In patients with moderate MR, the cumulative probability of the primary endpoint increased stepwise with worsening CKD stage (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Patients in CKD stage 4 + 5 had the poorest prognosis, whereas those in stage 1 + 2 had the most favorable outcomes. A similar pattern was observed in patients with severe MR, in whom the cumulative probability of the primary endpoint progressively increased with increasing CKD severity (log-rank P = 0.010; Fig. 2B). The effect of CKD stage on prognosis was similar across MR severity strata, and no significant interaction between MR severity and CKD stage was observed (CKD stage; HR: 2.23; 95% CI: 1.06 to 4.67; MR severity; HR: 1.87; 95% CI: 1.09 to 3.18; P for interaction = 0.278). Clinical outcomes were clearly stratified by MR severity and CKD stage (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3).

Kaplan–Meier curves for the cumulative probability of the primary endpoint (cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure) according to CKD stage in patients with moderate MR (A) and severe MR (B). The cumulative probability of the primary endpoint increased progressively with worsening CKD stage in both MR severity strata.

Kaplan–Meier curves for the cumulative probability of the primary endpoint (cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure) according to MR severity and CKD stage. Clinical outcomes were progressively worse with increasing MR severity and CKD stage.

Discussion

This multicenter observational study demonstrates a long-term association between CKD and AFMR. The principal findings were as follows. First, coexisting CKD was present in nearly half of the cohort. Second, worsening CKD stage was independently associated with a graded increase in the risk of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization. Third, this adverse association was consistent across MR severity levels, with no significant interaction between MR severity and CKD stage.

AFMR is a distinct subtype of MR characterized by mitral insufficiency secondary to left atrial enlargement and annular remodeling despite preserved left ventricular systolic function [13]. Recent studies have shown that patients with AFMR frequently exhibit heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), even when MR severity is moderate, reflecting advanced atrial myopathy and elevated filling pressures. Notably, Kagami et al. reported a high prevalence of HFpEF in patients with AFMR, accompanied by a high CKD burden, highlighting a close pathophysiological link among AFMR, HFpEF, and renal dysfunction [10]. Against this background, the coexistence of AFMR and CKD may represent a clinically important cardiorenal phenotype, although causality cannot be inferred from the present study.

The association between CKD and adverse outcomes in AFMR is likely multifactorial and may reflect cardiorenal pathophysiology. CKD promotes left atrial dilation, interstitial fibrosis, and elevated filling pressures through fluid retention, neurohormonal activation, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation [2], [21], [22]. These processes aggravate atrial remodeling and mitral annular dilation, contributing to leaflet malcoaptation and progression of AFMR. In addition, CKD frequently coexists with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and anemia, which further accelerate atrial remodeling and hemodynamic deterioration [23], [24]. Together, these systemic and structural abnormalities may form a vicious cycle linking renal dysfunction, atrial cardiomyopathy, worsening MR, and heart failure progression [25]. These findings build on prior research examining the prognostic links between CKD and valvular heart disease. Lo et al. reported that CKD independently predicted adverse outcomes in patients with MR, irrespective of the MR mechanism [26]. Agricola et al. demonstrated that severe MR increased mortality regardless of renal function, emphasizing the additive risk attributable to valvular dysfunction itself [27]. Focusing specifically on AFMR, this study extends these observations by showing that CKD is both a comorbidity and an independent, strong prognostic determinant in this distinct phenotype, characterized by atrial remodeling with preserved left ventricular function. Beyond prognostic insights, this study highlights the clinical relevance of coexisting AFMR and CKD in patients with AFMR. The consistent association between worsening CKD stage and adverse outcomes across different MR severities suggests that renal dysfunction is not merely a comorbidity, but an integral component of the pathophysiology linking atrial remodeling, MR, and HF progression. Recognizing this cardiorenal interplay may enable more refined risk stratification and support the need for future studies to evaluate whether integrated cardiorenal management or timely valve interventions can alter the natural history of AFMR in this high-risk population.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective observational design precludes causal inference, and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the coexistence of AFMR and CKD should not be interpreted as proof of bidirectional cardiorenal syndrome. Although key clinical covariates were adjusted for, residual bias due to factors such as medication use, volume management intensity, and socioeconomic factors may persist. Second, the cohort was restricted to Japanese patients, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic populations with different epidemiology of CKD and AF. Third, CKD was defined solely on estimated glomerular filtration rate, without quantitative assessment of proteinuria, which may have led to misclassification of CKD severity and underestimation of renal risk. Fourth, although MR severity was assessed using standardized echocardiographic methods, longitudinal changes in MR or CKD status during follow-up were not systematically captured. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate the prognostic significance of CKD stage in patients with AFMR. In addition, randomized clinical trials may be needed to determine whether interventions targeting CKD progression, integrated cardiorenal management, or mitral valve disease can improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Among conservatively managed patients with AFMR, CKD progression is independently associated with poorer prognosis. These findings support early identification and coordinated management of patients with coexisting CKD and AFMR. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate the prognostic impact of CKD stage, while randomized clinical trials may be needed to evaluate whether interventions targeting CKD progression or valvular disease can improve clinical outcomes.
