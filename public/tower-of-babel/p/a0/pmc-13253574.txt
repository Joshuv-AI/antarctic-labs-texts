Occupancy and activity patterns of a newly white-nose syndrome infected species in Texas, the Tricolored Bat (Perimyotis subflavus)
Authors: Sarah Rebecah Fritts, Madison Paige Gover, Maria Alessandra Ramirez, Donal I Solick, Michael R J Forstner, Samantha Leivers, Madison Taylor Nadler, Patrick Zollner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Perimyotis subflavus was once a common and widely distributed species of bat in the United States before white-nose syndrome (WNS) caused devastating population-level declines. The species has now been recommended for listing as federally endangered, although the ruling has not yet been finalized. Though WNS was discovered in the United States in 2006, Texas is currently at the leading edge of the expansion of the disease into the southwest. The first documentation of WNS occurred in Texas in 2020, when a die-off of Myotis velifer occurred; in 2024, WNS was detected on P. subflavus in Texas. To better manage populations of P. subflavus across Texas, it is imperative to fill knowledge gaps regarding seasonal distribution and activity of the species across the state, as well as habitat associations. Thus, our objectives were to assess the influence of weather and landcover on: (i) seasonal occupancy; (ii) nightly activity; (iii) hourly activity; (iv) the log-odds of a call being from P. subflavus if any bat was acoustically active; and (v) to examine diel activity patterns among seasons and Ecoregions. Activity varied by season and Ecoregion. Broadly, results suggest landcover—particularly forest (positive), wetland (positive), and urban (negative)—influenced occupancy probability, whereas weather more greatly influenced activity. Generally, activity was positively influenced by temperature, negatively influenced by wind speed and precipitation, and the effects of humidity varied seasonally. The log-odds of a call being P. subflavus given that any bat was acoustically active followed a similar pattern to overall activity. Results confirm widespread distribution of P. subflavus across Texas as well as provide baseline information on the influences of landscape characteristics and weather on activity for the 3-year period of 2020 to 2023, just prior to the first WNS detection for this species in this state.Keywords: activity, landcover, occupancy, Perimyotis subflavus, Texas, Tricolored Bat, weather, white-nose syndrome

Until recently, the Tricolored Bat (Perimyotis subflavus) was one of the most common and widely distributed bat species in the United States, with a distribution that extended from southern Canada into Central America and included much of the eastern and midwestern United States (Ammerman et al. 2012). However, dramatic population declines of this previously common species have been documented across its range since white-nose syndrome (WNS) was discovered in the United States in 2006 (Cheng et al. 2021). In fact, 90% to 100% declines in winter colony abundance of P. subflavus have been recorded at sites impacted by the disease (Cheng et al. 2021; Vanderwolf and McAlpine 2021; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Federal Register 2022). The US Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) species assessment concluded that range-wide winter abundance has declined by 52% and the number of extant winter colonies by 29% (USFWS 2021). The authors of the assessment then projected an approximately 90% decline in both abundance and colony counts by 2030, with a spatial decline of 65%. In response to population declines across much of range of the species, 10 US states have listed it as endangered and the USFWS has ­recommended endangered status (USFWS Federal Registrar 2022). Although the final federal listing has not been completed, P. subflavus is a priority species for conservation and management across its distribution.

Texas, a state in the southern United States, has the largest bat populations in the world, the greatest bat species richness of any state in the United States (Ammerman et al. 2012; Stepanian and Wainwright 2018), and is at the forefront of 2 major threats to bats, WNS and wind energy. PseudogymnoFascus destructans (Pd), the fungal pathogen that causes WNS, was first detected in Texas in 2017 on hibernating P. subflavus and 2 other species in the northern Panhandle of the state. The arrival of Pd to Texas prompted the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department (TPWD) to implement a WNS Action Plan in February 2017 (TPWD 2017). In March 2020, Myotis velifer had a mass mortality event in Central Texas that was attributed to infection by Pd, confirming the presence of WNS in the state for the first time. A few years later, in Winter 2023/2024, WNS was confirmed in 2 P. subflavus in Central Texas (TPWD 2024). Prior to P. subflavus in 2024, M. velifer had been the only species in Texas documented to exhibit WNS. State Pd surveillance has documented continued infiltration of Pd into roost sites such as culverts and caves.

In addition to WNS, P. subflavus fatalities have also been documented due to collisions with wind turbine blades at various facilities across its range. Documentation of those collisions prompted the USFWS to publish land-based wind energy voluntary avoidance guidance for the species (USFWS 2024). These guidelines include recommendations for curtailing wind turbines at specific temperatures and wind speeds, so a better understanding of the influences of weather on P. subflavus activity is critical. Moreover, burgeoning technology, such as smart curtailment systems (see Gottlieb et al. 2024), and a better understanding of specific weather conditions that influence activity of various bat species have become increasingly important for species-specific mitigation efforts. Texas leads the United States in wind energy production, with more than a quarter of US production coming from the state (US Energy Information Administration (EIA) 2022), further highlighting the importance of Texas for studying P. subflavus populations and activity.

Many previous studies of bat populations focus on Summer (Miller 2003; Hein et al. 2009; Ancillotto et al. 2017), whereas many studies during nonsummer months focus on hibernacula characteristics (Weller et al. 2018). While these time periods are important for bat populations due to reproductive timing and availability of winter roosts, baseline data on seasonal distributions (including Spring and Fall), along with associated habitat use and activity patterns, are warranted. For example, recent research suggests P. subflavus may be more active during winter months in Texas than previously documented (Stevens 2024). Estimates of winter activity are essential for the conservation of WNS-susceptible bat species because timing can be important for planning management activities, such as inoculating individual bats or treating winter roosts. Knowledge of bat activity patterns can also benefit wind energy producers by minimizing curtailment periods based on specific habitat-, seasonal-, or weather-related bat activity patterns.

To better manage populations of P. subflavus across Texas, it is imperative to document the seasonal distribution and activity of the species across the state as well as habitat associations. Thus, our objectives were to assess the influence of weather and landcover on: (i) seasonal occupancy; (ii) nightly activity patterns; (iii) hourly activity patterns; (iv) the log-odds that P. subflavus is acoustically active given any bat is active; and (v) diel activity patterns among seasons and Ecoregions in Texas. This project is timely because it represents 3 years of data prior to WNS being documented in P. subflavus in Texas and provides a baseline dataset documenting habitat use and activity patterns of P. subflavus prior to WNS population declines.

Methods

North American Bat Monitoring program

The North American Bat Monitoring (NABat) program is a multiagency, multinational effort to conduct long-term standardized bat monitoring (Loeb et al. 2015). This program is a direct response to the threat of WNS and aims to standardize bat research methods for effective conservation and management of at-risk bat species across North America. This protocol recommends 4 approaches for monitoring bats: stationary acoustic surveys, mobile acoustic surveys along transects, maternity colony counts, and winter hibernaculum surveys; however, we only used stationary acoustic surveys. The NABat sampling framework divides North America into 10 × 10 km grid cell sampling units with 4 5 × 5 km quadrants in each grid cell. There are 133,307 grid cells in the continental United States, and Texas has 6,563 full grid cells and 655 partial cells. NABat uses a generalized random-tessellation stratified (GRTS) survey design algorithm to spatially balance and randomly select grid cells for individual projects. Grid cells are ranked in order of importance and need for monitoring, where a grid cell ID of 1 equals the highest priority grid cell. The 2017 WNS Action Plan (TPWD 2017) required implementing NABat protocols for acoustic monitoring of Texas bats. We selected grid cells by starting at the highest priority NABat grid cell in Texas and identifying a willing landowner. If there was no willing landowner in that grid cell, we selected the next highest priority grid cell and continued in this manner until all grid cells were selected. All final grid cells used in the study are in the highest 200 priority NABat grid cells for Texas.

Field methods

We recorded bat echolocation calls at 49 NABat grid cells across Texas (Fig. 1). Within each survey grid cell, we deployed 2 Wildlife Acoustics Song Meter 4 BAT-FS passive acoustic monitors continuously from August 2020 to March 2023 (Year 1 = Fall 2020 to Summer 2021; Year 2 = Fall 2021 to Summer 2022; and Year 3 = Fall 2022 to Summer 2023). One monitor was placed in each of 2 of 5 × 5 km quadrants of a grid cell and at least 5 km apart (Loeb et al. 2015). We placed microphones ∼3.5 m above the ground in areas of perceived high bat activity, such as flyways or feeding areas; however, we were somewhat restricted regarding site selection on private property. In general, we followed the recommendations from Loeb et al. (2015) for the location of these stationary monitoring sites and deployment and effectiveness of microphones (e.g., to consider clutter and microphone height and orientation). We used a 2-s trigger window and a maximum file length of 15 s. Monitors remained in the field throughout the entire study period (September 2020 to July 2023) and recorded nightly 30 min before sunset to 30 min after sunrise. Solar panels and 12 V batteries were connected to the monitors to allow for continuous deployment with no battery changes. However, detectors sometimes would briefly stop recording due to low solar power, being knocked over by wind or animals, or rodents chewing on wires. Additionally, private landowners occasionally did not promptly switch SD cards, resulting in data loss.

According to NABat stationary acoustic monitoring protocols, data should be collected for a minimum of 4 nights at least once a year. We selected a 4-night period in each season (Fall, Winter, Spring, and Summer) for the 3 years (i.e., 12 seasons total). We selected the 4 nights in each season by determining the maximum spatial coverage across Texas of detectors actively recording to conduct these analyses. We used P. subflavus phenology to define seasons with Summer representing the pre-volancy period (March 16 to June 15) and then defined the other 3 seasons accordingly: Fall = June 16 to Sept. 15; Winter = Sept 16 to Dec. 15; and Spring = Dec. 16 to Mar 15. Because detectors were not always recording at each site during specific time periods for myriad reasons mentioned above, we selected each 4-night period by cross-referencing the time period when most detectors were active. If data from a detector were missing for that period, we analyzed data from the nearest period with 4 consecutive days of data if data were available during that season.

Acoustic analysis

We attributed, scrubbed, and automatically classified .wav files in SonoBat 4 (SonoBat, Inc., Arcata, California) to assign metadata, remove noise and poor-quality files, and classify files containing bat echolocation calls to species. Automated identification software can be highly inaccurate for some species, so it is recommended that a bat echolocation specialist trained in identification manually vet bat calls due to margins of error in auto identification software (Solick et al. 2024). We did not manually vet every call; instead, we manually vetted echolocation calls using a voucher approach, whereby we examined files classified as P. subflavus from each detector for each of the selected nights. Once we detected the species at a particular site on the specified night, the vetting process then proceeded to assessing the following night for detection of the species at that site. This procedure resulted in a matrix of P. subflavus either detected or not detected at each detector per night. We then pooled detections of P. subflavus over the 2 detectors for each grid cell for analyses, as recommended in Loeb et al. (2015).

To estimate activity, we summed the number of .wav files that were automatically classified as P. subflavus in SonoBat for each 4-night period. From the SonoBat output, we used the “1st” column, which identifies the primary or most likely species classification. The manually vetted data were cross-referenced with the auto-classified files, and we removed identifications of P. subflavus if the species was not manually vetted at a particular site, thereby omitting potential false positives. This approach is conservative but also enables the highest confidence for the species-specific results.

Covariates

We used landcover data at 3 spatial scales and weather data as covariates. We used the National Land Cover Database (Dewitz 2019) and ArcGIS Pro (Esri, Redlands, California, United States) to create a buffer around each detector at spatial scales 10, 25, and 50 km. We divided landcover data into 8 landcover classes: Forest (FOR); Grassland (GRA); Shrub (SHR); Urban (URB); Barren (BAR); Crop (CRO); Water (WAT); and Wetland (WET), and averaged landcover values across each of the 2 detectors per grid cell. All data are represented in hectares (ha) within the corresponding buffer size, which we used as covariates in subsequent analyses. For example, FOR10 is forest landcover at the 10 km scale, and GRA50 is grassland landcover at the 50 km scale.

We compiled weather data at 2 scales. First, we were interested in nightly weather averages to assess occupancy probability of P. subflavus across Texas and acquired 24 h weather data collected by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (National Centers for Environmental Information 2023) to use in occupancy models. The activity analyses, however, required hourly weather data, so we manually imported data from Weather Underground (Weather Underground, Inc. 1995) and pooled to 1 h increments if the increments were not hourly. We then averaged across each hour for the regression analyses. For both scales, we selected weather stations most proximal to and within the same Ecoregion of each grid cell (∼3.31 to 99.76 km; mean = 41.38 km, SD = 23.34 km), and we used the nearest weather station with available data.

Each grid cell occupied 1 of 8 TPWD wildlife management districts which correspond to major Texas Ecoregions (TPWD 2008). Ecoregions varied in weather (see Supplementary Data SD1 to SD3) and landcover (Supplementary Data SD4). Texas wildlife management districts (delineated as, “Ecoregions”) are: (i) Trans-Pecos; (ii) High Plains/Panhandle; (iii) Cross Timbers; (iv) Hill Country; (v) Post Oak Savannah; (vi) Pineywoods; (vii) Oak Prairie; and (viii) South Texas Plains.

Statistical analyses

We conducted all statistical analysis in Program R version 4.3.3 and R Studio 2023.12.1, including all packages mentioned (R Core Team 2021). We scaled covariates using a z-score transformation and assessed collinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF) before use in analyses. Covariates with VIF >5 were omitted from analyses (Zuur et al. 2009). We considered a covariate as a predictor of the response if the 95% confidence interval of β did not overlap “0.”

For each 4-night season within each year, we conducted 5 main analyses for which we first provide this overview and then specifics for each analysis. The first analysis was single-season, single-species occupancy modeling to fill knowledge gaps on seasonal occupancy of P. subflavus across the state. For this analysis, we attempted dynamic occupancy models; however, due to the sparsity of data during some seasons, models would not converge, so we conducted single-season occupancy analyses. We did not pool data over seasons for all years due to potential differences in detection and occupancy probabilities and in dates selected for analysis. Instead, we separated by each of the 12 primary periods, 1 for each season for each year, equaling 3 replicates of each of 4 seasons. We used this method to maximize simplicity of the models and minimize convergence issues. Second, we assessed the influence of weather, landcover, and distributional (latitude, longitude, and elevation) covariates on nightly activity at each grid cell during each of the 12 periods. We then pooled activity data across the 3 yr for each of the 4 seasons for the last 3 analyses. The third analysis examined the effects of weather on hourly activity rates, and the fourth analysis assessed the influence of weather on the log-odds of an acoustic call being P. subflavus if a bat of any species was detected. This latter analysis is a quasi-comparison of activity among species under varying weather conditions and could be of particular importance to management actions regarding wind energy, in which the USFWS provides voluntary recommendations for species-specific curtailment strategies based on weather conditions (USFWS 2024). Last, we ­compared diel activity patterns across the 4 seasons and 8 Ecoregions, as well as pairwise diel activity overlap among seasons and Ecoregions.

Occupancy

We assessed the influence of landcover and weather on seasonal occupancy probability of P. subflavus at each grid cell when naive occupancy = 20% to 80% for that primary sampling period in package “unmarked” (Fiske and Chandler 2011; Kellner et al. 2023). For the response variable, we used a matrix of detections/non-detections over the 4-d period and assessed each season separately. Site-level covariates included landcover at each of the 3 scales, latitude and longitude, as well as maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and temperature difference averaged over the 4-d period. Observation-level covariates, which were included for detection probability, included maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and temperature difference on the night of observation as well as the previous night to account for potential time-lags, both corrected for the night of monitoring. Temperature data for each 4-d period and Ecoregion are provided in Supplementary Data SD1 to SD3.

For occupancy analyses, we used a tiered approach to first find predictors of detection and then of occupancy (Fritts et al. 2016). We first evaluated 4 models to identify predictors of detection. The first and second models assessed the influence of each weather variable on detection probability by conducting a model with all weather parameters for the 24 h period of monitoring and a second model with weather parameters for the 24 h period before monitoring. The third model assessed the influence of effort using the number of detectors that were actively recording at each grid cell (1 or 2), and finally a fourth model examined the landcover types FOR10, BAR10, and URB10 on detection probability. We selected these 3 landcover variables to model because we expected the 10 km scale to be more influential on detection probability than broader scales. We used Akaike information criterion (AIC; Akaike 1973) to select which of these 4 detection models was the best fit for detection probability. Once the best detection model was determined, we compared the AIC to a model with only the significant covariates included (e.g., if the landcover detection model was the best fit but only FOR10 was significant, we compared the full landcover detection model to a detection model with only FOR10). The covariates from the detection model with the lowest AIC were used in subsequent analyses evaluating predictors of occupancy. We constructed separate models that represented each landcover scale (i.e., 10, 25, and 50 km) and other models for each landcover type at all scales (e.g., FOR10, FOR25, FOR50; Table 1). We also constructed a model with only the predictors of detection and latitude and longitude as predictors of occupancy (Table 1). The number of variables in the landcover models differed due to VIF.

Nightly activity using mixed effects linear regression

The goal of the second analysis was to assess the influence of landcover data at multiple scales (10, 25, and 50 km), weather (temperature [° C], humidity [%], wind speed [km/h], precipitation [mm], barometric pressure [hPa], and location [latitude, longitude, and elevation]) on the nightly activity of P. subflavus. To conduct this analysis, we used calls that were identified via automated-classification after cross-referencing with the manually vetted calls. We divided activity and averaged the weather variables by day. We then used linear mixed effects models with the count of bat calls as the response variable and a suite of either landcover, weather, or distribution data as independent and continuous fixed variables, and grid cell as a random effect (with 4 inputs for the 4 nights each season). We did not pool over season because we used 24 h specific weather data. We used the package “glmmTMB” (Bolker 2019) and compared 7 models (Table 2) using Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to identify the most parsimonious model and minimize overfitting of models, as BIC is available for these models and typically considered more conservative than AIC.

Hourly activity using mixed effects linear regression

We examined the effects of hourly weather on P. subflavus activity, given that a P. subflavus was active, using mixed effects linear regression models with a negative binomial response variable. For this analysis, we binned weather data and the count of bat calls by grid cell, date, and hour as the negative binomial response. We only used hours that had ≥1 P. subflavus call. We then conducted a global model with temperature (° C), humidity (%), wind speed (km/h), precipitation (mm), and barometric pressure (hPa) as independent and continuous fixed covariates. Because we were interested in only 5 variables and could conduct a global model without convergence issues, we used the “step” function in R to perform stepwise regression. We used the model with the lowest AIC resulting from the stepwise regression as the best model.

Hourly activity given 1 bat was detected

To understand differences in various bat species’ behavior, we conducted a mixed effects linear regression with a binomial response variable. We again pooled bat activity by grid cell, date, and hour. We used “1” if P. subflavus was detected during that hour and “0” if P. subflavus was not detected but at least 1 other bat species was detected. We then conducted a global model with temperature (° C), humidity (%), wind speed (km/h), precipitation (mm), and barometric pressure (hPa) as independent and continuous fixed covariates and grid cell as a random effect. We used the “drop1” function in R to conduct an analysis of deviance test if the least influential variable was dropped until the AIC value was lower than the null model.

Activity overlap

We fit activity patterns to a nonparametric kernel density distribution and compared them among seasons and among Ecoregions with the “overlap” package in R (Meredith et al. 2014) following the methods of Ridout and Linkie (2009). We used 2 coefficients of overlap estimates in the “overlapEst” function: Δ1 for samples with 20 to 75 observations and Δ4 for samples with >75 observations. Overlap values range from 0 (no overlap, complete separation in activity) to 1 (complete overlap, no separation in activity). We conducted 999 bootstrap samples with a 1.5 bandwidth adjustment as recommended by Rowcliffe et al. (2014). We used the “basic0” interval adjustment for calculating 95% ­confidence intervals. To supplement the overlap coefficient, we conducted pairwise Watson’s U2 tests to statistically test if the time of activity differed between the 2 variables (H0 = temporal overlap is the same). We also compared pairs of activity curves with the “compareCkern” function in the “activity” package (Rowcliffe 2023) to test if the activity curves were contained in the same distribution.

Results

We manually identified P. subflavus at 38 of the 49 grid cells over 3 yr of 4 seasons (Fig. 1; see Supplementary Data SD5). We manually and automatically identified 9,090 calls as P. subflavus (Fig. 1; see Supplementary Data SD5). We recorded 1,970 in Year 1 (1.866 calls/detector night; Fall 2020 to Summer 2021), 1,290 calls in Year 2 (1.446 calls/detector night; Fall 2021 to Summer 2022), and 5,830 calls in Year 3 (6.908 calls/detector night; Fall 2022 to Summer 2023). Of these, 967 calls were identified during Fall periods (1.374 calls/detector night), 3,306 in Spring periods (4.260 calls/detector night), 4,775 in Summer periods (6.523 calls/detector night), and 42 in winter periods (0.072 calls/detector night). There were 27 calls that were manually identified during vetting as a different species but automatically classified as P. subflavus that we omitted from analyses.

Occupancy

The number of grid cells included in analyses, the number of calls, and naïve occupancy varied by season and year (see Supplementary Data SD5) as did the influence of predictor variables (Fig. 2). Model selection criteria and beta values, SE, and upper/lower 95% confidence limits are provided in Supplementary Data SD6 and SD7. Forest landcover at the 10 and 50 km scales were the most commonly included covariates and positively influenced occupancy during at least 1 year of each season (Fig. 2); however, forest landcover covariates were not included in all of the top-ranked models. Urban landcover at the 50 km scale was the only covariate that negatively influenced occupancy, and based on models, only in Fall 2020 (Fig. 2).

Falls

In Fall 2020, the AIC best model included a negative influence of URB50 (β = −9.820) on occupancy probability (Fig. 2), and in Fall 2021, the AIC best model included a positive relationship between FOR10 (β = 1.640) and occupancy probability (Fig. 2). In Fall 2022, the AIC best model included effort as a predictor of detection probability, WAT10 as a negative predictor (β = −4.696) of occupancy, and WAT50 as a positive predictor (β = 3.791) of occupancy (Fig. 2).

Winters

In 2020, the number of grids that detected P. subflavus was too low for occupancy analysis. In 2021, the AIC best model included effort as a predictor of detection probability and WET10 (β = −0.152) and WET50 (β = 1.417) as covariates on occupancy probability; however, neither was significant (Fig. 2). In 2022, FOR10 was a predictor of detection probability and used in subsequent analyses. The AIC best model included FOR50 (β = 2.460), URB50 (β = −2.682), and BAR50 (β = 4.710) as covariates on occupancy probability, and FOR50 was a significant, positive predictor (Fig. 2).

Springs

In 2021 (Year 1), no covariate was a predictor of detection probability. The AIC best model included WET10 (β = −1.550) and WET50 (β = 2.292) as covariates on occupancy probability; however, neither was significant. In 2022, The AIC best model included effort as a predictor of detection probability and WET10 (β = −1.550) and WET50 (β = 2.292) as covariates on occupancy probability; however, neither was significant (Fig. 2). In 2023, FOR10 and effort were predictors of detection probability and included in subsequent models. The AIC best model included latitude (β = −1.189) and longitude (β = 0.634, neither of which was significant.

Summers

In 2021, the AIC best model included URB10 as a negative predictor of detection probability and a positive relationship between FOR10 (β = 0.996) and occupancy probability (Fig. 2). In 2022, the AIC best model included URB10 as a negative predictor of detection probability and FOR10 (β = 5.963) and FOR50 (β = −3.496) as covariates on occupancy probability; however, neither was significant (Fig. 2). In 2023, the AIC best model included FOR10 (β = 4.279) and FOR50 (β = −2.616) as covariates of occupancy probability with FOR10 as a predictor of detection probability (Fig. 2).

Grid-level nightly activity

Sample sizes were adequate to assess grid-level nightly activity in Fall 2020, Summer 2021, Spring 2022, Spring 2023, and Summer 2023 (see Supplementary Data SD8 and SD9; Fig. 3). In Fall 2020, no independent variables were predictors of activity. In both Spring 2022 and 2023, the weather model was the BIC best model. In both years, temperature positively influenced activity (β2022 = 1.286; β2023 = 0.623); however, humidity had a positive influence in 2022 (β = 0.800), but negative in 2023 (β = −0.707). Additionally, in 2023, wind speed (β = −1.117) negatively influenced Spring activity (Fig. 3). In Summers 2021 and 2023, no covariates were predictors of activity.

Weather and hourly activity

Results of backwards selection and predictors of hourly activity are given in Supplementary Data SD10 and Fig. 3. During Fall seasons, humidity (β = 0.456) had a positive influence on hourly activity. The best model also included temperature, but it was not a predictor of activity. Sample size during winters was too low to conduct this analysis. During Spring seasons, the top-ranked model included humidity, wind speed, barometric pressure, and temperature (β = 0.274), which was the only predictor (Fig. 3). During Summer seasons, the top-ranked model included 4 predictors of activity: humidity (β = 0.148) as a positive predictor and wind speed (β = −1.109), barometric pressure (β = −0.699), and precipitation (β = −0.137) as negative predictors.

Activity by P. subflavus given that any bat is active

Results from backwards selection and predictors of log-odds activity given that any bat was acoustically active are in Supplementary Data SD11 and visualized in Fig. 3. During the Fall seasons, temperature, humidity, wind speed, and barometric pressure influenced the log-odds of a P. subflavus being active if another bat was active (Fig. 3). Temperature (β = 0.828) and humidity (β = 0.921) positively influenced the log-odds, whereas wind speed (β = −0.985) and barometric pressure (β = −1.318) negatively affected the log-odds. The sample size during winters was too low for this analysis. During the Spring seasons, both humidity (β = −0.572) and wind speed (β = −0.122) negatively affected the log-odds of a P. subflavus being active given another bat was active (Fig. 3). During summers, wind speed (β = −1.750), precipitation (β = −0.143), and barometric pressure (β = −1.203) had negative influences, whereas temperature (β = 0.590) and humidity (β = 1.009) positively influenced the log-odds of P. subflavus being active given another bat was active (Fig. 3).

Diel activity overlap by season and Ecoregion

Diel activity results are visualized in Figs. 4 and 5. Perimyotis subflavus had the greatest coefficient of overlap between Fall and Spring (Δ4 = 0.76) and least coefficient of overlap between Summer and winter (Δ1 = 0.39; Fig. 4; see Supplementary Data SD12). The coefficients of overlap for the remaining pairwise season comparisons ranged from 0.57 to 0.66. Despite some moderately high coefficients of overlap, all pairwise comparisons had different temporal overlap (U2 P-values < 0.001) and came from different distributions (P-values < 0.001; see Supplementary Data SD13). Perimyotis subflavus activity was earliest in the winter with less activity later in the night, and activity was more consistent across the night during the Summer. The earliest P. subflavus call was recorded at 19:22 in Fall, 18:45 in winter, 19:20 in Spring, and 20:05 in Summer, whereas the most calls were recorded during the 20:00 h in Fall and Spring, 19:00 h in winter, and 21:00 h in Summer. Coefficients of overlap varied greatly among Ecoregions with the lowest overlap between Ecoregions 1 (Trans-Pecos) and 8 (South Texas Plains; Δ1 = 0.35) and the greatest overlap between Ecoregions 3 (Cross Timbers) and 8 (South Texas Plains; Δ4 = 0.92) with 24 pairwise comparisons total (see Supplementary Data SD13; Fig. 5). Five of the 24 Ecoregion pairwise comparisons did not differ from one another in terms of temporal overlap and distribution; other pairwise comparisons differed (see Supplementary Data SD13).

Discussion

Our results indicate widespread distribution of P. subflavus across Texas as well as provide baseline information on the influences of landscape characteristics and weather on activity for 3 yr before the first individuals were positively detected with WNS in the state. Although P. subflavus had been previously documented in various Ecoregions across Texas (Ammerman et al. 2012), this study acoustically documented the species in several new areas of Texas. However, we recommend verifying with either field capture or genetic identification to confirm these county records. As an outcome, there were no areas of Texas that can be easily excluded from the probable distribution of P. subflavus based on this assessment. Understanding the potential for false negatives in determining distributional occurrence in P. subflavus is not well documented currently, but Hauer et al. (2023) conclude that between 22 (Spring) and 105 (Summer) nights are likely required to prevent false negatives of P. subflavus. Whereas site-based presence–absence was not part of the design optimization in this study, it does indicate that sites without positive detections should not be considered as unoccupied based only on the data sampled herein.

Concurrent with recent documentation, results herein suggest that although P. subflavus had historically been considered as relying on forests, the species may be more of a habitat generalist (Schratz et al. 2017; Andersen et al. 2022). To emphasize this, studies have reported landcover types influential in P. subflavus presence, occupancy, and habitat use, including a preference for salt marshes and upland forests (Andersen et al. 2022), freshwater or brackish wetlands with no vegetation (Moore et al. 2018), mesic and hydric soil communities (Bellows and Mitchell 2017), meadows and beetle-killed trees (Jorge et al. 2021), preference against rangelands/wetlands and sand (Perez 2023), and no landcover type preferences in some seasons (Gorton and Hutchinson 2019; Jorge et al. 2021; Shute et al. 2021). Moreover, habitat associations of bats, including P. subflavus may differ by season (Andersen et al. 2022). Similar to our results that forests are predictors of occupancy at some times during the year, the importance of forest, urban, and water landcover on occupancy probability has also been reported in previous studies (Starbuck et al. 2015; Schimpp 2017; Schratz et al. 2017; Moore et al. 2018; Dwyer 2021; Burrell 2022; Perez 2023). Thus, it is important for managers to recognize that there is no single landcover type that affects occupancy year-round in Texas. This suggestion is not to negate that forests may be important for the species, particularly as the loss of forest landcover has been associated with decreases in P. subflavus populations (Farrow and Broders 2011); however, it may be important to consider management of a variety of landcover types for P. subflavus populations. Additionally, P. subflavus has been reported in the past as a clutter-adapted species but has also been reported to use open areas more frequently than densely vegetated areas (Carter et al., 2004; Menzel et al. 2005; Loeb and O’Keefe 2006).

Bat activity has long been documented to be related to weather conditions (O’Farrell and Bradley 1970; Geluso 2007; Frick et al. 2012), and results herein suggest that weather had greater influences on activity than landcover, although landcover had greater influences on occupancy. This difference could be due to scale; occupancy may more heavily indicate P. subflavus range across Texas, whereas the other analyses may focus more on activity within that range. Typically, bat activity increases with increasing temperatures and declines with increasing precipitation, wind speed, and barometric pressure (Johnson et al. 2011; Gorman et al. 2021), which agrees with the general trends suggested for P. subflavus specifically from results herein. Although specific variations in associations with weather conditions would be expected depending on location and season. Further, results suggest that P. subflavus may be more acoustically active than other species when it is warmer and more humid, but under conditions with less wind and lower barometric pressure. This result could be because we included any bat species detected in Texas as the comparison for P. subflavus activity (i.e., the log-odds of P. subflavus being active given at least 1 other bat was active) in this analysis, and some species occur in areas that may be cooler and drier. Weather can have a direct effect on bat activity and homeostasis or on prey availability, as insect availability typically increases with temperature (Hayes 1997; Gorman et al. 2021; Jorge et al. 2021). It is also worth noting that weather differences among years could prevent similar seasonal trends across the time window of our study.

Our results are concordant with the species-specific examination wherein factors such as wind speed and precipitation negatively influenced activity for P. subflavus when other bats were detected. Results also suggest seasonal and spatial differences in factor effects. For example, humidity had a net negative influence in Spring seasons but a positive influence in Summer and Fall seasons, which, for Texas, is a reflection of suitable conditions across species and logically follows the data when we examine the diel, or hourly, activity influences for P. subflavus alone. This is also represented in the examination of P. subflavus activity across the breadth of wildlife management districts, or Ecoregions, in Texas. Across the state, activity overlaps most strongly in Fall and Spring as activity changes going into winter or emergence are underway, but much less so when considering diel activity among Ecoregions.

We did not detect sufficient activity by P. subflavus during winters to enable modeling, but this does not mean that the species is inactive in winter; in fact, quite the contrary. The results herein further contribute to our understanding that P. subflavus may be more active during winter and early Spring than previously documented, which can have important implications for WNS management. Decades ago, P. subflavus was documented to be one of the last species to exit hibernacula in the Spring (Barbour and Davis 1969; LaVal and LaVal 1980) and was less active in winter hibernacula than other species (Davis 1964). More recently, culvert surveys in Texas detected large numbers of P. subflavus still roosting in their winter hibernacula in late March (Sandel et al. 2001), but hibernation ecology may differ today from what was observed 30 to 50 yr ago. Understanding winter activity is critical for the implementation of WNS treatment options that are applied to either an individual bat or the hibernacula seeking to decrease disease-related mortality (Cornelison et al. 2014; Palmer et al. 2018; Hoyt et al. 2019; Rocke et al. 2019), and the treatments should be timed to minimize disturbance.

This species hibernates in various structures in the southeastern United States, including culverts in Texas, which are candidate roost sites for WNS treatment (Meierhofer et al. 2019; Newman et al. 2021). Moreover, because Pd depletes energy reserves by disrupting torpor cycles (Reeder et al. 2012; Warnecke et al. 2012; Verant et al. 2014), bats that intermittently arouse from Pd-infected hibernacula to forage may benefit by supplementing their energy reserves. Many bat species in temperate areas, particularly at lower latitudes, are documented to forage at least periodically during winter (Whitaker Jr and Rissler 1993; Viele et al. 2002; Boyles et al. 2006), and bats remain active throughout winter regardless of Pd prevalence, with levels of activity positively related to ambient temperature at dusk in other southeastern US states (Johnson et al. 2012; Bernard and McCracken 2017). Similarly, P. subflavus activity corresponds to temperature in east Texas and Louisiana, with bouts of inactivity recorded only at the lowest sunset temperatures (e.g., <5 °C; Andersen et al. 2022; Andersen et al. 2024; Stevens 2024) and down to −4 °C in North Carolina (Parker et al. 2020). Although the sample size was too low for winter activity analyses in our sample set, P. subflavus was active during all 3 of the 4-day samples in December of the Winter season in our study; and because we defined season based on phenology, the Spring seasons represented March, which can still be relatively cold in much of Texas. During the Spring seasons, activity was related to temperature, but we recorded a single bat pass at −0.6 °C and another just below 0 °C, and 400 passes at temperatures <10 °C during Spring seasons.

Since WNS has been detected in Texas P. subflavus, population declines may follow similar trends to other areas of the United States (Ford et al. 2011; Pettit and O’Keefe 2017; Huebschman 2019; Cheng et al. 2021). Although we did not examine roost or hibernacula sites, the availability of cold hibernacula and non-subterranean roosts may be important for conservation of P. subflavus populations in warmer climates, including Texas. Cold hibernacula may decrease hibernation energy requirements and limit Pd growth, whereas non-subterranean roosts may decrease fungal growth with thermal instability (Newman et al. 2021; Loeb and Winters 2022). Researchers once thought that the apparent slower spread of WNS through bat populations in the southern US states indicated greater resilience to the pathogen due to shorter winters. However, relatively warmer hibernacula temperatures in southern states may actually increase growth rates of Pd and increase the severity of WNS infections (Langwig et al. 2016). As WNS continues to spread among Texas bats in the coming years, active conservation and management of this taxon will be essential for ensuring population stability and recovery. Additionally, previous models of WNS impact in Texas predicted P. subflavus in northern Texas would be more susceptible than populations in southern Texas (Meierhofer et al. 2021), but so far, the WNS-related fatalities are located in the central part of the state (TPWD 2024). Now that our results confirm that the range of the species likely encompasses more of the state than previously documented, research to determine why WNS mortality events have been geographically concentrated in Central Texas to date is crucially needed. Considering the variable activity by region and by season, more research is warranted.

As with any study of a cryptic species, a few limitations of this study should be recognized. First, identifying bat species by acoustic monitoring is not 100% accurate through manual identification nor automatic classifiers. Our method of using only calls from species manually identified by any specific detector minimizes false range expansions and false positive detections, but the risk of potential inaccurate activity metrics still exists. Specifically, the distribution of P. subflavus is sympatric with Parastrellus hesperus in portions of Texas (Ammerman et al. 2012), which has similar echolocation characteristics that can create difficulties with vetting (Riedle and Matlack 2013). We identified P. hesperus in 10 grid cells but also identified P. subflavus in those grid cells. The broad scale at which this project occurred was considered when developing our species identification methodology. We suggest manually vetting a greater number of calls instead of voucher calls per detector per night for more localized, site-specific management decisions. Second, we did not account for every condition that can influence detection probability, nor did we correct for the possibility that not all grid cells were independent throughout the year. For example, home range and activity levels of many bat species can depend on season, particularly for females while pregnant and lactating (Henry et al. 2002; Ford et al. 2011; Nocera et al. 2019; Thames 2020; Cable and Willcox 2024), which could lead to uneven detection probabilities. Additionally, no stationary acoustic studies using stationary microphones can separate activity with abundance or the count of individuals. Overall activity did not decline across the years of study; in fact, activity was estimated to increase. The increase in activity during Year 3 of the study was largely due to increased activity in only a couple of grid cells; thus, we suggest the appropriate caution needed in assuming population trends with acoustic data at this scale.
