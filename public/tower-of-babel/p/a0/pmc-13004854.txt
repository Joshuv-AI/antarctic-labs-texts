Anti-PD-1 antibody combined with P-GEMOX chemotherapy versus P-GEMOX chemotherapy with or without autologous stem-cell transplantation for previously untreated advanced natural killer/T cell lymphoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qihua Zou, Yi Cao, Liang Wang, Wuping Li, Qingsong Yin, Yudan Wu, Hongmei Jing, Zhigang Peng, Xiuhua Sun, Jun Rao, Ying Chen, Hongyu Zhang, Dongfeng Zeng, Bingzong Li, Ying Zhao, Xudong Zhang, Yuchen Zhang, Yan Gao, Bing Bai, Yi Xia, Yu Fang, Zouxiang Chen, Man Nie, Xiaojie Fang, Shenrui Bai, Huiqiang Huang, Qingqing Cai, Jun Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated the effectiveness of adding anti-PD-1 antibody to P-GEMOX regimen in previously untreated advanced-stage natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTCL) compared to P-GEMOX alone or with autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT). 418 patients (135 and 283 in the immunochemotherapy and chemotherapy groups, respectively) were analyzed from 15 Chinese centers (2014–2023). The median follow-up was 40.7 months. The immunochemotherapy group had a higher objective response rate (ORR) (89.6% versus 77.0%), complete response (CR) rate (77.0% versus 50.5%), 3-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate (64.1% versus 40.7%), and 3-year overall survival (OS) rate (79.5% versus 60.8%) compared to the chemotherapy group. Grade 3–4 neutropenia was more common in the immunochemotherapy group (40.0% versus 20.8% in the chemotherapy group, p < 0.001). Grade 3–4 hematologic toxicities were prevalent during ASCT, whereas anti-PD-1 maintenance was well tolerated. Grade ≥3 non-hematologic adverse events during anti-PD-1 maintenance were rare. In propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis of patients achieving CR after induction (n = 41 per group), the 3-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate was 72.6% for immunochemotherapy plus anti-PD-1 maintenance versus 50.9% for chemotherapy plus ASCT (p = 0.032), and the 3-year OS rate was 91.5% versus 72.9% (p = 0.029). First-line anti-PD-1 antibody plus P-GEMOX followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance shows better response and survival over P-GEMOX chemotherapy alone and P-GEMOX with ASCT, while maintaining acceptable toxicity.Subject terms: Immunotherapy, Medical research

Introduction

Natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTCL) is a highly heterogeneous and aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, with a higher prevalence in East Asian and Latin American populations [1–3]. Over the past decade, asparaginase- or pegaspargase-based chemotherapy regimens have improved survival outcomes in patients with advanced-stage NKTCL [4, 5]. Nonetheless, outcomes remain suboptimal. More than 50% of patients with advanced-stage NKTCL relapse or become refractory after treatment, with the median overall survival (OS) of approximately 24 months [6].

The frequent overexpression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) in NKTCL provides a compelling rationale for immunotherapy [7]. In recent years, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies have shown promising efficacy as monotherapy in relapsed/refractory NKTCL [8–13]. Combination treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy has also attracted increasing interest in the frontline setting, supported by the superior efficacy of such combinations in other malignancies [14–17]. In our previous retrospective study in nine patients, combining anti-PD-1 antibody with gemcitabine, oxaliplatin, and pegaspargase (P-GEMOX) chemotherapy yielded objective responses in eight patients [18]. The SPIRIT trial of sintilimab plus P-GEMOX followed by sintilimab maintenance in 34 treatment-naive patients with advanced-stage NKTCL demonstrated a complete response (CR) rate of 85%, a 2-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate of 64%, and a 3-year OS rate of 76%, with manageable treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) [19].

Autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) has been considered as a consolidative treatment option for patients following induction therapy [20–23]. Some studies have indicated that ASCT provide survival benefit in patients achieving CR before transplantation; however, these findings are limited by the lack of prospective randomized trials [24]. Other studies have shown comparable survival outcomes between patients who received ASCT and those who did not [25, 26]. With the development of frontline immunochemotherapy, the role of ASCT in the management of advanced-stage NKTCL has become increasingly uncertain, necessitating a reevaluation of ASCT in the current therapeutic landscape.

We conducted a multicenter retrospective study to compare the long-term survival outcomes of frontline anti-PD-1 antibody plus P-GEMOX regimen versus P-GEMOX regimen alone, either with or without ASCT consolidation in patients with newly diagnosed advanced-stage NKTCL. This study aimed to determine whether the addition of anti-PD-1 antibody to P-GEMOX chemotherapy improves survival in the first-line setting and to inform future treatment strategies in the modern asparaginase era.

Methods

Study design and patient population

In this retrospective study, we screened all adult patients (≥18 years) with newly diagnosed Ann Arbor stage III–IV NKTCL who received P-GEMOX regimen, with or without anti-PD-1 induction (referred to as immunochemotherapy and chemotherapy groups, respectively, below), at 15 Chinese centers from 2014 to 2023. The diagnosis of NKTCL was established histopathologically according to the World Health Organization classification of lymphoid neoplasms. Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), Ann Arbor stage, extranodal involvement, Prognostic Index for Natural Killer T cell lymphoma (PINK) [27], and PINKE (incorporating circulating Epstein-Barr virus [EBV] DNA status) model, were collected from medical records. This study was approved by the institutional review board (B2023-409) of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center and conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived as the study involved retrospective analysis of anonymized data.

Treatment

The P-GEMOX regimen consisted of pegaspargase (2000 IU/m² intramuscularly on day 1), gemcitabine (1000 mg/m² intravenously on days 1 and 8), and oxaliplatin (130 mg/m² intravenously on day 1), repeated every three weeks. Patients received anti-PD-1 antibodies according to standard or approved doses and schedules commonly used in clinical practice: sintilimab, pembrolizumab, tislelizumab, or camrelizumab 200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks; toripalimab 240 mg intravenously every 3 weeks; nivolumab 3 mg/kg intravenously every 2 weeks. Treatment decisions were made according to therapy availability and patient preference. Patients in the chemotherapy group who achieved CR after induction therapy did not receive further maintenance treatment. Patients with partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD) after induction therapy received salvage treatment. Patients in the immunochemotherapy group who achieved PR or CR after induction therapy continued treatment with the anti-PD-1 antibody as maintenance therapy. Patients with SD or PD after induction therapy received salvage treatments. ASCT was performed in some but not all patients who achieved CR after induction therapy in both groups.

Outcomes

Treatment response was evaluated after every two cycles of induction therapy, post-ASCT and every three months during maintenance therapy, and categorized into CR, PR, SD, and PD based on positron emission tomography/computed tomography, contrast-enhanced computed tomography or contrast enhanced magnetic resonance imaging, according to the 2014 Lugano criteria [28]. PFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to either disease progression or death from any cause. Modified PFS was defined as time from diagnosis to PR or SD at end of induction, disease progression, or death, whichever occurred first [29]. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from achieving CR to either disease relapse or death from any cause. TRAEs were graded according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, depending on distribution. Survival curves for PFS, OS, modified PFS, and DFS were estimated via the Kaplan-Meier method. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to identify prognostic factors and estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A 1:1 matching ratio was used in propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis with a caliper of 0.2. Matching variables included age, sex, Ann Arbor stage, ECOG PS, primary tumor location, lymph node involvement, circulating EBV DNA status, presence of B symptoms. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, Ann Arbor stage, ECOG PS, primary tumor location, lymph node involvement, circulating EBV DNA status, and PINK risk model. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The final analysis included total of 418 patients: 135 and 283 in the immunochemotherapy and chemotherapy groups, respectively. ASCT was conducted in 8 and 53 of the patients in immunochemotherapy and chemotherapy groups, respectively. The median age of the overall cohort was 47 years (interquartile range [IQR], 36–57). The Ann Arbor stage was III in 47 (11.2%) and IV in 371 (88.8%) patients. Percentage of the patients with high-risk was 70.8% based on the PINK risk model and 56.5% based on the PINKE risk model. Treatment response

Patients received a median of six cycles (range, 2–6) of induction therapy and a median of eight cycles of anti-PD-1 maintenance therapy (range, 2–34). At the end of induction therapy, the objective response rate (ORR) was 89.6% (121/135; 95% CI 83.2–94.2) in the immunochemotherapy group versus 77.0% (218/283; 95% CI 71.7–81.8) in the chemotherapy group (p = 0.002) (Table 2). The CR rate was 71.9% (97/135; 95% CI 63.5–79.3) in the immunochemotherapy group versus 50.5% (143/283; 95% CI 44.6–56.5) in the chemotherapy group (p < 0.001). Seven patients in the immunochemotherapy group who with PR at the end of induction therapy achieved CR during maintenance, resulting in a final CR rate of 77.0% (104/135; 95% CI 69.0–83.8).

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up was 40.7 months (range, 1.4–117.9). PFS at 3 years was 64.1% (95% CI 56.4–73.0) for immunochemotherapy group and 40.7% (95% CI 35.3–46.9) for chemotherapy group (HR 0.47; 95% CI 0.34–0.63; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). OS at 3 years was 79.5% (95% CI 72.7–86.9) for immunochemotherapy group and 60.8% (95% CI 55.3–66.9) for chemotherapy group (HR 0.44; 95% CI 0.29–0.65; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Modified PFS at 3 years was 53.2% (95% CI 44.5–62.6) for immunochemotherapy group and 29.0% (95% CI 24.1–34.8) for chemotherapy group (HR 0.48; 95% CI 0.37–0.63; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). DFS at 3 years was 71.0% (95% CI 62.1–81.2) for immunochemotherapy group and 53.2% (95% CI 45.6–62.2) for chemotherapy group (HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.33–0.77; p = 0.002) (Fig. 1D).

Survival outcomes were also assessed across the four subgroups. The 3-year PFS rates were 85.7% in patients receiving immunochemotherapy induction followed by ASCT, 62.8% in patients receiving immunochemotherapy induction followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance, 50.9% in patients receiving chemotherapy followed by ASCT, and 38.4% in patients receiving chemotherapy without ASCT (p < 0.001) (Fig. S1A). The 3-year OS rates were 85.7%, 79.1%, 69.6%, and 58.8%, respectively (p < 0.001) (Fig. S1B). The 3-year modified PFS rates were 85.7%, 51.3%, 46.4%, and 25.0%, respectively (p < 0.001) (Fig. S1C). The 3-year DFS rates were 85.7%, 68.8%, 55.9%, and 48.8%, respectively (p = 0.012) (Fig. S1D).

In the chemotherapy group without ASCT, 162 patients (70.4%) experienced disease progression. Among them, 86 (53.1%) had documented second- or later-line therapy, including 35 (40.7%) patients with anti-PD-1-based therapy and 51 (59.3%) with non-anti-PD-1-based chemotherapy. The 3-year OS rates were 71.4% and 48.9%, respectively (HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.24–0.91; p = 0.025) (Fig. S2).

Multivariate Cox regression and subgroup analysis

In univariate Cox regression analysis, poor survival outcomes (both PFS and OS) were associated with distant lymph node involvement, non-nasal involvement, detectable circulating EBV DNA, and chemotherapy (versus immunochemotherapy) (Table S2). In multivariate Cox regression analysis, poor survival outcomes (both PFS and OS) were also associated with distant lymph node involvement (PFS: HR 1.68; p = 0.001; OS: HR 1.52; p = 0.020), non-nasal involvement (PFS: HR 1.81; p < 0.001; OS: HR 2.21; p < 0.001), detectable circulating EBV DNA (PFS: HR 1.57; p = 0.013; OS: HR 1.65; p = 0.034) (Table S3). Immunochemotherapy (versus chemotherapy) was associated with favorable PFS (HR 0.47; p < 0.001) and OS (HR 0.45; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis demonstrated that most patient subgroups benefited from immunochemotherapy (Figs. S3–S4). Although the benefit was not statistically significant in EBV DNA-undetectable patients (HR 0.73; p = 0.407), a clear survival advantage was observed in EBV DNA-detectable patients (HR 0.41; p < 0.001). Stratification by PINK risk confirmed survival benefit of immunochemotherapy in both high-risk (HR 0.45; p < 0.001) and intermediate-risk group (HR 0.38; p = 0.017).

The anti-PD-1 antibodies used in the immunochemotherapy group included sintilimab (n = 64, 47.4%), pembrolizumab (n = 34, 25.2%), nivolumab (n = 20, 14.8%), and others such as tislelizumab (n = 8), camrelizumab (n = 6), toripalimab (n = 2), and unknown (n = 1) (Table S4). No significant differences were observed in either ORR or survival outcomes among patients receiving different anti-PD-1 antibodies. The 3-year PFS rates were 68.4%, 61.0%, 57.1%, and 59.1%, and the corresponding 3-year OS rates were 81.9%, 73.0%, 65.0%, and 81.4% for sintilimab, pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and others, respectively (p = 0.793 for PFS and 0.606 for OS) (Fig. S5).

PSM analysis

The PSM cohort included 125 patients in each group. Demographic and baseline characteristics of the two groups in PSM analysis are shown in Table S5. In comparison with the chemotherapy group, the immunochemotherapy group had higher ORR (88.8% versus 72.8%, p = 0.002), higher CR rate (72.0% versus 47.2%, p < 0.001), higher 3-year PFS rate (64.7%, 95% CI 56.7–73.8 versus 43.6%, 95% CI 35.7–53.3; HR 0.47; 95% CI 0.33–0.67; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A), as well as higher 3-year OS rate (79.9%, 95% CI 73.0–87.5 versus 64.4%, 95% CI 56.4−73.4; HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.31–0.79; p = 0.003) (Fig. 2B). In comparison with the patients who achieved CR but not receiving ASCT after chemotherapy, patients receiving immunochemotherapy followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance (n = 71 per group) (Table S6) had higher 3-year DFS rate (70.4%, 95% CI 59.9–82.8 versus 48.2%, 95% CI 37.7–61.6; HR 0.45; 95% CI 0.27–0.75; p = 0.002) (Fig. S6A), and higher 3-year OS rate (89.5%, 95% CI 82.4–97.2 versus 75.7%, 95% CI 66.3–88.5; HR 0.33; 95% CI 0.16–0.71; p = 0.004) (Fig. S6B). In comparison with patients receiving ASCT after chemotherapy, patients receiving immunochemotherapy followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance (n = 41 per group) (Table S7) had higher 3-year DFS rate (72.6%, 95% CI 59.3–89.0 versus 50.9%, 95% CI 37.6–68.9; HR 0.46; 95% CI 0.23–0.94; p = 0.032) (Fig. 2C), and higher 3-year OS rate (91.5%, 95% CI 82.8–100.0 versus 72.9%, 95% CI 60.5–88.0; HR 0.29; 95% CI, 0.09–0.88; p = 0.029) (Fig. 2D).

Safety

Grade 3–4 hematologic toxicities were more frequent during induction therapy in the immunochemotherapy group versus chemotherapy group (40.0% versus 20.8% for neutropenia, 44.4% versus 23.0% of leukopenia, 20.7% versus 13.8% for anemia, and 20.0% versus 16.3% for thrombocytopenia) (Table 3). Grade 3–4 non-hematologic toxicities were rare in all treatment groups. During ASCT consolidation, hematologic toxicities were prevalent, with grade 3–4 neutropenia (84.9%), leukopenia (83.0%), and thrombocytopenia (83.0%). Grade 3–4 non-hematologic adverse events during ASCT included aminotransferase increased (7.5%), nausea or vomiting (7.5%), and decreased appetite (5.7%). Anti-PD-1 maintenance therapy was well tolerated, with no grade 3–4 hematologic adverse events and low rate of grade 3–4 hypothyroidism (1.6%) (Table S8). No treatment-related deaths occurred in any group.

Discussion

Optimal treatment strategies for advanced-stage NKTCL remain controversial, with ongoing debate surrounding the use of first-line immunotherapy and the role of ASCT as consolidation therapy. In this multicenter retrospective study, the addition of anti-PD-1 antibody to P-GEMOX induction chemotherapy was associated with higher ORR and CR rate compared with P-GEMOX induction alone. Patients receiving immunochemotherapy induction followed by anti-PD1 maintenance achieved superior PFS and OS even in comparison with the patients receiving ASCT after chemotherapy. The immunochemotherapy group had a higher rate of grade 3–4 hematologic toxicities during induction, but these toxicities were generally transient and manageable. Also, anti-PD-1 maintenance was associated with fewer severe toxicities than ASCT, underscoring its potential as a safer consolidation strategy.

This study offers a direct comparison between anti-PD-1-based asparaginase-containing immunochemotherapy and asparaginase-based chemotherapy in newly diagnosed advanced-stage NKTCL. Previous studies evaluating regimens such as SMILE (dexamethasone, methotrexate, ifosfamide, L-asparaginase, and etoposide) [30], AspaMetDex (L-asparaginase with methotrexate and dexamethasone) [31], and DDGP (dexamethasone, cisplatin, gemcitabine, and pegaspargase) [32] reported 38.5–67.5% CR rate. The CR rate in the chemotherapy group of the current study (50.5%) falls within this reported range, and PFS/OS outcomes were also comparable to those previously observed. In the current study, the immunochemotherapy group had 89.6% ORR, 71.9% CR rate, and 3-year PFS and OS rates of 64.1% and 79.5%, respectively. These findings are consistent with the results of the SPIRIT trial for sintilimab plus P-GEMOX followed by sintilimab maintenance (100% ORR, 85% CR rate, 64% 2-year PFS rate, and 76% 3-year OS rate) [19]. The immunochemotherapy group in the current study also had better modified PFS, a measure that excludes PR based on the poor survival outcomes and the need for early salvage therapy in T-cell lymphoma patients who achieve PR [29]. All these findings were verified in the PSM analysis.

ASCT is recommended by current guidelines in NKTCL patients achieving CR after induction therapy [23, 24], but its survival benefit remains debatable due to the lack of randomized trials. In the current study, patients who received immunochemotherapy followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance had superior PFS and OS compared to chemotherapy with ASCT, suggesting more durable remission. In patients who received immunochemotherapy followed by ASCT, the estimated 3-year PFS and OS rates were both 85.7%. These results were consistent with a prospective phase 2 study that reported 56.6% 4-year PFS rate and 75.2% 4-year OS rate in stage IV patients treated with anti-PD-1 antibody-based immunochemotherapy [33]. In that study, among patients who achieved CR after induction (n = 17), those who received consolidative ASCT demonstrated significantly lower relapse rate compared to those who did not (17.6% versus 60.0%; p < 0.05). Given the limited number of patients who received immunochemotherapy followed by ASCT in the current study, these observed benefits should be considered exploratory. Prospective randomized studies are warranted to determine whether anti-PD-1-based immunochemotherapy combined with ASCT can provide superior long-term outcomes compared with anti-PD-1 maintenance in advanced-stage NKTCL.

The rate of hematologic toxicities was higher in the immunochemotherapy group versus the chemotherapy group in the current study. Nevertheless, the overall tolerability remained acceptable, as most toxicities were manageable and the incidence of grade 3–4 leukopenia and neutropenia was comparable to that reported for other regimens in advanced NKTCL. For instance, grade 3–4 leukopenia and neutropenia occurred in 85.0% of patients treated with SMILE regimen [30, 32], whereas the corresponding rates in the DDGP regimen were 62.5% and 65.0%, respectively [32]. In addition, hypothyroidism was observed in the immunochemotherapy group as an immune-related adverse event attributable to anti-PD-1 therapy. Most cases were grade 1–2, manageable with routine monitoring and levothyroxine replacement, and occurred at frequencies similar to those reported in solid tumor cohorts treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [34, 35]. In the current study, ASCT consolidation was associated with a higher rate of severe hematologic toxicities compared with anti-PD-1 maintenance, likely related to the myeloablative conditioning required prior to infusion. Specifically, grade 3–4 neutropenia and leukopenia occurred in 85.0% and 82.5% of patients, respectively. These findings suggest that anti-PD-1 antibody plus P-GEMOX regimen followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance appeared to be associated with a more favorable safety profile than ASCT in our cohort.

In multivariate Cox regression analysis, distant lymph node involvement, non-nasal involvement, and detectable circulating EBV DNA independently predicted inferior PFS and OS. Biologically, these features reflect heavier tumor burden as well as a more aggressive disease phenotype [36–38]. Accordingly, these variables may capture risk more precisely than anatomic stage alone, which lost significance after adjustment. Age >60 years was associated with shorter PFS but not OS, which may reflect treatment heterogeneity and the mitigating effect of subsequent salvage therapies. In subgroup analyses, the survival advantage of immunochemotherapy over chemotherapy was consistent across baseline categories. Survival benefits with immunochemotherapy versus chemotherapy were observed in patients with detectable circulating EBV DNA but not in patients with undetectable EBV DNA at the baseline. This pattern is biologically reasonable and aligns with the known prognostic importance of EBV DNA in NKTCL [27, 39–41].

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective study design may have introduced selection bias and confounding biases. Second, limited data are available on baseline comorbidity burden, such as the Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation-Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI) or other comorbidity indices across centers, which may have further introduced selection bias. Third, the small sample size of patients who underwent immunochemotherapy followed by ASCT limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the role of ASCT. Finally, although the median follow-up was sufficient to assess 3-year survival, longer-term data are needed to capture late relapses.

In conclusion, this multicenter retrospective study suggests that anti-PD-1 antibody plus P-GEMOX chemotherapy followed by anti-PD-1 maintenance was associated with improved response rates and survival outcomes compared with P-GEMOX chemotherapy in patients with advanced-stage NKTCL, while maintaining an acceptable toxicity profile. Randomized controlled trials that compare immunochemotherapy versus chemotherapy for advanced-stage NKTCL are needed.
