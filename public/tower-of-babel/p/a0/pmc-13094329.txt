Harnessing Deep Learning in Searching Wild Relatives of Domestic Animals

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWild relatives of domestic animals are crucial reservoirs of genetic diversity, yet pervasive hybridization with domestic animals poses significant conservation challenges. Here, we developed a deep learning–based pipeline, consisting of a multi‐layer perceptron for SNP panel selection and a Deep & Cross Network for model training, to discern wild relatives from their closely related domestic animals using genomic SNP data. Leveraging the 1960 genomes from 164 red jungle fowl (RJF; Gallus gallus ) and 1796 domestic chicken samples, we applied this pipeline to yield the RJF identification model based on a 285‐SNP panel. We employed this model to characterize domestic chickens, RJF, and hybrids in the independent genomic datasets from contemporary samples and historical specimens, respectively. The accuracy was 97.8% for historical samples with missing genotypes. The benchmarking multiple hybrid detection tools indicated that the RJF identification model was effective and practical. The further application to the genomic data from wild boar ( Sus scrofa ), domestic pigs, and their hybrids validated the pipeline. Our method has potential in not only monitoring genetic diversity in wild relatives of domestic animals but also supporting animal genetic resource conservation and management.

Introduction

Wild relatives of domestic animals serve as vital reservoirs of biodiversity for conservation biology and agriculture (Smith et al. 2022; Turcotte et al. 2017). In addition to serving as ancestral references for domestication and feralization studies (Frantz et al. 2020; Gering et al. 2019; Houston et al. 2020), wild relatives of domestic animals provide genetic resources for improving animal breeding (Bosse et al. 2019; Lv et al. 2022), enhancing infectious disease resistance (Cao et al. 2021; Shi et al. 2025), and facilitating rapid adaptation to local environments (Ai et al. 2015; Cao et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022). The introgression from wild relatives also has shaped the phenotypic evolution in domestic animals, such as the introgressed β‐carotene oxygenase 2 (BCO2) allele from grey junglefowl ( Gallus sonneratii ) resulting in the yellow skin phenotype and its derived allele buffering low‐carotenoid diets in chickens (Eriksson et al. 2008; Fallahshahroudi et al. 2019). The conservation and availability for utilization of wild relatives of domestic animals have attracted wide concerns (Smith et al. 2022).

Yet increasing anthropogenic contact frequently results in hybridization and introgression from domestic forms into wild populations, threatening the genetic integrity and long‐term viability of wild lineages. It is evidenced by series of genetic and genomic studies across populations of wild canids (Donfrancesco et al. 2019; Lobo et al. 2023, 2025; Tyagi et al. 2023), wild boars (Anderson et al. 2021; Schleimer et al. 2022), and wild junglefowl (Lawal et al. 2020; Thakur et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2023), leading to genetic swamping (Howard‐McCombe et al. 2023; Tensen and Fischer 2024), loss of ancestral genotypes (Wu et al. 2023), reduced fitness (Glover et al. 2017; McGinnity et al. 2003), and even altered ecosystem functioning (Porretta and Canestrelli 2023). Additionally, hybridization between escaped or feralized domestic animals and wild species further exacerbates the situation (Barmentlo et al. 2024; Kidd et al. 2009). These anthropogenic hybridizations posed ecology and biodiversity conservation management challenges (Bonacic et al. 2019; Smith et al. 2022).

For red junglefowl (RJF) in particular, long‐standing concerns regarding the conservation of RJF (Lawler 2012; Yue 2025), as the intricate hybridization process poses the significant challenges for RJF delimitation. Traditional morphological criteria fail to reflect genetic purity for RJF or have a weak correlation with genome‐wide ancestry (Brisbin Jr and Peterson 2007; Wu et al. 2020) as a result of ongoing introgression from chickens (Thakur et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2023). RJF and domestic chickens share multiple mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) haplogroups (e.g., A, B, and E), while only rare haplogroups (e.g., X, Y, and W) are unique to certain RJF lineages, limiting the usefulness of mtDNA alone in distinguishing wild RJF from domestic chickens (Hata et al. 2021; Miao et al. 2013). Current microsatellite panels and SNP arrays are often biased toward domestic chicken breeds, while a part of RJF gene pool was not involved in the domestication process (Hata et al. 2021), leading to ascertainment bias that can distort population genetic inferences (Ajmone‐Marsan et al. 2011; Lachance and Tishkoff 2013; Malomane et al. 2019). The thriving of whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) data for multiple RJF subspecies and their neighbouring domestic chickens (Wang et al. 2020) provides a valuable reference panel with reduced SNP ascertainment bias for developing new approaches to characterize genetic differences among RJF, chickens, and their hybrids.

Decades of work in hybrid zone theory and simulation have clarified how hybrid‐detection power depending on the number and informativeness of loci (McFarlane and Pemberton 2019; Vähä and Primmer 2006). A rich toolbox exists for hybrid detection and ancestry estimation, including model‐based clustering methods STRUCTURE (Pritchard et al. 2000) and ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al. 2009); Bayesian hybrid‐classification approaches such as NewHybrids (Anderson and Thompson 2002); hybrid‐index inference with HIest/entropy/gghybrid and genomic‐cline analysis with introgress/bgc/bgchm (Bailey 2024; Fitzpatrick 2012; Gompert and Alex Buerkle 2010; Gompert and Buerkle 2012; Gompert et al. 2024; Shastry et al. 2021). However, ancestry proportions and hybrid‐class assignments are re‐estimated with the reference cohort when adding new samples in unsupervised analyses, causing potential issues in frequent data merging and increased computational load. Meanwhile, clustering‐based approaches are sensitive to sampling schemes and cohort composition (Bohling et al. 2013; Lawson et al. 2018; Meirmans 2019; Puechmaille 2016). The hybrid calls made by combining of clustering‐based and Bayesian hybrid‐classification tools are often based on study‐specific thresholds (Gunn et al. 2023; Harmoinen et al. 2021), limiting standardization and cross‐study comparability. These challenges are evident in conservation and management of wildlife as well as molecular forensics, especially when various customized SNP panels are developed and degraded or low‐quantity DNAs are genotyped for routine hybridization assessment and monitoring (Martin et al. 2025; von Thaden et al. 2020).

Leveraging the “train‐once‐deploy‐many” advantage (Schrider and Kern 2018), we provide a pipeline based on deep learning approaches serving as state‐of‐the‐art tools for population genomic inference (Huang et al. 2024). The hybrid identification model was trained on the genomic reference panel comprising 164 RJF and 1796 chicken samples and then validated on independent contemporary genomes (newly sequenced, n = 128) and historical genomes from museum specimens (n = 45; Wu et al. 2023). The pipeline was further applied in the genomic dataset of wild boars, domestic pigs, and hybrids. The protocol and models are available in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/icefire080/Deep_SNP_screen_and_RJF_identification).

Materials and Methods

Training and Testing Dataset Preparation

We retrieved WGS data for 1796 domestic chickens and 164 RJF from the Chicken2K database (http://chicken.ynau.edu.cn/index/download/index). The autosomal SNPs met the following quality control criteria: (1) Biallelic SNPs only; (2) Minor allelic frequency ≥ 0.05; and (3) Genotyping rate ≥ 0.9. We allocated 1666 genomes from 1522 domestic chickens and 144 RJF to the training dataset for identifying informative RJF SNPs. The remaining 294 genomes served as the testing dataset to evaluate model performance (Table S1).

Pre‐Screening of SNPs Panel

To reduce dimensionality and computational load for deep learning, we filtered SNPs from the training dataset using pairwise Wright's FST values calculated with vcftools v0.1.13 (Danecek et al. 2011) and the ‐weir‐fst‐pop parameter. We assigned RJF subspecies and domestic chickens into three groups: GG group (G. g. murghi, G. g. jabouillei, G. g. gallus, and G. g. bankiva), GGS group (G. g. spadiceus), and DC group (Domestic chickens), with reference to the phylogeny (GG, (GGS, DC)) described before (Wang et al. 2020). As the major ancestor for domestic chickens, GGS presented the shortest Fst distance with DC as compared with GG (Wang et al. 2020). We grouped GGS separately from GG. We retained SNPs meeting all the following FST criteria simultaneously: (1) F ST ≤ 0.05 for GG vs. GGS; (2) FST ≥ 0.25 for GG vs. DC; and (3) F ST ≥ 0.25 for GGS vs. DC. This filtering process yielded 3719 SNPs for model training.

Deep Learning Architectures Construction

We employed multiple deep learning architectures for SNP data analysis, including: Deep & Cross Network (DCN) (Wang, Fu, Fu, Wang, and Acm 2017), DeepFM (DFM) (Guo et al. 2017), Self‐Attention (SA) (Vaswani et al. 2017), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) (LeCun et al. 2015; Rosenblatt 1958). Linear regression (LR) was included in the baseline comparison. The main differences among these architectures were ascribed to the genetic patterns or characters being learned from SNP data (Table S2a). DCN and DFM modelled SNP–SNP interactions efficiently to learn the patterns where specific pairs (or larger sets) of SNPs jointly carrying information about ancestry membership. Especially, DFM showed superior performance in handling both low‐ and high‐order feature interactions (Guo et al. 2017). SA could learn to up‐weight the most informative SNPs for each sample and capture dependencies among SNPs (Vaswani et al. 2017). MLP consisted of one, two, and four hidden layers configurations to learn non‐linear relationships based on a combination of SNPs rather than simply discrete SNPs. And each hidden layer contained 256 neurons. The output layer comprised two neurons. Taking above, we selected and integrated architectures according to the practical requirements in the SNP panel selection and model training procedures, respectively. All deep learning architectures were implemented using PyTorch v1.21.1 (Imambi et al. 2021).

SNP Panel Selection via Iterative Pruning

Considering the computational load, SA involving a (4 × 1980) × (4 × 1980) matrix could overwhelm GPU memory when handling a large number of loci (> 1000). DFM was excluded from this step because it was not affordable. We employed generalized linear regression, MLP and DCN architectures in the SNP panel selection. The selection process consisted of four steps as following. (1) Baseline establishment: We constructed a baseline model using all SNP tokens initialized with uniform distribution, obtaining a baseline prediction score. (2) Iterative pruning: We generated SNP token subsets and calculated corresponding masked scores. The pruning criterion was based on the SNP Importance Score (Δ), defined as: Δ = |Masked score—Baseline score|. (3) Selection threshold: SNPs were removed from the panels if Δ < 0.5, otherwise retained. This process continued iteratively until reaching the predetermined SNP count (Figure S2). (4) Panel set: We optimized SNPs panels for subsequent training the RJF identification model to ensure both computational efficiency and predictive performance. We screened six SNPs panels with sizes of 900, 700, 500, 300, 100, and 50 SNPs through gradient descent optimization. To evaluate the performance of each SNP panel size, we conducted three identification model training iterations using DCN architecture with the same initialized parameters under unfixed random seed condition. The performance was evaluated by the metrics AUC and F1 scores. AUC summarizes performs in distinguishing classes, ranging from 0.5 (no discrimination as random ranking) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination). F1 score is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall as 2*Precision*Recall/(Precision+Recall), indicating the overall performance. Precision is the proportion of predicted RJF that are true RJF. Recall is the proportion of true RJF correctly predicted.

Model Training, Hyperparameter Optimization and Robustness Testing

We integrated DFM, SA, and MLP through weighted fusion to capture SNP data features, and developed a novel architecture called DFM‐SA‐MLP (Figure S1). We abandoned DCN for fusion because attention mechanisms had been integrated with FM‐style interaction modelling to adaptively weight informative interactions/features (Xiao et al. 2017), aligning well with SNP data. For adjusting learning and avoiding overfitting in the RJF identification model training, we tuned several model parameter settings (Table S2b): batch size (32; number of individuals per update), epochs (15; passed training through the data), and learning rate (0.001; step size of parameter update). We also used AdamW optimization, together with weight decay (0.01; mild shrinkage applied to all model parameters) and an additional embedding regularization weight (regular_weight = 0.0001; an extra penalty applied to the SNP embedding table that discourages reliance on noisy SNP features), to reduce overfitting in high‐dimensional SNP data. We stabilized training with a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (factor = 0.5, patience = 3, min_lr = 1e‐6; reducing the learning rate when validation performance stops improving) and early stopping (delta = 0.005, patience = 5; stopping training once validation improvements become negligible). Because the RJF samples were fewer than domestic chickens, we adopted focal loss (Lin et al. 2017) to address the class imbalance, with α = 0.9 (up‐weighting the minority class) and γ = 1.5 (focusing learning on harder‐to‐classify individuals). Each model was trained five times with the above‐mentioned hyperparameters with an unfixed random seed condition. To assess the candidate model's robustness to missing genotype data, we randomly introduced missing SNPs at rates ranging from 5% to 50% (in 5% increments) and ran the models 20 times for each missing rate. The performance was evaluated by the metrics of AUC and F1 scores.

Genomic Sequencing

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Kunming Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IACUC‐OE‐2021‐12‐004). The samples suspected of being RJF were obtained from confiscated illegal wildlife trade specimens in Yunnan province, southwestern China, through the Animal Branch of the Germplasm Bank of Wild Species, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Table S1). Genomic DNA was extracted from feather and tissue samples using the EasyPure Micro Genomic DNA Kit (TransGen Biotech Co. Ltd) following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA library preparation was performed by BGI using the VAHTSTM Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI (VAZYME, NDM607‐02). WGS of 128 samples, including 24 suspected RJF individuals, 29 hybrids, and 75 indigenous chickens, was performed using the DNBSEQ‐T7 platform (MGI Tech Co. Ltd.) with 30× depth by BGI.

Genomic SNP Calling and Quality Control

We merged the genomic data of 71 free‐roaming junglefowl samples, eight domestic chickens and five G. g. bankiva from previous study (Wang et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2023) with the newly sequenced 128 genomes (Table S1). We performed quality control using fastp v0.23.2 (Chen et al. 2018) to remove adapters, discard reads shorter than 30 bp and filter low‐quality reads (MAPQ score < 30). The filtered reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (GenBank: GCA_000002315.5) using BWA‐MEM (Li 2014). Duplicate reads were marked using Picard v2.26.4 MarkDuplicates command. Variant calling was performed with Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.2.0 (McKenna et al. 2010) using HaplotypeCaller ‐output_model EMIT_ALL_SITES parameter to retained all sites. We applied the following hard filters using GATK VariantFiltration command: MQ < 40.0, QUAL < 30.0, QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, ReadPosRankSum < −8.0, SOR > 3.0. Variants failing these filters were removed using vcftools v0.1.16.

Population Genomic Analysis

We applied PLINK v1.90b6.17 to exclude variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05 and remove variants with linkage disequilibrium (r 2 > 0.1) using a 50 kb window and 10 kb step size. We retained 5,309,030 autosomal SNPs across 212 samples for maximum likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction using FastTree2 (Price et al. 2010) with default parameters. We assessed population structure using ADMIXTURE v1.3 (Alexander et al. 2009) with k = 2 to k = 10 for 200 replicates for each k. For the selected SNP panel, we estimated hybrid indices for each individual based on ADMIXTURE Q‐values with HIest (Fitzpatrick 2012) and NewHybrids (Anderson and Thompson 2002).

Independent Dataset of Historical Samples

For 45 historical samples with low‐coverage WGS (Wu et al. 2023; Table S1), we referred to the protocol for ancient genomic analysis (Zhu et al. 2024). According to the DNA damage detection before (Wu et al. 2023), we trimmed five base pairs from both ends of each sequencing read using BamUtil v1.0.15 (Jun et al. 2015). A pileup on the 3719 SNP array positions was generated and pseudo‐haploid genotypes were called by using pileupCaller with the option ‐randomHaploid which represents a genotype by randomly choosing one read at each site, the same as the option in the previous study (Neumann et al. 2022).

Application to East Asian Wild Boars

We applied the pipeline to the genomic data of East Asian wild boars, domestic pigs, and their hybrids (Table S3). The genomic data of 43 East Asian wild boars and 427 domestic pigs from IAnimal (https://ianimal.pro/index; Fu et al. 2023) were compiled as the training dataset. We combined the genomic data of 52 wild boars, 12 hybrids, 218 domestic pigs, and 20 outgroup samples (from Porcula salvania and Phacochoerus africanus ) reported before with the newly sequenced eight wild boar and 30 domestic pig genomes (with ethical approval ID: IACUC‐OE‐2023‐06‐001) as the test data to validate the pipeline. The experiments and data analyses were in accordance with those for RJF described above. More details about the SNP panel selection and identification model can be found on Github repository (https://github.com/icefire080/Deep_SNP_screen_and_RJF_identification).

Results

Pipeline Overview

In the first part of our pipeline, we generated an initial genomic dataset for SNP panel selection after applying Fst threshold filtering (Figure 1a). We encoded chromosome position and genotype information as initial SNP tokens. In the second part, the deep learning architecture consists of three modules running on the GPU platform (Figure 1b). (1) SNP panel optimization for feature selection: this module uses initial SNP tokens for panel selection, aiming to eliminate redundant SNPs until the desired panel size (e.g., with 500, 300, 100, or 50 SNPs) is achieved. (2) Training the identification model using the optimized SNP panel: this involves training a model for identifying the target populations/lineages/samples (e.g., RJF or wild boar) based on the optimized SNP panel sizes. (3) Model evaluation and hyperparameter adjustment: the module evaluates each model by using metrics of AUC, F1 score, precision, and recall (MATERIALS AND METHODS), and then adjusts hyperparameters (if necessary) to improve model performance for each SNP panel size. Consequently, the pipeline can optimize the identification model along with its corresponding SNP panel. We applied the pipeline to a chicken genomic SNP dataset with 39.75 Mb SNPs of 1960 samples on different platforms (Table S4). The SNP selection process reduced the panel from 3719 to 285 SNPs below 40 min—using MLP architecture for all GPU platforms. Subsequently, training the identification model with the 285‐SNP panel required less than 2 min using DCN architecture.

Schematic of wild relative identification framework based on deep learning. (a) Training and testing dataset generation; (b) The process of SNP panel optimization and identification model training. The predict labels (1, 2 and 0) were defined as wild, hybrid and domestic based on the probability thresholds with > 0.95, 0.95–0.05 and < 0.05, respectively. Probabilities were derived by the softmax.

Performance of SNPs Panel Selection Model Architectures

After Fst threshold filtering, the dataset of 3719 diverged SNPs between RJF and chickens were used, and different SNP panel sizes were tested described as METHODS above. The one‐hidden‐layer MLP showed robustness across varying SNP panel sizes. Increasing MLP depth correlated with severe fluctuation of AUC and F1 scores (Figure 2a). The one‐hidden‐layer MLP exhibited minimal F1 score degradation (0.958 to 0.913) with SNP sizes reduction from 300 to 50 by a more pronounced recall decrease (0.920 to 0.840; Figure 2b). The one‐hidden‐layer MLP presented superior performance, with AUC scores ranging from 0.998 to 1.000, as compared with LR (0.959 to 1.000), DCN (0.927 to 1.000), 2‐layer MLP (0.960 to 1.000), and 4‐layer MLP (0.943 to 1.000) architectures across under 500 SNP panel sizes (Figure 2a). Consequently, we selected the one‐hidden‐layer MLP architecture for SNP panel selection, ultimately identifying a 285‐SNP panel (Table S5) for the RJF identification model training.

LR: Linear regression; DCN: Deep & Cross Network; MLP: One‐hidden‐layer perceptron; MLP2: Two‐hidden‐layers perceptron; MLP4: Four‐hidden‐layers perceptron; (b) Performance comparison using different numbers of selected SNPs based on MLP.

RJF Identification Model Architecture

We evaluated the RJF identification models using a 285‐SNP panel, with positive scores representing RJF classification probability (> 95% probability). The DCN architecture achieved exceptional performance (AUC: 0.999–1.000; F1: 0.913–0.980). DFM consistently outperformed self‐attention across all metrics, while MLP and self‐attention showed limited classification efficacy. Notably, the hybrid DFM‐SA‐MLP model indicated superior performance (AUC: 0.995–1.000; F1: 0.941–0.962) compared to the architectures of DFM (AUC: 0.987–0.999; F1: 0.889–0.913), MLP (AUC: 0.986–1.000; F1: 0.333–0.810), and self‐attention (AUC: 0.994–1.000; F1: 0.485–0.718; Figure 3a). Thus, we implemented the DCN architecture, yielding the optimal model for 285‐SNP panel (AUC: 0.999; F1: 0.958; precision: 1.000; recall: 0.920; Figure 3a).

Model Robustness to Missing Genotype Data

Given missing genotypes in genomic sequencing especially with low depth or coverage (Card et al. 2021; Lou and Therkildsen 2022), we simulated missing‐at‐random scenarios with missing rates ranging from 0% to 50% based on the 285‐SNP panel to test the model robustness. The model demonstrated strong robustness, maintaining high AUC (median: 0.992–1.000) and recall (0.920–1.000) across all missing rates (Figure 3b). While F1 scores remained stable (median: 0.939–0.958) until 25% missing rate (0.883), further reduction to 50% resulted in greater decline (0.281). Similarly, precision decreased from 1.000 (omission error: 0.080) at 20% missing rate to 0.819 at 25% missing rate (omission error: 0.020); further reduction to 50% resulted in greater decline (0.163). These results indicate the RJF identification model is robust to data missingness no more than 20% missing rate.

Performance of the RJF Identification Model With 285‐SNP Panel

We applied the RJF identification model to an independent test dataset consisting of 212 samples, including domestic chickens, RJF, and hybrids (Supplementary Table S1). The results demonstrated high concordance with those of phylogenetic and population structure analyses (Figure 4a, Ring 5). The samples with high RJF ancestry components (> 50%, Figure 4a, Ring 2–4 red and light red colour) were accurately identified as RJF (Figure 4a, Ring 5 red colour). The RJF identification model characterized 17.2% (5/29) of the proposed hybrid individuals collected from southwestern Yunnan and Hainan Island as hybrids, while 82.8% (24/29) were identified as domestic chickens. The hybrids from villages in southwestern Yunnan harbour higher proportions of RJF ancestry components (2.14%–24.83%) compared to those (with proportions of 5.44%–7.34%) from farms in Hainan Island (Figure 4a, Ring 1–4). Among the suspected RJF samples, 50.0% (12/24) were classified as RJF, 41.7% (10/24) as domestic chickens, and 8.3% (2/24) as hybrids. In addition, we applied the RJF identification model to the historical samples with missing genotypes. Despite 13.3% (6/45) samples having > 30% missing data, 97.8% (44/45) were positively identified as RJF and 2.2% (1/45) as hybrids (Figure 4b). The results validate the model's effectiveness for both contemporary samples and museum specimens.

Benchmarking Multiple Hybrid Detection Tools

Leveraging the dataset consisting of 212 samples (RJF vs. chicken vs. hybrids), we compared the performance of our deep‐learning model based on the selected 285‐SNP panel with the clustering‐based ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al. 2009), Bayesian hybrid‐classifier NewHybrids (Anderson and Thompson 2002), and hybrid‐index tool HIest (Fitzpatrick 2012; Figure S3). The wild‐ancestry probabilities inferred by the RJF identification model showed strong association with the ADMIXTURE Q‐values based on the whole‐genomic SNP set (Figure S3a; Pearson's r = 0.832, p = 1.29e‐55) and HIest hybrid index based on the 285‐SNP panel (Figure S3b; Pearson's r = 0.855, p = 7.52e‐62). The deep‐learning classifications were further supported by the hybrid patterns inferred by NewHybrids (Figure S3b). The benchmarking analysis proposed the deep‐learning model was comparable to traditional tools with different algorithms and had potential to provide statistical support to ad hoc thresholds for these tools.

Genetic Identification of East Asian Wild Boars

We applied our pipeline to the genomic data of East Asian wild boars and domestic pigs (Table S3) to generate the wild boar identification model with a panel of 215 SNPs. We then employed the identification model in the test dataset of 340 samples (Table S3). The results (Figure 5) showed that, among the wild boar samples, 50.0% (30/60) were classified as positive, while the rest of the samples were identified as hybrids. For the samples labelled as hybrids, 25.0% (3/12) were classified as wild boars, 8.3% (1/12) as hybrids, and 66.7% (8/12) as domestic pigs. Of the 248 domestic pigs, from 29 Chinese indigenous breeds, the majority (97.6%; 242/248) were classified as domestic. Interestingly, six samples (from three Ningxiang, two Erhualian, and one Diannan small‐ear pigs) were identified as hybrids, implying introgression from wild boar in some recent breeding practices (Li et al. 2024). Our results were in agreement with the reported scenario of ongoing gene flow between wild boar and domestic pigs (Frantz et al. 2015).

Discussion

In this study, we have integrated deep learning strategies into the pipeline for monitoring wild relatives, domestic animals, and their hybrids, which generally have close genetic affiliations. The resolution can be achieved with genotyping of hundreds of SNPs, which is appropriate for target capture sequencing and reduced representation sequencing (Jones and Good 2016; Kaelin et al. 2024; More et al. 2025; Nugent et al. 2023). It is helpful to reduce sequencing cost for experiment and computational load for data analysis. The pipeline can support both population‐level and individual‐based analyses. In particular, it shows robust to missing genotypic data and extends application to genomic data from molecular forensics, museum specimens, and ancient DNA (Card et al. 2021; Frantz et al. 2020; Kling et al. 2021). Thus, our pipeline, together with the generated models, have broad application prospects.

Reintroducing the RJF into its natural habitat is a crucial component of efforts to recover RJF populations. In Thailand, habitat suitability modelling has been combined with soft‐release protocols to facilitate the reintroduction of genetically verified RJF into natural habitats (Singchat et al. 2022). Prior to release, individuals were subjected to DNA sampling and microsatellite genotyping, along with preliminary disease screening to confirm health status. Although these reintroductions of RJF relied on microsatellite markers in accordance with FAO guidelines, they faced resolution limitations due to the limited number of loci (28) and complex allelic variation (Hata et al. 2021; Singchat et al. 2022). Therefore, to achieve more precise and high‐quality results with RJF, the application of SNP panels and deep learning strategies is essential. Our method can be integrated into the RJF conference strategy, reintroduction programs, and extended to various other areas, enhancing overall effectiveness.

Applying the pipeline to the genomic data of RJF, domestic chickens, and their hybrids, which come from China, Indonesia, Thailand, Laos and Singapore (Table S1), the results extend understanding for the RJF conservation. Although some potentially adaptive introgression from RJF to domestic chickens were reported (Ashari et al. 2025; Budi et al. 2023), the hybrids retain limited proportions of RJF ancestry in Yunnan (2.14%–24.83%; Figure 4a). The proportion is much lower in Hainan Island hybrids (5.44%–7.34%; Figure 4a). The results suggest that the sporadic and/or arbitrary hybridizations had a limited effect on reserve the genetic diversity of RJF. For endangered species, altering alleles through targeted genetic intervention, which by changing the occurrence or frequency of specific alleles using artificial selection, synthetic biology, and targeted gene flow within a population, may represent a more effective strategy (Kosch et al. 2022) according to the principles of conservation management (Schwartz et al. 2024). Moreover, we classified individuals as RJF with > 95% assignment probability, domestic chicken with < 5%, and those between 5% and 95% as hybrids in the RJF identification model. The simplified identification of hybrids may be vacillated by complicated ancient hybridization and/or incomplete lineage sorting (Wang et al. 2020), which have been widely reported in wild relatives of domestic animals (Santos et al. 2021; Sinding et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024). More efforts are required to discern genetic background of proposed hybrids.

It should be noted that our pipeline has several limitations: (1) training data relied on accurate prior population assignment which may suffer from sample misidentification and/or labeling errors (Carné et al. 2025); (2) filtering minor alleles may reduce the resolution of individual identification (Hemstrom et al. 2024); (3) identification models are only based on autosome SNP dataset, potentially missing sex‐biased hybridization signals (Ashari et al. 2025; Godinho et al. 2011). To address these issues, the increasing availability of genomic data from wild relatives of domestic animals (Li et al. 2020; Lv et al. 2022; Mariadassou et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2025) allows for the application of unsupervised learning and/or semi‐supervised frameworks to mitigate the challenges of missing or erroneous labeling (Karbstein et al. 2024; Millán Arias et al. 2022; Millan Arias et al. 2023). Incorporating sex chromosome and mtDNA variations (More et al. 2025), together with enhancing the accuracy of rare mutation detection (Barbitoff et al. 2022; Poplin et al. 2018; Yun et al. 2020), will further improve our model resolution. Adopting a decision‐tree approach (Tensen and Fischer 2024) or high‐resolution ancestral recombination graphs (Nielsen et al. 2025) may empower cross‐method validation of hybrid identification. The thriving multimodal studies show potential to improve identification accuracy for genetically similar and morphologically indistinguishable taxa (Kösters et al. 2025), motivating future extensions of our framework toward multimodal fusion with molecular, phenotypic, and meta data.
