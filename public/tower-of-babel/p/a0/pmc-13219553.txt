How do senior pharmacy students at King Saud University perceive their pharmaceutical calculation skills and preparedness for clinical practice?
Authors: Njoud Altuwaijri, Rawan Fitaihi, Alanoud M Altalal, Fai Alkathiri, Alyaa Alsalhi, Samiah Alhabardi, Eram Eltahir, Rihaf Alfaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPharmaceutical calculations are a core competency in pharmacy education and are essential for patient safety and effective clinical care. In the context of pharmacy education, diverse delivery and assessment strategies exist, and there are ongoing modifications to the pharmacy education landscape. This study explored the perceptions, confidence levels, and educational experiences of senior PharmD students regarding their pharmaceutical calculation skills and perceived preparedness for clinical practice. A qualitatively driven mixed-methods design was employed. A structured, anonymous survey comprising Likert-scale and open-ended questions was distributed to all fifth-year PharmD students at KSU (N = 141). A total of 141 complete responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics, the Chi-square test, and the Mann–Whitney U test for quantitative data, as well as thematic analysis for qualitative responses. The results revealed that most students (76.9%) reported positive experience with pharmaceutical calculations, and 79% indicated that extra academic support was available. The mode confidence rating was 5 (very confident), with a median of 4, suggesting a generally high self-reported perceived confidence. No statistically significant gender differences were observed in terms of confidence or experience. Students predominantly relied on digital tools (e.g., ChatGPT, YouTube, Excel) rather than traditional academic resources, and the majority expressed a desire for more real-world applications and practice-based learning. In summary, while students reported high perceived confidence in their pharmaceutical calculation abilities, many expressed uncertainties about their real-world relevance. These findings highlight the importance of practice-based, context-driven instruction, and the thoughtful integration of digital learning tools to enhance students' perceived relevance and engagement with pharmaceutical calculations.Keywords: Pharmaceutical calculations, Pharmacy education, Student perception, Student confidence, Digital learning tools, Clinical preparedness

Introduction

Numeracy and mathematical competence are essential skills for all healthcare professionals, underpinning safe and effective patient care. Among these professionals, pharmacists are frequently required to perform precise calculations for various aspects of clinical and pharmaceutical practice. These calculations include drug dosage adjustments, intravenous infusion rates, pharmacokinetic estimations, compounding formulas, and interpretation of prescription instructions. Hence, numerical proficiency is not just an academic requirement but a core competency critical for the professional practice of pharmacists (Minty-Walker 2024), (Arkell and Rutter 2012), (Bellottie 2018).

Globally, regulatory and accreditation bodies, such as the Accreditation Council for Pharmacy Education (ACPE) and the General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC), explicitly recognize the centrality of pharmaceutical calculations within pharmacy curricula. These organizations mandate the integration of calculation-based competencies into pharmacy education to ensure that graduates are well-prepared for clinical responsibilities. ACPE’s 2025 standards, for instance, highlight pharmaceutical calculations as a distinct content area, with an emphasis on developing mastery in mathematical skills required for the accurate preparation of prescriptions, including extemporaneously compounded dosage forms, and tailoring patient-specific nutritional and medication dosing (Education 2025). Failure to perform such calculations correctly can have severe consequences, including suboptimal treatment outcomes, adverse drug events, or even fatal medication errors. These risks underscore the high stakes of pharmaceutical numeracy and the necessity of developing strong calculation skills during undergraduate training (Mulac et al. 2022), (Lesar 1998), (Brown and Hanggi 2007).

Pharmaceutical calculations were introduced early in the PharmD program in the context of pharmacy education at King Saud University (KSU), reflecting their foundational importance. Students begin learning these skills in their first year, and the content is reinforced through a vertically integrated curriculum. Calculation topics are taught alongside other pharmaceutical sciences, allowing students to apply mathematical principles in diverse contexts, such as pharmacokinetics, pharmaceutics, and pharmacology. This integration aims to enhance conceptual understanding and promote the practical application of these concepts. However, evidence from the literature suggests that student retention of pharmaceutical calculation knowledge can diminish over time, particularly when not consistently reinforced or practiced (Brown and Hanggi 2007; Hegener et al. 2013). In addition, a recent study has indicated that many pharmacy students begin their programs with inadequate mathematical foundations, which may contribute to difficulties in mastering pharmaceutical calculations during subsequent courses (Frankart 2025).

Moreover, the ability to perform pharmaceutical calculations accurately is not merely a cognitive task but also an affective one. Confidence and self-perception play a significant role in students’ performance. Students who perceive their skills as inadequate may experience anxiety, reduced engagement, or avoidance of mathematical tasks, all of which can hinder their learning. Conversely, students with positive perceptions of their abilities tend to be more proactive and perform better academically. This psychological component highlights the importance of assessing how students evaluate their preparedness and capabilities in pharmaceutical calculations as they transition to clinical practice (Ha 2023).

Research has also shown that various factors influence pharmacy students' confidence in and performance in pharmaceutical calculations. These include the teaching methods employed, the level of mathematical background students bring into the program, opportunities for hands-on practice, availability of learning resources, and the perceived relevance of calculation tasks to real-world scenarios (Dilles 2011). For instance, traditional didactic instruction may be less effective in fostering long-term retention and application skills than active learning approaches, simulation-based learning, or problem-based activities. Integrating pharmaceutical calculations into clinical case studies or therapeutic decision-making exercises can help bridge the gap between theory and practice, thereby enhancing students’ engagement.

Furthermore, pharmacy students may face psychological and structural barriers in acquiring pharmaceutical calculation proficiency. These challenges can include math anxiety, limited feedback from instructors, lack of context-based applications, or insufficient repetition. Addressing these barriers requires a multifaceted approach, including redesigning the curriculum to incorporate more experiential learning, providing varied and interactive resources (such as e-learning modules or simulation labs), and creating a supportive learning environment that encourages inquiry and practice (Bergen 2011; Ghazal 2024).

However, the delivery and assessment of pharmaceutical calculations is diverse. A recent review highlighted multiple course designs and assessment tools for this fundamental course (Joshi and Klausner 2022; Fredrickson 2025). For instance, in a recent study, video recordings were used to deliver the topic, and the assessment was quiz-based. The study indicated that a significant number of the study sample found that video delivery was effective for understanding and comprehension (Ghazal 2024). In support of this, video assignments on pharmaceutical calculations were provided; this assessment strategy was associated with improved student engagement and learning outcomes (Ahmed and Al-Jumaili 2024).

Along with diverse delivery and assessment strategies, there have been modifications to the pharmacy education landscape. Another contributing factor to this topic is the progressive development of emerging tools and software (McMullan 2018; Chandran 2022). Furthermore, a recent report showed that performance in the calculation topic declined during the Pharmacist Licensure Examination, highlighting the need to examine the delivery, assessment, and course design of pharmaceutical calculations (Crawford 2024). Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic has impacted the educational framework. Since then, experts have suggested that delivery and assessment should be modified in medical education (Ahmed et al. 2020). Given this background, it is imperative to evaluate how pharmacy students perceive their pharmaceutical calculation skills, particularly during the final stages of their academic training when they transition into clinical practice. Understanding students’ perceptions, confidence levels, and challenges offers valuable insight for educators seeking to improve curriculum design and instructional strategies. These developments highlight the complexity of teaching pharmaceutical calculations within contemporary pharmacy curricula. Although innovative instructional approaches aim to enhance learning, their influence on how students perceive the relevance of pharmaceutical calculations and experience learning barriers remains insufficiently explored in the context of senior pharmacy training. In particular, limited attention has been given to students’ perceived preparedness at the point of transition to clinical practice, underscoring the need to examine senior pharmacy students’ perspectives to inform instructional alignment and learning support.

This study aimed to assess the perceptions and self-reported confidence of senior PharmD students at KSU regarding their pharmaceutical calculation competencies and readiness for clinical practice. Additionally, it examines their experiences with the educational process, the barriers they encounter, and recommendations for improvement. The findings of this study may inform curriculum enhancements at KSU and contribute to broader efforts in pharmacy education to support curriculum enhancement to better align pharmaceutical calculations instruction with students’ learning needs and perceived readiness for practice.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a qualitatively driven mixed-methods research design to explore senior pharmacy students’ perceptions of their pharmaceutical calculation skills and their preparedness for clinical practice (Lee 2019).

A structured survey instrument was used to collect quantitative data through closed-ended Likert-scale items and qualitative data through open-ended prompts. This combination allowed the study to identify broad patterns across participants while also capturing detailed insights into students’ personal experiences and reflections.

The rationale for using a qualitative-dominant approach stems from the nature of the research questions, which focus on subjective constructs such as self-confidence, perceived preparedness, and educational experiences. These topics are best understood through rich and descriptive data. The quantitative component provides complementary information by highlighting trends, enabling subgroup comparisons, and supporting the interpretation of qualitative themes.

This integrated design strengthened the study's methodological rigor through triangulation, which allowed the convergence of numerical data and thematic analysis to form a more complete picture of the learning environment (Flick 2004).

The structured survey ensured consistency in responses while also giving participants space to elaborate on their perspectives. This balance between standardization and flexibility is well suited for research in educational settings, where both common patterns and individual differences are important for curriculum development (Albuam 1993).

Participants

The participants were senior pharmacy students enrolled in the PharmD program at KSU. Male and female students in their fifth year of the six-year program were included, with the sixth year designated for the internship phase. By this stage of the program, all participants had completed dedicated pharmaceutical calculation courses during their second year, in addition to integrated coursework covering pharmaceutical calculations in subsequent academic years.

Eligibility was based on enrollment in the fifth year of the PharmD program and completion of the pharmaceutical calculation coursework. All eligible students were invited to participate in the study through institutional email communications and classroom announcements.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of KSU (KSU-HE-24–912) prior to data collection, ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all students prior to their involvement in the study.

Sample size

All enrolled senior pharmacy students participated in the study, totaling 141 students, 69 males and 72 females. Inclusion of the full cohort ensured a comprehensive representation of the target population, thereby minimizing sampling bias and enhancing the generalizability of the findings within the institutional context. The sample size was deemed adequate to support both the qualitative thematic analysis and the planned statistical comparisons, including subgroup analyses by gender.

Data collection

Data was collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed in Google Forms. A QR code linked directly to the survey was generated and distributed to students during in-class sessions to facilitate convenient access. The survey included both closed-ended (Likert-scale) and open-ended questions designed to capture quantitative and qualitative data.

The questionnaire was anonymous, and no personal identifiers were collected. The introductory page provided information about the study’s objectives, confidentiality assurances, and voluntary participation. The completion of the survey was considered implied informed consent. Data collection was conducted over a one- to two-week period during the second semester of the academic year, following ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board. A total of 141 completed responses were received, reflecting a 100% response rate from the eligible fifth-year student cohort.

Survey instrument

The survey consisted of seven questions designed to explore students’ experiences, confidence, and perceptions regarding pharmaceutical calculations in the context of academic training and clinical preparedness. Five closed-ended questions assessed: overall experience with pharmaceutical calculation coursework, self-reported confidence in applying calculations in real-world pharmacy practice, availability of academic support, personal utilization of that support, and perceived relevance of pharmaceutical calculation skills to future pharmacy careers. Two open-ended questions captured (1) the resources or tools students use when performing pharmaceutical calculations and (2) students’ suggestions for improving the teaching and delivery of pharmaceutical calculations within the program. Gender was not included as a survey item, as it was recorded separately based on sex-segregated data collection sessions.

A two-phase qualitative validation process was performed to ensure the face validity of the instrument. In the first phase, a panel of five academic experts in pharmaceutical education reviewed the draft survey. The experts were asked to evaluate each item for clarity, relevance, and alignment with the study’s objective of exploring student experiences and perceptions. Several revisions were made based on their feedback, including the simplification of technical terminology, refinement of item wording, and enhancement of question clarity.

In the second phase, the revised survey was piloted with 14 Sixth-year PharmD students. Piloting was conducted with sixth-year interns to ensure clarity among students who had completed the calculations course and progressed into practice-based training. Among them, nine students (64.3%) provided constructive feedback, primarily regarding how pharmaceutical calculations were taught and understood. Their responses confirmed the overall clarity and relevance of the instrument, and their input was used to make minor refinements. This two-stage validation process, involving both expert and student feedback, supports the validity of the questionnaire and its suitability for broader data collection.

Data analysis

The data analysis incorporated both quantitative and qualitative techniques to provide a comprehensive understanding of students' perceptions and experiences. After data collection, the survey responses were cleaned, coded, and analyzed using Microsoft Excel.

For the quantitative component, categorical responses were numerically coded for analysis (e.g., Male = 1, Female = 2; Yes = 1, No = 2; experience ratings as Positive = 1, Challenging = 2, Neutral = 3). Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation (SD), median, mode, skewness, and kurtosis, were calculated to summarize the main variables of interest.

A Chi-square test was applied to examine potential differences in experience ratings between male and female students, using a significance level of 0.05. Normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection via histograms. As the distribution of confidence scores did not meet the assumption of normality, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed to compare confidence levels between subgroups.

For the qualitative data, thematic analysis was conducted using an inductive approach to identify patterns and recurring themes in students' open-ended responses. The analysis followed a systematic approach: initial familiarization with the data, generation of preliminary codes, organization of related codes into broader categories, and development of overarching themes. These themes were then synthesized to provide insights into students' experiences, challenges faced during pharmaceutical calculation learning, and suggestions for course improvement.

The integration of qualitative themes with quantitative findings enabled a triangulated interpretation of the results, thus enhancing the overall validity and depth of the study’s conclusions.

Results

Participant demographics

The final analysis included 141 senior PharmD students enrolled in the College of Pharmacy at KSU. The study cohort was nearly evenly distributed by gender, comprising 69 male students (48.9%) and 72 female students (51.1%). All participants were in the fifth year of the PharmD program and had successfully completed the pharmaceutical calculations course as part of their curriculum. The inclusion of the full eligible senior cohort provided comprehensive representation of the target population and ensured that the demographic characteristics were appropriate for evaluating students’ perceptions and confidence related to pharmaceutical calculations.

Overall, students demonstrated positive perceptions and high confidence regarding their pharmaceutical calculation abilities. Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for the primary survey items. The student experience with pharmaceutical calculations was rated positively, with a mean score of 1.36 (SD = 0.73) where 1 represented a positive experience, 2 represented a challenging experience, and 3 represented a neutral experience. The most common (mode) response was 1, indicating a trend toward a positive experience.

Confidence in performing pharmaceutical calculations in real-world pharmacy settings was measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not confident, 5 = very confident). The mode score was 5, suggesting that many students felt very confident. The median confidence score was 4.0, and the mean was 3.84 (SD = 1.16), indicating a generally high level of self-reported confidence.

Regarding access to additional support during the course, 79% (n = 111) of students responded "Yes", while 21% (n = 30) indicated that support was not available. However, when asked whether they personally sought help, the most frequent response was "No", as reflected by mode 2.

Distribution of experience levels

Figure 1 shows the distribution of student experiences with pharmaceutical calculations. The majority of students (75.9%, n = 107) reported a positive experience, 14.9% (n = 21) reported a neutral experience, and 7.0% (n = 10) found it challenging. A small number of students either did not respond (1.41%, n = 2) or indicated uncertainty about their experience (0.7%, n = 1). These results suggest that most students viewed their experience positively, although some faced difficulties or uncertainty.

Overall experience with pharmaceutical calculations

A comparison of experiences by gender revealed that 77% of male students and 75% of the female students reported positive experiences (Fig. 1). A Chi-squared test was conducted to determine whether this difference was statistically significant. The results showed no significant difference in experience based on gender (χ2 = 0.801, p = 0.670), suggesting that male and female students had similar perceptions of their pharmaceutical calculation training.

Confidence in performing pharmaceutical calculations

Students were asked to rate their confidence in performing pharmaceutical calculations using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not confident, 5 = very confident). The responses varied between male and female students (Fig. 2). Among the male students (n = 69), 4.3% rated their confidence as 1, 7.2% as 2, 21.7% as 3, 31.9% as 4, and 34.8% as 5. Among female students (n = 72), 6.9% rated their confidence as 1, 4.2% as 2, 30.6% as 3, 18.1% as 4, and 40.3% as 5. These results show a broad distribution of confidence levels across both groups, with the most significant proportion of both males and females reporting very high confidence (score of 5), and a slightly higher proportion of females reporting top confidence scores.

Normality and statistical testing for confidence scores

To assess the distribution of confidence scores, histograms were initially reviewed, revealing non-normally distributed patterns. This was confirmed through the Shapiro–Wilk test, which showed significant deviations from normality for both males (W = 0.854, p < 0.001) and females (W = 0.826, p < 0.001). As the assumption of normality was violated, a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare the confidence levels by gender. The test yielded U = 254.50 with a p-value of 0.984, indicating no statistically significant difference in confidence levels between male and female students, despite minor differences in the distribution.

Access to and utilization of academic support

Students were asked about the availability and use of academic support during their pharmaceutical calculations' coursework. A total of 79% reported that extra support was available, whereas 21% reported that it was not. When analyzed by gender, a slightly higher proportion of males (84%) reported access to support compared to females (75%). However, only 45% of all students reported that they personally sought or used this support, whereas 55% did not (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that although academic support was largely available, many students did not actively utilize it.

Resources used for pharmaceutical calculations

The students were asked to indicate the resources or tools they used to assist with pharmaceutical calculations. Notably, 38% of students did not respond to this question. Among those who responded, 29% reported using digital tools and online platforms, including ChatGPT, YouTube, Google, and Microsoft Excel. University-provided materials, including lecture slides and peer or faculty support, were cited by 20% of students. Traditional resources, such as textbooks and pharmacopeia, were the least used, mentioned by 19%. These results suggest a preference for accessible technology-based tools over conventional academic materials.

Perceived relevance to future careers

When asked about the perceived relevance of pharmaceutical calculations to their future careers, the majority of students (73%) indicated that these skills would be important in their professional practice. A smaller proportion expressed uncertainty (14%), while 11% believed that such skills would not be necessary. Only a minimal percentage did not respond (2%).

These findings suggest that most students recognize the importance of pharmaceutical calculations; however, the presence of uncertainty and negative responses indicates a need to further emphasize the applicability of these skills across diverse pharmacy career pathways, beyond traditional clinical or hospital settings.

Student suggestions for course improvement

Students were asked to suggest improvements to the pharmaceutical calculations course. Approximately 36% of the respondents did not provide any suggestions. Several themes emerged among those who responded. One group of students expressed satisfaction with the current program, while others recommended specific changes. Among students who provided suggestions, the most frequently reported theme was positive feedback (19%), followed by improving teaching methods (14%) and incorporating more practical and real-world applications (11%). Fewer responses emphasized additional practice materials and resources (10%) or collaborative learning (3%), while 7% of students indicated no suggestions. Some students also advocated simplifying the course content and focusing more on essential core concepts rather than on extensive theoretical details. The responses are summarized in Fig. 4.

Distribution of student suggestions for improving the pharmaceutical calculations course (N = 141). Responses were categorized into thematic areas

A Chi-square test was used to determine whether the types of suggestions varied significantly by gender. The result (p = 0.9968, Cramer’s V = 0.0879) indicated no significant association and a very weak relationship between gender and suggestion category. Both male (20.0%) and female (16.7%) students expressed an interest in reduced content density and improved instructional delivery. Overall, the responses from both genders were aligned, with a common emphasis on increasing practical relevance and reducing theoretical overload.

Discussion

Overview and context

Pharmaceutical calculations are a fundamental competency in pharmacy education, with direct implications for patient safety and accuracy of clinical care. This study examined confidence, resource utilization, and perceptions of preparedness among senior PharmD students at KSU in clinical practice. These findings provide insight into a growing global conversation, as numeracy and calculation proficiency are increasingly emphasized by accreditation bodies and professional standards (Alrabiah 2022).

The results indicate a notable discrepancy between students’ self-reported confidence and their engagement with support resources, rather than their perceived applicability. While the majority of respondents reported moderate to high confidence in performing pharmaceutical calculations, most students (73%) also recognized their relevance to future pharmacy practice. However, a notable proportion expressed uncertainty (14%) or perceived these skills as unnecessary (11%). In addition, although 79% of students reported the availability of academic support, more than half (55%) did not utilize these resources, suggesting a potential overestimation of perceived readiness or limited self-recognition of learning needs. These numerical patterns highlight that perceived confidence does not necessarily translate into engagement with available support resources or awareness of learning needs.

Student confidence and experience

Most students reported positive experiences and high levels of self-reported confidence in their calculation skills. This pattern is consistent with global literature describing pharmacy students’ confidence in pharmaceutical calculations, while recognizing that confidence does not necessarily equate to objective competence or curricular effectiveness (Alrabiah 2022). For example, Barry et al. reported a mismatch between student self-perception and actual performance, with high scores achieved on registration exams despite the reported insecurity, highlighting the complex relationship between confidence and performance (Barry et al. 2007).

The absence of gender-based differences in reported experience and confidence reflects findings from prior studies suggesting that gender is not a significant predictor of reported confidence or experience when curricular exposure is equal. Instead, factors such as prior mathematical preparation, engagement with academic support resources, and perceived preparedness appear to play a more influential role in shaping students’ confidence (Wongwiwatthananukit 2001).

Interestingly, the relatively high self-reported confidence observed among KSU students contrasts with findings from other studies in which confidence levels were more moderate, particularly when evaluated using performance-based rather than self-assessment measures (McCafferty and Nadithe 2018). This discrepancy suggests that institutional factors, such as course structure, assessment methods, and educational context, may influence how students perceive and report their abilities.

Resources utilization and learning preferences

A notable trend in this study was the preference for digital tools, such as calculators, ChatGPT, YouTube, and Excel, over traditional learning resources such as textbooks and pharmacopeias. This mirrors global shifts in pharmacy education, in which technology-enhanced learning is favored because of its accessibility and flexibility. Prior studies have found that video-based and digital learning tools can significantly improve student confidence and performance (Ghazal 2024).

However, heavy reliance on digital tools can raise concerns about the retention of foundational skills. Alrabiah et al. found that even mathematically strong students often default to calculators, which may weaken their manual calculation proficiency (Alrabiah 2022). This suggests the importance of maintaining instructional balance, reinforcing both conceptual understanding and the use of practical tools.

Curriculum design: standalone vs. integrated courses

The findings from this study provide insight into students’ perceptions of their preparation in pharmaceutical calculations within an integrated curriculum; however, they do not, on their own, offer direct evidence in favor of dedicated, standalone pharmaceutical calculations courses. McCafferty et al. demonstrated that students in standalone formats performed better in both objective structured clinical exams (OSCEs) and long-term retention assessments than those in integrated courses (McCafferty and Nadithe 2018).

Moreover, repeated practice and spaced assessments across the curriculum have been shown to support lasting proficiencies. Hegener et al. reported that improvements from a single course persisted for over six months, although continued reinforcement was necessary to maintain these gains (Hegener et al. 2013).

Relevance to clinical practice and curriculum evolution

Although most students acknowledged the relevance of pharmaceutical calculations in clinical and hospital pharmacy settings, a notable proportion expressed uncertainty regarding how these skills apply across broader pharmacy roles. While this study does not assess the real-world use or clinical relevance of specific calculation types, similar concerns about perceived relevance have been discussed in the pharmacy education literature (Smith and Kinsey 2025). These discussions emphasize the importance of aligning calculation instruction with clearly articulated practice contexts rather than informing decisions about curriculum content or prioritization. From an educational perspective, such perceptions do not provide evidence for determining which calculation topics should be retained or removed from the core curriculum; however, they are important for understanding how students interpret the relevance of foundational skills and how effectively these skills are contextualized during instruction. Aligning pharmaceutical calculation teaching with explicit clinical and practice-based examples may help bridge this perceived gap and enhance learning transfer.

Accrediting bodies such as ACPE emphasize mastery of pharmaceutical calculations directly related to patient care, dosing, and compounding, as well as the use of authentic, case-based assessment strategies to promote engagement and reduce math-related anxiety (Minty-Walker 2024; Education 2025). Within this framework, the present findings support the need for improved instructional framing and contextualization of pharmaceutical calculations, rather than advocating curriculum modification based on calculation type.

Student suggestions and educational implications

Students recommended more practical, real-world applications and simplified teaching approaches. These preferences are consistent with the evidence supporting active and contextual learning methods. For example, Anderson et al. found that flipped classrooms and problem-solving tasks lead to greater knowledge retention and improved performance (Anderson 2017; Hogan and Lundquist 2006).

Some students also suggested the use of early diagnostic assessments and targeted support; a strategy echoed in literature. Predictive analytics and personalized interventions can help identify at-risk students early and provide timely remediation (Sharif 2007; Feild 2023).

To translate these findings into practice at the university level, student perspectives may inform future consideration of instructional enhancements within pharmacy programs. pharmacy programs can utilize existing governance, training, and quality assurance structures. Curriculum committees can formally map pharmaceutical calculation competencies to program learning outcomes and approve syllabus revisions that emphasize applied learning objectives, case-based activities, and performance-based assessments such as OSCE-style calculation tasks, in line with outcome-based education principles outlined in ACPE Standards 2025 (Education, A.C.f.P. 2025). Implementation can be further supported through targeted faculty development initiatives delivered by teaching and learning centers, focusing on active-learning strategies, case design, and the pedagogical use of digital tools, as recommended by both ACPE and international pharmacy education frameworks (Federation, I.P. 2017). At the institutional level, early low-stakes diagnostic assessments can be embedded into academic policy to identify at-risk students and trigger structured support pathways, including mandatory tutorials or peer-assisted learning, thereby reducing reliance on voluntary self-referral (Feild 2023).

Finally, integrating pharmaceutical calculation outcomes into program-level assessment and continuous quality improvement cycles using student feedback, performance data, and periodic curriculum review may support sustainability and alignment with evolving accreditation and professional standards (Education, A.C.f.P. 2025; Federation, I.P. 2017).

Aligning student-identified challenges with curriculum-based solutions

One of the challenges identified in this study was the presence of uncertainty among some students regarding the real-world relevance of pharmaceutical calculation, despite their central role in safe medication use, dose individualization, and compounding accuracy. To address this, calculation instruction may benefit from being more explicitly anchored to authentic practice contexts (e.g., renal dose adjustment, infusion-rate verification, dilution/compounding accuracy checks), with explicit “patient-safety implications” stated for each calculation domain. This approach aligns with ACPE Standards 2025, which emphasize graduates being “practice-ready” and prepared for patient-centered care through applied competence (Education 2025).

From our findings, students’ request for increased practical application supports a shift from predominantly procedural instruction toward structured active-learning formats. Evidence from pharmacy education demonstrates that redesigning large lecture courses into interactive, small-group discussion/problem-solving formats can replace passive delivery with higher-order learning activities and improved student engagement (Persky and Pollack 2010).

The observed reliance on digital tools, including AI-enabled platforms, should be addressed through a guided-use approach rather than prohibition. This aligns with the opportunities-and-risks of large language models in education, which highlights the need for critical evaluation to mitigate overreliance and error propagation (Kasneci 2023).

Although academic support may be available, its underutilization suggests the need for structured triggers that normalize early engagement. A pragmatic curricular response could include early interventions (e.g., scheduled tutorials, peer-assisted sessions, and structured practice sets) for students falling below a defined threshold. This approach operationalizes student feedback into an implementable support model consistent with competency-oriented curricular expectations (Education, A.C.f.P. 2025).

Finally, the potential discrepancy between self-reported confidence and competence suggests the potential value of incorporating performance-based checkpoints (e.g., OSCE-style applied stations, simulation-based error-identification tasks) to triangulate self-report with observed performance. Simulation-based and OSCE-aligned assessment approaches are well established across health professions education and increasingly emphasized in pharmacy training as part of integrated competency assessment (Ayoub J et al 2022).

Limitations and research gaps

This study relied on self-reported data collected from a single cohort of senior PharmD students at one institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and may not fully reflect actual performance. Although inclusion of the full eligible cohort reduces internal selection bias, cohort-specific factors such as teaching style, assessment design, and curricular sequencing may have influenced student perceptions. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as context-specific and exploratory rather than generalizable evidence of student preparedness or competence.

Future research should triangulate subjective perceptions with objective performance measures, such as examination results, OSCE scores, or simulated error rates. Additionally, multi-cohort, multi-institutional, and longitudinal studies are needed to assess the consistency, retention, and real-world impact of pharmaceutical calculation skills on clinical performance and patient safety outcomes (Feild 2023; Ayoub 2022).

Conclusion

This study examined the experiences, confidence, resource utilization, and perceptions of senior pharmacy students regarding pharmaceutical calculations at KSU. Most students reported high confidence and positive experiences, with many acknowledging their access to academic support. However, some have expressed uncertainty about the practical relevance of these skills in future roles.

A clear preference emerged for digital learning tools and practice-oriented content, with students suggesting real-world applications, simplified instruction, and targeted support. These findings provide insight into student learning preferences and perceptions, highlighting the importance of balancing conceptual skill-building with accessible technology and practice-based learning.

In addition to high self-reported confidence, this study identified several learning and teaching barriers that may limit the effective development of pharmaceutical calculation skills. These barriers included uncertainty among some students regarding the real-world applicability of calculations, insufficient exposure to practice-based and clinically contextualized examples, heavy reliance on digital tools rather than foundational calculation skills, and underutilization of available academic support despite its accessibility. These factors may contribute to a disconnect between perceived confidence and practical readiness for professional practice.

To better prepare students for clinical responsibilities, pharmacy programs may consider implementing early diagnostic assessments, integrating pharmaceutical calculations into case-based instruction, and revisiting the curriculum content to ensure alignment with modern practice expectations. These measures may help support the development of graduates who are not only confident but also better prepared to apply pharmaceutical calculations in real-world settings.
