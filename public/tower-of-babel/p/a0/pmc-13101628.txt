Oral vaccines for rainbow trout: Promising solutions to combat bacterial and viral pathogens in aquaculture
Authors: Mahboobeh Nazari, Mahmood Jeddi-Tehrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral vaccination represents a transformative approach for disease prevention in rainbow trout aquaculture, offering the promise of mass immunization with minimal handling stress and improved welfare. As rainbow trout remain a cornerstone species in global aquaculture, infectious diseases such as bacterial (e.g., Yersinia ruckeri, Aeromonas salmonicida) and viral (e.g., IHNV, VHSV) pathogens pose persistent threats to productivity and sustainability. Traditional injection and immersion vaccines, while effective, are labor-intensive and stressful, especially for large-scale operations. Oral vaccines, if optimized, could revolutionize disease management by enabling scalable, welfare-friendly immunization strategies. Oral vaccination offers economic and welfare advantages, but efficacy must be optimized to match injection methods. Mechanized feed delivery and improved formulations are key to enhancing cost-effectiveness.Common pain points for oral vaccines in rainbow trout include significant antigen degradation in the gastrointestinal tract, leading to diminished vaccine efficacy; the risk of oral tolerance induction due to repeated low-dose exposure, which may blunt immune responses; and lower cost-effectiveness due to variable protection compared to injection methods. Cutting-edge trends in this field involve the use of novel encapsulation strategies (alginate-chitosan, PLGA, starch hydrogels), bio-encapsulation with live feeds like Artemia salina, recombinant bacterial vectors delivering DNA vaccines, and the emerging application of mRNA- lipid nanoparticle vaccine platforms, all aimed at enhancing mucosal immune responses while overcoming stability and tolerance challenges. The most important immunity in oral vaccination of rainbow trout involves a combination of robust humoral and cellular responses. Key elements include the production of specific antibodies (IgM and IgT), activation of T-cells, and upregulation of cytokines. While oral vaccines require higher doses and may benefit from booster administrations, they offer significant protection and are a promising strategy for enhancing fish health in aquaculture.

Introduction

Oral vaccination represents a practical and non-invasive strategy for immunizing rainbow trout against a range of pathogens, though its efficacy depends strongly on both antigen dosage and the biology of the target organism [1]. Oral vaccination offers a practical and less labor-intensive alternative to injectable immunization for protecting rainbow trout against viral pathogens, and a growing body of research demonstrates its potential despite notable challenges. Experiences with enteric redmouth disease (ERM) highlight this variability. While oral administration of Yersinia ruckeri bacterin can stimulate protective immunity, it typically requires substantially higher antigen loads compared with parenteral routes [2]. High-dose oral delivery has achieved full protection in some studies, whereas lower doses yield inconsistent outcomes, and even primary immunization followed by an oral booster may produce weak responses, possibly due to oral tolerance [3]. In contrast, the development of a starch hydrogel-based oral vaccine for furunculosis caused by Aeromonas salmonicida has produced more encouraging findings [4]. Repeated dosing markedly elevated antibody titers and improved survival, demonstrating that booster administration can effectively strengthen mucosal and systemic immunity. Oral vaccination strategies against infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) have also advanced, particularly through DNA vaccines delivered via bacterial vectors or yeast surface-display systems [5,6]. These platforms activate innate and adaptive responses, inducing notable upregulation of immune-related genes and yielding higher post-challenge survival. Even so, their protective efficacy remains lower than that achieved with conventional injectable DNA vaccines, underscoring both the promise and the current limitations of oral immunization approaches in aquaculture.

Rainbow trout can be immunized against a variety of pathogens through several administration routes, each offering distinct advantages in terms of practicality and immune efficacy. Injection-based delivery remains the most reliable method, with intramuscular (IM) vaccination widely applied for DNA vaccines such as those targeting infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) and consistently producing strong and durable protection [[7], [8], [9]]. Intraperitoneal (IP) injection is similarly effective and frequently used for both viral and bacterial vaccines, providing high levels of protection against diseases including enteric redmouth disease (ERM) and IHNV [10,11]. Immersion techniques, such as bath and dip vaccination, offer less labor-intensive alternatives suitable for large-scale aquaculture. Bath immersion exposes fish to diluted vaccine solutions and can elicit meaningful mucosal immune responses, whereas dip immersion, using more concentrated preparations for shorter exposure times, has demonstrated substantial protective capacity and is often employed as a primary vaccination strategy [3,10,12]. Oral administration represents an attractive option for mass vaccination, as vaccines can be incorporated into feed or applied as sprays or coatings, enabling easy distribution in production settings (Table 1). Although oral delivery has been explored for DNA vaccines, live vectors, and bacteriophage-coated formulations, it generally requires higher doses and further optimization such as encapsulation to enhance stability and immune stimulation [5,9,11,13,14]. Nasal vaccination has emerged as another practical non-invasive approach, capable of inducing rapid and robust immune responses against both bacterial and viral pathogens, thus bridging the gap between ease of administration and immunological performance [[15], [16], [17]]. Comparatively, injection methods produce the most consistent and long-lasting immunity, while immersion techniques offer operational efficiency with somewhat reduced efficacy [10,18,19]. Oral and nasal routes provide practical alternatives for large-scale operations, though their effectiveness can vary and may depend on formulation improvements. Range of antigens used in oral vaccines for rainbow trout

A wide spectrum of antigens has been explored for the oral vaccination of rainbow trout, reflecting efforts to protect against diverse bacterial, viral, and parasitic pathogens. Among bacterial antigens, Lactococcus garvieae has been administered in both encapsulated and non-encapsulated forms, with alginate-based encapsulation demonstrating enhanced protection when used for primary or booster immunizations [24,25]. For Y. ruckeri, various formulations incorporating lipopolysaccharides and formalin-killed cells within alginate-chitosan micro- and nanoparticles have successfully elicited both specific and non-specific immune responses [1,2,26]. Similarly, formalin-killed Aeromonas salmonicida cells have been evaluated at different antigen concentrations to optimize vaccine efficacy [27,28], while immunogenic components of Flavobacterium psychrophilum have also been identified and shown to confer substantial protection [29]. Viral antigens have been incorporated through DNA vaccines encoding the glycoprotein G of infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV), delivered using bacterial carriers such as Salmonella enterica and Lactobacillus casei, both of which induced strong immune responses and notable levels of protection [5,30,31]. Parasitic antigens have likewise been investigated, with subunit vaccines targeting Ichthyophthirius multifiliis achieving partial yet meaningful protective outcomes [32]. In addition to single-pathogen vaccines, polyvalent formulations combining antigens from pathogens including Y. ruckeri, A. salmonicida, Listonella anguillarum, and Renibacterium salmoninarum have produced levels of protection comparable to monovalent vaccines, along with evidence of cross-protection [28] (Table 2).Table 2Range of antigens used in oral vaccines for rainbow trout.Table 2 dummy alt textPathogenAntigen TypeFormulationEffectivenessRefLactococcus garvieaeEncapsulated/Non-encapsulated cellsAlginate-encapsulatedEffective as booster[24,25]Y. ruckeriLPS, Formalin-killed bacteria (FKC)Alginate-chitosan micro/nanoparticlesEnhanced immune response[1,2,26]Aeromonas salmonicidaFormalin-killed bacteriaVarious concentrationsImproved efficacy with higher concentrations[27,28]Flavobacterium psychrophilumImmunogenic regionsElectro-elution, emulsified with FCASignificant protection[29]Infectious Hematopoietic Necrosis Virus (IHNV)Glycoprotein G (DNA vaccine)Bacterial vectors (Salmonella, Lactobacillus)Significant immune response[5,30,31]Ichthyophthirius multifiliisRecombinant proteinsSubunit vaccinePartial protection[32]Multiple pathogensVarious antigensPolyvalent vaccinesComparable to monovalent, cross-protection[28]

Specific viral pathogens targeted by oral vaccination in rainbow trout

Oral vaccination in rainbow trout has been extensively explored as a practical strategy for controlling major viral pathogens that threaten salmonid aquaculture, with particular emphasis on infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV), infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV), and viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV). The immunization of rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) against viral pathogens frequently relies on the use of whole viruses either inactivated or attenuated as effective vaccine platforms. Inactivated whole-virus vaccines, produced through treatments such as formaldehyde, binary ethylenimine (BEI), or β-propiolactone (BPL) have been widely applied against infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV), with BPL-inactivated formulations achieving the highest relative percent survival (RPS) values among tested preparations [33]. Attenuated live virus vaccines have also shown strong immunogenicity and practicality for large-scale aquaculture, particularly when delivered by immersion. Live attenuated IHNV vaccines consistently induce robust immune responses and high levels of protection [12,15] and advances in reverse genetics have enabled the development of recombinant viruses with modified genomes, offering a pathway to safer and more cost-effective live vaccine candidates [34]. Recombinant viral vaccines further expand these possibilities, such as IHNV constructs engineered to express the VP2 protein of infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV), which have produced strong protection and substantial reductions in viral load following immunization [35,36]. Complementing these whole-virus approaches, DNA vaccines encoding viral glycoproteins have demonstrated exceptional efficacy against rhabdoviruses including viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV) and IHNV, eliciting both early nonspecific antiviral responses and durable antigen-specific immunity [[37], [38], [39]] (Table 3). Several strategies have been evaluated for infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV), where oral delivery of a DNA vaccine including attenuated Salmonella enterica vectors carrying the viral glycoprotein gene and yeast cell surface display systems that present the antigen in a highly immunogenic form [5,6,41]. This approach activates both innate and adaptive immunity characterized by strong upregulation of antiviral markers such as IFN-1, Mx-1, and IgM elevating serum neutralizing antibodies and upregulating key immune-related genes and lead to meaningful reductions in mortality following viral challenge [6,30]. Genetically engineered Lactobacillus casei expressing viral antigens induces strong mucosal and systemic immunity, accompanied by significant reductions in viral load. Phage-based delivery systems incorporated into feed pellets offer an additional platform, although their success depends on achieving appropriate dosing. Live-feed bioencapsulation, particularly using Artemia salina, provides an efficient route for antigen transfer by exploiting natural feeding behaviors to promote intestinal uptake [42]. Yeast-based systems, especially Pichia pastoris, further expand the repertoire of oral carriers. These platforms function as robust antigen delivery vehicles capable of triggering rapid innate responses in the gut followed by systemic activation in lymphoid tissues, whether administered directly to juveniles or incorporated into live-feed strategies for larvae [43]. Yeast cell-surface display technology extends this approach by eliciting strong mucosal immunity against viral pathogens such as infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus. Recombinant baculovirus-derived IHNV glycoproteins administered through feed have similarly reduced mortality [31], albeit with more modest protective effects, while live-vector vaccines containing IHNV glycoprotein regions administered by immersion have induced robust mucosal immunity and yielded exceptionally high survival rates [12]. Efforts to combat infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV) have also advanced through the development of genetically engineered Lactobacillus casei expressing viral VP2 protein, which elicits strong mucosal and systemic responses, significantly lowers viral loads, and enhances immune gene expression following oral administration [44]. Molecular vaccination strategies have likewise diversified oral antigen formats. Alginate-plasmid complexes encoding VP2 or other viral antigens for infectious pancreatic necrosis virus induce strong immune activation and high survival rates, while DNA vaccines targeting Ichthyophthirius multifiliis or viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus stimulate pathogen-specific antibody responses and modulate key immune genes [32]. Recombinant subunit vaccines based on parasite proteins provide partial yet biologically meaningful protection, and starch hydrogel-based DNA formulations enhance immunogenicity against furunculosis, with booster doses significantly increasing antibody reactivity and immune gene expression. For VHSV, oral delivery of recombinant Lactococcus lactis encoding the VHSV G gene has been shown to increase immune gene expression and specific antibody production, conferring substantial protection against infection [45]. Recognizing that IHNV and IPNV frequently co-occur in aquaculture settings, researchers have also developed bivalent oral vaccines using recombinant adenoviruses or other vectors capable of expressing antigens from both viruses simultaneously. These dual-target vaccines offer improved disease management by providing broad protection and significantly lowering mortality rates in co-infected fish populations [35,36,46] (Table 4). These findings highlight the advancements in oral vaccination strategies for rainbow trout, targeting key viral pathogens to enhance fish health and reduce economic losses in aquaculture. Collectively, these advances highlight the growing potential of oral vaccination to serve as a practical, scalable, and immunologically effective tool for managing viral diseases in rainbow trout. Despite these successes, major limitations persist, particularly the degradation of unprotected antigens in the stomach, which necessitates higher oral doses to achieve protective immunity comparable to parenteral vaccines. Technological advances overcoming the challenges for oral vaccination in rainbow trout

Conventional bacterins continue to occupy a practical niche in oral vaccination of rainbow trout, particularly when high antigen loads are used. Formalin-killed Y. ruckeri preparations administered at sufficiently high doses can confer full protection, provided that a critical fraction of the antigen survives gastrointestinal passage. In contrast, bacterins derived from pathogens such as Aeromonas salmonicida or Vibrio anguillarum generally show reduced efficacy at lower doses and often require adjuvantation to sustain protective immunity. To broaden coverage within intensive aquaculture systems, feed-based polyvalent formulations have been developed that combine antigens from multiple bacterial species, including Vibrio spp., Streptococcus agalactiae, A. hydrophila, A. salmonicida, Listonella anguillarum, and Renibacterium salmoninarum. These multicomponent vaccines enable cross-protection and offer a more comprehensive defensive strategy under field conditions [28].

Oral vaccination has become an attractive approach for the control of viral diseases in fish because it enables large-scale immunization while minimizing handling stress, a critical consideration in intensive aquaculture systems [47,48]. Its success, however, depends on overcoming a set of interrelated biological and technical barriers, foremost among them the rapid degradation of vaccine antigens within the gastrointestinal tract. Acidic gastric conditions and digestive enzymes can inactivate viral antigens before they reach immune-inductive sites in the intestine, substantially reducing efficacy [49]. To counter this, a range of encapsulation systems, including alginate, chitosan, and other biodegradable microsphere-based formulations, have been developed to protect antigens during gastric transit and promote their release in the distal gut, where uptake by mucosal immune cells is more efficient. In parallel, more advanced molecular designs, such as fusion proteins that couple viral peptides to gut-adhesive domains, have been introduced to enhance antigen stability and facilitate closer interaction with the intestinal epithelium, thereby improving mucosal absorption and immune priming [47].These strategies reflect a broader shift toward addressing the fundamental gastrointestinal constraints that limit oral vaccine performance, namely enzymatic degradation, inefficient mucosal uptake, and the limited durability of induced immune responses. As a result, encapsulation technologies have become central to contemporary oral vaccine development, serving not only as protective carriers but also as modulators of antigen delivery and immune engagement within the fish gut [24,42].

The physicochemical properties of encapsulating matrices govern antigen stability and release during transit from the acidic stomach to the intestine. Systems based on alginate-chitosan interactions, PEG inclusion, lipid or oil coatings, protein-polysaccharide composites, and polymeric carriers such as PLGA differ substantially in release kinetics and protective capacity, reflecting both material composition and interactions with diet and host physiology. Dietary protein and lipid ratios, for example, influence gastrointestinal blood flow and digestive enzyme expression, thereby affecting the timing and extent of antigen release and absorption [50].

Alginate, chitosan, and polyethylene glycol remain widely used because of their acid stability and adjustable release behavior. Alginate-chitosan systems exhibit strong retention at low pH, which is critical during gastric transit, whereas PEG inclusion lowers retention while increasing intestinal release [50]. Sodium alginate and PLGA effectively protect antigens from digestion while permitting controlled release, resulting in significant protection against pathogens such as Lactococcus garvieae, with relative percentage survival often exceeding 50 % [25]. Hybrid formulations further optimize performance. Alginate-bentonite-chitosan composites improve encapsulation efficiency and regulate intestinal protein release [51], while sodium alginate-soy protein isolate matrices coated with carboxymethyl cellulose combine pH-responsive swelling with enhanced mucoadhesion, reducing wash-off and supporting site-specific delivery [48]. Lipid-based carriers add complementary advantages. Solid lipid microparticles based on trimyristin or tristearin show minimal gastric release, with trimyristin providing superior intestinal delivery [50]. PLGA microparticles extend these benefits through sustained release over several days, as demonstrated for gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs, supporting their use in long-acting oral delivery strategies in aquaculture [52]. High-protein diets tend to delay peak postprandial hyperemia while prolonging absorption, whereas balanced diets support more rapid release. Protein source further modifies digestive dynamics; plant-based formulations alter the expression of digestive enzymes and nutrient transporters compared with fishmeal diets, with downstream effects on release kinetics [62].

Beyond passive protection and controlled release, encapsulation platforms increasingly serve as immunological enhancers. Alginate-based formulations used as oral boosters following intraperitoneal priming substantially increase survival, an effect amplified when alginate is combined with chitosan to generate micro- or nanoparticles with enhanced mucoadhesion and uptake. Such systems markedly strengthen both specific and nonspecific immunity against Y. ruckeri, reflected by elevated antibody titers, reduced mortality, and robust induction of immune-related genes [3]. The inclusion of aluminum-based adjuvants within chitosan carriers further potentiates responses, driving strong upregulation of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-12, and IFN-γ alongside increased immunoglobulin expression [63]. Potent mucosal immunostimulants have also gained attention. Lipopolysaccharide and engineered enterotoxin derivatives, notably Escherichia coli LT(R192G/L211A), amplify transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and antimicrobial peptides, thereby enhancing the immunogenicity of orally delivered antigens [64].

Parallel advances exploit biological carriers to actively engage the mucosal immune system. Probiotics and recombinant bacterial strains, including Kocuria SM1, modulate both cellular and humoral responses and have shown efficacy in controlling vibriosis [65]. Probiotic supplementation, such as with Pediococcus acidilactici, can shift amino acid profiles in serum and feces, implying altered nutrient metabolism and potentially modified release behavior [46]. Superimposed on these factors, the intestinal microbiome contributes an additional layer of regulation by shaping nutrient digestion and epithelial uptake through its metabolic activity [47].

Taken together, these developments illustrate the rapid maturation of oral vaccination strategies for rainbow trout. Progress in encapsulation chemistry, molecular platforms, microbial and yeast vectors, nanoformulations, and polyvalent feed-based vaccines is steadily transforming oral immunization from a convenient alternative into a central component of sustainable disease control in aquaculture, with clear implications for fish health, production efficiency, and long-term industry resilience.

Mechanisms of action and role of mucosal antigens in oral vaccination of fish species

Once administered, oral vaccines are capable of inducing coordinated systemic and mucosal immune responses, extending their effects well beyond the intestinal lumen. Increases in serum and tissue IgM, together with the upregulation of antiviral and immune regulatory genes, indicate that oral delivery can activate systemic immune pathways alongside local defenses. These responses can be further amplified by adjuvants such as flagellin C (FlaC), which enhances cytokine expression and enzymatic activity, thereby strengthening downstream immune signaling [68]. At the same time, improving antigen uptake across the intestinal epithelium remains a central objective. Natural inflammatory enhancers, including capsaicin have been explored as transient modulators of epithelial permeability, promoting transepithelial transport of vaccine particles and improving access to underlying immune cells.

Mucosal antigens are therefore central to effective oral vaccination strategies in fish, as they directly engage the extensive mucosal immune system distributed across the gut, skin, gills, and nasal tissues [69]. These interfaces continuously exposed to waterborne pathogens are equipped with well-developed mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues rich in B cells and diverse immunoglobulin isotypes that support adaptive immunity [70,71]. Successful oral immunization depends on delivering antigens to these surfaces in forms that can be efficiently taken up and recognized, a process often reflected by elevated antibody levels in bile and mucus, signaling activation of local immune defenses. Among teleost immunoglobulins, IgM, IgD, and IgT or IgZ contribute in distinct ways. IgM predominates in systemic immunity, occurring at high concentrations in serum and showing marked upregulation in lymphoid organs such as the spleen and head kidney following infection or vaccination [72,73]. IgD also participates in systemic responses, although its precise function remains less clearly defined. In contrast, IgT is specialized for mucosal immunity and represents the dominant isotype involved in pathogen clearance and maintenance of microbial homeostasis at mucosal surfaces [74].

The pivotal role of IgT in mucosal protection has been demonstrated across multiple teleost species through direct measurement in mucosal tissues. In grass carp, the development of IgT-specific monoclonal antibodies revealed higher IgT concentrations in gill mucus than in serum, with immersion immunization selectively increasing antigen-specific IgT in gill mucus while IgM responses were primarily systemic [75,76]. In half-smooth tongue sole, both membrane-bound and secretory forms of IgT were identified, with broad distribution across skin, gill, intestine and head kidney, and strong induction of IgT transcripts following bacterial infection [77]. Studies in rainbow trout further confirmed this specialization, showing accumulation of IgT-positive B cells and robust bacteria-specific IgT responses in gill lamellae after infection with Flavobacterium columnare [78]. Despite this immunological potential, the gastrointestinal environment poses substantial challenges for oral vaccination. Acidic conditions and digestive enzymes readily degrade unprotected antigens, reducing their availability at immune-inductive sites and increasing the risk of oral tolerance, an undesired state in which repeated antigen exposure leads to immune unresponsiveness. To overcome these barriers, encapsulation strategies and carefully selected adjuvants have been developed to preserve antigen integrity, enhance delivery to target tissues, and bias responses toward active immunity rather than tolerance. At the molecular level, orally delivered vaccines can engage a broad spectrum of immune pathways, including phagosome formation, antigen processing and presentation, and signaling cascades mediated by Nod-like receptors and MAPKs, all of which contribute to a robust mucosal response [67]. Taken together, the ability of oral vaccination to elicit integrated mucosal and systemic immunity, combined with its ease of administration and low associated stress, makes it a particularly attractive and practical approach for disease prevention in aquaculture.

Comparisons between oral and injectable vaccination strategies against viral infections in rainbow trout

Comparisons between oral and injectable vaccination strategies against viral infections in rainbow trout highlight clear distinctions in efficacy, immune activation, and practical application. Oral vaccines developed against infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) have demonstrated moderate protection, with yeast surface-displayed antigens achieving a relative percent survival (RPS) of 45.8 % compared to 2 % in controls [6], and Lactococcus lactis vectors producing dose-dependent RPS values of 62 % and 78 % [45]. Similar approaches for infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV), including oral delivery of genetically engineered Lactobacillus casei expressing VP2, generated strong antiviral responses and significantly reduced viral loads [44]. However, oral immunization against enteric redmouth disease caused by Y. ruckeri typically requires considerably higher antigen doses to achieve protection comparable to other routes [1]. In contrast, injectable vaccines consistently provide stronger and more reliable protection. Intramuscular delivery of a bivalent DNA IHNV vaccine resulted in cumulative mortality of only 6–10 % compared with 90–94 % in mock-vaccinated fish [80], while an injected live-vector IPNV vaccine produced an RPS of 88.24 % [81]. Injection also offers superior protection against Y. ruckeri relative to immersion methods, particularly when adjuvanted formulations are used [82]. These differences reflect distinct immunological profiles. Oral vaccines predominantly stimulate mucosal responses, characterized by upregulation of antiviral and immunoglobulin-related genes such as IFN-1, Mx-1, and IgM [5,6,30,44,45], along with enhanced innate and adaptive markers including cytokines and antibody titers [[4], [5], [6],30,44,45]. Injectable vaccines, by contrast, drive robust systemic immunity, with marked increases in serum IgM and other systemic indicators [17,80,82], and their protective effects often persist for extended durations, in some cases up to 12 months post-vaccination [17]. From a practical standpoint, oral vaccination offers advantages for large-scale aquaculture due to its ease of administration and reduced handling stress [64], yet it frequently requires higher antigen doses and exhibits variable efficacy depending on formulation and delivery conditions [1,3]. Injectable vaccines, though more labor-intensive and potentially stressful with risks of handling-related immunosuppression and mortality [17,64] remain the most consistently effective option, providing high and durable protection with relatively low antigen requirements (Table 8) [80,82]. The concentration of antigens in oral vaccines varies depending on the formulation and the pathogen targeted. For instance, in one study, rainbow trout were fed with experimental diets containing IHNV G or IHNV G-C5a proteins for 2 weeks, followed by a booster 4 weeks later [31]. Another study used Y. ruckeri lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and formalin-killed cells (FKC) encapsulated in alginate-chitosan micro/nanoparticles, administered orally in the first and third weeks [26]. Discussion

Oral vaccination has emerged as a promising strategy for protecting rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) against a range of bacterial and viral pathogens, offering practical advantages in aquaculture through simplified administration and reduced handling stress. For bacterial diseases, oral immunization against Y. ruckeri, the causative agent of enteric red-mouth disease, has shown the capacity to induce protective immunity using formulations such as AquaVac Enteric Redmouth Disease (ERM). Oral or experimental bacterins, although substantially higher antigen doses are required compared with anal administration, and oral boosters generally provide lower efficacy than dip or bath methods. Encapsulation technologies have improved outcomes for Lactococcus garvieae, with alginate-encapsulated vaccines achieving a relative percent survival (RPS) of 50 % when used orally as a primary immunization and increasing to 87 % when administered as a booster following intraperitoneal injection, suggesting their value in extending protection duration. Oral vaccines developed for viral pathogens have also demonstrated encouraging results. For infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV), an oral DNA vaccine employing attenuated Salmonella as a carrier elicited both innate and adaptive immune responses, yielding an RPS of 58.2 %, whereas yeast-based oral vaccines similarly induced strong immune marker upregulation and achieved an RPS of 45.8 %. Genetically engineered Lactobacillus casei expressing the VP2 protein of infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV) has also shown effectiveness, produced robust immune responses and significantly reduced viral loads. Efforts to improve oral vaccine performance have increasingly focused on the use of mucosal adjuvants such as β-glucans, flagellin, CpG, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), with LPS demonstrating particularly strong immunostimulatory potential in rainbow trout. Collectively, these findings highlight oral vaccination as a viable approach for disease control, though its performance remains variable and often dependent on higher antigen doses or the inclusion of potent adjuvants. Continued development of optimized formulations, delivery systems, and adjuvant strategies will be essential to fully realize the potential of oral vaccines in aquaculture.

Oral vaccination in rainbow trout remains attractive for large scale aquaculture, yet its performance is constrained by a set of recurring biological and practical limitations. A central problem is antigen instability within the gastrointestinal tract. Digestive enzymes and low gastric pH substantially reduce the amount of intact antigen that reaches immune inductive sites in the intestine. This has been demonstrated clearly for Y. ruckeri, where an orally delivered bacterin required a dose approximately fiftyfold higher than anal administration to achieve comparable protection, indicating extensive antigen loss during digestion [83]. Closely related to this issue is the risk of oral tolerance. Repeated exposure to low antigen concentrations can bias the mucosal immune system toward tolerance rather than protective immunity, increasing susceptibility to subsequent infection [84]. This phenomenon reflects the physiological role of the intestinal mucosa, which must continuously discriminate between harmful pathogens and a dense background of dietary and commensal antigens, a balance that inherently favors tolerance under suboptimal stimulation conditions [85,86].

From a practical standpoint, these biological constraints translate into economic challenges. High antigen doses or complex formulations are often required to compensate for degradation and tolerance, raising production costs. Comparative studies have shown that although both sodium alginate and PLGA encapsulation can improve vaccine efficacy, sodium alginate is often preferred due to its lower preparation cost while maintaining acceptable immune-protection [53]. Even when formulation and dosing are optimized, immune responses following oral vaccination can remain variable. Oral priming followed by an oral booster has, in several cases, elicited weaker systemic and mucosal responses compared with alternative delivery strategies [87]. Moreover, efficacy depends strongly on pathogen type and on which immune markers are evaluated, with some antigens inducing robust innate responses but limited adaptive protection, or vice versa [88,89].

In response to these limitations, recent research has focused on strategies that actively enhance mucosal immunogenicity rather than merely protecting antigens from degradation. The inclusion of potent mucosal adjuvants such as β-glucans, flagellin, CpG motifs, and bacterial lipopolysaccharide has shown promise in amplifying local and systemic responses, with LPS displaying particularly strong immunostimulatory effects in the fish intestine [86]. In parallel, modified enterotoxins such as the E. coli LT(R192G/L211A) toxoid (dmLT) have emerged as effective and relatively safe adjuvants for oral antibacterial vaccines [85]. More innovative platforms are also gaining attraction. Yeast cell surface display systems have enabled the presentation of viral and bacterial antigens in a particulate form that naturally targets mucosal immunity. Oral yeast-based vaccines against IHNV, for example, have induced clear upregulation of immune related genes and significantly improved post challenge survival [88]. Similarly, attenuated bacterial vectors such as Salmonella enterica have been engineered to deliver DNA vaccines orally, successfully triggering both innate activation and antigen specific responses, as evidenced by broad transcriptional upregulation of immune genes [90]. Finally, dual or combined vaccination strategies are being explored to compensate for the intrinsic weaknesses of single route delivery. Conclusion

Oral vaccination in rainbow trout has emerged as a practical and attractive approach for disease prevention in aquaculture, offering clear advantages in terms of ease of administration and reduced handling stress. Its effectiveness, however, remains closely tied to the ability to protect antigens from gastrointestinal degradation and to compensate for the relatively high doses often required to achieve protective immunity. Advances in formulation, particularly the use of potent adjuvants, protective delivery systems, and appropriately timed booster administrations, have demonstrated that both mucosal and systemic immune responses can be significantly enhanced, resulting in more reliable protection under experimental conditions. At the same time, persistent variability in immune activation and the inherent fragility of orally delivered antigens highlight the need for continued optimization of vaccine design, dosing strategies and administration schedules.

Current evidence underscores both the promise and the limitations of oral vaccination in rainbow trout. While it represents a valuable low-stress alternative to injectable vaccines, consistent induction of strong and durable immunity has not yet been uniformly achieved. Much of the supporting data derives from laboratory-based or pilot-scale farm studies, and large-scale commercial validation remains limited. In addition, economic feasibility is highly context dependent and may vary with farm size, production intensity and regional factors. Regulatory and practical challenges associated with advanced vaccine platforms, including recombinant vectors and mRNA-based systems, are also insufficiently addressed in the existing literature. Finally, key biological factors such as gut microbiota modulation and the mechanisms underlying oral tolerance remain incompletely understood and require deeper mechanistic investigation before they can be reliably exploited in applied vaccine strategies. Continued research integrating immunology, formulation science, microbiology and field-level validation will therefore be essential for translating the potential of oral vaccination into consistent and scalable solutions for rainbow trout aquaculture.
