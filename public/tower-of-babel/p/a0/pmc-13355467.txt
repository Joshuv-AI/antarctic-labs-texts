Comment on “Willingness to adopt preventive measures among international travellers attending a vaccination centre during COVID-19: A cross-sectional study”
Authors: Deepti M. Sati, Vanashri Dhananjay Shinde, Chhaya Agarwal
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by García-Marín et al. examining willingness to adopt preventive measures among international travellers attending a vaccination centre after the reopening of international travel.1 The study provides timely insights into behavioural adaptation during a period of intense public health communication and offers valuable observations regarding the apparent alignment between traveller intentions and expert recommendations.

A notable issue relates to the interpretation of behavioural intention as a surrogate for sustained preventive practice. Although the authors appropriately assessed intended adoption of preventive measures, several behaviours with high reported acceptance, including hand hygiene and mask use, are known to decline substantially over time once immediate public concern diminishes. The study period coincided with heightened global anxiety regarding severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), which may have transiently amplified socially desirable responses. This temporal context becomes particularly relevant because only 8% of participants reported no intended behavioural modification compared with prior travel experiences. Such near-universal behavioural adaptation may therefore reflect acute pandemic-related salience rather than durable integration of preventive habits into long-term travel medicine practice.

The reported equity in preventive intentions also warrants more cautious interpretation. Although education level and occupational class were not statistically associated with most preventive behaviours, the cohort itself was disproportionately composed of travellers with tertiary education and middle-to-high occupational status.2 This composition raises the possibility of restricted socio-economic variability, potentially reducing the ability of multivariable models to detect meaningful inequities. In addition, attendance at a specialised international vaccination centre may itself represent a marker of greater health literacy, healthcare engagement, and financial accessibility. Consequently, the findings may not adequately capture behavioural patterns among travellers who bypass formal pre-travel consultation services, particularly migrants, informal workers, or travellers from resource-constrained backgrounds.

Another clinically relevant observation involves the discordance between high adherence to pandemic-focused measures and lower intention to adopt classical travel medicine precautions such as food and water safety. The proportion of travellers intending to become more careful with food and water remained comparatively modest despite these measures being central to the prevention of enteric infections in intermediate-to-high health-risk destinations.3 This discrepancy suggests that intensive pandemic messaging may have unintentionally narrowed traveller risk perception toward respiratory pathogens while diminishing attention to endemic non-respiratory travel-associated illnesses. Future communication frameworks in travel medicine may therefore benefit from integrating respiratory and destination-specific infectious risk counselling into a unified preventive model rather than emphasizing isolated behavioural domains.

These considerations may help strengthen interpretation of the study findings and support the development of more durable, inclusive, and behaviourally balanced travel-health communication strategies.
