Low‐Barrier Fibrosis Screening in Hepatitis C Treatment: The Decompensated Cirrhosis in Hepatitis C Evaluation Questionnaire
Authors: Hunter Spencer, Barbara Brumbach, Anna Geduldig, Lisa Nelson, Andrew Seaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAchieving hepatitis C virus (HCV) elimination requires innovative paradigms that overcome barriers to treatment, such as requiring pre‐treatment elastography or phlebotomy‐based fibrosis assessment. To identify patients at low risk of advanced fibrosis or decompensated cirrhosis within our HCV treatment programme, we developed and retrospectively assessed a clinical decision tool, the Decompensated Cirrhosis in Hepatitis C Evaluation Questionnaire (DCHEQ). Here we describe the development of DCHEQ and its test characteristics. We conducted a retrospective, nested, case–control study within a cohort of 1743 patients with HCV enrolled in an urban HCV elimination programme. The primary analysis defined cases of decompensated cirrhosis by chart review. A secondary analysis defined cases of advanced fibrosis as those with aspartate aminotransferase (AST)‐platelet ratio index (APRI) > 1.5. The primary outcome was the area under the receiver operator curve (AUC) of the total DCHEQ to predict decompensated cirrhosis. We also compared the AUC of the total DCHEQ to the AUC of components of the DHEQ. For detecting cases of decompensated cirrhosis, the total of all DCHEQ items resulted in a mean AUC = 0.991 (95% CI = 0.963–1.000), which was significantly better than the discrimination of any alternative combination of DCHEQ items (p < 0.00001). For detecting cases of advanced fibrosis, the total DCHEQ score resulted in a mean AUC = 0.921 (95% CI = 0.871–0.966), which was superior to all other DCHEQ item combinations (p < 0.02). DCHEQ exhibited exquisite discrimination between cases of both decompensated cirrhosis and advanced fibrosis versus controls. After prospective validation, DCHEQ could be included in test‐and‐treat HCV treatment paradigms.Keywords: chronic hepatitis C, liver cirrhosis/blood, liver cirrhosis/diagnosis, substance use

Introduction

The hepatitis C virus (HCV) epidemic continues in the United States (U.S.), with 69,800 estimated acute cases and 13,895 associated deaths in 2021 [1]. HCV causes advanced hepatic fibrosis over two to three decades in 20%–25% of those who develop chronic infections with advanced fibrosis preceding complications of end stage liver disease such as decompensated cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma and death.2 The World Health Organization (WHO) goal to eliminate HCV as a public health threat by 2030 requires implementing highly effective Direct Acting Antivirals (DAAs), a group of well‐tolerated oral treatment regimens lasting eight to twelve weeks in people with and without advanced liver disease with cure rates over 90% [2, 3]. DAAs cure infection, prevent severe morbidity related to liver disease and decrease mortality, yet widespread implementation remains poor and inequitable, especially among people who use drugs (PWUD) [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Curing HCV in PWUD correlates with broad societal benefits, including reduced HCV transmission [11], decreased risky substance use [12, 13] and decreased mortality [14].

The U.S. lags behind other nations in progressing to WHO targets with only approximately 30% of people diagnosed with HCV initiating treatment [15]. Many barriers contribute to this among PWUD, including the requirement to complete additional tests after HCV diagnosis [16]. The American Academy for the Study of Liver Disease and Infections Disease Society of America (AASLD/IDSA) joint guidelines recommend utilizing point‐of‐care (POC) diagnosis of HCV viremia to enable same‐day “test‐and‐treat” models of HCV care [17]. In this setting, AASLD/IDSA recommends that fibrosis assessment can be completed concurrent with treatment initiation, for example acquiring phlebotomy to test for biomarkers of hepatic fibrosis (i.e., platelets, transaminases), as long as clinical signs and symptoms of decompensated cirrhosis are excluded. This guidance is based in part on the fact that pangenotypic first‐line DAAs are highly effective in both those with and without hepatic fibrosis. The guidelines recommend evaluating for the presence of decompensated cirrhosis in those at risk, due to a historic association between protease‐inhibitor (PI)‐containing DAA regimens and hepatic decompensation [17, 18, 19, 20]. While there remains considerable debate surrounding whether PI‐associated hepatic decompensation is a real or spurious finding [21, 22], the perceived risk of such events, ongoing presence in the guidelines and existence of related black box warnings may play a significant role in decreased uptake of POC treatment approaches [23].

Screening for decompensated fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis is frequently done with data, such as the Child‐Pugh score, that utilizes results of phlebotomy‐based tests [24]. Acquiring phlebotomy is often stigmatizing, painful and traumatizing for PWUD and can be a barrier to treatment initiation [10, 25]. Newer validated testing strategies can confirm HCV viremia without the use of phlebotomy, can increase testing uptake among PWID [26] and are preferred by patients [27]. POC HCV diagnosis tests and dried blood spot (DBS) tests can confirm active HCV and rule out hepatitis B virus (HBV) and HIV coinfection in community settings, but completing fibrosis assessment and excluding decompensated cirrhosis still requires phlebotomy‐based labs or imaging [10, 26, 28]. In some settings, such as mobile outreach and telehealth programmes, especially those serving rural areas, access to complete medical records, phlebotomy and imaging to confirm hepatic fibrosis status is limited. Yet, in these settings, test‐and‐treat interventions may hold the most promise to achieve HCV elimination. Furthermore, many PWUD with HCV are at very low risk for advanced fibrosis and decompensated cirrhosis, particularly those without risk factors, including older age, extended duration of HCV infection and exposure to other hepatotoxins, especially alcohol [17]. In one cohort of PWUD seeking HCV treatment, age younger than 35 years was highly predictive of a non‐fibrotic liver [29]. This creates a gap: PWUD with HCV are offered treatment contingent on completing labs or imaging that, for many, would not ultimately impact clinical decision making. A tool that reliably excludes decompensated cirrhosis and identifies those at risk of hepatic fibrosis could resolve this gap and permit test‐and‐treat programmes in such settings to expand their impact.

To identify those at low risk of hepatic fibrosis or decompensated cirrhosis utilizing only information available in mobile outreach and telemedicine treatment settings, we developed a clinical decision tool, the Decompensated Cirrhosis in Hepatitis C Evaluation Questionnaire (DCHEQ). Before implementing the DCHEQ in our clinical setting, we retrospectively assessed it within a cohort of patients treated for HCV in our programme. Here we describe the development and test characteristics of the DCHEQ.

Methods

Study Setting and Population

The study was conducted at an HCV elimination programme at Central City Concern, a healthcare for the homeless organization that serves a population with high rates of substance use disorders, injection drug use and homelessness in Portland, Oregon. The programme delivers low‐barrier hepatitis C screening and treatment to hard‐to‐reach populations in Multnomah County through two federally qualified health clinics, 11 supportive housing buildings, a medically supported withdrawal centre, three opioid treatment programmes and multiple street outreach sites. A prospective cohort was created in May of 2017, which collects data assessing the HCV care cascade among all individuals receiving health services, including rates of screening, pre‐treatment evaluation, treatment initiation, treatment completion and sustained viremic response at 12 weeks after treatment. All individuals with a detectable HCV RNA undergo a provider review to assess liver disease status and recommend DAA treatment. Between 2017 and 2022, a cohort including all 1743 HCV viremic patients who were reviewed by a clinician and given HCV treatment recommendations was identified within the electronic medical record for inclusion. This study was conducted as part of quality improvement activities and was determined to be non‐human subjects research by the Oregon Health and Sciences University Institutional Review Board (STUDY00026628).

DCHEQ Development

DCHEQ was developed for use at the POC, in a setting without ready access to medical records, phlebotomy and imaging for fibrosis assessment. We used an evidence‐guided process based on both existing literature and expert clinical experience. The primary aim was to identify those with a known history of advanced liver disease, those with symptoms congruent with decompensated cirrhosis and those with risk factors for advanced liver disease, such as history of alcohol use or increased age [30]. The assessed version of the DCHEQ included four items (the rationale for each of these is described in detail below) with responses assigned points, including: (1) Age (> 40 years = 2 points; ≤ 40 years = 1 point); (2) Recent heavy alcohol (RHA) use (Yes, in the last 3 months = 3 points; Yes, in the last year = 2 points; No = 1 point); (3) Lifetime heavy alcohol (LHA) use (Yes = 2 points; No = 1 point); and (4) Personal history of cirrhosis or symptoms of decompensated cirrhosis (Yes = 8 points, No = 1 point).

DCHEQ is reported as the sum of points (Table 1). Point values were based on clinical evidence and our judgement of each response's contribution to the risk of advanced fibrosis and decompensated cirrhosis. The cut‐off age of 40 is based on the increased rate of progression of fibrosis in those older than 40 years [30] and the minimal risk of fibrosis among those younger than 40 years in one cohort of PWUD [29]. Items related to alcohol use were adapted from the Addiction Severity Index (ASI) Lite, a validated tool for assessing alcohol consumption, where “heavy drinking” is defined as “drinking to intoxication” or five drinks or more in a setting and “regularly” is defined as drinking “three or more times per week, engaging in binge drinking or problematic use in which normal activities are compromised.” [31, 32] Thresholds for current and former drinking were included based on a meta‐analysis of seven cohort studies and two case control studies done in North America, Europe and Asia (n = 2,629,272) that demonstrated consumption of five or more drinks of alcohol per day correlates with a significantly increased risk of cirrhosis in men (RR = 3.80, 95% CI: 0.85–17.02 for 5–6 drinks) and women (RR = 12.44, 95% CI: 6.65–23.27 for 5–6 drinks) [33]. Most studies asked respondents to provide an estimation of the drinks per day or drinks per week they consumed in the last six to twelve months and these cross‐sectional estimates were correlated with cirrhosis. People with previous heavy drinking had an increased risk of cirrhosis compared to long‐term abstainers (RR = 2.52). A lifetime history of five years of regular use is a cut‐off designed to capture people within this group. Specifying between current and recent drinking was included to gauge risk for current alcoholic hepatitis, which could influence clinical decisions regarding further testing or treatment regimen.

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective, nested case–control study design to assess DCHEQ within our HCV treatment centre. To identify cases, we identified all instances within our cohort of patients receiving treatment consistent with decompensated cirrhosis (either 24 weeks in duration or a treatment regimen containing ribavirin) and then one reviewer (AS) reviewed all these charts to assess for a clinical diagnosis of decompensated cirrhosis. Decompensated cirrhosis was defined as a Childs‐Pugh score of 7 or higher [34]. The diagnosis of decompensated cirrhosis was derived from a standardized programmatic pre‐treatment laboratory evaluation (including liver transaminases, albumin, creatinine, bilirubin, platelets), elastography for those clinically felt to be at increased risk of advanced fibrosis, clinical evaluation for complications of advanced liver disease (including hepatic encephalopathy and ascites) and abdominal ultrasound imaging in individuals with laboratory or elastography indicating possible decreased synthetic function or advanced fibrosis. The reviewer had access to the complete medical chart and all charts flagged as potential cases contained adequate clinical information to calculate the Childs‐Turcotte‐Pugh score. Controls were defined as those recommended for HCV treatment with an 8‐ or 12‐week regimen without ribavirin. After removing cases, 200 potential controls were selected from the broader cohort of 1743 HCV viremic participants using a random number generator. These were reviewed sequentially until the pre‐specified enrollment target was reached. Participants were excluded if clinical documentation was insufficient to determine clinical history of cirrhosis, decompensated cirrhosis or alcohol use, as determined by two reviewers (AG, LN or AS) (Figure 1). One reviewer (HS) scored each DCHEQ item for each case, two reviewers (AG or LN) scored each DCHEQ item for each control. If both reviewers agreed, the score was accepted; if there was incongruence, a third reviewer (AS) issued the final score. The sum of scores of DCHEQ items was reported as the DCHEQ score. With 128 controls, the study was powered to demonstrate that DCHEQ detects cases of decompensated cirrhosis with at least 80% specificity at the maximum sensitivity, with a 95% confidence interval of 70%–90%.

Identification of cases and controls.

In a secondary analysis, advanced fibrosis was defined as the aspartate aminotransferase (AST)‐platelet ratio index (APRI) > 1.5. APRI is a validated marker of fibrosis in people with HCV with 95% specificity for advanced fibrosis and 35% sensitivity at this cut‐off [24]. Gold standard diagnosis of fibrosis is usually defined by liver biopsy or transient elastography [24, 35], but neither of these modalities was routinely reported in this cohort. Only four controls had documented transient elastography; thus, we excluded this variable from further chart abstraction. APRI was chosen over the Fibrosis‐4 score (FIB‐4), another validated biomarker of fibrosis, because the inclusion of age in the FIB‐4 score overlaps with the inclusion of age in DCHEQ [24]. Participants were excluded from this analysis if the components of the APRI (AST and platelets) were not fully recorded.

Data Analyses

Linked patient‐level data were de‐identified, and statistical analyses were done using SPSS v 29 and Stata v 18. Independent two‐tailed student's t‐tests were used to compare mean age and DCHEQ scores between study groups. Aspartate AST, platelets and responses to specific DCHEQ items were compared using χ [30] tests. These tests were two‐tailed with alpha set at 0.05 to determine statistical significance. Diagnostic validity in detecting cases of decompensated cirrhosis was assessed by calculating sensitivity and 1‐specificity for each DCHEQ score. Four alternative analyses of modified DCHEQ scores were performed which included DCHEQ items in different combinations, including: (1) age, RHA and LHA (DCHEQ items 1–3); (2) age and RHA (DCHEQ items 1 and 2); (3) age and LHA (DCHEQ items 1 and 3); and (4) age as a continuous variable.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) were computed for DCHEQ. To test for statistically significant differences between ROC curves, comproc Stata routines were used (statistical significance set at 0.05). To estimate AUC, we used a 5‐fold cross‐validation method and report the mean AUCs and corresponding bootstrap bias corrected 95% confidence intervals [36]. Optimal cut‐offs for each curve were defined as the threshold with 100% sensitivity that retained the highest Youden's J index [37]. Diagnostic validity of DCHEQ to detect advanced fibrosis was similarly assessed by sensitivity and 1‐specificity reflected by ROC curves. The same four alternative analyses were completed. In this case, the optimal cut point was defined as the threshold with the highest Youden's J index [37].

Results

Thirty‐five cases of decompensated cirrhosis were identified within the full cohort of 1743 (2.0%). 129 controls were included. We excluded four participants initially selected as controls (Figure 1). The sample was predominantly male (64.6%), about one‐third female (31.1%) and had few transgender participants (1.2%). Mean age was 46.6 years, with cases significantly older than controls (53.5 vs. 46.7 years; p < 0.01). For the total of all DCHEQ items, assigned scores ranged from 4 to 15, with mean score 7.38 (SD = 3.9). Total DCHEQ was higher in cases than in controls (Mean DHCEQ 14.2 vs. 5.5; p < 0.01). No previously unidentified cases were found in the randomly selected control cohort. The assigned score for each item is shown for cases and controls in Table 2. Cases were significantly more likely to be age > 40 years, and report recent and LHA use. All cases had pre‐existing documentation of advanced liver disease compared to only 3 controls.

DCHEQ Discrimination of Decompensated Cirrhosis

For detecting cases of decompensated cirrhosis, the total of all DCHEQ items resulted in a mean AUC = 0.991 (95% CI = 0.963–1.000), which was significantly better than the discrimination of any alternative combination of DCHEQ items or APRI (p < 0.00001) (Figure 2). The AUC when including only age > 40, RHA, and LHA was significantly greater than the AUC when including only age > 40 and LHA (p < 0.00001). However, the AUC when including age > 40 and LHA was not significantly different from the AUC when including age > 40 and RHA (p = 0.12). Including age > 40 and RHA resulted in a mean AUC = 0.854 (95% CI = 0.657–0.870). AUC comparisons between age and all combinations of DCHEQ items were statistically significant at p < 0.05 or smaller. For the total of all DCHEQ items, the optimal cut point was DCHEQ < 12 (sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 97.6%, Youden's J Index = 0.976). For the modified DHCEQ created by including age > 40, RHA, and LHA, the optimal cut point was < 4 (sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 21.7%, Youden's J Index = 0.217). The optimal cut point for the modified DCHEQ created by including age > 40 and RHA was < 3 (sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 30.2%, Youden's J Index = 0.302). The optimal cut point for the modified DCHEQ created by including age > 40 and LHA was < 3 (sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 22.5%, Youden's J Index = 0.225). For age as a continuous variable, the optimal cut point was ≤ 31 years (sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 12.4%, Youden's J Index = 0.124).

DCHEQ Discrimination of Advanced Fibrosis

One participant was excluded from the analysis of advanced fibrosis because labs did not contain sufficient data to calculate APRI. For detecting cases of advanced fibrosis (defined as APRI > 1.5) (Figure 3), the total DCHEQ score resulted in a mean AUC = 0.921 (95% CI = 0.871–0.966), which was superior to all other DECHEQ item combinations (p < 0.02 or smaller). The difference in AUC between the modified DCHEQ created by including age > 40, RHA, and LHA and that created by including only age > 40 and LHA was statistically significant (p < 0.01). However, the AUC from the modified score created by including age > 40 and LHA was not significantly different from that of the modified score created by including age > 40 and RHA (p = 0.14). AUC comparisons between age and all combinations of DCHEQ items were statistically significant at p < 0.05 or smaller. For the total of all DCHEQ items, the optimal cut point was DCHEQ < 8 (sensitivity = 88.2%, specificity = 86.2%, Youden's J Index = 0.744). For the modified DCHEQ including age > 40, RHA, and LHA, the optimal cut point was < 5 (sensitivity = 91.2%, specificity = 67.7%, Youden's J Index = 0.589). The optimal cut point for the modified DCHEQ including age > 40 and RHA was < 4 (sensitivity = 73.5%, specificity = 83.1%, Youden's J Index = 0.566). The optimal cut point for the modified DCHEQ including age > 40 and LHA was < 4 (sensitivity = 82.4%, specificity = 90.8%, Youden's J Index = 0.547). For age as a continuous variable, the optimal cut point was ≤ 46 years (sensitivity = 82.4%, specificity = 54.6%, Youden's J Index = 0.37).

Discussion

The Decompensated Cirrhosis in Hepatitis C Evaluation Questionnaire (DCHEQ) exhibited excellent discrimination between cases of decompensated cirrhosis and controls. Utilizing DCHEQ in HCV treatment to exclude decompensated cirrhosis can simplify HCV treatment, reducing barriers for PWUD. AASLD/IDSA guidelines include a recommendation for rapid POC HCV diagnosis and recommend clinically excluding decompensated cirrhosis prior to treatment initiation [17]. DCHEQ offers a structured approach to excluding decompensated cirrhosis that is achievable in settings with limited access to complete medical records, phlebotomy derived lab values [38] or imaging [35]. Other barriers to same‐day test‐and‐treat models exist, such as the AASLD/IDSA‐recommended screening for HBV coinfection despite the lack of available POC HBV diagnostics in some regions. However, bundling POC HCV diagnosis, DCHEQ and available decentralized HBV screening modalities like DBS testing can obviate the need for pre‐treatment phlebotomy in most cases [27]. DCHEQ could amplify the impact of rapid diagnosis with POC testing by enabling both HCV testing and fibrosis assessment to be completed within minutes, allowing for HCV treatment initiation at the time of diagnosis. Utilizing DCHEQ to facilitate HCV treatment without requiring extensive testing does not replace comprehensive healthcare for PWUD with HCV, but similar programmes which decrease patient burden during treatment show improved HCV cure rates [39, 40, 41].

The relative contribution of individual DCHEQ items to the overall discrimination varied widely. A personal history of advanced liver disease drove the discriminatory performance of the total DCHEQ. This could be explained by several factors. First, the reviewers attempted to identify clinical documentation demonstrating participant self‐knowledge of liver disease in addition to chart‐diagnosed cirrhosis or decompensated cirrhosis, though retrospective chart review likely overpredicted participant knowledge of underlying liver disease. Second, people seeking HCV treatment in this urban clinic may have had more healthcare exposures than average PWUD with HCV and thus be more likely to be aware of pre‐existing liver pathology. However, in alternative analyses that did not include the item regarding history of advanced liver disease, discrimination remained high and decompensated cirrhosis could be ruled out at cut points associated with younger age and alcohol abstinence, which is consistent with known risk factors for hepatic fibrosis [30]. Furthermore, people seeking HCV treatment often have some knowledge about pre‐existing advanced liver disease, since symptoms like variceal bleeding, encephalopathy and ascites are frequently obvious. Thus, it is probable that self‐reported history of advanced liver disease, while less reliable than that assessed by a clinical chart review, could still contribute to the discriminatory performance in real‐world applications.

Recent alcohol use can cause reversible hepatic injury characterized by steatosis, increased liver stiffness and decreased platelet count [42], thus including recent alcohol use in DCHEQ may increase the false positive rate for detecting decompensated cirrhosis. In this study, lifetime history of heavy alcohol use appeared to contribute little to the discriminatory performance in terms of overall AUC and in selecting cut points. This could have been due to the reviewer's ability to identify recent drinking more easily than historic drinking. Future prospective studies of DCHEQ should explore the relative discriminatory power contributed by the questions related to recent and historical alcohol use.

This study has several limitations. As a quality improvement project at a single centre, it has inherent low generalizability. The retrospective, case–control design is subject to selection bias, which was partially addressed by random identification of controls and the nested design. Although dual review was performed, reviewers were not blinded to the outcome so observer bias may have been present. Conclusions regarding the DCHEQ's predictive accuracy for advanced fibrosis are limited due to the use of APRI as the reference standard, which is an inferior standard to elastography. The composition of the DCHEQ also has limitations. Assigning a value of 1, rather than 0, to negative responses makes DCHEQ similar to the numeric scheme used in the Child‐Pugh score, but is arbitrary. We utilized the age 40 years as a cut point with the intent to exclude advanced fibrosis; however, a future iteration of DCHEQ could be designed to rule in advanced fibrosis and use higher age ranges. Finally, DCHEQ does not include gender as a predictive factor, despite the variance between genders in the association between cumulative lifetime dose of alcohol and cirrhosis.

Despite these limitations, these data serve as a proof of concept for non‐laboratory‐based liver disease risk stratification to aid simplified HCV treatment. DCHEQ demonstrates excellent discriminatory function as a tool to predict decompensated cirrhosis without phlebotomy‐based labs or imaging. After prospective validation, DCHEQ could be applied within simplified HCV treatment pathways that utilize DBS or POC rapid HCV testing to improve the HCV treatment experience for PWUD and deployed in various settings, including in conjunction with opioid use disorder treatment. While further iteration and testing of the Decompensated Cirrhosis in Hepatitis C Evaluation Questionnaire (DCHEQ) are needed, the promise of improving HCV treatment initiation in priority populations warrants further evaluation. History‐based screening for decompensated cirrhosis has the potential to streamline HCV treatment initiation when combined with other low‐barrier and rapid tests for HCV, like DBS testing and POC HCV diagnostic tests. DCHEQ could contribute to patient‐centred HCV treatment protocols, furthering global HCV elimination goals.
