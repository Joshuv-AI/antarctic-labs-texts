Insights into ubiquitinome dynamics in the host‒pathogen interplay during Francisella novicida infection
Authors: Luyu Yang, Yanfeng Li, Qingqing Xie, Tao Xu, Xiaopeng Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ubiquitination functions as an important posttranslational modification for orchestrating inflammatory immune responses and cell death during pathogenic infection. The ubiquitination machinery is a major target hijacked by pathogenic bacteria to promote their survival and proliferation. Type I interferon (IFN-I) plays detrimental roles in host defense against Francisella novicida (F. novicida) infection. The effects of IFN-I on the ubiquitination of host proteins during F. novicida infection remain unclear. Herein, we delineate the dynamic ubiquitinome alterations in both wild-type (WT) and interferon-alpha receptor-deficient (Ifnar–/–) primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) during F. novicida infection. Using diGly proteomics and stable isotope labeling (SILAC), we quantified ubiquitination sites in proteins from primary WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs with and without F. novicida infection. Our mass spectrometry analysis identified 2,491 ubiquitination sites in 1,077 endogenous proteins. Our study revealed that F. novicida infection induces dynamic changes in the ubiquitination of proteins involved in the cell death, phagocytosis, and inflammatory response pathways. IFN-I signaling is essential for both the increase and reduction in ubiquitination in response to F. novicida infection. We identified IFN-I-dependent ubiquitination in proteins involved in glycolysis and vesicle transport processes and highlighted key hub proteins modified by ubiquitination within cell death pathways. These findings underscore the significant influence of IFN-I signaling on modulating ubiquitination during F. novicida infection and provide valuable insights into the complex interplay between the host and F. novicida.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01887-1.

Introduction

Francisella tularensis, a highly pathogenic facultative intracellular bacterium, causes the zoonotic disease tularemia [1]. One of its less virulent subspecies, Francisella novicida (F. novicida), can induce severe illness in mice but usually does not significantly affect immunocompetent humans, making it a commonly used mouse model for studying the pathogenic mechanisms of tularemia [2].

Francisella lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has unique features and poorly activates host inflammatory responses due to its lack of engagement with toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which significantly contributes to the robust immune evasion capacity of Francisella species [2, 3]. However, cytosolic recognition of F. novicida via DNA engagement induces two sequential events, type I IFN (IFN-I) signaling and AIM2 inflammasome activation, and IFN-I is essential for F. novicida infection-induced AIM2 inflammasome activation [4–6]. The IFN-I signaling-dependent regulators IRF1, GBP proteins, and IRGB10 are critical for F. novicida intracellular bacteriolysis, cytosolic DNA release, and subsequent AIM2 inflammasome activation [7, 8]. However, IFN-I signaling and the AIM2 inflammasome play opposite roles in host defense against F. novicida infection, and IFN-I signaling is detrimental to host defense, possibly through regulating different cell death pathways or suppressing Th17 immune responses during intracellular bacterial infection; however, the detailed mechanism remains elusive [9–12].

Ubiquitination, a type of posttranslational modification (PTM), predominantly occurs on the lysine residues of proteins and plays important roles in protein degradation, signal transduction, DNA repair, and endocytosis [13]. This modification is pivotal for the regulation of various cellular processes and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. During bacterial infections, ubiquitination is widely involved in the process of pathogen recognition and the activation of host immune responses, which makes the ubiquitination machinery a prime target for subversion by bacterial effector proteins [14]. Bacteria have evolved sophisticated strategies to evade or neutralize host ubiquitination-mediated immune responses, and in some cases, to hijack the ubiquitin system for their own benefit [15]. On the other hand, upon pathogen attack, host cell signaling networks undergo significant ubiquitin-dependent modifications to safeguard the cell, encompassing the coordination of innate immunity, the reshaping of organelles, the reorganization of the cytoskeleton, and the reprogramming of metabolic pathways to restrict pathogen proliferation [16].

Ubiquitination has been demonstrated to be critical for the regulation of multiple factors involved upstream and downstream of the IFN-I signaling pathway in response to viral infection [17]. During bacterial infection, ubiquitination is also defined as a critical regulator of inflammasome assembly and activation [18]. Additionally, ubiquitination significantly influences the IFN-I signaling pathway during bacterial infections by targeting various pathway components. For example, the bacterial effector SopA from Salmonella was identified as a HECT-type E3 ligase that targets and mediates the ubiquitination of the host E3 ligases TRIM56 and TRIM65, thereby promoting type I IFN production and enhancing innate immune responses [18, 19]. In the context of inflammasomes, Shigella’s IpaH7.8 E3 ubiquitin ligase mediates the ubiquitination of GLMN, which in turn promotes the activation of both the NLRP3 and NLRC4 inflammasomes, highlighting the extensive role of ubiquitination in inflammasome regulation during bacterial infection [18]. During F. novicida infection, our previous work revealed that the E3 ligase HECTD3 mediated the K63-linked polyubiquitination of TRAF3 and that HUWE1 mediated the K27-linked polyubiquitination of AIM2 [20]. Thus, HECTD3 and HUWE1 both promote the activation of IFN-I signaling and AIM2 inflammasome activation triggered by F. novicida and play negative and positive roles in host defense against F. novicida infection, respectively [21]. How the ubiquitination regulated by IFN-I signaling is involved in the host defense against F. novicida remains unknown.

The use of mass spectrometry (MS)-based diGly proteomics, a technique designed for the enrichment of endogenously ubiquitinated peptides, has significantly advanced our ability to detect and quantify discrete ubiquitination events on proteins [22]. In this study, we utilized diGly proteomics to comprehensively explore alterations in the ubiquitination of primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) challenged with F. novicida, particularly the effects of interferon-alpha receptor (IFNAR) deficiency on the ubiquitination response. We found that F. novicida infection triggered dynamic changes in the ubiquitination of endogenous proteins involved in cell death, phagocytosis, and inflammatory response pathways. IFN-I signaling was essential for both increased and decreased ubiquitination triggered by F. novicida infection. Our work revealed changes in IFN-I-dependent ubiquitination in proteins involved in glycolysis and vesicle transport and revealed central hub proteins modified by ubiquitination within cell death pathways. Our observations highlight the pivotal role of IFN-I signaling in the dynamic regulation of ubiquitination during F. novicida infection and pave the way for a deeper understanding of the elaborate interplay between the host and F. novicida.

Results

Characterization of the ubiquitinome in WT and Ifnar–/– primary BMDMs

To examine the role of IFN-I in the cellular ubiquitinome in the response of BMDMs to F. novicida infection, we performed diGly proteomics for quantitative ubiquitination site analysis in both WT and interferon-alpha receptor (IFNAR)-deficient (Ifnar–/–) BMDMs with and without F. novicida infection (Fig. 1A). This approach integrates stable isotope labeling in cell culture (SILAC) with immunoprecipitation enrichment of diGly-terminated peptides following trypsin digestion of ubiquitinated proteins. The dynamics of the host ubiquitinome induced by F. novicida infection were investigated at 4 and 8 h post infection (Fig. 1A). Mass spectrometry analysis yielded 122,630 secondary spectra, with 22,472 spectra successfully matched, leading to the identification of 3,857 peptides, of which 2,467 peptides were ubiquitinated. A total of 1,077 proteins with 2,491 ubiquitination sites were identified (Fig. 1B, Table S1). Quantitative analysis revealed differences in the ubiquitination profiles between WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs and a decrease in the number of quantified ubiquitination sites in Ifnar–/– BMDMs compared with WT BMDMs (Fig. 1C). To verify the ubiquitination sites detected in our data, we conducted literature search for some well-known proteins and confirmed 25 ubiquitination sites in 23 proteins reported in previous studies (Table S2). These data indicate the potential loss of original ubiquitination sites on certain proteins in Ifnar–/– BMDMs and reveal a dynamic change in ubiquitin modification sites after F. novicida infection.

We subsequently performed Venn diagram analysis to compare the quantification of ubiquitination sites in different samples, including shared and specific ubiquitination sites, in WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs at both 4 and 8 h post infection (Fig. 1D). Notably, 597 ubiquitination sites were commonly detected in all the samples. Sixty-seven, 35, and 94 ubiquitination sites were specifically detected in WT BMDMs at 0, 4, and 8 h after F. novicida infection, respectively. In contrast, 50, 13, and 84 specific ubiquitination sites were detected in Ifnar–/– BMDMs at 0, 4, and 8 h, respectively (Fig. 1D). The distribution analysis of ubiquitination sites across the identified proteins revealed that the majority of proteins contained one or two ubiquitination sites, suggesting that the ubiquitination sites were conserved among these samples (Fig. 1E). Motif analysis of the amino acids upstream and downstream of the ubiquitination sites revealed an increased frequency of leucine (L) and glutamic acid (E) residues surrounding the ubiquitination sites (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these data reveal a dynamic ubiquitin landscape during F. novicida infection and a differential ubiquitination profile between WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs, suggesting the subtle role of ubiquitination regulated by IFN-I in regulating the interplay between host cells and bacterial pathogens.

Profile of changes in the ubiquitination site in WT BMDMs during F. novicida infection

To investigate the changes in ubiquitination triggered by F. novicida, we quantified the dynamic profile of total ubiquitinated sites within each protein in WT BMDMs in response to F. novicida infection. We found that the total ubiquitination of 454 proteins was reduced by F. novicida infection; F. novicida infection caused increased ubiquitination of 194 proteins, and the ubiquitination of 263 proteins was comparable before and after F. novicida infection (Fig. 2A). The top list of proteins with reduced ubiquitination included the cell integrity and cytoskeleton-regulating proteins VIM and TUBA1B, the ribosomal subunit proteins RPS3 and RPS27A, the nuclear envelope protein LMNA, the receptor for colony stimulating factor CSF1R, the ATPase VCP, and the ubiquitin-regulating proteins USP5, USP25, and RNF213 (Fig. 2B). The greatest increase in protein ubiquitination was observed for the cell death-modulating protein PARP14, the serine proteinase inhibitor SERPINB6, the NADPH oxidase complex subunit CYBB, the actin-binding protein gelsolin GSN, the adaptor protein STIP1, the ion channel protein KCNAB2, the phosphofructokinase PFKL, the GTPase GVIN1, the translin-associated factor X TSNAX, and the transcription factor NME2 (Fig. 2B). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the proteins involved in pathways related to cell death, protein degradation, and inflammatory responses were mostly ubiquitinated during F. novicida infection (Fig. 2C). The Venn diagram showed the results of the statistical analysis of proteins with increased, decreased, and comparable degrees of ubiquitination involved in the cell death, protein degradation, and inflammatory response pathways. Approximately two-thirds of the proteins in these pathways exhibited dynamic changes in ubiquitination during F. novicida infection (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, many proteins involved in regulating pyroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy, such as NLRP3, IL-1β, GBP2, IL-1α, RIPK1, and BID, exhibited dynamic changes in ubiquitination in response to F. novicida infection (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these results indicate that host cells coordinate multiple signaling pathways through ubiquitination and underscore the important role of dynamic ubiquitination in regulating immune responses and pathogenicity during F. novicida infection.

Effects of IFN-I signaling on ubiquitination in BMDMs

IFN-I plays both positive and negative roles in host defense against extracellular and intracellular bacterial infection [23, 24]. To examine the role of IFN-I in host protein ubiquitination during F. novicida infection, we compared the changes in the ubiquitination of host proteins between WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs at 0, 4, and 8 h after F. novicida infection. Ubiquitination sites in 502 proteins were reduced in Ifnar–/– BMDMs compared with WT BMDMs (50%), ubiquitination sites in 160 proteins were increased in Ifnar–/– BMDMs (16%), and ubiquitination sites in 336 proteins were comparable between WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs (34%) (Fig. 3A, B). The similar trend of dynamic change in the ubiquitination proteins involved in inflammatory response, protein degradation, and cell death were observed in WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs during F. novicida infection (Fig. 3C). The proteins whose ubiquitination was increased by F. novicida infection in WT BMDMs further increased ubiquitination in Ifnar–/– BMDMs, and vice versa (Fig. 3A, C), suggesting that IFN-I signaling was essential for both the increase and decrease of ubiquitination in response to F. novicida infection. In the subsequent analysis of the quantified proteins, we identified 37 E3 ubiquitin ligases, of which 6 E3 ligases exhibiting increased ubiquitination and 15 E3 ligases showing reduced ubiquitination in Ifnar–/– BMDMs compared with WT BMDMs. In addition, 14 deubiquitinating enzymes were detected, of which 3 proteins showing increased ubiquitination and 7 proteins showing reduced ubiquitination in Ifnar–/– BMDMs compared with WT BMDMs (Fig. 3D, Table S3). The decrease in total ubiquitination, K48- and K63-linked ubiquitination in WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs induced by F. novicida infection was confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. 3E, S1A). To elucidate the specific impact of IFNAR deficiency on ubiquitination in BMDMs during F. novicida infection, we conducted a functional enrichment analysis of proteins according to the altered ubiquitination levels. These analyses included Gene Ontology (GO) classification and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and aimed to reveal the biological significance of alterations in ubiquitination in the absence of IFNAR. Increased ubiquitination in Ifnar–/– BMDMs was observed for proteins predominantly enriched in biological processes such as membrane proteins, organelle fusion, and ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation (Fig. 3F). Additionally, these proteins were enriched in pathways involving SNARE interactions with vesicular transport, small cell lung cancer-related pathways, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and the NF-κB signaling pathway according to KEGG analysis (Fig. S1B). In contrast, proteins with decreased ubiquitination in Ifnar–/– BMDMs were significantly enriched in biological processes related to cytoplasmic translation, the regulation of protein metabolism, cytoskeletal organization, and the regulation of innate immune responses (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, the proteins associated with decreased ubiquitination in Ifnar–/– BMDMs were enriched in critical metabolic pathways, such as spinocerebellar ataxia, ribosomal structure, proteasome, prion disease, and peroxisome pathways, according to KEGG analysis (Fig. S1C).

IFN-I signaling affects the ubiquitination of proteins regulating cell death and ubiquitination pathways in response to F. novicida infection

To specifically elucidate the effect of IFN-I on the ubiquitination of proteins during F. novicida infection, we profiled the top list of decreased and increased ubiquitination sites of proteins in Ifnar–/– BMDMs compared with WT BMDMs (Fig. 4A), as well as the dynamic changes at each specific ubiquitination site in these proteins (Fig. S2). Notably, the ubiquitination sites were highly detected in glycolysis-related proteins, including glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI), transketolase, an enzyme involved in the pentose phosphate pathway (TKT), aldolase A, a crucial glycolytic enzyme (ALDOA), poly (ADP‒ribose) polymerase member 14 (PARP14), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), in WT BMDMs but not in Ifnar–/– BMDMs (Fig. 4A, S2). In addition, the actin cytoskeleton-regulated proteins MYH9 and WDR1, the ribosomal protein RPL4, the E3 ubiquitin ligase ring finger protein RNF213, and the guanylate binding protein GBP2 were identified as ubiquitination site-rich proteins in WT BMDMs; however, these ubiquitination sites were not detected in Ifnar–/– BMDMs (Fig. 4A, S2). On the other hand, the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β, membrane transporters, receptors such as TNFRSF1B, VAMP8, ATP7A, FUNDC2, and the voltage-gated proton channel HVCN1 were highly ubiquitinated in Ifnar–/– BMDMs but not in WT BMDMs (Fig. 4A, S2). Furthermore, the dynamic ubiquitination changes of NLRP3 and GAPDH in response to F. novicida infection were confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. S3A-C).

We further investigated the ubiquitination sites within different proteins closely. In PARP14 and MYH9, the ubiquitination sites were mostly identified within the conserved macro- and PARP domains of PARP14 and the myosin motor and coiled coil domains of MYH9 (Fig. 4B and C). Instead, the ubiquitination sites observed in the RNF213, GBP2, ALDOA, and TKT proteins were not restricted to conserved domains (Fig. 4D and E).

Interaction network and dynamics of major ubiquitinated proteins in cell death pathways

Cell death plays essential roles in both host defense against bacterial infection and bacterial evasion of host immune responses [25]. In this study, we found that ubiquitinated proteins associated with cellular death pathways such as pyroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis were significantly enriched in both WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs (Fig. 5A). Subcellular localization analyses of these proteins revealed a predominant distribution in the cytoplasm (45.76%) and nuclear localization (21.47%). A notable fraction localized to the cell membrane (10.17%) and the mitochondrial environment (7.91%) (Fig. 5B). Further investigation into the ubiquitination patterns of proteins involved in cell death pathways confirmed that IFNAR deficiency caused mostly decreased ubiquitination levels of the proteins involved in the cell death pathways (Fig. S4).

Given the impact of IFN-I on ubiquitination dynamics, we further analyzed the protein interaction networks within cell death pathways affected by ubiquitin modifications. The proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β, the tyrosine kinase JAK1, the apoptotic regulator BCL2L1, the multifunctional scaffolding protein p62/SQSTM1, and the molecular chaperone HSP90AB1 are central hub proteins in these networks, potentially playing a leading role in signal transduction and crosstalk during F. novicida infection (Fig. 5C). The resulting heatmap accurately displays the levels of ubiquitination at specific sites on five central hub proteins within the protein interaction network of the cell death pathway. Notably, there was a significant increase in the level of ubiquitination at 5 sites of IL-1β and 10 sites of SQSTM1 in the absence of IFNAR (Fig. 5D). In contrast, the ubiquitination levels of the HSP90AB1 protein significantly decreased at 14 sites in Ifnar–/– BMDMs (Fig. 5D). Overall, these data reveal that cell death pathways are major targets of the ubiquitination machinery during F. novicida infection and that IFN-I signaling is essential for the regulation of ubiquitination modifications.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the ubiquitinome of BMDMs in response to F. novicida infection and explored the regulatory role of IFN-I signaling. Our findings provide significant insights into the dynamics of ubiquitination during bacterial infection and the modulatory function of IFN-I.

Our analysis revealed a comprehensive list of ubiquitinated proteins in BMDMs, reflecting key targets modulated by F. novicida infection and IFN-I signaling. This broad spectrum of ubiquitination events underscores the complexity of the regulatory network controlled by ubiquitin modifications, which is consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of ubiquitination in various cellular processes, including protein degradation [26], signal transduction [27], cell death [28], and immune responses [29]. We previously reported that cathepsin B and NCF1/2/4 played important roles in the AIM2 inflammasome activation and host defense upon F. novicida infection [7, 30, 31] Here, we identified the different ubiquitination pattern of cathepsin B and NCF2 in WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs with and without F. novicida infection, which might contribute to the activity regulation of target proteins in response to F. novicida infection. Notably, the differential ubiquitination profiles between WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs highlight the pivotal role of IFN-I in orchestrating host defense mechanisms [32], as corroborated by studies on IFN-I signaling in bacterial infections [20].

One limitation of this study is the lack of mechanistic insights into the observed changes in ubiquitination. The increased or decreased number of ubiquitination sites could result from either increased ubiquitination or reduced deubiquitination. Understanding the precise mechanisms requires further investigation into the activity of the involved E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) [33]. E3 ligases are key enzymes that mediate the specific ubiquitination of proteins, whereas DUBs are responsible for removing ubiquitin tags, thereby regulating protein stability and function [34]. Recent advances in proteomics and the use of specific inhibitors can significantly facilitate such studies. For example, improvements in mass spectrometry and the development of novel chemical probes have allowed us to study the dynamic changes in ubiquitination modifications with increased resolution [22, 35].

In addition, our study did not distinguish between the various types of ubiquitin chains, each of which can signal distinct cellular outcomes. For example, K48-linked polyubiquitination typically targets proteins for proteasomal degradation [36], whereas K63-linked chains are usually involved in signal transduction and DNA repair [37]. Moreover, other types of ubiquitin chains, such as K6 [38], K11 [39], and K27 [40], also play important roles in different cellular processes, including mitochondrial quality control and autophagy [41]. Future studies should incorporate techniques such as linkage-specific antibodies or advanced mass spectrometry methods to dissect the types of ubiquitin chains formed during F. novicida infection and their functional implications.

Our results also demonstrated that IFN-I signaling significantly impacts the ubiquitination landscape during F. novicida infection, suggesting a multifaceted role for IFN-I in intracellular parasitism. The identification of numerous IFN-I-regulated ubiquitination sites on the proteins involved in glycolysis, cytoskeletal organization, and vesicle transport indicates that IFN-I may modulate metabolic and structural components of the cell to counteract bacterial infection. IFNAR deficiency was reported to maintain glycolytic flux and mitochondrial function during Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection [42]. Key enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, such as phosphofructokinase (PFK) and aldolase A (ALDOA), were found to be ubiquitinated in an IFN-I-dependent manner, which aligns with previous research on the importance of metabolic reprogramming in immune responses. Additionally, the regulation of cytoskeletal dynamics is crucial for pathogen invasion and host cell defense mechanisms, with cytoskeletal proteins such as the actin cytoskeleton-regulated proteins MYH9 and WDR1 showing significant IFN-I-dependent ubiquitination. Proteins involved in vesicle transport, such as the membrane transporters and receptors TNFRSF1B, VAMP8, ATP7A, FUNDC2, and the voltage-gated proton channel HVCN1 and SNARE complexes, are also regulated by IFN-I signaling, indicating the crucial role of IFN-I in modulating intracellular transport and pathogen clearance.

Moreover, our study highlights the impact of ubiquitination on cell death pathways during F. novicida infection. Ubiquitinated proteins associated with pyroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis were significantly enriched, indicating that these cell death pathways are major targets of the ubiquitination machinery. The role of ubiquitination in regulating cell death has been well documented, with studies showing that ubiquitination can modulate the stability and activity of key proteins involved in these pathways. For example, the activation of the inflammasome is closely related to its ubiquitination status, which plays a critical role in regulating inflammasome-mediated cell death [43]. Our data suggest that IFN-I signaling may influence the ubiquitination of proteins within these pathways, thereby affecting the host’s ability to manage bacterial infections. Key proteins in the necroptosis pathway, such as RIPK1 and RIPK3, are influenced by their ubiquitination status, which impacts the progression of cell death. Additionally, apoptosis-related proteins from the Bcl-2 family [44] and the IAP family [45] are subject to IFN-I-regulated ubiquitination, which may play significant roles in modulating host cell responses to pathogen infection. These findings suggest that the absence of IFNAR possibly modulates these pathways to enhance host defensive capabilities against intracellular bacterial infections.

In conclusion, this study presents a comprehensive characterization of the ubiquitinome in response to F. novicida infection, emphasizing the regulatory role of IFN-I. While our findings offer valuable insights into the ubiquitination targets and pathways involved, further research is necessary to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and functional consequences of these modifications. Understanding the interplay between ubiquitination and IFN-I signaling will enhance our knowledge of host‒pathogen interactions and could favor the development of novel therapeutic strategies against intracellular bacterial infections.

Experimental procedures

Mice

Ifnar−/− mice were previously described [10]. WT and knockout mice were kept under specific pathogen-free conditions in the Animal Resource Center at Shandong University, Jinan, Shandong Province, China.

Preparation of BMDMs, bacterial culture, treatment and bacterial infection

To generate BMDMs, bone marrow (BM) cells were cultured in L929 cell-conditioned DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% non-essential amino acids, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin for 5 days. The bacterial strains used in this study included F. novicida WT strain U112, that were growing as previously described [20]. WT and knockout BMDMs were infected with bacterial pathogens for indicated times as previously described [2].

Ubiquitination analysis

For polyubiquitination analysis in WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs, samples were separated by 6% SDS-PAGE, followed by electrophoretic transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and membranes were blocked and then incubated with primary antibodies. For ubiquitination analysis of NLRP3 and GAPDH in BMDMs, WT and Ifnar–/– BMDMs were infected with F. novicida for the 8 h. Treated BMDMs were lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 1.5% SDS) and then boiled for 15 min. The denatured samples were immunoprecipitated with specific primary antibodies, and the IP products were analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-ubiquitin and specific antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Ub (P4D1, CST3936, 1:1000); anti-K48 (CST4289, 1:1000); anti-K63 (CST5621, 1:1000); anti-GAPDH (CST, 5174, 1:1000); anti-NLRP3 (Adipogen, AG-20B-0014, 1:1000). HRP-labeled anti-rabbit and anti-mouse at 1:10000 dilution was used as the secondary antibody.

LC-MS/MS analysis and database search

The supernatants from F. novicida -infected WT and knockout BMDMs were performed with Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis by Applied Protein Technology at the PTM BIO (Hangzhou). Briefly, protein digestion was performed by trypsin. For modification enrichment, peptides were dissolved in IP buffer, incubated with ubiquitin resin (PTM-1104, PTM Bio) at 4 °C overnight, washed, and eluted with 0.1% TFA. Eluted peptides were desalted using C18 ZipTips and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Tryptic peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid and loaded onto a reversed-phase column. A gradient elution (6-80% acetonitrile) over 37 min was performed at 400 nL/min using an EASY-nLC 1000 UPLC system. Peptides were analyzed by MS/MS in a Q Exactive™ Plus (Thermo). MS/MS data were processed using MaxQuant (v1.5.2.8) against SwissProt Mouse and Francisella novicida databases for identification and quantitation analysis [46].

Data retrieval and processing

The signal abundance of the modified peptide in each sample was detected by mass spectrometry, and the LFQ intensity of the modified site in each sample was obtained by non-standard quantitative calculation method, and the relative quantitative value of each sample was obtained according to the LFQ intensity of the modified site between different samples [47]. The list of identification and quantification of ubiquitinated proteins that were identified in F. novicida -infected WT and knockout BMDMs are listed in Table S1. In the analysis of ubiquitination dynamics in WT BMDMs during F. novicida infection, proteins were classified into Ubi_up (increased ubiquitination), Ubi_down (decreased ubiquitination), and Comparable (similar ubiquitination). Ubi_up proteins had either a more than 2-fold increase at 4 h and at least 0.8-fold at 8 h, or at least 0.8-fold at 4 h and more than 2-fold at 8 h. Ubi_down proteins had either less than 0.5-fold at 4 h and no more than 1.2-fold at 8 h, or no more than 1.2-fold at 4 h and less than 0.5-fold at 8 h. Ratios were defined as WT_4 h / WT_0 h (Ratio_0 h_4 h) and WT_8 h / WT_0 h (Ratio_0 h_8 h). In the quantification analysis of ubiquitinated proteins in Ifnar–/– BMDMs compared to WT BMDMs, Ubi_up proteins had more than 2-fold at 0 h and at least 0.8-fold at both 4 and 8 h, or similar patterns at 4–8 h. Ubi_down proteins had less than 0.5-fold at 0 h and no more than 1.2-fold at both 4 and 8 h, or similar patterns at 4–8 h. Ratios were defined as KO_4 h / WT_0 h (Ratio_0 h), KO_4 h / WT_4 h (Ratio_4 h), and KO_8 h / WT_8 h (Ratio_8 h).

Bioinformatics methods

The analysis of proteins with differential ubiquitination levels was conducted using the R packages org.Mm.eg.db (version 3.18.0) and clusterProfiler (version 4.10.1) for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment [48]. GO annotations were categorized into biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, while KEGG pathways were used to identify enriched pathways. The heat map was plotted using the R package pheatmap version 1.0.12. Differentially ubiquitinated proteins were identified and compared against the STRING database (version 12.0) using their protein accessions. Interactions with a confidence score greater than 0.7 (high confidence) were extracted and visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) to form interaction networks [49]. For subcellular localization, protein accessions were read from an Excel file and queried against the UniProt database using Python. The resulting localization data was visualized with pie charts created using the R package ggplot2 (version 3.5.1). Protein structure prediction and the analysis of differentially ubiquitinated sites involved obtaining known 3D structures from the UniProt database. These structures were visualized and annotated using ChimeraX (version 1.7) to highlight the ubiquitination sites [50].
