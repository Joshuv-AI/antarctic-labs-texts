Paraneoplastic minimal change disease in malignant thymoma: a rare association progressing to end-stage renal disease
Authors: Vinson James, Yonique Petgrave
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThymoma, commonly seen in middle-aged and elderly individuals, is associated with autoimmune disorders. While renal involvement is rare, its occurrence remains poorly characterized.Case PresentationWe report a 24-year-old female with a ten-year history of malignant thymoma who developed nephrotic syndrome. Renal biopsy revealed minimal change disease (MCD), a rare paraneoplastic manifestation of thymoma. Despite treatment with steroids and rituximab, her renal function deteriorated, leading to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Background

Thymomas are associated with a wide array of autoimmune diseases, including myasthenia gravis, systemic lupus erythematosus, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, acquired red cell aplasia, bullous pemphigoid, among others [1, 2]. Additional autoimmune conditions reported include polymyositis, pernicious anemia, thyroiditis, hyperthyroidism, rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis, dermatomyositis, scleroderma, Takayasu syndrome, Graves’ disease, and encephalitis [1].. Renal involvement is a rare association, with minimal change nephrotic syndrome as the most common pathology reported. Various authors have suggested T cell dysfunction to be the main driving force [2–8]. While treatment typically involves corticosteroids, immunosuppressive drugs, and thymectomy, there’s no established therapy. Rituximab, targeting CD20 antigen on B lymphocytes, has shown promise in treating steroid-resistant MCD. However, its efficacy in thymoma-associated cases lacks sufficient evidence [9, 10]. Accumulating such cases is crucial to evaluate rituximab’s efficacy in this context. Here, we present a case of steroid-resistant MCD linked with thymoma, with no remission on steroid and rituximab therapy, who developed progressive worsening of renal function and subsequently end-stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis [11].

Case presentation

A 24-year-old multiracial female presented with a two-week history of progressive weight gain and swelling involving all four limbs, torso, face, and periorbital regions. She reported associated abdominal and bilateral flank discomfort, constipation, nausea, anorexia, and new-onset exertional dyspnea and orthopnea. Two days prior, she noticed urinary frequency and volume decreased without gross hematuria or dysuria. Notably, her symptoms were preceded by a week of viral upper respiratory symptoms. Three days before admission, a PET-CT was performed to rule out superior vena cava thrombus considering her facial edema.

Her medical history was significant for a thymoma classified as type B3, stage IVa, diagnosed in 2012. Initial treatment included two courses of cisplatin, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide. Surgical management in July 2014 involved en-bloc resection of a mediastinal mass, including portions of the left upper and lower lobes, left phrenic nerve, pericardium, and thymus, with placement of a Gore-Tex pericardial patch and pleurectomy. This was followed by three months of radiotherapy. Unfortunately, she experienced multiple disease recurrences in March 2016, July 2017, March 2018, and June 2018. A biopsy performed in September 2018 confirmed metastatic malignant thymic epithelial neoplasm.

On physical examination, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.4°C, heart rate 65 beats/minute, respiratory rate 20 breaths/minute, blood pressure 99/66 mmhg, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Her weight was 57.5 kg, reflecting a gain of 6.1 kg since her last clinic visit seven days earlier. She was alert, oriented, and in no acute distress but exhibited significant anasarca with weeping from her limbs. Findings from the respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal systems were unremarkable. Her baseline creatinine was 0.91 mg/dl, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was 17 mg/dl, corresponding to an estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of 75 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the creatinine-based ‘Bedside Schwartz’ equation (2009). At presentation, her creatinine had increased to 2.08 mg/dl and BUN to 44 mg/dl, resulting in a GFR of 33 ml/min/1.73 m2.

Her initial post admission labs were significant for Na: 134 mmol/l, K: 3.6 mmol/l, Cl: 103 mmol/l, CO2: 22 mmol/l, Anion Gap (without K): 9 mmol/l BUN: 42 mg/dl, Creatinine: 2.25 mg/dl, Calcium: 0.9 mg/dl, Albumin: 2.5 g/dl, Total Cholesterol: 238 mg/dl, HDL Cholesterol: 55 mg/dl, LDL Cholesterol: 164 mg/dl, Triglyceride: 99 mg/dl.

PET CT showed stable extensive post therapy changes in the left hemithorax and mediastinum without evidence of tumor recurrence. Urine dipstick showed Protein: 3+. Fractional excretion of sodium was suggestive of prerenal injury with a fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa): 0.1%. Her clinical features and laboratory findings suggested KDIGO Stage 3 oliguric AKI. Her AKI had multiple potential etiologies, including use of contrast, nephrotoxic drugs, and decreased effective circulatory volume from nephrotic syndrome. For her edema, she was started on sodium restriction of less than 2000 mg/day, and a fluid restriction of 1 liter/day. She was treated with albumin-assisted diuresis using furosemide. Given her atypical presentation, a kidney biopsy was performed, which revealed normal glomeruli with effacement of the foot process on light microscopy and a podocyte dusting staining pattern on electron microscopy. Within the first three days of admission, her GFR worsened, prompting treatment with pulse methylprednisolone at 1 gram daily for three consecutive days, followed by a transition to prednisolone 60 mg/day. This intervention led to an improvement in her edema and acute kidney injury (AKI). She was discharged after 22 days, with a discharge weight of 71.6 kg. Renal function at discharge showed sodium: 137 mmol/l, BUN: 55 mg/dl, creatinine: 2.10 mg/dl, calcium: 7.4 mg/dl, total protein: 3.9 mg/dl, and albumin: 2.6 mg/dl.

She was subsequently readmitted twice for increased edema, requiring albumin assisted diuresis. During her first admission, she required 25 g of 25% albumin every twelve hours daily followed by 20 mg of furosemide during the 12-day hospital course. Her weight remained elevated at 74.2 kg. Her echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular size and systolic function with and ejection fraction of 55%–60%. Her laboratory reports during this admission were Na: 133 mmol/l, BUN: 76 mg/dl, Creatinine: 2.03 mg/dl, Calcium Level: 7.7 mg/dl, Protein Total: 3.9 mg/dl, Albumin Lvl: 1.9 mg/dl and CD19: 0.4. Considering persistent fluid overload, a temporary dialysis catheter was placed, and she underwent slow continuous ultrafiltration. She also developed stage 2 hypertension and started on losartan 25 mg daily and labetalol 100 mg twice daily. After discharge, she received 50 g of albumin on alternate days with scheduled ultrafiltration therapy.

During her second admission, she presented with refractory anasarca despite diuretic therapy, accompanied by worsening azotemia. After eight weeks of steroid therapy without remission, she was treated with two doses of rituximab (1000 mg each) administered two weeks apart, resulting in complete depletion of CD19+ B cells. While pitting edema of both upper and lower extremities improved significantly during this admission, she ultimately failed to achieve clinical remission. She was discharged with diuretics therapy metolazone 7.5 mg BID, lasix 50 mg BID and spironolactone 100 mg daily. She experienced a progressive decline in renal function and was diagnosed with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in May 2022, requiring dialysis three times per week. In May 2023, her thymoma recurred, and she received a single dose of Keytruda (pembrolizumab, an anti-PD-1 antibody). Unfortunately, she developed cardiac failure following treatment and passed away in July 2023. The patient did not receive treatment for either minimal change disease or thymoma prior to the development of end-stage renal disease.

Discussion and conclusion

Thymomas represent the most frequent tumors of the anterior-superior mediastinum, particularly prevalent among middle-aged or older adults. In a study by Karras et al., the majority of patients with minimal change disease or FSGS (11 out of 13) responded to steroid therapy. However, despite this favorable response, 38% of patients died from thymoma-related complications, and 17% progressed to end-stage renal disease [4]. Renal complications associated with thymoma are seldom documented, with retrospective studies indicating an incidence of less than one percent, often presenting as MCD, the most prevalent form. Despite this, there are no established clinical guidelines for managing nephrotic syndrome concurrent with recurrent metastatic thymoma, typically initiating treatment with steroid therapy [4, 13, 14].

While thymoma-associated MCD typically demonstrates a favorable response to steroids, approximately eighty-four percent sensitivity, certain cases exhibit resistance [2]. Rituximab, an effective therapy for primary MCD and autoimmune manifestations of thymoma, has been less explored in thymoma-associated MCD [15–17]. Given the lack of response to eight weeks of steroid therapy in our case, we opted for rituximab based on several factors. Renal biopsy findings suggestive of podocyte dusting, observed in our case and corroborated by previous studies, highlighted a potential pathogenic link between autoantibodies targeting nephrin and B cell dysregulation, endorsing consideration for B cell–targeted therapy. Podocyte dusting refers to nephrin autoantibodies with podocyte-associated punctate IgG in renal biopsies [17, 18]. Biopsy images referenced in this case were not available for publication. Rituximab’s documented ability to preserve kidney function, mitigate nephrotoxicity, and elicit a positive response in MCD further supported its use. Moreover, rituximab’s mechanism of action, involving B cell depletion and its indirect impact on T cells through disruption of T and B cooperation, along with its role in maintaining podocyte cytoskeleton integrity after binding to sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase acid-like 3B (SMPD L3B), provided additional rationale for its efficacy in thymoma-associated MCD [18] [Table 1].

Paraneoplastic glomerulopathies are a heterogeneous group of renal diseases triggered by immune responses to neoplasms, most commonly solid tumors or hematologic malignancies. The association between thymoma and glomerular disease, particularly minimal change disease (MCD), is exceedingly rare and poorly understood, often thought to arise from thymoma-induced immune dysregulation. Increased awareness of such paraneoplastic phenomena may prompt earlier recognition and tailored immunosuppressive therapy, especially in patients with refractory or atypical nephrotic syndrome. In paraneoplastic minimal change disease (MCD) linked to thymoma, immune dysregulation appears central to the disease process. Thymoma may disrupt T-cell selection, leading to autoreactive lymphocytes that target podocytes. Interleukin-13 (IL-13), a cytokine often elevated in MCD, can increase glomerular permeability and induce CD80 expression on podocytes. CD80, normally expressed on antigen-presenting cells, when upregulated in podocytes, contributes to proteinuria and podocyte dysfunction [12]. These immune-mediated mechanisms support the theory that paraneoplastic MCD results from aberrant immune responses triggered by the underlying malignancy.

This case highlights the limitations of rituximab therapy in managing thymoma-associated minimal change disease (MCD) that is resistant to steroids and thymectomy. The potential role of nephroprotective mechanisms, particularly those involving nephrin autoantibodies, warrants further exploration through prospective studies to better understand their prognostic and therapeutic implications. Notably, our patient represents the youngest recorded case of recurrent metastatic thymoma concurrently presenting with MCD, a distinction that underscores the rarity and complexity of this clinical scenario. This case emphasizes the need for individualized therapeutic strategies informed by a deeper understanding of the underlying pathophysiology and the patient’s unique clinical course.
