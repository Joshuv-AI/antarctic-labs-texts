Biosynthesis of biobased products from lignocellulose using filamentous fungi-integrated microbial consortia
Authors: Runze Pan, Sijie Liu, Yuwei Chen, Weiliang Dong, Wankui Jiang, Wenming Zhang, Yujia Jiang, Fengxue Xin, Min Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural microbial consortia exhibit characteristics of environmental robustness and the ability to utilize complex substrates. Inspired by them, various synthetic microbial consortia capable of utilizing economical substrates have been constructed. As an abundant renewable biomass resource, lignocellulose is regarded as the most promising raw material for replacing fossil energy to produce biofuels and bioproducts. The hydrolysis process of lignocellulose is a key step limiting its bioconversion. Filamentous fungi, due to their strong ability to secrete and hydrolyze cellulases, are widely used in synthetic microbial consortia to achieve lignocellulosic biorefinery. This paper reviews the latest progresses in the biosynthesis of biofuels and bioproducts from lignocellulose by various microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi. In addition, the construction strategies of synthetic microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi are also reviewed. Finally, the applications of filamentous fungi in synthetic microbial consortia are prospected.Keywords: Filamentous fungi, Microbial consortia, Lignocellulose, Biorefinery

Introduction

Microbial consortia possess advantages such as metabolic synergy and complementarity, functional diversity, and strong environmental adaptability, which show enormous application potential in multiple fields. Generally, microbial consortia can alleviate the metabolic burden of a single host by distributing metabolic pathways among different hosts [1]. To achieve more complex tasks, various microbial consortia composed of the same or different species have been constructed [2]. In recent years, the applications of microbial consortia have been continuously expanding from traditional fields such as food and bioremediation to other areas, including the production of biofuels and biological products, as well as the high-value conversion of waste biomass (Fig. 1) [3–6].

Application of synthetic microbial consortia in lignocellulose bioconversion

Lignocellulose is an abundant renewable biomass resource, which has been considered an important raw material that can substitute for fossil energy. Lignocellulose is a polymeric material with a complex three-dimensional network structure, mainly consisting of lignin (15–20%), hemicellulose (25–30%), and cellulose (30–50%) [7]. The hydrolysis of lignocellulose releases soluble sugars such as pentose and hexose, which can then be converted into various high-value products. However, the hydrolysis process usually involves multiple enzymes and the biochemical synthesis pathways also contain various metabolic modules, so accomplishing multiple functions in a single strain often leads to low efficiency [8]. Constructing synthetic microbial consortia where each member performs specific functions, has become a promising way to improve the efficiency of lignocellulose bioconversion.

The key step in lignocellulose bioconversion is enzymatic hydrolysis [7]. A variety of microorganisms exist in nature that can hydrolyze lignocellulose, including bacteria and fungi. In particular, filamentous fungi, due to their complete enzyme expression systems and unique extracellular enzyme secretion systems, have been widely used in the pretreatment and saccharification of lignocellulose [9–12]. For example, white-rot fungi and brown-rot fungi are widely used in the microbial treatment of lignocellulose. It is reported that the newly isolated white-rot basidiomycete Peniophora incarnata T-7 was used to delignify poplar wood via submerged fermentation, with the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin contents decreasing by 16, 48, and 70%, respectively, thus significantly enhancing the enzymatic saccharification efficiency of the biomass [13]. Given that filamentous fungi possess the ability to efficiently degrade lignocellulose, a growing number of successful cases involving microbial consortia that contain them for the high-value conversion of lignocellulosic materials have been reported (Table 1). For example, when beechwood cellulose was used as the carbon source, the co-culture of Aspergillus oryzae and Rhizopus delemar achieved the combined production of 37.9 g/L malic acid and 16.2 ± 0.2 g/L fumaric acid [14].

Application of filamentous fungi-integrated microbial consortia in the synthesis of biobased products

This review summarizes the latest advances in the biosynthesis of biofuels and bioproducts from lignocellulose by various microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi. Additionally, it reviews the construction strategies of synthetic microbial consortia containing filamentous fungi. Finally, the prospects for the application of filamentous fungi in synthetic microbial consortia are discussed.

The important role of filamentous fungi in lignocellulosic biorefining

In nature, many microorganisms including bacteria, actinomycetes, and fungi can secrete plant polysaccharide-degrading enzymes, which facilitate the degradation of lignocellulosic biomass [15]. Benefiting from this characteristic, numerous lignocellulolytic enzyme-producing microorganisms have been isolated. Among them, filamentous fungi are highly favored in the industrial production of lignocellulolytic enzymes due to their strong growth capacity, rich lignocellulolytic enzyme systems, and unique extracellular protein secretion systems [16]. So far, Trichoderma reesei is the main producer of industrial cellulase preparations. A strategy using the acid-catalytically synthesized mixture as an inducer for cellulase production has been developed, which enables T. reesei RUT-C30 to achieve a cellulase activity of 19.2 U/mL in fed-batch fermentation. The preparation cost of the acid-catalytically synthesized mixture is 52.4% of that of lactose and 41.4% of that of microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), realizing low-cost cellulase production [17].

The genomes of filamentous fungi contain more protein-encoding genes than those of bacteria and yeasts, which endows them with a more abundant protein synthesis capacity [18]. With the development of gene-editing technologies, filamentous fungi have become excellent hosts for heterologous and homologous protein expression [19]. In addition, filamentous fungi can grow on almost any substrate [20]. These characteristics enable filamentous fungi to be used in biorefineries for processing various wastes, especially lignocellulosic biomass resources. In general, filamentous fungi make up for the lack of biomass degradation ability of product-producing strains, enabling synthetic microbial consortia to directly synthesize biobased products from biomass.

In addition, constructing a stable or dynamically balanced synthetic microbial consortium is key to lignocellulosic biorefining [21]. Due to the strong adaptability of filamentous fungi to the environment, they can better adapt to the living conditions of other microorganisms in the consortium to achieve a stable population ratio [22]. The study by Liu et al. on the co-culture of the filamentous fungus Aspergillus niger and the bacterium Rhodococcus jostii for treating the hydrothermal liquefaction aqueous phase (HTLAP) of corn stover supports this view. Specifically, at a low chemical oxygen demand (COD) loading (3800 mg/L), A. niger and R. jostii formed a synergistic consortium, achieving a 69% COD removal efficiency while producing value-added products such as oxalic acid. Even at higher COD loadings (4500–7800 mg/L) where R. jostii was significantly inhibited, A. niger maintained stable growth due to its excellent tolerance to HTLAP, sustaining effective COD removal (58–65%) and increased oxalic acid production [23]. This adaptability allows A. niger to dynamically adjust its role in the consortium, ensuring the functional stability of the microbial consortium under different environmental conditions. Therefore, filamentous fungi are often used in microbial consortia for lignocellulosic biorefining.

Filamentous fungi-integrated microbial consortia for biobased product synthesis

Filamentous fungal–filamentous fungal consortia

In recent years, the application of microbial consortia composed of two or more filamentous fungi in the biological pretreatment of lignocellulose has been put into practice [24]. The enzyme system of a single filamentous fungus is usually limited and may only efficiently synthesize one or several types of enzymes, while microbial consortia sometimes obtain better enzyme composition output [25]. For example, the mixed culture of A. niger and R. oryzae inoculated simultaneously in solid-state fermentation showed the highest enzyme activities at 96 h, with the proportions of xylanase, cellulase, amylase, pectinase, and protease increasing by 25% compared to monoculture (Fig. 2A) [26]. In addition, microbial consortia can make up for the deficiency of enzyme components in individual strains. It is well known that T. reesei lacks the ability to synthesize β-glucosidase, which hinders the synergistic effect of cellulase components and impairs its capacity to degrade cellulose. A. niger, on the other hand, is a good producer of β-glucosidase. The mixed culture system of T. reesei and A. niger further improves the production of cellulase and its enzymatic hydrolysis performance, with the enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency of this system reaching 89.35% [27].

Additionally, filamentous fungal–filamentous fungal consortia have been successfully constructed to achieve high-value chemical production from lignocellulose. Different filamentous fungi often share similar growth conditions, such as temperature, aeration, and pH, which makes the construction of consortia easier [28]. Consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) is considered an economical and efficient approach for producing target chemicals from lignocellulose, which integrates the production of lignocellulose-degrading enzymes, hydrolysis of lignocellulose, and microbial fermentation in one step [29]. Through screening of cellulolytic strains and chemical producers, Scholz et al. successfully designed and constructed synthetic consortia for organic acid production. Among them, T. reesei was used as the producer of cellulase, while R. delemar and R. oryzae were used for fumaric acid and lactic acid production, respectively (Fig. 2B). When 40 g/L MCC was used as the substrate without expensive supplements (e.g., enzymes or yeast extract), 6.87 g/L fumaric acid was obtained. Another microbial consortium composed of T. reesei and R. oryzae could produce 4.4 g/L lactic acid from MCC [30].

Filamentous fungal–yeast consortia

In consortia of filamentous fungi and yeasts, filamentous fungi secrete cellulases and hydrolyze lignocellulosic biomass into corresponding monomers, which are subsequently converted into specific products by yeasts. Compared with filamentous fungi–filamentous fungi consortia, the division of labor in filamentous fungi–yeast consortia is more explicit, and yeasts serve as excellent producers that can promptly convert hydrolyzed monomers into target products [31, 32]. Microbial consortia constructed by filamentous fungi and yeasts have been widely applied in the production of bioethanol. To achieve the value-added utilization of distiller’s grains wastewater, A. oryzae and R. oligosporus were co-cultured with Candida utilis to utilize the components in corn thin stillage (Fig. 2C). In the system where A. oryzae and C. utilis were added after 24 h of culturing R. oligosporus, up to 18.6 g/L of biomass and 4.5 g/L of ethanol could be produced [18].

In addition to biofuels, the good genetic editability of yeasts enables consortia to produce a wider range of higher-value chemicals from lignocellulose. For example, the biosynthetic pathway for D-glucaric acid synthesis was constructed in the S. cerevisiae INVSc1 with the opi1 gene knocked out, by expressing the miox4 gene derived from Arabidopsis thaliana and the udh gene derived from Pseudomonas syringae. Subsequently, CBP was carried out using the microbial consortium composed of T. reesei Rut-C30 and S. cerevisiae LGA-1 (Fig. 2D). After 7 days of fermentation, the consortium produced 0.54 ± 0.12 g/L and 0.45 ± 0.06 g/L of glucaric acid from 15 g/L MCC and 15 g/L steam-exploded corn stover, respectively [33]. In addition, due to the similar growth requirements of filamentous fungi and yeasts, consortia are expected to be applied in more biorefinery scenarios.

Filamentous fungal–bacterial consortia

Constructing synthetic microbial consortia containing filamentous fungi and bacteria is a relatively new direction [34]. Filamentous fungi and bacteria usually require different microenvironments, such as pH and oxygen, which will limit their applications [1]. Generally, the division of labor in filamentous fungal–bacterial consortia is also clear. After fungi break the recalcitrant bonds of lignocellulose and hydrolyze cellulose and hemicellulose into soluble sugars, bacteria convert them into valuable products. In a microbial consortium composed of T. reesei and Escherichia coli, T. reesei secretes cellulases to hydrolyze lignocellulosic biomass into soluble sugars, which are metabolized into isobutanol by E. coli (Fig. 2E). Enabled by a developed comprehensive mathematical model, the consortium provides a mechanism to regulate population balance. Using pretreated corn stover as the substrate, it achieved an isobutanol production of 1.88 g/L [35].

Although there is a problem of growth incompatibility between filamentous fungi and bacteria, the consortia of filamentous fungi and bacteria still have advantages in the synthesis of alcohols and organic acids [36]. In addition, due to the complete gene-editing tools available in some model bacteria, the consortium of filamentous fungi and bacteria has a broader product spectrum [37]. In recent years, a series of strategies have been developed to achieve the co-culture of filamentous fungi and bacteria. For example, one approach to simultaneously meet the oxygen requirements of aerobic T. reesei and anaerobic lactic acid bacteria in the same reactor is as follows. The reactor is constructed using tubular membranes made of polydimethylsiloxane (with a pore size of 0.22 μm), and locally restricted aeration is achieved through the oxygen-permeable membranes. In this process, the aerobic fungus T. reesei directly forms a biofilm on the surface of the tubular membrane and produces/secretes cellulases into the fermentation broth. Since all oxygen is consumed in the biofilm, anaerobic conditions prevail in the bulk phase where enzymatic hydrolysis occurs, and lactic acid bacteria ferment the released sugars into the target product lactic acid. Finally, 34.7 g/L of lactic acid was obtained using MCC as the substrate (Fig. 2F) [38].

Construction strategy of filamentous fungi-integrated microbial consortia

Modular design

Reconfiguring modular pathways through division of labor represents a fundamental principle in constructing synthetic microbial consortia [6]. However, the design of synthetic microbial consortia inevitably leads to physical isolation, which affects the concentration of intermediates and mass transfer efficiency, thereby creating potential obstacles [39]. Therefore, appropriate intermediates must be carefully selected as the criteria for pathway allocation. In the lignocellulosic biorefinery process, members of the microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi often have a clear division of labor. Given that filamentous fungi have a better ability to degrade lignocellulose, like T. reesei, they generally act as lignocellulose degraders. The other members serve as chemical producers, including filamentous fungi, bacteria or yeasts, giving full play to their advantages in chemical synthesis. The soluble sugars produced by cellulase degradation of lignocellulose can easily pass through the cell membrane [40]. Through the synergistic effect of microbial consortia, soluble sugars can be promptly consumed and converted into target products, which helps alleviate the inhibitory effect of accumulated soluble sugars on cellulase activity. A study on the production of butanol from unbleached hardwood kraft pulp via microbial consortia has shown that the synergistic effect of white-rot fungus KO77 and Clostridium acetobutylicum in anaerobic co-culture improves saccharification efficiency and the final butanol production (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, knockout of the pyruvate decarboxylase gene in white-rot fungus inhibited ethanol synthesis, and butanol production increased from 2.5 g/L to 3.2 g/L [41].

Rational design of interactions

Microbial interactions are considered to be central to the survival, stability, and productivity of consortia [8]. There are mainly six different types of pairwise interaction modes in microbial consortia, including neutralism, commensalism, amensalism, mutualism, competition, and predation [42]. Due to these complex relationships, different microorganisms exhibit interspecific dependency and unique phenotypes. For synthetic microbial consortia aimed at establishing stable interactions, a key challenge lies in the rational allocation of microbial consortium functions in production environments to achieve high-level production of the consortia.

The most effective relationship between strains is commensalism and cooperation [6]. A common approach is to construct nutrient-deficient complementation, enabling strains to provide each other with essential compounds that they individually lack. This complementary effect enhances the synergistic effect between strains and strengthens the connection between metabolic pathways and functions. This type of nutrient-deficient complementation has been widely applied in model strains. For example, the biosynthetic pathway of salidroside was allocated to two E. coli strains, where the aglycone (AG) strain was used for synthesizing tyrosol, and the glycoside (GD) strain was employed for producing UDP-glucose and synthesizing salidroside. To synergistically regulate the interactions between strains, a phenylalanine-deficient AG strain and a tyrosine-deficient GD strain were designed to achieve mutualistic cooperation through cross-feeding of nutrient metabolites. By balancing the intensity of metabolic pathways, the syntrophic microbial consortium was confirmed to exhibit stability and produce 6.03 g/L of salidroside [43].

Due to the characteristics of filamentous fungi such as apical growth, heterokaryosis, low homologous recombination efficiency, and scarcity of genetic markers [44], nutrient-deficient complementation has not been applied in synthetic microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi. However, in known microbial consortia, microorganisms can promote each other’s growth by providing nutrients and regulating the surrounding environment without genetic engineering modification [45]. Studies have shown that in the co-culture system of Trichoderma asperellum HG1 and Bacillus subtilis Tpb55, strain HG1 promoted the spore formation of strain Tpb55 (the spore rate of Tpb55 in co-culture was 64.29 ± 10.91%, significantly higher than 27.78 ± 2.78% in monoculture) and reduced its cell membrane fluidity, while strain Tpb55 promoted the carbon metabolism and amino acid degradation of strain HG1 (genes related to amino acid degradation and metabolism in HG1, such as eca39, Mccc1, ItaE, and gcvP, were significantly upregulated, and genes involved in glycolysis, the glyoxylate cycle, and the TCA cycle, such as FBA1, mcl, OXCT1, and MDH1, were also significantly upregulated). In addition, under this interaction, co-culture accelerated the intracellular production of octyl ketone, an anti-oomycete compound (Fig. 3B) [46].

Spatially ordered distribution

An ordered spatial distribution of microbial consortia is crucial for constructing stable consortia for the biodegradation and bioconversion of lignocellulose [6, 47]. The spatial distribution of strains can be optimized by ensuring effective material exchange and providing each member with a relatively independent living space. Such an arrangement can enhance the synergistic interactions between strains and improve the controllability of the system [48]. Cell immobilization is a commonly used technique to achieve a spatially ordered distribution, which has been widely applied in microbial consortia containing bacteria or yeasts. For example, a synthetic microbial consortium was constructed by separately printing bacteria producing 3,4-dihydroxy-l-phenylalanine and yeast capable of converting 3,4-dihydroxy-l-phenylalanine into betaxanthins using F127-bisurethane methacrylate hydrogel. The consortium enabled efficient betaxanthins production without the need to control the symbiotic relationship [49]. However, for filamentous fungi, which primarily take the form of multicellular mycelia with slender, branched hyphae, the grid structure of hydrogels often has pores that are insufficiently large to accommodate or restrict hyphal growth, resulting in impaired mass transfer. Although the interior of the hydrogel cannot provide a suitable living space for filamentous fungi, its surface is easy for filamentous fungi to attach to, which makes it possible for the spatially ordered distribution of filamentous fungi and other microorganisms. For example, T. asperellum could attach to hydrogel carriers embedding Lactobacillus paracasei and consume oxygen outside the carriers, providing an anaerobic environment for the L. paracasei aggregated granule inside to produce lactic acid (Fig. 3C). This successfully constructed microbial consortium creates independent ecological niches to meet different oxygen demands, thereby achieving more efficient synergistic effects in lactic acid production from lignocellulosic biomass [50].

Additionally, the design and development of bioreactors play a key role in regulating the spatially ordered distribution of strains in synthetic microbial consortia [51]. Filamentous fungi have a strong ability to form biofilms [52]. By virtue of this characteristic, they can be segregated into defined spatial niches together with other microorganisms in the microbial consortium in a specific bioreactor to meet the requirements of different abiotic conditions in the microbial consortium, such as pH, oxygen demand, etc. [47]. For example, a synthetic microbial consortium composed of the aerobic fungus T. reesei, facultative anaerobic lactic acid bacteria, and the obligate anaerobic Clostridium tyrobutyricum has been used to construct a membrane-aerated bioreactor that satisfies oxygen niches (Fig. 3D) [53]. T. reesei could form biofilms on the membrane surface to consume oxygen and produce reducing sugars from cellulose. The oxygen consumption of T. reesei created a relatively anaerobic environment in the liquid medium. However, the metabolic activity of T. reesei alone was insufficient to create an environment with a redox potential suitable for obligate anaerobes. Therefore, facultative anaerobic L. pentosus was introduced to further consume oxygen and metabolize reducing sugars into lactic acid. After introducing C. tyrobutyricum, the by-products of lactic acid and acetic acid were co-consumed at a molar ratio of 3:1, and 10.7 g/L butyric acid was eventually produced.

Advanced technologies enhance the performance of synthetic microbial consortia

Strategies such as modular design, interaction regulation, and spatial distribution provide a key framework for constructing microbial consortia that can efficiently degrade lignocellulose and directionally synthesize biobased products. However, to further break through the efficiency bottleneck of lignocellulose bioconversion and realize the precise regulation of consortium performance and its large-scale application, relying solely on basic construction strategies is still insufficient. With the development of interdisciplinary technologies including synthetic biology, metabolic engineering, and artificial intelligence, a series of advanced tools have offered new solutions for analyzing the metabolic mechanisms of consortia, optimizing the dynamic balance of populations, and improving the efficiency of product synthesis.

Synthetic biology and metabolic engineering tools

Advanced technologies such as synthetic biology and metabolic engineering will significantly expand the capabilities of synthetic microbial communities and enhance their efficiency in lignocellulose depolymerization and bioconversion [39, 54]. A multitude of synthetic biology tools developed with the support of genetic engineering has strengthened the construction and functionality of synthetic microbial consortia (Fig. 4A) [6].

Synthetic biology has brought new genetic and computational tools to fungal metabolic engineering, such as low-cost genome sequencing, gene synthesis, genome editing, and directed evolution technologies [55]. For example, through comparative genome analysis, the genome of T. asperellum ND-1 encodes a more diverse range of polysaccharide-degrading enzymes, particularly those associated with hemicellulose deconstruction. Compared with T. reesei QM6a, ND-1 exhibits higher hemicellulase (especially xylanase) activity and comparable cellulase activity [56]. With the improvement of gene-editing tools for filamentous fungi, it will provide better hosts for synthetic microbial consortia.

With the in-depth understanding of the principles underlying the construction of synthetic microbial consortia, enhancing the ability to regulate the composition and behavior of consortia is an important direction for future research [57]. Recent studies have shown that population-level behaviors can be engineered into synthetic systems through intrinsic devices for quorum sensing and metabolite sensing [58]. Similarly, tunable promoters have the property of responding to biotic or abiotic factors (such as sugars, amino acids, vitamins, metals, light, or temperature) and can dynamically regulate the behavior of microbial consortia [59]. For example, under the regulation of the cellulose carbon source-inducible promoter cbh1, the gene xyn2 was expressed in T. reesei Rut-C30, achieving xylanase activity as high as 5256 U/mL and β-xylosidase activity of 9.25 U/mL [60]. In response to the needs of specific synthetic microbial consortia, synthetic biology tools can be used to precisely regulate the population levels, metabolic division of labor, and spatial distribution within the consortia.

Artificial intelligence and mathematical models

Artificial intelligence (AI) plays a crucial role in integrating, processing, recognizing, and classifying extensive multidimensional datasets (Fig. 4B) [6]. In recent years, AI-based models and algorithms have gained increasing attention. Common artificial intelligence algorithm models include decision trees, linear regression, deep neural networks, and naive Bayes, among others [61]. Mutlu et al. proposed predicting the syngas composition and calorific value of downdraft lignocellulosic biomass gasification using machine learning classifiers. They trained binary least squares support vector machines and multi-class random forest classifiers with 5237 data samples and tenfold cross-validation. The study found that the temperature distribution has a greater impact on syngas composition than fuel parameters. After preprocessing the selected features, the performance of the models is better than that of traditional stoichiometric and non-stoichiometric models [62]. In the future, the efficiency and predictive capabilities of artificial intelligence hold great promise for the rational construction of microbial consortia for the valorization of lignocellulose.

It is often challenging to determine co-culture conditions, growth compatibility, and to screen cross-feeding metabolites among different strains in a synthetic microbial consortium [63]. The emergence of metabolic modeling of microbial consortia provides a new approach to describe the operation mode of the community, understand the community, and identify the relationships within the community. Among them, genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) represent a scalable formulation for simulating biological activity over whole cells and cellular communities [64]. For example, a mathematical modeling framework called MICOM can effectively extend metabolic modeling to entire microbial consortia. Through a two-step optimization procedure, it is able to simulate the growth and metabolic exchange fluxes of GEMs of ecologically diverse bacterial systems. In addition, this framework incorporates microbial abundance estimates from metagenomic shotgun sequencing to make quantitative and personalized metabolic predictions [65]. However, a large number of GEMs for microorganisms have not yet been established. Research on genome-scale network reconstruction of filamentous fungi started relatively late. The first genome was published in 2003, which is the genome of Neurospora crassa. Subsequently, in 2005, the genomes of Aspergillus nidulans, A. oryzae, and Aspergillus fumigatus were also published one after another [66]. Since filamentous fungi are an important source of new natural products and excellent cell factories for cellulase production, the application of GEMs will further increase, providing technical support for the design and optimization of microbial consortia with specific functions.

Challenges and prospects of applying filamentous fungi in synthetic microbial consortia

Filamentous fungi, by virtue of their powerful lignocellulose degradation capacity and metabolic diversity, have become core members of bioconversion in synthetic microbial consortia. In particular, given the excellent cellulose-degrading ability of filamentous fungi, they can provide abundant soluble sugars for product-synthesizing strains. Through functional division of labor, the microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi can significantly enhance the efficiency of biofuel synthesis and platform chemical production from lignocellulose. Additionally, compared with monoculture, co-culturing filamentous fungi with other microorganisms not only helps discover novel enzymes and small-molecule compounds, but also optimizes the production process [25]. However, most of the currently known synthetic microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi are constructed in a product-oriented manner, where filamentous fungi are responsible for the saccharification of lignocellulose and other microorganisms are responsible for product synthesis. In this process, the interactions between members have not been able to be analyzed in detail, which often leads to the collapse of the consortia. Therefore, dynamic monitoring of metabolic interactions and information exchange occurring within the consortium will help improve its stability and synthetic efficiency.

Although filamentous fungi are powerful producers of cellulases, the branching, extension, and entanglement of their hyphae form a dispersed hyphal network [67], which increases the difficulty of suspended co-culture with other microorganisms. Fortunately, customizing the microenvironment using materials science can achieve the orderly distribution of microorganisms, which helps to construct a robust, stable, and efficient synthetic microbial consortium [47]. In addition to enabling different members to have independent living spaces, the rational design of bioreactors allows microorganisms with different environmental growth requirements to coexist in the same space. A membrane bioreactor achieved a decreasing oxygen gradient, enabling the production of ethanol with a 67% yield from undetoxified dilute acid-pretreated wheat straw whole slurry through the synergistic action of T. reesei, S. cerevisiae, and Scheffersomyces stipitis [68]. Notably, the three-dimensional network structure formed by the mycelia of filamentous fungi may hinder the diffusion of small molecules (such as signaling molecules and metabolites). Therefore, the mass transfer efficiency of synthetic microbial consortia in biorefining processes needs to be considered.

In recent years, with the development of synthetic biology, tools for metabolic engineering of filamentous fungi have been developed, including genome engineering based on the CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing system, promoter and transcription factor engineering, and signal transduction pathway regulation [59]. For example, the expression level of the xyr1 gene in T. reesei was tailored through promoter engineering. In the strain QE2X with xyr1 gene overexpression driven by the promoter Pegl2, the cellulase activity increased by 3.2-fold [69]. However, reports on the application of engineered filamentous fungi in synthetic microbial consortia are still rare, and most of the reported engineered strains are limited to model filamentous fungi such as T. reesei. One of the reasons is that the interaction mechanisms between filamentous fungi and other microorganisms have not been systematically studied. In contrast, quorum sensing systems and metabolic cross-feeding among bacteria have been widely studied, making it easier to design synthetic microbial consortia. On the other hand, the rapid spread of hyphae of filamentous fungi can physically squeeze the living space of other microorganisms. Moreover, the secreted extracellular enzymes can efficiently decompose complex substrates and preferentially acquire nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen sources, leading to a decrease in the proportion of other microorganisms due to resource limitation. Additionally, research on filamentous fungi as chassis strains started relatively late, limiting their application in synthetic microbial consortia. Nevertheless, filamentous fungi have unique advantages in the efficient degradation of lignocellulose, enabling them to become core members of synthetic microbial consortia for biomass valorization.

Conclusions

Synthetic microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi have gained increasing attention due to their unique advantage of enabling efficient production of biobased products directly from lignocellulose. Filamentous fungi can serve not only as cell factories for cellulase production, but also as producers of biofuels and biochemicals. Such multifunctional chassis strains will play a more significant role in synthetic microbial consortia. Although current synthetic microbial consortia integrating filamentous fungi are still limited to simple co-culture of wild strains, the established concept of spatial niches has provided new ideas for constructing metabolically interactive and stable consortia. With the development of synthetic biology, materials science, and artificial intelligence, the development of more efficient and cost-effective synthetic microbial consortia integrated with filamentous fungi will become a promising approach for producing more high-value biobased products using lignocellulose as a raw material.
