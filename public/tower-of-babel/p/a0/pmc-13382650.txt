Mechanistic Study of Tritium Retention and Permeation in Hydroxyl-Functionalized Nuclear Graphite for Thorium Molten Salt Reactors
Authors: Sijie Duan, Sida Xia, Jiaxin Xia, Wenguan Liu, Guo Yang, Xijun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In thorium molten salt reactors (TMSRs), nuclear graphite is subjected to direct contact with high-temperature molten salts and intense neutron irradiation, leading to oxidation and structural evolution that critically influence tritium retention and release. Motivated by oxidation processes in TMSR environments, this work employs first-principles calculations based on hydroxyl (–OH)-functionalized graphite edge models to systematically investigate the effects of edge functionalization on hydrogen/tritium adsorption, migration, and release. The results indicate that, compared with pristine graphite edges, all edge configurations exhibit markedly enhanced hydrogen adsorption, while hydroxyl functionalization induces pronounced spatial heterogeneity in adsorption strength. Strongly bound sites act as effective hydrogen trapping centers, whereas adjacent weak-binding sites facilitate surface migration. Hydrogen diffusion along graphite edges generally proceeds with moderate energy barriers (∼1.0–2.0 eV), and the overall reaction kinetics are governed by the final desorption step. Regarding hydrogen desorption, the H2 desorption barriers at AC edges are markedly lower than those for H2O, whereas at ZZ edges, H2 is the predominant desorption product. Reconstructed edges generally display higher migration and desorption barriers, indicating stronger hydrogen confinement. Electronic structure analysis shows pronounced charge accumulation around C–H and O–H bonds upon adsorption, and the introduction of hydroxyl groups induces the formation of intermediates such as CH­(OH), significantly altering hydrogen migration pathways and desorption kinetics. This study reveals the synergistic effects of hydroxyl functionalization and edge reconstruction on hydrogen/tritium behavior at graphite edges, providing qualitative atomic-scale insights from simplified hydroxyl-functionalized edge models into tritium retention and release, with the understanding that molten salt interactions, temperature effects, and irradiation evolution are not explicitly included.

Introduction

Nuclear graphite is a key moderator and structural material in thorium molten salt reactors (TMSRs), performing essential functions in neutron moderation, structural support, and thermal management. − Its service environment is extremely demanding, involving long-term operation at high temperatures (∼700 °C), exposure to molten fluoride salts such as FLiBe, and intense neutron irradiation. Under such coupled high-temperature, chemical, and irradiation conditions, various defectsincluding vacancies, interstitials, and edge reconstructionsas well as surface oxidation are introduced, which play a critical role in governing tritium retention, migration, and release of tritium. , In TMSRs, tritium is mainly produced from neutron capture of lithium isotopes, with minor contributions from fission products, and its accumulation and release in core materials directly impact operational safety and environmental risks. , Hydrogen is commonly used as a surrogate for tritium due to their similar chemical properties, and its adsorption, diffusion, and desorption behaviors in carbon materials are widely employed to approximate the microscopic behavior of tritium. In graphite under such conditions, T transport is highly sensitive to oxidation and irradiation-induced defects, which can modify trap states and introduce functional groups, substantially modifying associated kinetics compared with ideal graphite.

Graphite structural defects and edge configurations play a decisive role in H/T behavior. Ideal graphite or graphene surfaces exhibit weak hydrogen adsorption, primarily via physisorption. In contrast, point defects such as single or double vacancies act as strong hydrogen trapping centers, reducing adsorption energies to several electronvolts and significantly increasing desorption barriers. , Moreover, graphite edge structures show much higher chemical reactivity than basal planes. The adsorption strength varies significantly between zigzag (ZZ) and armchair (AC) edges, while irradiation-induced edge reconstructions (e.g., ZZR and ACR) further alter stability and chemical reactivity. Previous studies have indicated that reconstructed configurations such as zz(57) are energetically more stable and may represent realistic edge structures under high-temperature or irradiation conditions. − These findings suggest that irradiation defects and edge structures are key regulators of tritium retention and release in nuclear graphite.

In actual reactor service, nuclear graphite surfaces and near-surface regions are often partially oxidized. Oxidation may arise from trace oxidizing impurities in molten salt or from irradiation-induced defects, which lower the activation energy of carbon atoms and facilitate reactions with surrounding species, leading to the formation of oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl (–OH). In theoretical studies, water dissociation is often employed as a simplified model to investigate hydroxyl formation and surface reactivity on graphite. First-principles studies indicate that water mainly physisorbs weakly on ideal graphite surfaces, with interactions accurately described only when van der Waals corrections are included. − On defect-containing or low-coordination carbon sites, water can chemisorb or dissociate to form hydroxyl and other oxygenated intermediates, significantly enhancing interfacial reactivity. −

These hydroxyl species can significantly modify local electronic structures and bonding characteristics, thereby regulating hydrogen adsorption strength and desorption pathways. For instance, cooperative adsorption of two water molecules can reduce hydrogen desorption barriers to approximately 1.53 eV. Hydroxyl groups have also been reported to facilitate the “adsorption-reaction-desorption” mechanism, enabling tritium release in the form of HTO with lower activation barriers compared to direct H2 desorption. In addition, reactions involving C–H2O systems may proceed via multistep pathways, where O–H bond cleavage can act as a rate-limiting step. − These studies demonstrate that hydroxyl groups, together with defect structures and edge configurations, play an important role in determining reaction barriers and product selectivity. − However, a systematic understanding of how hydroxyl functionalization at different graphite edge structures regulates H/T adsorption, migration, and desorption behavior is still lacking.

Although these studies provide a foundation for understanding hydrogen reactions on hydroxyl-functionalized graphite surfaces, and such models have been demonstrated to be structurally stable, as hydroxyl groups can bind stably on graphite surfaces, as supported by first-principles studies, , current research mainly focuses on ideal surfaces or isolated defects. A comprehensive understanding of complex graphite interfaces under coupled irradiation and oxidation effects, as relevant to TMSR environments, remains insufficient. In particular, the stability of hydroxyls on different edge structures and reconstructed edges, as well as their cooperative effects on T adsorption, migration, and desorption, have not been systematically elucidated. To address this, the present work employs first-principles calculations to systematically investigate hydrogen adsorption, migration, and desorption on hydroxyl-functionalized graphite edges (AC, ZZ, and their reconstructions ACR and ZZR) within a simplified model framework. By analyzing adsorption energies, migration and desorption barriers, and electronic structures, this study reveals the cooperative effects of hydroxyl functionalization and edge reconstruction on hydrogen behavior, providing atomic-scale theoretical insights into tritium-related processes in nuclear graphite relevant to the TMSR environment.

Methodology

Compared with the adsorption behavior on the graphite surface, the van der Waals interaction between adjacent graphite layers can be neglected when modeling surface adsorption phenomena. − Therefore, a single graphene layer was adopted to represent the graphite surface in this study. As illustrated in Figure , different edge configurations were constructed to capture the structural diversity of graphite edges. The armchair (AC) and zigzag (ZZ) edges were represented by hexagonal supercells of 2 × 4 (36 carbon atoms) and 4 × 4 (40 carbon atoms), corresponding to the (110) and (100) orientations, respectively. To further account for structural reconstruction effects, reconstructed armchair (ACR) and zigzag (ZZR) edge models were also considered. These were built using 2 × 3 and 4 × 3 supercells, respectively, while preserving the total number of carbon atoms during reconstruction. Based on these edge models, hydroxyl-functional groups were subsequently introduced to represent possible oxidation of graphite edges under irradiation and chemically reactive environments relevant to TMSR conditions, enabling a systematic investigation of hydrogen adsorption, migration, and desorption on functionalized graphite surfaces.

Potential hydrogen adsorption sites and corresponding adsorption energies on hydroxyl-functionalized edges: (a) AC, (b) ACR, (c) ZZ, and (d) ZZR. The blue arrows indicate the possible migration pathways of hydrogen atoms.

In this work, hydrogen was employed as a surrogate for tritium due to their nearly identical electronic structure within the density functional theory (DFT) framework and similar chemical properties, and first-principles calculations based on DFT were performed to investigate the adsorption and desorption behavior of hydrogen atoms on hydroxyl-functionalized graphite edges. It should be noted that the present models represent idealized hydroxyl-functionalized graphite edges and are intended to capture local oxidation effects, rather than explicitly simulating the complex operating environment of TMSRs. All calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The exchange–correlation interactions were treated within the generalized gradient approximation using the Perdew–Wang (PW91) functional. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 450 eV was adopted to ensure computational accuracy. Brillouin zone sampling was performed using a Γ-centered 4 × 4 × 1 k-point mesh. The electronic self-consistency convergence criterion (EDIFF) was set to 1 × 10–6 eV, and the force convergence threshold during structural optimization was 0.01 eV·Å–1. The diffusion and desorption energy barriers were evaluated using the climbing-image nudged elastic band method. − The number of intermediate images was chosen as 3, 4, or 6 depending on the structural difference between the initial and final states of each step, ensuring accurate resolution of the minimum energy pathway.

The adsorption energy ( Ead ) was defined as the energy difference between the total energy of the hydrogen–graphite system and the sum of the energies of the isolated components, according to the following equation: 1 Ead=(E0−EG−nEH)/n E 0 represents the total energy of the optimized adsorption system, E G represents the total energy of the optimized substrate corresponding to the adsorption system (i.e., bare graphite for nonfunctionalized models and hydroxyl-functionalized graphite for functionalized models), E H denotes the energy of an isolated hydrogen atom, and n is the number of adsorbed hydrogen atoms. It should be noted that adsorption energies are referenced to the corresponding substrate, therefore are not used to compare stability between different functionalized systems.

Results and Analysis

As a validation step, the adsorption behavior of a single H atom on four types of graphite edge configurations was simulated to evaluate the feasibility of the constructed models. The most stable adsorption energies for the AC, ACR, ZZ, and ZZR edges were calculated to be −3.71 eV, −2.78 eV, −4.84 eV, and −3.06 eV, respectively. These values are consistent with the results reported by Zhang et al., whose corresponding most stable adsorption energies were −3.84 eV, −2.62 eV, −5.10 eV, and −2.89 eV. The deviations between the two data sets fall within a reasonable adsorption energy range, confirming that the constructed models are reliable and suitable for subsequent calculations.

Hydrogen Desorption from Hydroxyl-Functionalized AC Edges

To mimic the hydroxylation of nuclear graphite edges under conditions relevant to molten salt chemical oxidation and neutron irradiation in TMSRs, hydroxyl (–OH)-functional groups were introduced on representative graphite edge models. This model is intended to provide atomic-scale insights into the key regulatory effects of oxidized functionalized edges on the adsorption and desorption behavior of hydrogen. As shown in Figure a, eight potential adsorption sites with distinct local structural characteristics were identified on the AC edge to systematically evaluate the influence of the adsorption position on reaction pathways and energy barriers. The calculated adsorption energies of hydrogen at different sites (Table ) exhibit pronounced site-dependent variations. Among them, the T1 and T2 sites show the lowest adsorption energies of −1.06 eV and −1.05 eV, respectively, indicating the formation of highly stable adsorption configurations. This strong adsorption behavior mainly originates from the robust C–H interactions between hydrogen atoms and low-coordinated edge carbon atoms, while the local electronic structure modified by neighboring hydroxyl-functional groups further enhance the hydrogen-binding strength. In contrast, the adsorption energies at the T3 and T4 sites are −0.70 eV and −0.75 eV, respectively, suggesting relatively weaker hydrogen-surface interactions and a higher tendency for surface migration or desorption. This reduced adsorption stability can be attributed to the decreased availability of effective bonding sites in the local structure, together with the weakened synergistic effect of adjacent hydroxyl groups. The adsorption energies at the T5 and T8 sites are approximately −0.90 eV, indicating an intermediate stability. Overall, the T3 and T4 sites are likely to act as key surface-active sites that play an important role in subsequent surface reactions and hydrogen desorption processes.

Adsorption Energies of Hydrogen at Different Sites on Various Edge Configurations

To explore the mechanism of hydrogen desorption in the form of H2 or H2O modified by hydroxyl groups, this paper systematically searched for the transition states of migration paths between multiple adsorption sites and constructed possible desorption paths. The AC edge path is shown in Figures a and , with arrows indicating the migration direction of hydrogen atoms.

Desorption pathway at the hydroxyl-functionalized AC edge: (a) Path 1:T8-T5-T4-T1-H2O and (b) Path 2:T7-T6-T3-T2-CH2–H2.

Along Path 1 (T8 → T5 → T4 → T1 → H2O, Figure a), hydrogen migration between adjacent sites proceeds with relatively low energy barriers in the range of 0.95–1.23 eV, indicating that surface diffusion is kinetically accessible. In contrast, the final desorption step involving the formation of H2O exhibits a significantly higher energy barrier of 4.27 eV, which becomes the rate-determining step along this pathway. This pronounced increase in the energy barrier originates from the strong stabilization of surface intermediates induced by the participation of hydroxyl groups, which effectively suppresses the formation and release of water molecules.

For Path 2 (T7 → T6 → T3 → T2 → CH2 → H2, Figure b), the migration barriers are comparable to those of Path 1, while the final H2 desorption barrier is considerably lower (3.21 eV). The formation of H2 only involves hydrogen recombination and does not require the breaking of strong C–O or O–H bonds, thereby resulting in a reduced desorption barrier. In addition, further calculations reveal that the reaction barrier for the conversion of CH2 into H2O via interaction with a neighboring hydroxyl group is 3.88 eV, which still requires overcoming a relatively high energy barrier. Notably, the lowest-energy configuration along this pathway corresponds to a CH2 intermediate, indicating a locally stabilized state prior to desorption.

These results indicate that hydrogen surface migration along the hydroxyl-functionalized AC edge remains kinetically accessible with energy barriers falling within a relatively narrow range across different pathways. In contrast, hydroxyl functionalization plays a more pronounced role in the desorption process by stabilizing surface intermediates and increasing the kinetic barrier for the formation of H2O. Consequently, hydrogen desorption in the form of H2 is kinetically favored and becomes the dominant desorption product under the considered conditions.

Hydrogen Desorption from Hydroxyl-Functionalized ACR Edges

As shown in Figure b, the ACR edge exhibits multiple potential hydrogen adsorption sites. Among them, the T1 site shows the strongest binding capability, with an adsorption energy of −2.19 eV, indicating that it will act as a key trapping center during the hydrogen desorption process, exerting a significant constraint on hydrogen behavior in the subsequent migration and desorption steps. The T2 and T4 sites, with adsorption energies of −1.57 eV and −1.69 eV, respectively, also display stable adsorption characteristics. In contrast, the adsorption strengths at the T3 and T5 sites are noticeably weaker, while those at the T6 and T7 sites decrease further, forming a clear gradient along the edge. The adsorption characteristics indicate that the local chemical environment of the ACR edge plays a dominant role in modulating hydrogen-binding strength: sites located near reconstructed regions and functionalized areas tend to stabilize hydrogen adsorption, whereas sites farther from these regions are more favorable for hydrogen migration and desorption.

For the possible desorption pathways, compared with the AC edge, the reaction pathways on the hydroxyl-functionalized ACR edge exhibit a distinct intermediate state, CH­(OH), in which a hydrogen atom and a hydroxyl group are coadsorbed on the same carbon atom. This unique structural configuration significantly affects the hydrogen desorption behavior. Notably, this CH­(OH) configuration represents a locally stable intermediate along the reaction pathway. As illustrated in Figures b and , Path 1 (T7 → T3 → T4 → CH­(OH) → H2O, Figure a), involves energy barriers of 0.83, 1.07, 1.76, and 5.21 eV for the successive elementary steps, with the highest barrier of 5.21 eV, corresponding to the rate-determining step along this pathway. Path 2 (T6 → T2 → T1 → T5 → CH2 → H2, Figure b) features corresponding energy barriers of 1.10, 0.91, 1.38, 1.99, and 7.08 eV, where the H2 desorption step (7.08 eV) represents the rate-determining barrier.

Desorption pathway at the hydroxyl-functionalized ACR edge: (a) Path 1:T7-T3-T4-CH­(OH)–H2O and (b) Path 2:T6-T2-T1-T5-CH2.

The rate-determining barriers of both pathways are significantly higher than the corresponding values on the AC edge, indicating stronger hydrogen binding at the ACR edge. This increased desorption barrier arises from the synergistic effects of edge reconstruction and hydroxyl functionalization, which stabilize hydrogen-related intermediates on the potential energy surface and, thereby, enhance hydrogen retention. Compared with the AC edge, the stronger C–H and C–O interactions in the ACR structure require hydrogen to overcome a higher energy cost prior to desorption.

Notably, the presence of hydroxyl groups facilitates the formation of the CH­(OH) intermediate, which is associated with a lower kinetic barrier for H2O formation compared to that for H2 desorption. As a result, based on the rate-determining barriers along each pathway, H2O formation is kinetically more favorable than H2 desorption on the ACR edge under the considered conditions.

Hydrogen Desorption from Hydroxyl-Functionalized ZZ Edge

The possible adsorption sites and adsorption energies at the ZZ edge are shown in Figure c and Table . The adsorption energy distribution at the selected sites shows significant gradient differences. The T1 and T2 sites exhibit the strongest hydrogen binding, with adsorption energies of −1.46 eV and −1.50 eV, respectively. These sites are located at unsaturated carbon atoms in hydroxyl-functionalized regions, where incomplete local coordination and electron-rich characteristics favor the formation of strong C–H interactions, leading to significantly enhanced chemisorption. The T5 and T6 sites show moderate adsorption strengths with adsorption energies of −1.07 eV and −1.05 eV, respectively. At these sites, the carbon atoms are relatively well-coordinated, and the spatial coupling with neighboring hydroxyl groups is weakened, resulting in an intermediate hydrogen-binding strength. In contrast, the T3 and T4 sites exhibit significantly higher adsorption energies of −0.79 eV and −0.53 eV, respectively, implying that hydrogen adsorption at these sites is relatively weak and likely dominated by physisorption. Consequently, H atoms adsorbed at these positions are more prone to desorption or migration toward adjacent sites with a higher reactivity.

Compared with the AC and ACR edges, the migration and desorption pathways at the ZZ edge are more complex and involve multiple competing channels. All identified pathways are illustrated in Figure c, and their corresponding energy barriers for Path 1–3 are presented in Figure . Along Path 1 (T6 → T3 → T2 → CH­(OH), Figure a), the energy barriers are 1.10, 0.70, and 4.28 eV, respectively. Due to the high stability of the CH­(OH) intermediate near the T2 site, the subsequent formation of H2O requires overcoming an extremely high energy barrier, rendering water formation kinetically unfavorable on this pathway. Path 2 (T6 → T3 → T1 → CH2 → H2, Figure b) and Path 3 (T5 → T4 → T1 → CH2 → H2, Figure c) both result in H2 as the final product. Path 2 presents energy barriers of 1.10, 0.70, 2.33, and 4.73 eV. The H2 desorption step (4.73 eV) represents the rate-determining barrier. Notably, the barrier for the T3 → T1 step is identical to that of the T3 → T2 step in path 1, indicating the presence of two competing migration directions originating from the T3 site. In contrast, Path 3 exhibits relatively higher early stage migration barriers of 1.90 and 1.52 eV, reflecting a less favorable initial diffusion process. The high kinetic hindrance for H2O formation can be primarily attributed to the stabilization of the CH­(OH) intermediate formed through the interaction between local hydroxyl groups and strong adsorption sites.

Desorption pathway of hydrogen at the hydroxyl-functionalized ZZ edge: (a) Path 1:T6-T3-T2-CH­(OH), (b) Path 2:T6-T3-T1-CH2-H2, and (c) Path 3: T5-T4-T1-CH2-H2.

As a result, compared with H2 desorption, H2O formation involves a higher rate-determining barrier on the ZZ edge, as it requires multiple bond rearrangements involving C–H and O–H bonds. By comparison, Path 2 features lower barriers in the early migration steps, with hydrogen migration proceeding along an adsorption energy gradient from weakly bound sites toward strongly bound sites, which is favorable for stepwise desorption. Owing to the higher initial migration barriers, Path 3 is kinetically less favorable than Path 2 in the early diffusion stage. Overall, based on the rate-determining barriers of each pathway, hydrogen desorption in the form of H2 is kinetically more favorable than H2O formation on the ZZ edge under the considered conditions. This behavior is closely related to the regulation of adsorption stability and intermediate formation by the local edge structure and hydroxyl functionalization.

Hydrogen Desorption from Hydroxyl-Functionalized ZZR Edge

The adsorption sites on the ZZR edge are shown in Figure d. Compared to the pristine ZZ edge, the overall adsorption strength of this configuration is reduced. Among all sites, the T5 site exhibits the strongest binding ability with the lowest adsorption energy (−2.06 eV). The relatively high activity of the T1 site (−1.98 eV) may be attributed to the electronic modulation induced by the adjacent oxygen-containing functional group. The adsorption energies at the T3 and T6 sites are −1.34 eV, while that of T8 further decreases to −1.12 eV, indicating a gradual weakening of the binding strength across these sites. The spatial distribution of adsorption energies forms a clear gradient: T5, T1, and T2 constitute a high-activity region and are likely to serve as preferred reaction sites; in contrast, the lower-activity sites, such as T3, T6, and T8, may function primarily as temporary hydrogen storage or transmission sites.

Similarly, for the ZZR edge, the migration pathways are shown in Figure d, and the corresponding energy barriers are presented in Figure . Along Path 1 (T8 → T3 → T4 → T5 → CH­(OH) → H2O, Figure a), the early stage migration barriers fall within the range of 0.91–1.32 eV, followed by higher barriers of 2.82 and 7.31 eV for the subsequent steps. The sharply increased barrier associated with the final H2O formation indicates that this pathway is thermodynamically highly unfavorable, with water formation and desorption constituting the primary limiting steps. Path 2 (T8 → T3 → T2 → T1 → CH2 → H2, Figure b) exhibits early migration barriers ranging from 1.06 to 1.17 eV, followed by lower barriers of 1.61 and 3.70 eV in the later steps. Notably, a clear branching of migration pathways is observed at the T3 site, where migration toward T2 is associated with a lower barrier than migration toward T4. This indicates that the local geometric configuration and electronic environment preferentially drive hydrogen accumulation toward sites with stronger binding strength, thereby facilitating CH2 formation and ultimately promoting H2 desorption.

Desorption pathways at the hydroxyl-functionalized ZZR edge: (a) Path 1:T8-T3-T4-T5-CH­(OH)-H2O, (b) Path 2:T8-T3-T2-T1-CH2-H2, (c) Path 3:T6-T4-T5-CH­(OH)-H2O, and (d) Path 4:T7-T2-T1-CH2-H2.

To further investigate the possibility of direct desorption, an additional calculation was performed based on Path 1, focusing on the final step of direct H2O formation from the T5 site. The resulting barrier of 4.66 eV remains significantly higher than the final barrier along the H2 formation pathway, indicating that water formation is still kinetically suppressed even in the presence of edge reconstruction and hydroxyl functionalization. Among the identified pathways, Path 3 (T6 → T4 → T5 → CH­(OH) → H2O, Figure c) and Path 4 (T7 → T2 → T1 → CH2 → H2, Figure d), corresponding to migration pathways originating from the upper-layer of the T8 site in different spatial directions, exhibit initial migration barriers of 0.99 and 1.16 eV, respectively, indicating the feasibility of hydrogen migration from multiple spatial directions.

Compared with the ZZ edge, the final desorption barriers for both H2 and H2O are significantly reduced, suggesting that hydrogen atoms are more readily released. Although molecular hydrogen remains the predominant desorption product, hydrogen mobility and reactivity are more strongly regulated by the reconstructed and functionalized edge structures. The reconstruction and hydroxyl functionalization of the ZZR edge markedly enhance hydrogen migration and lower the overall desorption barriers without altering the reaction selectivity. Since the terminal barrier for H2 desorption is consistently lower than that for H2O formation, H2 remains the thermodynamically and kinetically favored release form, and the practically accessible desorption pathways are primarily governed by the magnitude of the final desorption barriers.

On the basis of the above analysis of different edge configurations, it is noted that although locally stabilized intermediates (e.g., CH2 and CH­(OH)) appear along the migration pathways, these configurations do not correspond to strongly bound hydrogen trapping states. The relatively high desorption barriers are mainly associated with the kinetic limitations of molecular formation and release rather than the existence of stable trapping sites.

Electronic Structure Analysis

To elucidate the chemical bonding and charge transfer between hydrogen and graphite, the changes in the charge density difference (CDD) and density of states (DOS) after hydrogen adsorption on the initial models were analyzed. The atomic interactions can be effectively characterized by the two electronic structure descriptors. The DOS provides information about the distribution and occupancy of electronic orbitals associated with hydrogen and carbon atoms, thereby reflecting the bonding strength between them. In contrast, the CDD reveals the spatial redistribution of the charge density before and after bonding, illustrating both the formation of interatomic bonds and the regions of charge accumulation or depletion.

Figure presents the total density of states and projected density of states of four edge structures after single-H adsorption, including the C–p, O–p, and H–s orbital contributions. All systems exhibit finite electronic states near the Fermi level, dominated primarily by C–p orbitals, indicating that the edge carbon framework remains the principal contributor to the electronic structure.

Density of states (DOS) of graphite edge structures after adsorption of a single hydrogen atom: (a) AC, (b) ACR, (c) ZZ, and (d) ZZR.

Notably, the variations in the distribution of C–p states near the Fermi level among different edges and adsorption sites are in good agreement with the trends in the hydrogen-binding strength obtained from the adsorption energy calculations. Sites with lower adsorption energies generally exhibit a more pronounced enhancement of localized states near the Fermi level, reflecting stronger C–H interactions. The O–p states mainly appear between −2 and −8 eV, corresponding to the bonding states of O–H and C–O. In the −5 to −9 eV region, the pronounced overlap between H–s and O–p orbitals indicates strong sp hybridization, indicating the covalent nature of the O–H bond within the hydroxyl groups.

This orbital hybridization not only stabilizes the hydroxyl-functional groups themselves but also modulates the electronic environment of neighboring carbon atoms, thereby influencing the local electronic environment that is relevant to hydrogen adsorption. Consequently, these electronic effects provide insights into the electronic characteristics of hydrogen at the most stable adsorption sites. It should be noted that the present DOS analysis is based on the most stable adsorption configurations and thus provides representative information about strong adsorption sites, which is consistent with the trends observed in the adsorption energy calculations and subsequent kinetic analyses.

To further elucidate the electronic origin of hydrogen adsorption, Figure displays the CDD of the structure before and after the adsorption of one hydrogen atom at the most stable adsorption site. Yellow regions indicate an increase in the charge density, while blue regions represent a decrease. For all edge structures, a pronounced charge accumulation region is observed between the hydrogen atom and the bonded carbon atom, while charge depletion appears around the hydrogen atom itself and in the carbon nuclear region. This redistribution indicates that hydrogen forms stable C–H covalent bonds with the graphite edges through electron transfer. These features are consistent with the high stabilities of hydrogen at these adsorption sites. The strong charge accumulation in the bonding region suggests significant orbital overlap and strong bonding interactions, which are unfavorable for the direct desorption. It should be noted that this CDD analysis focuses on the most stable adsorption configurations and therefore provides representative insight into the electronic characteristics of strong adsorption sites, which is consistent with the overall trends observed in migration and desorption calculations.

Charge density difference (CDD) before and after adsorption of a single hydrogen atom: (a) AC, (b) ACR, (c) ZZ, and (d) ZZR.

During the desorption stage, distinct electronic rearrangement patterns are observed for the different desorption products. As shown in Figure , during H2 desorption, a portion of the electronic density remains within the carbon framework, and the overall charge redistribution is relatively localized, indicating a relatively limited extent of electronic reorganization. This feature is consistent with the relatively lower desorption barriers observed for H2 formation in kinetic calculations. This trend is in agreement with the migration-desorption pathway analysis, in which H2 formation generally involves lower terminal energy barriers. In contrast, the formation of H2O requires cooperative rearrangement between hydrogen atoms and hydroxyl groups, accompanied by a more extensive redistribution of the electronic density.

Charge density difference (CDD) before and after H2 desorption: (a) AC, (b) ACR, (c) ZZ, and (d) ZZR; CDD before and after H2O desorption: (e) AC, (f) ACR, (g) ZZ, and (h) ZZR.

After water formation, the electron density is mainly localized within the molecular orbitals of the H2O molecule, which indicates a weakened interaction with the edge carbon atoms and is associated with relatively higher desorption barriers. These electronic structure characteristics help explain the higher kinetic hindrance of H2O desorption observed in the present system.

Overall, the DOS and CDD analyses provide insight into how hydroxyl modification and edge reconstruction affect the local electronic structure, which is consistent with their influence on hydrogen adsorption, migration, and desorption behaviors observed in the calculations.

Conclusions

In this work, first-principles calculations were employed to systematically investigate the effects of hydroxyl functionalization on hydrogen adsorption, migration, and desorption at graphite edges using model systems representative of hydroxyl-functionalized graphite edges. The results show that compared with pristine graphite surfaces, edge sites exhibit significantly stronger hydrogen adsorption. The introduction of hydroxyl groups modulates the local electronic structure, leading to pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the adsorption strength along the edges. Strong adsorption sites act as effective hydrogen-trapping centers, whereas neighboring weak adsorption sites facilitate surface migration and subsequent desorption.

Migration and desorption pathway analyses indicate that hydrogen diffusion along graphite edges is associated with relatively low energy barriers, indicating a high surface mobility. The overall reaction kinetics are primarily governed by the final desorption step. For all investigated edge structures, the H2 desorption barriers on the AC and ZZ edges are markedly lower than those for H2O formation, indicating that molecular hydrogen is the dominant release product. For reconstructed edges, the ACR edge exhibits migration barriers comparable to those of the AC edge but significantly increased desorption barriers; notably, the barrier for H2O desorption is relatively reduced, indicating a tendency toward the formation of oxygen-containing species under these modeled conditions. In contrast, the overall desorption barriers on the ZZR edge are lower than those on the ZZ edge, indicating that edge reconstruction weakens the local confinement of hydrogen, enabling easier desorption while maintaining a relatively strong adsorption. Accordingly, the relative difficulty of H2 release follows the order AC < ZZR < ZZ < ACR, whereas the difficulty of H2O release follows the order AC < ACR < ZZR < ZZ.

Electronic structure analyses further elucidated these behaviors. The DOS and CDD results reveal pronounced orbital hybridization and charge accumulation in the C–H and O–H bonding regions upon hydrogen adsorption, accounting for the structural stability of the strong adsorption sites. By contrast, H2 desorption involves only localized electronic rearrangements, whereas H2O formation requires more extensive charge redistribution, which is associated with higher desorption barriers observed in the calculations. Overall, unreconstructed edges favor hydrogen migration and desorption, while edge reconstruction and hydroxyl functionalization significantly regulate the desorption pathways and reaction activity.

This study provides qualitative atomic-scale insights into the adsorption and desorption behavior of hydrogen isotopes at hydroxyl-functionalized graphite edges, elucidating the electronic and kinetic factors that govern hydrogen/tritium mobility and release in model systems incorporating local oxidation effects via hydroxyl functionalization of graphite-based materials. Although the present calculations are based on simplified models and do not explicitly include temperature, molten salt environments, irradiation effects, or dynamic oxidation processes, the identified mechanisms may influence tritium retention and permeation behavior in nuclear graphite under TMSR conditions and provide a theoretical basis for future studies under more realistic operating environments.
