Exploring the shared genetic architecture between leukocyte telomere length and renal cell carcinoma: a cross-trait analysis
Authors: Li Wang, Jian-wei Yang, Kang-yu Wang, Si-yu Chen, Li Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile epidemiological studies suggest an association between leukocyte telomere length (LTL) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), the genetic basis underlying this relationship remains to be elucidated.MethodsWe analyzed genome-wide association studies (GWAS) summary statistics from LTL (n = 472,174) and RCC(3,926 cases; 378,749 controls) cohorts. Comprehensive cross-trait analyses examined genetic correlations, causality, tissue enrichment, and pleiotropic loci.ResultsWe identified significant genetic correlation between LTL and RCC (correlation = 0.0379, p = 0.0012) at both genome-wide and local levels. Meta-analysis revealed 25 novel pleiotropic loci, including a co-localized variant (rs1874330). Novel tissue-specific enrichment was observed in uterine and testicular tissues, with shared functional genes affecting both traits. Mendelian randomization provided evidence for a causal effect of LTL on RCC risk.ConclusionThis study establishes genetic links between LTL and RCC through shared architecture, tissue-specific patterns, and causal pathways, suggesting potential therapeutic targets and risk markers for future validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03565-1.Keywords: Leukocyte telomere length, RCC, Genome-wide cross-trait analysis, Genetic architecture

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) represents a significant global health burden, accounting for approximately 3–5% of all adult malignancies with increasing incidence worldwide [1]. It ranks among the top ten most common cancers, with clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) as the predominant histological subtype [2]. Despite advances in early detection and treatment modalities, the 5-year survival rate for metastatic disease remains below 15% [3]. RCC continues to demonstrate significant resistance to conventional therapeutic approaches, including chemotherapy, hormonal interventions, and radiation treatments [4]. This therapeutic recalcitrance creates an urgent imperative to develop deeper insights into the molecular underpinnings of renal malignancies. Enhanced molecular characterization has the potential to substantially improve diagnostic accuracy, prognostic stratification, and therapeutic efficacy for patients currently affected by RCC.

Telomeres, specialized nucleoprotein structures that cap and protect chromosome ends from degradation and fusion, play crucial roles in maintaining genomic stability [5]. These structures exhibit progressive shortening with each cellular division and aging, eventually triggering cellular senescence or apoptosis. Leukocyte telomere length (LTL) has emerged as a potential biomarker for cancer susceptibility, with epidemiological studies suggesting complex and sometimes paradoxical associations between altered LTL and various malignancies [6]. Several observational studies have reported associations between LTL and RCC risk, with some indicating that longer telomeres may predispose to renal carcinogenesis. However, the underlying biological mechanisms and genetic architecture linking telomere dynamics to renal carcinogenesis remain poorly understood and insufficiently characterized at the molecular level [7]. Despite epidemiological evidence indicating a relationship between LTL and RCC, previous studies have been limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, and insufficient genetic characterization of this association.

Genetic approaches can overcome traditional observational limitations such as reverse causation and confounding. Leveraging the largest available genome-wide association studies (GWAS) datasets, we conducted a systematic analysis incorporating genetic correlation assessment, cross-trait meta-analysis, tissue-specific enrichment analysis, and bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) to elucidate the genetic relationship between LTL and RCC.

Methods

Datasets

We obtained leukocyte telomere length (LTL) summary-level data from the IEU OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/, ID: IEU-b-4879), which included 472,174 samples from the UK Biobank, and RCC summary-level data from the FinnGen Consortium’s 12th edition dataset (https://www.r12.finngen.fi/) [8]. Comprising 382,675 samples (3926 cases and 378,749 controls) classified under ICD-10 code C64. quality control measures included the exclusion of participants with excessive heterozygosity, high genotyping deletion rates (> 5%), uncertain gender status, or non-Finnish ancestry. All analyzed data were restricted to European ancestry populations and underwent rigorous quality control procedures. We excluded SNPs with minor allele frequencies below 1% and deleted SNPs with duplicate or missing RSIDs for further analysis. Additionally, we verified that there was no sample overlap in the datasets used for our analysis.

This study utilized previously ethically approved, publicly available GWAS summary statistics, therefore, no additional ethical approval was required. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Mendelian randomization (STROBE-MR) were followed [9] (Supplementary Table 1). The flow diagram of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Heritability and genetic correlation

Genetic correlation between LTL and RCC was assessed using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC, https://github.com/bulik/ldsc) [10]. This method quantifies polygenicity and confounding bias effects by analyzing the relationship between test statistics and linkage disequilibrium. After excluding SNPs with minor allele frequencies < 0.01, we estimated single-trait SNP heritability for both phenotypes using LDSC with baseline LD models. Given the absence of sample overlap, we implemented unconstrained intercept bivariate LDSC to estimate the genetic correlation coefficient (rg) between LTL and RCC.

Partitioned heritability

Functional genomic architecture underlying the LTL- RCC genetic correlation was investigated using Stratified Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression (S-LDSC) [11]. This approach partitions SNPs into distinct functional categories based on genomic annotations from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel [12]. We examined over 30 functional elements, encompassing coding sequences, evolutionarily conserved regions, and regulatory elements such as DNase I hypersensitive sites.

Local genetic correlation analysis

Shared genetic architecture between LTL and RCC was investigated at the regional level using Local Analysis of Variant Association (LAVA) [13]. The genome was segmented into 2,495 regions (~ 1 Mb each) based on the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel [14]. We focused our local genetic correlation analyses on regions displaying significant SNP heritability (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05/2495) for both phenotypes.

Cross-trait GWAS meta-analysis

To explore shared genetic architecture between LTL and renal cell carcinoma, we implemented two distinct cross-phenotype analytical approaches. The primary analysis utilized Multi-trait Analysis of GWAS (MTAG) [15], which operates under two key principles: the potential pleiotropic effects of individual SNPs and the multivariate normal distribution of effect sizes across traits. MTAG enhances statistical power by integrating a covariance matrix of trait effect sizes while accounting for sample overlap and genetic correlations. In addition, we supplemented this with Cross-Phenotype Association Analysis (CPASSOC) [16], which examines genetic variants across multiple phenotypes. This method incorporates two complementary statistics: SHet, which accounts for effect size heterogeneity through sample-size weighted meta-analysis, and SHom, which employs fixed-effect meta-analysis. The SHet approach offers particular advantage in scenarios with cross-phenotype heterogeneity, providing robust statistical power while accommodating effect size variations between traits.

To identify significant genetic variants, we implemented a three-step analytical approach. First, we identified significant variants using a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−8) in both MTAG and CPASSOC analyses. we then performed clumping analysis using PLINK v1.9 with specific criteria (primary significance threshold: 5 × 10−8, secondary significance threshold: 1 × 10−5, LD threshold: r2 = 0.2, 500 kb) to identify independent signals [17]. novel loci were defined by two conditions: no previous genome-wide significance (P ≥ 5 × 10−8) and no LD with known variants (r2 ≤ 0.01 within 1000 kb) in LTL or RCC GWAS studies.

Colocalization analysis and SNP annotation

To investigate shared genetic determinants between LTL and RCC, we performed colocalization analysis using the “Coloc” package. We extracted GWAS summary statistics within 1 Mb regions centered on each independent SNP and applied Bayesian inference to calculate posterior probabilities. Loci with posterior probability of shared causal variant (PPH4) > 0.7 were considered evidence of colocalization between the two traits [18]. Finally, we characterized the identified variants using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) for functional prediction and 3DSNP for chromatin interaction analysis [19, 20].

Tissue-specific enrichment analysis

To characterize tissue-specific genetic architecture, we employed a multi-layered analytical approach. We first conducted stratified-LDSC (S-LDSC) enrichment analysis using GTEx V8 data spanning 53 tissue types [11, 21], evaluating heritability enrichment through regression coefficient Z-score p-values with adjustments for baseline models and gene sets. Subsequently, we examined tissue-specific gene expression patterns and their associations with LTL and RCC, validating our findings through MAGMA analysis [22]. Gene-trait associations were quantified using MTAG summary statistics of proximal SNPs [23], followed by enrichment analysis using predefined Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) gene sets [24]. Finally, we assessed tissue specificity through MAGMA gene characterization, leveraging GTEx v8 expression profiles across 54 tissues to elucidate the relationship between tissue-specific expression patterns and disease-associated genes. We did not correct for multiple testing in this exploratory study, P less than 0.05 was considered positively significant [25].

Bidirectional Mendelian randomization

We explored the causal relationship between LTL and RCC through complementary Mendelian randomization approaches. For instrumental variable selection, we applied rigorous criteria including genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8), LD independence (r² < 0.001, 10,000 kb window), and strength assessment (F-statistic > 10). Our primary analysis utilized inverse variance weighted regression [26], supported by multiple sensitivity analyses (MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode) [27]. To assess result robustness and potential bias, we performed horizontal pleiotropy detection through MR-Egger intercepts [28], outlier identification via MR-PRESSO, and leave-one-out analysis [29]. We further implemented generalized summary data-based Mendelian randomization (GSMR) analysis, which uniquely accounts for both SNP linkage disequilibrium and effect size uncertainties while incorporating genome-wide significant variants [30]. Using PhenoScanner, we screened the exposed SNPs for potential confounders and excluded any whose effects could bias the outcome.

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization

We performed Summary-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) to examine pleiotropic associations between gene expression and phenotypes using eQTL and GWAS data from shared tissue types identified in enrichment analyses [31]. Causal genes were identified using significance thresholds of PSMR < 0.05 and heterogeneity in dependent instrument (HEIDI) test PHEIDI >0.01. All analyses were conducted using the SMR web portal (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/smr-portal/).

Results

Genetic correlations

LDSC analysis revealed significant positive genetic correlation between LTL and RCC (genetic correlation = 0.122, p = 0.0012) (Supplementary Table 2). Further LAVA analysis identified six genetic regions spanning 1.23 Mb across different chromosomes with significant heritability (P < 0.05/4595) for both traits. Among 31 regions showing significant heritability, seven loci exhibited significant local genetic correlations (P < 0.05/31), with chr 20: 62,179,242–62,963,627 being the strongest for LTL with RCC (p = 5.23E-09) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). Our functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct patterns for LTL and RCC. LTL-associated SNPs showed enrichment in 26 functional categories, with the strongest signal in MAF_Adj_ASMCL (enrichment = 2.22 × 1013, p = 1.31E-07). For RCC, SNP enrichment was identified in 10 functional categories, primarily in GERP.RSsup4L2_0 (enrichment = 32.68, p = 0.026). Notably, both traits shared significant enrichment in three categories: background selection, CpG content within 50 kb windows, and super enhancer regions (Supplementary Tables 4–6).

Integrating MTAG and CPASSOC approaches in our cross-trait GWSA analysis between LTL and RCC, we identified 87 novel pleiotropic SNPs linked to both traits (Supplementary Table 7). This identification excluded SNPs that either reached genome-wide significance (p < 5E-8) or showed LD (1,000 kb, r2 = 0.3) in single-trait GWAS only. Tissue-specific heritability analysis using S-LDSC showed LTL enrichment in EBV-transformed lymphocytes and RCC enrichment in subcutaneous adipose tissue, with both traits showing shared enrichment in testis (Supplementary Tables 8–9). Further MAGMA analysis across 54 tissues revealed additional joint genetic enrichment in uterus and ovary for both phenotypes (Figs. 4 and 5; Supplementary Tables 10–11).

Identification of shared functional genes

Leveraging GTEx_eQTL data, SMR analysis identified shared functional genes between LTL and RCC, with both traits showing substantial SNP heritability in S-LDSC and MAGMA analyses. Two genes emerged as significant in both uterus and testis: IRF5 (uterus: pSMR=0.0001, pHEIDI=0.74; testis: pSMR=0.0007, pHEIDI=0.87) and UCN2 (uterus: pSMR=4.86E-05, pHEIDI=0.025; testis: pSMR=8.44E-05, pHEIDI=0.018) (Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 12). Notably, the identification of IRF5 in our analysis corroborates previous findings where it was reported as a novel gene, strengthening the robustness of our results.

Mendelian randomization

Bidirectional MR analysis explored the causal relationship between LTL and RCC. All instrumental variables (IVs) met quality criteria with F-statistics > 10 and no evident confounding (Supplementary Tables 13–14). Forward analysis revealed a positive causal effect of LTL on RCC, supported by both IVW (β = 0.706, p = 1.61E-04) and GSMR (β = 0.521, p = 1.8E-09) methods (Fig. 7). The MR-Egger intercept (p = 0.054) showed no directional pleiotropy, and leave-one-out analysis confirmed result stability (Supplementary Figs. 1). Conversely, reverse analysis found no significant causal effect of RCC on LTL using IVW (β= −0.011, p = 0.125) and GSMR (β= −0.059, p = 0.119), with no horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger p = 0.377) and stable leave-one-out results (Supplementary Figs. 2, Table 15).

Discussion

Through comprehensive genetic analyses integrating large-scale GWAS and tissue-specific expression data, we uncovered previously unrecognized genetic links between LTL and RCC. Our systematic investigation revealed significant genetic overlap at both genome-wide and local levels, suggesting shared biological mechanisms underlying these traits. The genetic architecture was further characterized by identifying pleiotropic loci using MTAG and CPASSOC approaches, detecting functional shared genes through SMR analysis, and establishing directional relationships via MR methods. These findings provide the first evidence of genetic convergence between LTL and RCC pathogenesis.

Direct measurement of telomere length in renal epithelial cells would be optimal for assessing its relationship with RCC risk. While genetic variants specific to renal telomere length are currently unavailable, the observed correlation (r = 0.44) between leukocyte and kidney tissue telomere lengths [32] suggests that longer leukocyte telomeres may reflect extended telomeres in renal cells. We hypothesize this could promote tumor development through enhanced cellular replication capacity, though further validation is needed. These findings suggest caution in the long-term use of telomerase-activating treatments (e.g., androgen therapy) [33], particularly in high-risk individuals.

Our genetic analyses revealed shared architecture between LTL and RCC through both genome-wide and local genetic correlations. Three co-enriched functional categories - background selection signals, CpG content, and super-enhancer elements - indicate that these shared regions are evolutionarily conserved and play crucial roles in transcriptional regulation. The convergence of these features provides molecular evidence for common regulatory mechanisms potentially underlying both telomere length and RCC risk. Through genome-wide genetic correlation analysis and cross-trait GWAS meta-analysis combined with LAVA-associated regions, we identified 25 novel SNPs. Among these, IRF5 emerged as a significant candidate through co-localization testing. As a member of the interferon regulatory factor family, IRF5 functions as a transcription factor with diverse roles in immune regulation, inflammatory responses, cell death, and cancer development [34]. This factor critically modulates the tumor microenvironment by regulating type I interferon (IFN-α/β) and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [35]. Clinical evidence has established IRF5 as an independent prognostic indicator in non-metastatic ccRCC, where lower expression correlates with aggressive tumor behavior and poorer survival outcomes [35]. However, the precise role of IRF5 in cancer development remains complex, as evidenced by its consistent downregulation or absence in various immortalized cancer cell lines and primary hematological malignancies [36].

Integrative analysis of GTEx tissue expression profiles revealed significant shared genetic enrichment between LTL and RCC across reproductive tissues, particularly in uterus, testis, and ovary. Through blood and tissue-specific eQTL analysis, combined with SMR and HEIDI methodologies, we identified IRF5 and UCN2 as potential mediating genes linking these traits, with evidence specifically observed in uterine and testicular tissues. Epigenetic modifications unique to reproductive tissues may influence telomere maintenance and tumor-related gene expression. As a CRF family peptide hormone, UCN2 acts through CRF2 receptor binding. In tumors, UCN2 exhibits dual effects: it can suppress tumor growth while also promoting tumor progression by modulating immune responses, angiogenesis, and cell survival in the tumor microenvironment [37, 38].

We employed both IVW and GSMR methods to enhance result reliability. Our findings revealed that genetically determined longer LTL increased RCC risk, contrasting with traditional case-control studies that linked shorter telomeres to kidney cancer susceptibility [39]. While telomere shortening promotes genetic instability and malignant transformation during tumorigenesis, Morais and colleagues suggest a dual mechanism: shorter telomeres may initiate RCC through genetic instability, while longer telomeres potentially drive tumor progression [40]. Our study has several limitations to note: the exclusive use of European-ancestry data, while reducing population stratification bias, limits generalizability to other ethnicities; summary statistics prevented analysis of age and gender effects; and computationally identified pleiotropic elements require cautious interpretation. While we demonstrated a genetic link between leukocyte telomere length and RCC, the underlying biological mechanisms await validation through prospective studies and molecular experiments.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates genetic links between LTL and RCC, highlighting shared genetic elements, tissue-specific regions, and causal relationships. These findings offer insights for therapeutic development and risk assessment in RCC, though further validation studies are warranted.
