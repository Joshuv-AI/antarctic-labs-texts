Food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome: Disease burden for patients and families
Authors: Bianca N Patel, Jonathan S Tam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The impact of food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES) extends beyond the direct effects after consumption of an inciting food. Although elimination of the trigger food does alleviate the symptoms of FPIES, it does not address the burden of disease among patients and families living with FPIES. When managing FPIES, consideration should be taken with regard to the potential impact and complications attributed to food avoidance.Objective:The objective was to review the epidemiology of FPIES and discuss the associated complications of FPIES, such as nutritional deficiencies, food aversions, financial burden, and overall effect on quality of life of patients and families with FPIES.Methods:A literature review was conducted through the aid of the University of Southern California Norris Medical Library services. Only peer-reviewed sources were used.Results:The incidence of FPIES ranges from 0.0041 to 0.7%. When compared with individuals with immunoglobulin E (IgE) meditated food allergies, caregivers of children with FPIES reported poor quality of life and increased feeding difficulties. Individuals with multiple trigger foods are more likely to develop food aversion compared with individuals with one food trigger. Failure to thrive, poor weight gain, and nutritional deficiencies can be seen because of food restriction in patients with FPIES and do not just represent presenting symptoms of FPIES.Conclusion:There was poorer quality of life and increased psychosocial burden among individuals with FPIES and caregivers of individuals with FPIES. Given the associated complications of nutritional deficiency, feeding difficulties, food aversion, and psychosocial stress, FPIES management should involve a multidisciplinary approach that involves nutrition, speech therapy, occupational therapy, and/or mental health services. Further research and data are needed to assess the financial burden of disease on individuals and families affected by FPIES and the long-term impact that FPIES may have on the growth and nutrition.Keywords: food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome, burden, complications

Food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES) is a non-immunoglobulin E (IgE) mediated food allergy which leads to delayed (1–4 hours) gastrointestinal symptoms. As outlined in the 2017 international consensus guidelines,1 FPIES can present as a life-threatening condition, with symptoms that range from profuse and repetitive emesis, bloody diarrhea (within 24 hours of ingestion), dehydration, metabolic derangements, hypotension, lethargy, hypotonia, hypothermia, pallor, and/or failure to thrive.1 If the trigger food is regularly ingested, then patients may have chronic FPIES and primarily present with intermittent emesis, frequent diarrhea, poor weight gain, and/or failure to thrive. Analysis of current epidemiologic data suggests a potentially higher prevalence than previously thought. The delay in symptoms, lack of noninvasive testing and biomarkers, and varying presentation has led to FPIES generally being underrecognized.

Food triggers for FPIES commonly include, but are not limited to, cow’s milk, grains, rice, soy, eggs, seafood, shellfish, meats, fruits, and vegetables.2–4 FPIES can be caused by one or multiple foods. Recent changes in food introduction guidelines and cultural practices may contribute to the differences of commonly reported trigger foods across different groups.5 For example, there have been reports of an increase in the number of peanut-triggered FPIES, which could potentially be secondary to changes in practice parameters that promote the early introduction of allergenic foods in infancy.6 Although more commonly seen in infancy and within the first year of life during solid-food introduction, adult cases are increasing in number.5,7,8 Treatment for FPIES involves removing the trigger food from the diet with the possibility of later introducing and tolerating the trigger food. Although avoidance of the trigger food(s) remains the mainstay of treatment, it is still critical to recognize how a multidisciplinary approach may be necessary given the multiplicity of effects that FPIES may have on the patient and family. Elimination of the trigger food does not eliminate the associated complications of FPIES, such as nutritional deficiencies, food aversions, financial burden, stress in the introduction of other foods, and the overall effect on quality of life. Identifying the associated trends and complications of FPIES can perhaps lead to improved patient-centered care and help ameliorate the burden of disease.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

The incidence of FPIES ranges from 0.0041 to 0.7% based on various large population studies across the world. A cross-sectional, population-based survey conducted in the United States by Nowak-Wegrzyn et al.9 estimates the lifetime prevalence of FPIES to be 0.51% among children and 0.22% in adults. Of American children with FPIES, higher rates of incidence were found among those of Asian and/or non-Hispanic descent. Among parent-reported data, 4.9% of families had multiple children with FPIES and 1.9% reported a parent with FPIES.9 Both children and adults were noted to have increased rates of IgE-mediated food allergy and other atopic diseases. Based on these estimates, ∼1 million people in the United States may be affected by FPIES.9 In another U.S.-based retrospective chart review2 conducted among pediatric patients, researchers found the overall incidence of FPIES to be 0.17 to 0.42%, with a peak incidence occurring at 6 months of age. Ruffner et al.2 also found the incidence of new-onset FPIES to be 0.0041% in children ages 3 to 12 years.

By using the Australian Pediatric Surveillance Unit, Mehr et al. administered an Australia-wide survey to identify infants up to the age of 24 months with FPIES. The incidence of FPIES among Australian children was noted to be 15.4 of 100,000 births per year (0.15%). At least 8% of infants identified were noted to have a sibling with a history of FPIES. A concurrent history of at least one allergic disorder was noted among 49% of infants. Thirty percent of infants with atopic dermatitis and FPIES were also found to have an IgE-mediated food allergy. Sixty-eight percent of those identified had one food trigger.3

Further variations in the incidence of disease can be seen across other population-based studies. For example, in a prospective longitudinal study in Spain, known as the Prevalence of food allergies in Leganés pediatric population (PREVALE) study,10 found the incidence of FPIES to be 0.7%. By comparison, in a prospective large population-based study conducted among infants in Israel, an incidence of 0.34% was found in infants with cow’s milk FPIES.11 Similarly, in a retrospective, questionnaire-based study among different neonatal intensive care units in Japan, there was 0.21% incidence of cow’s milk FPIES.4

These variations in the incidence may exist due to the differences in data collection, lack of well-controlled prospective studies, and underreporting of FPIES. The methods in these retrospective studies vary in how patients were identified, including population-based surveys, physician surveys, and multicenter chart reviews. Due to its presentation, limitations in the understanding of the disease process, and recent establishment of International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision code for FPIES in 2016, the diagnosis of FPIES may have been missed and, therefore, historically underreported by physicians.12

COMPLICATIONS

Although FPIES may be a self-limiting food allergy, there are long-term consequences associated with FPIES. The diagnosis of FPIES is typically made during a crucial period of child development. The process of food introduction is a developmental milestone, which is not only essential for growth and nutrition but for the development and acquisition of sensory, motor, oral, and self-regulatory skills. This period of development is challenging in itself and is further complicated by a restrictive diet in those with FPIES. Associated complications with FPIES are feeding difficulties, food aversions, poor weight gain, malnutrition, failure to thrive, financial burden, and decreased quality of life for both family members and patients (Table 1).

Quality of Life

Given the severity of symptoms and dietary restrictions, individuals with FPIES require a degree of vigilance that may affect their psychosocial health. Because most cases occur in children, parents and/or guardians are often the ones most impacted by the burden of disease management. Parents of children with FPIES are affected by the psychosocial impact of disease and management, and should be considered when managing patients with FPIES.

Increased psychosocial stress was noted by Maciag et al.13 among caregivers of individuals with FPIES who used various validated and unvalidated surveys and questionnaires. The validated questionnaires were composed of the Food Allergy Quality of Life-Parental Burden, Parent Perceived Stress Scale-10, and the Penn State Worry Questionnaire. These surveys were distributed to caregivers who self-identified as having a child with FPIES at the FPIES Education Conference in Philadelphia and through the International FPIES Association website and Facebook page.13 Although 410 responses were obtained, their responses may not be reflective of the majority of FPIES caregivers given their personal engagement in the FPIES community. A high degree of burden was reported by caregivers with regard to making vacation plans, choosing a restaurant, or attending social engagements. Caregivers also reported high rates of general anxiety, separation anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorders compared with published norms. Caregivers whose incomes were < $100,000 and caregivers who reported multiple triggers had poorer quality of life and self-efficacy scores but not increased stress, worry, or anxiety when compared with high-income households or to single-trigger groups.13 Poorer quality of life, increased stress, and lower self-efficacy were found among caregivers whose child had cow’s milk FPIES when compared with other FPIES triggers. Of the children with FPIES who did not attend daycare, 54% did not attend specifically because of their FPIES.13 Caregivers of children with FPIES who did not attend daycare reported increased stress, worry, anxiety, and poor quality of life compared with caregivers of children with FPIES who did attend daycare.13

Similarly, a study by Flom et al.,14 which used the Montreal Children’s Hospital Feeding Scale (MCH-FS), a validated survey, found parents of children with FPIES reported lower quality of life and increased stress in comparison with parents of children with IgE-mediated food allergies. In a smaller study, of 61 parents with children with FPIES and 131 parents of children with IgE-mediated food allergy, the parents with children with FPIES had reported lower quality of life and increased feeding difficulties in comparison with parents with a child with an IgE-mediated food allergy as measured by an adapted quality of life survey and the MCH-FS.15

Given that FPIES is less commonly seen in adults, data with regard to the quality of life in adults with FPIES are more limited. One study found variation among the quality of life based on their trigger food. Adults with egg FPIES had reported poorer quality of life compared with other trigger foods, whereas adults with shellfish FPIES had reported better quality of life compared with other trigger foods.16 Further research is needed to assess the quality of life and stress associated with adult FPIES.

Increased stress, increased degree of burden, and decreased quality of life are commonly seen among caregivers of those with FPIES.13–15 Providers should consider addressing quality of life on both the patient and guardians during routine assessments of a child with FPIES to develop a holistic, patient-centered approach to care. Offering psychotherapeutic treatment options to families may be beneficial to parents, improve quality of life, and decrease stress associated with FPIES.17

Feeding Difficulties and/or Food Aversion

Although feeding difficulties in infancy are a common occurrence, there is concern that there are higher rates of feeding difficulties and food aversions among children with food allergies. Although the prevalence of feeding difficulties or food aversions in the general pediatric population is difficult to estimate based on various diagnostic criteria, questionnaires, and recall reports, Hill et al.18 suggest that the feeding difficulties in healthy children can range from 20 to 45%. This discrepancy among the prevalence of food aversions in the general pediatric population makes it difficult to compare reports of food allergy–related food aversion, which is estimated to range from 13.6 to 40% among either patients with IgE- or with non–IgE-mediated food allergy.18 In a case report by Mattingly et al.,19 the researchers discuss the case of a 9-month old with FPIES who developed food aversions and the multidisciplinary approach that was required to facilitate the introduction of certain foods into her diet. Current literature suggests that non–IgE-mediated food allergies and FPIES may be associated with a higher risk of feeding difficulties, food aversions, and longer-term complications.19,20

Feeding difficulties may be more prevalent in patients with FPIES versus other IgE-mediated food allergies. When using the MCH-FS, Flom et al.14 found that parents of children with FPIES reported a statistically significant increase in feeding difficulties compared with parents of children with IgE-mediated food allergies. A high feeding difficulty score of 52 was reported by parents of children with FPIES compared with a median score of 33 reported by parents of children with IgE-mediated food allergies.14 Similarly, when using the MCH-FS, Groetch et al.15 found that parents of children with FPIES reported significantly higher rates of feeding difficulties in comparison with parents of children with IgE-mediated food allergies. This increase in feeding difficulty may be related to the severity of symptoms associated with FPIES in comparison with other food allergies. Increased attention and education surrounding feeding difficulties in patients with FPIES may help to ameliorate food aversions and assist with the safe introduction of other foods.

In a U.S.-based retrospective study that identified 203 patients with FPIES, Su et al.21 found that patients who had multiple triggers were more likely to develop food aversions (36.4%) in comparison with patients with just one trigger (14.2%). Su et al.21 found that there was a 1.65 time increase of food aversion development with the addition of each individual trigger food. In addition, the development of food aversions was more common in those with a wheat trigger (13.6%) or a family history of food allergies (45.4%).21 These findings may suggest a further need for closer collaboration with nutrition, speech, or occupational therapy to help reduce the impact of long-term feeding difficulties among patients with FPIES. Earlier intervention may further benefit individuals with multiple food trigger FPIES and may help to promote dietary diversity and tolerance of nontrigger foods.

Food aversion and feeding difficulties may persist after the resolution of FPIES. Even though it is known that FPIES can be self-limited and children may later tolerate their trigger food, there is concern for the development or persistence of a food aversion to the specific trigger food. Although not specific to FPIES, in a cross-sectional survey among 197 pediatric patients with food allergies, Vanijcharoenkarn et al.22 found a 39% prevalence of food aversion among patients despite passing physician-witnessed oral food challenges. The implications of this may help to guide management of patients with FPIES, which suggests closer follow up and the continuation of collaboration with dietitians and/or speech and/or occupational therapy after having passed an oral food challenge.

A multidisciplinary approach among physicians, dieticians, and speech and occupational therapists may help to address the increased risk of food aversion and feeding difficulties associated with FPIES. It is also important to recognize that this increased risk of food aversion persists despite the successful tolerance of the trigger food and suggests the need for close continued follow up despite the resolution of FPIES.

Nutritional Impact

Although part of the diagnostic criteria for chronic FPIES includes poor growth, it is important to recognize the effects of growth and nutrition in patients with FPIES. Patients with FPIES are at a higher risk of vitamin D, zinc, and iron deficiencies, and may require supplementation to ensure adequate intake and closer follow up to assess longitudinal growth. Those with multiple food triggers are presumably at higher risk for nutritional deficiencies given the increase in dietary restriction. Patients with FPIES would benefit from dietary and/or nutritional consultation to assess for any nutritional deficiencies and assist in dietary planning and education once elimination of the trigger food occurs.23

Not only is poor weight gain a presenting feature of chronic FPIES, but it also represents a known complication of acute FPIES. There have been reports of malnutrition, failure to thrive, and poor weight gain related to disease presentation, food restriction, and secondary to issues associated with safe introduction of new foods. Of the 74 pediatric cases identified by Blackman et al.,24 16 subjects (22%) were noted to have failure to thrive. The diagnosis of failure to thrive among the group identified by Blackman et al.24 was made as a separate diagnosis unrelated to FPIES and was due to poor nutritional intake in the setting of a restrictive diet and limited education with regard to the introduction of other foods.

There is also evidence to suggest that increased rates of poor weight gain have not only been associated with chronic FPIES, but also with multiple trigger foods, cow’s milk, and banana FPIES.21 Meyer et al.25 found that improved weight was observed after the elimination of the trigger food, the introduction of appropriate dietary alternatives, and nutrient supplementation over a 4-week period. Many alternative cow’s milk products or allergen alternative products are low in protein and differ in the micronutrient and macronutrient content, which may further impact growth rates in restrictive diets.25 Further longitudinal research may be required to assess the long-term implications of FPIES on growth and nutrition.

Financial Burden

Data with regard to the cost of care specifically related to FPIES are limited. For infants with cow’s milk or soy FPIES, parents may be restricted to breast-feeding or to hypoallergenic formulas. Commercially, hypoallergenic, amino acid–based, or extensively hydrolyzed formulas are often more expensive than standard infant formulas and represent an increased financial burden associated with FPIES management compared with infants who do not require alternative formula options. Although allergen alternative food products are available on the market, they are not equivalent in cost and regional availability.

Cost-effectiveness analyses of the use of hydrolyzed versus cow’s milk based formulas in patients with cow’s milk protein allergy (CMPA) demonstrate an overall cost savings to the health-care system due to the decreased need for additional resources secondary to food allergy complications.26,27 In an analysis by Paquete et al.,28 the average largest cost, at 69%, was due to hypoallergenic formulas and represents the largest economic burden associated with CMPA management. Similarly, Suratannon et al.26 found that the majority of cost-associated CMPA management was due to alternative formulas. It can be extrapolated that a similar trend is seen in patients with FPIES who require formula alternatives.

Current analyses demonstrate a decreased financial burden on the health-care system through the use of appropriate dietary substitutes but do not address the individual and the direct costs accrued, ranging from provider visits, transportation, child-care accommodations secondary to food restrictions, and/or time taken off work for appointments.29 Further research to assess the both direct and indirect costs may be required to better understand the true financial burden associated with FPIES.

CONCLUSION

The prevalence of FPIES may be underestimated in children and is certainly underrecognized in adults. The burden of disease among individuals diagnosed with FPIES and caregivers of those with FPIES remains high. This subsequently results in restrictive food behaviors, increased psychosocial stress, diminished quality of life, poor growth, and reduced nutritional intake. When managing FPIES, it is important to use a multidisciplinary approach that focuses on the patient and the family. Further education and close collaboration with dieticians and speech and/or occupational therapy may help to minimize the risk of nutritional deficiencies, poor growth, feeding difficulties, and food aversions. Increased utilization of dieticians may also help families navigate dietary needs and assist in the comfort level of families with regard to the introduction of new foods. Referral to mental health services may alleviate the psychosocial burden and improve the quality of life of families and those with FPIES. Unfortunately, there are limited data available with regard to the effects on child development, burden of disease among adults, and the financial impact of FPIES. These areas represent a clear unmet need in the literature and research that may help improve patient care.
