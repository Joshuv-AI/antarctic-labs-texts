The Association Between Usual Diet and Bowel Disorders of Gut-Brain Interaction: Analyses From the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study
Authors: Daniel Bushyhead, Rutaba Khatun, Shrikant I Bangdiwala, Olafur Palsson, Ami D Sperber, Eamonn M M Quigley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsFood is thought to play a central role in the pathophysiology of disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI). Data on these associations come largely from Western countries. We evaluated associations between diet and common bowel DGBI using the database of the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study (RFGES).MethodsThe RFGES database contains data collected via the Internet, personal interviews, or both from 33 countries, providing prevalence rates for common DGBI. Logistic regressions were used to analyze associations between DGBI and vegan, vegetarian, lactose-free and bread-, pasta-, and rice-predominant diets.Results54 127 internet and 20 973 household survey subjects were included. Rates of adherence to a vegan or vegetarian diet were low in both surveys (0.4% to 9.8%) and differences in dietary preference were also noted between surveys with lactose-free and rice-predominant diets being more common among household survey respondents. While discrepancies between results from the 2 surveys limited the interpretation of the data, some trends were evident with a lactose-free diet linked to an increased prevalence and bread- and rice-predominant diets to lower prevalence rates of several bowel DGBI.ConclusionsThough complicated by variations in results between the 2 survey populations, this global survey has revealed dietary factors that may impact, in the general population, on reported prevalence rates for DGBI.

INTRODUCTION

The association between diet and gastrointestinal health has been the topic of much recent discussion and scientific inquiry. An increasing body of medical literature has underscored the role of dietary factors in the development and management of disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI), formerly known as functional gastrointestinal disorders, which include irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and other functional bowel disorders (FD).1-3 These disorders, characterized by symptom clusters and codified by the Rome criteria, are some of the most common gastrointestinal disorders, and contribute substantially to decreased health related quality of life and high medical costs.4-7

The pathophysiology of DGBI included under the category of bowel disorders (IBS, functional constipation, functional diarrhea, and functional bloating) is multifactorial and includes brain-gut axis disruption, changes in the gut microbiome, immune system dysregulation and visceral hypersensitivity.8-10 The role of diet in DGBI has received recent attention as both contributing to the pathophysiology of, and informing therapeutic options for, DGBI.1-3 Most of the data on diet and DGBI comes from intervention studies where diet is altered, and the therapeutic impact assessed. While such studies have identified dietary strategies, such as a diet low in fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols (FODMAPs), that reduce symptom burden, the relationship between an individual’s usual or customary diet and the presence of DGBI has been studied less. While individual studies have variably revealed relationships between higher intake of spicy foods,11,12 caffeine,13,14 as well as consuming diets high in fat, protein and sugar (as in Western, “fast food” and pro-inflammatory),12,14-17 ultra-processed foods18,19 or FODMAPS20 and an increase in prevalence of IBS, others have reported an association between higher intake of fiber,13,21 and vegetables15,22 as well as vegetarian16,23 and Mediterranean diets24 and a lower prevalence of IBS. These associations were not universally consistent,25,26 however, and were performed in restricted populations (eg, by age, sex, geographic location or occupation), thereby, exposing their results to the influence of the many socio-economic and cultural factors that influence dietary choices.

The primary aim of this study is to analyze the database of the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study to provide a more global assessment of relationships between diet and DGBI by evaluating associations between certain dietary patterns and the prevalence of 5 common DGBI. The secondary aim is to evaluate associations between diet as defined by starch predominance and DGBI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample

The data analyzed in this study were derived from a comprehensive survey questionnaire. To ensure consistency and accessibility the complete study questionnaire was translated by a professional company into 21 languages with linguistic validation (cognitive debriefing) and cultural adaptation. Where appropriate the translations were “localized” to dialects within a given language. All translations were reviewed by clinical monitors and the final versions were approved by native professional experts in gastrointestinal disorders. Dietary data were collected in the same manner as all other data in the questionnaire – all questionnaires were de-identified.

Adult participants from both the Internet and Household surveys of the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study were studied, which has been described previously.4,27 The surveys were conducted in 33 countries: Argentina, Australia, Bangladesh, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, China, Colombia, Egypt, France, Germany, Ghana, Holland, India, Indonesia, Iran, Israel, Italy, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, Nigeria, Poland, Romania, Russia, Singapore, South Africa, South Korea, Spain, Sweden, Turkey, the United Kingdom and the United States.

In countries where most adults use the Internet, a secure, anonymous online survey was conducted using population samples.27 In countries in which an Internet survey was not feasible, personal interviews were conducted in selected villages and cities without national representation.27 The household survey countries were Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, Iran, Malaysia, and Nigeria. In China and Turkey, data were collected with both in-person and Internet surveys.

For the Internet surveys a global market research company (Qualtrics Inc, Provo, Utah, USA) enrolled subjects already registered to participate in Internet surveys. For the household surveys directions were provided in each country by a principal investigator experienced in DGBI research and face-to-face interviewing methodology. Surveys were performed according to a standardized operations manual provided by the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study (RFGES). In each country, the local principal investigator selected study areas in several distinct geographic areas and balanced between urban vs rural residence, where possible. One survey participant was selected per household for surveying.27

In China, household and Internet surveys were conducted independently of each other. In Turkey, the Internet survey was performed first throughout the country and the household survey then designed to sample a population with the same demographic characteristics and geographic distribution throughout the country as the Internet survey.27

Precise details of the conduct of the survey in each of the household survey countries have been published previously.27 To summarize, for each country a sampling frame was developed which included basic demographics and then sorted into strata to ensure appropriate representation by sex and age groups. A simple random sample of the appropriate size (proportional to the size of the stratum) was then randomly selected for a total of at least 2000 individuals. The randomly selected individual was then contacted to set up the interview.

Variables

Bowel DGBI (irritable bowel syndrome, functional constipation, functional diarrhea, and functional bloating) were defined using Rome IV criteria,28 based on responses to the Rome IV Diagnostic Questionnaire. Subtyping of IBS according to predominant bowel habit, ie, constipation, diarrhea, mixed or unclassified, was also performed based on Rome IV guidelines.

The vegan diet was defined as not consuming milk products, meat from animals, fish and eggs; vegetarian diet as not consuming meat from animals and fish; and lactose-free diet as not consuming milk products. Bread-, pasta- and rice-predominant diets were defined as eating bread, pasta or rice 4-7 days a week, respectively, and eating the other 2 carbohydrate sources 0-3 days a week.

Statistical Methods

Data are presented separately for Internet and household surveys participants as prior studies demonstrated important differences in prevalence of DGBI between these survey methods, even when performed in the same country.27 This may have been related to a difference in willingness to report DGBI when interviewed in person rather than when completing a survey online.

The frequency distribution of the various types of diets was initially examined separately, by mode of survey. Bivariate logistic regression examined the unadjusted association of the various types of diet with bowel DGBI. Associations between diets and bowel DGBI identified in this analysis were then further explored in multivariate analyses. Multivariable logistic regression provided the adjusted odds ratios and 95% CI for the associations between the diets and bowel DGBI, adjusting for age, sex, religion, geographical region, and living conditions. Details of age, sex, and religion distributions are presented in Table 1A. With respect to geography, sampling was performed in urban and rural areas and in larger countries in different geographic regions of the country (detailed in Sperber et al27). With regard to living conditions, the following parameters were included in the survey: whether living on a farm with animals, whether home had running water on tap or has electric power. Access to medical care was also a component of the questionnaire, ie, whether free, available for a small fee or at substantial cost. Here again and for the reasons stated above, all analyses were performed separately for results from the Internet and household surveys.

On all analyses differences which achieved a P-value ≤ 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons.

RESULTS

A total of 54 127 internet survey subjects and 20 973 household survey subjects were included.

Details of the distribution of sex, age and religion in the internet and household surveys are provided in Table 1A. While the 2 survey populations were well matched for sex and age, significant differences were noted, as expected, for religion with Christianity being the most common affiliation in the Internet survey population and Islam and Eastern/Asian religions in the household survey.

Results for dietary preferences are presented in Table 1B. For internet survey subjects, the mean age was 44 years and 49.1% were female. The majority (93.3%) did not meet criteria for either a vegan or vegetarian diet. A milk-free diet was consumed by 5.1%. The most common starch-predominant diet was bread-predominant (ie, high in bread and low in rice and pasta, consumed by 38.2%) (Table 1B).

For household survey subjects, the mean age was 44 years and 50.7% were female. Although, again, most (88.6%) subjects did not meet criteria for a vegan or vegetarian diet, more household survey respondents reported consuming vegetarian (9.8% vs 2.2%) or lactose-free (22.9% vs 5.1%) diets than those in the internet survey. In terms of starch source, consumption of a rice-predominant diet was more common (39.8% vs 11.8%) than in the Internet survey subject. In both surveys pasta-predominant diets were more common at 1.6% and 0.5%, respectively, in internet and household surveys (Table 1B).

Bivariate analyses (see Table 2)

In Internet survey countries, a vegetarian diet was associated with a higher prevalence of any bowel DGBI diagnosis (odds ratio [OR] = 1.14; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02-1.28) and functional bloating (OR = 1.66; 95% CI, 1.27-2.18), in particular, and a lactose-free diet with IBS (OR = 1.26; 95% CI, 1.06-1.51) and diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D), in particular (OR = 1.48; 95% CI, 1.09-2.00). Bread and rice predominant diets were associated with lower likelihood of any bowel DGBI (OR = 0.95; 95% CI, 0.92-0.99 and OR = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.86-0.96, respectively) and IBS (OR = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.83-0.93 and OR = 0.67; 95% CI, 0.57-0.78, respectively). A vegan diet was associated with a lower prevalence of functional constipation (OR = 0.56; 95% CI, 0.32-0.96) and a rice-predominant diet with lower odds of functional bloating (OR = 0.64; 95% CI, 0.54-0.77), constipation-predominant IBS (IBS-C) (OR = 0.55; 95% CI, 0.41-0.74) and mixed IBS (IBS-M) (OR = 0.68; 95% CI, 0.52-0.89).

In household survey countries, no associations were evident for a vegan diet while, with the exception of IBS-C and IBS-M, a vegetarian diet was associated with a lower prevalence of all bowel DGBIs, with ORs ranging from 0.2 (0.05-0.81) for IBS-D to 0.5 (0.38–0.67) for functional constipation. As in internet surveys, a lactose-free diet was associated with a higher prevalence of any bowel DGBI (OR = 1.7; 95% CI, 1.57-1.83), IBS (OR = 2.54; 95% CI, 2.03-3.17), functional constipation (OR = 1.44; 95% CI, 1.25-1.66), functional diarrhea (OR = 1.66; 95% CI, 1.27-2.17), IBS-C (OR = 2.67; 95% CI, 1.85-3.84) and IBS-M (OR = 2.42; 95% CI, 1.42-4.15), and a rice-predominant diet with IBS (OR = 1.31; 95% CI, 1.05-1.63) and IBS-D (OR = 2.05; 95% CI, 1.56-3.09). This same diet was associated with a lower prevalence of functional bloating (OR = 0.73; 95% CI, 0.56-0.95).

Multivariate analyses (see Table 3)

These analyses corrected for several factors that could influence dietary preferences: age, sex, religion, geographical region and living conditions.

In Internet survey countries, a vegan diet was associated with a lower prevalence of functional constipation (OR = 0.54; 95% CI, 0.31-0.94). In contrast, vegetarian diet was associated with a higher prevalence of functional bloating (OR = 1.39; 95% CI, 1.06-1.83), and a lactose-free diet with IBS-D (OR = 1.57; 95% CI, 1.15-2.13). Bread and rice predominant diets were associated with a lower prevalence of IBS (OR = 0.86; 95% CI, 0.78-0.95 and OR = 0.80; 95% CI, 0.67-0.95, respectively) and IBS-C (OR = 0.78; 95% CI, 0.66-0.92 and OR = 0.67; 95% CI, 0.49-0.93, respectively), in particular.

In household survey countries, in contrast to the internet survey respondents, a vegan diet was linked to a higher prevalence of any bowel DGBI (OR = 1.63; 95% CI, 1.19-2.23). Here, a vegetarian diet was linked to a lower prevalence of any bowel DGBI (OR = 0.66; 95% CI, 0.55-0.79) and functional constipation (OR = 0.62; 95% CI, 0.46-0.85) but a higher prevalence of IBS (OR = 2.44) while a lactose-free diet was associated with functional constipation (OR = 1.26; 95% CI, 1.08-1.46), IBS-C (OR = 2.43; 95% CI, 1.64-3.60) and IBS-M (OR = 2.61; 95% CI, 1.44-4.73) and a rice-predominant diet with IBS-D (OR = 1.99; 95% CI, 1.28-3.09).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found, first, that only a small minority in both surveys selected a vegan or vegetarian diet and that the predominant carbohydrate in the diet differed substantially between internet and household respondents. The latter was not surprising given geographic and demographic differences between the 2 survey populations4 with internet surveys performed in North and South America and Western Europe and household surveys in more developing countries, including those in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and India.4 Both vegetarian and lactose-free diets were more prevalent in household survey countries. This may reflect the influence of diets dictated by religious practices: whereas 40% of internet survey countries reported no religion, Islam and Eastern/Asian religions were common in household survey countries (37.0% and 36.3%, respectively). Second, associations between a diet and DGBI were variable, influenced by both methods of analysis and survey methodology. The differences in results between internet and household surveys are a concern. In the main survey of DGBI prevalence,4 significant differences in DGBI prevalence were found depending on survey methodology with the overall prevalence of at least one DGBI being 20.7% in household and 40.3% in internet surveys. Furthermore, variation in country prevalence for any DGBI was much higher between household than internet survey countries.4,27 Factors that might contribute to such a discrepancy have been explored in a prior publication.27 While issues with the quality of data entry were more frequent in household surveys, their frequency was still too low to account for the significant differences in survey results.27 Similarly, while socioeconomic and cultural factors may have differed between survey countries, these were accounted for in our analysis and did not impact on outcomes. Where both survey methods were employed, the results have been compared but must be interpreted with caution, as in China the nature of the populations surveyed by the 2 methods differed substantially, and in Turkey, where efforts were made to match the populations surveyed by both methods, the level of education achieved by internet respondents was higher than household respondents. It was concluded that survey methodology per se was the primary factor influencing the prevalence of DGBI27 and while the reasons for these discrepancies cannot be fully explained, they may include a difference in response to a face-to-face, in person interview in contrast to the relative anonymity of an internet survey. In terms of the results of the dietary survey, caution must be exerted in interpreting findings such as the contrasting impacts of a vegan or vegetarian diet on DGBI prevalence between surveys as rates for vegan diets were very low in both (0.4% and 1.6%) resulting in very low numbers of responses and exposing analyses to error. Which survey method was more accurate cannot be determined with confidence. We surmise that because of a reluctance to discuss quite intimate symptoms in face-to-face interviews, the internet survey method may have provided more accurate data on symptom prevalence; while we could speculate that diet, a more “neutral” subject, might have been less affected, we have no data to support this.27

By correcting for several potential confounding factors, the multivariate analysis provided the most accurate assessment of diet-DGBI associations and, in general, confirmed but refined the findings from the bivariate analyses. It must be stressed that relationships between vegan and vegetarian diets and DGBI have been inconsistent in other surveys with some reporting lower rates for DGBI for those on these diets15,23 and others not.25,26,29-32 There was a more consistent association between a lactose-free diet and various bowel DGBI: IBS-D in the internet survey and functional constipation, IBS-C and IBS-M in the household survey. Indeed, lactose is a common trigger for gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea and abdominal bloating in IBS.33-35 Consequently, those with DGBI may have either consciously or unconsciously avoided dairy products even though the survey results suggest that this strategy may not have proven wholly successful. Individuals with IBS are known to modify their diets to reduce symptoms of their own accord without input from health care professionals.36

In terms of the influence of the predominant carbohydrates in the diet, no significant associations were noted for pasta, which is likely attributable to the low numbers who reported this dietary pattern in both surveys (1.6% and 0.5% for internet and household surveys, respectively). Among internet survey respondents where bread was the predominant source of carbohydrate, a bread-predominant diet was associated with a lower prevalence for all IBS and IBS-C; no associations were noted in household survey respondents who had a much lower rate of a bread predominant diet (7.7% vs 38.2% for internet respondents). Regarding a rice-predominant diet, it too was associated with a lower prevalence for all IBS and IBS-C among internet respondents but not among household respondents (who were 4-fold more likely to consume this diet at 39.8% vs 11.3% for internet respondents), where this diet was associated with a higher prevalence of IBS-D. The reasons for such discrepant results are not apparent and may be related to the factors that may have influenced responses to household surveys. The credibility of the internet survey responses is supported by other data suggesting that a rice-based diet is associated with fewer symptoms of IBS37 related, perhaps, to the lower impact of rice, than other carbohydrate sources, on intestinal gas.38 A switch from a bread-predominant to a rice-predominant diet would reduce the intake of fructans which are present in a high concentration in wheat and related grains and linked with IBS symptoms.20,39-42 It was surprising to note, therefore, that a bread-predominant diet (the main source of starch among these respondents) was associated with a lower prevalence of IBS and IBS-C in the internet survey. This observation seems credible and speaks against a major role for fructans, as well as gluten (also been implicated in the pathogenesis of IBS symptoms),43,44 assuming that gluten-containing grains were the sources of flour for most breads. A report of lower FODMAP intake among IBS sufferers might seem to question the role of these compounds in IBS45 but could also represent another example of dietary adjustment.

The link between diet and DGBI may be mediated by several factors, including the impact of dietary patterns on gut microbiota46-50 and/or its immune response.51

The range of dietary patterns evaluated in this study was limited to those included in the survey questionnaire and judged feasible in the context of the study. Clearly other factors, not assessed, such as intakes of dietary fiber and prebiotics52-57 or consumption of artificial sweeteners, emulsifiers and other additives might also play a role in symptom development.58

Strengths of our study include the rigor of its methodology, which sampled countries across the world with varying ethnicities, cultures, and dietary patterns. On the other hand, survey data can only identify associations between variables and cannot assign causation or even temporal relationships. Small numbers in relation to some of our prespecified diets further limited our ability to define associations with any DGBI.

In this large, global survey study of DGBI, we failed (albeit within the limitations of small sample sizes) to identify a consistent association between vegan or vegetarian diets and bowel DGBI. Somewhat unexpected were the associations noted between lactose-free (linked to a higher prevalence) and bread- and rice-predominant diets (linked to lower prevalence) and some bowel DGBI. Longitudinal, prospective studies are needed to elucidate the directionality of these associations. Do dietary habits cause DGBI, are they adopted in response to DGBI, or is combination of both involved depending on demographic, socio-economic and cultural factors?
