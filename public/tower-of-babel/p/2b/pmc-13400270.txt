Peritoneal lymphomatosis mimicking complicated hydatid cyst on imaging: A rare presentation of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Masoud Faghieh Dinavari, Javad Ghadimi Haji Agha, Kasra Khodadadi, Sahand Jafarnejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatic hydatid cysts are often asymptomatic; however, uncommon complications such as intraperitoneal rupture may present with massive ascites and mimic other intra-abdominal conditions. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma rarely involves the peritoneum and omentum, a presentation referred to as peritoneal lymphomatosis, which can closely resemble complicated hydatid disease or peritoneal carcinomatosis on imaging. We describe a 73-year-old Iranian man who presented with progressive abdominal distension and lower extremity edema. Vital signs were stable, and no systemic features of infection were noted. Laboratory evaluation revealed mild anemia with preserved liver function tests. Ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced computed tomography identified a large multiloculated hepatic cyst in segments VI and VII, accompanied by extensive ascites and omental thickening. Analysis of the ascitic fluid demonstrated an exudative, protein-rich effusion with markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase, raising suspicion for a malignant process. Omental biopsy followed by immunohistochemical studies showed diffuse positivity for CD45, CD20, and PAX5, with negative staining for mesothelial, epithelial, and T-cell markers. These findings confirmed a high-grade B-cell lymphoma consistent with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of differentiating peritoneal lymphomatosis from complicated hydatid disease or peritoneal carcinomatosis, particularly in patients with a prior history of hepatic pathology. A multidisciplinary approach integrating clinical, radiologic, and histopathologic data is crucial for establishing an accurate diagnosis. Early recognition enables prompt initiation of appropriate immunochemotherapy and may significantly improve clinical outcomes in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.Keywords: Omentum, Immunohistochemistry, Hydatid cyst

Introduction

A rare but potentially life-threatening complication of hepatic hydatid disease is spontaneous rupture of the cyst into the peritoneal cavity [1]. In some instances this rupture manifests solely as ascites, without peritonitis or any systemic inflammatory response [2], and can therefore be mistaken for ascites of other etiology.

Conversely, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) occasionally spreads to the peritoneum and meninges—a pattern designated peritoneal lymphomatosis (PL). On computed tomography scan (CT scan), peritoneal lymphomatosis is characterized by peritoneal thickening or enhancement, omental caking, and ascites [3]. In radiological evaluations, the presence of omental caking on cross-sectional imaging creates substantial diagnostic ambiguity, as its CT features can be virtually indistinguishable among peritoneal carcinomatosis, tuberculous peritonitis, and peritoneal lymphomatosis.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old Iranian man with a remote history of hepatic hydatid cyst surgery 18 years earlier presented to Emam Reza Hospital in Tabriz with progressive abdominal distension over the preceding 2 weeks, accompanied by recent bilateral lower extremity edema. His vital signs were stable, and there were no clinical signs of infection. He was afebrile and denied night sweats or unintentional weight loss. Physical examination revealed marked abdominal distension without tenderness, rebound, or other signs of peritonitis; the abdomen was soft and nontender. No peripheral stigmata of chronic liver disease were noted. Initial laboratory investigations obtained in the emergency department prior to imaging revealed a white blood cell count of 7.5 × 103/µL, hemoglobin level of 11.7 g/dL, and platelet count of 313 × 103/µL. Liver function tests showed aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 32 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 18 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 134 U/L, total bilirubin 0.8 mg/dL, and direct bilirubin 0.4 mg/dL. Abdominopelvic ultrasonography demonstrated hepatomegaly (liver span 172 mm) with increased parenchymal echogenicity. A large heteroechoic cystic lesion measuring 162 mm was identified in segments VI and VII of the right hepatic lobes. The lesion contained multiple internal hyperechoic foci, small daughter cysts, and calcifications with posterior acoustic enhancement, findings suggestive of a hydatid cyst WHO Type CE3b. In addition, a considerable amount of free fluid was noted within the abdominal and pelvic cavities.

Ascitic fluid analysis revealed a low serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG), elevated total protein levels, and markedly increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (7332 U/L), findings that raised suspicion for an underlying malignant process.

Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis confirmed a 166-mm multiloculated, well-defined cystic lesion with peripheral calcifications in the right hepatic lobe. Notably, no significant retroperitoneal or mesenteric lymphadenopathy was observed, which further complicated the initial differentiation from peritoneal carcinomatosis or hydatid cyst rupture. (Fig. 1A). A large volume of ascites was present, along with areas of soft-tissue density within the omentum suggestive of omental caking (Fig. 1B and C). These findings were concerning for a hydatid cyst complicated by malignant ascites.

Cytologic examination of the ascitic fluid showed a highly cellular specimen composed predominantly of discohesive large atypical lymphoid cells. The cells exhibited a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, irregular nuclear contours, vesicular chromatin, and prominent nucleoli, set against a relatively clean background (Fig. 2).

Ascitic fluid cytology.

Based on the above findings, the patient underwent an omental biopsy. Histopathological examination revealed a highly cellular lesion characterized by diffuse infiltration of large atypical lymphoid cells arranged in uniform sheets, with complete effacement of the normal tissue architecture, including the omentum and adjacent adipose tissue.

The neoplastic cells exhibited a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, round to irregular nuclei with vesicular chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and a moderate amount of cytoplasm. A markedly elevated proliferative index (approximately 80%) was observed, supporting the diagnosis of a high-grade neoplasm (Fig. 3A and B).

The cells display round to irregular nuclei, vesicular chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed a hematolymphoid origin, demonstrated by strong CD45 positivity (Fig. 4A). Diffuse and strong expression of PAX5 (Fig. 4B), along with CD20 positivity in a subset of tumor cells (Fig. 4C), supported B-cell lineage differentiation.

(A) the tumor cells show diffuse strong membranous positivity for CD45, confirming hematolymphoid origin. (B) the tumor cells show diffuse strong nuclear positivity for Pax5, confirming B-cell lineage. (C) some tumor cells show strong membranous positivity for CD20.

The neoplastic cells were negative for T-cell markers (CD3) and other markers including CD30 and Cyclin D1. In addition, immunostaining for epithelial, mesothelial, and myogenic markers—WT1, calretinin, CEA, EMA, desmin, and TTF1—was negative. Taken together, these findings support the diagnosis of a high-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, most consistent with diffuse DLBCL.

Discussion and conclusions

Peritoneal involvement represents an uncommon presentation of DLBCL [4]. Given that the peritoneum and omentum normally lack native lymphoid tissue, primary or direct lymphomatous infiltration of these structures is rare. Consequently, establishing the diagnosis of peritoneal lymphoma can be challenging, particularly in cases where the clinical and radiologic features resemble those of more prevalent conditions [5].

Radiologic features of PL closely resemble those seen in peritoneal carcinomatosis, metastatic peritoneal involvement, and tuberculous peritonitis, among other conditions. This significant overlap in imaging characteristics often complicates the diagnostic process. Unlike peritoneal carcinomatosis, PL characteristically does not cause bowel obstruction. Furthermore, despite their large volume, these lymphomatous masses typically do not invade or occlude the major abdominal vessels; instead, they tend to surround them without significant luminal narrowing, producing the classic “sandwich sign” on cross-sectional imaging [6].

Omental caking accompanied by bulky, relatively homogeneous soft-tissue masses is considered the most common radiologic manifestation of PL [5].

In our patient, the presence of a large hepatic cyst initially raised concern for cyst rupture. However, analysis of the ascitic fluid—demonstrating a low SAAG, elevated protein content, and markedly increased LDH—was more suggestive of a malignant etiology. Furthermore, the identification of omental caking on CT imaging strengthened the suspicion of an underlying malignancy.

However, definitive diagnosis was established by histopathologic examination, which remains the gold standard. Immunohistochemical findings, including positivity for CD20, CD45, and PAX5, supported the diagnosis of DLBCL.

Early recognition of peritoneal lymphoma is clinically crucial because, despite its often-aggressive presentation, DLBCL is generally considered a potentially curable malignancy with appropriate systemic therapy. Numerous studies have demonstrated that outcomes significantly improved following the introduction of rituximab in combination with standard anthracycline-based chemotherapy (eg, R-CHOP), making rituximab-containing regimens the current standard of care for most patients with DLBCL [7]. Delayed diagnosis is associated with poorer outcomes and may adversely affect overall prognosis.

In conclusion, this case underscores the importance of considering peritoneal lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of patients with a history of hydatid disease who present with ascites and inconclusive or atypical imaging findings.

PL can mimic complicated hydatid disease or peritoneal carcinomatosis, especially in patients with prior hepatic pathology. Accurate diagnosis relies on integration of clinical, radiologic, and histopathologic findings, including immunohistochemistry. Early recognition allows timely chemotherapy and improves outcomes in DLBCL.
