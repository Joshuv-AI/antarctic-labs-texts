Ethical challenges in end-of-life cancer care: a qualitative study among health-care providers in Iran
Authors: Razieh Talebi, Elahe Ramezanzade Tabriz, Gholamreza Roshandel, Reza Mohammadi, Seyed Amir Aledavood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is one of the most critical and ethically challenging health issues worldwide. Providing end-of-life care to cancer patients often places health-care providers in morally complex situations, where professional duties, cultural expectations, and legal–religious mandates intersect. Despite growing global interest in end-of-life ethics, there is limited understanding of how these challenges are experienced in culturally specific contexts such as Iran. This study aimed to explore the ethical challenges faced by health-care providers caring for cancer patients during the end-of-life stage.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative conventional content analysis approach. Data were collected between July 2024 and June 2025 in oncology and palliative care centers affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. Fourteen participants—including physicians, nurses, psychologists, and psychiatrists—were selected through purposeful sampling with maximum variation. Data were gathered via semi-structured, in-depth interviews, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed inductively following the framework of Elo and Kyngäs (2008). Rigor and trustworthiness were ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability.ResultsAnalysis yielded 237 initial codes, grouped into 14 categories, 6 sub-themes, and 3 themes: Deliberately Avoiding Telling the Truth, Decision-Making Immersed in Distress and Doubt, and Absence of Purposeful Coherence in the End-of-Life Care Trajectory. These findings illustrate how ethical practice in end-of-life care is shaped by the interplay of individual emotions, institutional limitations, and sociocultural norms.ConclusionEnd-of-life care for cancer patients in Iran is morally demanding and requires ethical, emotional, and institutional support for health-care providers. Reform efforts should include developing national ethical guidelines, establishing hospital ethics committees, enhancing communication and ethics training, and promoting cultural openness toward death and dying. These measures can foster compassionate, patient-centered, and ethically coherent care at the end of life.Keywords: End-of-life care, Cancer, Ethical challenges, Palliative care, Qualitative research

Introduction

Cancer is recognized as one of the most significant challenges for health-care systems worldwide [1]. Evidence indicates that the incidence of cancer and its associated burden are rising both globally and in Iran [2, 3], with more than 60% of deaths in developing countries attributed to cancer [1]. In Iran, cancer is the third leading cause of death [4], with over 100,000 annual fatalities according to World Health Organization statistics [4, 5]. Consequently, cancer represents a major public health concern. Advances in early diagnosis and modern therapeutic interventions have improved treatability, yet they have also increased the complexity of cancer care, making ethically sound practice more challenging [6].

Cancer differs from other chronic non-communicable diseases due to its unique nature and course [7]. Each stage may present specific ethical challenges for patients and care providers, such as decisions related to end-of-life care or selecting the most appropriate treatment options [8]. More than 52% of cancer patients die in hospital settings and require end-of-life care [9].

Caring for patients in the terminal phase is among the most complex areas of medical ethics. Decision-making extends beyond clinical considerations and is closely intertwined with individual, family, and societal values. Physicians and nurses, as primary care providers, face critical ethical questions daily: Is it ethical to continue aggressive treatment when there is no clear evidence of benefit? How can the family’s wish to preserve hope be balanced with the patient’s right to truthful information? When palliative sedation is used for refractory symptoms, is it an act of mercy or a practice that may shorten life? These dilemmas highlight the constant interplay and occasional conflict among autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice [10–14].

Over the past two decades, numerous studies have examined ethical challenges in end-of-life care. However, much of this literature is quantitative or policy-oriented, offering limited insight into how these challenges are experienced in everyday clinical practice [15, 16]. Evidence suggests that health-care professionals often face conflicts that differ from those described in formal guidelines, as clinical decision-making is shaped by cultural norms, family expectations, organizational pressures, and prognostic uncertainty [17, 18]. These contextual factors contribute to moral distress and may adversely affect the quality of end-of-life care [19–21].

Given the complexity and contextual nature of ethical challenges in end-of-life cancer care, qualitative approaches are particularly suitable for exploring health-care professionals’ lived experiences and ethical reasoning in real-world clinical settings. Recent studies increasingly examine ethical challenges in end-of-life and palliative care, especially in contexts where cultural, religious, and legal factors strongly shape practice. Such studies have shown that ethical decision-making at the end of life is embedded in social relationships, institutional norms, and broader cultural frameworks, and cannot be fully understood through formal guidelines alone [7, 8, 15].

In Iran, existing qualitative research has primarily focused on isolated ethical issues, such as truth-telling, communication dilemmas, or do-not-resuscitate decisions, or on single professional groups, most commonly nurses or, in some cases, pediatricians [9, 22, 23]. Some studies have examined nurses’ motivations for providing end-of-life care, highlighting professional, spiritual, and family-related influences, but without addressing broader ethical challenges faced across multiprofessional teams [24]. Similarly, research on family caregivers has revealed complex negotiations of hope, uncertainty, and ethical dilemmas within familial decision-making processes [25]. While informative, these studies provide limited understanding of how ethical challenges are collectively experienced, interpreted, and managed by multiprofessional health-care providers in real-world settings where ethical principles intersect with religious, legal, and institutional constraints [7, 17].

Moreover, previous qualitative studies in Iran and comparable cultural contexts often conceptualize ethical dilemmas as discrete events or individual moral conflicts, implicitly drawing on principlist or issue-based ethical frameworks [26, 27]. Such approaches do not fully capture the dynamic, longitudinal, and process-oriented nature of ethical decision-making across the end-of-life trajectory. Consequently, they provide limited insight into how emotional burden, fear of legal consequences, cultural expectations, and organizational pressures interact over time to shape routine ethical practices in end-of-life cancer care [15, 21].

To address these gaps, this study adopts a multiprofessional, inductive qualitative approach to explore ethical challenges in end-of-life cancer care in Iran. This approach conceptualizes ethical action as an evolving process rather than discrete dilemmas, advancing qualitative scholarship by integrating relational ethics, moral distress, and structural constraints within a culturally specific context, thereby extending understanding of ethical practice beyond individual-level reasoning [28, 29].

Therefore, the present study aimed to explore and interpret the ethical challenges of end-of-life cancer care from the perspectives of multiple health-care professionals in Iran. Using an inductive qualitative approach, this study examined how ethical challenges are experienced, negotiated, and managed in everyday practice, illuminating the contextual, relational, and organizational factors shaping ethical decision-making at the end of life.

Method

Study design

This study employed an interpretivist qualitative content analysis approach using a conventional (inductive) method. The participants included health-care providers working in oncology wards of hospitals affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, as well as palliative and hospice care centers in Mashhad, during 2024–2025.

A qualitative design was selected to achieve a deeper understanding of a poorly explored phenomenon, emphasizing participants’ experiences, beliefs, and perspectives [30, 31]. The inductive approach enables researchers to develop conceptual understanding directly from the data and to generate contextually grounded knowledge about the phenomenon under study. The COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist was used to guide the design and reporting of the methods and findings.

Research team

The research team consisted of five experts with academic and clinical backgrounds in nursing, bioethics, clinical psychology, and oncology:

First Author: Female faculty member, PhD in Nursing, instructor and researcher in nursing management and education, with several years of experience in adult and pediatric hospital care.

Second Author (Corresponding Author): Female faculty member, PhD in Nursing, with extensive teaching and clinical experience in adult oncology wards, and multiple studies in cancer care ethics.

Third Author: Male faculty member, PhD in Epidemiology and MPH in Bioethics, lecturer and researcher in medicine and ethics.

Fourth Author: Male researcher, MSc in Clinical Psychology, affiliated with Sayyad Shirazi Educational and Medical Center, working on psychological aspects of palliative and end-of-life care.

Fifth Author: Male faculty member, MD, Clinical Radiation Oncologist, researcher and lecturer in oncology, with several years of administrative and academic experience as head of specialized cancer hospitals.

This multidisciplinary composition allowed the study to examine the ethical challenges of end-of-life care from medical, ethical, psychological, and organizational perspectives.

The overall aim of the team was to provide a foundation for educational and supportive interventions that enhance the psychological well-being of care providers and the quality of end-of-life care for cancer patients and their families.

Setting

The study was conducted in the oncology wards of educational and medical centers affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, including Omid Hospital, Imam Reza (AS) Hospital, Shahid Hasheminejad Hospital, Ghaem (AJ) Hospital, and Nazaran Hospital. These hospitals are among the main centers for the diagnosis and treatment of cancer in northeastern Iran. In addition, data collection also took place in palliative and hospice centers in Mashhad, such as the Khatib Palliative Medicine Center, which specializes in end-of-life care services.

Participants

Participants were health-care providers (physicians, nurses, psychologists, and psychiatrists) working in oncology wards and palliative or end-of-life care settings within two tertiary referral hospitals and affiliated hospice services. These multidisciplinary environments included professionals with diverse clinical roles and responsibilities, enabling the study to capture a wide range of perspectives related to truth-telling in end-of-life care. Sampling was conducted between July 2024 and June 2025. After explaining the study objectives, methods, and data collection process, volunteers were recruited through in-person invitations at their workplace.

Participants were selected using purposive sampling with maximum variation to obtain diverse professional experiences related to truth-telling in end-of-life care. Inclusion criteria were: (1) health-care providers with at least five years of clinical experience (2), direct involvement in caring for patients at the end of life, and (3) willingness to share their experiences. Individuals without direct clinical contact with terminally ill patients or those unwilling to participate were excluded. To ensure maximum variation, participants were selected based on diversity in age, sex, educational level, years of professional experience, and professional role.

Fifteen individuals initially expressed willingness to participate and provided written informed consent. However, due to technical problems with the audio recording, one interview could not be retrieved and the participant could not be re-interviewed. Therefore, data from fourteen participants were included in the final analysis, resulting in a total of fourteen individual interviews. Sampling and data analysis were conducted concurrently until data saturation was reached. Saturation was defined as the point at which no new codes emerged from the data. This stage was reached during the final three interviews, when no additional codes were identified and the stability of the coding structure was confirmed through constant comparison and regular analytic discussions among the research team. Importantly, these final interviews involved participants from different professional backgrounds (a physician, a nurse, and a psychologist), ensuring that informational redundancy was confirmed across the multidisciplinary sample rather than within a single professional group.

Each participant was interviewed once using a semi-structured, in-depth, face-to-face interview format with open-ended questions. During the interviews, probing and clarification questions were used when necessary to obtain deeper explanations and clarify emerging concepts.

Data collection

Data were collected using semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted face-to-face. A number of general questions were developed as an interview guide requiring open and interpretive responses. Based on participants’ answers, additional questions were asked to explore emerging issues in greater depth.

All interviews were conducted by the second author (ERT), a faculty member with prior clinical experience in palliative nursing and academic training in qualitative interviewing techniques. Although participants were employed in the same affiliated clinical centers, the interviewer had no prior professional relationship or collaboration with them and held no supervisory, managerial, or evaluative role in relation to the participants. Before beginning each interview and recording the participant’s voice, the interviewer reintroduced herself, explained the objectives of the study, assured participants of confidentiality, and obtained permission to audio-record the interview. To minimize potential bias, reflexive notes were maintained throughout the interviews and discussed regularly with the research team.

In the present study, end-of-life patients were defined as those considered to be near death based on clinical symptoms, disease stage and prognosis, and the judgment of the specialist physician. This included patients with advanced, progressive cancer who were receiving palliative or hospice services and for whom curative treatment was no longer the primary goal [32]. Accordingly, before starting the interviews, the interviewer ensured that participants had direct experience in caring for or treating such patients. Participants were asked to reflect on their experiences caring for patients in this stage, reflecting a shared, practice-based understanding of end-of-life within their clinical settings. A brief set of demographic information, including name and surname, age, education level, years of professional experience, and professional role, was then collected.

Prior to the main study, the interview guide was piloted in one preliminary interview with a health-care professional who met the inclusion criteria. This pilot interview helped refine the wording and sequence of questions to ensure clarity and relevance. As no substantial modifications were required, the pilot interview was included in the final analysis.

Each interview began with a warm-up question to establish rapport and then proceeded with open-ended questions to explore participants’ experiences in depth. Main interview questions included: “Please describe your experience of caring for a cancer patient at the end of life,” “What ethical issues or challenges did you face while providing this care?”, “From your point of view, what were the causes of these challenges?”, and “How did you act or respond when faced with these challenges?” Where necessary, probing questions were used to encourage elaboration (e.g., “Could you explain more?”, “Do you mean that…?”, “Can you give an example?”) were used to encourage participants to elaborate on their experiences (Table 1).

The interviewer sought to obtain rich detailed accounts of participants’ experiences of caring for a cancer patient at the end of life and the ethical challenges associated with this care by establishing effective communication and guiding the discussion toward the study topic. Participants were encouraged to speak freely in a safe and comfortable atmosphere. At the end of each interview, participants were invited to share any additional experiences through open-ended questions such as, “Is there anything else that comes to mind?” or “Is there anything you would like to add that we have not discussed?” During and at the conclusion of each interview, attempts were made to clarify any ambiguities in participants’ responses by restating key points or summarizing their statements.

Interviews were conducted individually in a quiet environment without the presence of others. They were held at a time and location convenient for participants and lasted approximately 30 to 45 min on average. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent using a Sony ICD-PX470 digital voice recorder and were transferred immediately after completion to a password-protected computer to ensure secure data storage.

After listening to the recordings, the interviews were transcribed verbatim in Microsoft Word 2021. Each interview was assigned a code to ensure that participants’ identities remained confidential. Following transcription, the researcher listened to the audio recordings again and compared them with the written transcripts to eliminate any possible errors.

Data analysis

A conventional (inductive) content analysis approach was employed because limited prior qualitative research has explored truth-telling practices in end-of-life care within the Iranian cultural and health-care context. This approach allowed themes and categories to emerge directly from participants’ experiences without imposing predefined theoretical frameworks.

Data analysis was conducted inductively and concurrently with data collection, using the approach described by Elo and Kyngäs (2008), which consists of three main phases: preparation, organization, and reporting [33]. After each interview, the recordings were transcribed as soon as possible. The transcripts were read several times to achieve immersion in the data. Meaning units, consisting of sentences or paragraphs related to the phenomenon under study, were identified and assigned initial codes. Codes were then compared and grouped according to conceptual and semantic similarity. Through an iterative process of abstraction and data reduction, subcategories and main categories were developed. This analytic process was repeated with each additional interview, and categories were continuously refined as the analysis progressed. Statements that were not directly related to the research question were excluded from the analysis.

The first author (RT), who has prior clinical experience in palliative nursing, conducted the initial coding using MAXQDA version 20. Coding was performed by a primary analyst, and the resulting coding framework and category structure were subsequently reviewed and discussed with other members of the research team. Throughout the analytic process, reflexive and analytic memos were maintained to document coding decisions, emerging interpretations, and the development of categories, thereby creating an audit trail. To maintain reflexivity during data collection, analysis, and reporting, the research team held regular meetings to reflect on their own professional experiences and beliefs and to discuss how these perspectives might influence data interpretation. Disagreements regarding codes or category structures were discussed during team meetings, documented through analytic memos, and resolved through consensus. Data saturation was determined based on category redundancy, stability of the coding framework, and team consensus that no new codes were emerging from subsequent interviews [31].

Data trustworthiness

To ensure rigor and trustworthiness, the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985) were applied [30, 31]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement and peer review of selected codes. In addition, the findings, interview excerpts, analytical units, and initial codes were shared with two participants, and their feedback was obtained. Necessary corrections were made based on their comments. Dependability was strengthened through careful documentation of the research process, systematic data management, and continuous review of analytic procedures by the research team. Confirmability was ensured by maintaining an audit trail documenting all stages of data collection, analysis, and interpretation. Transferability was supported through purposive sampling with maximum variation and by providing detailed contextual descriptions of participants and study settings.

All interviews were conducted in Persian, the native language of the participants, to facilitate rich and authentic expression of experiences. The interviews were transcribed verbatim in Persian and used for the initial analysis. After the main themes were developed, the findings were reviewed and confirmed by two participants through member checking.

Following the preparation of the manuscript in Persian, the text was translated into English. The translator was instructed to translate participants’ quotations accurately and without interpretive modification. The translated manuscript was subsequently reviewed and approved by one participant. To ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence, bilingual members of the research team compared the English version with the original Persian transcripts, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus.

Theme 1: deliberately avoiding telling the truth

This theme consists of two categories and reflects participants’ experiences of deliberate and intentional avoidance of truth-telling or breaking bad news to patients or their families during end-of-life care. According to their accounts, “The complex and challenging nature of truth telling/breaking bad news” and “Feeling responsible for protecting the patient” were the two main factors contributing to this experience.

The complex and challenging nature of truth telling/breaking bad news

Participants stated that the complex and challenging nature of truth-telling and breaking bad news often makes them reluctant to engage in this process. They explained that implementing truth disclosure in a manner that minimizes psychological and emotional harm to patients and their families requires multiple specific competencies and conditions. Specialized knowledge, clinical experience, openness to the patient’s situation, understanding of the patient’s needs and preferences, and the availability of multidisciplinary teams were described as key individual and organizational requirements. The absence of these factors has contributed to health-care providers’ avoidance and reluctance to fulfill this professional responsibility.

“Anyway, breaking bad news to a patient is very difficult. First, breaking bad news to the patient and their companion involves several stages—when the diagnosis is made, during the course of treatment, when a relapse occurs, or when the patient enters the terminal phase. Second, we do not have a specific team for such a process (breaking bad news). Generally, the physician is the one who informs the patient and their family. However, many physicians choose not to do so.” (P14).

In addition, participants reported that a lack of personal skills and sufficient psychological capacity to face such situations was another contributing factor. They explained that compassion and empathy toward patients, chronic emotional fatigue, repeated exposure to patient deaths, and limited communication skills can generate severe emotional and psychological pressure, including anxiety, mental preoccupation, and helplessness, when confronting end-of-life cases. This pressure increases their tendency to avoid communication with patients and families in the short term and, over time, may lead to requests for leave, seeking work in other hospital departments outside oncology, or even leaving the profession altogether.

“I had a young patient whose doctor finally said we should tell him not to come for further treatment. How could I say that to this young man? He was still hopeful of recovery. For two or three months, I was in a very bad mental state. I just couldn’t bring myself to tell him that nothing more could be done. Eventually, his condition deteriorated, and he passed away. I was deeply emotionally affected.” (P8).

“In my opinion, even accepting this situation carries a huge emotional burden in itself. When you have to tell the patient’s family that we did everything possible and the rest is in God’s hands, it feels like pouring cold water over them—their entire world changes. Saying this alone is difficult […] Each of these stages is a challenge for the nurse […] When a nurse is personally struggling […], it becomes impossible to communicate properly with a dying patient or family.” (P1).

Participants’ statements also indicated that dominant cultural beliefs discourage informing patients directly about their illness. Families, who serve as the main caregivers and supporters of the patient and are often considered part of the treatment team, may insist on concealing the truth, believing that doing so protects the patient and reduces fear. Because families may view this as part of their moral and legal right, health-care providers who refuse to comply may face reactions such as formal complaints or legal action. This situation has contributed to reluctance among health-care providers, particularly physicians and nurses, to disclose the truth to patients about their condition.

“In our culture, being direct is not acceptable. The approach used in Western hospitals—where the truth is openly told to the patient—doesn’t exist here, or at least not commonly.” (P7).

“The challenge arises when physicians know that no treatment is possible but don’t know how to convey that to the patient and family. Although the literature emphasizes that patients should be informed about their condition, in our culture it is still not widely accepted to tell patients directly about their deteriorating status. Ultimately, only family members are informed, and they, in turn, withhold the information from the patient.” (P14).

Participants further stated that truth-telling or breaking bad news often carried negative consequences, which contributed to avoidance of disclosure in similar situations. Feelings of guilt and moral distress following truth disclosure were described as major challenges, imposing significant emotional burden and psychological exhaustion. Participants’ experiences suggested that witnessing the emotional suffering of patients and families after receiving bad news generated feelings of harmfulness and inadequacy among care providers, becoming a major source of guilt and remorse.

“One of the main challenges of truth-telling and breaking bad news to patients is the feeling of guilt it creates in us. Telling the prognosis to the family makes me feel guilty. I have to be the bearer of bad news that might worsen the mental state of the patient and family, and they may not be able to cope with it—then I feel responsible for that situation. I can’t bear to see the patient and family crying, and I blame myself for causing it.” (P10).

Another consequence of truth-telling mentioned by participants was facing violent or offensive behavior from family members after the disclosure. They believed that denial, anger, emotional attachment to the patient, and blaming physicians or nurses for the patient’s condition were among the main causes of such aggressive or disrespectful reactions.

“There was a case where the patient’s family attacked us—not because of the patient’s death, but simply because we told them there was no hope left for treatment. They physically assaulted the staff, and the case even led to a legal complaint.” (P2).

Feeling responsible for protecting the patient

Participants’ experiences showed that their sense of responsibility to protect the patient often led them to prioritize psychological and emotional support and the maintenance of physical strength rather than disclosing the truth. They stated that they had frequently observed that informing patients of the truth could weaken morale and instill a sense of hopelessness. In addition to provoking feelings of anger, fear, and anxiety, disclosure was perceived as contributing to a deterioration in the patient’s physical condition and reduced tolerance, making it more difficult for patients to navigate the stressful and painful stages of the end-of-life period. For this reason, health-care providers were generally reluctant to disclose the full truth and instead sought to preserve hope and provide supportive care until the final moments.

“Honestly, when we are caring for patients, we don’t think about them dying. We plan everything as if the patient will survive. Even when families ask, we usually say, ‘It’s not clear what will happen.’ If patients are told about their condition, they die sooner. When you tell them they are finished, they lose all hope. We must help them live whatever time they have left with hope.” (P11).

“These patients and their families are extremely emotionally vulnerable, and their biggest problem is the sense of hopelessness they experience. We have to keep them hopeful all the time. I never make them lose hope; I always tell them to trust in God.” (P9).

Theme 2: decision-making immersed in distress and doubt

This theme emerged from two main categories—“Inconsistency Between Ethical Principles and Religious–Legal Regulations” and “Facing erosion challenges.” It reflects participants’ experiences of being placed in a decision-making process characterized by moral distress and uncertainty while caring for patients at the end-of-life stage.

Inconsistency between ethical principles and religious–legal regulations

Participants stated that in many encounters with patients during the end-of-life stage, they experienced confusion and hesitation when deciding whether to continue or discontinue non-beneficial treatments. They explained that the primary source of this uncertainty was the perceived incongruity between religious–legal mandates and the four core principles of medical ethics, which guide health-care providers’ decision-making in such situations. Their experiences indicated that even when they were confident that interventions such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) or life-prolonging medications were futile, they felt legally obligated to perform them due to judicial and religious requirements and the need for accountability before legal authorities. This obligation was described as conflicting with the ethical principles of beneficence and non-maleficence, which emphasize avoiding actions that may cause additional harm or suffering. Participants also described their understanding of religious obligation as requiring continuation of care as long as signs of life were present, shaping their clinical decision-making in practice.

“This is exactly where the problem lies […] Islam dictates that as long as the patient is breathing, we are obliged to continue providing care. But […] we can clearly see that the patient will not survive more than a day or two. Yet, some physicians order additional procedures like an MRI for legal reasons […] It’s an unnecessary cost, not to mention the physical burden of transferring the patient.” (P13).

“Another challenge is that we are under legal and judicial pressure […] Many times, legal and ethical concerns overlap. Often, instead of thinking about our ethical duty, we think about our legal duty […] There are many situations where the law requires one thing, but ethics demands another—and law takes precedence over ethics.” (P10).

Participants’ experiences also revealed that inconsistency between ethical principles and religious–legal mandates often result in the violation of patient autonomy. They explained that, contrary to the ethical principle of respect for autonomy, these conflicts may create situations in which patients’ preferences and wishes are disregarded. According to participants, many patients—and even their families—during the end-of-life stage, due to fatigue and suffering from prolonged and exhausting treatments, prefer not to continue aggressive or life-prolonging interventions. In some cases, patients or families even expressed requests for early death (euthanasia) as a means of ending suffering. However, due to legal and religious constraints within society and the lack of legal protection for health-care providers in such decisions, physicians and nurses reported being compelled to disregard patients’ expressed preferences.

“There were cases where the patient’s family, claiming exhaustion from the situation, requested that no further interventions be performed. Most patients want it to end quickly, and families also wish for closure because they are tired of caregiving. Even if the patient has a ‘No CPR’ order, we still perform all the required interventions just like for any other patient.” (P3).

“We had a patient in the final stage who had been receiving high doses of norepinephrine and dopamine for about three days, but they were no longer effective. The family requested that no further action be taken and even signed a written consent form. However, legally this is not accepted, and we are required to continue treatment, regardless of its futility.” (P13).

Facing erosion challenges

Participants described prolonged and exhausting ethical challenges as another major factor contributing to moral distress and hesitation in the decision-making process. One of these challenges was the non-acceptance of medical and care-related decisions by patients, family members, and even other members of the treatment team. Such contradictions, occurring at both individual and group levels, were perceived as disrupting the fulfillment of patients’ physical and psychological needs. They also required physicians and nurses to expend considerable mental and emotional energy justifying their actions to those who disagreed. As a result, participants reported having less capacity to focus on treatment and care processes. Over time, these circumstances were described as leading to substantial fatigue and psychological stress among health-care providers, particularly physicians and nurses.

“Sometimes the family refuses to accept that the patient is in the terminal stage and insists on continuing treatment. No matter how much we explain, it doesn’t help. Even though the patient has been in this condition for a long time, the family doesn’t accept the physician’s or care team’s decision and still expects recovery. The family’s anxiety and struggles also bother the patient.” (P5).

“We had a patient in the final stage of life who was hospitalized for about a month. Every day, we told the family that there wasn’t much we could do and that it would be better to take the patient home for palliative care. But the family became agitated at even the smallest clinical change and demanded more interventions […] When the patient passed away, they insulted and threatened the nurse.” (P12).

Another challenge that emerged from participants’ experiences was the individualized nature of decision-making. They stated that during end-of-life care, there was often no interdisciplinary team or collective decision-making process. Responsibility for making and justifying critical decisions—especially those involving the continuation or discontinuation of life-sustaining treatment—was frequently left to a single individual. This lack of team-based ethical consultation placed an excessive moral and psychological burden on health-care providers, contributing to feelings of anxiety, guilt, and self-blame.

“Many times, nurses are forced to make decisions on their own. These decisions carry legal consequences, and we don’t know what the right action is. This makes me blame myself for continuing to work with these patients. I ask myself why I keep putting myself in a situation where I must take full responsibility alone.” (P13).

Theme 3: absence of purposeful coherence in the end-of-life care trajectory

This theme consisted of two main categories: “Lack of effective therapeutic communication with the patient” and “Failure to Meet the Patient’s Special Needs in the End-of-Life Stage.” It reflects a type of systemic neglect and discoordination that unintentionally occurs during end-of-life care processes, leading to patients’ feelings of isolation and confusion, and causing ethical and emotional distress among healthcare workers.

Lack of effective therapeutic communication with the patient

Participants explained that during the process of caring for patients at the end-of-life stage, effective communication is often disrupted, which may be related to professional and occupational constraints. They emphasized that both patients and their families at this stage are overwhelmed with numerous questions, fears, and concerns. At the same time, health-care providers—particularly physicians and nurses—due to limited time, heavy workloads, and the high level of care needs of such patients, are often unable to establish effective and responsive communication. This situation may contribute to patients and their family’s experiencing loneliness and confusion during the demanding end-of-life period.

“Patients place their greatest trust in their physicians. When there is good communication between them, the treatment process definitely becomes smoother and less stressful. But now, because of various problems such as the large number of patients, physician fatigue, and low salaries, this communication doesn’t happen.” (P14).

Moreover, participants’ statements revealed that deficiencies in essential personal competencies, such as self-awareness, professional experience, communication skills, emotional regulation, and understanding of patients’ beliefs and values, may hinder the development of effective therapeutic relationships. Patients in the terminal stages of life often experience varying degrees of depression, fear, aggressive behavior, and resistance to communication with others, particularly health-care providers. The patient’s emotional and psychological condition, combined with limited communication skills among providers, may prevent the formation of a supportive and therapeutic relationship between end-of-life patients and the care team.

When this emotional state is combined with a lack of interpersonal competence among health-care providers, the result may be the absence of a supportive and therapeutic relationship between the dying patient and the care team.

“Experience is a very important factor […] The next factor is expertise and skill—knowing how to break bad news, how to interact with a dying patient, and how to communicate with family members […] All of these are essential for communicating with and caring for patients and families. We truly need education and training to acquire these skills.” (P1).

Failure to meet the patient’s special end-of-life needs

Based on participants’ experiences, failure to meet patients’ special needs during the end-of-life stage was identified as another factor contributing to perceived abandonment during this period. Participants emphasized that shortages in human resources, particularly the absence of multidisciplinary and specialized teams composed of physicians, nurses, psychologists or psychiatrists, social workers, and spiritual counselors, were among the most important reasons for the inability to address patients’ multidimensional needs.

In addition, the limited availability of hospice and palliative care centers, combined with the lack of insurance coverage for services provided by these facilities, was described as a major contributor to unmet care needs. Participants stated that in many cases, hospitals and physicians may refuse to admit terminally ill patients, instead referring them to their homes for family-based care. However, many families are psychologically unprepared and may lack the resources or capacity to provide home care, leading them to resist assuming this responsibility. As a result, patients may endure a more difficult transition through the final stages of life, a situation that contradicts the ethical principles of professional care and the fundamental philosophy of palliative care.

“Sometimes we have patients who have been treated in multiple hospitals and clinics. When they reach the final stage—when they become end-stage—they are abandoned everywhere. The doctor says, ‘Don’t come to my office anymore; there’s nothing more I can do.’ The hospital also refers them elsewhere. The patient is left completely unattended in the terminal phase. Continuing care should be supportive, but that requires a specialized treatment team.” (P8).

“Just as birth is important, death is also important, and this stage should be handled properly. For every end-stage patient, there is this gap—at the very last phase, they are abandoned. There’s a need for the patient and family not to feel lost but to know that a specialized team exists to provide spiritual and emotional support.” (P7).

Participants also pointed to the lack of structured and targeted educational programs designed according to the needs and characteristics of patients and their families as another underlying factor contributing to unmet patient needs. They stated that throughout the course of the disease—from diagnosis to the end-of-life stage—patients and families often do not receive adequate or accurate information about the nature of the illness, its stages, available treatments, or palliative care options. Consequently, they may experience this process with uncertainty and unanswered questions, making it difficult to participate effectively in important treatment decisions. This lack of knowledge may generate confusion, loneliness, and anxiety, which can manifest as anger, denial of the incurable condition, or aggressive behavior toward health-care providers.

“At the end-of-life stage, we normally expect that the patient and family would be aware of the condition. But here, we often see patients […] close to death—and yet their family says things like, ‘If you give another infusion, won’t he get better?’ or ‘If we change the doctor, will he improve?’ These are all signs of the family’s or patient’s lack of understanding about the nature of the illness […] All of this stems from a lack of awareness. It is really necessary that when a patient is diagnosed with cancer, both the family—and to some extent the patient […]—receive training about what to expect and what lies ahead.” (P14).

Discussion

According to the findings of the present study, “Deliberately avoiding telling the truth,” “Decision-making immersed in distress and doubt,” and “Absence of purposeful coherence in the end-of-life care trajectory” were identified as the main ethical challenges experienced by health-care providers in caring for cancer patients during the end-of-life stage. Together, these findings highlight those ethical challenges in Iranian end-of-life cancer care are not limited to individual clinical dilemmas but are deeply embedded in cultural expectations, legal uncertainty, and systemic gaps in palliative infrastructure. Truth disclosure, decision-making, and continuity of care emerge as relational and structurally constrained practices rather than isolated ethical acts. This underscores the need for integrated ethical, communicative, and institutional support for health-care providers and families.

The findings of this study can be interpreted through the lens of principlism, as participants’ accounts reflected persistent tensions between core ethical principles in end-of-life cancer care, including respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and veracity. These principles provide an appropriate framework for understanding the ethical dilemmas surrounding truth disclosure, decision-making, and continuity of care in this clinical context.

Our findings suggest that truth-telling in Iranian end-of-life cancer care is not a straightforward clinical obligation but rather an ethically complex, mediated practice. This complexity highlights the need for advanced ethical judgment and communication competence, particularly in emotionally charged end-of-life situations. Disclosure decisions appear to be strongly shaped by cultural norms that prioritize family-centered protection over individual autonomy, a pattern also observed in other Asian and Middle Eastern contexts. In this respect, our findings extend existing qualitative evidence showing that clinicians often experience nondisclosure as a relational and morally burdensome strategy rather than a simple act of deception.

Nikbakht Nasrabadi et al. (2020) also found that breaking bad news is one of the most challenging situations nurses’ encounter. They reported that in most cases, nurses refrain from telling the truth due to a lack of knowledge about effective strategies for breaking bad news [22]. Ibrahim et al. (2024) reported that nurses in Egypt experience communication difficulties when facing complex ethical decisions and therefore require structured communication training [10]. Several other studies have confirmed these results, identifying limited communication skills as one of the most important barriers to breaking bad news by nurses [19, 34–36]. Similarly, Shih et al. (2025) found that in Chinese cultural contexts, families often conceal the truth and the severity of illness from patients, which forces health-care providers to remain silent [37]. Nikbakht Nasrabadi et al. (2020) also reported that nurses, due to patients’ emotional crises and loss of hope after learning the truth, are often compelled to tell white lies [22]. Wahyuni et al. (2023), in their review, found that concerns about patients’ emotional reactions and the ability to manage them were among the main barriers to breaking bad news among nurses [36]. In line with these results, Mansoursamaei et al. (2023) also reported that medical residents—particularly in internal medicine, surgery, and emergency departments—considered fear of emotional outbursts and aggression from patients or their families as the most common barriers to breaking bad news [38]. Additionally, Francis et al. (2023) demonstrated that breaking bad news causes considerable distress among health-care providers, especially when they have developed close emotional relationships with patients [39].

From an ethical perspective, the reluctance of health-care providers to engage in truth-telling reflects tensions between core ethical principles in clinical practice. Respect for autonomy requires that patients receive accurate and complete information to make informed decisions about their care. Failure to disclose the truth undermines informed consent and limits patients’ ability to exercise self-determination. In contrast, the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence are often invoked to justify partial disclosure or non-disclosure. Health-care providers may believe that withholding distressing information protects patients from psychological harm, preserves hope, and supports emotional and physical resilience. In this context, participants often framed nondisclosure as an act of care rather than deception. The principle of veracity further complicates this dilemma, as it emphasizes the moral obligation to be honest with patients. When truth-telling is compromised, trust in the health-care professional–patient relationship may be weakened, potentially affecting long-term care outcomes. Additionally, justice is implicated when disclosure practices vary based on cultural background, family influence, or clinician judgment, leading to unequal access to information. Ultimately, ethical truth-telling requires balancing respect for patient autonomy with professional duties of beneficence and non-maleficence, while maintaining honesty and fairness. This balance is context-dependent and demands ethical competence, cultural sensitivity, and emotional resilience from health-care providers.

Our findings suggest that end-of-life decision-making in Iranian cancer care is ethically fraught and shaped by pervasive uncertainty, placing clinicians in sustained states of moral distress. This tension appears to emerge from the intersection of structural constraints and conflicting moral frameworks governing end-of-life care. First, clinicians face a critical disjunction between professional ethical principles and the religious–legal regulations that shape permissible end-of-life practices. Although autonomy and beneficence call for respecting patients’ wishes and alleviating suffering, participants described how religious obligations and legal mandates often constrain clinicians’ ability to act on these principles. Such conflicts illustrate how decision-making becomes less a matter of clinical judgment alone and more a negotiation shaped by legal anxiety and culturally embedded moral expectations. Consistent with these results, Luna-Meza et al. (2021) found that fear of confrontation with family members and the possibility of legal prosecution compels health-care workers to perform interventions such as artificial feeding and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), even when no expected clinical benefit exists [40]. Alanazi et al. (2024) reported that nurses often experience ethical dilemmas in balancing patient autonomy and beneficence [17]. A systematic review also showed that, on average, 33–38% of end-of-life patients receive non-beneficial treatments, and the prevalence of CPR among patients with advanced illness is approximately 28% [41].

Other evidence confirms that even when physicians recognize life-prolonging treatments as futile, they often have limited capacity to resist “clinical momentum” if organizational factors (protocols, resources, hospital culture) and systemic factors (national health systems, laws, regulatory frameworks, cultural norms) fail to support them [15, 19, 29, 42]. Furthermore, religion and spirituality have been identified as key influences on medical decision-making regarding life-sustaining treatments [43] and continue to shape end-of-life care approaches in many societies [44].

In this regard, participants reported that, based on their religious commitments, they felt morally obliged to continue care as long as signs of life, such as breathing, were present. Participants’ accounts suggest that this perspective reflects a lived and experiential interpretation of religious duty, in which sustaining life is viewed as an absolute moral obligation. However, religious and cultural contexts rooted in Islamic values may further shape how these ethical dilemmas are perceived and managed in practice. In Islamic teachings, although the preservation of life is a central principle, it is not considered absolute and must be balanced against other ethical obligations, including avoidance of harm and prevention of futile interventions [45, 46]. Islamic jurisprudence recognizes that life-sustaining treatments may be ethically withheld or withdrawn when they merely prolong suffering or lack reasonable clinical benefit, provided that the intention is not to hasten death [47]. The divergence between participants lived religious experiences and formal Islamic ethical guidance highlights a critical gap in end-of-life care. This gap may contribute to moral distress and challenges in decision-making, underscoring the need for ethically informed communication that integrates clinical judgment with religious values in end-of-life contexts.

Second, the study showed that erosion challenges—such as non-acceptance of end-of-life decisions by patients, families, and even health-care team members, along with the individualized nature of decision-making—generate significant doubt and moral distress among health-care providers. Ethically, these challenges reflect unresolved tensions between autonomy, beneficence, and non-maleficence. Conflicting views regarding treatment goals may undermine respect for patient autonomy and place clinicians in situations where no option appears ethically sound.

The absence of shared, team-based decision-making concentrates ethical responsibility on individual caregivers, intensifying emotional burden and fear of causing harm. This erosion of collective ethical deliberation may compromise justice, as decisions become inconsistent and dependent on personal capacity rather than structured ethical reasoning. As a result, health-care providers experience moral distress, emotional exhaustion, and reduced moral confidence, which ultimately threaten the ethical integrity and sustainability of end-of-life care. In line with these findings, numerous studies have identified end-of-life decision-making as a key source of conflict in intensive care units and among health-care providers [48–50].

It has also been shown that these contradictions can undermine clinicians’ sense of competence and professional dignity, negatively affecting mental health, job satisfaction, and clinical performance, while potentially endangering patient safety and increasing medical errors [49–52]. Carter et al. (2019) further reported that conflicts within patients’ families and among health-care providers are strong predictors of the provision of non-beneficial treatments, with the likelihood increasing nearly tenfold in the presence of family conflict and 8.4 times when disagreement exists among providers [53].

Several studies have demonstrated that a weak ethical decision-making climate—in which team members are unable to participate in discussions or advocate for patients—results in adverse outcomes, including inappropriate patient decisions [54, 55], conflict [56], moral distress [57, 58], post-traumatic stress symptoms [59, 60], compassion fatigue [54], and burnout among health-care workers [55, 61]. When patients and families face life-threatening conditions, the ethical dimensions of care become more profound than in other clinical contexts [62]. From an ethical standpoint, effective end-of-life decision-making requires alignment between ethical principles, legal and religious contexts, and institutional support. Interdisciplinary, team-based decision-making grounded in ethical consultation can reduce moral distress, promote fairness, and ensure that autonomy, beneficence, and non-maleficence are addressed in a balanced and consistent manner.

Our findings suggest a significant absence of purposeful coherence in the end-of-life care trajectory, which raises serious ethical concerns. One major contributing factor is the failure to establish an effective therapeutic relationship between health-care providers and patients. This failure is largely attributed to systemic constraints such as time limitations and heavy workload, as well as individual limitations including insufficient communication skills, low self-awareness, and inadequate abilities in emotional regulation and stress management.

From an ethical standpoint, these deficiencies undermine the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence, as the inability to communicate empathetically and consistently may increase patients’ psychological suffering rather than alleviate it. In line with these findings, previous studies have reported incompetence and lack of knowledge [51, 63], time constraints [64], limited experience [65, 66], fragmented or discontinuous care and language barriers [64], lack of training, cultural differences, and perceived reluctance of patients or families [67] as key obstacles to establishing effective communication in end-of-life and palliative care settings.

These findings indicate that despite significant advances in cancer treatment and increased survival rates, the emotional, psychological, spiritual, and cultural dimensions of care—particularly those requiring open, supportive, and constructive communication—remain neglected. Such deficiencies may directly affect the ethical integrity and human dignity of end-of-life care. Open communication and shared decision-making among health-care providers, patients, and families can prevent many ethical conflicts in end-of-life care [68]. Therefore, strengthening communication skills that are sensitive to the values and cultural characteristics of patients and their families is essential to ensure the provision of ethical and compassionate care by health-care professionals. Another critical factor is the failure to adequately meet the specific needs of patients in the final stages of life. This is driven by structural shortcomings, including the lack of specialized palliative care professionals, insufficient hospice facilities, inadequate insurance coverage, and the absence of structured and goal-oriented educational programs. Such gaps violate the principle of justice, as patients at the end of life do not receive equitable access to appropriate care based on their needs. They also compromise respect for human dignity, a foundational ethical value in end-of-life care.

The cumulative effect of this lack of coherence is a reported sense of abandonment. Health-care providers experience moral distress, feeling that patients are left alone during their most vulnerable period, while families remain confused, unsupported, and emotionally isolated. Ethically, this situation reflects a breakdown not only in beneficence and justice but also in relational responsibility, which is central to palliative care ethics. End-of-life care, when fragmented and uncoordinated, fails to uphold the ethical obligation to accompany patients and families through dying with compassion, continuity, and moral accountability.

Consistent with these results, multiple studies have shown that when patients’ physical function declines or when they become fully dependent due to cancer progression, caregivers have a greater need for hospice or palliative care support [69, 70]. As a result, they are often unprepared for hospital discharge [71]. Moreover, when adequate post-discharge professional support for home care is lacking, caregivers are frequently unable to manage the stress of patients’ symptoms and emergency events at home, which increases their tendency for hospital readmission [72]. Therefore, several studies have recommended the establishment of team-based and supportive systems to meet patients’ and families’ complex needs [68, 71, 73].

Interdisciplinary care teams support the primary caregivers who provide most of the patient’s daily care [73], while palliative and hospice teams offer emotional and spiritual support, social services, nutritional counseling, and bereavement support for patients and families [68]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that palliative care significantly improves the quality of life and hope among terminally ill patients [74]. Thus, health-care providers caring for terminally ill patients should possess primary palliative care competencies. In addition, to ensure equity of access, palliative care services should be covered by national health insurance systems in all countries [68].

Designing support programs and interventions for family caregivers, as well as providing timely and accurate information to assist them in making informed decisions in advanced cancer, is also essential and should become a routine part of clinical practice [75, 76]. Such efforts reduce caregiver burden and improve their quality of life and psychological outcomes [77]. Moreover, it has been recommended that palliative care should begin as early as possible [78], as early integration has been shown to significantly reduce caregiver stress and depression [79].

This study contributes theoretically by showing how tensions between autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and veracity shape ethical decision-making in Iranian end-of-life cancer care. In particular, our findings highlight how religious–legal accountability may at times displace ethical reasoning in everyday clinical micro-decisions, and how nondisclosure can function as an emotionally protective strategy for health-care providers. Our findings suggest the need for an integrated, multidisciplinary decision-making model that supports clinicians through ethical consultation, team-based deliberation, and improved communication practices. Such an approach may reduce moral distress and promote more coherent and ethically consistent end-of-life care.

Ethical challenges in end - of - life care process

In summary, the participants’ experiences in this study indicated that they often intentionally avoid truth-telling during end-of-life care, both to protect the patient and to provide themselves with emotional and social protection. This practice can disrupt the creation of a shared, supportive environment among the care team, the patient, and the family when making critical end-of-life decisions. As a result, healthcare providers may experience ethical and emotional distress, hesitation, uncertainty in decision-making, rejection, lack of trust, and excessive pressure. Simultaneously, the absence of coherent, goal-oriented alignment at individual, team, and organizational levels regarding end-of-life care not only impedes the delivery of high-quality care to patients and families but also generates a sense of inefficacy and increases ethical and emotional stress among healthcare providers. These findings suggest that end-of-life care involves multiple, interacting ethical challenges that influence the entire care process. Therefore, end-of-life care requires a comprehensive and interactive approach that enables healthcare providers to perform their duties ethically and effectively. This includes truth-telling, participating in shared decision-making, maintaining therapeutic communication, and delivering care based on patient needs. Moreover, healthcare providers involved in end-of-life care require ongoing training as well as emotional and psychological support to navigate the complex ethical and emotional demands of this process.

Implications for practice, policy, and education

The findings of this study underscore the ethical challenges surrounding end-of-life care and highlight the need for careful, patient-centered clinical decision-making. Recognizing these challenges can support health-care professionals in balancing ethical principles, respecting patient autonomy, and improving the quality of end-of-life care in clinical practice.

Strengthening ethical competence and communication skills

Health-care providers may benefit from structured training in breaking bad news protocols (e.g., SPIKES), moral distress debriefing sessions, ethics consultation pathways, and advanced communication skills.

Promoting shared and team-based decision-making

End-of-life decisions should be made through interdisciplinary collaboration, including physicians, nurses, palliative care specialists, and when appropriate, ethicists and religious advisors. This approach distributes moral responsibility, reduces individual distress, and promotes justice and ethically consistent care.

Balancing autonomy with relational and cultural contexts

Respect for autonomy should be upheld through honest, compassionate, and gradual disclosure, while acknowledging cultural, familial, and religious values. Relational models of palliative care ethics can help reconcile autonomy with beneficence in culturally sensitive ways.

Improving structural and system-level support

Expanding access to hospice services, specialized palliative care teams, insurance coverage, and standardized end-of-life care pathways is essential to uphold justice and prevent inequities in care delivery. In addition, institutional mechanisms such as structured family meetings, documentation standards for goals-of-care discussions, clinical supervision, peer support, and access to psychological counselling may strengthen ethical care and support both families and healthcare workers.

Institutionalizing ethical and palliative care education

Healthcare systems should implement continuous, goal-oriented education programs integrating ethical principles with palliative care practice. Such programs can reduce moral distress, enhance ethical consistency, and ensure continuity of care for patients and families at the end of life.

Limitations and strengths

One of the main strengths of this study was its inclusion of diverse health-care professionals—including oncologists, physicians, nurses, psychiatrists, and psychologists—which provided a comprehensive understanding of ethical challenges in end-of-life cancer care. This diversity enabled a broader perspective on ethical dilemmas and helped identify strategies for improved management of such challenges. Another strength was the participation of professionals from both hospital settings and hospice or palliative care centers. Given the structural and operational differences between these environments, this study was able to capture a wider range of ethical experiences related to end-of-life care. However, due to the qualitative nature of the research, the findings are context-specific and may be transferable only to similar clinical and organizational settings, rather than generalizable across all contexts.

Recommendations for future research

Future studies are recommended to design and evaluate interventions that aim to enhance communication between health-care providers, patients, and families; facilitate patient and family participation in end-of-life decision-making; and improve the ethical climate within oncology wards and hospice settings. Such research may contribute to the development of evidence-based frameworks for ethical practice in palliative and end-of-life care.

The findings of this study indicate that nurses and other health-care providers delivering end-of-life care experience not only patients’ suffering but also significant moral, legal, and emotional burdens. Participants described being situated within ongoing tensions between truth disclosure and silence, ethical obligations and legal constraints, and professional responsibility and limited institutional support. These challenges reflect broader structural, cultural, and regulatory contexts shaping end-of-life practice. Addressing such gaps requires a multi-level approach, including communication and ethics training at the individual level; development of integrated ethical guidelines, clinical ethics consultation pathways, and psychological support mechanisms at the organizational level; greater alignment between legal frameworks and ethical principles at the policy level; and increased societal openness toward end-of-life communication. Strengthening these dimensions may enhance ethical coherence and support more compassionate and dignified palliative care.

Conclusion

This study reveals profound ethical challenges in end-of-life care, manifested through avoidance of truth-telling, distress-laden and uncertain decision-making, and a lack of purposeful coherence in the end-of-life care trajectory. These challenges reflect persistent tensions among core ethical principles—autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice—within complex clinical, cultural, legal, and religious contexts. Healthcare providers often withhold the truth to protect patients and themselves emotionally. They also struggle with end-of-life decisions and frequently prioritize judicial and religious requirements to maintain accountability. However, fragmented decision-making, insufficient communication, and inadequate structural support undermine patient dignity, informed participation, and equitable access to appropriate care. The cumulative effect is moral distress among providers and a sense of abandonment experienced by patients and families. From an ethical and palliative care perspective, these findings emphasize the need for integrated, ethically grounded, team-based approaches to end-of-life care.
