Nickel/Photoredox-catalyzed diastereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes using a carboxylic acid as a cooperative ligand and hydrogen surrogate
Authors: Jiaqian Zhang, Na Zhang, Wenjin Sun, Mi Jie, Zhiqiang Pan, Chengfeng Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Z-selective semihydrogenation of alkynes plays a key role in the industrial manufacture of fine chemicals. Considerable research attention has focused on the development of reagents, catalysts, and protocols for stereoselective reduction of alkynes to afford pure cis-alkenes. Here, we report the efficient and diastereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes to Z-alkenes through synergistic nickel/photoredox catalysis with pivalic acid as the cooperative ligand and proton and electron sources. The mild catalytic approach enables highly efficient, Z-selective semihydrogenation of diverse substrates, including dialkyl alkynes, aryl-alkyl alkynes, and diaryl alkynes, with excellent functional group tolerance. A series of mechanistic experiments and DFT calculations suggest that the stereospecific construction of cis-alkenes was enabled by both the in situ generation of nickel hydrogen and the cooperative ligand effects between the nickel catalyst and carboxylic acid.

Introduction

Since different physical, chemical, and biological properties are exhibited in the geometric isomerism of alkenes, the diastereoselective synthesis of olefins is of tremendous importance. The alkyne semihydrogenation ranks as one of the most straightforward approaches among tremendous methods1–3. The efficient control of diastereoselective reduction to Z-alkenes, the prevention of over-reduction to alkanes, and the avoidance of migration of the C − C double bond are pivotal factors and main targets for alkyne semihydrogenation4–6. Palladium-based heterogeneous catalysts, such as the Lindlar catalyst, represent the earliest and remain the most widely adopted strategies for Z-stereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes with molecular hydrogen (Fig. 1a)7,8. Despite its broad applicability, products are sometimes contaminated with 5 − 10% of E-isomers due to competitive alkyne−palladium surface interactions9. To enhance the diastereoselectivity while suppressing over-reduction and double bond migration, substantial progress has been made in heterogeneous catalysis based on various noble metals (Pd, Ru, Pt, Au, Ir) and earth-abundant metals (Co, Mo, Ni, Fe, Cu, Mn)10,11. Meantime, impressive advances for Z-stereoselective alkyne semihydrogenation were achieved using homogeneous metal catalysis12–15. Meanwhile, most heterogeneous and homogenous alkyne semihydrogenation employ gaseous hydrogen as a hydrogen source. However, these processes often require elevated temperatures and high hydrogen pressure, which should be conducted with additional safety precautions and the use of specialized apparatus. Transfer hydrogenation provides a safe and convenient alternative for stereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes at ambient pressure22. A broad range of hydrogen surrogates, including silanes23–25, alcohols26,27, formic acid28,29, hypophosphorous acid30, amines31, and amine–boranes32,33, were evaluated for the in situ generation of metal-hydrogen34.

With the development of photochemical transformations, Weiss reported a cobalt/photoredox-catalyzed conversion of acetylene to ethylene using sodium ascorbate as a sacrificial reductant and water as a proton source35. Currently, the photochemical semihydrogenation of substituted alkynes has only been achieved36,37, by the synergistic nickel/photoredox dual catalysis (Fig. 1b)38–41. A Ni−H species, which was in situ generated by β − H elimination of trialkylamine or methanol, was responsible for the syn-insertion into the alkyne. However, these methods typically exhibited poor to moderate Z-selectivities due to the competitive alkenylnickel isomerization40. Furthermore, the substrate scope has been largely confined to aryl alkynes with significant limitations. A general nickel/photoredox catalyzed high diastereoselective semihydrogenation of a wide scope of alkynes to Z-alkenes is, so far, absent from the literature.

To access the Z-stereoselective alkyne semihydrogenation, a ligand-cooperative nickel catalytic protocol would be an efficient tool42–46. Carboxylic acids were shown to serve as effective coordinating groups for nickel catalysis47,48. The introduction of carboxylic acid as a cooperative ligand might efficiently inhibit the alkenylnickel isomerization (Fig. 1c). Moreover, the coordination of carboxylic acid would also enable a sufficient kinetics of the protonolysis of alkenylnickel complex before its isomerization40,49. Meanwhile, the reaction of carboxylic acid with Ni0 could in situ generate Ni−H species50, providing a new hydrogen surrogate for the semihydrogenation of alkynes. However, a major challenge in carboxylic acid-mediated metal-hydrogen formation is the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)18,21, which might diminish the addition of Ni−H to alkyne. Furthermore, the carboxylic anion can also serve as an efficient electron donor, recycling the photocatalyst via photochemical decarboxylation51–55. Here, we show that the carboxylic acid serves as both a cooperative ligand and a hydrogen surrogate to enable the nickel/photoredox catalyzed diastereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes with a wide substrate scope (Fig. 1d).

Results and Discussion

Optimization of the reaction conditions

The unactivated dialkyl alkyne 1 was chosen as the model substrate for the nickel/photoredox catalyzed Z-selective semihydrogenation. Carboxylic acids played vital roles in both diastereoselectivity and yield. The pivalic acid was identified as the optimal cooperative ligand, as well as for the proton and electron donors, for the diastereoselective semihydrogenation of alkyne 1. After screening substantial reaction parameters, the cis-alkene 2 was afforded in high diastereoselectivity (99:1 Z/E) in 98% yield (Table 1, entry 1). Under standard conditions, Ir(dtbppy)2(dtbbpy)PF6 was used as a photocatalyst, and NiBr2.diglyme with 2,2’-bipyridine (bpy) was used as a nickel catalyst. No over-reductive alkane, trans-alkene, or migrative alkene was detected in this synergetic catalytic system. Other carboxylic acids, including AcOH, EtCO2H, iPrCO2H, CyCO2H, BnCO2H, and nBuCO2H, showed low yield and/or poor Z-stereoselectivity (entries 2–7). We found that more acidic carboxylic acids resulted in lower yields. These might be due to the competitive HER, where the Ni−H species was quenched by a proton before its addition to the alkyne. In addition, secondary and tertiary carboxylic acids with larger steric hindrance exhibited higher diastereoselectivity, indicating that the cooperative ligation of carboxylic acids effectively inhibited the alkenylnickel isomerization. Since benzoic acid could not effectively recycle the photocatalyst, no semihydrogenation was observed (entry 8). Several nickel catalysts were also evaluated, furnishing the semihydrogenation product in high diastereoselectivity, albeit with slightly reduced yields (entries 9 and 10). We found that 50% mol Cs2CO3 could provide alkene 2 in 81% yield and 99:1 Z/E ratio (entry 11), however, 10% mol Cs2CO3 makes the lower efficiency (entry 12). Considering the higher yield, the condition of 1.3 equiv Cs2CO3 was still as “standard conditions“ for subsequent studies. The presence of a nickel catalyst for the formation of Ni−H species was necessary for the semihydrogenation (entry 11). Scope of the reaction

With this set of “standard conditions” in hand, a variety of dialkyl alkynes were diastereoselectively semihydrogenated via the synergetic nickel/photoredox catalysis to provide cis-alkenes (Fig. 2). Ketones (4), esters (3 and 5), thiazole (5), alkene (6), phosphates (7 and 8), amides (9 and 12), ethers (6, 10, and 14), and acetal (11 and 13), were well-tolerated with absolute Z-selective alkenes afforded in good to excellent yields (79%–94% yields, 99:1 Z/E). The semihydrogenation of propargyl alcohols is full of challenges due to the overreduction, and a protecting-deprotecting protocol is necessary to avoid this side reaction4. With this carboxylic acid-facilitated nickel/photoredox method, propargyl alcohol derivatives were smoothly semihydrogenated to alkenes (15–20) in excellent yields without the formation of alkanes. Impressively, excellent diastereoselectivities were obtained wherever the alkyne moiety was adjacent to secondary (15), tertiary (16–18), or quaternary carbons (19 and 20), demonstrating the practicability of this method. Dialkyl alkynes (21–24) derived from biologically activated compounds were also evaluated to afford corresponding cis-alkenes in excellent diastereoselectivity. Moreover, two pharmaceuticals, Linagliptin and Mifepristone, with alkynes were also examined for the semihydrogenation (25 and 26). Remarkably, this photoredox/nickel catalyzed semihydrogenation proceeded smoothly in the presence of Lewis-basic heterocycles pyridine (22) and imidazole (25), which were persistently challenging for transition-metal catalyzed alkyne semihydrogenation21,56.Fig. 2Substrate scope for alkynes.Standard reaction conditions: alkyne (0.2 mmol), Ir(dtbppy)2(dtbbpy)PF6 (0.002 mmol), NiBr2.diglyme (0.02 mmol), bpy (0.02 mmol), PivOH (0.4 mmol) and Cs2CO3 (0.26 mmol) in THF (4.0 mL), irradiated by 18 W blue LEDs (455 nm) at 25 °C for 12 hours. Previous nickel/photoredox semihydrogenation was primarily restricted to aryl alkynes with poor to moderate diastereoselectivities. To evaluate this carboxylic acid-facilitated protocol, the arylalkyl-substituted alkynes were first subjected to the standard reaction conditions. cis-Alkenes were obtained in good yields with excellent diastereoselectivity (27–30, 86%–94% yields, 99:1 Z/E). Diaryl alkynes were then examined using this method and also exhibited excellent diastereoselectivities, regardless of whether they had electron-donating or electron-withdrawing arene substituents (31–38, 99:1 Z/E). Conventional semihydrogenation protocols for terminal alkynes often suffer from undesired oligomerization and over-reduction20. By using the carboxylic acid-facilitated synergistic catalytic protocol, vinyl products were obtained in excellent yields (39–41, 85%–95% yields). Finally, this method also provides an effective semihydrogenation of cyclo-alkynes. Of note, the Z-selective reduction of macrocyclic alkynes (42 and 43) provided a practical strategy for the total synthesis of macrocyclic lactones. Although considerable ring strain reduced the reactivity of the 8-membered ring alkyne, the cis-alkene 44 was smoothly obtained in 75% yield.

Mechanistic study

A series of experiments was conducted to gain the mechanism of the carboxylic acid-facilitated nickel/photoredox semihydrogenation. Gas chromatography (GC-TCD) analysis showed significant molecular hydrogen evolution when PivOH was added to the synergistic nickel/photoredox system and irradiated with 455 nm LED, indicating the existence of HER (Fig. 3a). When the alkyne 1 was added to the reaction mixture, the evolution of hydrogen was distinctly diminished. Moreover, these experimental results indicated that the addition of Ni−H species to the alkyne was more competitive than its protonation for hydrogen evolution. To rule out the possibility of semihydrogenation by molecular hydrogen under nickel catalysis, H2 was used in place of PivOH under standard conditions with or without photocatalysis. No alkene 2 was detected under these controlled experiments, illustrating that the reduction was initiated from in situ generated Ni−H species from Ni0 with carboxylic acid, but not the released molecular hydrogen by-product (Fig. 3b)21,57. To determine which was the hydrogen source for the semihydrogenation, a controlled deuteration was conducted with PivOD or d9-PivOH (Fig. 3c). The deuterated alkene 2 was detected under reaction conditions with PivOD but not with d9-PivOH, demonstrating that the proton of the carboxylic acid serves as the hydrogen source for semihydrogenation (Fig. S8). Parallel kinetic isotope effect (KIE) studies showed KH/KD as 1.02, indicating that in situ generation of NiII − H species is not the rate-determining step (Fig. 3d).

The photocatalyzed geometrical isomerization of E-alkenes is an alternative protocol for the preparation of Z-alkenes, albeit primarily restricted to activated alkenes58. To determine whether a similar photochemical geometric isomerization occurred during this semihydrogenation, a controlled experiment with the E-alkene 2 was performed. No Z-alkene 2 was afforded when the E-alkene 2 was treated under standard conditions for 12 h (Fig. 3e), suggesting that the high diastereoselectivity resulted from the cooperative effects of carboxylic acid instead of the geometrical isomerization.

Beyond serving as a proton source, the carboxylic acid should also undergo an electron-transfer process, which is essential for recycling the catalyst. When 5.0 equivalents of TEMPO were added to the reaction, the semihydrogenation of alkyne was completely inhibited, supporting a radical pathway involved in this reaction (Fig. 3f). Furthermore, the detection of TEMPO-tBu 5 confirmed that the electron transfer was initiated by the decarboxylation of PivOH. The Stern-Volmer fluorescence quenching experiments show that Ir(dtbppy)2(dtbbpy)PF6 could be quenched by deprotonated PivOH, while no quenching tendency for alkyne 1 was observed (Fig. 3g). These results indicate that the photoredox/nickel catalytic hydrogenation is a photoinduced electron transfer (PET) process between the photocatalyst and deprotonated PivOH. As shown in Fig. 3h, left, Ir(dtbppy)2(dtbbpy)PF6 (E1/2red [*IrIII/IrII] = +1.36 V versus SCE) possessed enough oxidation potential to oxidize deprotonated PivOH (Ep/2red = +0.96 V versus SCE) via a single electron transfer (SET) process. The NiII displayed an irreversible reductive wave with an onset potential of −1.43 V, which could be reduced by the IrII intermediate (E1/2red [IrIII/IrII] = −1.49 V versus SCE, Fig. 3h, right) via a SET event. Further CV studies showed that the redox properties of the nickel catalyst were affected in the presence of pivalic acid (Fig. 3h, right, green line). The cooperative ligand effects of pivalate slightly elevated the reductive potential of the nickel redox couple (E1/2red = −1.37 V versus SCE), rendering it more reversible and facilitating its reduction by IrII species48,59.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to explain this Z/E selectivity via the cooperative effects of various carboxylic acids to inhibit isomerization (Source Data). As shown in Fig. 3i, the Gibbs free energy change for the Z → E isomerization (ΔG‡) is about 0.55-11.42 kcal/mol. When the cooperative ligand is PivOH, the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG‡) for the Z → E isomerization of alkenyl nickel transition state is 11.42 kcal/mol, which could efficiently inhibit Z/E isomerization rather than AcOH and BnCOOH as cooperative ligands. These results are in accordance with the experimental Z/E selectivity of alkene 31. A Ni complex 47 was prepared by reaction of NiBr2·diglyme or Ni(COD)2 with stoichiometric amount bpy and PivOH. The X-ray analysis showed that there had two pivalate anions cooperation with the nickel (Fig. 3j). Moreover, when the Ni complex 47 was used as a catalyst in this nickel/photoredox catalyzed semihydrogenation of alkyne, (Z)-alkene 2 was obtained in 88% yield and 99:1 Z/E selectivity.

We operated DFT calculations for the nickel catalytic cycle of the semihydrogenation of alkyne 31, which further explain the full reaction profile about the nickel catalysis, the PivOH-cooperative effect, and the kinetic protonolysis for the synthesis of the high Z-selective alkene (Fig. 4a, Source Data). Computational results suggest that NiII − H species B is generated via the addition of Ni0(bpy)(diglyme) A with PivOH, undergoing the NiII intermediate Int 1 and the transient state TS 1. The activation free energy of TS 1 is 11.9 kcal/mol, which is feasible for the formation of Ni−H as the transition state. Subsequent syn-addition of alkyne 46 with NiII-H B through TS 2 generates vinyl NiII species C with the energy barrier of 23.5 kcal/mol. At this time, the intramolecular or intermolecular protonation provides a diverse pathway for the synthesis of alkene 31. For intramolecular protonation, PivOH as the proton donor, coordinates with vinyl NiII C forming two intermediates Int 2 A (intra) and Int 2B (intra), which undergo the intramolecular protonation transient state TS 3 A (intra) and TS 3B (intra) to provide cis-alkene 31 and NiII complex 47 (intra). Due to the higher energy barrier of TS 3B (intra) (ΔG‡ = 21.4 kcal/mol), TS 3 A (intra) (ΔG‡ = 14.9 kcal/mol) is the favorable pathway for the intramolecular protonation. This result corresponds to our experimental data. As a comparison, the intermolecular protonation would directly cross the higher energy barrier of the transient state of TS 3 A (inter) and TS 3B (inter) (25.9 and 21.7 kcal/mol) to generate alkene 31 and NiII complex 47 (inter). Finally, the photoredox SET transfer provide adequate energy for the conversion from NiII complex 47 (inter) to Ni0(bpy)(diglyme) A.Fig. 4Proposed mechanism.a DFT calculations for nickel catalytic cycle. b Proposed catalytic cycle.

Based on the experimental results and the DFT calculations, a full photoredox/nickel dual catalytic cycle was proposed as below (Fig. 4b). Firstly, PivOH reacted with Ni0 (A) for the in situ generation of NiII − H species B. The syn addition of alkyne 46 with NiII − H (B) afforded vinyl NiII species C. At this point, another PivOH as the proton donor could coordinate with vinyl NiII species C and form a more profitable transition state TS 3 A (intra), then undergo the intramolecular kinetic protonolysis for the generation of (Z)-alkene 31 and NiII complex 47. Meanwhile, the deprotonation of PivOH G as an electron donor could be oxidized by the excited state *IrIII E via a single electron transfer (SET) process to give the reduction state IrII species F and tBu radical H, then leading to the dismutation reaction. The SET event between IrII F and NiII 47 closes the catalytic cycle, generating PivO− G and then getting a proton from the reaction system for the acid-base equilibrium.

In conclusion, we reported a nickel/photoredox catalyzed highly diastereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes with PivOH in multifaceted roles. The diastereoselective semihydrogenation protocol benefited from PivOH acting as a cooperative ligand and the protonolysis of the alkenylnickel complex. Moreover, the carboxylic acid was developed as a hydrogen surrogate for the semihydrogenation by serving as both a proton and an electron source. Notably, the carboxylic acid-mediated semihydrogenation exhibits remarkably broad substrate scope and wide functional group tolerance, along with high geometrical selectivities and yields.

Methods

General procedures for the diastereoselective semihydrogenation of alkynes

To a 10 mL glass tube equipped with a septum and a magnetic stir bar was added Ir(dtbppy)2(dtbbpy)PF6 (2.3 mg, 0.002 mmol), 2,2’-dipyridine (bpy) (3.4 mg, 0.02 mmol), NiBr2.diglyme (7.5 mg, 0.02 mmol), PivOH (40.8 mg, 0.4 mmol), Cs2CO3 (84.5 mg, 0.26 mmol), alkyne (40.4 mg, 0.2 mmol), and THF (4.0 mL). The resulting mixture was sealed, wrapped with electrical tape, and irradiated with an 18 W LED (450 nm) at a distance of approximately 4 cm at 25 oC for 12 h. After the reaction was completed, as monitored by TLC, the reaction mixture was filtered with celite. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by silica chromatography.
