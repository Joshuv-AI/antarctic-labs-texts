Protocol for Fit4Transplant: iterative optimisation of an exercise prehabilitation service for haematological cancer patients using phase II adaptive trials and co-design
Authors: Camille E Short, Lara Edbrooke, Shaza Abo, Tamara Jones, Amit Khot, Robert K Mahar, Jasmyne Lee, Jess Crowe, Sarah Stratulate, Catherine L Granger, Selina M Parry, Linda Denehy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHematopoietic stem cell transplantation is a critical intensive treatment option for many people with haematological cancer but often leads to long-term reductions in physical function. Exercise-based prehabilitation can help to mitigate these effects and improve wellbeing. This study aims to evaluate and iteratively refine co-designed enhancements to an existing exercise-prehabilitation service.MethodTwo intervention packages will be evaluated in sequential single-arm Bayesian optimal phase II adaptive trials. Package 1 will include two staff training modules (focused on trauma-informed service delivery and delivering engaging exercise classes online) and a behaviour Change booklet for patients. Package 2 will include refined (if necessary) Package 1 components, as well as an interactive self-management website/mobile application for patients. Outcome assessments will be conducted at baseline and post-intervention. The rate of promising response (a composite efficacy and acceptability outcome) in each trial will be monitored with interim analyses after 30 participants have completed post-intervention assessments. The intervention will be ceased prior to reaching the recruitment target (n = 60) if inadequate promising responses are observed (≥ 11 participants not meeting promising response criteria). A sub-sample of participants will also be invited to participate in an interview regarding stakeholder experiences delivering and receiving the intervention packages. Recruitment will commence in February 2025.DiscussionThis sequential adaptive design allows trialling a simpler intervention while the digital component is finalised, with iterative refinements guided by interim analyses and co-design. The outcomes of this study will help inform future prehabilitation programs involving exercise programs and provide an example of how co-design and adaptive trials can be combined to optimise behavioural health interventions within routine care.Trial registrationAustralian NewZealand Clinical Trial Registry ACTRN12624000363583p. Background

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (SCT) is an important treatment option for aggressive haematological malignancies [1]. While SCT results in significant survival benefits, patients treated with SCT experience impaired physical capacity [2] and post-transplant comorbidities [3] that are associated with reduced psychological wellbeing [4, 5], short- and long-term health related quality of life [6–8], and increased all-cause mortality [9–11]. For many patients, it may take over a year for physical capacity to recover to pre-transplant levels and tends to remain below population norms [6, 12].

Several studies have shown that exercise intervention before SCT can help maintain physical capacity [13, 14] and is also likely to have social and economic benefits (e.g., reduced hospital length of stay [15]). Providing exercise intervention at this time point may also encourage ongoing exercise participation post-transplant [16], conferring additional benefit [17]. However, providing exercise support to haematological cancer patients prior to a stem cell transplant, particularly in changing clinical situations as regards to symptoms, treatment regimens and toxicities, in a way that is feasible, patient-centred and affordable for health services is a major service delivery challenge. To date, there have been several models of care evaluated in single-site evaluations (e.g [18, 19]), and there are other single-site studies currently underway [20, 21], all of which will help to inform uptake of evidence-based prehabilitation into practice. A common element of these models is the use of technology and home-based program components.

Technology-supported models of exercise support are considered a promising approach for increasing the scale of exercise oncology services, whilst also addressing patient preferences and needs for flexible home-based programs [22–24]. Technological advances now make it possible to embed important exercise intervention components, including supervision, tailored exercise prescription, behaviour change and social support [25] into home-based programs, or indeed any location with an internet connection (e.g., a favourite park). As a result, remote models of exercise support can now be equally effective as in-person programs, whilst also being more accessible [26, 27]. However, effectiveness depends on intervention design, the technology employed and how well the intervention delivered [24]. There is also a significant evidence practice/translation gap, with few digital interventions for cancer patients being sustained or made publicly available after research trials, even when found to be effective [28].

Several recommendations have been made in the broader digital health and behaviour change fields to facilitate uptake of digital interventions and optimise their potential. These include: (1) Developing interventions within the implementation context they are to be delivered [29–31]; (2) engaging meaningfully with intended end-users and stakeholders [32, 33]; (3) conducting intervention optimisation trials prior to conducting confirmatory trials [34]; and (4) iteratively refining digital interventions in a continual quality improvement process before scaling them in order to maximise reach, adoption, implementation and effectiveness [35].

Acting on these recommendations, we sought to optimise an existing prehabilitation service offered to haematological cancer patients scheduled to have an autologous SCT at a large tertiary treatment centre. Over a two-year period, an extensive co-design process was undertaken with patients, hospital staff and other stakeholders to design service improvements. Two intervention packages were developed, that build iteratively on each other. This research study aims to implement these co-designed intervention packages, evaluate them and the service as a whole and make additional improvements where necessary. The intended outcomes are an effective and acceptable prehabilitation model optimised for delivery within the intended implementation site and the development of new knowledge and resources for improving exercise-based prehabilitation services at other sites.

Methods

Trial design

A pragmatic optimisation protocol will be employed to evaluate two co-designed intervention packages. The protocol involves sequential single-arm Bayesian optimal phase II adaptive trial designs, with nested qualitative studies [36, 37]. An overview of the optimisation protocol is provided in Fig. 1. All available data will be reviewed after each trial by the co-design team, consisting of patients, hospital staff and members of the research team. The participating site is the Peter MaCallum Cancer Centre. All participants will provide informed consent prior to taking part in this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre Ethics Committee (HREC/91280/PMCC). The trial has been prospectively registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12624000363583). Any modifications to the trial protocol will be updated on the registry. The study protocol is reported in accordance with the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials) guidelines [38] (see Supplementary File 1). Requests for data and other study materials can be made to the corresponding author.

Participants and recruitment

Patients

The primary study population are adult patients with haematological cancer who have been referred for exercise prehabilitation prior to SCT at the participating site (see Table 1 for Eligibility Criteria). Patients scheduled for their initial appointment will be informed by the prehabilitation team that the service is being evaluated, provided with study information, and invited to participate. Interested patients will be followed-up by a member of the research team via email or phone within 2–10 days, and eligible patients will undergo the consent and enrolment process. Participants will be provided with a $25 gift card for assessments and interviews that are not part of routine care. There are no restrictions on other interventions participants can join.

Staff

Exercise professionals or managing staff directly involved in delivering prehabilitation will be eligible to participate in qualitative interviews focused on intervention acceptability and implementation fidelity. Staff will be purposively approached by email, phone, or in-person by a research team member and invited to participate. Named investigators will be excluded from interview participation but can provide feedback as part of the ongoing co-design process.

Co-designed intervention packages

The intervention packages were developed over a two-year period using co-design methodology guided by the British Design Council’s Double Diamond Framework [32]. In brief, the co-design process to date has involved comprehensive discovery research (e.g., direct observation of the exercise service, and independent research [14, 28, 39], and six interactive workshops to define, develop and refine solutions to the opportunities identified during discovery. Data collected during discovery has shown that the prehabilitation service at the participating site prior to the COVID-19 pandemic was feasible to deliver and positively impacts patient physical function [19]. However, changes to the service model were made in response to COVID-19 and areas for improvement were identified, including attendance rates for telehealth classes.

Co-design participants involved in core workshops included patients with a lived experience of stem cell transplant and prehabilitation, clinicians (haematologist, specialist nurse, exercise professionals, psychologist), software developers, the digital lead at the Leukemia Foundation, and the research team (exercise and cancer researchers). Key recommendations and intervention elements from the co-design process are described in Table 2. Relating to Recommendation 2, a spin off project based on social identity leadership theory, which has shown promise for enhancing engagement in exercise classes [40] was conducted to design the training module for online exercise classes. The spin-off project involved a single co-design workshop with twelve patients with haematological cancer recruited from our survey work.

Co-design recommendations and intervention components

Intervention package 1

Intervention Package 1 includes two training modules for staff and a patient booklet (see Supplementary File 2 for illustrative content, full access can be requested via email to the corresponding author). The first training module, titled ‘Delivering exercise services in a trauma-informed way’, focuses on raising awareness about trauma and skills for delivering exercise services in a trauma-informed way. It takes ~ 20 min to complete and consists of text-based education, videos, scenarios, and a learning check. The second training module, titled ‘Delivering engaging online exercise classes’, focuses on strategies that exercise leaders could use to make online classes more socially engaging and supportive. The training module takes ~ 15 min to complete and consists of educational content, theory-informed and consumer-suggested strategies, learning checks, self-reflection tasks, and a call to action. The ‘ExerciseGuide’ booklet was designed to completement existing education provided by the site. Drawing on relevant evidence [41–43] and theory [44–47] it provides guidance on overcoming barriers to exercise and additional behaviour change advice for adhering to exercise programs, such as how to establish an exercise habit.

Intervention package 2

The website (for use in Trial 2), called ReadyFit is under development. It includes similar educational content to the booklet, but also has interactive features such as exercise videos and session logs. It will be available as a website, or to download from app stores. See Supplementary File 2 for a wireframe developed during co-design.

Implementation strategies

Several implementation strategies (described below) have been developed to introduce the co-design packages to the prehabilitation service. To facilitate generalisability, we have mapped each strategy to the corresponding term from the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy [48] (shown in brackets).

Update to peter mac protocols (develop education materials)

Links to the training modules are included in relevant Peter Mac Protocols (Exercise Class Guidelines; Prehab Manual) and prompts have been included throughout to assist staff in implementing core learning outcomes from the training.

Brief educational materials for staff (develop/distribute education materials)

A simple, single page document was produced to inform stakeholders about the co-designed intervention packages and the optimisation trial and emailed to staff (see Supplementary File 3).

Purposely reexamine implementation (conduct cyclical small tests of change)

All available data will be reviewed after Trial 1 and the co-design team will consider how improvements to the quality of care can be achieved.

Feedback on performance (audit and provide feedback)

Data collected after both trials will be summarised for the Prehabilitation team and their managers. Data will be presented in a brief report or via a presentation, depending on provider preference and availability.

Outcome assessments

As part of the co-design process, co-designers undertook activities to define success of the project and outline what a revised exercise prehabilitation service should achieve. Key themes included: “Helping patients” – empowering behaviour change and improving health outcomes; “Access for all” – despite location, risk level and language spoken; “Acceptability” – patient satisfaction and care that meets individual needs, and; “Sustainability” – requiring benefit on outcomes important to funders. Outcome assessments have been selected and prioritised based on these themes, as well as previous literature and current practice at the participating site.

Objective and patient-reported outcomes will be assessed at baseline and post-intervention (i.e., pre-procedure). Data relating to intervention delivery outcomes (adherence, cost) will be collected during the intervention period. Data relating to intervention feasibility and fidelity will be collected via qualitative interview with a subset of patient and staff participants after interim and/or final anlayses. A copy of the interview guides are available in Supplementary File 4. See Table 3 for assessment schedule. Study specific surveys will be administered online via REDCap, unless a paper copy is requested by participants. Qualitative interviews will be conducted online using Zoom videoconferencing software or via telephone, unless a face-to-face interview is preferred by the participant (providing it is logistically possible). To promote retention and completion of follow-up measures a research assistant will contact all participants by phone and work closely with prehabilitation staff to monitor completition of follow-up measures completed as part of standard care. Outcome and assessment methods are describe in detail in Table 4.

Demographic information and medical characteristics will be collected at baseline through self-report questionnaire and medical record review. Medical characteristics will include diagnosis type, time from diagnosis to transplant date, pre-transplant conditioning regimen, medication use for symptom management, and other releavant medical information, such as comorbidities. Demographic information collected from medical records will include age, postcode (used to classify patient’s home location as urban, regional or remote) and gender. Marital status, education, employment and languages spoken from home will be collected via survey.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome will be a composite binary endpoint including an efficacy and acceptability outcome. The rate of promising response is assessed using this binary endpoint, with a promising response defined as when the intervention is determined to be both efficacious AND acceptable. Efficacy and acceptability cut-points were chosen based on historic data [19], information collected during co-design workshops, and the clinical standard expected to be reached after implementation of the intervention (see sample size justification for more information).

Efficacy will be assessed via the 30-second sit-to-stand (30STS), which measures lower limb strength and function [49] and can be conducted face-to-face or via telehealth [50]. Efficacy will be determined based on the proportion of participants achieving at least 12 repetitions during the 30STS. This cut-point was chosen as the minimum standard because the participating site classifies patients who achieve less than 12 repetitions on the 30STS prior to treatment as “higher risk” (based on [51]).

Acceptability will be assessed via the 7-item acceptability of health care intervention questionnaire [52] and a star rating item (“how many stars would you give the exercise prehabilitation service out of five”). The 7-item questionnaire accesses seven constructs theorised to capture the key dimensions of acceptability. The total score assumes equal weighting for each construct, which may or may not be the case depending on an individual’s circumstances [52]. The star rating item is included in addition to the 8-item questionnaire as it is proposed to be a good measure of people’s “gut feeling”, which is theoretised to drive consumer engagement with health services [53]. Acceptability will be determined based on the proportion of participants rating the intervention as ≥ 26/35 on the 7-item acceptability of health care intervention questionnaire [52] or a star rating of ≥ 3/5 [53]. As there are no established cut-points for these measures, the minimum standards were set by the research team (see supplementary file 5).

Secondary outcomes

Assessment of secondary outcomes will provide co-designers with further information to guide the improvement process, evaluate the service, and develop a business case for continuing to implement the co-designed service improvements at the participating site. Secondary outcomes include efficacy outcomes assessed as per standard practice (i.e., objectively assessed functional outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, and hospital outcomes) and implementation outcomes assessed using mixed methods guided by the Implementability of Healthcare interventions conceptual framework [54]. In addition, we will also assess theory-based mediators of intervention effects on exercise behaviour change. These outcomes are not currently assessed as part of standard care. Consideration of underlying psychosocial intervention mechanisms is recommended to improve the efficacy of prehabilitation interventions in this setting [55].

Statistical analyses

Sample size and stopping rules

We know that at baseline, patients complete a mean of 13.77 (SD 4.86) repetitions in the 30STS and 15.95 (SD 5.35) repetitions after standard prehabilitation [19]. From this we can show that at baseline, approximately 64.2% of patients perform over 12 repetitions, increasing to 77% after participating in standard prehabilitation. We expect to maintain this level of efficacy after implementing the co-designed interventions. Additionally, within the current service model, acceptability is estimated to be ~ 60% (based on data collected during the discovery phase of co-design). We believe that the co-designed interventions can improve acceptability to 75%. From these data, we define our response rate in our primary composite endpoint for the null hypothesis (0.642 × 0.6 = 0.385) and the alternative (0.77 × 0.75 = 0.58).

The maximum sample size of each trial is 60 with an interim analysis at 30. Within each trial, if the number of promising responses is less than 11 at the interim analysis and less than 29 at the final analysis, then the intervention is deemed ineffective. The Bayesian optimal phase 2 design (see Supplementary File 6) guarantees type 1 error of 0.0451 and power of 0.912 for each of the trials.

For the qualitative interviews, all eligible staff involved in delivering the intervention will be invited to participate (~ N = 6). For patient participants, a purposive maximum variation approach will be used to achieve diversity across haematological malignancy, physical functioning status (low, medium and high support needs) and demographic characteristics (geographical location, gender, and age). Given rolling recruitment, interview selection will occur in stages, with early participants sampled broadly, followed by adaptive selection to increase sample diversity where needed. This process will continue until data saturation is reached, or the trial is stopped at interim analysis. It is estimated 20–25 interviews will be needed to achieve sufficient depth.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics will be calculated for all baseline and outcome data. The primary composite endpoint, and its components, will be estimated as a binomial proportion with 95% confidence intervals. Secondary endpoints will be analysed using statistical methods that are appropriate to the endpoint (e.g. means and medians for continuous endpoints, proportions for binary endpoints), with 95% confidence intervals.

Further exploratory analyses to gain a better understanding of how the intervention worked will also be undertaken. This will include regression analyses to: (1) examine changes in the proposed mechanisms (e.g., self-efficacy) from baseline to follow-up; (2) examine associations between the proposed mechanisms (e.g., self-efficacy) and the study outcomes (e.g., adherence to exercise sessions, fitness), and; (3) examining associations between participant characteristics (potential moderators) and intervention adherence (sessions and intensity) as well as changes in fitness outcomes. These analyses will not allow us to examine any causal effects but will assist with gaining a preliminary understanding of intervention components that are not working as intended. The analyses will also assist in prespecifying subgroups who may benefit more from the intervention and corroborate theory-driven effect modifiers that should be considered in the future trials. Missing outcome data is expected to be minimal, and analyses will be performed on a complete case basis. All quantitative analyses will be overseen by an experienced biostatistician.

Qualitative interviews will be transcribed and analyzed inductively by trained mixed methods researchers using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis method [68]. Following this, a deductive analysis will be conducted, applying the specific frameworks used in the quantitative analysis (e.g., Implementability of healthcare interventions framework) to further interpret the data and align findings with existing theoretical perspectives.

Discussion

The overall aim of this project is to implement and iteratively refine co-designed enhancements to an established exercise prehabilitation service. The intended outcome is an effective and acceptable prehabilitation model optimised for delivery at the participating cancer centre, and new knowledge and resources useful for improving exercise-based prehabilitation services at other sites.

The quality improvement process and trial design utilised in this project is a novel and promising approach for the optimisation of prehabilitation and other behavioural interventions. While co-design is increasingly reported in the development of supportive care interventions [32], many investigators have not followed central tenets of co-design described by co-design pioneers, such as sharing power and using participatory design approaches to develop insights [69]. Rather, traditional methods like qualitative interviews or focus groups are often relied on to obtain consumer input and feedback. Whilst this is still meaningful involvement and a step in creating person-centred interventions [70], it may not yield the same design benefits [71]. The interventions being trialled in the current study, as well as the evaluation protocol have been developed based on a comprehensive co-design process, starting with defining the issues, and moving through to developing solutions and defining criteria for success.

A key outcome of defining the issues in partnership with co-designers has been the development of intervention components through a trauma-informed lens. This was not originally identified by the research team, despite comprehensive background research and a high level of experience and familiarity with the patient group and prehabilitation service. It is hoped that this insight from co-design, and the associated outputs will increase the acceptability of prehabilitation, and in turn, increase benefits to patients through increased engagement and well-being.

The evaluation approach employed recognises a broader movement in behavioural science to incorporate optimisation trials prior to larger RCTs [72], and follows recommendations from implementation science and digital design by focusing on iteratively optimising the program within the intended implementation context [31, 54, 73]. An adaptive trial design, where outcomes are observed and analysed at prespecified interim time points and modifications to study design are made [74], will provide the best opportunity to efficiently optimise the intervention, whilst simultaneously collecting data on acceptability and efficacy. The nested qualitative study will provide valuable information on stakeholder experience delivering and receiving the intervention packages.

The evaluation of two single-arms sequentially serves both a pragmatic and scientific purpose. The website app is still under development and will not be ready for implementation for six months. This design allows us to start the trial, pilot a less complex intervention, and make iterative improvements prior to trialling the website component.

By publishing this protocol, we aim to ensure transparency around our prespecified binary outcome and rate of promising response criteria. We also aim to inform interested parties of the intervention components we have developed and the upcoming trial, as well as aid replication and critical review of study methodology.
