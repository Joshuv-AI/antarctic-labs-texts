Improved platelet separation performance from whole blood using an acoustic fluidics system
Authors: Kazuko Sakai, Shuta Ohara, Junko Tanaka, Kenichi Suda, Takamichi Muramatsu, Chihiro Uematsu, Yasuhiro Tsutani, Tetsuya Mitsudomi, Kazuto Nishio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effectiveness of acoustic separation for platelet analysis in patients with non–small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC), comparing it with traditional centrifugation methods. In total, 10 patients with NSCLC and 10 healthy volunteers provided peripheral blood samples, which were processed using either acoustic separation or centrifugation to isolate platelets. The study included whole transcriptome analysis of platelets, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and tumor tissue samples, employing hierarchical clustering and Gene Ontology analysis to explore gene expression differences. Acoustic separation proved more efficient than centrifugation in terms of platelet yield, recovery rate, and RNA yield. Gene expression profiles of platelets from patients with NSCLC showed distinct patterns compared with healthy volunteers, indicating tumor‐influenced alterations. Gene Ontology analysis revealed enrichment in pathways associated with platelet activation and the tumor microenvironment. This finding indicates the potential of acoustic isolation in platelet separation and its relevance in understanding the unique gene expression profile of platelets in patients with NSCLC. The findings of this study suggested that platelets from cancer patients separated by acoustic techniques exhibited tumor‐specific alterations and provided new insights into the diagnosis of cancer in platelet analysis systems in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer remains the predominant cause of cancer‐related mortality globally, with a grim 5‐year survival rate of about 15%. 1 Therapeutically, lung cancer is classified as small‐cell carcinoma and NSCLC. 2 Molecularly targeted therapies or immune checkpoint inhibitors tailored to genetic mutations are used for advanced NSCLC, as are cytotoxic chemotherapies. 3 Personalized therapies, targeting genetic alterations within the tumor DNA, have become central to NSCLC treatment strategies, emphasizing the need for precise genetic profiling. Tissue biopsy is the conventional method for mutation detection, but it has considerable limitations due to its invasive nature and high failure rates in mutation analysis, as reported for EGFR, KRAS, and ALK testing. 4 Furthermore, a singular biopsy may not fully capture the disease's heterogeneity, hence the growing interest in liquid biopsies.

Liquid biopsy, a less invasive modality, utilizes biofluids—predominantly blood—to assess tumor traits and monitor disease trajectory, including progression, recurrence, and treatment response. 5 , 6 Over the past decade, liquid biopsy has centered on analyzing circulating tumor cells, circulating tumor DNA, and circulating free DNA. 7 , 8 Technological advancements in molecular biology have enabled the analysis of mRNA expression profiles via microarrays and next‐generation sequencing, although the instability of mRNA in liquid biopsies presents challenges. 9 , 10 In the realm of cancer, platelets, which are integral to processes beyond hemostasis and wound healing, such as epithelial–mesenchymal transition, metastasis, angiogenesis, and immune evasion, have shown differential platelet gene expression in cancer patients compared with healthy individuals. 11

Subsequent efforts have developed classification algorithms for cancer detection based on TEPs, with a focus on optimizing the purity and quality of the collected specimens, particularly platelet RNA. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 For clinical applications, a protocol describing a complete procedure for collecting and processing TEPs and training particle swarm optimization classifiers has been published. 15 The TEP collection method is the most important in clinical practice, ensuring the purity of the target specimen, the quantity and quality of the specimen (in this case, the quality of the platelet RNA), and the simplicity of the process. Standard centrifugation methods during preparation require a series of manual steps and a skilled operator. Current standard procedures for platelet collection, reliant on centrifugation, are manual and labor‐intensive. 15

This study aims to appraise the acoustophoretic method as a viable alternative for platelet analysis.

Acoustophoresis uses ultrasound to generate waves in a microchannel to affect the movement of cells in a fluid, either by capturing and constraining cell movement as the fluid flows through the channel 16 or by moving cells between two fluids flowing side by side along the microchannel. The latter method is used to move cells between two fluids flowing side by side along a microchannel. 17 In this study we focused on the latter application. The technique of manipulating cell movement with ultrasound‐induced waves in microchannels could streamline platelet collection and set the stage for automated systems. 18 In addition, this study investigated exploratively how platelet RNA is “educated” by the tumor environment and its subsequent impact on tumors in terms of gene expression profiles.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

This study enrolled 10 patients with NSCLC. Prior to surgery, 5 mL of blood was collected from each patient to obtain plasma. Additionally, blood and plasma samples were collected from 10 healthy volunteers as controls (Figure 1).

Sample processing and flow diagram. This diagram depicts the processing of samples from ten stages I–IVA non–small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients who had undergone surgical resection and 10 healthy volunteers. Each participant contributed 5 mL of peripheral blood, which was subjected to either acoustic separation or centrifugation to isolate platelets. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted using the Ficoll‐Paque method. Additionally, formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) surgical resection specimens were collected from lung cancer patients. All samples underwent RNA extraction, followed by whole transcriptome analysis.

Centrifugal separation of platelet fractions

Three mL of blood was centrifuged at 150 × g for 20 min using a swing‐rotor centrifuge to separate the platelet‐containing plasma fraction, carefully preserving up to 5 mm above the buffy coat. Platelet‐containing plasma fraction solution was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was removed leaving 250 μL and resuspended in 750 μL of Isogen‐LS (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan).

Acoustic separation of platelet fractions

Platelet separation by ultrasound was performed from blood using the AcouWash system (AcouSort, Sweden) (Figure 2). The device consists of a microfluidic channel placed on a piezoelectric ultrasound transducer with two inlets and two outlets at each end of the channel. Next, 1 mL volume of blood diluted in PBS at 2.5×, 70% Ficoll‐Paque PREMIUM 1.084 (GE Healthcare) in PBS, were injected into the microfluidic channels from the side inlet and center inlet, respectively. The flow rates of the inlet and outlet were set at 190 μL/min (side inlet), 380 μL/min (center inlet), 270 μL/min (side outlet), and 300 μL/min (center outlet), respectively. Blood cells were separated by acoustophoretic forces with 2 MHz standing waves in the microfluidic channel, and leukocytes and red blood cells were collected from the center outlet and platelets from the side outlet. Platelet solution was then added with EDTA to a final concentration of 6 mM. After centrifugation at 2000 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was removed leaving 250 μL and resuspended in 750 μL of Isogen‐LS (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan).

Acoustic platelet separation mechanism. this figure illustrates the AcouWash system used for platelet separation from blood samples. The system features a microfluidic channel placed on top of a piezoelectric ultrasound transducer and is equipped with dual inlets and outlets at each end.

Separation of buffy coat

For buffy coat isolation, 0.4 mL of blood was diluted twofold with PBS and layered onto 0.6 mL of Ficoll‐Paque PREMIUM 1.084. After centrifugation at 400 × g for 30 min in a swing‐rotor centrifuge, and removal of the plasma fraction, the buffy coat layer was carefully extracted.

Counting of blood cells

A multiparameter automated hematology analyzer XN‐330 (Sysmex, Japan) was employed to count leukocytes, erythrocytes, and platelets in both whole blood and post‐separation samples.

RNA extraction from platelets and PBMC samples

Total RNA was isolated using Isogen‐LS (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

DNA/RNA extraction from tumor tissue

DNA/RNA from FFPE tumor specimens was extracted using an AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen). Nucleic acid quality and quantity were verified using a NanoDrop 2000 system, PicoGreen dsDNA Reagent, and RiboGreen RNA reagent (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Whole transcriptome analysis

The AmpliSeq Transcriptome Human Gene Expression Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was utilized for whole transcriptome analysis. Library preparation involved creating cDNA from 10 ng of total RNA using the SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). This was followed by adding PCR Master Mix and AmpliSeq human transcriptome gene expression primer pool (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with subsequent amplification of cDNA for 12 cycles. After multiplex PCR, Ion Xpress Barcode Adapters (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were ligated to the PCR products and purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Libraries were then pooled and sequenced on an Ion Torrent S5 instrument with an Ion 550 Chip Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The Ion Torrent Suite v5.12 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for base calling, alignment to the human reference genome (hg19), and quality control. Gene‐level expression values for all 20,802 RefSeq human genes were generated using the AmpliSeqRNA plugin.

Gene selection and pathway analysis

The 20,802 RefSeq human gene expression values were normalized using Transcriptome Analysis Console ver. 4.0.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 200 most significant genes, based on the highest SNR between cancer patients and healthy volunteers in the platelet RNA signature from acoustic separation, were selected for hierarchical clustering. This clustering used 1 − the Pearson correlation coefficient as a distance measure, employing the average linkage method. Functional and Pathway Enrichment Analysis was performed using Metascape ver.3.5.

Mutational analysis

Analysis of somatic mutations was performed using next‐generation sequencing and digital PCR. Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables between groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism software, version 8.4 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Platelet yields from patients with NSCLC and healthy volunteers were compared for both separation methods. Centrifugation yielded 4.6 ± 1.3 (104/mL plasma) for healthy volunteers and 5.8 ± 3.0 for patients with NSCLC. Acoustic separation, however, yielded significantly higher amounts: 15.1 ± 3.7 for healthy volunteers and 11.0 ± 4.6 for patients with NSCLC (p = 0.0020) (Figure 3A,B).

This set of graphs showcases the comparison of platelet yields in healthy volunteers (A) and patients with NSCLC (B); platelet purity in healthy volunteers (C) and patients with NSCLC (D); platelet recovery rates in healthy volunteers (E) and patients with NSCLC (F); and RNA yields in healthy volunteers (G) and patients with NSCLC (H). Median values are represented by a line in each plot. Statistical significance was determined using the Mann–Whitney U‐test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.005).

Purity was assessed based on the percentage of leukocyte contamination. With centrifugation, purity was 100.00 ± 0.01% for both groups. Acoustic separation resulted in slightly lower purities of 99.97 ± 0.04% for healthy volunteers and 99.95 ± 0.04% for patients with NSCLC (Figure 3C,D).

The recovery rate, calculated from the amount of platelets in 1 mL of blood before separation, was higher with acoustic separation. For healthy volunteers, the recovery rates were 25.7 ± 3.6% for centrifugation and 72.0 ± 10.4% for acoustic separation. For patients with NSCLC, the rates were 33.2 ± 5.8% and 70.9 ± 8.2%, respectively (p = 0.0020) (Figure 3E,F).

RNA yield from platelets was significantly higher with acoustic separation. Healthy volunteers had yields of 20.5 ± 7.2 ng/mL plasma with centrifugation and 77.8 ± 28.3 with acoustic separation. Patients with NSCLC had yields of 25.0 ± 11.0 and 67.6 ± 20.6, respectively (p = 0.0020) (Figure 3G,H).

Overall, platelets collected via acoustic separation demonstrated higher yield, recovery rate, and RNA yield compared with centrifugation. Although acoustic separation showed a slightly higher rate of leukocyte contamination, the median rate was less than 0.1%. Therefore, RNA obtained by acoustic separation was selected for further platelet profiling in cancer patients.

Gene expression profile of platelets from cancer patients

This analysis conducted a whole transcriptome analysis on RNA samples from lung cancer patients and healthy volunteers. RNA was extracted from platelets, PBMC, and tumor tissue samples. Out of the 70 samples, several were excluded from the analysis due to low sequencing quality (valid read less than 80%): one platelet sample obtained via acoustic separation from a cancer patient, two platelet samples via centrifugation from cancer patients, one platelet sample via centrifugation from a healthy volunteer, and two FFPE tumor samples from a cancer patient.

After normalization, genes with low expression were filtered out. The top 200 genes exhibiting SNR between cancer patients and healthy volunteers in platelets obtained by acoustic separation were selected for further classification. The gene expression dataset underwent unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on 1 − the Pearson correlation coefficient (Figure 4). This analysis identified four primary clusters (A–D), distinctly categorizing platelets from cancer patients, platelets from healthy volunteers, PBMC, and tumor tissue from cancer patients. The gene signature in platelets was notably different from those in PBMC and tumor tissue. The PBMC profile did not differentiate between cancer patients and healthy volunteers.

Somatic mutations were detected only in tumor tissue RNA (Figure S1) and were not found in platelet RNA. It is inferred that TEPs are platelet RNAs influenced by the tumor microenvironment.

Cluster and Gene Ontology analysis of platelets from cancer patients

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed on the top 200 genes with the highest SNR, effectively segregating cancer patients and healthy volunteers into distinct clusters (Cluster A and Cluster B). Platelets isolated by both acoustic separation and centrifugation methods fell into the same cluster, with no clear distinction between the two. Notably, most cancer patients were categorized in Cluster A, while healthy volunteers predominantly fell into Cluster B (Figure 5A). This delineation implies that patients with NSCLC possess a unique platelet gene expression profile, likely to have been influenced by the presence of the tumor, suggesting a tumor‐educated state of the platelets.

Gene Ontology analysis was conducted on gene sets within each cluster (Figure 5B). Among the top upregulated gene sets enriched in Cluster A, significant pathways included platelet activation, signaling, and aggregation. Furthermore, additional functional pathways were identified, such as VEGFA–VEGFR2 signaling, RHO GTPase effectors, ROCKs, and cytokine production. These findings indicated that the platelet RNA assessed in this study originated from platelets and that their gene expression profile was notably shaped by the internal tumor environment.

This analysis underscores the significant impact of cancer on platelet gene expression, revealing distinct pathways and mechanisms activated in cancer patients that differentiated them from healthy individuals.

DISCUSSION

This study's whole transcriptome analysis clearly distinguished between the platelets of cancer patients, platelets of healthy volunteers, leukocytes, and tumor tissues of cancer patients.

In the unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the gene expression dataset (Figure 5), platelets obtained through acoustic separation and centrifugal separation were categorized into the same cluster, showing no distinct separation. They formed a different cluster from the platelets of healthy volunteers. This suggests that platelets obtained through acoustic separation yielded the same TEPs as those obtained by centrifugal separation, indicating their utility in evaluating TEPs. One case was classified in the cluster of healthy controls based on the gene expression profile. Based on the quality of the platelets, the rate of leukocyte contamination in this case was 0.11%, approximately three times higher than other platelets, and the low purity may have been the cause of the misclassification of this case. Furthermore, enrichment analysis revealed an upregulation in factors related to tumor microenvironment, such as angiogenesis, in the platelets of cancer patients, thus biologically validating the suitability of the acoustic separation method for platelet RNA extraction.

The comparison of RNA yield and its quality between acoustic separation and centrifugal separation generally demonstrated their equivalence. Although a slightly higher leukocyte contamination rate was observed in the acoustic separation method, gene expression profiles suggested that this contamination did not significantly impact the results. Additionally, the initial blood volume used in the acoustic method was 2 mL, whereas 3 mL was used in the centrifugal method to obtain a comparable yield of platelet RNA, suggesting good recovery efficiency. The sample size of 10 patients and 10 healthy volunteers is considered relatively small. It would be preferable to have a larger sample size. However, it appears to be a robust comparison of two platelet isolation methods in patients with NSCLC and healthy volunteers. However, as this was an early‐stage study, it was suggested that the presented data were significant in demonstrating the validity of the method. Despite the small sample size, the results of the biological experiments were supported by statistically significant findings, and the Gene Ontology analysis provided reasonable evidence of pathways related to the intrinsic function of platelets. The findings that platelets isolated using acoustic separation had a biological phenotype similar to that of conventional centrifugation were considered to provide further evidence of the usefulness and equivalence of acoustic separation. This study demonstrates the validity of the acoustic separation method for TEP evaluation, potentially paving the way for future automation. Further investigations will also be necessary for standardizing the procedure.

The present study revealed that the gene expression profile of platelets differs from those of tumor tissue. As previously reported, the RNA profile of platelet fractions is affected by tumor cells and is considered to be caused by specific splicing activity changes in platelet‐derived RNA due to tumor‐associated stimulation. 19 On the other hand, it has been hypothesized that changes in platelet mRNA in patients with cancer are due to the transfer of tumor‐derived mRNA. 20 The transfer of tumor components is a direct way of education. According to this hypothesis, TEP includes tumor‐derived transcripts such as EGFRvIII and EML4‐ALK. 20 , 21 In our sample cohort, we used digital PCR to search for somatic mutations in platelet samples from patients with lung cancer, but we did not detect any such mutations (Figure S1). Despite the limited sample size, the findings indicated that platelet RNA was derived from the platelet’s own mRNA, and tumor‐derived RNA was not directly transferred into platelets.

The remaining question was where and how platelets are educated; it has been reported that platelets are produced in megakaryocytes, aid thrombosis and hemostasis, are involved in immune surveillance and communication, aid vascular remodeling, and inhibit inflammation. 18 , 22 , 23 Pathways associated with chromosome segregation and organization, genes related to primary cilium development, cell cycle, and mitotic anaphase were downregulated in platelets in cancer patients. Downregulation or dysregulation of these signal pathways in tumor cells can lead to genomic instability, abnormal cell cycle control, impaired chromosome segregation, and altered cellular responses to external signals. 21 , 24 , 25 , 26 These molecular alterations collectively contribute to the development, progression, and characteristics of tumor cells. In the present study, enrichment analysis also showed that platelets in cancer patients were enriched in platelet activation pathways, indicating enhanced platelet function in cancer patients. Billroth described clots containing malignant cells 27 and the hypothesis that clots are a factor in metastasis has been proposed through platelet activation. 28 In addition, platelets that are upregulated in tumors are suggested to act on the tumor microenvironment through enhancement of VEGFA–VEGFR2 signaling 29 and the RHO GTPase‐ROCK pathway. 30

The specific RNA signatures in TEPs can be an important tool in cancer diagnosis, progression monitoring, and treatment prediction in the future, and automated platelet collection has the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.

In conclusion, platelet gene expression of cancer patients isolated by acoustic procedure was tumor educated, suggesting the enhancement of platelet functions such as cellular response to chemical stress and cell chemotaxis. The profile of platelets is specific to cancer patients, suggesting that it can be used to predict the presence or recurrence of cancer in the future.
