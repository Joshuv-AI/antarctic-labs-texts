Tetracycline Resistance Pattern Among Lymphatic Filariasis‐Hyperendemic Communities in Southern Ghana: A Mixed‐Method Approach
Authors: Priscilla Osei‐Poku, Mary Boampomaa Wilson, Abdul Latif Koney Shardow, Arnold Abakah, Emmanuel Kobla Atsu Amewu, Cynthia Adu‐Asiamah Kyerewaa, Amma Larbi, Alexander Kwarteng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimTetracyclines continue to serve as a cost‐effective and essential treatment for common infections, particularly in resource‐limited regions such as lymphatic filariasis (LF)‐hyperendemic communities. The growing use of tetracycline‐based antibiotics by symptomatic filarial lymphedema patients, likely influenced by the demonstrated benefits of anti‐Wolbachia therapy, especially following doxycycline trials, underlines the necessity for vigilant monitoring of tetracycline resistance patterns. This study evaluated patients' antibiotic knowledge, attitude and practice in LF‐endemic communities, and the resistance profiles of tetracycline‐class antibiotics to assess the clinical impact of resistance on current anti‐Wolbachia treatments.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study in Ghana's Ahanta West District enrolled 71 filarial lymphedema patients from four LF‐hyperendemic communities. Structured questionnaires evaluated KAP on antibiotic use and resistance, complemented by microbiological analysis of wound swabs from 28 patients to profile the wound microbiome and assess tetracycline resistance.ResultsMean antibiotic knowledge (1.8 ± 1.0), attitude (1.4 ± 1.1), and practice (1.9 ± 1.1) scores were low, yielding a combined KAP score of 5.0 ± 1.9 (31.2% ± 11.9%) with no significant correlation to the educational level of patients. the facilities survey, the primary healthcare facilities in the area stocked at least one tetracycline‐class antibiotic (specifically tetracycline and/or doxycycline). Microbiological testing identified 68 distinct bacterial isolates (20 genera, 36 species; dominated by S. sciuri, S. aureus). Varied tetracycline resistance observed: doxycycline (35.3%), tetracycline (26.5%), and minocycline (17.6%), with nine (13.2%) isolates resistant to all three. High‐resolution melting analysis detected tet(M) gene in 34.1% (14) of isolates with Tm variation (69.27°C–73.06°C), suggesting variants.ConclusionMultifaceted strategies are essential to curb tetracycline resistance and overuse. These include culturally adapted education to enhance antibiotic stewardship among patients and providers, optimized wound care protocols addressing polymicrobial infections through improved hygiene and tailored regimens, and ongoing resistance surveillance to track trends while promoting therapeutic alternatives.

Introduction

Lymphatic filariasis (LF) is caused by parasitic nematodes such as Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, and Brugia timori and is transmitted by several species of mosquitoes. LF is endemic in 72 countries, affecting over 120 million people [1]. At least 36 million people have been reported to be living with these LF‐related pathologies, particularly hydrocele and lymphedema [2]. LF remains a significant public health concern as the clinical manifestations of the advanced stages of LF result in substantial morbidity and disability. This leads to profound socioeconomic consequences, including reduced productivity and increased healthcare costs [3]. The disease often manifests as a significant enlargement of the limbs, including entire legs or arms, and impacts the genitals, vulva, and breasts. These physical manifestations undermine patients’ well‐being, imposing substantial and deep psychological burdens [4].

The mass drug administration (MDA) program, implemented in 38 of 54 African countries, which are LF‐hyperendemic, primarily involves distributing annual or biannual doses of albendazole, ivermectin, and diethylcarbamazine citrate to interrupt transmission. These efforts have achieved major successes in reducing LF prevalence in many regions. However, these MDA control program's chemotherapeutics (albendazole, ivermectin, and diethylcarbamazine citrate) predominantly target the microfilariae in human tissues with low macrofilaricidal activity [2, 5]. The adult filarial worms are responsible for the development and progression of LF‐associated pathologies [6]. The death of the adult worms induces chronic inflammation and causes damage to lymphatic vessels, impairing fluid drainage and leading to lymphatic dysfunction. Unfortunately, despite the MDA's success in decreasing LF transmission rates, pathological manifestations due to the resultant lymphatic damage persist in affected individuals [7].

Recognizing the urgent need for a macrofilaricidal agent, the WHO emphasizes the importance of such macrofilaricidal agents in the prevention and management of lymphatic filariasis (LF)‐related conditions as part of the WHO Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD) roadmap for 2021–2030 [8]. Researchers are actively exploring the discovery and development of novel antifilarial drugs with potent macrofilaricidal properties. Currently, alternative research approaches include repurposing existing chemotherapeutics. For example, antibacterial agents with significant macrofilaricidal activity have been used to treat the late‐stage pathological manifestations of LF. Antibacterials from the tetracycline class, notably doxycycline and minocycline, have demonstrated significant efficacy in sterilizing female filarial nematodes and inhibiting their proliferation [9, 10, 11]. These anti‐Wolbachia agents provide the added benefit of substantial improvements in clinical pathology. The broad‐spectrum properties of these tetracycline‐based antibiotics reduce secondary skin bacterial infections and cellulitis complications, thus limiting the occurrence of filarial lymphedema‐related infective wounds [12, 13]. Despite the improvement in overall health outcomes for patients who receive anti‐Wolbachia therapy, there is a need to consider the implications and increased risk of resistance associated with increased exposure following the off‐label use of these available antimicrobial agents, particularly in resource‐limited regions.

Tetracycline‐based antibiotics, such as tetracycline and doxycycline, remain cost‐effective treatments for community‐acquired respiratory and urinary tract infections [14]. In LF‐hyperendemic regions, typically characterized by low socioeconomic status, affordability and accessibility of therapeutics are critical. Given the increasing prevalence of antibiotic‐resistant and multidrug‐resistant bacterial strains within LF‐hyperendemic communities [15, 16], it is crucial to monitor local bacterial resistance patterns to tetracycline‐based anti‐Wolbachia agents. Considering the deceleration in the discovery of new antibiotics in recent decades, it is imperative to safeguard the effectiveness and efficiency of existing antibiotics [17]. The development of resistance to first‐line antibiotic agents poses significant risks to both current healthcare demands and future global health initiatives. Therefore, assessing the resistance profile of tetracycline‐based antibiotics in these filarial‐endemic populations is essential for accurately forecasting the clinical impact of the off‐label use of tetracyclines as macrofilaricidal agents [10]. Therefore, this study aims to assess knowledge and attitudes toward antibiotic usage, and to determine tetracycline resistance patterns within LF‐hyperendemic communities in the Ahanta West District of Ghana. Ultimately, this will help measure the clinical impact and the threat of tetracycline resistance to the effectiveness of the anti‐Wolbachia therapy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

The study employed a cross‐sectional design. This was conducted in four LF‐hyperendemic communities (Butre, Ampatano, Princess Town, and Akatakyi) in the Ahanta West District, Ghana. The Ahanta West District, located in the Western Region of Ghana (5.1949° N, 2.2215° W), has an estimated population of approximately 200,000. This district is a known hyperendemic region and a hotspot for W. bancrofti infection [18] and was a selected site for the doxycycline trial, where patients received intentional doxycycline administration. These factors informed the selection of this study site.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publication, and Ethics (CHRPE), School of Medicine and Dentistry, KNUST, with approval number CHRPE/AP/036/21. Each participant signed an informed consent form prior to participating in the study.

Patient Recruitment

The microbiological sub‐study enrolled 28 participants for molecular investigation of tetracycline resistance patterns. This sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) to achieve > 80% power at α = 0.05 (two‐sided test), assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.55–0.6). The sample size was derived using standard formulas for independent cohort studies among these participants.

Eligible participants included symptomatic LF patients presenting with chronic lymphedema with at least one open wound/ulcer, residing in hyperendemic LF areas, and providing written informed consent. Individuals living outside study sites' LF‐hyperendemic zones, presenting with an acute or wound of non‐LF origin, or deemed mentally incompetent were excluded. Symptomatic lymphedema patients who were currently taking or had been on antibiotics within the last 3 months were excluded. Participants were recruited between July 2021 and August 2021.

Furthermore, recruitment was expanded by 43 additional symptomatic LF patients (with or without wounds, whether receiving medication or not) (total n = 71) to conduct an exploratory KAP survey. This assessed their knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to antibiotic use and resistance, providing essential preliminary insights into behavioral patterns among this population.

Patients presenting with possible infective wounds/ulcers received primary wound cleaning and care from the study team, along with written referrals to the appropriate district hospitals for further management.

Data Collection

A comprehensive questionnaire was administered to recruited participants. The questionnaire was divided into three sections and administered in detail both in English and with further explanations in the local dialects (Twi, Fante, and Ahanta/Nzema). The Demographic and Socioeconomic Characteristics section collected information on age, gender, educational level, and occupation. The Medical History and Pathology section provided details about previous lymphatic filariasis (LF) diagnoses, lymphedema history, medication usage (including antibiotics and participation in MDA), and the severity of lymphedema, including the presence and absence of wounds. Additionally, the lymphedema leg staging of all participants was measured and recorded by trained research assistants using Dreyer's 7‐stage classification [19].

The Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Regarding Antibiotics section comprises questions that assess participants' understanding of antibiotics, including their purpose, identification, the concept of antibiotic resistance, and potential health implications. The KAP survey comprised 13 multiple‐choice questions, designed by the principal investigator and reviewed by public health experts. The knowledge domain (Q1–5) evaluated antibiotic awareness (Yes/No), purpose (bacterial infections*, viral infections, pain/inflammation, don't know), ability to cure all illnesses (Yes/No*/Not sure), resistance familiarity (Yes*/No), and its definition (body resists, bacteria resist*, antibiotics fail all, don't know). Attitudes (Q6–9, 11–13) and practices (notably Q10) probed usage frequency (prescribed only*/when sick/rarely/never), course completion (Yes*/No), sharing (Yes/No*), improper use–resistance link (Yes*/No/Not sure), consultation (always*/sometimes/never), prescription needs (Yes*/No), prophylactic use (Yes/No*/Not sure), and sources (pharmacy*/Over‐the‐counter/market/friend). Asterisks denote correct responses.

Additionally, antibiotic access within the selected communities was assessed. This involved 6 structured questions on facility practices. Q1 identifies sources (facility, over‐the‐counter, none, clinic), Q2 details personnel present in the primary health facilities (community health workers, nurses, medicine assistants, N/A), Q3 maps prescription authority, Q4 inventories stocked antibiotics in the facilities (e.g., amoxicillin, doxycycline, metronidazole), Q5 verifies tetracyclines availability within primary health facilities (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline) and Q6 assesses prescription requirements for dispensing.

This survey data was collected using EpiInfo 3.5.4 (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, USA). Prior to the main study, the questionnaire underwent pilot testing to ensure clarity and reliability, with necessary revisions made based on the pilot results.

Sample Collection

Wound samples were obtained from the 28 selected patients for the molecular investigation. Wound swabs were obtained using the Levine technique (rotating the tip of the swab sticks over a 1 cm area of the wound and skin) [20]. The swab was immediately placed into a sterile tube with a transport medium, and the lid was tightly shut, as described previously [21, 22]. To preserve the samples' integrity, the collected swab samples were immediately transferred into a liquid nitrogen tank and transported to the Kumasi Centre for Collaborative Research in Tropical Medicine laboratory in Kumasi for further laboratory analysis.

Microbiological Investigations

Media Preparation

The media preparation was performed according to the standard instructions provided by the manufacturers. Columbia Nalidixic Acid (CNA) agar supplemented with 5% Sheep, MacConkey agar, and Mueller Hinton agar (MH) (Oxoid CM0337) were used in this study. As a quality control mechanism, negative and positive controls were used to determine the quality of Blood and Mannitol salt agar. S. aureus ATCC 25923 was used as the positive control, while E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as a negative control. Each batch of prepared media was tested for quality by incubating a plate of each medium for 18–24 h at a temperature of 37°C. Antibiotics were tested for accuracy, and their expiry dates were recorded and checked monthly.

Microbial Culturing and Isolation

Bacteria Culturing

To determine the bacterial composition of the swab samples, aliquots of the suspended wound samples were plated on both CNA agar supplemented with 5% Sheep and MacConkey agar. Each of the swab sticks was used to create a swirl on the media, and a 10 µL inoculation loop was used to streak the swirl on the media. The streaked plates were then incubated in a CO2 incubator at 35°C–37°C for 24 h. The plates were read after overnight incubation, and after which, distinct colonies that were morphologically inconsistent in terms of size, shape, color, and edge, showing mucous and hemolysis, were picked and streaked out on a new blood agar plate. The same procedure was performed for colonies from the MacConkey agar after the overnight incubation at 35°C–37°C, for which distinct colonies were sub‐cultured on MacConkey agar to obtain a pure culture. The pure culture isolates from CNA and MacConkey were preserved in cryotubes with Brain Heart Infusion medium and stored at −80°C for later identification.

Gram Staining

Gram staining was conducted on air‐dried, heat‐fixed smears of the pure isolates following the procedure outlined by Koneman et al. [23]. Crystal violet was applied to the smear for 60 s. The slides were then washed with distilled water for 60 s before applying Gram's iodine. After rinsing the slides with water, acetone was used to decolorize them for 10–15 s, followed by another rinse with water. The slides were then counterstained with safranin for 2 min before a final wash with water. Once air‐dried, the slides were examined under a microscope to identify Gram‐positive (GP), purple/blue, and Gram‐negative (GN), pink/red isolates. These Gram stain reactions were documented, and further identification was conducted.

Bacterial Identification

To identify the bacterial isolates in the swab samples, the culture isolates were typed using the VITEK 2 Compact System. The isolates were cultured, and a sterile 1 µL loop was used to transfer enough pure colonies from each isolate into a clear plastic test tube filled with 3 mL of sterile saline (0.45% NaCl, pH 4.5 to 7.0) to obtain a turbidity of 0.50 to 0.63 McFarland. In a second tube containing 3.0 mL of saline, 145 µL of the prepared suspension was transferred for the GP cards, and for the GN cards, 280 µL was transferred. The two labeled tubes containing the organism suspension were placed into the bar‐coded 10‐well cassette cardholders, with their positions noted. The straw of the appropriate ID card (GN or GP), depending on the Gram stain result, was placed into the initially prepared suspension. The cassette information was entered into the workstation computer before loading the cassette into the instrument. The bar‐coded 10‐well cassette cardholders, containing the cards, were placed into the filler box on the left side of the instrument, and the run was initiated. Upon completion, each identification result was read, and with a confidence level of ≥95% probability, the identification results were accepted.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The antibiotic susceptibility test was conducted using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method, in accordance with the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing guidelines [24]. Two colonies from an overnight culture on blood agar were selected with a 1 μL sterile loop and suspended in 3 mL of sterile saline, adjusted to match a 0.5 McFarland standard. A sterile cotton swab was used to collect the inoculum, which was then uniformly spread over the surface of Mueller–Hinton agar (MH agar). To ensure an even bacterial lawn, the entire surface was swabbed, rotated 90 degrees, and swabbed again. Filter paper disks impregnated with specific antibiotic concentrations were placed on the MH agar surface using an Oxoid disk dispenser, distributing six disks equidistantly on a 90 mm diameter plate. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–24 h. Zones of no bacterial growth indicated where the antibiotic concentration was sufficient to inhibit bacterial proliferation. The diameters of these inhibition zones were measured in millimeters. Three antibiotics were tested: Doxycycline (30 μg), Tetracycline (30 μg), and Minocycline (30 μg). The results were interpreted as Sensitive (S) or Resistant (R) based on the breakpoints established by the EUCAST 2024. The EUCAST [24] breakpoints for the various antibiotics are doxycycline: < 23 mm (R), ≥ 23 mm (S); tetracycline: < 23 mm (R), ≥ 23 mm (S), and minocycline: < 23 mm (R), ≥ 23 mm (S). Each tested antibiotic was validated using susceptible S. aureus ATCC 25923 for the Gram‐positive bacteria and E. coli ATCC 25922 for Gram‐negative bacteria.

Molecular Analysis

Extraction of DNA

DNA was extracted from overnight cultures grown on blood agar plates using the boiling method. A 10 μL loopful of bacteria from the overnight culture was transferred into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline and thoroughly mixed by vortexing. The mixture was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and 200 μL of nuclease‐free water was added to resuspend the cell pellet. The solution was vortexed to ensure complete mixing and then briefly centrifuged for 30 s to settle the mixture. The resuspended solution was incubated at 95°C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatant, containing the extracted DNA, was carefully transferred to a well‐labeled 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and stored at −20°C for subsequent molecular analysis.

Optimization of PCR and high‐resolution melting (HRM)

HRM analysis was performed to detect the tet(M) gene using primers listed in Table 1. The reaction mixture (25 μL total volume) consisted of 12.5 μL 2× master mix (Biolab, New England), 1 μL forward primer (10 pmol), 1 μL reverse primer (10 pmol), 0.3 μL SYBR Green I dye, 2 μL DNA template, and 5.5 μL deionized, sterile water. The 2× master mix contained 2.5 mM MgCl2.

DNA amplification was performed using the LineGene 9600 thermal cycler system (Bioer Technology). The cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 60°C for 20 s, and extension at 68°C for 60 s. A melting curve analysis was performed post‐amplification by gradually increasing the temperature from 60°C to 95°C in 0.3°C increments with a 20‐second hold at each step. This allowed for the identification of melting temperature (Tm) variations in the amplified products. Positive and negative controls were included in each assay.

Statistical Analysis

A standardized scoring system was implemented, assigning numerical values to responses, which were subsequently summed and converted into percentage scores. Bloom's cutoff points, with slight modifications, were used to categorize KAP levels as low (< 60%), average/moderate (60%–79%), and high (≥ 80%). For simplification, and responses other than the appropriate/correct response, as well as “Not sure” and “I do not know” responses, were treated as incorrect. Initial data preprocessing and analysis of questionnaire data were performed using EpiInfo 3.5.4 (CDC) to generate frequency distributions, means, and SD. Specifically, the effect of sex and educational level on antibiotic knowledge, attitudes, and practices was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Bacterial population proportions were calculated using descriptive analysis in Microsoft Excel. GraphPad was employed to analyze the distribution and frequency of drug‐resistant and drug‐sensitive strains, as well as bacterial strain clustering across study communities.

Results

Study Population

The study included a total of 71 participants from four communities: Akatakyi (32), Butre (13), Ampatano (20), and Princess Town (6). Most participants were women (82%), and the majority had attained the highest education level of basic school (78.9%). The mean age of the participants was estimated as 50.4 years (SD ± 14.3). Most participants were into farming (43.7%), with a significant proportion, 15.5%, being unemployed (Table 2).

Antibiotic Usage and Resistance Knowledge and Attitude Assessment – Questionnaire‐Based Survey

Antibiotic Knowledge and Practices Assessment

All participants reported having used antibiotics at some point. However, a significant proportion (64.8%) incorrectly believed that antibiotics are primarily for pain and inflammation, while only 23.9% accurately identified their use in treating bacterial infections. Most participants (83.1%) self‐medicated with antibiotics, often obtaining them from pharmacies (60.6%). While more than half (53.5%) sometimes consulted healthcare providers, not all sought medical advice before using antibiotics. Notably, 84.5% did not complete the full course of antibiotics, and 93.0% did not share antibiotics. A large majority (90.1%) used antibiotics without a prescription. Although none of the participants had heard of antibiotic resistance, 84.5% said they did not know what it meant. Over half (52.1%) were uncertain about the link between antibiotic misuse and the development of resistance (Table 3).

Facility Surveillance of Antibiotic Availability Within Selected LF‐Hyperendemic Communities

The facility surveillance of the selected LF‐hyperendemic communities (Butre, Ampatano, Princess Town, and Akatakyi) revealed varying access to healthcare and antibiotics. While Butre and Akatakyi had over‐the‐counter facilities, Princess Town had a clinic staffed by nurses and Community Health Workers. There were no healthcare facilities within the Ampatano community. The community informed us that they access healthcare in neighboring communities or rely on a medicine hawker who occasionally visits the community. Antibiotic availability varied considerably, while Butre, Princess Town, and Akatakyi stocked a range of antibiotics, including amoxicillin, flucloxacillin, doxycycline, and metronidazole. All three also stocked at least one tetracycline‐class antibiotic (tetracycline or doxycycline). A critical finding across all communities where antibiotics were available, even those with healthcare workers or nurses, was that antibiotics were not always dispensed by prescription (Table 7).

Microbiological Investigations

Bacterial Profiling of Filarial Lymphedema Wounds

A total of 68 bacteria were isolated from the study population, comprising 46 (68%) gram‐positive and 22 (32%) gram‐negative isolates. A total of 35 distinct species were identified using the Vitek system, and one unidentified species was determined to be a gram‐positive rod through basic microscopic analysis. These bacteria belong to 20 different genera, with Staphylococcus having the predominant number of species [27/68] (Figure 1). A total of 22 specific gram‐positive species were identified, with Staphylococcus sciuri being the highest, followed by Staphylococcus aureus (Figure 2). A total of 13 distinct gram‐negative species were identified, with Escherichia coli being the most abundant species (Figure 3). Polybacterial isolates were observed in the wounds of patients. According to the tree map analysis, although Akatakyi had an abundance of bacterial species, Princess Town exhibited the greatest bacterial diversity (Figures 4 and 5).

Tetracycline Resistance Profile of Isolated Bacteria

The identified isolates were tested against three tetracycline‐based antibiotics: tetracycline, doxycycline, and minocycline. Susceptibility varied depending on the organism and the specific antibiotic used. Among 68 bacterial isolates, resistance levels were observed as follows: doxycycline [24/68], tetracycline [18/68], and minocycline [12/68] (Figure 7). Notably, nine bacterial isolates exhibited resistance to all three antibiotics (Figure 6). These nine isolates are Staphylococcus sciuri, Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis, Pantoea spp, Enterococcus saccharolyticus, Proteus mirabilis, Bordetella hinzii, Ralstonia mannitolilytica, Acinetobacter Baumanni and Sphingomonas paucimobilis. Most bacterial isolates were resistant to doxycycline, with the majority being gram‐negative. Notably, 12 bacterial isolates were resistant to Minocycline (Figure 7).

Discussion

Antibiotics have revolutionized medicine as they have significantly reduced mortality rates [25], improved patient recovery [26], and enabled complex medical procedures [27, 28]. However, widespread antibiotic use has led to the rise of antibiotic resistance, a critical global health concern [27]. Given the recent increasing multidrug‐resistant strains in Ghanaian LF‐hyperendemic regions [29], this study assessed antibiotic use patterns, availability impact with regard to the tetracycline resistance profile within LF‐endemic communities in southern Ghana to measure the clinical impact of tetracycline resistance on current anti‐Wolbachia therapy post‐doxycycline trials.

With the assessment of the antibiotic knowledge and attitudes among 71 LF patients in southern Ghana, 64.8% of participants mistakenly believed antibiotics primarily treat pain and inflammation rather than bacterial infections, despite prior antibiotic experience. Only 23.9% correctly identified their purpose, which is consistent with the general population's knowledge gaps [30]. This misconception contributes to inappropriate antibiotic use and exacerbates antibiotic resistance, a concept unfamiliar to over 80% of participants. Over half (53.5%) of participants admitted to self‐medicating with antibiotics occasionally, mirroring findings on general population self‐medication practices [31]. A large majority (83.1%) self‐medicated, often obtaining antibiotics without a prescription. This is facilitated by the unrestricted availability of over‐the‐counter antibiotics in Ghana, which contributes to their inappropriate use [32]. Such misuse fuels antibiotic resistance, a broader public health concern. Leftover antibiotics, resulting from non‐compliance or excessive dispensing [31, 33], also contribute to self‐medication. Specifically, 90.1% of respondents used antibiotics without a prescription, and 84.5% did not complete the full course. Although most (93.0%) did not share leftover antibiotics, many reused them. These findings reveal substantial non‐adherence and inappropriate antibiotic use, contributing to an increased risk of antibiotic resistance development. Additional analysis reported consistently very low average scores for each component (Knowledge: 1.8, Attitude: 1.4, Practice: 1.9) and an overall KAP percentage of only 31.2%. Such low KAP scores suggest an association with the heightened risk of inappropriate antibiotic use, including misuse and overuse, which implies a high risk of resistance development and, consequently, poor health outcomes. This low KAP suggests a widespread deficit in the population's understanding, beliefs, and behavior regarding antibiotic use, necessitating targeted educational interventions to improve antibiotic stewardship. Furthermore, this study found no significant link between education level, sex, and age and the KAP scores observed, indicating that other factors, such as cultural beliefs, socioeconomic status, and healthcare access, are likely more influential.

From the microbiological investigation of the study population, the isolation of different bacterial species from this study population suggests a significant wound microbial diversity and burden. Within the 68 identified bacterial isolates, the identification of 35 distinct species across 20 genera underscores the diverse microbial landscape. Among the bacterial population, most gram‐positive bacterial isolates, including Staphylococcus sciuri and Staphylococcus aureus, were particularly prominent, raising potential clinical concerns given the known pathogenicity of S. aureus and the emerging resistance profiles of coagulase‐negative staphylococci, such as S. sciuri. Though Staphylococcus includes many commensal bacteria frequently found on healthy skin, certain species and strains are known to colonize and infect wounds, often leading to complications and delayed healing [34]. S. aureus and S. sciuri have been isolated from chronic wounds, including those associated with LF [16, 22, 35]. Additionally, resistant genes, such as those encoding methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), have been isolated within LF populations [15, 26]. The predominance of Escherichia coli among gram‐negative isolates is clinically significant due to its association with diverse infections and its propensity for acquiring antibiotic resistance genes. As the most frequent uropathogenic and enteropathogenic bacterium [36], its prevalence is unsurprising. While E. coli, along with other gram‐negative bacteria like Klebsiella and Proteus, were once considered opportunistic, they are now recognized as causes of serious infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, such as those with filarial lymphedema [37]. The diverse range of gram‐positive and gram‐negative bacteria identified is concerning, as their colonization of filarial wounds can lead to secondary infections, exacerbating lymphedema.

Regarding the management of such infectious wounds, the rapid rise of antibiotic‐ and multidrug‐resistant bacteria has increased associated morbidity and mortality due to potential treatment failures [17]. While tetracycline‐based anti‐Wolbachia agents have improved outcomes for filarial lymphedema patients by reducing secondary bacterial infections and cellulitis [10, 11, 12, 13], this might have led to increased off‐label use and overuse, driven by perceived patient benefits. This raises concerns about escalating resistance, particularly in resource‐limited regions that lack robust antimicrobial surveillance. The potential benefits of anti‐Wolbachia therapy must be weighed against the increased resistance risk associated with this off‐label use. This study's assessment of tetracycline resistance in the selected LF‐hyperendemic regions revealed concerning levels of tetracycline‐class resistance among the bacterial isolates. The higher resistance to doxycycline (24/68 isolates) is a significant clinical concern, as doxycycline stands as a key orally available and extensively studied anti‐Wolbachia agent [38], as well as its common first‐line use for various infections due to its safety and affordability. Notably, 9 isolates, spanning both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative species, demonstrated resistance to all three tetracyclines, indicating a significant limitation in treatment options. This finding suggests that empirical treatment with tetracyclines may be ineffective and highlights the need for alternative antibiotic therapies guided by further susceptibility testing. Notably, the detection of minocycline resistance in this population (12/68 isolates) is troubling, as minocycline was not available in any local primary healthcare facilities within these LF‐hyperendemic communities, raising concerns about the spread of tetracycline class resistance.

Despite the various resistance mechanisms, tet(M) gene, the most common determinant of ribosomal protection‐mediated tetracycline resistance [39], has been previously reported as the predominant tet gene (79%) in bacterial isolates [14]. The presence of tet(M) gene in many of the resistant isolates in this study provides a potential mechanism for the observed resistance. However, the observed Tm variations (69.27°C and 73.06°C) potentially indicating sequence polymorphisms in tet(M). This variability in the tet(M) gene among the resistant strains may be attributed to mutations or the presence of alternative resistance genes, as seen in other studies where multiple resistant determinants coexisted within bacterial populations [40]. To better understand the clinical impact, future research should look at expanding the sample size through multi‐center cohort studies and incorporate detailed clinical data on the wounds to provide deeper insights into the clinical relevance of these identified and medically significant microbes. Here, we recommend that further studies investigate precisely characterizing the observed tet(M) gene variations via sequencing and screen for additional resistance genes to help establish comprehensive resistance surveillance programs in resource‐limited areas.

The study findings of profound antibiotic knowledge deficits, prevalent non‐prescription practices, and over‐the‐counter dominance among LF patients alongside prevalent tet(M)‐mediated tetracycline resistance identified within wound isolates, necessitate integrated public health strategies. This advocate embedding antibiotic stewardship within the Ghana Health Service's MDA framework through targeted education, particularly to symptomatic patients who abuse antibiotics on resistance risks during filariasis clinics. Additionally, community health workers training on rational dispensing must be enhanced with stricter Over‐the‐counter sales regulations in hyperendemic zones to mitigate non‐prescription dispensing of antibiotics towards curbing rate of resistance development.

Conclusion

There is a concerning gap in antibiotic knowledge and practices among individuals with lymphatic filariasis in southern Ghana. The widespread misconceptions regarding antibiotic use, coupled with high rates of self‐medication and non‐adherence to prescribed regimens, significantly contribute to the risk of antibiotic resistance development and may be a contributing factor to the tetracycline‐resistant strains observed among the study population. The diverse bacterial community identified in LF wounds, including potentially pathogenic species such as S. aureus, S. sciuri, and E. coli, highlights the risk of polymicrobial infections and underscores the need for comprehensive wound management strategies. Furthermore, the alarming levels of tetracycline resistance, particularly to doxycycline, and the detection of the tet(M) gene in many resistant isolates pose a significant threat to the effectiveness of anti‐Wolbachia therapy. Therefore, the findings here highlight the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship programs, robust surveillance practices, and further investigation into resistance mechanisms. These findings emphasize the importance of accurate diagnosis, tailored treatment approaches, and the judicious use of antibiotics to improve patient outcomes and combat the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance in this vulnerable population.
