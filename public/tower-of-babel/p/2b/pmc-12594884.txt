Insight into the performance and mechanism of Iron(II, III)-polyphenol particles on the adsorption of malachite green cationic dye
Authors: Yu Hu, Xin Han, Nan Zhang, Li Guo, Lingfan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe green synthesis of iron nanoparticles (FeNPs) using plant extracts has attracted considerable attention because of their potential to effectively decolorize dye-containing wastewater. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism remains a topic of debate, mainly due to the complex composition of the extracts. In this study, we introduced a novel and well-defined model system by employing a single polyphenol, ellagic acid (EA), as both reducing and capping agent to synthesize iron particles (Fe-EA). This strategic approach not only circumvents the compositional variability of conventional plant extracts but also enables a fundamental molecular-level understanding of the adsorption process. The resulting Fe-EA particles exhibited eminent malachite green (MG) removal performance, achieving high removal efficiency (over 86.4% within 10 min), which was comparable to that of FeNPs derived from complex pomegranate extracts. Comprehensive characterizations (SEM, EDS, XRD, FTIR, XPS) confirmed that the particles consist of a Fe(II, III)-EA complex self-assembled into unique hollow spherical structures. Adsorption isotherm data were best described by the Langmuir model, indicating homogeneous monolayer adsorption and a maximum calculated capacity of 4149.4 mg (g Fe)−1. Thermodynamic parameters revealed that the adsorption process was spontaneous and endothermic. Combined with LC-MS analysis and kinetic studies, the primary removal mechanism was identified as a chemisorption process, governed by the synergy of strong electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding. This work provides molecular-level insights that shift the design of green-synthesized iron particles from empirical testing to rational engineering.Keywords: Green synthesis, Iron, Ellagic acid, Malachite green, Mechanism, AdsorptionSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Over the past twenty years, iron nanoparticles (FeNPs) have garnered significant attention due to their large specific surface area, low cost, and relatively low toxicity1. They have been extensively utilized in the remediation of soil and wastewater polluted by various substances, including heavy metals, nitrates, dyes, chlorinated organic compounds, and antibiotic drugs2. Nevertheless, conventional approaches for synthesizing FeNPs typically involve high energy consumption and the use of hazardous chemicals, such as borohydride, which contradicts the principles of green chemistry and further exacerbates the financial burden3,4. Consequently, it is of utmost urgency to devise an alternative synthetic route that is environmentally friendly and sustainable.

To date, a variety of biological methods have been utilized for the green synthesis of nanoparticles. Bacteria, fungi, algae, or plants have been employed as a straightforward, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective solution in environmental applications5. Among these, plants are perhaps the most appealing due to their ability to reuse biomass, simple preparation process, and high efficiency6. The bioactive extracts present in different plant tissues, such as leaves, stems, roots, flowers, and seeds, serve as the reducing and capping reagents for the synthesis of nanoparticles. These extracts mainly consist of flavonoids, polyphenols, terpenes, alkaloids, and ascorbic acid7. They can chelate with metal ions through -OH and -COOH functional groups. This process has the potential to reduce particle interactions, prevent aggregation, and improve colloidal stability8. Recently, diverse plant extracts such as those from Ocimum sanctum, Myrtus communis, green tea, and orange peel have been successfully applied in the green synthesis of FeNPs and have been used to degrade various dyes9–12.

However, the composition and chemical structure of FeNPs vary considerably depending on the preparation conditions. Under ambient circumstances without any further treatment, when starting from Fe(II) or Fe(III) salts along with plant extracts, the FeNPs that are generally prepared tend to be amorphous13,14. Some researchers have pointed out that the extremely weak signal observed in X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) characterization, which is located around 45° and corresponds to the α-Fe0 crystalline type15,16. However, others hold the view that due to the absence of other diffraction lines, this signal cannot be identified as Fe0 but rather as an Iron (Ⅱ, Ⅲ)-polyphenol complex17,18. Additionally, a core-shell structure of FeNPs with hydrous iron oxides on the surface has also been hypothesized when Fe2+ reacts with leaf extract under ambient conditions19,20. When the synthesis process is conducted in the presence of a base (such as NaOH, NH3·H2O or NaOAc) and at elevated temperatures (above 80 °C), the samples exhibit well-defined phases of iron oxides possessing paramagnetic properties21–23.

Up until now, the mechanism underlying the removal of dyes by FeNPs derived from plant extracts remains unclear due to the complex composition of these extracts. Wang15 and Ahmed24 et al. have proposed that the -C = C- and = C = N- bonds linked to the benzene ring of dyes are cleaved and degraded as a result of the loss of electrons from Fe(0), which is reduced and stabilized by tea polyphenols and Ricinus Communis seed extracts, respectively. However, Wang25 and Markova17 have failed to detect any Fe(0) through Mössbauer and X-ray absorption spectroscopy.

In a previous study26, we found that Fe3+ is partially reduced by pomegranate (PG) extracts and coordinates with polyphenols in the form of amorphous Iron(Ⅱ, Ⅲ)-polyphenol complex. Electrostatic adsorption was speculated to be the major mechanism in the adsorption of malachite green (MG), but without definite proof. Hou27 has compared tannic acid, epigallocatechin gallate, and pyrogallic acid, each as a single type of polyphenol to modify zero-valent iron, and found that the number of ortho-phenolic hydroxyl groups is crucial for the efficiency of hexavalent chromium removal. Therefore, the composition of the polyphenols in plant extracts is the key to the activity and for elucidating the removal mechanism of dyes28.

Unlike most previous studies that relied on complex and variable plant extracts, this study employs a well-defined molecular compound by utilizing ellagic acid (EA), a principal and specific polyphenol found in pomegranate extracts29,30. This approach offers a significant advantage by simplifying the complex system into a model reaction, thereby providing clarity in elucidating the formation mechanism of the iron-polyphenol complex and its subsequent interaction with dyes. The Fe-EA particles were first synthesized from Fe2(SO4)3 and EA, thoroughly characterized, and evaluated for MG removal from aqueous solution. The adsorption mechanism was further elucidated by analyzing the residual products via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). This work not only demonstrates the efficacy of a single polyphenol system, but more importantly, establishes a fundamental molecular-level understanding of the chemisorption process. It advances the field from empirical testing toward rational design, enabling the tailored development of green-synthesized iron-based adsorbents. These insights are essential for designing high-performance and reproducible adsorbents for environmental remediation.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Fe2(SO4)3·xH2O (AR) and MG (BS) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd (Shanghai, P.R. China). Methanol (HPLC) and EA (98%) were provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. (P.R. China) and used directly without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was used in all experiments.

Synthesis of FePs

The synthesis of FePs using EA was performed according to our previous report with the same concentration of Fe31. Briefly, 1.5 g Fe L− 1 of a Fe2(SO4)3 aqueous solution was mixed with EA at molar ratios of 1:10, 1:5, and 1:1 under ambient conditions and stirred for 1 h. After that, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min and washed three times with methanol. The as-obtained FePs were then redispersed in DI water at a concentration of 50 mg Fe L− 1 as Fe-EA and stored at 4 °C for further use.

Characterization

FESEM (Gemini SEM500, Carl Zeiss, Germany), equipped with EDS (AZtec X-Max Extreme) for the measurement of elemental composition, was used to examine the microstructure and surface elements of FePs. XRD patterns of FePs were recorded by an X-ray powder diffractometer (D/MAX 2550 VB/PC, Rigaku corporation, Cu Kα). The source consists of Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). Each sample was scanned in the 2θ range of 10-80o. With a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1, FTIR (Nicolet 6700, Thermo electron Corp) with a resolution of 4 cm− 1 in transmittance mode confirmed the functional groups on the surface of FePs. The surface composition and valence state of the FePs were measured by XPS (Escalab250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific Corp.), which was fitted assuming Gaussian-Lorentzian distribution for each peak and all binding energies were referenced and normalized to the neutral carbon peak at 285.0 eV for calibration.

Batch experiments

Batch experiments were carried out by adding 10 mL of MG (200 mg L− 1) to 10 mL aqueous solution of Fe-EA (50 mg Fe L− 1). The mixed solution was stirred on a rotary shaker (300 rpm) for the desired time intervals at constant temperatures (T = 273, 293, 313 K). Sample aliquots were then drawn and filtered through 0.45 μm membranes to determine the residual concentration of MG. The absorbance was measured on a UV-vis spectrophotometer (DR6000, Germany) at the wavelength of MG maximum absorption (λ = 617 nm).

LC-MS analysis

The degradation products in the solution were identified using LC-MS. An Agilent 1290 UPLC system was coupled to a 6530 Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), equipped with a binary pump, a thermostatted autosampler, and an electrospray interface. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent Eclipse Plus C18 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 ℃. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% aqueous formic acid and (B) acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.2 mL min− 1. The gradient program was as follows: 0–8 min, 5% to 95% B; 8–10 min, 95% B, with post time of 5 min. The injection volume was 1 µL and all samples in the autosampler were kept at 4 ℃ during the whole run.

Results and discussion

Comparison of the efficiency of Fe-PG and Fe-EA

As shown in Fig. 1, the adsorption efficiency of MG increased sharply from 1.6% to 60.3% within 1 min as the EA/Fe molar ratio was raised from 1:1 to 10:1, a trend consistent with the Fe-PG system32. At the highest ratio of 10:1 and under identical reaction conditions, Fe-EA exhibited a removal efficiency comparable to the Fe-PG system31, reaching 86.4% and 92.8% within 10 min, respectively. In contrast, other polyphenols such as catechol, tiron, gallic acid or luteolin showed almost no reactivity. This significant enhancement in performance with increasing EA ratio can be attributed to the increased availability of polyphenolic groups, which not only facilitate the reduction of Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, but also provide more coordination sites for the formation of a stable Fe-EA complex. The superior performance of EA, compared to other polyphenols, highlights the critical role of its specific molecular structure, likely its four hydroxyl groups in a fused aromatic system, in forming the active adsorbent. These results confirm that EA is not only a feasible but a functionally representative model compound for elucidating the MG removal mechanism by PG extract-based FeNPs.

Characterization

SEM

SEM analysis was performed to track the morphological evolution of the adsorbent throughout the synthesis and adsorption process, which provided direct visual evidence for the proposed mechanism. As shown in Fig. 2a, EA particles were seed-shaped with a uniform size distribution of approximately 2 μm. Remarkably, upon the introduction of the Fe2(SO4)3 solution, the EA molecules instantly self-assembled into hollow spherical structures (Fe-EA), accompanied by a distinct color change from light yellow to blue-grey (Fig. 2b, and insets). This immediate transformation and color shift confirm the rapid coordination between Fe³⁺ and EA33, leading to the nucleation and growth of a coordinated polymer network. Most importantly, after the adsorption of MG, these hollow spheres underwent a conspicuous structural collapse, resulting in a rougher and more aggregated morphology34 (Fig. 2c). This dramatic morphological change strongly suggests that the adsorption of MG molecules disrupts the intermolecular forces (e.g., hydrogen bonds) that stabilize the hollow architecture, providing key structural insight into the adsorption mechanism.

SEM images of (a) EA, Fe-EA before (b) and after (c) reaction with MG.

EDS

The elemental compositions of EA and Fe-EA before and after they reacted with MG were determined by EDS to further confirm the roles of EA as shown in Fig. 3. The spectra and corresponding elemental contents clearly show the transition from a system dominated by C and O in pure EA (Fig. 3a and insert) to one containing 5.68% Fe in the synthesized Fe-EA particles (Fig. 3b), confirming the incorporation of iron into the complex. After reaction with MG (Fig. 3c), the atomic percentage of Fe decreased to 4.13%, while the C and O contents increased. This change in elemental composition is a direct consequence of the surface deposition of carbon- and oxygen-rich MG molecules, which effectively masks the underlying iron signal and provides tangible evidence for the surface adsorption event. The data quantitatively support the premise that MG is adsorbed onto the surface of the Fe-EA particles.

EDS spectra of (a) EA, Fe-EA before (b) and after (c) reaction with MG.

XRD

XRD was employed to determine the crystallographic structure and phase composition of the synthesized particles, which is crucial for identifying the true active species. By comparing the XRD patterns of EA and Fe-EA (Fig. 4), the additional distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 12° and 17° can be attributed to the formation of an Fe-EA complex induced by the coordination of Fe3+ with -OH and -COO- functional groups in EA molecules18, which is analogous to the FeO(OH) crystalline phase (PDF#34-1266). Meanwhile, no diffraction peak at 44.9° of Fe(0) was detected in accordance with Fe-PG from our former analysis32. These findings conclusively demonstrate that the active material is a molecular coordination polymer, not metallic iron, thereby redirecting the mechanistic focus towards adsorption and surface complexation, rather than reductive degradation. The amorphous nature of the complex is consistent with the variable coordination geometry of EA with iron ions.

XRD patterns of EA and Fe-EA.

FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was used to probe the changes in functional groups and surface chemistry during particle formation and dye adsorption, offering molecular-level insights into the binding mechanism. Figure 5a represents the FTIR spectrum of EA showing bands at 3560 cm− 1, 3480 cm− 1, 3160 cm− 1, 1730 cm− 1, 1630 cm− 1 and 1040 cm− 1, which correspond to the stretching vibration peaks of O-H (free O-H group), O-H (intramolecular hydrogen bonded OH group)35, O-H (intermolecular hydrogen bonded OH group)36, C = O, C = C, and C-O37, respectively. After the addition of Fe3+, the hydrogen bonded OH stretching vibration band of Fe-EA shifted from 3160 cm− 1 to 3110 cm− 1 (Fig. 5b), indicating the involvement of hydroxyl groups in hydrogen bonding and coordination with Fe ions38, which facilitated the self-assembly into hollow spheres as shown in Fig. 2b. In addition, most FTIR bands of Fe-EA were slightly moved when compared with EA, representing the coating of the synthesized FePs with EA10. The appearance of a new peak at 579 cm− 1, assigned to Fe-O vibration, further confirmed the metal-phenolate coordination23. After MG adsorption, the main functional groups such as C = O, C = C, C-O and Fe-O remained virtually unchanged, with no emergence of new peaks (Fig. 5c). This spectral stability rules out the formation of covalent bonds or degradation of MG. The absence of new covalent peaks, coupled with the cationic nature of MG and the anionic surface of Fe-EA, strongly suggests that the adsorption is governed by strong, specific electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding39,40.

FTIR spectra: (a) EA, Fe-EA before (b) and after (c) reaction with MG, (d) MG.

XPS

To determine the valence states of Fe presented in Fe-EA, XPS was used for inspection, and the correlation spectra were displayed in Fig. 6. With a corresponding satellite peak at around 716.8 eV, binding energy peaks appearing at ca. 710.9 eV and 713.4 eV represent the mixture of Fe(II) and Fe(III)20,41. The coexistence of both Fe(II) and Fe(III) states, and the complete absence of a peak at 707.0 eV for Fe(0)27, unequivocally demonstrate that EA acts as a reducing agent, partially reducing Fe(III) to Fe(II), but does not produce zero-valent iron. This result, consistent with XRD data, confirms that the particles are a mixed-valence Fe(II, III)-polyphenol complex. The coexistence of the Fe(II)/Fe(III) after MG adsorption (Fig. 6b) further supports that the removal process is primarily adsorption-based, not involving redox reactions with the iron center. This is a key mechanistic distinction from some previously reported plant-extract systems where reductive degradation was postulated15,24.

XPS spectra of Fe 2p of Fe-EA: before (a) and after (b) reaction with MG.

Identification of metabolites using LC-MS

Products were identified by LC-MS to further study the removal mechanism of MG by Fe-EA. Figure 7 compares the LC spectra of EA, initial MG, MG reacting with Fe-EA and reacted Fe-EA extracted using methanol. The peaks for EA and MG were observed at Rt = 2.81 min and Rt = 4.50 min, respectively (Fig. 7a and b), corresponding to [M-H]+ (M = EA/MG), which were confirmed by the m/z ratios of 30342 and 32943 (Table 1). However, the MG peak was significantly weakened after reacting with Fe-EA. A small amount of residual MG was found with no new peaks appearing, affirming that MG removal by Fe-EA was predominantly via an adsorption process (Fig. 7c)19,44, as no soluble degradation intermediates or products were detected in the solution. To identify the variation of adsorbed metabolites on the Fe-EA surface, the reacted MG solution was centrifuged and the separated Fe-EA was extracted with methanol (Fig. 7d). Obviously, the peaks at Rt = 2.81 min and Rt = 4.50 min for EA and MG reappeared without other new metabolites, indicating that the adsorbed MG could be desorbed by a strong organic solvent. This reversibility, coupled with the absence of new covalent bonds (as evidenced by FTIR), suggests that the adsorption is primarily driven by strong electrostatic interactions rather than irreversible covalent bonding. Besides, EA was partially desorbed from the surface of Fe-EA after the extraction with methanol, which indirectly elucidates that EA had been successfully coordinated with Fe3+/Fe2+ as a capping/reducing agent during the preparation process. The LC-MS test results collectively provide strong evidence against a degradation mechanism and firmly support an adsorption-dominated process.

Kinetic studies

The pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models were employed to investigate the adsorption mechanism of Fe-EA toward MG, as illustrated in Fig. 8; Table 2. The pseudo-second-order model demonstrated much better correlation (R22 = 0.8423) compared with the pseudo-first-order model (R12 = 0.5484), suggesting that chemisorption predominated in the adsorption process10,45. The superior fit of the pseudo-second-order model indicates that the rate-limiting step of the adsorption process is likely an electron sharing or exchange process between the negatively charged adsorbent (Fe-EA) and the cationic adsorbate (MG), which is consistent with the proposed mechanism involving strong electrostatic interaction46.

Adsorption isotherm studies

The equilibrium data were analyzed using the Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherm models. The experimental isotherm data and the corresponding model fits are presented in Fig. 9, and the fitted parameters are summarized in Table 3. The Langmuir model provided a significantly better fit (R² = 0.9858) than the Freundlich model (R² = 0.617), suggesting that MG adsorption onto Fe-EA primarily occurs as a monolayer on a homogeneous surface47. That is consistent with the proposed chemisorption mechanism involving specific and uniform active sites on the Fe-EA complex. The calculated maximum adsorption capacity (qm) from the Langmuir model was 4149.4 mg (g Fe)−1, indicating a remarkably high affinity of Fe-EA for MG cations. The essential characteristic of the Langmuir isotherm can also be expressed by a dimensionless separation factor RL, defined as RL=1/(1 + KLC0)48. The RL values in this study ranged between 0 and 1, confirming that the adsorption process was favorable under the studied conditions.

The possible mechanism for the MG removal

Based on the collective evidence from all characterizations and analyses, a comprehensive mechanism for MG adsorption by Fe-EA is proposed (Fig. 11): Firstly, Fe3+ ions are partially reduced to Fe2+ by EA and coordinate with its phenolic groups to form a mixed-valence Fe(Ⅱ, Ⅲ)-EA complex17,26,50. Subsequently, this complex self-assembles via coordination bonds and intermolecular hydrogen bonding into metastable hollow spherical structures18, as directly observed by SEM. Finally, due to the negatively charged surface of Fe-EA, the addition of cationic MG molecules destroys partial hydrogen bonds and causes the cage structure collapse of the Fe-EA via chemisorption39,40. The adsorption isotherm and thermodynamic studies further substantiate this mechanism, revealing a spontaneous, endothermic, and monolayer-dominant adsorption process47,49,51. Critically, the absence of Fe(0) (XRD and XPS), lack of dye degradation products (LC-MS), and the applicability of a chemisorption kinetic model collectively rule out reductive degradation and confirm chemisorption as the primary mechanism.

Conclusions

To overcome the compositional complexity of PG extracts, a well-defined model system was established using EA to synthesize iron particles for elucidating the MG removal mechanism. Multiple characterization techniques provided consistent evidence: SEM revealed the self-assembly of Fe-EA into hollow spheres and their collapse after MG adsorption. EDS confirmed iron incorporation and subsequent MG deposition. XRD and XPS verified the formation of a mixed-valence Fe(II, III)-EA complex with no zero-valent iron. FTIR demonstrated the involvement of hydroxyl groups in coordination and the absence of new covalent bonds after adsorption. Most importantly, LC-MS analysis detected no degradation byproducts, unequivocally establishing adsorption as the primary removal pathway. Kinetic studies supported a chemisorption process, while adsorption isotherm confirmed monolayer adsorption with a high capacity (4149.4 mg (g Fe)−1). Thermodynamic analysis revealed the adsorption to be spontaneous and endothermic.

Building on the mechanistic insights from this well-defined model system, future work should focus on translating this molecular-level understanding into practical applications. A primary challenge is the stability and reusability of the Fe-polyphenol nanoparticles. Although their hollow spherical structure confers high initial activity, it collapses after MG adsorption. To address this, strategies such as cross-linking or immobilization on solid supports are necessary to enhance their robustness and enable regeneration. Furthermore, the economic feasibility of scaling up synthesis must be evaluated. A promising path is to replace EA with low-cost, polyphenol-rich agricultural wastes (e.g., fruit peels), guided by the established structure-activity relationship. Finally, expanding the application scope beyond MG to complex multi-pollutant systems and real wastewater matrices, where competing ions and organic matter are present, will be essential to develop tunable Fe(II, III)-polyphenol adsorbents for targeted and practical environmental remediation.
