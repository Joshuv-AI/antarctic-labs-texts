Introduction

Diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis (DPL) is an extremely rare lymphatic disorder characterized by diffuse proliferation of abnormal lymphatic vessels involving the lungs, pleura, and mediastinum [1]. The disease is generally considered congenital and is most commonly present in children and young adults; however, sporadic cases in older patients have been reported [2–4]. Because of its rarity and nonspecific clinical manifestations, DPL remains a significant diagnostic challenge.

Radiologically, DPL typically manifests as smooth interlobular septal thickening, peribronchovascular thickening, mediastinal soft-tissue infiltration, lymphadenopathy, pleural thickening, and pleural effusion [3, 5]. These findings may overlap with a broad spectrum of diseases, including malignancy, particularly lymphangitic carcinomatosis. Although consolidation may occasionally be observed and mimic lung cancer, this presentation is relatively uncommon [3]. Most reported cases of DPL demonstrate a progressive clinical and radiologic course, often requiring medical or surgical intervention, and rapid radiologic improvement without DPL-directed therapy has rarely been described [3, 6].

Here, we report a pathologically confirmed case of DPL in an elderly patient that initially mimicked advanced lung cancer on imaging but showed radiologic improvement on follow-up without DPL-directed therapy, highlighting an unusual clinical course and a potential diagnostic pitfall.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department with dyspnea and high fever up to 40 °C that had started one week earlier. He also complained of cough and right-sided chest discomfort. His medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia, all well controlled with medication. He was an ex-smoker with a 50 pack-year history. Laboratory findings revealed a hemoglobin level of 15.5 g/dL, a white blood cell count of 6,200/µL, and a platelet count of 165,000/µL. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 178 mg/L and a procalcitonin level of 2.2 ng/mL. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a pH of 7.44, PaO2 of 84.6 mmHg, PaCO2 of 28.6 mmHg, and HCO3− of 19.3 mmol/L, suggesting respiratory alkalosis with metabolic compensation. Urinalysis and blood chemistry results were unremarkable. Blood and urine cultures were negative, and a PCR panel for 23 respiratory viruses yielded negative results. Echocardiography showed normal left ventricular systolic function, mild diastolic dysfunction, and mild mitral regurgitation, while the N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level was elevated at 2,228 pg/mL.

Anteroposterior chest radiograph demonstrated bilateral perihilar lymph node enlargement with increased interstitial markings. Contrast-enhanced chest CT revealed an enhancing nodular lesion measuring up to 1.7 cm in greatest dimension, mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy up to 3.5 cm in greatest dimension, right-sided pleural effusion, and pleural thickening. On lung window images, diffuse smooth interlobular septal thickening involving both lungs was observed (Fig. 1). No prior radiologic records were available for comparison. Given the radiologic suspicion of malignancy, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration of the mediastinal lymph nodes was performed one day after the initial CT; the specimens were nondiagnostic, showing only a few atypical cells. Percutaneous pleural biopsy was performed two days after the initial CT and was also unremarkable. During hospitalization, empirical intravenous antibiotics were administered from the day of admission. The patient also received oral prednisolone at 40 mg/day with tapering over 16 days for a cutaneous drug eruption. Intravenous diuretics were administered because pulmonary edema was considered a possible contributor to the interlobular septal thickening observed on chest CT. Oral antitussives, expectorants, and acetaminophen were also administered for symptomatic relief. Daily follow-up chest radiographs showed no significant change, and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery with wedge resection of the right upper and lower lobes was performed for definitive diagnosis 10 days after the initial CT.

Histopathologic examination of the surgical lung biopsy specimens demonstrated diffuse proliferation of anastomosing lymphatic channels expanding the pulmonary interstitium. The lymphatic vessels were thin-walled and lined by a single layer of endothelial cells, without significant cytologic atypia or nuclear pleomorphism. Furthermore, mitotic figures were extremely rare. The presence of proliferative, anastomosing, and budding lymphatic networks rather than simple passive ectasia of pre-existing, regularly distributed lymphatics helped distinguish DPL from lymphangiectasia or secondary/reactive lymphatic dilatation. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for D2-40 and CD31, supporting the diagnosis of diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis (Fig. 2).

Three days after surgery, 18F-FDG PET/CT was performed to evaluate the possibility of concurrent malignancy. It showed marked improvement of the septal thickening with only mild hilar lymph node enlargement showing low FDG uptake, suggesting a benign process rather than concurrent malignancy. Without DPL-directed therapy, the patient’s symptoms resolved completely, and he was discharged after 3 weeks. Follow-up chest CT performed two months later demonstrated marked regression of mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, as well as complete resolution of interlobular septal thickening and pleural effusion (Fig. 3). The patient was followed for one year after discharge, with no clinical symptoms or chest CT evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

We report a pathologically confirmed case of diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis (DPL) in a 79-year-old male that initially mimicked advanced lung cancer with lymphangitic carcinomatosis on chest CT. Notably, the patient experienced rapid clinical and radiologic improvement without DPL-directed therapy, and no recurrence was observed during 1 year of follow-up.

Compared with previously reported cases, the present case is unusual in several respects. First, DPL usually presents in children or young adults, whereas our patient was elderly. Second, although DPL is known to mimic malignant or inflammatory thoracic diseases, the combination of an enhancing pulmonary nodule, extensive lymphadenopathy, pleural involvement, and diffuse septal thickening led to an initial impression of advanced lung cancer. Third, most reported cases show a progressive course or require treatment such as sirolimus, surgical intervention, or supportive management for chylous effusion, whereas this case demonstrated rapid radiologic improvement without DPL-directed therapy [3, 5, 6].

DPL is a rare lymphatic disorder characterized by diffuse proliferation of lymphatic channels involving the lungs, pleura, and mediastinum [1]. Because of its rarity, most published data are limited to sporadic case reports or small case series, and its natural history remains poorly defined. The disease typically manifests during childhood or early adulthood, without a clear gender predilection, although adult-onset cases have occasionally been reported [2, 4]. Clinical presentation varies widely, ranging from asymptomatic disease to nonspecific respiratory symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis, or fever [7]. Diagnosis is usually confirmed by lung biopsy, and no standardized treatment has been established [6].

Radiologic findings of DPL are variable and include mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, pleural thickening, peribronchovascular thickening, interlobular septal thickening, ground-glass opacity, and consolidation [5, 8]. These findings overlap substantially with those of lymphangitic carcinomatosis, pulmonary sarcoidosis, lymphoma, and other interstitial lung diseases, often leading to misdiagnosis [9]. In particular, the combination of pulmonary parenchymal lesions, extensive lymphadenopathy, pleural involvement, and diffuse septal thickening may closely mimic advanced lung cancer with lymphangitic spread.

Despite these similarities, several imaging features may help differentiate DPL from lymphangitic carcinomatosis. DPL typically demonstrates smooth interlobular septal and pleural thickening, whereas lymphangitic carcinomatosis more often shows irregular or nodular septal thickening. In addition, lymph node enlargement in DPL tends to show relatively homogeneous enhancement without necrosis, while malignant lymphadenopathy frequently demonstrates heterogeneous enhancement or necrotic change [5, 8–10]. In the present case, retrospective review of the imaging findings revealed predominantly smooth septal and pleural thickening and homogeneously enhancing lymphadenopathy, features that were more suggestive of a lymphatic disorder than malignancy.

Most reported cases of DPL describe a progressive clinical and radiologic course, often requiring medical therapy such as sirolimus or surgical intervention [3, 11]. In this context, the present case is unusual in that marked radiologic improvement occurred without DPL-directed therapy. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The pulmonary nodule was surgically removed, and the patient received empirical antibiotics, corticosteroids, diuretics, and supportive symptomatic treatment. Therefore, a superimposed reversible inflammatory or reactive process cannot be completely excluded and may have contributed, at least in part, to the rapid improvement. Nevertheless, the initial disease distribution and histopathologic confirmation support DPL as the underlying diagnosis.

Another distinctive feature of this case is the advanced age at presentation. To our knowledge, DPL presenting in patients aged 70 years or older has rarely been documented. Although DPL is considered a congenital disorder, slow progression of lymphatic proliferation may allow delayed clinical manifestation in later life [7, 12]. This case highlights the importance of considering diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis in the differential diagnosis of lymphangitic carcinomatosis–like imaging findings, even in elderly patients. Recognition of characteristic imaging features, together with histopathologic confirmation, is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary aggressive treatment. Furthermore, this case expands the currently recognized clinical spectrum of DPL by demonstrating an unusual course of rapid radiologic improvement without DPL-directed therapy.

In conclusion, diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis can closely mimic advanced lung cancer with lymphangitic metastasis on imaging while exhibiting a potentially reversible clinical course. These findings highlight the importance of considering DPL in the differential diagnosis of malignancy-like imaging features, particularly in atypical settings such as elderly patients, to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary aggressive treatment. This case further expands the clinical spectrum of DPL and provides insight into its heterogeneous and potentially indolent natural history.
