Construction and metabolic efficacy evaluation of sleeve gastrectomy and modified Roux-en-Y gastric bypass in a mouse model with obesity and MASLD
Authors: Zheng Wang, Zhehong Li, Chenxu Tian, Chengyuan Yu, Xiyuan Chen, Yilan Sun, Weijian Chen, Dezhong Wang, Liang Wang, Dongbo Lian, Nengwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionObesity and related metabolic diseases are major global health challenges. Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) is an effective treatment. Yet exploring its molecular mechanisms remains limite due to the challenge of obtaining postoperative tissue samples. While rat models are more convenient in size and operation, mouse models offer unique advantages such as lower breeding costs and easier genetic modification. However, research on mouse MBS models is still limited because of their small size and surgical complexity, highlighting the need for optimized techniques to advance the field.ObjectiveThis study aims to establish a high-fat diet-induced (HFD) obesity combined with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) mouse model, and to evaluate the MBS models assisted by microsurgery, so as to provide a reliable tool for mechanism research.MethodsMale SPF C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to the normal diet (ND) group and the HFD group. The mouse in the HFD group were induced to develop obesity with MASLD through a high-fat diet for 16 weeks. The HFD group was further divided into sham operation group (Sham), sleeve gastrectomy (SG) group, and modified Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) group (n = 6). Metabolic efficacy was evaluated by weight, metabolic parameters, and pathological staining analysis at the 4th week post-surgery.ResultsCompared with the ND group, the weight of the HFD group increased by 38.25% (P < 0.001), and the liver tissue showed pathological features of MASLD. Compared with the Sham group, the weight of the SG group and the modified RYGB group decreased significantly at 4 weeks after operation (P < 0.05). Compared with the Sham group, the insulin sensitivity of the RYGB group was improved. Hepatic steatosis was significantly reduced in SG and RYGB groups, and serum TG and LDL levels were significantly improved. The postoperative survival rate of SG and Sham mice was 100%, and RYGB was 83.3% (1 case died of anastomotic stenosis).ConclusionThe establishment of a modified RYGB and SG mouse model has good reproducibility, safety, and efficacy. This study provides a useful tool for exploring the mechanism of MBS in the treatment of obesity with MASLD.

Introduction

Obesity has become a worldwide public health problem [1]. Obesity is often accompanied by a variety of comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2, 3], non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hypertension [4], obstructive sleep apnea syndrome [5], cancer [6], and other diseases, affecting the quality of life and even life expectancy [7, 8]. Lifestyle interventions such as diet control, increased exercise, precision nutrition [9–12] and drug therapy [13, 14] are commonly used in the treatment of obesity. Studies by Perdomo et al. have shown that weight loss of 10% can effectively improve the complications caused by obesity and improve the quality of life [13]. In recent years, with the development of metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS), laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) and laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) have been widely used, and the two kinds of bariatric surgery have accounted for more than 80%, and the clinical treatment effect is remarkable [15, 16]. Related studies have shown that bariatric surgery can reduce weight and alleviate metabolic syndrome through a variety of mechanisms, including bile acid pathway, gastrointestinal hormone changes, etc. [17], but more and more people have proposed new ideas, such as fat, exosomes, intestinal flora, etc [18]. However, the research on the mechanism of MBS mainly relies on preoperative biological samples, postoperative non-invasive specimens, and analysis based on predictive models at present. The acquisition of postoperative tissue samples is restricted by factors such as the complex recovery period of patients and the limitation of invasive procedures, which greatly hinders the in-depth exploration of the mechanism of MBS. Therefore, the construction of stable and reliable MBS-related animal models is of great significance for revealing the molecular mechanism of the specific effects of surgery, exploring the key targets for metabolic improvement, and promoting the research and development of non-surgical bariatric therapies.

At present, most of the research on the mechanism of MBS relies on the rat experimental model [19–21]. Although the rat model is relatively convenient in size and operation, the mouse model is gradually attracting attention due to its unique advantages of low breeding costs and ease of genetic manipulation [22]. However, compared with the rat model, the mouse model is still scarce due to its small size, high requirements, and complex surgical procedures. The successful establishment of mouse models can provide a useful tool for further research on the mechanism of MBS in the treatment of obesity. In this study, we successfully established RYGB and SG animal models of high-fat diet-induced (HFD) obesity mice by using microscopic instruments with the assistance of an operating microscope, so as to provide a safe and effective animal model for studying the mechanism related to obesity combined with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiment began with a one-week acclimation period for 30 male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks. They were then randomly divided into a normal diet (ND) group and a high-fat diet (HFD) group. The HFD group was fed the rodent diet with 60 kcal% fat for 16 consecutive weeks to induce an obesity model combined with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). After successful model establishment, the 24 HFD mice were randomly assigned to four groups: the sham operation group (Sham), the sleeve gastrectomy group (SG), the modified Roux-en-Y gastric bypass group (RYGB), and the high-fat diet control group (CK). All mice underwent a 12-hour fast and water-fast prior to surgery, followed by 7 days of standardized postoperative management. Approximately 4 weeks after the intervention (around week 20 of the experiment), an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and an intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT) were performed on all mice to assess metabolic function. Finally, blood and liver tissues were collected for serological and histological analyses.

Breeding of experimental animals

Thirty SPF grade C57BL/6J male mice aged 6–8 weeks were used in this experiment, which was purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (SCXK (Beijing) 2021-0011). The mice were bred at the Animal Experimental Center of Beijing Shijitan Hospital affiliated with Capital Medical University. After 7 days of adaptive feeding in standard barrier conditions (Temperature 23 ± 1 ℃, Humidity 50 ± 10%, 12 h circadian rhythm), the experimental procedures were started. All animals were housed in an environment with a constant room temperature (22–24℃) and humidity (40%–60%), under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with five mice per cage and free access to food and water. This experiment was approved by the Ethical Committee of Laboratory Animal Management of Beijing Shijitan Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University (Ethics approval code: KYD-2024-0025-001) and conformed to the 3R principle of experimental animals. All animal experiments followed the normative requirements of the “Guiding Opinions on the Good Treatment of Laboratory Animals”.

Establishment of obesity combined with MASLD mouse model

After adapting to the environment and observing the health status, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the ND group (n = 6); and the HFD group (n = 24). The ND group was fed a standard diet, while the HFD group was fed a high-fat diet with 60 kcal% fat (Purchased from BEIJING KEAO XIELI FEED CO., LTD.) to induce obesity combined with MASLD model over 16-week period.

Criteria for the mouse model of obesity: the weight of the HFD group was 20% higher than that of the ND group [23].

MASLD model success criteria: after liver tissue HE staining, the area of hepatic steatosis lobules accounted for more than 1/3 under the light microscope, and the MASLD model was judged to be successful [24].

Establishment of MBS mouse model

After 16 weeks of high-fat diet induction, the obesity combined with the MASLD model was successfully constructed. Then according to the random number method, 24 obesity combined with MASLD model mice were divided into 4 different experimental groups, which were: HFD control check group (CK, n = 6), HFD-SG group (SG, n = 6), HFD-RYGB group (RYGB, n = 6), HFD-Sham operation group (Sham, n = 6). Parts of the surgical procedures were performed with reference to methods described in the literature [25, 26]. The construction of this MBS represents a phased achievement based on our continuous groundwork, and we will continue to refine our approaches going forward.

SG mouse model

Based on the existing research methods and optimized, the SG mouse model was established by the following procedures in this study: (1) Anesthesia induction and disinfection of the surgical area: Deep anesthesia (corneal reflex disappeared) was achieved by intraperitoneal injection of a ready-use anesthetic (Tribromoethanol, 0.02 mL/g, Cat. No. JT0781, Beijing Jitian Biotechnology Co., LTD.). The surgical field was sterilized with iodopor and alcohol after skin preparation, and a sterile sheet was placed. (2) The surgical approach: A longitudinal incision of approximately 3 cm was made along the midline of the abdomen to access the abdominal cavity, and a retractor was employed to maintain exposure of the abdominal cavity. (Fig. 1A); (3) Vascular ligament treatment and sleeve gastrectomy: the sterile cotton swab infiltrated with prewarm normal saline (37 ℃) was gently placed outside the gastric tissue, and a warm saline gauze pad was placed around to maintain organ humidity. Using small electric coagulation pen (Model: German small white; Purchased from Zhengzhou Kangbelai Medical Technology Co., LTD.) to accurately cut off the small blood vessels and ligaments around the stomach to achieve full liberation of the gastric body (Fig. 1B). (4) Primary suture and reinforcement suture: The gastric wall was clamped with a sterile clamp, and more than 60% of the gastric tissue on the greater curvature and fundus of the stomach outside the sterile clamp was resected. Cotton swabs were used to remove the residual gastric contents, and the gastric incision was disinfected with iodophor. Using 9 − 0 nylon thread (Product name: Lingqiao belt suture needle; Manufacturer: purchased from Ningbo Medical Suture Needle Co., Ltd.) for continuous suture to establish a gastric tubular structure, remove the temporary sterile clip, and use 9 − 0 nylon thread for seromuscular layer embedding suture to strengthen the anastomosis (Fig. 1C-D). (5) Abdomen closure: After anatomical reduction, the abdominal cavity was closed by intermittent suture with 6 − 0 nylon suture and closed with 3 − 0 silk suture (Manufacturer: Johnson (China) Medical Device Co., Ltd.; Model: W2511) for skin incision by interrupted suture, abdominal incision disinfection, the end of the operation. Note: A 12-hour standardized fast and water-fast were performed before the operation. The operation was completed with the assistance of a constant temperature operating Table (37 ℃).

Modified RYGB mouse model

Based on the existing research methods and optimized, the modified RYGB mouse model was established through the following procedures: compared with the traditional RYGB procedure, the modified RYGB procedure has the following technical innovations: (1) Construct an end-side functional esophagojejunal anastomosis to replace the traditional gastric pouch; (2) The lower cardia esophageal ligation was performed to achieve food route regulation. The standardized operation procedure of the modified RYGB mouse model is as follows: (1) Surgical approach after anesthesia induction: Intraperitoneal injection of a ready-to-use anesthetic (Tribromoethanol, 0.02 mL/g) was administered to achieve deep anesthesia (absence of corneal reflex). After skin preparation, the operation area was disinfected with iodophor and alcohol. After laying sterile sheets, a 3 cm longitudinal incision was made along the median line of the abdomen to cut the abdominal wall. A retractor was used to maintain abdominal exposure. The small intestine was then carefully mobilized for subsequent manipulation (Fig. 2A). (2) Jejunum treatment: the jejunum was severed at 4 cm from the Treitz ligament, and the distal end was ligated with a 6 − 0 nylon suture. The proximal and distal side-to-side anastomosis of the jejunum was performed using a 9 − 0 nylon (Batch number: 220411; Purchased from Ningbo Medical Suture Needle Co., LTD.;) line at 6 cm from the broken end (Fig. 2B); (3) Esophageal treatment: The lower segment of the esophagus was mobilized, with preservation of the neurovascular bundle. A 6 − 0 nylon suture was used to ligate above the cardia (Fig. 2C). (4) Digestive tract reconstruction: A 9 − 0 silk suture was used for side-to-side anastomosis of the distal jejunum and the anterior wall of the esophagus (Fig. 2D). (5) Abdominal closure: After anatomical reduction, the abdominal cavity was closed in two layers: the abdominal cavity was closed by intermittent suture with 6 − 0 nylon suture, the skin was intermittently sutured with 3 − 0 silk suture, and the abdominal wall incision was disinfected to end the operation. Note: A 12-hour standardized fast and water-fast were performed before the operation. The whole operation was completed with the assistance of a constant temperature operating Table (37 ℃) and operating microscope (×10).

Sham mouse model

The specific operation procedures were as follows: (1) Anesthesia induction was the same as before (Fig. 3A-B); (2) The stomach and jejunum were exteriorized from the abdominal cavity and covered with gauze soaked in normal saline for a duration of 60 ± 5 min (Fig. 3C); (3) At the end of the operation, the abdomen was closed after the organs were reset according to the anatomical level (Fig. 3D). (4) Other perioperative management was consistent with the HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups.

Postoperative management

(1) Immediate resuscitation: intraperitoneal injection of crystalloid solution (0.9% NaCl, 1 mL/mouse) was performed immediately after the operation. (2) Environmental control: after the operation, the mice were transferred to a constant temperature recovery cage (37 ± 0.5 ℃) and continuously monitored until the righting reflex recovered. (3) D1: intraperitoneal injection of normal saline (20 mL/kg, TID); (4) D2: 5% glucose saline (20 mL/kg, TID) was injected intraperitoneally on the second day after operation; (5) D3-6: progressive excessive diet, liquid diet (60% fat energy gel, purchased from Jiangsu Collaborative Pharmaceutical and Bioengineering Co., Ltd.), free drinking water; (6) D7: return to normal high-fat diet; (7) Anti-infection (D1-2): intramuscular injection of penicillin (30,000 U/kg/d, QD; Manufacturer: Beyotime; Product No. ST488-1) to prevent infection; (8) Analgesia (D1-2): subcutaneous meloxicam (0.5 mg/kg/d, QD; Manufacturer: Beyotime; Product No. Y268362-50 mg) analgesia; (9) D1-7: The degree of activity of mice was evaluated, and the postoperative recovery and wound healing were observed.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining

(1) Deparaffinization and rehydration: xylene (Manufacturer: Beijing Yili Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd.; Product No. 20241231) Ⅰ (10 min), xylene Ⅱ (10 min), absolute alcohol Ⅰ (10 min), absolute alcohol Ⅱ (5 min), gradient ethanol (95%, 90%, 80%, 70%, 5 min each), distilled water for 5 min; (2) HE staining (Hematoxylin-Eosin(HE) Stain Kit; Manufacturer: Solarbio science & technology(Bei Jing) Co., Ltd.; Lot. No. 2500030004): hematoxylin staining solution was dropped and stained for 10 min, and the floating color was washed off with distilled water. The differentiation solution was separated for 30 s, and then the cells were immersed in tap water twice, 5 min each time. The cells were stained with eosin staining solution for 1 min, and the excess staining solution was poured out and quickly dehydrated. (3) Dehydration and sealing: Dehydrate in alcohol (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100% alcohol(l)), each for 2–3 s, and rinse in 100% alcohol(ll)for l min.; Transparent by xylene and seal with resinene (Manufacturer: Solarbio science & technology(Bei Jing) Co., Ltd.; Lot. No. 2504270011).

IPGTT experiment

Standardized fasting for 12 h before the experiment. At the end of the fasting period, fasting blood glucose was measured from the tail vein, and the baseline blood glucose value was recorded. Then, 10% glucose solution (1.5 g/kg) was injected intraperitoneally, and blood samples were collected from the tail vein at 15 / 30 / 60 / 120 min (± 1 min) after the intervention to complete the blood glucose measurement. Finally, the IPGTT curve was drawn according to the measured data, and the area under curve (AUC) value was calculated.

IPITT experiment

Pre-experimental preparation, baseline sampling and IPGTT were the same. Then the blood glucose was measured by intraperitoneal injection of recombinant human insulin (0.75 IU / kg). Finally, dynamic detection and data analysis are the same as IPGTT.

Sirus red (SR) staining

(1) Deparaffinization and Hydration of Paraffin Sections: sections were sequentially placed in Eco-friendly Dewaxing Solution I for 15 min → Eco-friendly Dewaxing Solution II for 15 min → absolute ethanol I for 5 min → absolute ethanol II for 5 min → 75% ethanol for 5 min, followed by rinsing with running water. (2) Mordanting: sections were immersed in Sirius Red Solution A (Modified Sirius Red Staining Kit; Manufacturer: Servicebio; Product No: G1078) and placed in a 65 ℃ oven for 30 min. (3) Sirius Red Staining: sections were slightly blotted dry, then immersed in Modified Sirius Red Solution B for 2 min; after brief rinsing, they were transferred to Modified Sirius Red Solution C and stained for 1 h. (4) Dehydration and Mounting: sections were rinsed briefly with water, quickly dehydrated by dipping sequentially in three jars of absolute ethanol (3–5 s each), cleared in xylene for 5 min, and finally mounted with neutral resin. (5) Microscopic Examination and Image Acquisition: sections were observed under a microscope, and images were captured for analysis.

Lee’s index

This index is commonly used as a indicator for evaluating the degree of obesity in mice. It assesses the extent of obesity based on the relationship between the mouse’s body weight and body length (the distance from the tip of the nose to the anus in mice). The calculation formula is as follows: Lee’s index = ³√[body weight (g)] × 10 ÷ body length (cm).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism 10.1 software was used for data preprocessing, statistical operation and visualization. Independent sample Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between the two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used to correct for multiple comparisons among multiple groups. The area under the curve (AUC) was used to compare the trajectories of metabolic indices between groups. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Experimental grouping and baseline data

After randomization, the mean weight of mice in the ND group was 22.59 ± 0.62 g, and the mean weight of mice in the HFD group was 22.89 ± 1.22 g, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.572).

The weight of mice in the HFD group increased

After 16 weeks of dietary intervention, compared with the ND group, the mice in the HFD group had a significant increase in weight (40.84 ± 3.42 g vs. 29.54 ± 1.34 g, P < 0.001, Fig. 4A), with an increase of 38.25%. The weight of the HFD group was more than 20% of the weight of the ND group. Compared with the ND group, Lee’s index of the HFD group was significantly increased (3.94 ± 0.18 vs. 3.58 ± 0.10, P < 0.001, Fig. 4B).

Glucose metabolism disorder in mice of the HFD group

The results showed that the blood glucose level of mice in the HFD group increased significantly at 30–60 min after glucose injection, and did not return to the baseline level at 120 min. Compared with the ND group, the AUC of the IPGTT curve in the HFD group was significantly increased (2334.18 ± 199.25 vs. 1045.0 ± 234.29, P < 0.001, Fig. 4C-D), suggesting that the HFD group had abnormal glucose tolerance.

The experimental results showed that the glucose clearance rate of the HFD group was decreased, which was manifested as delayed blood glucose trough and significantly increased AUC (497.69 ± 66.73 vs. 321.03 ± 15.41, P < 0.01, Fig. 4E-F), suggesting that the mice in the HFD group met the typical characteristics of insulin resistance.

The adipose and liver tissue of mice in HFD group showed MAFLD related pathological characteristics.

HE staining revealed that adipocytes in the blank control, SG, and RYGB groups were significantly smaller than those in the sham group, indicating that bariatric surgery not only reverses obesity-induced adipocyte hypertrophy but can also restore adipocyte size to normal levels. HE staining showed that the structure of hepatocytes in ND group was complete, the liver plates were arranged regularly. The hepatocytes in the HFD group showed hepatic lobular structure disorder abundant lipid vacuoles (Fig. 5).

Experimental grouping and surgical survival rate

Twenty-four mice in the HFD group were further randomly divided into HFD-CK, HFD-SG, HFD-RYGB and HFD-Sham groups with average weight of 41.71 ± 4.30 g, 40.42 ± 2.43 g, 40.58 ± 2.18 g and 40.65 ± 4.83 g, respectively. There was no significant difference in baseline weight between the two groups (P = 0.921). One mouse died of postoperative complications (abdominal infection caused by intestinal fistula and anastomotic stenosis) in the HFD-RYGB group, and the 4-week survival rate of RYGB was 83.33%. The 4-week survival rates of HFD-SG and HFD-Sham groups were both 100%.

Weight loss and body shape recovery were observed in mice after MBS.

The weight of mice in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups decreased significantly 1 week after MBS, and then gradually remained stable. However, the HFD-Sham group showed a V-shaped rebound after a transient weight loss (Fig. 7A). Overall, there was no significant increase in the weight of mice in the ND group at 4 weeks after MBS. The weight of mice in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups decreased significantly. However, after 4 weeks of high-fat induction, the weight of HFD-CK and HFD-Sham groups increased to a certain extent (Fig. 7B). At 4 weeks after MBS, the average weight of mice in each group was: ND group 30.71 ± 1.55 g, HFD-CK group 46.91 ± 4.67 g, HFD-SG group 32.94 ± 1.82 g, HFD-RYGB group 29.60 ± 1.89 g, HFD-Sham group 43.34 ± 5.25 g. ANOVA post hoc Dunnett test all showed that the weight of mice in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups was significantly lower than that in HFD-Sham groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 7C). 4 weeks after MBS, each group of mice Lee’s index is respectively: ND group: 3.48 ± 0.12, HFD-CK group: 3.88 ± 0.25, HFD-SG group: 3.49 ± 0.13, HFD-RYGB group: 3.45 ± 0.14, HFD-Sham group: 3.81 ± 0.16. The results of the ANOVA post hoc Dunnett test showed that the Lee’s index of mice in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups was significantly lower than that in HFD-Sham groups (P < 0.01, Fig. 7D).

MBS improves glucose metabolism disorders

The results of IPGTT showed that the increase of blood glucose in the HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups was significantly slower than that in the HFD-CK and HFD-Sham groups after MBS. However, compared with the ND group, the HFD-CK and HFD-Sham groups maintained a higher blood glucose level throughout the experiment (Fig. 8A). The AUC values of the IPGTT curve in ND, HFD-CK, HFD-Sham, HFD-SG, HFD-RYGB groups and were 1131 ± 76.29, 2512 ± 86.81, 2412 ± 31.82, 1958 ± 61.22 and 1681 ± 90.96, respectively. ANOVA post hoc Dunnett test showed that the AUC values of the IPGTT curve in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups were significantly lower than those in HFD-Sham groups (P < 0.001, Fig. 8B). Taken together, the results suggested that SG and modified RYGB surgery effectively improved glucose tolerance in mice.

IPITT results showed that compared with the ND group, the HFD-CK and HFD-Sham groups had a slower decrease in blood glucose after insulin injection, and the IPITT low peak shifted back. After MBS, compared with the HFD-Sham groups, the IPITT low peak of the HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups moved earlier, and insulin resistance was relieved to a certain extent, that is, the mice in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups showed faster insulin-mediated glucose clearance (Fig. 8C). The IPITT AUC values of ND, HFD-CK, HFD-Sham, HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB groups were 438.77 ± 16.27, 856.17 ± 152.66, 842.68 ± 84.78, 699.77 ± 60.01 and 605.30 ± 53.03, respectively. ANOVA post hoc Dunnett test showed that the IPITT AUC value of the HFD-SG group was lower than that of the HFD-Sham group, but not significantly (Fig. 8D). In summary, the results suggest that SG and modified RYGB surgery can improve insulin resistance, and modified RYGB is more effective than SG.

Serum metabolic indexes recovered after MBS

MBS in HFD-SG and HFD-RYGB group mice showed significant metabolic improvement after surgery, namely compared with the HFD-Sham group, TG, TC, and LDL were significantly reduced HDL levels were significantly elevated (P < 0.05). In general, the mice returned to normal or nearly normal levels (Fig. 9A-F).

Discussion

The increase in global obesity is accompanied by a sharp increase in the incidence and prevalence of NAFLD [27, 28]. Based on projected population changes, an estimated 458 million people globally will be affected by MAFLD by 2030 [29]. As the first-line basic treatment strategy for MAFLD, guideline-recommended lifestyle intervention faces the double bottleneck of poor long-term treatment compliance and limited metabolic benefits in clinical practice [30, 31]. In addition, in June 2023, a panel of 53 leading experts from across the globe advocated for renaming NAFLD to MASLD [32], thereby further underscoring the critical importance of this condition [33–35].

Although clinical studies have confirmed that MBS can significantly improve metabolic abnormalities in patients with obesity, its mechanism of action has not been fully elucidated. Due to the ethical and technical limitations of human studies. Therefore, the construction of a safe and effective MBS animal model is of great significance for the study of the mechanism of obesity-related diseases and provides a feasible application basis for the diagnosis and treatment of clinical obesity patients. The mouse model has the advantages of high genetic diversity, easy genetic manipulation, and low cost, which is suitable for large-scale experimental research. However, the small size of mouse increases the difficulty of surgery. In this study, two common MBS models, SG and RYGB, were successfully established. These two surgical types are widely used clinically for the treatment of weight loss and metabolism-related diseases and have achieved good results in mouse models. In the SG model, a sterile clip combined with a double-layer suture technique was used to simulate the effect of linear cutting stapling device and reinforcement. These two key operations greatly improved the postoperative survival rate of mice and the stability of the SG model. In the RYGB model, a modified RYGB surgical protocol was used to better simulate the clinical surgical path while optimizing the procedure and reducing the surgical risk in mice. The modified procedure replaced the traditional gastric pouch construction with side-to-side esophagojejunostomy to simplify the operation process and avoid the increased risk caused by too long or complicated operation in mice. This optimized design retained the key features of RYGB surgery and significantly improved the postoperative survival rate of mice.

In addition, this study found that intraoperative and postoperative care of mice is very important, such as in the operation to a great extent to comply with the aseptic operation standards of human surgery, to ensure that the whole operation in a sterile environment. Postoperative care also pays attention to maintaining aseptic operation. At the same time, the mice were given moderate pain relief and anti-infection after operation. In the case of fasting and water-fast after operation, fluid replacement and nutrient supplementation were given, and the liquid diet was gradually transitioned to a normal diet. The above contents are the key to improve the survival of mice after operation. The whole process is to simulate the patients with gastrointestinal surgery as much as possible, which greatly improves the probability of successful operation in mice.

In this study, by establishing an animal model of MBS, the physiological indicators of mice were systematically observed and analyzed by multi-angle detection methods, including weight dynamic detection, Lee’s index, glucose homeostasis regulation evaluation, and HE staining. Through the comprehensive experimental data, the significant efficacy of MBS in the treatment of obesity combined with metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes mellitus and MASLD was further confirmed. The operation not only significantly reduced the weight of the mouse, but also significantly improved the related symptoms of diabetes and alleviated the pathological manifestations of MASLD. These findings provide a strong experimental basis for the clinical application of MBS in the treatment of metabolic diseases. At present, gene editing mouse model is a general trend to explore the pathogenesis and treatment of diseases. The success of the MBS mouse model can further improve the exploration of the mechanism based on MBS and provide a platform and tool for subsequent research.

However, this study has several limitations. First, in terms of experimental design, we used fixed limb lengths without measuring the total small bowel length in each animal, and we did not perform injection desensitization training prior to the IPGTT. Second, regarding observational metrics, we did not monitor postoperative feeding behavior or assess the specific contributions of coprophagy or the incretin effect. Furthermore, the modified RYGB model employed in this study differs anatomically from the classical surgical procedure. Finally, on the mechanistic level, because we did not conduct fecal energy or indirect calorimetry analyses, we cannot distinguish the specific roles of reduced fat absorption versus systemic metabolic adaptation in the observed improvement of blood lipids. However, it is important to emphasize that all these potential limitations applied equally to all experimental groups; therefore, they do not undermine the validity of the core conclusion of this study—that surgical intervention effectively ameliorates body weight and multiple metabolic abnormalities in obese mice. Future studies will aim to incorporate the aforementioned measurements to provide deeper mechanistic insights.

Conclusion

SG and modified RYGB were successfully constructed in obesity with the MASLD mouse model. Both procedures can effectively reduce weight, and improve glucose and lipid metabolism and MASLD pathology. This model provides a reliable tool for the follow-up mechanism study in the field of weight loss metabolism.
