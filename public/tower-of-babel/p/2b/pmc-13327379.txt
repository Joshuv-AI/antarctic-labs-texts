Nontraumatic Acute Abdominal Pain in the Emergency Department: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Haris Duljević, Amina Zorlak-Čavčić, Verica Mišanović, Emina Duljević, Fahrudin Husić, Mustafa Omanović, Emina Dervišević
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Acute abdominal pain (AAP) constitutes a frequent presenting complaint in the emergency department (ED). Distinguishing between mild and potentially life-threatening cases requires a structured approach and effective triage. This study aimed to describe the main nontraumatic causes of AAP, assess urgency, and analyze case distribution by age and gender, as well as admission and discharge patterns.Patients and Methods:All records of adult patients presenting with abdominal pain between August and December 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. The entire patient sample (n = 257) was classified into 15 different diagnostic groups.Results:From the total count of patients, n = 257, there were 154 females (59.92%) and 103 males (40.08%). According to our study, the leading cause of AAP was renal colic (n = 67; 26.07%), followed by nonspecific abdominal pain (NSAP) (n = 48, 18.68%), urinary tract infections (UTIs) (n = 38, 14.79%), and biliary colic and cholecystitis (n = 32, 12.45%). In terms of hospitalization, 195 patients (75.88%) were discharged, and 62 patients (24.12%) were admitted. Logistic regression analysis revealed that the likelihood of hospitalization was approximately twofold higher in males (odds ratio [OR] = 2.03), whereas females exhibited a reduced probability (OR = 0.49).Conclusion:A higher proportion of AAP cases was observed among females in our ED sample, with renal colic, NSAP, UTI, and biliary disorders being the most common diagnoses. A high discharge rate (75.9%) reflects that most AAP cases were non-emergent; however, careful triage remains essential, particularly in male patients due to their higher admission risk.

Introduction

Abdominal pain remains one of the most common and challenging reasons for visits to the emergency department (ED). It represents a significant diagnostic challenge for emergency physicians. Knowledge of the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with abdominal pain is crucial. Understanding the underlying disease and its clinical course is essential to establish the correct diagnosis and manage these patients effectively in the ED.[1]

Nontraumatic abdominal conditions are among the most common reasons for emergency room (ER) visits, accounting for 5%–10% of consultations.[2]

These cases also demonstrate a high rate of hospital admission, with 60% of patients admitted, according to a 2004 report by the French Association of Surgery. Clinical history, physical examination, and laboratory tests rarely provide sufficient information for a definitive diagnosis, even when conducted in detail. As a result, imaging studies are frequently used, though they are often redundant. No exact data exist on the proportion of patients who undergo at least one imaging study.[3] A study by Hastings and Powers in 2007 found that, out of 1000 consecutive ER patients with abdominal pain, 21% had abdominal plain radiographs and 42% had ultrasound (US) or computed tomography (CT) scans.[4] Currently, no scientific evidence has established a standardized approach to fully optimize the benefits of these imaging techniques – such as diagnostic accuracy, safety, speed, and cost-effectiveness. However, recent technological advancements, along with the increasing availability of magnetic resonance imaging, should lead to more strategic use of imaging to achieve these goals.

Differentiating conditions with mild presentations of acute abdominal pain (AAP) from conditions which demand immediate intervention is crucial. Efficient triage in the ED is the basis of successful work and has a very high impact on the survival of patients. Several factors may compromise effective triage, including an imbalance between medical staff and patient volume, a high proportion of non-emergency AAP cases, patient age and comorbidities, and limited clinical experience among healthcare providers. Accurate triage ensures that ED patients receive appropriate and timely care. Accurate triage helps to minimize injuries and complications. Conversely, inadequate triage may lead to additional harm and adverse outcomes.[5]

Furthermore, current triage systems are limited primarily by their insufficient sensitivity and specificity. Due to the wide range of clinical variables encountered in emergency settings, designing a universally applicable triage system remains challenging. The causes of AAP are varied, encompassing gastrointestinal, urological, and gynecological origins, among others.

This study aimed to identify the main causes of nontraumatic abdominal pain, highlight any differences based on the gender of patients presenting to the ED with AAP, analyze the cases in terms of hospitalization – whether patients were admitted or discharged – according to age and gender, and finally, determine the proportion of truly emergent cases of AAP.

Patients and Methods

The distribution and clinical outcomes of nontraumatic AAP were investigated through a retrospective analysis of all adult patients presenting with abdominal pain to the General Hospital “Prim. dr. Abdulah Nakaš” in Sarajevo over a 4-month period, from August to December 2023. Inclusion criteria were nontraumatic abdominal pain and age between 18 and 90 years. The exclusion criteria were traumatic abdominal pain and return visits because of the same disease. The diagnostic classification was subsequently applied to the entire patient population as well as to selected subgroups (i.e. males and females, patients younger or older than 65 years, and those admitted to the hospital or discharged from the ED).

A total of 257 adult patients met the inclusion criteria and comprised the study cohort. Diagnoses were determined based on clinical evaluation, laboratory tests, and imaging studies as indicated, and final diagnoses were extracted from patient charts and discharge summaries. Renal colic was defined as acute, severe flank pain caused by ureteral obstruction, typically due to calculi, and confirmed by US or non-contrast CT scan. Non-specific abdominal pain (NSAP) was defined as abdominal pain for which no definitive organic cause could be established after standard clinical assessment, laboratory testing, and imaging studies. Urinary tract infection (UTI) was defined as the presence of dysuria, urinary frequency, pyuria, bacteriuria, or a positive urine culture when indicated. Biliary colic was defined as episodic right upper quadrant pain due to cystic duct obstruction, whereas acute cholecystitis was defined as right upper quadrant tenderness with fever, leukocytosis, and imaging evidence of gallbladder inflammation. Gastritis was defined as inflammation of the gastric mucosa diagnosed based on clinical presentation and supported by laboratory or endoscopic findings when indicated. Bowel obstruction was defined as colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, and abdominal distension, confirmed by imaging demonstrating dilated bowel loops with air–fluid levels. Gastroenteritis was defined as acute onset diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and vomiting, diagnosed clinically and supported by stool studies when indicated. Acute appendicitis was defined as migratory right lower quadrant pain, tenderness at McBurney’s point, elevated inflammatory markers, and imaging confirmation. Hernias were defined as protrusions of abdominal contents through an abdominal wall defect, confirmed clinically or via imaging, with or without signs of incarceration. Gastrointestinal bleeding was defined as hematemesis or melena, confirmed by laboratory tests or endoscopy. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) was defined as flare-ups confirmed by clinical assessment, endoscopic evaluation, and histologic findings consistent with Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis. Acute pancreatitis was defined as abdominal pain with elevated pancreatic enzymes and imaging confirmation of pancreatic inflammation. Gynecologic pain was defined as abdominal pain of gynecologic origin, confirmed by clinical evaluation and, if indicated, imaging studies. Short bowel disease was defined as malabsorption syndrome in patients with a history of significant bowel resection, supported by clinical signs and imaging findings. Cases with missing key data (<5%) were excluded from analyses. All diagnostic categories were coded according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision.[6]

Patients with trauma-related abdominal pain, as identified by diagnosis or the MTS flow chart, were excluded. Patients requiring immediate surgical intervention or ICU admission were considered truly emergent. This was a retrospective cross-sectional study based on medical records collected during 2023. Although the data were collected in 2023, formal ethical approval was obtained in 2025 before manuscript preparation and data analysis for publication purposes. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital “Prim. dr. Abdulah Nakaš,” Sarajevo (Approval No. 26-213-178/25, dated September 1, 2025). All participants provided written informed consent before study enrollment.

Results

During the period from August to December 2023, 1,962 visits were registered in the local ED, with AAP identified as the primary presenting symptom in 257 patients, representing 13.09% of all adult ED visits during the study period. The mean age of the patients was 48.59 ± 18.41 years. Of 257 patients included in the study cohort, most were female (59.92% vs. 40.08% males). Age distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (Lilliefors corrected) test, which indicated no significant deviation from a normal distribution (Statistic = 0.11; P > 0.01).

The highest case proportion was observed in the 25–31-year age group, followed by the 60–66-year age group.

The descriptive statistics revealed that the female group exhibited a higher mean age (M = 50.14, standard deviation [SD] = 17.82) than the male group (M = 46.27, SD = 19.11) [Figure 1].

A logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the effect of gender on hospitalization, with gender included as the sole covariate in the model. Male gender was found to be a significant predictor of hospitalization (β = 0.71, standard error = 0.30, z = 2.40, P = 0.016), with an odds ratio of 2.03 (95% confidence interval [CI] [1.14, 3.62]). This indicates that males had approximately twice the odds of being hospitalized compared to females. The model demonstrated a modest but statistically significant fit (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.033).

Discussion

Abdominal pain is among the most frequent reasons for visits to the ED and may reflect a wide spectrum of underlying conditions. It accounts for approximately 7%–10% of all ED visits. In this study, we examined the main etiological factors of nontraumatic abdominal pain, as well as their distribution according to patient age and gender, among individuals admitted to the Emergency Center of the General Hospital in Sarajevo.

Our study found that abdominal pain accounted for 13.09% of all adult ED visits over a 4-month period. This finding may assist in improving triage organization and resource allocation in the ED. By recognizing that abdominal pain constitutes a significant portion of ED visits, healthcare providers can implement strategies to ensure timely and effective care, improving both patient outcomes and operational efficiency.

In our study, the leading cause of abdominal pain was renal colic (67 visits, 26.07%), followed by NSAP (48 visits, 18.68%), UTIs (38 visits, 14.79%), and biliary colic and cholecystitis (32 visits, 12.45%).

In one retrospective observational study conducted at PES Institute of Medical Sciences and Research, Kuppam, data were collected from 326 patients presenting with nontraumatic acute abdomen between November 2016 and June 2018. In this study, the most common conditions were acute appendicitis (49%), followed by perforative peritonitis (26%). While appendicitis was the most frequent cause in that study, it was observed in only 9 patients (3.5%) in our cohort.[7]

In a prospective single-center study at University Hospital Basel, Switzerland, gastroenteritis (n = 60, 10.8%) was the most common diagnosis, followed by nonspecific abdominal pain (NSAP, n = 58, 10.4%) and cholelithiasis (n = 25, 4.5%). While gastroenteritis was the most frequent cause in this study, in ours, it was noted in only 9 patients (3.5%), the same as appendicitis.[8] These findings differ from those of a descriptive retrospective study conducted by Ahmadullah Danish over a 1-year period (March 21, 2018–March 21, 2019) in the General Surgery Ward of Aliabad Teaching Hospital, Kabul, Afghanistan. In this study, the most common diagnosis was acute appendicitis, presented in 172 out of 299 patients with acute abdomen, accounting 57.5%.[9]

In our study, causes of abdominal pain were both surgical and medical. In terms of surgical reasons, according to our study, bowel obstruction was the most common condition (4.28%), followed by appendicitis (3.5%), hernias (3.11%), and pancreatitis (0.78%). Similarly, a study by Ahmadullah Danish identified appendicitis as the most common surgical indication, with open appendectomy being the most frequently performed procedure.[9]

In our study, UTI, NSAP, and renal colic were more frequent in women, whereas hernias, gastrointestinal bleeding, and bowel obstruction were more frequent in men. However, in a study conducted by Ahmadullah Danish, only acute cholecystitis was more frequent in women (19 patients) comparing to men (12 patients), whereas acute appendicitis and acute intestinal obstruction were more frequent in men.[9] In our study, the majority of patients were women (59.9%), with the median age of 48.59 years. The present study differs in gender distribution from the study by Kumar et al., in which the majority of the 326 patients were men, with a male-to-female ratio of 3.4:1.[7] In line with this, Ahmadullah Danish reported that among 299 patients, 176 were male (58.7%) and 123 females (41.3%), with a mean (±SE) age of 36.5 ± 1.09 years and ages ranging from 15 to 94 years.[9]

Osterwalder et al. reported a median age of 47 years (interquartile range: 32–68) among patients with abdominal pain, with females comprising a significantly higher proportion (53%). In our study, 24.1% of patients presenting with abdominal pain were hospitalized, whereas 75.9% were discharged. In our analysis, male gender was identified as a statistically significant predictor of hospitalization, with males having approximately twice the odds of admission compared to females. Although the explanatory power of the model was modest (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.033), this finding suggests that gender may be associated with admission decisions in patients presenting with AAP. However, as gender was the only covariate included in the model, this result should be interpreted cautiously.

Several large cohort studies have demonstrated that AAP constitutes a consistent proportion of ED visits, usually ranging from approximately 5% to 10% of all adult ED presentations. For example, in a large urban ED cohort of over 5000 patients with AAP, NSAP and renal colic accounted for more than 60% of diagnoses, and about 16.6% of patients required hospital admission. These findings are generally consistent with our data, where AAP represented 13.09% of ED visits, and the leading diagnoses were renal colic, NSAP, and UTIs.[10]

Efficient triage is crucial to identify patients with potentially serious abdominal conditions while safely managing the majority of cases that are benign. Structured triage systems, including validated scoring tools and early clinical assessment, can help prioritize patients who need urgent intervention.[11] Our findings, with 75.9% of patients discharged and 24.1% admitted, reinforce the importance of effective triage in optimizing patient flow and resource allocation in the ED.

In our study, male gender was identified as a statistically significant predictor of hospitalization, with males having approximately twice the odds of admission compared to females (odds ratio = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.14–3.62, P = 0.016). This aligns with findings from other cohorts, including Ahmadullah Danish in Afghanistan, where 58.7% of patients were male, Kumar et al. in India, with a male-to-female ratio of 3.4:1, and Osterwalder et al. in Switzerland, who observed a slightly higher proportion of women (53%). These data suggest that gender may influence admission rates across different populations, emphasizing the need for gender-sensitive evaluation protocols in emergency care.[789]

Although most cases of AAP are benign and managed conservatively, a smaller proportion may represent life-threatening intra-abdominal conditions requiring urgent intervention. Early recognition of such cases remains a critical responsibility in the ED setting. Therefore, structured diagnostic pathways and timely surgical consultation are essential to prevent adverse outcomes. Importantly, the majority of patients (75.9%) were discharged from the ED, indicating that most cases of AAP were managed conservatively without the need for hospitalization. This finding is clinically relevant for ED organization, as it highlights the high proportion of non-life-threatening conditions among patients presenting with abdominal pain. Efficient triage protocols and structured diagnostic assessment are therefore essential to identify the minority of patients requiring admission while safely discharging others. The study by Osterwalder et al. reported that 83 patients (58.9%) with abdominal pain required hospitalization.[8]

The advent of damage control surgery (DCS) has greatly advanced the management of nontraumatic abdominal emergencies. Conditions such as generalized peritonitis, acute mesenteric ischemia, pancreatitis, necrotizing enterocolitis, and aortic dissection can rapidly lead to life-threatening events, including hemorrhagic and septic shock.[1213]

Abdominal sepsis was identified as a life-threatening condition that severely compromised patient physiology, making surgical interventions such as anastomosis particularly high-risk due to the increased likelihood of anastomotic leaks. In these critically ill patients, aggressive resuscitation, administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, and the use of DCS were essential for managing abdominal contamination.[141516]

In this hospital-based sample, the most common diagnoses were renal colic, NSAP, and UTIs, all of which were more prevalent among women. The underlying reasons for this distribution were not examined in the present study.

However, hernias, gastrointestinal bleeding, and bowel obstruction are more frequent in men. These findings reflect the distribution observed in our cohort, but further research would be necessary to clarify potential contributing factors.

Nontraumatic AAP requires a structured diagnostic and management approach. The application of DCS in managing acute nontraumatic abdominal emergencies – such as acute mesenteric ischemia, pancreatitis, and generalized peritonitis – has demonstrated potential in improving patient survival and reducing complications. As this approach continues to evolve, its application could significantly enhance outcomes in critically ill patients by allowing for quicker stabilization and better management of complex abdominal pathologies. Future studies focusing on optimizing DCS techniques and expanding its use in AAP could provide invaluable insights, potentially revolutionizing how abdominal emergencies are treated and improving survival rates in high-risk patients. To improve clinical practice, a consistent routine should be followed during the diagnostic process to cover all possible differential diagnoses.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single center over a relatively short observation period. Data were collected retrospectively from medical records, which may limit completeness and accuracy. In addition, follow-up data after hospital admission or discharge were not systematically available, preventing assessment of longer-term outcomes. Future multicenter studies with longer observation periods would provide more comprehensive insights.

Conclusion

AAP represents a significant proportion of ED visits in our setting. In this cohort, most cases were due to renal colic, NSAP, and UTIs, with the majority of patients being discharged after evaluation. Male gender was associated with higher odds of hospitalization, although the overall predictive value was modest. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the local epidemiology of non-traumatic abdominal pain and may support optimization of triage strategies and resource allocation in emergency care settings.
