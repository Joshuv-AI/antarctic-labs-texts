Color modulates disgust on surfaces, but not fear in scenes
Authors: Hiromi Sato, Shiori Muranaka, Isamu Motoyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Negative emotions such as disgust and fear play an important role in helping humans rapidly avoid threats and dangers in the natural environment. Although these emotions are influenced by context and situation, they can also be directly evoked by simple image features encoded in the early visual system. The present study examined how chromatic information in visual stimuli contributes to the perception of two negative emotions: disgust and fear. Participants rated the degree of disgust and fear elicited by 100 chromatic images and their achromatic counterparts. Results showed that the removal of chromatic information significantly reduced perceived disgust, while having little effect on perceived fear. Furthermore, an analysis of the relationship between image category and negative emotion indicated that distant “scenes” (e.g., landscapes), which tend to depict wide and distant spaces, were more likely to elicit fear, whereas proximal “surfaces” (e.g., close-up views of textures or objects) were more likely to elicit disgust. These findings suggest that chromatic information plays a particularly important role in the perception of disgust, especially for surface-related stimuli, whereas fear appears to rely less on chromatic cues.

Introduction

Humans receive numerous emotional cues from their environment, which significantly influence their subsequent actions and decision-making processes. Negative emotions, such as disgust and fear, are essential for humans to instantly avoid threats and dangers in their surroundings. For example, primates’ fear of snakes promotes innate defense responses that have been acquired through evolutionary processes (Isbell, 2006; Öhman, 2009; Öhman & Mineka, 2001). Similarly, disgust is thought to promote behaviors that reduce the likelihood of exposure to parasites and pathogens (Curtis et al., 2004; Schaller & Park, 2011). Therefore, efficiently and rapidly extracting emotional information, particularly negative emotions, from the environment is crucial for survival.

In psychological literature, disgust and fear, both representative negative emotions, have traditionally been considered closely related, as they occupy the second quadrant of the Emotional Valence Plane, characterized by negative valence and high arousal (Russell, 1980). However, recent research suggests that fear and disgust may be mediated by different information processing pathways (Lang et al., 2013; Russell, 1980). Kveraga (2014) demonstrated that fear is associated with the rapid detection of more immediate threats. Their study reported that threatening stimuli activate the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), which is involved in decision making, with a short latency of approximately 130 ms, whereas nonthreatening stimuli activate the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), associated with threat level estimation and revaluation, with a slower latency of around 220 ms (Kveraga, 2014; Kveraga et al., 2007). Similarly, Lachaux et al. (1999) found that threat stimuli induced synchronization of theta waves between the OFC and anterior temporal cortex at a latency of 130 ms, whereas nonthreatening negative stimuli induced theta synchronization between the OFC and VLPFC at a slower latency.

Recent psychophysical studies on the affective valence of visual stimuli have shown that several affective dimensions, particularly negative ones such as unpleasantness, disgust, and discomfort significantly depend on simple image features encoded in the early visual system. Typically, visual stimuli that deviate from the statistical regularity of natural images in terms of spatial frequency or orientation spectra have been shown in a large number of studies to robustly induce unpleasantness or discomfort (Cole & Wilkins, 2013; Fernandez & Wilkins, 2008; Juricevic et al., 2010; Le et al., 2015; O'Hare & Hibbard, 2011; Ogawa & Motoyoshi, 2020, 2021; Penacchio & Wilkins, 2015).

Color also plays a significant role in emotional arousal and preference, as evidenced by its importance in design and art. It is well-known that humans find certain colors or combinations of colors pleasant or beautiful, while others are perceived as unpleasant or dirty (Bekhtereva & Müller, 2017; Codispoti et al., 2012; Palmer et al., 2013; Valdez & Mehrabian, 1994). Traditional color-emotion research has frequently employed context-free stimuli, such as isolated color patches or color words, to identify universal associations between specific hues and affective states. As highlighted in a recent systematic review covering over 100 years of empirical research on color–emotion correspondences, humans tend to systematically associate certain colors with emotional meanings (e.g., dark colors with negative emotions including fear and disgust; Jonauskaite & Mohr, 2025). However, according to the Color-in-Context theory (Elliot, 2015; Elliot & Maier, 2012), the psychological meaning and effect of color are inherently dependent on the context in which it is perceived. For instance, while a specific color might be perceived as neutral in an abstract patch, it can evoke strong negative emotions when encountered in an ecologically valid context, such as the color of mold on a surface or rotten food. This theoretical perspective motivates the present study, which directly examines how chromatic information modulates disgust and fear in natural images rather than in context-free stimuli. Looking at natural scenes in the real-world rather than artificial patches, which are ecologically invalid stimuli, for instance, the color of mold or stains on walls and the color of rotten food strongly evoke negative emotions such as unpleasantness and disgust. On the other hand, it is less likely that specific colors or combinations of colors in the natural scenes strongly elicit fear. Indeed, fear is not diminished in the absence of chromatic information, as demonstrated by the fact that horror films are often intentionally produced in black and white, and in certain contexts, fear may even be heightened. These casual observations lead us to a notion that chromatic information plays a critical role in disgust, but not in fear. However, studies directly examining the influence of color on negative emotions remain limited, with Lu et al.'s (2015) work being one of the few to focus only on fear.

On the basis of the above background, we hypothesized that chromatic information in the natural image plays a more important role in evoking disgust than fear. To test this, we compared how participants’ ratings of these emotions for the same set of emotionally evocative natural images changed when the chromatic images were converted to achromatic (i.e., grayscale). Our results showed that achromatized images significantly reduced disgust but had little effect on fear. Additionally, an analysis of the distribution of images that elicited fear or disgust revealed a double dissociation between image categories (scenes, objects, and surfaces) and the two negative emotions: distant “scene” images were more likely to elicit fear but not disgust, while proximal “surface” images elicited disgust but not fear. This suggests that disgust is closely tied to the perception of surfaces, and the negative emotional response triggered by these disgusting images may be attributed specifically to disgust.

Methods

Observers

The experiment involved 23 naive participants, all of whom had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal color vision (18 males and 5 females; mean age = 22.8 years, SD = 1.59).

To evaluate the sensitivity of the present design, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using G*Power (v3.1.9.7). The analysis indicated that, given the current sample size (N = 23), the within-subject design (2 × 2) was able to detect interaction effects of approximately f ≥ 0.25 (corresponding to ηp2 ≈ 0.06) with 80% power at α = .05. This suggests that the study was sufficiently sensitive to detect effects of at least moderate magnitude, although smaller effects may not have been reliably detected.

Furthermore, we performed a precision-based estimation using the data from the participant-based behavioral analysis to evaluate the sensitivity of the experimental design. The 95% confidence interval for the observed interaction (ηp2 = 0.12) was [0.00, 0.39]. While this wide interval indicates limited precision for the omnibus interaction, the primary phenomenon was robustly observed. Crucially, the simple main effect of chromatic information on disgust was highly significant in both the participant-based and image-based analyses (participant-based: F(1, 22) = 14.12, p < .01, ηp2 = 0.39; see the Results section for the image-based analysis), confirming that the study was sufficiently sensitive to detect the core effect of interest despite the uncertainty in the overall interaction.

Finally, the sample size used in the present study is comparable to those employed in previous psychophysical and electrophysiological studies examining the relationship between visual features and emotion. For example, Kato et al. (2022) reported robust effects of facial color on emotion recognition with sample sizes ranging from 9 to 17 participants. Similarly, studies investigating the influence of color on emotional processing using electrophysiological measures have reported reliable findings with approximately 16 participants (e.g., Cano et al., 2009; Codispoti et al., 2012). Thus, the present sample size falls within the range commonly used in this field.

The study was approved by the ethics committee for experiments on humans at Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, The University of Tokyo, and informed written consent was obtained from all participants. The procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Potential participants were informed in advance that the study would involve exposure to unpleasant images, and those who expressed hesitancy were excluded from participation. Furthermore, participants were assured that they could withdraw from the experiment at any time.

Apparatus

The visual stimuli were generated using a graphics card (NVIDIA Quadro 2000) controlled by a PC (Dell Precision T1600) and presented on a 27-inch LCD monitor (BenQ XL2720). The monitor had a frame rate of 60 Hz and a pixel resolution of 2.14 min/pixel at a viewing distance of 50 cm, which was used throughout the study. The mean background luminance of the display was 95.7 cd/m2.

Stimuli

The visual stimuli consisted of 100 digital photographs (9.14°×9.14°, 256 × 256 pixels) depicting various natural scenes (Figure 1). The images were collected via Google Image Search. Since the original calibration of these images was unknown, we assumed a camera gamma of 0.5. Each of the 100 images was converted to achromatic (grayscale) using the National Television System Committee method.

Examples of stimuli used in the experiment.

Procedure

Twenty-three naive observers rated the disgust and fear evoked by the images in separate blocks. The experiment was divided into four blocks, consisting of two image sets and two tasks. The images were divided into two sets: Image Set A (50 images) and Image Set B (50 images). To ensure that observers evaluated chromatic and achromatic images using the same criteria within each block and did not directly compare the chromatic and achromatic versions of the same scene, one block presented the chromatic images from Set A and the achromatic images from Set B in random order. In a separate block, the chromatic images from Set B and the achromatic images from Set A were presented in random order. For each image set, one block involved rating the disgust evoked by the images, while another block involved rating the fear. The order of the four blocks was counterbalanced across participants.

In each trial, one of the 100 images in the block was presented without a time limit, and observers rated the image on a 9-point scale ranging from “extremely disgusting (8)” to “not disgusting at all (0)” or “extremely fearful (8)” to “not fearful at all (0),” depending on the task. Observers were strongly instructed not to confuse disgust with fear. In each trial, participants were encouraged to respond by pressing a key within approximately 3 s. After each response, a 0.5 s blank screen was displayed before the next trial began. The presentation order of the images was randomized. Each observer rated each image twice for both fear and disgust. All instructions were provided in Japanese (In the Japanese language, the terms “unpleasantness” and “disgust” are not clearly differentiated). After the experiment, the mean rating across participants for each image was calculated and used as the rating value for that image.

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for mixed design with Emotion (disgust vs. fear) and Chromatic Information (chromatic vs. achromatic) as factors was conducted on the images whose ratings exceeded 4 for disgust (N = 46) and fear (N = 40). The analysis revealed significant main effects of Emotion, F(1, 84) = 4.99, p < .05, partial η2 = 0.056, and Chromatic Information (F(1, 84) = 135.51, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.617), as well as a significant interaction between the two factors (F(1, 84) = 55.01, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.396). These results indicate that the influence of chromatic information on emotional ratings differed depending on the type of emotion. Simple main effect analyses showed that, under the chromatic condition, there was no significant difference between disgust and fear ratings (F(1, 84) = 0.14, n.s., partial η2 = 0.002), whereas under the achromatic condition a significant difference between the two emotions emerged (F(1, 84) = 16.06, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.161). Crucially, evaluating the simple main effects of chromatic information within each emotion revealed that removing color significantly and robustly reduced disgust ratings (F(1, 84) = 181.59, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.684). While the reduction in fear ratings was also significant, the effect size was substantially smaller (F(1, 84) = 8.92, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.096). These results demonstrate that the presence of color information eliminates the difference in intensity between disgust and fear, whereas its absence leads to a selective and highly robust reduction in disgust ratings compared to fear.

A two-way mixed ANOVA with Emotion (disgust vs. fear) and Chromatic Information (chromatic vs. achromatic) as factors was conducted on the top 50 images with the highest ratings for each emotion. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Chromatic Information (F(1, 98) = 147.17, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.600) and a significant interaction between the two factors (F(1, 98) = 64.73, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.398). The main effect of Emotion was not significant (F(1, 98) = 0.72, n.s., partial η2 = 0.007). Simple main effect analyses of Emotion revealed that under the chromatic condition, there was no significant difference between disgust and fear ratings (F(1, 98) = 0.71, n.s., partial η2 = 0.007). In contrast, under the achromatic condition, a significant difference between the two emotions emerged (F(1, 98) = 6.66, p < .05, partial η2 = 0.064), indicating that disgust ratings were significantly lower than fear ratings when chromatic information was removed. Furthermore, an analysis of the simple main effects from the perspective of emotion revealed that the removal of chromatic information significantly and robustly reduced disgust ratings (F(1, 98) = 203.55, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.675). While the reduction in fear ratings was also statistically significant, the effect size was substantially smaller (F(1, 98) = 8.35, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.079). These results consistently demonstrate that the presence of color information eliminates the difference in intensity between disgust and fear, whereas its absence leads to a selective reduction in disgust ratings.

Relationship With Image Categories

While inspecting the original chromatic images to determine which types of images were associated with disgust or fear, we have realized the possibility that disgust is more often evoked by surface images, whereas fear is more frequently associated with more distant scene or object images. In vison research, the perception and cognition of scenes, objects and surfaces have been studied as independent topics (Fleming, 2014: Logothetis & Sheinberg, 1996; Oliva & Torralba, 2007). They have also been assumed to be based on distinct neural mechanisms. For example, while the perception of objects and surfaces mainly involves the processing of statistical features and global contours in the ventral stream (Krizhevsky et al., 2012; Motoyoshi et al., 2007; Storrs & Kriegeskorte, 2019; Yamins & DiCarlo, 2016), scene perception involves the processing in the dorsal stream, related to spatial navigation (Epstein, 2008). To test this possibility, we classified the images used in the experiment into three categories—surface, object, and scene—by labeling each image according to the category that best described it (as judged by HS and SM). The most frequently chosen category for each image was assigned as its label. To ensure that the labeling was consistent with common sense judgments, the category assignments were reviewed by several naive observers. Among the 100 images, 29 were classified as surfaces, 60 as objects, and 11 as scenes. On the other hand, images that were rated as highly disgusting were often surface images, and converting these images to achromatic significantly reduced their disgust ratings.

To examine whether the effects of chromatic information depend on emotional category, separate two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted for disgust and fear ratings, with chromatic information (chromatic vs. achromatic) and image category (surface, object, and scene) as within-subject factors.

Effect of Image Category on Color-Emotion Association for Disgust

A two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Chromatic Information (chromatic vs. achromatic) and Image Category (surface, object, and scene) as factors was conducted on the disgust ratings (N = 23).

The analysis revealed significant main effects of Chromatic Information (F(1, 22) = 10.94, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.332) and Image Category (F(2, 44) = 118.83, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.844). Crucially, a significant interaction between the two factors was observed (F(2, 44) = 13.06, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.373), indicating that the impact of color on disgust varied depending on the image category.

Simple main effect analyses revealed that the presence of color significantly enhanced disgust ratings for surfaces (F(1, 22) = 14.60, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.399) and objects (F(1, 22) = 9.38, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.299). In contrast, for scenes, no significant effect of chromatic information was found (F(1, 22) = 0.26, p = .62, partial η2 = 0.012).

Regarding the main effect of Image Category, post hoc comparisons using Holm's method revealed that disgust ratings were significantly highest for surfaces (M = 5.70), followed by objects (M = 4.15), and lowest for scenes (M = 2.53), with significant differences between all pairs (ps < .05).

These results indicate that while chromatic information generally enhances disgust responses, specifically for surfaces and objects, this effect is absent for scenes. Furthermore, the baseline intensity of disgust is strongly dependent on the image category, being most potent for surface textures.

Effect of Image Category on Color-Emotion Association for Fear

A two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Chromatic Information (chromatic vs. achromatic) and Image Category (surface, object, and scene) as factors was conducted on the fear ratings (N = 23).

In contrast to the results for disgust, the main effect of Chromatic Information was not significant for fear (F(1, 22) = 1.52, p = .23, partial η2 = 0.065), indicating that the presence of color did not universally enhance fear perception. The interaction between the two factors was marginally significant (F(2, 44) = 2.97, p < .10, partial η2 = 0.119).

Simple main effect analyses confirmed that the effect of chromatic information was not statistically significant in any image category: surfaces (F(1, 22) = 0.31, p = .58, partial η2 = 0.014), objects (F(1, 22) = 1.23, p = .28, partial η2 = 0.053), and scenes (F(1, 22) = 3.11, p < .10, partial η2 = 0.124).

However, a significant main effect of Image Category was observed (F(2, 44) = 46.38, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.678). Post hoc comparisons using Holm's method revealed that fear ratings were highest for objects (M = 5.18), followed by scenes (M = 4.33), and lowest for surfaces (M = 3.09), with significant differences between all pairs (ps < .05).

These findings demonstrate that while image content strongly influences the intensity of fear, chromatic information plays a negligible role in modulating this emotion, regardless of the context.

Comparative Interpretation

Taken together, these results demonstrate a clear dissociation between disgust and fear in their reliance on chromatic information. While disgust is strongly modulated by color in a category-dependent manner—particularly for stimuli implying potential contact or contamination—fear responses are largely insensitive to chromatic information and are primarily governed by object- and scene-level properties. This dissociation suggests that chromatic information plays a selective functional role in affective processing, contributing preferentially to disgust-related evaluations rather than to fear, even when both emotions are elicited by the same visual stimuli.

Discussion

In the present study, we presented observers with a variety of natural images and their achromatized versions, and asked them to rate the emotional responses evoked by these images. Our aim was to examine how simple image features, such as color, contribute to emotional responses elicited by natural images. When observers rated the degree of disgust and fear evoked by both the original chromatic images and their achromatic versions, achromatic conversion was associated with a reduction in disgust ratings, whereas its effect on fear ratings was comparatively small. These findings suggest that chromatic information, a low-level visual feature, may contribute to emotional responses. Furthermore, when the images were classified into categories (surface, object, or scene), images that elicited higher levels of disgust tended to be surface images, whereas those that elicited higher levels of fear were more often objects or scenes (e.g., landscapes). Taken together, these results suggest that chromatic information may play a more prominent role in the elicitation of disgust, particularly for surface-related stimuli.

At first glance, our findings may appear to contradict those of Lu et al. (2015), who reported that the removal of chromatic information from natural images led to reduced fear ratings. However, several methodological differences between the two studies warrant consideration. In Lu et al.'s study, the stimulus set comprised a highly heterogeneous range of contents—including animals, human body parts, weapons, and various scenes—with substantial variability in the degree of fearfulness. Moreover, participants were asked to provide only fear ratings, without explicitly distinguishing fear from other negative emotions. Under such conditions, it is possible that stimuli eliciting strong negative affect more generally, including disgust, were categorized as fearful by observers. In contrast, the present study explicitly dissociated disgust and fear, both at the level of experimental design and statistical analysis. Our results showed that chromatic information exerted a significant main effect on disgust, but not on fear, and that its influence was further modulated by image category. When negative emotions are treated as a unitary construct, chromatic information may indeed appear to modulate fear-related responses, as suggested by Lu et al. (2015). However, when distinct emotional processes are examined separately, a more differentiated pattern emerges. From this perspective, our findings do not contradict those of Lu et al. (2015), but rather refine and extend them by demonstrating that chromatic information plays a selective role in disgust, while its contribution to fear is limited.

Both disgust and fear are negative emotions, but the physiological and psychological mechanisms underlying their elicitation differ. Disgust tends to respond strongly to visual cues that signal decay or the presence of pathogens, such as discoloration or mold (Curtis et al., 2004; Rozin et al., 1999). Fear, on the other hand, is triggered by visual cues indicating physical danger or direct threats, such as sharp objects, predators, or dark environments, especially when these threats are imminent or when the observer is in close proximity to the source of danger (LeDoux, 1998; LeDoux, 2012; Ohman, 2005; Öhman & Mineka, 2001). Our findings suggest that chromatic information plays a particularly strong role in the elicitation of disgust, which aligns with the idea that chromatic information is an important visual cue for detecting signs of decay or disease. In contrast, the visual information that triggers fear, such as the presence of predators or threatening environments, is not directly related to color. Direct threats often require immediate action, and thus rapid information processing is crucial.

The distinct psychological factors associated with disgust and fear are also reflected in our finding that these emotions are linked to different image categories. As mentioned earlier, images that elicited disgust were often associated with decayed food or mold-covered surfaces, which are potential threats that are typically perceived at close range (proximal) and are recognized as threats when viewed up close (c.f., Oliva & Schyns, 2000). These disgusting images were largely static, whereas images that evoked fear were more dynamic and typically associated with life-threatening scenes, such as the presence of predators or dark, unfamiliar environments. These fearful images were often perceived as distant (distal) landscapes or threatening objects. Our findings, which show that disgusting images were mostly surface images (or objects), while fearful images were predominantly scenes or objects, likely reflect the fundamental differences between fear and disgust. These results resonate with the Color-in-Context theory (Elliot, 2015; Elliot & Maier, 2012), which posits that color does not carry a fixed emotional meaning but instead functions as a cue whose impact is modulated by the physical and situational context. In our study, chromatic information served as an important and informative cue particularly for surface images, where color often signals biologically relevant properties such as decay or contamination. In contrast, for the perception of distal threats in scenes, chromatic information played a more secondary role.

The asymmetry in the impact of chromatic information on disgust and fear observed in our study may reflect the fact that these emotions are processed by different neural pathways. It has been suggested that direct threats, which elicit fear, require faster responses compared to other negative emotions like disgust (LeDoux, 2000; Öhman & Mineka, 2001). Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), which is involved in goal-directed behavior related to rewards and punishments, receives rapid input from the magnocellular (M) pathway (Maunsell, 1990; Merigan & Maunsell, 1993; Sharpley, 1990), which is specialized for transient information processing (Kveraga et al., 2007; Rolls, 2004; Wallis, 2007). This pathway processes achromatic information (Bullier & Nowak, 1995; Chen et al., 2007), which explains why the removal of chromatic information had little effect on fear ratings in the present study. In contrast, other negative emotions, such as disgust, may rely on different processing pathways.

Moreover, when we categorized the images used in our study into surface, object, and scene, we found that images rated highly for disgust were mostly surfaces, while those rated highly for fear were predominantly objects or scenes. It is well-known that proximal stimuli, such as surfaces, tend to contain high spatial frequency components, while distal stimuli, such as scenes, contain more low spatial frequency components (Ginsburg, 1986; Oliva & Schyns, 1997). This supports the idea that fearful images are transmitted via the M pathway, which is sensitive to low spatial frequencies.
