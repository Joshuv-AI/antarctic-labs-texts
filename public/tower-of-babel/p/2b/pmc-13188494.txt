Association between time-domain heart rate variability, diastolic dysfunction and unplanned readmission to cardiovascular department in older type 2 diabetes mellitus patients
Authors: Fenqin Chen, Sha Sha, Yanyan Meng, Le Qu, Huanrui Zhang, Lina Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a risk factor for the development of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD). Reduced heart rate variability (HRV) was linked to autonomic nervous system dysfunction and higher risk of cardiovascular mortality. HRV has been associated with systolic dysfunction, recent studies indicate its relevance to diastolic dysfunction. The objective of our study was to investigate the association between time-domain HRV, diastolic dysfunction and unplanned readmission (UR) in older patients with T2DM.MethodsOlder patients with T2DM were included in this study. HRV was obtained from 24-hour Holter electrocardiographic monitoring. Measures included the triangular index (VFC), SDNN, and SDANN. Echocardiographic measures assessed left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), the E/A ratio and the E/e’. Logistic regression, adjusted for demographics and cardiovascular risk factors, was used to explore the association of VFC, SDNN, and SDANN with LVEF, E/A, and E/e’. Cox regression models were then used to examine the associations between VFC, SDNN, and SDANN and UR to the cardiovascular department based on cut-off points for HRV indicators.ResultsA total of 235 patients were included in the study, mean age was 69.75 ± 8.8 years. During a median follow-up of 44 months, 43 patients were readmitted to the cardiovascular department. Higher VFC and SDNN were associated with an elevated E/A ratio, with each standard deviation (SD) increase in SDNN linked to a higher E/A ratio. Regarding the E/e’ ratio, increased VFC, SDNN, and SDANN were associated with a decrease in that measure. Higher quartiles of VFC and SDNN were also linked to increased LVEF. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed non-linear relationships with EF and the E/e’ ratio (all P < 0.05). Patients with VFC, SDNN, and SDANN values below the respective thresholds of 24.1, 98.8, and 75.9 had a higher risk of cardiovascular-related unplanned readmission, with hazard ratios of 3.12 for VFC, 2.37 for SDNN, and 2.09 for SDANN, respectively.ConclusionsLower HRV was associated with a lower E/A ratio, a higher E/e’ ratio, and worse diastolic function of the heart. Our study identified specific cut-off points for HRV indices; values below these thresholds indicate an increased risk of UR due to heart disease in older T2DM patients.

Introduction

The global diabetes prevalence in adults aged 20–79 years standardized to the 2021 United Nations world population was estimated at 10.5%. Prevalence increases with age with the highest prevalence of diabetes (24.0%) observed in those aged 75–79 years of age [1]. Similarly, the number of older adults with diabetes is also rising in the United States. Nearly one-third of U.S. adults over the age of 65 years have diabetes. Approximately half of these cases are undiagnosed, and an additional one-third of older adults have prediabetes [2]. Japan, as a super-aging society, already has one-third of its population aged 65 years or older. Consequently, a substantial increase in diabetes prevalence is expected in Japan over the next few decades [3]. The rising prevalence of diabetes has been attributed principally to the ageing of populations. Whereas type 2 diabetes (T2DM) is due to the combined effects of excess body weight, sedentary behavior, dietary changes, and other factors that have become more prevalent worldwide as the environment has changed, while the incidence of T2DM in adults is an order of magnitude higher and peaks in middle and older ages. Lee and Halter [4] provided an updated current perspective on the pathophysiology of T2DM among older adults and the implications for hyperglycemia management in this population. Specifically, they state that “usual defects contributing to T2DM are further complicated by the natural physiological changes associated with aging as well as comorbidities and functional impairments often present in older people.” According to the most recent data from the International Diabetes Federation, the global number of adult diabetes sufferers reached 537 million in 2021. Compared to the global trend, the increase in diabetes among the older people in China is even more pronounced. In 2019, the number of older diabetes patients in China was approximately 35.5 million, accounting for 30.5% of the total, making China the country with the highest number of older diabetes patients in the world. By 2021, China had the largest number of people with diabetes, with estimates exceeding 140 million, and this figure is projected to rise to over 174 million by 2045, with the majority of these individuals being older people [1].

Patients with T2DM are known to be at a significantly higher risk of mortality and cardiovascular events when compared to the general population [5]. Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) is another common but often under-recognized early complication of T2DM, which increases the risk of arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, and sudden death [6–9]. CAN was defined in the Toronto Consensus as “a disorder of the autonomic nervous system in the setting of diabetes or metabolic derangements of pre-diabetes after the exclusion of other causes.” Additionally, CAN has also been described as “the impairment of autonomic control of the cardiovascular system” [10].

With an increasingly aging population and a limited supply of acute hospital beds, the financial burden of unplanned readmissions (UR) among older patients poses a significant challenge to healthcare systems. In 2004, hospital readmissions accounted for roughly 17% of total Medicare hospital expenditures in the United States [11].

Time-domain heart rate variability (HRV) analysis has been widely used as a non-invasive tool to assess cardiac autonomic function, with long-term 24-hour recordings considered the gold standard [12]. Time-domain analysis is suitable for analyzing data across various durations, from short-term (e.g., 5 min) to long-term (e.g., 24 h). It imposes fewer constraints on data stationarity and handles non-stationary features like physiological fluctuations effectively. Time-domain analysis reveals overall variability and parasympathetic activity, while frequency-domain analysis provides a detailed breakdown of autonomic nervous system components, including the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic activities. Frequency-domain analysis requires higher data quality and stable conditions. Since 1996, the standards for measuring, interpreting, and clinically applying HRV have been well documented [13]. Reduced HRV has been linked to left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction [14–16], with numerous studies highlighting its association with adverse outcomes in heart failure (HF) patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), thereby establishing a connection between autonomic dysfunction and systolic impairment. More recently, increasing evidence has pointed to the role of elevated sympathetic activity in the development of diastolic dysfunction in HF patients [17, 18]. However, inconclusive results from studies on therapies for HF with preserved LVEF underscore the need for a better understanding of the pathophysiology of the sympathetic nervous system in LV function [17]. Currently, some studies have demonstrated the association between HRV and HF in older people [19] and postmenopausal women [20]. However, further research is warranted to elucidate this relationship more comprehensively.

Based on our current understanding, limited research has been conducted on the relationship between long-term HRV and cardiac diastolic function in older inpatients with T2DM. Furthermore, there is a lack of studies investigating the correlation between HRV indices and readmission rates due to heart disease in older diabetic patients after hospital discharge.

In this research, we aimed to examine the association between long-term time-domain HRV indices and cardiac function, particularly diastolic function, in older patients with T2DM. We also explored whether HRV indices could serve as predictors for UR to the cardiovascular department and determined the cut-off points of HRV indices associated with UR.

Methods

Study population

A total of 662 patients aged ≥ 60 years were screened, all from the Department of Geriatric Cardiology at the First Hospital of China Medical University. Of these, 235 older patients with T2DM were included in the study. These patients underwent 24-hour Holter monitoring 3 to 5 days after hospitalization. T2DM was defined by one of the following criteria: documentation in the medical record, a physician’s diagnosis, self-reported history, use of anti-diabetic agents, or random serum glucose levels ≥ 200 mg/dL (or ≥ 11.1 mmol/L) or fasting serum glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (or ≥ 7.0 mmol/L). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before enrollment. A comprehensive clinical examination was conducted for all study participants, during which concurrent diseases and medications were meticulously documented.

Exclusion criteria

Subjects were excluded from the study if they had atrial fibrillation or flutter, pacemaker therapy, severe hepatic or renal diseases [21–23], or an uncertain fasting state at the start of the oral glucose tolerance test. The present analysis focused on the remaining 235 participants.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of China Medical University (Shenyang, China, AF-SOP-07-1.0–01), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed about the use of their personal information, the benefits of the study, the medical procedures involved, and the confidentiality of their data. For participants who were illiterate, written informed consent was obtained from their relatives.

Heart rate variability

HRV was assessed using 24-hour Holter recordings (MIC-12 H-3 L System, Jinco Med, China) from all subjects during normal daily activities. Holter recordings were automatically analyzed by computer to identify and label each QRS complex. Recordings were considered eligible for time-domain analysis if they contained at least 18 h of data, with a minimum of 50% normal-to-normal inter beat intervals. The time-domain parameters used were as follows: the triangular index (VFC), the standard deviation of all normal sinus RR intervals over a 24-hour period (SDNN), and the standard deviation of the average normal sinus RR intervals for all 5-minute segments (SDANN) [24, 25].

Echocardiography

Cardiac dysfunction was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) with the EPIQ CVx and GE Vivid E95 systems (USA). All measurements were performed in accordance with the standards set by the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) using two-dimensional Doppler echocardiography. The following indices were evaluated: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), early diastolic trans-mitral flow velocity (E), the ratio of early to late diastolic trans-mitral flow velocity (E/A), the E/e’ ratio (the average value of the peak E velocity of the mitral valve blood flow divided by the lateral wall and the early interval diastolic velocity at the mitral annulus), left atrial volume indexed to body surface area (LAVI), and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) velocity. LVDD was diagnosed based on the ASE and European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) guidelines [26].

Follow-up

Follow up for a maximum of five years, a median follow-up period lasting approximately 44 months. The follow-up time for enrolled patients was defined as follows: For those with unplanned readmission, follow-up time was calculated from enrollment to the event. For those without unplanned readmission by January 2024, follow-up time was calculated from enrollment to January 2024.

In our study, an unplanned readmission was operationally defined as the first readmission of a patient for cardiovascular disease following hospital discharge. Specifically, the conditions encompassed acute coronary syndromes, heart failure, and arrhythmia.

Statistics

The data underwent processing and statistical analysis utilizing R version 4.0.3, where a statistically significant level was determined with a two-sided P-value < 0.05. Continuous variables are expressed as mean values and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are presented in the form of counts and percentages. To examine the associations between VFC, SDNN, and SDANN with the E/A ratio, E/e’ ratio, and ejection fraction, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed. This analysis provided regression coefficients (β) and standard errors (SE). Potential non-linear relationships were investigated using models incorporating restricted cubic splines. The X-tile software was employed to determine the threshold values of VFC, SDNN, and SDANN for predicting the risk of cardiovascular-related readmission. To assess the impact of VFC, SDNN, and SDANN on the risk of cardiovascular-related readmission, we performed Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards analysis, calculating hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three different models were used for analysis: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age and gender), and Model 3 (adjusted for additional factors including hypertension, coronary heart disease, BMI, SBP, DBP, FBG, HbA1c, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, BUN, and Cr).

Results

From January 2019 to January 2024, a total of 235 older individuals diagnosed with T2DM were admitted to the geriatric ward for the purpose of this study. The average age of the participants was 69.75 ± 8.8 years, consisting of 196 males (83.4%) and 39 females. Hypertension was observed in 174 patients (74.0%), while coronary heart disease affected 105 patients (44.7%). Throughout a median follow-up duration of 44 months (IQR:33.5 to 51 months), there were instances where unplanned readmissions occurred within the cardiovascular department, involving a total of 43 patients (18.3%) (Table 1).

Table 2 presents the correlation between VFC, SDNN, and SDANN with the E/A ratio in older hospitalized patients diagnosed with diabetes. After adjusting for all relevant factors, it was observed that patients in the highest quartile of VFC exhibited a significantly higher E/A ratio compared to those in the lowest quartile (β = 0.13, P = 0.026). Table 4 shows the correlation between VFC, SDNN, and SDANN with ejection fraction (EF) in older hospitalized patients with diabetes. In the fully adjusted model, higher quartiles of HRV were associated with increased EF compared to the lowest quartile (Q2: β = 3.45, P = 0.004; Q3: β = 2.50, P = 0.048; Q4: β = 2.86, P = 0.023). Similarly, the highest quartile of SDANN was associated with a higher EF compared to the lowest quartile (β = 2.70, P = 0.036). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between VFC, SDNN, and SDANN and EF (all P for nonlinearity < 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that HRV parameters were significantly impaired in older diabetic patients, which aligns with previous studies reporting autonomic imbalance in T2DM patients [27–30]. Specifically, VFC, SDNN, and SDANN components were all significantly lower in the geriatric diabetic cohort, indicating a marked disruption in autonomic nervous system activity. Notably, lower HRV, especially in VFC and SDANN, was associated with a reduced E/A ratio and an elevated E/e’ ratio, both of which are indicators of diastolic dysfunction. Additionally, higher quartiles of VFC and SDNN were associated with improved EF. Our findings further highlight that specific HRV indicators-namely, VFC ≤ 24.1, SDNN ≤ 98.8ms, and SDANN ≤ 75.9ms-are predictive of an increased risk of readmission in older diabetic patients. Both aging [31] and diabetes [32] are known to contribute to an elevated risk of LVDD.

Diabetes mellitus is a global epidemic, with its prevalence rising steadily worldwide, particularly among the aging population [33]. The condition of cardiac autonomic dysfunction (CAD) is extensively researched and recognized as one of the most debilitating forms among various manifestations of diabetic neuropathy [34]. Despite its critical role in these outcomes, it is striking that CAN remains under-researched in diabetic patients [35]. In individuals with T2DM, CAD typically results in an imbalance between sympathetic and parasympathetic activity. Such dysfunction can alter the metabolism of cardiomyocytes, impairing their ability to relax properly during diastole. Additionally, increased sympathetic activity may lead to coronary microvascular dysfunction, compromising myocardial perfusion, which is believed to contribute to abnormal left ventricular relaxation [36]. Several diagnostic methods can be used to assess CAN, including HRV testing, cardiovascular autonomic reflex tests, and myocardial scintigraphy. Notably, the detection of CAN in its subclinical stages can be achieved through a non-invasive and cost-effective test, namely the reduction in HRV.

HRV measurement is a cost-effective method that analyzes the oscillations in RR intervals between consecutive heartbeats. These intervals are not fixed and reflect the combined activity of both the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system [37]. A decrease in HRV is closely associated with an elevated susceptibility to cardiovascular evens [37, 38]. Additionally, HRV has been identified as a predictive factor for silent myocardial infarction and post-myocardial infarction mortality [39].

HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), often characterized by LVDD, is of significant clinical concern in patients with diabetes. In fact, approximately 45% of patients with HFpEF also have diabetes [40]. The Rochester Diabetic Neuropathy Study revealed that in all instances of sudden death, regardless of the presence or absence of diabetes, there was a significant occurrence of severe CAN or left ventricular (LV) dysfunction [41, 42]. The present finding is supported by studies demonstrating an independent association between CAN and increased mortality, even after adjusting for cardiovascular covariates [43, 44].

This study demonstrates two key findings: Firstly, there is a significant inverse correlation between HRV and LV diastolic function; Secondly, lower HRV is positively associated with higher readmission rates for cardiovascular reasons. Moreover, individuals with both recent myocardial infarction and abnormal HRV exhibit higher mortality rates [45, 46]. These findings underscore the importance of HRV as a non-invasive biomarker for assessing cardiovascular health, particularly in older patients with diabetes. We observed that as HRV decreases, LV diastolic function worsens, suggesting that reduced HRV is an early indicator of cardiac dysfunction. Furthermore, our study found that lower HRV levels were associated with an increased likelihood of readmission for cardiovascular causes, suggesting that improving HRV could reduce the risk of future hospitalizations. Given the strong relationship between HRV, LV diastolic function, and cardiovascular readmissions, interventions aimed at improving HRV could be a valuable strategy to enhance patient outcomes. In conclusion, our study offers novel insights into the role of HRV in evaluating cardiac function and predicting cardiovascular readmissions. These findings not only contribute to the scientific understanding of cardiovascular health but also offer practical guidance for improving heart disease prevention and management in older patients with diabetes.

An interesting aspect of HRV research is that some studies have reported an association between higher HRV and worsened diastolic function [47, 48]. For instance, one study based on 10-second ECGs found that higher HRV (specifically, the root mean square of successive differences, rMSSD > 44ms) was linked to a higher risk of incident HF [20]. Moreover, the Rotterdam study has indicated that a higher level of HRV may serve as a more robust predictor for cardiac mortality compared to a lower HRV [49]. Recent data also indicates that low-frequency and high-frequency power may be influenced by factors such as respiration, vagal tone, and posture, suggesting that these measures may be less sensitive indicators of HRV [50].

When it comes to time-domain HRV parameters, studies must be based on recordings of the same length to ensure comparability [12]. This requirement arises because variations in recording duration can significantly affect the calculated HRV metrics, such as SDNN or rMSSD. Shorter recordings, for example, may fail to capture sufficient data points to accurately reflect physiological fluctuations, potentially leading to misleading interpretations. Conversely, longer recordings might provide a more comprehensive picture but could introduce confounding factors related to participant behavior over time. Thus, maintaining consistent recording lengths is crucial for advancing our understanding of HRV and its relevance to both research and clinical practice.

Our findings suggest that specific measures of HRV, including VFC ≤ 24.1, SDNN ≤ 98.8ms, and SDANN ≤ 75.9ms, are associated with an elevated risk of readmission in older patients diagnosed with diabetes. As China transitions into a “deeply aged” society, the healthcare system is facing unprecedented challenges that demand urgent attention and innovative solutions. Chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and arthritis are increasingly prevalent among older adults. Compounding this situation is the growing shortage of hospital beds in various regions across China. Given the predictive value of HRV metrics in forecasting adverse events post-discharge, it is imperative that healthcare providers prioritize these indicators during patient assessments. By implementing proactive measures-such as tailored follow-up plans or enhanced monitoring protocols for patients identified as high-risk based on HRV data-both individual patient well-being and broader societal health outcomes can be positively impacted. An effective preventive strategy for CAN should encompass weight management, regular physical activity, smoking cessation, adoption of a healthy diet, glycemic control, regulation of cardiovascular risk factors such as blood pressure and dyslipidemia, and a behavioral approach focused on stress management. Furthermore, education plays a crucial role in this context. In line with these principles, the Toronto Consensus Statement highlighted that lifestyle interventions may enhance HRV in individuals with pre-diabetes and diabetes, and recommended their implementation as a foundational preventive measure [35]. Additionally, pharmacological options that improve HRV, such as anti-diabetic medications including metformin, pioglitazone, and SGLT2 inhibitors, can be considered based on the patient’s specific condition [51–53].One of the most efficacious approaches for augmenting HRV involves deliberate modulation and regulation of respiration, specifically engaging in slow-paced breathing at a frequency approximately equivalent to 6 cycles per minute [54, 55], a technique that has also been referred to as HRV biofeedback [56]. Rehabilitative respiratory exercises should be systematically incorporated into post-discharge care plans for patients to implement at home.

Chiang et al. [57] founded that lower frequency domain HRV indices, such as total power, low-frequency and high-frequency power - on admission were significantly associated with increased risk of UR in geriatric patients. These results suggest that HRV assessment may represent an effective and sensitive method for evaluating the risk of UR in older diabetic patients. Moreover, the cut-off points for HRV indices are crucial in predicting readmission risk. Patients whose HRV indices fall below these cut-points warrant special attention after discharge, including regular follow-up through phone calls or outpatient visits. HRV is associated with LVDD in older patients with diabetes. Given that both CAN and LVDD are linked to increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, screening for HRV and LVDD in diabetic older patients may help identify those at high risk. Future studies should aim to refine these HRV cut-points as valuable tools for identifying high-risk patients who would derive the greatest benefit from targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at reducing the risk of UR.

It is important to note that the determination of HRV cut-points may vary depending on different factors. First, there are differences in HRV cut-points across various populations. Second, the method of HRV measurement also impacts the determination of cut-points. Taking these factors into account is essential for determining accurate HRV cut-points in future research.

Limitations

To ensure the validity and reliability of our study findings, it is important to acknowledge the limitations related to our sample size. Specifically, the relatively small sample size—particularly among older women—may affect the generalizability of our conclusions. To address these limitations and strengthen future research, efforts should be made to increase both the size and diversity of the sample population, particularly in studies involving older women. While larger sample sizes are not always feasible due to practical constraints, it remains crucial for researchers to recognize and account for the potential biases introduced by small sample sizes when interpreting study results, particularly those concerning specific demographics like older women.

Conclusion

We investigated the association between long-term time-domain HRV and the risk of UR in the cardiovascular department among older patients with T2DM. Our findings revealed that lower HRV was associated with a lower E/A ratio and a higher E/e’ ratio, both of which are indicators of diastolic dysfunction. Specifically, certain HRV cut-points were identified as correlating with an increased risk of readmission due to heart disease in this population. These results suggest that older T2DM patients with lower HRV values are at a higher risk of UR, underscoring the importance of monitoring HRV as a predictive marker for cardiovascular complications in this high-risk group.
