Unlocking the capacity of Mn-based Prussian blue cathodes in capacitive deionization
Authors: Yuhao Lei, Shiyong Wang, Gang Wang, Xiaoyan Shen, Lin Zhao, Xueyang Zhang, Shuwen Du, Chen Yang, Jieshan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManganese (Mn)-based Prussian blue analogs (PBAs) typically exhibit suboptimal electrochemical performance due to their inherent Jahn-Teller effect distortion and low electrical conductivity. Here, a potassium substitution and high entropy strategy is proposed to synergistically construct K2(FeMnCoNiCu)[Fe(CN)6] (K-HEHCF) cathode to address the aforementioned issues. K-HEHCF exhibits excellent capacitive deionization (CDI) performance, with a high adsorption capacity of 104.92 mg g−1 at 1.4 V, and capacity retention close to 100% after 200 cycles. Under optimized conditions, the K-HEHCF electrode exhibits a significant removal effect on the cations in the simulated circulating cooling water sample, demonstrating certain practicality. By comprehensively characterizing various physical and chemical properties, it was found that the synergistic effect of high entropy and K substitution enhanced the electronic conductivity, constructed a charge compensation network, inhibited phase transformation, and due to the incomplete exchange of Na+ and K+ during the reaction process, some residual K+ served as the backbone to stabilize the framework structure. Additionally, the assembled CDI cell can drive LED as a desalination battery, enabling energy recovery. This work further exploits the potential of manganese-based PBAs cathodes and provides a ideas for the design of PBAs electrodes with both capacity and stability.Subject terms: Materials chemistry, Pollution remediation

Introduction

Demand for freshwater resources is on the rise due to factors, such as population growth, accelerated industrialization and climate change, yet the shortage of freshwater resources is increasing1,2. Therefore, the development of effective desalination methods to treat salt water has become a key initiative to address the shortage of freshwater resources3,4. Compared with traditional desalination technology, capacitive deionization (CDI) technology is simple, has low energy consumption, high efficiency, and also has the possibility of easy operation and selective treatment of certain trace impurities in the water, which provides a great potential to solve the serious global water shortage problem5,6. The ion-trapping mechanism of CDI is usually similar to that in sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), which is a reversible electrochemical process based on the formation of an electric double layer (EDL) or a redox reaction under a direct external voltage. Therefore, the importance of electrode materials in electrochemical reactions is self-evident7,8.

In general, mainstream CDI electrodes consist of carbon materials, but their inherent low adsorption capacity and poor stability often lead to poor desalination, restricting the optimization and expansion of their performance9,10. For non-carbon electrode materials, Prussian blue analogs (PBAs) are considered one of the most promising electrode materials for CDI, which is attributed to their excellent theoretical sodium storage capacity, three-dimensional open framework for fast Na+ transport, and ionic storage mechanisms different from those of carbonaceous materials11,12. Their general formula is AxM1[M2(CN)6]1-y□y·nH2O, where A typically represents alkali ions, M1/M2 refer to transition metals (with M2 usually being Fe, M1 are other metals, such as Mn, Ni, Fe, Cu, and Co), and □ denotes the vacancies in the [M2(CN)6]13,14. Among them, manganese-based Prussian blue is one of the most promising cathode materials because of the electrochemical activity of its Mn atoms15–17. However, the high water content (>10 wt%) and the instability of the framework structure of the sodium manganese hexacyanoferrate (NaMnHCF) greatly limit their sodium-ion intercalation capability, resulting in a sodium storage capacity that is far below the theoretical value. This has become a bottleneck for its widespread application in the CDI field18–20. Several reports have shown that doping of K ions during PBA synthesis helps to inhibit the generation of vacancies and water of crystallization21–23. Notably, the PBA cathode framework exhibits a notably stronger affinity for K ions, which can be attributed to the fact that the ionic radius of K+ (1.38 Å) is larger than that of Na+ (1.02 Å), suggesting that the replacement of Na with K reduces the likelihood of interstitial H2O molecule occupation24. Nevertheless, the larger ionic radius of potassium also increases the risk of lattice distortion during ion movement, and the inevitable Jahn-Teller effect induces unfavorable phase transitions during charging and discharging, leading to gradual rupture of the KMnHCF lattice, resulting in irreversible degradation and capacity loss25,26.

In recent years, the concept of high entropy (HE) has quietly emerged and rapidly developed in materials science. Yeh et al. listed four factors affecting the properties of high entropy materials, namely, configuration entropy, sluggish diffusion, cocktail effect and lattice distortion27. These factors together contribute to the excellent mechanical properties, corrosion resistance and thermal stability of high entropy alloy materials28,29. The research of high entropy concepts is gradually being extended to the field of electrode materials, in particular to address key challenges by introducing more elements to increase the configurational entropy30–32. These elements are randomly distributed in the lattice positions, which reduces the Gibbs free energy and improves the capacity and cycling performance of the electrode materials33,34. Previously, our group introduced the concept of high entropy to CDI for the first time and constructed high entropy hexacyanoferrate (HE-HCF) by introducing five transition metals, which enabled the system to achieve a configurational entropy of 1.61 R. As a result, the developed HE-HCF exhibited excellent cycling properties35. Despite the great achievements, the high water content in the HE-HCF framework still limits the Na+ transport during desalination and fails to meet the requirements of advanced CDI cathodes. Recent reports on high-entropy PBAs research have mostly focused on single-cation systems or remained limited to comparisons with low-entropy PBAs, demonstrating improved CDI performance36–38. However, comprehensive and systematic comparisons between K+ and Na+-based PBAs have been scarce. It is foreseen that by combining the entropy engineering with the strategy of replacing Na with K in the PBAs crystal, a great potential of PBAs for desalination applications will be stimulated.

Herein, K-based high entropy hexacyanoferrate (K-HEHCF) was synthesized by a modified co-precipitation method combining entropy engineering and potassium incorporation strategies, in which five transition metals sharing N coordination occupy the M1 site equimolarly, and the K ions replace Na to enter into a site completely. The results show that K-HEHCF maintains the impressive long cycle life of Na-HEHCF, attributed to the advantage of high configurational entropy, which not only enhances the electronic conductivity and optimizes the electronic structure of K-MnHCF but also effectively suppresses the Jahn-Teller effect and unfavorable phase transitions. Meanwhile, the introduction of potassium ions greatly reduces the water content from 8.61% to 1.66%, which improves the adsorption capacity. In addition, we found that incomplete K+/Na+ ion exchange occurred during the charging and discharging processes, and the K+ remaining in the framework could play a pillar effect and stabilize the framework structure together with the high entropy effect39. This study deepens the understanding of the sodium storage mechanism by integrating the high-entropy concept with K+ into Mn-based Prussian blue analogs as CDI electrode materials, providing a perspective for advanced electrochemical desalination technologies.

Results

Structure and morphology characterization

The synthesis of K-HEHCF by co-precipitation at room temperature is shown in Fig. 1a. Potassium ferricyanide (II) was added to the equimolar amount of five transition metal salts mixed solution, and K-HEHCF was obtained after the reaction and aging for 24 h. The addition of potassium citrate as a chelating agent can effectively reduce the reaction and nucleation rate of K-HEHCF and reduce the generation of defective vacancies. The standard definition of high entropy material is a material with system configuration entropy (ΔSconf) exceeding 1.5 R, which usually contains five or more elements with equimolar or near-equimolar ratios27. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) results indicate that the main transition metal elements in K-HEHCF are Fe, Mn, Co, Ni, and Cu, and the mole fractions of the five elements are close to each other, with about 0.2. The ΔSconf can be determined using the definitions derived from statistical thermodynamics, based on the Eq. 1−2, we can calculate the ΔSconf of K-HEHCF as 1.61 R, which exceeds 1.5 R and reaches a high-entropy category (Supplementary Table 1)26,35. Refinement of the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern by the Rietveld method (Rp=4.96%, Rwp = 3.61%) (Fig. 1b) reveals that all peaks of K-HEHCF belong to the Fm-3m space group, with lattice parameters a = b = c = 10.0063 Å and α = β = γ = 90°, and no impurities were observed (Supplementary Table 2). Additionally, the lattice parameters of K-MnHCF are described in Supplementary Fig.1 and Table 3, these include a = 10.1097 Å, b = 7.3209 Å and c = 6.9823 Å, α = γ = 90.0000° and β = 90.298°, the space group is P 21/n. The structure of the sample was analyzed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Supplementary Fig. 2a), and a strong peak at 2079 cm−1 can be observed that corresponds to the typical stretching vibration of the C ≡ N bond13,30. The peaks at 1633 and 3463 cm−1 indicate the bending and stretching vibrations of the O-H bond, respectively, while the peak at 592 cm−1 is related to the Fe-C bond40. The stretching vibrations of the C ≡ N group of the samples can be studied using Raman spectroscopy, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 2b41. Apparently, both K-HEHCF and K-MnHCF exhibit two distinct vibrational peaks. Among them, 2082.73 and 2078.88 cm−1 belong to the bending vibration of the Fe-C bond, and 2125.06 and 2116.09 cm−1 belong to the bending and stretching vibration of the M-N bond42,43. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analysis showed that the specific surface area of K-HEHCF was 108 m2 g−1, much higher than that of K-MnHCF, which was 33 m2 g−1 (Supplementary Fig 3). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images displayed that both samples consisted of particles with smooth surfaces (Supplementary Figs. 4–5). The High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images revealed lattice fringes with an interplanar distances of 0.51 nm, corresponding to the (200) plane of K-HEHCF (Fig. 1c). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 1d) indicated a uniform distribution of Fe, Mn, Co, Ni, Cu and K in the K-HEHCF, confirming its purity and structural integrity. The K-MnHCF obtained the same information (Supplementary Fig. 6). The surface chemical composition and elemental valence states of K-HEHCF and K-MnHCF were further investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), which are consistent with those in previous literature (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Fig. 8). Moreover, the local coordination environments and valence states of different metal ions were investigated by X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). As seen in Fig. 1e, the corresponding Fourier transform of the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) indicates similar radial distributions, suggesting that the different transition metal cations are uniformly distributed in the mixed-metal sites, confirming the successful construction of high entropy configurations. Meanwhile, it can reveal the coordination-structure information of different metal atoms. As shown in Fig. 2a, in the EXAFS spectra of all iron samples, the Fe-O/N and Fe-Fe bonds in K-HEHCF are located at 1.32 Å and 2.33 Å. Similarly, in the EXAFS spectra of the manganese samples, only two peaks are observed at 1.56 Å and 2.85 Å for the Mn-O/N and Mn-Mn bonds, respectively. The first main peak for cobalt atoms is located at an average distance of 1.63 Å, this peak is attributed to the average distance of the Co-O bond. For nickel atoms, the average distance to the first scattering atom (average Ni-O/N bonding distance) is 1.47 Å; for copper atoms, this distance is 1.52 Å. Supplementary Figs. 9–13 and Tables 4–8 provide EXAFS spectra of each metal present in the K-HEHCF, which are intended to extract quantitative information about the coordination environments of the absorbing metals. The oscillations of the energy space also strongly confirm the similarity of the localized structure near each metal to the simulated K-HEHCF structure44. Further, normalized X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analyses were performed to gain insight into the average electronic states of the metals in the K-HEHCF bulk phase. The absorption edges of Fe, Mn, Co, Ni, and Cu show different edge positions near Fe2O3, MnO, Co3O4, NiO, and CuO, respectively, suggesting that the stabilized valence states of these five transition metal elements are in the range of +2 to +3 (Fig. 2b). The wavelet transform (WT) results of the EXAFS plots provided in Fig. 2c, d allow comparison of the local coordination structure information of different metal atoms in K-HEHCF. Obviously, the main features of the WT-EXAFS plots are consistent with the results of the FT-EXAFS analysis.

Electrochemical performance and stability analysis

To further investigate the K-HEHCF electrode, the electrochemical behavior of the samples was evaluated in a three-electrode system using 1 M NaCl solution as the electrolyte. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of K-HEHCF are depicted in Fig. 3a, which exhibits a pair of distinct redox peaks in the 0.4-0.8 V range, which corresponding to the intercalation and deintercalation of Na+ in the electrode, respectively45. As the scanning rate increases, the shapes of the CV curves remains unchanged, which proves the electrochemical stability and Faraday response of the K-HEHCF electrode for Na+ capture. The CV plot of K-MnHCF at the same scan rate is similar, but the redox peaks are not symmetrical (Supplementary Fig. 14a). The specific capacitance of K-MnHCF is better than that of Na-MnHCF, which may result from reduced lattice defects in MnHCF due to K substitution for Na (Supplementary Fig. 15)46. In addition, three consecutive CV tests were performed on K-HEHCF at a scan rate of 0.05 mV s-1, and it was found that the curves almost overlapped, indicating that it has good cyclic stability and can reduce the generation of side reactions (Fig. 3b). The specific capacitances at different scan rates calculated from the resulting CV plots are shown in Fig. 3c. The specific capacitance of K-HEHCF is noticeably larger than that of K-MnHCF at differernt scan rates, and a high specific capacitance of 320.6 F g-1 at 0.05 mV s−1 is achieved, indicating that the high entropy effect can enhance the electrochemical performance. Compared with the low specific capacitance of K-MnHCF, the better electrochemical performance contributes to rapid desalination8,47. Supplementary Fig. 16 demonstrates the galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) of different samples at different current densities, and the results are consistent with those of CV48. Besides, The K-HEHCF was subjected to 1000 charge/discharge cycles at a current density of 2 A g-1, and the capacity retention was 91.8% and the average Coulombic efficiency was maintained at 100% (Fig. 3d). From the Supplementary Fig. 17, it can be found that the electrolyte after cycling of K-MnHCF was obviously discolored and even some of the material was detached, indicating that serious side reactions occurred during the cycling process. On the contrary, the electrolyte remained colorless and transparent after K-HEHCF cycling, which indicated that no distinct degradation occurred during the reaction process, confirming that the high entropy effect endowed K-HEHCF with an excellent stabilizing structure and inhibiting dissolution properties49.

The logarithmic equation can be used to express the relationship between the scan rate (v) and the response current (i), and in this way to investigate the charge transfer kinetics of the electrode material. When b = 0.5, it indicates that the sodium storage mechanism at the electrode is a battery reaction dominated by ion diffusion; conversely, when b = 1, it is a capacitance-controlled process dominated by surface or near-surface reactions50,51. To obtain a more reliable kinetic contribution ratio, we performed calculations using a low scanning rate range of 0.2-1.0 mV s-1. After calculation, the b values of the two peak currents of K-HEHCF are 0.95 and 0.94, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 18). Subsequently, using Dunn’s method to quantify the current contribution of capacitive control and diffusion control52. It can be observed that the capacitive contribution increases with the increase in scan rate, from 67.8% at 0.2 mV s-1 to 82.1% at 1 mV s−1 (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating that the ion/charge migration is mainly controlled by capacitive control48. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were used to analyze the charge transfer efficiency of the electrode materials. Supplementary Fig. 20 demonstrates the Nyquist plots for both samples, consisting of semicircles in the high-frequency region and inclined straight lines in the low-frequency region, and the inset shows the fitted equivalent circuit diagram. Compared with K-MnHCF, K-HEHCF possesses a larger inclined slope, indicating that K-HEHCF has more rapid ion diffusion kinetics. The Warburg coefficient (σw), which characterizes the Na+ ion diffusion rate of the electrode in the low-frequency region, can be obtained by plotting the Z’ and w−1/2 (w is the angular frequency)53. The slope of the K-HEHCF curve is smaller, indicating that its Na+ ion diffusion rate is higher than that of the K-MnHCF electrode, which is more favorable for the rapid sodium removal process (Supplementary Fig. 21).

The structural evolution of K-HEHCF and K-MnHCF during charging and discharging was investigated by ex situ XRD. As illustrated in Fig. 3f, the characteristic peaks corresponding to the (200) and (400) planes are slightly shifted to the left during charging and return to their original positions during discharging, and in general, there is no significant change, exhibiting the classical solid solution behavior of high entropy materials54. Compared with K-HEHCF, the peak positions at (200), (220) and (400) of K-MnHCF have shifted more greater, and they cannot return to their original positions after cycling (Supplementary Fig. 22). Apparently, manganese-based materials are susceptible to intrinsic Jahn-teller effects that lead to partial phase transitions within the structure during GCD measurements, which may account for the poor electrochemical performance of K-MnHCF55.

Advantages of replacing Na ions with K ions

To reveal the effect of K introduction on PBAs, we synthesized Na-MnHCF and Na-HEHCF under the same conditions. Similar to the shape of K-based PBAs, the SEM and TME images of Na-based PBAs are also irregular particles with smooth surfaces (Supplementary Figs. 23–24). Rietveld refinement of the XRD plots of Na-HEHCF and Na-MnHCF was also performed. As indicated in Supplementary Fig. 25 and Tables 9−10, the lattice parameters of Na-HEHCF and Na-MnHCF are less varied, which indicates that the variation of K/Na ions had little effect on the crystal structures of HEHCF and MnHCF. The density of states (DOS) and diffusion kinetics of K/Na-HEHCF and K/Na-MnHCF were compared based on theoretical calculations (DFT). The main components of the conduction band of K-MnHCF are Fe and Mn, and the DOS peaks are far away from the Fermi energy level with a band gap of 3.53 eV. After the introduction of a high–entropy strategy, the conduction band of K-HEHCF extends toward the Fermi energy level, and the band gap disappears, indicating that the electrical conductivity is greatly enhanced (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 26a). Similar results were obtained for Na-HEHCF compared to Na-MnHCF, with an improvement in electrical conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 26b, c). Notably, K-MnHCF (3.53 eV) is closer to the Fermi energy level and has a smaller band gap compared to Na-MnHCF (3.70 eV), suggesting that PBAs synthesized by the K-substitution method are more conductive. In addition, adsorption structure models for K-HEHCF and K-MnHCF were developed and optimized (see inset) to serve as a basis for calculating their adsorption energies; the calculated results are displayed in Fig. 3h. K-HEHCF (−4.25 eV) exhibits a larger negative Na+ adsorption energy than that of K-MnHCF (−2.81 eV), which indicates that the HE framework structure is more capable of trapping Na+ and improving its desalination performance. In addition to the ability to trap Na+, the low ion migration energy barrier is also a key factor in determining the rate capability of the electrode during the electrosorption/desorption process56. The Na+ migration energy barrier maximum of K-HEHCF was calculated to be 0.32 eV, which is obviously lower than the migration energy barrier maximum of K-MnHCF (0.87 eV) (Fig. 3i). Overall, these theoretical calculations provide a fundamental basis for understanding the superiority of K-HEHCF electrode under the synergistic effect of K substitution and high entropy engineering. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) exhibits that the weight loss of HEHCFs and MnHCFs at 25 °C–500 °C. The weight loss of Prussian blue materials in different temperature ranges, especially below 100 °C, between 100-170 °C and 170 °C–270 °C, can be attributed to the loss of adsorbed water, interstitial water and coordinated water, respectively24,57. The interstitial water occupies the available sites for Na+ storage, leading to capacity decay, while the presence of a large amount of coordinated water tends to lead to the deterioration of the structural stability of PBAs58. For HEHCFs, 4.32% of interstitial water and 1.66% of coordinated water were measured for K-HEHCF, which were lower than the 8.24% and 8.61% for Na-HEHCF (Supplementary Fig. 27a). Similarly, for MnHCFs, K-MnHCF had 2.82% of interstitial water and 1.2% of coordinated water, which were lower than those of Na-MnHCF, which were 4.15% and 9.01% (Supplementary Fig. 27b). It can be seen that the water content of K-based PBAs is reduced compared to Na-based PBAs, which is due to the fact that K ions with a larger ionic radius can fill the structural interstices better and reduce the generation of defects. This may be the reason why K-HEHCF has better electrochemical performance than Na-HEHCF (Supplementary Fig. 28).

Performance for electrochemical water desalination

The hybrid capacitive deionization (HCDI) system was constructed utilizing activated carbon (AC) as the anode and the synthesized electrode material as the cathode to assess the desalination performance. It is worth noting that due to the poor stability of MnHCFs, the electrode sheets experience severe discoloration when encountering water, leading to material loss. However, this phenomenon did not occur in HEHCFs, indicating that the high-entropy effect plays a non-negligible role in optimizing structural stability. Therefore, CDI desalination experiments were performed on K-HEHCF and Na-HEHCF to reveal the effect of K substitution on the CDI performance of HCFs. The desalination experiments were first carried out under different voltage conditions at 500 mg L-1 NaCl solution, and the adsorption capacity of K-HEHCF gradually increased from 54.24 mg g−1 at 0.6 V to as high as 104.92 mg g−1 at 1.4 V (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, as can be seen from the plot of desalination adsorption rate with time, K-HEHCF rapidly increased to the maximum value (as high as 34.5 mg g−1 min-1) within 60 s and then gradually decreased (Fig. 4b), which was better than Na-HEHCF, confirming that the K substitution optimized the ion transport rate. It is worth noting that, because desalination performance testing is strongly influenced by electrode mass and area, we also prepared three CDI electrodes with different masses or areas and tested them to fully validate the optimal desalination conditions for K-HEHCF. The results indicate that the electrode conditions employed in this experiment exhibit optimal desalination performance (Supplementary Fig. 29). Not only that, the energy consumption of K-HEHCF for capturing ions is obviously lower than that of Na-HEHCF (Supplementary Fig. 30). The Ragone plot of Fig. 4c demonstrates that K-HEHCF located in the upper right corner has better adsorption capacity and desalination rate than Na-HEHCF. The adsorption capacities of both electrodes were elevated with increasing NaCl concentration, but both K-HEHCF had higher adsorption capacities than Na-HEHCF under the same conditions (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, cyclic stability testing and TGA of other single-metal-center PBAs also indicate that the mechanism underlying the improved performance of K-based PBAs over Na-based PBAs is not limited to Mn-based materials. The K-HEHCF material, synthesized by further coupling K-substituted MnHCF with a high-entropy strategy, exhibits improved electrochemical and desalination performance. This strategy provides an approach and design paradigm for constructing electrode materials with stable structures, low lattice defects, and high sodium-ion storage efficiency (Supplementary Figs. 31, 32).

We designed a long tandem CDI system with five treatment units for treating simulated circulating cooling water, to develop additional application scenarios to demonstrate its potential value (Supplementary Fig. 33)59. Surprisingly, the coupled CDI system also exhibited excellent mixed ion separation in simulated circulating cooling water. As demonstrated in Fig. 4e, the concentrations of Na+, Mg2+, and Ca2+ in the solution were reduced from 199.4, 11.6, and 90.7 mg L-1 to 154.4, 2.9, and 1.6 mg L-1, respectively, after the first run. The Ca2+/Mg2+ concentrations of the treated simulated circulating cooling water were very low, which indicates that the K-HEHCF also has good removal efficiency for the common multivalent salt ions (eg. Ca2+and Mg2+). In fact, numerous research studies have already investigated the feasibility of selectively extracting different ions from saltwater using CDI technology60,61. Meanwhile, Prussian blue analogs, such as K-HEHCF, possess specific ion exchange sites within their crystal structures. The size, shape, and charge distribution of these sites confer selectivity toward different ions, with multivalent ions potentially exhibiting a more suitable size and charge distribution for these exchange sites62. During adsorption, Na+ with lower charge density and smaller hydration radius typically adsorb preferentially due to their faster diffusion rate, but their binding is unstable. As adsorption approaches saturation, Ca2+ and Mg2+ with higher charge density and larger hydration radius are more readily attracted and fixed by the high-charge-density regions on the K-HEHCF surface. At this point, Na+ is often at a disadvantage in the adsorption competition and is eventually replaced by Ca2+ and Mg2+. Consequently, K-HEHCF demonstrates excellent removal efficiency for Ca2+ and Mg2+.

Cyclic stability is a critical metric for evaluating CDI performance. After a long cycle of up to 200 times, the K-HEHCF indicated excellent stability with a retention rate close to 100%, indicating that the high configuration entropy effect could stabilize the structural framework and inhibit the unfavorable phase transitions during the desalination process (Fig. 4f). As cycling progresses, the electrode system gradually achieves a stable adsorption-desorption equilibrium, with the coulombic efficiency stabilizing and maintaining above 90%, indicating excellent reversibility and structural stability of the electrode. Since the charge-discharge voltage during cycling is ±1 V, the calculated true energy recovery rate aligns with the coulombic efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 34). The SEM and TEM characterization of K-HEHCF after cycling indicated that there was no significant change in its morphology compared to that before the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 35). Further, the XRD spectra before and after cycling were also compared and analyzed, and it was found that the major diffraction peaks were retained, confirming the stability of the material (Supplementary Fig. 36). It should be noted that the new peak observed around 26.5° corresponds to the C peak from graphite paper residue. Additionally, it was found that after 200 cycles, the amount of metal ions leached into the NaCl solution was as low as 0-0.17 ppm, further confirming the excellent stability of K-HEHCF (Supplementary Table 11). Figure 4g and Supplementary Table 12 compare the desalination performance of K-HEHCF with that of other recently reported Faradaic materials, demonstrating its exceptional adsorption capacity and cycling stability under similar voltage and NaCl concentration conditions63–78.

Sodium-ion storage mechanism

To investigate the ions storage mechanism of K-HEHCF in NaCl electrolyte, it was subjected to in situ Raman tests at different stages of the CDI process (Fig. 5a). During a complete CDI cycle, the characteristic peaks of the adsorption stage shifted gradually to the left by approximately 4 cm-1, while at the stage of desorption, the positions of the characteristic peaks were slowly returned to the initial positions, indicating that K-HEHCF undergoes a highly reversible ion storage reaction. In addition, it is necessary to verify the presence of Na+/K+ ion exchange during the reaction process since smaller Na+ tends to displace K+ by ion exchange, occupying some of the K sites and leading to changes in the composition and structure of the material39. As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the line-scan spectra of the K-HEHCF electrodes after CDI cycling showed significant K and Na signals, while the EDS spectra of K 2p also indirectly confirmed the residual of trace K+ (Supplementary Fig. 37). Analysis of the evolution of high-resolution K 2p and Na 1 s spectra before and after the K-HEHCF cycle further verifies the occurrence of reversible ion exchange between K+ and Na+ (Supplementary Fig. 38). It is worth noting that the peak of K 2p of the sample after cycling hardly appears in the full XPS spectra due to the shielding of the high-density spectra of C 1 s, so high-resolution K spectra were used to analyze the presence of K+. The K+ remaining in the framework during cycling is thought to reduce structural strain and stabilize the framework due to its “pillar effect”, thus improving the stability of K-HEHCF samples79. To further probe the chemical composition and homogeneity within the electrode, the sample was depth-profiled using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS). The false-color three-dimensional (3D) view of the sputtering volume constructed from the depth profiles displays a uniform distribution of K + , Fe + , Mn + , Co + , Ni+ and Cu+ in the pristine sample, which once again verifies the successful introduction of the five transition metals (Fig. 5c). It is worth mentioning that the transition metal ion signals not only remain strong and stable throughout the sputtering process, but also have very similar signal intensities, as can be seen from the depth distributions of the different metal secondary ions in the pristine K-HEHCF electrodes demonstrated in Fig. 5d. This suggests that five transition metal elements co-occupy the M1 site in equimolar or approximately equimolar ratios, and that equimolar doping of these metals at high spin sites enhances stability and sodium storage properties due to the “cocktail effect”80.

We attempted to further confirm the Na+/K+ exchange of K-HEHCF during the charge-discharge process by 2D mapping spectra of TOF-SIMS. As seen in Fig. 5e–g, Na+ was not present in the maps of the initial sample. However, from the maps of the sample after ten times of charging, it can be found that the Na+ signals are obviously strengthened and the K+ signals are weakened, indicating that Na+ replaces part of the K+ in the lattice, and the replaced K+ is dissolved into the electrolyte. The sample maps after the subsequent discharge show a reversible recovery of K+, but not back to the initial strength, while the Na+ signal strength continues to be strengthened. This may be due to the fact that the concentration of free Na+ in the electrolyte is much higher than that of K+, and the radius of Na+ is smaller than that of K+. In summary, K+ and Na+ undergo ion exchange during the reaction process, and the remaining K+ is believed to be the backbone for reducing structural strain and stabilizing the framework, which may lead to long-term cycling performance39.

To determine the reaction mechanism of each active center in K-HEHCF, three CDI cycles were performed on K-HEHCF, and both the original samples and the samples after different stages in each cycle were analyzed by ex situ XPS. After the desorption stage of the CDI cycle, two new characteristic peaks corresponding to Fe3+ appeared on Fe 2p, indicating that part of the Fe2+ inside the electrode was oxidized to Fe3+. After the adsorption stage is completed, the characteristic peaks of Fe3+ disappeared again and are completely transformed into Fe2+ (Supplementary Fig. 39a). The Mn 2p spectra display the mutual conversion of the mixed valence states of Mn2+/Mn3+ under different states. During the adsorption process, Mn3+ is partially reduced to Mn2+, and Mn2+ is partially oxidized to Mn3+ during the desorption process (Supplementary Fig. 39b). The Co2+/Co3+ and Cu1+/Cu2+ exhibited the same trend as Mn2+/Mn3+, with an increase in the proportion of the characteristic peak area of Co2+/Cu1+ and a decrease in the proportion of the characteristic peak area of Co3+/Cu2+ at the adsorption stage in the three CDI cycles (Supplementary Fig. 39c, d). This suggests that with the insertion of Na+, Co3+/Cu2+ was partially reduced to Co2+/Cu1+, respectively. The desorption process is reversed, with Co2+/Cu1+ being partially oxidized to Co3+/Cu2+ as Na+ is released from the lattice. The valence states of several of the above elements have similar trends, suggesting that they are jointly involved in reversible redox reactions. Supplementary Fig. 39e displays the ex situ XPS spectra of Ni 2p. Ni in K-HEHCF is mainly in the form of Ni2+, and Ni2+/Ni3+ remains stable during CDI cycling, demonstrating that Ni is not involved in the redox reaction, but is mainly used to stabilize the structure in order to offset the structural strain during ion intercalation and deintercalation, and to maintain the integrity of the structure81. Based on the above analysis, Fe2+/3+, Mn2+/Mn3+, Co2+/3+ and Cu1+/2+ are the main redox centers in K-HEHCF.

Capacitive Deionization Energy Recovery

During the CDI desalination process, Na+ ions are inserted into the K-HEHCF electrode, while Cl- ions are captured by the AC electrode. Upon removal of the external voltage, the Cl- and Na+ adsorbed on the electrodes are released back into the solution, accompanied by the generation of electric current (Fig. 6a). Therefore, theoretically, the CDI cell can store electrical energy during the desalination process and supply power to electrical devices during the desorption process, thereby achieving energy recovery. To explore the resource-recovery potential of the CDI system, we tested the energy-regeneration performance of K-HEHCF/AC batteries. After adsorption for 30 min at 1.4 V in a 500 mg L-1 NaCl solution, we removed the external voltage and measured the open-circuit voltage of different numbers of CDI cells connected in series (Fig. 6b). The voltage exhibited a gradient increase with the number of CDI units, reaching a maximum of 5.386 V at five CDI units. Since desorption occurred simultaneously upon removal of the external voltage, the open-circuit voltage gradually decreased. After continuous discharge for 60 minutes, the open-circuit voltage retention rate for five units was 52.5% (Supplementary Fig. 40). Additionally, since the rated voltage of light-emitting diode (LED) bulbs is 1.7 V, only desalination cells composed of two or more CDI units can drive it. As shown in Fig. 6c, the instantaneous open-circuit potential upon bulb activation ranges between 1.7 and 1.8 V and gradually decreases over time. During continuous discharge for 40 min from a desalination battery comprising five CDI units, the LED maintained illumination while the open-circuit voltage decreased from 1.774 to 1.621 V, achieving a retention rate of 91.3%. This fully demonstrates its practical application potential (Fig. 6d). The results above demonstrate that the energy release during the desorption process of the CDI cell exhibits stable and continuous characteristics. Furthermore, the CDI cell assembled using K-HEHCF and AC materials effectively achieves the recovery and reuse of electrical energy, providing strong support for the application potential of PBA-based CDI cells in the field of energy recovery.

Discussion

We demonstrated a strategy that combines K substitution and high-entropy engineering for manganese-based Prussian blue, thereby enhancing its conductivity and suppressing its Jahn-Teller effect. Prussian blue synthesized using K complete substitution of Na as a feedstock has the advantages of low water content and structural stability. Meanwhile, we found that the incomplete exchange of Na+/K+ ions during the reaction allows some K+ to remain in the lattice to act as a pillar effect, further stabilizing the framework. The unique high-entropy effect can then confer stronger capacitive performance and alleviate the unfavorable phase transition of Na+ during repeated intercalation/deintercalation in the PBAs lattice, realizing fast and high-capacity ion trapping. The adsorption capacity of this K-HEHCF in 500 mgL-1 NaCl solution was as high as 104.92 mg g-1, while maintaining excellent cycling stability (capacity retention of nearly 100% after 200 cycles). The high adsorption capacity was achieved even in a mixed ion environment simulating circulating cooling water. Meanwhile, in terms of energy recovery, CDI cells assembled with K-HEHCF also demonstrate potential. Our work provides a strategy to address the Jahn-Teller distortion and low conductivity issues in manganese-based Prussian blue, facilitating the development of CDI electrodes and offering insights for other widely used electrochemical applications.

Methods

Material

Manganese chloride (MnCl2, 98%, AR), Ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl2•4H2O, 98%, AR), Cobalt chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2•6H2O, 99%, AR), Nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2•6H2O, 99%, AR), Copper chloride dihydrate (CuCl2•2H2O, 99%, AR), Potassium Citrate (C6H5K3O7, 98%, AR), Potassium ferrocyanide trihyrate (K4Fe(CN)6•3H2O, 99%, AR), Sodium citrate (C6H5Na3O7, 98%, AR), Sodium hexacyanoferrate (Na4Fe(CN)6, 99%, AR) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of HEHCFs

A series of Prussian blue analogs was synthesized by a modified co-precipitation method. Specifically, 2 mmol each of MnCl2, FeCl2•4H2O, CoCl2•6H2O, NiCl2•6H2O, CuCl2•2H2O and 10 mmol of potassium citrate were added to 100 ml of deionized water as solution A, and 15 mmol of K4Fe(CN)6•3H2O was added to 100 ml deionized water as solution B. Solution B was poured into solution A and stirred for 30 min, and then aged at room temperature for 24 h. The product was collected by centrifugation, washed several times with deionized water and ethanol, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C. The procedures for the synthesis of Na-HEHCF are similar to those of K-HEHCF except that Na4Fe(CN)6 and sodium citrate were used instead of K4Fe(CN)6•3H2O and potassium citrate, respectively.

Synthesis of single-metal-center HCFs

The synthesis steps of single-metal-center HCFs are similar with that of HEHCFs, FeHCFs, MnHCFs, CoHCFs, NiHCFs and CuHCFs samples were similarly synthesized by using only FeCl2•4H2O, MnCl2, CoCl2•6H2O, NiCl2•6H2O, CuCl2•2H2O in Solution A, respectively.

Materials characterization

The crystal structure of the samples was investigated via powder XRD using either a Bruker D8 Advance (Cu-Kα1 radiation, λ = 1.54056 Å). The diffraction pattern was first indexed and then refined by the Rietveld method with GSAS software. The morphologys were characterized by SEM (VeriosG4UC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20KV) and TEM (TALOS F200S G2), the SEM samples were prepared using conductive tape. The EDS (X-MAX N80) mapping images were obtained through SEM. The FTIR spectra were recorded with an FTIR spectrometer (FRONTIER). XPS with an Al Kα source was applied to collect the surface information through an ESCALAB XI spectrometer. To perform ex situ XPS analysis, the CDI modules were disassembled after different reaction stages. The resulting electrodes were rinsed with ethanol, carefully scraped, centrifuged, and dried to obtain K-HEHCF powder.

The XAS, including XANES and EXAFS, of the samples at Fe, Mn, Co, Ni and Cu K-edge were collected at the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS) center, where a pair of channel-cut Si (111) crystals was used in the monochromator. TOF-SIMS measurements were conducted on a TOF-SIMS 5. The measurements were conducted using a bismuth (Bi3+) liquid-metal primary ion gun (LMIG). The primary ion source, with short pulses of 20 ns at a voltage of 30 keV, bombarded the target with an average current of 1 pA, and the studied area had a size of 200 × 200 μm2.

Theoretical calculations

The computational analysis was carried out based on DFT, as implemented in Vienna ab initio simulation packages (VASP)82,83. The projector augmented wave (PAW) scheme and Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional were used for geometric optimizations84,85. In the geometric optimization, all atoms were allowed to relax until the calculated Hellmann-Feynman forces were smaller than 0.03 eV Å-1. The kinetic energy cutoff of 520 eV was chosen for the plane wave expansion.
