Role of transcranial Doppler pulsatility index for predicting neurological deterioration in mild-to-moderate traumatic brain injury patients in intensive care unit
Authors: Sachreet Kaur, Nisha Rani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, particularly among young adults in low- and middle-income countries. Even patients presenting with mild-to-moderate TBI may experience secondary neurological deterioration due to evolving intracranial pathology. Early identification of patients at risk remains challenging. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography provides a noninvasive bedside assessment of cerebral hemodynamics, and the pulsatility index (PI) reflects downstream cerebrovascular resistance and intracranial compliance.Methods:This prospective observational study included 100 adult patients with mild-to-moderate TBI admitted to a tertiary care intensive care unit (ICU). Demographic data, mechanism of injury, clinical status, and computed tomography (CT) findings were recorded. TCD examination was performed at admission to measure middle cerebral artery velocities and calculate PI. Patients were followed for seven days. Neurological deterioration was defined as a decrease in Glasgow Coma Scale score by ≥2 points, requirement of mechanical ventilation, or need for neurosurgical intervention. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and multivariate logistic regression were used to assess the predictive value of PI.Results:Neurological deterioration occurred in 22% of patients. Admission PI was significantly higher in patients who deteriorated compared with those who remained stable (1.35 ± 0.18 vs. 1.14 ± 0.17; P < 0.001). ROC analysis demonstrated that PI predicted neurological deterioration with an area under the curve of 0.82 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.73–0.91). A PI cutoff value of ≥1.25 yielded a sensitivity of 77% and a specificity of 80%. On multivariate analysis, admission PI ≥1.25 independently predicted neurological deterioration (adjusted OR 4.3; 95% CI 1.7–10.8).Conclusion:Admission PI measured by TCD is a reliable, noninvasive predictor of early neurological deterioration in patients with mild-to-moderate TBI and may aid early risk stratification in the ICU.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major global health problem and remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability, particularly among younger individuals.[123] More than 50 million people sustain TBI annually worldwide, many of whom require hospitalization, intensive care, or prolonged rehabilitation.[1] The epidemiology of TBI varies across regions. While falls and motor vehicle collisions predominate in high income countries, road traffic accidents account for the majority of cases in low and middle income nations due to inadequate road safety measures and increasing vehicular density.[2345] In India, trauma registry data indicate that approximately one third of trauma related deaths are attributable to TBI.[3] Survivors frequently experience persistent cognitive, behavioral, and physical impairments, resulting in reduced quality of life and substantial socioeconomic burden.[456] Despite advances in neuroimaging and critical care, outcomes in these patients remain unpredictable. Some individuals with relatively preserved Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores deteriorate rapidly, whereas others with severe injuries may recover unexpectedly. This clinical variability underscores the need for tools that can identify patients at risk of early neurological decline.

Conventional monitoring strategies have important limitations. Although GCS remains a cornerstone of neurological assessment, it is influenced by factors, such as sedation, intoxication, and systemic illness. CT provides valuable structural information but may fail to capture dynamic intracranial changes and exposes patients to ionizing radiation. Invasive intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring is considered the reference standard but requires neurosurgical expertise, is not universally available, and carries procedural risks.[21] These challenges highlight the need for noninvasive, bedside techniques capable of detecting evolving intracranial pathology safely and in real time.

Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography offers a portable, noninvasive method for assessing cerebral hemodynamics at the bedside.[7] Among TCD-derived parameters, the pulsatility index (PI) is of particular interest. PI reflects downstream cerebrovascular resistance and is influenced by intracranial compliance. Elevated PI values have been shown to correlate with increased ICP and impaired cerebral perfusion,[1516] making it a potential surrogate marker of adverse intracranial dynamics.

Several studies have demonstrated the prognostic utility of PI in TBI. Bouzat et al.[8] reported that abnormal TCD parameters predicted early neurological worsening even in patients with mild-to-moderate TBI. Zaytoun et al.[9] identified elevated PI as a reliable predictor of poor outcomes in severe TBI. In the Indian population, Prasad et al.[10] demonstrated a strong correlation between abnormal PI values and clinical outcomes in TBI patients. A meta-analysis by Fatima et al.[11] further confirmed consistent associations between elevated PI and mortality or poor neurological recovery. Abdelhaleem et al.[12] also noted improved prognostic accuracy when PI was combined with admission GCS.

Despite this growing body of evidence, most existing studies have focused predominantly on severe TBI, and data specific to mild-to-moderate TBI remain limited. This subgroup represents a clinically important yet vulnerable population. Patients often present with GCS scores between 9 and 13 and may appear stable initially but remain at risk of secondary brain injury due to cerebral edema, hematoma expansion, or impaired autoregulation. Early identification of deterioration in this group is crucial, as timely medical or surgical intervention may prevent irreversible neurological damage or death. However, no universally accepted noninvasive tool currently exists to reliably predict early worsening in these patients.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the predictive significance of admission PI measured by TCD ultrasonography in patients with mild-to-moderate TBI admitted to a tertiary care intensive care unit (ICU) in India. Specifically, we sought to determine whether admission PI could predict early neurological deterioration, prolonged ICU stay, or in-hospital mortality. We hypothesized that PI values would be higher in patients who deteriorated and that PI would demonstrate superior predictive performance compared with raw velocity parameters, such as mean flow velocity (MFV). If validated, PI may serve as a simple, noninvasive bedside tool for early risk stratification, particularly in resource-limited settings where ICP monitoring is not readily available.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective observational study was conducted in the multipurpose ICU of a tertiary care teaching hospital in North India. The study was performed after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from the legally authorized representatives of patients who were unable to provide consent due to their neurological condition. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Study population

A total of 100 consecutive adult patients admitted to the ICU with mild-to-moderate TBI were enrolled. TBI severity was defined using the GCS at admission, and patients with GCS scores between 9 and 15 were included.

Only neurological deterioration was considered; patients requiring ventilation for nonneurological reasons were excluded.

Clinical and radiological assessment

Baseline demographic data, including age, sex, mechanism of injury, and comorbidities, were recorded at admission. Mechanisms of injury were categorized as road traffic accidents, falls, assaults, or other causes.

A detailed neurological assessment was performed on admission using the GCS. Pupillary size and reactivity were documented. Systemic physiological parameters, including heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and oxygen saturation, were recorded concurrently.

All patients underwent a noncontrast CT scan of the head at presentation. Intracranial findings were classified as diffuse cerebral edema, cerebral contusion, subdural hematoma, epidural hematoma, or other lesions. These findings were used to correlate radiological injury patterns with physiological parameters obtained from TCD.

Transcranial Doppler examination

TCD examinations were performed as part of routine ICU assessment by two trained intensivists experienced in neurocritical care ultrasonography. Treating clinicians were aware of TCD findings, and blinding was not feasible due to the observational nature of the study.

The transtemporal acoustic window was used to insonate the middle cerebral artery (MCA) using a 1–4 MHz pulsed Doppler probe. The depth of insonation ranged from 50 to 65 mm. A standardized insonation protocol was followed. Poor acoustic window was defined as the inability to obtain a reproducible MCA waveform despite optimal probe positioning and adjustment of insonation depth and gain.

TCD measurements were obtained from both MCAs. Three consecutive measurements were recorded for each artery, and mean values were calculated to minimize intraobserver variability. For analysis, the higher PI value was used, as it was considered to represent the worst intracranial hemodynamic status.

TCD examinations were performed under routine ICU conditions. Sedative use, vasoactive drug administration, and ventilatory parameters were not standardized at the time of measurement. Hyperventilation was avoided during TCD acquisition whenever clinically feasible.

Follow-up and outcome measures

Patients were monitored clinically and hemodynamically during their ICU stay. The primary outcome was neurological deterioration within seven days of admission. Patients were censored at day seven, and deterioration occurring beyond this period was not included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

Group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive accuracy of PI and MFV for neurological deterioration. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated at the optimal cutoff derived from the ROC curve.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of neurological deterioration. Variables entered into the model included age, admission GCS score, CT lesion type, mechanism of injury, and admission PI. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. A post hoc power analysis based on the observed area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 demonstrated adequate statistical power.

Patient characteristics

A total of 100 patients with mild-to-moderate TBI were included in the study. The age of the patients ranged from 19 to 59 years, with a mean age of 41.6 ± 12.8 years. Males constituted 72% of the study population.

Neurological deterioration

During ICU follow-up, 22 patients (22%) experienced secondary neurological deterioration within the first seven days. Of these, 11 patients required ventilatory support, seven patients underwent urgent neurosurgical intervention (hematoma evacuation or decompression), and six patients (6%) died in hospital. The remaining 78 patients remained neurologically stable.

Transcranial Doppler parameters

The mean admission PI for the entire cohort was 1.18 ± 0.19. Patients who deteriorated had significantly higher baseline PI values compared with those who remained stable (1.35 ± 0.18 vs. 1.14 ± 0.17, P < 0.001).

MFV was significantly lower in the deteriorated group compared with the stable group (52.4 ± 13.6 cm/s vs. 60.4 ± 14.1 cm/s, P = 0.04). Baseline PSV and EDV did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Predictive accuracy of PI

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed that PI predicted neurological deterioration with an AUC of 0.82 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.73–0.91) Figure 1. A PI cutoff value of >1.25 yielded a sensitivity of 77% and a specificity of 80%.

On multivariate logistic regression analysis, admission PI ≥1.25 remained independently associated with neurological deterioration after adjustment for age, admission GCS score, CT lesion type, and mechanism of injury (adjusted OR 4.3; 95% CI 1.7–10.8; P = 0.002).

MFV demonstrated modest predictive accuracy, with an AUC of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.52–0.76).

Secondary outcomes

The mean ICU stay was longer among patients who deteriorated compared with those who remained stable (9.4 ± 2.8 vs. 5.4 ± 1.9 days; P < 0.001). Mortality occurred only in the deterioration group.

Discussion

In patients with mild-to-moderate TBI, this prospective research assessed the predictive significance of PI, as determined by TCD. We discovered that cutoff of PI ≥1.25 had strong sensitivity and specificity for predicting unfavorable outcomes, and that patients who worsened had considerably higher PI levels upon admission than those who were stable. These results reinforce the usefulness of PI as a noninvasive bedside tool to detect evolving intracranial pathology.

Comparison with previous studies

Our study demonstrated that 22% of patients with mild-to-moderate TBI deteriorated during ICU stay. This proportion is consistent with earlier work, showing that even patients who appear clinically stable are at risk of secondary decline due to edema, hematoma expansion, or impaired autoregulation.[8] Bouzat et al.[8] reported similar findings, highlighting that abnormal TCD parameters predicted early worsening in patients with GCS scores in the mild-to-moderate range.

The association of high PI with poor outcomes was demonstrated in several other cohorts. Zaytoun et al.[9] confirmed PI as a reliable prognostic marker in severe TBI. In India, Prasad and colleagues[10] also showed that abnormal PI correlated strongly with outcome, underlining its relevance in our population. Fatima’s[11] meta-analysis further consolidated this evidence, showing consistent associations between elevated PI and mortality or poor neurological recovery. Abdelhaleem et al.[12] noted that combining PI with GCS enhanced predictive accuracy. These findings are in agreement with our results, which showed that PI had better predictive accuracy than MFV alone.

In contrast, MFV was less reliable in our study. Although patients who deteriorated had lower MFV values, its predictive accuracy was modest (AUC 0.64). Transcranial Doppler has also been widely recognized as a useful bedside non-invasive neuromonitoring tool in neurocritical care settings.[13] This is consistent with earlier reports, suggesting that raw velocity values are influenced by systemic factors like blood pressure, cardiac output, as well as arterial CO2 levels, whereas PI more directly reflects cerebrovascular resistance and intracranial compliance.[1415]

In the present study, PI demonstrated better prognostic accuracy than MFV. MFV is known to be influenced by systemic hemodynamic factors such as blood pressure, arterial carbon dioxide levels, sedation, and vasoactive therapy, which may reduce its specificity for intracranial pathology. In contrast, PI reflects distal cerebrovascular resistance and compliance, making it a more robust marker of intracranial dynamics in TBI.

Clinical implications of pulsatility index cutoff

In the present study, a PI cutoff value of ≥1.25 was identified as predictive of neurological deterioration. This threshold is comparable to values reported in earlier studies. Elevated PI values, particularly above 1.2–1.3, were associated with impaired cerebral compliance and raised ICP.[16] The similarity between the identified cutoff in the present study and previously reported thresholds supports the physiological relevance of PI as a marker of adverse intracranial dynamics. However, PI is influenced by multiple physiological and systemic variables and may not always directly correlate with intracranial pressure.[17181920]

From a clinical perspective, an admission PI ≥1.25 may serve as an early warning indicator in patients with mild-to-moderate TBI. Patients exceeding this threshold may benefit from closer neurological monitoring, early repeat neuroimaging, and timely escalation of therapy, even in the absence of initial clinical deterioration. Incorporation of PI into routine ICU assessment may therefore aid risk stratification and guide proactive management decisions.

It is important to interpret these findings with appropriate caution. Although elevated admission PI was independently associated with neurological deterioration, the present observational study does not establish a causal relationship. PI should be regarded as a physiological marker reflecting underlying intracranial dynamics rather than a direct cause of clinical worsening. The observed associations likely represent the impact of evolving intracranial pathology on cerebral hemodynamics.

Physiological interpretation

The pathophysiological explanation for elevated PI in patients who deteriorate lies in its relationship between ICP and cerebrovascular resistance. PI increases when diastolic flow decreases disproportionately compared to systolic flow. This pattern typically occurs when ICP rises or when cerebral compliance is impaired.[1516] In simple terms, PI functions as an indirect marker of ICP and cerebral perfusion. Thus, its elevation at admission signals that the brain is already under hemodynamic stress, even when clinical signs are not yet apparent.

Clinical implications

There are several clinical implications of our results. First, PI may be used at the bedside as a reproducible, noninvasive monitoring technique. In contrast to intrusive ICP monitoring, which is expensive and not always accessible, TCD can be performed quickly and safely, even in resource limited settings. Several ultrasound based non invasive ICP assessment techniques have been studied with variable reliability in neurocritical care.[212223242526] Second, PI abnormalities can serve as an early warning signal, allowing clinicians to intensify monitoring, repeat imaging earlier, or initiate therapies such as osmotic agents, hyperventilation, or surgical decompression before irreversible deterioration occurs.

Third, in centers where invasive ICP monitoring is available, PI can complement other modalities as part of multimodal monitoring. It provides physiological data that may guide therapeutic decisions alongside clinical examination and imaging. Finally, in countries like India, where the burden of TBI is high and resources unevenly distributed, incorporating PI into ICU protocols could help bridge gaps in monitoring and improve triage decisions.

In the present study, neurological deterioration was defined based on clinical worsening and need for escalation of care during ICU stay. However, a detailed stratification of deteriorated patients based on surgical versus medical management was not systematically performed. Similarly, although deaths occurred among patients with neurological deterioration, attribution exclusively to intracranial pathology versus systemic causes could not be definitively established.

Strengths and limitations

Our study’s prospective design, standardized TCD assessments, and emphasis on patients with mild-to-moderate TBI—a demographic that received little attention—are among its strong points. Our results are particularly pertinent since these individuals often slip into a “grey zone” where clinical care is difficult. Lack of blinding may have introduced management bias, as awareness of elevated PI could have influenced clinical vigilance or early intervention. single center, small sample, no long-term follow-up, and TCD dependency. MFV was assessed only as a single admission measurement, and serial trend analysis was not performed. As MFV is susceptible to systemic physiological variations, its prognostic role should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating standardized serial MFV measurements may provide additional prognostic insight.

MFV demonstrated lower predictive accuracy than PI. Absolute flow velocities are influenced by systemic hemodynamic factors, such as blood pressure, arterial carbon dioxide levels, sedation, and vasoactive therapy, which may reduce their specificity for intracranial pathology. In contrast, PI reflects downstream cerebrovascular resistance and intracranial compliance.

PI was assessed as a single admission measurement in the present study, and serial trend analysis was not performed. Although dynamic changes in PI over time may provide additional prognostic information, the study was designed to evaluate admission PI as an early risk stratification tool for neurological deterioration. Future studies incorporating serial PI measurements may further enhance prognostic accuracy.

Nonetheless, it is necessary to recognize some limits. The research’s generalizability may be constrained by its small sample size and single-center approach. TCD is operator dependent, and although standard techniques were followed, interobserver variability cannot be ruled out.

Hemodynamic and physiological variables, such as blood pressure, arterial carbon dioxide levels, sedation, and vasoactive drug use, were not systematically recorded at the exact time of TCD measurement. As these factors can influence cerebral blood flow velocities and PI values, their absence may have acted as potential confounders. However, TCD examinations were performed under routine ICU conditions, reflecting real-world clinical practice. Future studies with standardized hemodynamic control at the time of TCD assessment may further refine the prognostic accuracy of these parameters.

Despite the use of a standardized protocol and trained operators, the operator-dependent nature of TCD remains a limitation, and formal interoperator agreement analysis was not performed.

Outcomes were limited to in-hospital deterioration and mortality; long-term neurological outcomes, such as functional status at three or six months, were not assessed. In addition, systemic hemodynamic variables and ventilatory status can influence TCD values, though we attempted to minimize these confounders with strict inclusion criteria.

The absence of long-term follow-up outcomes was acknowledged as a limitation.

Future directions

Future research should aim at larger, multicenter trials to validate the PI thresholds observed in our study. Studies could also explore whether PI-guided interventions lead to improved outcomes compared with standard care. Combining PI with other noninvasive tools—such as optic nerve sheath diameter, near-infrared spectroscopy, or automated pupillometry—may further enhance predictive accuracy. Advances in portable ultrasound devices and structured training programs could also facilitate wider adoption of TCD monitoring in ICUs.[27282930]

Outcomes beyond ICU stay—such as hospital discharge status and 30-day mortality—were not recorded. Therefore, the prognostic value of admission PI for long-term neurological and survival outcomes could not be assessed.

Future prospective studies should include standardized documentation of management strategies and longer-term outcomes to better define the clinical impact of PI-guided risk stratification and should also focus on structured training programs and standardized protocols for TCD acquisition in ICUs to improve reproducibility and wider clinical adoption.

Overall interpretation

In summary, our study supports the role of TCD-derived PI as a valuable predictor of neurological deterioration in mild-to-moderate TBI. Elevated PI at admission was strongly associated with worsening clinical status, longer ICU stay, and mortality. While further validation is needed, these findings suggest that PI could be integrated into routine ICU monitoring protocols. Its ability to provide real-time, noninvasive insights into cerebral hemodynamics makes it a promising tool for early risk stratification, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

This research shows that a straightforward, noninvasive, and accurate indicator for anticipating early decline in patients with mild-to-moderate TBI is TCD-derived PI. Patients with elevated PI at admission were more likely to worsen neurologically, require intensive interventions, and had longer ICU stays and higher mortality. In contrast, raw velocity parameters, such as MFV, were less useful. These findings align with earlier reports,[891011121516] and highlight PI’s potential as a bedside tool to complement clinical assessment and imaging. Increased use of PI monitoring might aid in early determination in patients with high risk and enhance results, especially in environments with limited resources.
