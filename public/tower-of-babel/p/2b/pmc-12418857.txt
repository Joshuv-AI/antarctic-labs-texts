Correspondence regarding “excess adiposity and cancer: evaluating a preclinical-clinical obesity framework for risk stratification”
Authors: Gang Hu, Yun Shen
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the recent article on the risk stratification based on the newly proposed definition of preclinical and clinical obesity for the risks of incident cancer cases by Leitzmann et al., using the data from the UK biobank.1 The authors identified 3 groups according to the Lancet classification: no obesity, preclinical obesity, and clinical obesity. Additionally for comparison, the authors used BMI solely based definition for adiposity. They concluded that preclinical obesity was related to increased cancer risk even in the absence of organ dysfunction, while clinical obesity might confer greater risk of cancer.

The current BMI-based definition alone may underestimate or overestimate adiposity, providing insufficient information regarding individual health status. Therefore, the Lancet commissioners have introduced a new definition of clinical obesity designed to better inform clinical decision-making and policy development, facilitating the prioritization of clinical interventions and public health strategies.2 We commend the authors for their efforts to address the gap concerning how this new definition relates specifically to cancer risk. Nevertheless, we have identified several important concerns regarding the grouping strategy used in this study.

Firstly, according to the Lancet Commission's definition and diagnostic criteria for clinical obesity, the presence of excess body fat should be confirmed by at least one additional anthropometric measure (e.g., waist circumference) or by direct fat measurement when available, in conjunction with body mass index (BMI). Moreover, individuals with very high BMI levels (e.g., >40 kg/m2) could reasonably be assumed to have excess adiposity. We noticed that the authors have excluded those with BMI <25 kg/m2 but a waist circumference >102 cm (men) or >88 cm (women) from the analysis. However, it appeared to us that individuals with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 but normal waist circumference might have been incorrectly classified as having ‘no obesity’. Based on our understanding, these individuals should instead be categorized differently as ‘others’, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Secondly, the authors used BMI ≥25 kg/m2 as the threshold for defining obesity. However, we believe that the Lancet commissioners intended elevated BMI, in the context of clinical obesity, to refer specifically to a BMI of ≥30 kg/m2 accompanied by an additional measure indicating excess adiposity.

Finally, given the comprehensive availability of data on other anthropometric measures such as height and hip circumference, it would be appropriate to consider alternative measures such as waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to-height ratio, or even percentage body fat assessed by Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA), as alternatives to waist circumference alone in determining excess adiposity.

In conclusion, using BMI ≥25 kg/m2 instead of BMI ≥30 kg/m2 as well as the inclusion of those who we believe should be categorized as others would significantly bias the analysis. We recently published a similar study demonstrating the dynamic phenotypes of preclinical and clinical obesity in relation to new-onset cancer risk.4
