Artificial intelligence for urodynamic studies: systematic review of methods, performance, and clinical applications
Authors: Apisith Saraluck, Jittima Manonai, Athasit Kijmanawat, Rujira Wattanayingcharoenchai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveInterpretation of urodynamic studies (UDS) is essential for the assessment of lower urinary tract dysfunction but remains resource-intensive and highly operator-dependent. Artificial intelligence (AI) has increasingly been applied to support urodynamic interpretation; however, the available evidence is heterogeneous and its clinical relevance remains uncertain. This systematic review aimed to synthesise current evidence on AI applications in urodynamic assessment and to summarise diagnostic performance and methodological quality.MethodsA systematic literature search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020. Studies applying AI to uroflowmetry, cystometry, or pressure–flow studies in human participants were included. Eligible studies were categorised according to data representation as parameter-based, raw signal–based, or image-based models. Diagnostic performance was summarised narratively, and risk of bias was assessed using the QUADAS-AI framework.Key findings and limitationsEighteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Parameter-based models generally demonstrated modest to good diagnostic performance, particularly for binary classification tasks. Raw signal–based models more frequently reported high discriminative performance, with several studies achieving area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values above 0.85 and, in some cases, exceeding 0.90. Image-based models demonstrated feasibility for automated interpretation of urodynamic traces, although reported performance varied substantially. Most studies were retrospective and single-centre, relied on expert-defined reference standards, and lacked external validation, resulting in frequent high or unclear risk of bias.Conclusions and clinical implicationsArtificial intelligence has been widely applied to urodynamic assessment and often demonstrates high diagnostic performance in research settings. However, substantial methodological limitations limit confidence in clinical generalisability. At present, AI-assisted urodynamic interpretation should be considered exploratory, and robust prospective studies with independent external validation are required before routine clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03592-0.

Introduction

Urodynamic study (UDS) is a cornerstone investigation for the functional assessment of lower urinary tract dysfunction, providing objective information on bladder storage, detrusor contractility, outlet resistance, and coordination during voiding [1]. Despite its established clinical value, UDS is invasive, time-consuming, and resource-intensive, and its interpretation remains highly dependent on operator expertise [2]. Visual assessment of urodynamic traces is subject to interobserver variability, particularly in complex or borderline cases, which may limit reproducibility and confidence in clinical decision-making. In response to these challenges, artificial intelligence (AI)–based approaches have been increasingly explored as tools to assist urodynamic interpretation. Early studies focused on applying machine learning to derived urodynamic parameters, while more recent work has extended to the analysis of raw physiological signals and image-based representations of urodynamic traces [3]. Proposed applications include automated classification of bladder outlet obstruction, detrusor overactivity, detrusor underactivity, and voiding patterns, as well as recognition of typical urodynamic signals during testing. Collectively, these studies reflect growing interest in the potential role of AI to support consistency and efficiency in urodynamic assessment [4]. In addition to diagnostic interpretation itself, the quality of urodynamic signal acquisition is fundamental to reliable analysis. Common technical issues, including pressure drift, catheter displacement, poor subtraction, cough-test inadequacy, and motion or abdominal artifacts, may substantially affect trace interpretation and diagnostic validity [5]. These challenges contribute to interobserver variability and may also influence the reliability of datasets used for artificial intelligence model development. Accordingly, emerging AI applications may have potential roles not only in automated diagnostic support, but also in urodynamic quality control and artifact recognition [6].

However, the existing literature on AI in urodynamics is heterogeneous and fragmented. Studies differ substantially in data representation, analytical approach, clinical targets, and reported performance metrics, making direct comparison difficult [7]. Moreover, many investigations rely on single-center retrospective datasets, expert-defined reference standards, and limited validation strategies, raising concerns regarding methodological quality and risk of bias [8]. As a result, the extent to which current AI-based approaches are ready for clinical integration remains unclear. Although prior reviews have discussed artificial intelligence applications in urology and lower urinary tract dysfunction, few have specifically focused on artificial intelligence applications in urodynamic studies using a structured comparison of data representations together with formal methodological appraisal [9, 10].

Therefore, this systematic review aims to summarize and critically appraise the current evidence on the use of artificial intelligence in urodynamic interpretation. We classify published studies into three categories according to urodynamic data representation (parameter-based models, raw time-series or signal-based models, and image-based models) and summarize their reported diagnostic performance. In addition, we assess methodological quality and risk of bias using the QUADAS-AI framework [11]. By integrating performance outcomes with structured quality assessment, this review seeks to clarify the current evidence base and identify key limitations relevant to future clinical translation of AI-assisted urodynamics.

Methods

Study design and reporting standards

This study was conducted as a systematic review following a predefined protocol and PRISMA 2020 guidance [12]. The review protocol was developed a priori and registered in PROSPERO (PROSPERO Registration number CRD420251247778) [13]. Eligibility criteria, search strategy, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment methods were predefined. Owing to substantial heterogeneity in study populations, target conditions, AI inputs, reference standards, and reported performance metrics, findings were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively pooled. For this review, AI/ML approaches were interpreted to include machine-learning and deep-learning methods, while borderline conventional predictive models were described conservatively and interpreted separately where appropriate.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in four electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore. Search terms combined concepts related to urodynamic studies and artificial intelligence, including machine learning and deep learning. Database-specific search strategies were adapted for each platform and are detailed in Supplementary Appendix 1. The final search was conducted on 9th December 2025. Reference lists of included studies were also manually screened to identify additional eligible articles.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they included human participants undergoing uroflowmetry or urodynamic testing for assessment of lower urinary tract function; applied artificial intelligence, machine learning, or closely related algorithmic pattern-recognition approaches to urodynamic data; used real human urodynamic inputs, including derived parameters, raw physiological time-series signals, or image-based representations of urodynamic traces; and reported diagnostic, classification, or interpretation-related outcomes relevant to urodynamic assessment.

Studies were excluded if they applied AI exclusively to non-urodynamic data or conditions unrelated to urodynamic function; were based solely on simulated, synthetic, or non-human datasets; focused exclusively on fluoroscopic or video-urodynamic studies; involved populations not relevant to the review question; were review articles, editorials, or conference abstracts lacking sufficient methodological detail; or were published in languages other than English.

Study selection

After removal of duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers. Full-text articles were subsequently assessed for eligibility using the predefined criteria. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion and consensus. The study selection process is presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). We included studies explicitly applying algorithmic prediction or pattern-recognition methods presented by the original authors as AI- or machine learning–based approaches. Conventional regression-based models, where included, were described cautiously to avoid overstating them as modern AI methods.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form guided by PRISMA and Cochrane principles [14]. Extracted data included study characteristics, urodynamic modality, type of data input, AI approach, target clinical or urodynamic task, reported diagnostic performance metrics, and key methodological characteristics. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Classification of AI approaches

Included studies were classified into three groups according to the representation of urodynamic data used as input for AI models:

Parameter-based models, using engineered or derived urodynamic parameters;Raw signal–based models, analyzing raw physiological time-series data;Image-based models, applying AI to image representations of urodynamic traces or graphs.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias and methodological quality were assessed using the QUADAS-AI framework. Four domains were evaluated: patient selection, index test (AI model), reference standard, and flow and timing. Risk-of-bias assessment was conducted independently by two reviewers using predefined criteria. Domain-level judgments of low, unclear, or high risk of bias were assigned based on prespecified decision rules (Supplementary Appendix 2). Assessments were conducted independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis

Due to heterogeneity in AI methods, data representations, diagnostic targets, and performance metrics, results were synthesized narratively. Diagnostic performance was summarized as reported in individual studies without pooling of effect estimates. Findings are presented stratified by data representation category, with emphasis on methodological quality and consistency of evidence.

Results

Study selection

The literature search identified 910 records across four databases. After the removal of 328 duplicates, 582 records were screened by title and abstract. Sixty-five full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 47 were excluded for predefined reasons. Eighteen studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (Fig. 1).

Overview of included studies and outcomes

The 18 included studies were published between 2009 and 2025 and predominantly used retrospective, single-center datasets. The characteristics of included studies, including study design, sample size, input modality, AI approach, model evaluation strategy, and key limitations, are summarised in Table 1. Artificial intelligence was applied across the urodynamic assessment spectrum, including uroflowmetry alone, invasive multichannel urodynamic studies, or combinations of both. Across studies, AI models were developed to address a range of diagnostic and classification tasks, most commonly bladder outlet obstruction, detrusor overactivity, detrusor underactivity, abnormal uroflow patterns, or composite voiding dysfunction. Reported outcomes varied substantially, with most studies presenting diagnostic accuracy metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), where an AUC of 0.5 indicates no discrimination and 1.0 perfect discrimination. Sample sizes, reference standards, and validation strategies differed widely between studies.

AI approaches by data representation

Parameter-based models

Parameter-based models used engineered or derived urodynamic features as inputs, derived primarily from uroflowmetry or pressure–flow studies. Typical variables included maximum flow rate, voided volume, pressure-derived indices, and composite parameters based on established diagnostic frameworks. These models were generally developed using conventional machine-learning or predictive modelling approaches, including artificial neural networks, support vector machines, decision trees, and related classifiers. Earlier work by Altunay et al. (2009) demonstrated the feasibility of applying artificial neural networks to engineered uroflowmetry features for automated uroflow pattern classification [15]. More recent studies demonstrated the feasibility for diagnostic classification using support vector machines, neural networks, and related predictive models. Hobbs et al. (2022) applied machine-learning models to pressure-derived time- and frequency-domain variables from invasive urodynamic recordings for the detection of detrusor overactivity, reporting area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of approximately 0.90 using support vector machines [16]. In female populations, Zhou et al. (2024) used a back-propagation neural network incorporating nine urodynamic features to diagnose female BOO, achieving an AUC of 0.95 with high sensitivity and specificity [17]. Zhang et al. (2025) proposed automated cystometry evaluation using local linear modelling for detection of detrusor overactivity in spinal cord injury populations [18]. Studies addressing detrusor underactivity also demonstrated moderate to good performance; for example, Matsukawa et al. (2023) used an interpretable predictive modelling approach based on expert-defined uroflowmetry parameters associated with detrusor contractility, reporting AUC values around 0.85 [19]. Ding et al. (2024) further developed machine-learning models using structured urodynamic parameters for automated diagnosis of multiple lower urinary tract dysfunctions [20]. Arslan et al. (2025) applied multiple machine-learning algorithms to pediatric uroflowmetry pattern interpretation and demonstrated acceptable classification performance [21]. More recently, Wang et al. (2025) explored large language model–based interpretation of structured urodynamic parameters and reported high diagnostic performance, with Deepseek-R1 achieving an accuracy of 92.5% and AUC values up to 0.92 [22].

Across studies, reported diagnostic performance varied substantially depending on the diagnostic target, feature set, and classification strategy. When AUC was reported, values generally ranged from approximately 0.70 to the high-0.80s, with higher performance typically observed in binary classification tasks compared with multi-class models that included borderline or equivocal categories. Cohort sizes ranged from fewer than 100 to several hundred patients. Model evaluation was most commonly based on internal validation strategies, such as cross-validation or random train–test splits, and external validation using independent datasets was rarely described.

Raw signal–based models

Raw signal–based models analyzed urodynamic data directly as physiological time-series signals, including uroflowmetry curves and multichannel pressure recordings obtained during cystometry or pressure–flow studies. These approaches avoided predefined feature engineering and instead relied on data-driven representation learning. Most studies in this category employed deep-learning architectures, including convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, or transformer-based models. Several studies demonstrated that uroflowmetry waveforms alone contain diagnostically relevant temporal information. Matsukawa et al. (2021) applied a neural network to one-dimensional uroflowmetry waveforms to estimate bladder contractility index and bladder outlet obstruction index, reporting moderate correlations with pressure–flow study–derived indices (r = 0.60 and r = 0.46, respectively; both p < 0.001) [23]. Using flow morphology alone, the model achieved an overall diagnostic accuracy of approximately 84% for differentiating detrusor underactivity and bladder outlet obstruction, outperforming visual interpretation by urologists. Similarly, waveform-based approaches reported high accuracy for classifying normal and abnormal voiding patterns using uroflowmetry time-series data [24]. Raw signal–based models applied to invasive multichannel urodynamic recordings frequently focused on event-level or pattern-level detection. Wang et al. (2021) treated detrusor overactivity as a recurring signal motif and applied machine-learning–based pattern recognition to pressure recordings, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of approximately 0.84; however, this estimate was derived from internal cross-validation without independent external validation, which limits confidence in generalisability [25]. Building on this concept, Zhou et al. (2023) used convolutional neural networks with event segmentation to identify detrusor overactivity, reporting high sensitivity at the patient level, although specificity varied [26]. More recent studies employed advanced architectures to model temporal dependencies across multiple pressure channels. Similarly, Ge et al. (2025) introduced a multi-sequence fusion network for classification of detrusor overactivity and bladder compliance–related abnormalities using multichannel urodynamic pressure signals, achieving AUC values above 0.95 for detrusor overactivity detection [24]. In studies applying frequency-domain transformations, Mei et al. (2025) combined short-time Fourier transforms with convolutional neural networks, reporting AUC values up to approximately 0.94 for bladder outlet obstruction and detrusor underactivity, with preserved performance in external validation cohorts [27]. Pong et al. (2022) explored deep-learning analysis of vibration-derived uroflow surrogate signals and reported classification accuracies approaching 98% for multiple voiding patterns [28]. For Mei et al. (2025), the reference standard was insufficiently described, limiting the interpretability of the reported performance and raising concerns regarding reference standard validity.

Across studies reporting AUC, discriminative performance was frequently high, commonly exceeding 0.85 and, in several cases, above 0.90 for binary classification tasks. Dataset sizes ranged from several hundred to several thousand urodynamic recordings. Despite these encouraging results, model evaluation was predominantly limited to internal validation strategies, and independent external or temporal validation was infrequently reported.

Image-based models

Image-based models applied artificial intelligence to visual representations of urodynamic data, such as plotted uroflowmetry curves or pressure–flow graphs captured as images. These approaches treated urodynamic traces as visual patterns and primarily employed convolutional neural networks or object-detection frameworks for classification or pattern recognition. Several studies explored the feasibility of diagnosing lower urinary tract dysfunction using static images of uroflowmetry graphs alone. Bang et al. (2022) applied a convolutional neural network to simple uroflowmetry curve images from 1,792 male patients to predict detrusor underactivity and bladder outlet obstruction without using pressure data [29]. The best-performing model achieved area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of approximately 0.72–0.73 for both conditions, with sensitivity and specificity in the range of 65–70%. Visualization using gradient-based attention mapping demonstrated that the model focused on clinically relevant regions of the flow curve, supporting internal validity. These findings suggest that graphical flow morphology alone contains diagnostically relevant information, although performance remained insufficient for standalone diagnosis. Other image-based studies focused on real-time recognition of predefined urodynamic signal patterns during active testing rather than post-hoc diagnostic classification. Liu et al. (2025) developed a real-time deep learning system using an object-detection framework (YOLOv5) to identify typical urodynamic signal patterns, including cough test quality, detrusor overactivity, rectal peristalsis, and abdominal straining, from live multichannel urodynamic images [6]. Using more than 2,600 annotated urodynamic images, the model achieved mean average precision values up to approximately 0.89 and F1 scores between 0.81 and 0.84, with inference times compatible with real-time deployment. This approach primarily targeted quality control and pattern recognition during urodynamic acquisition rather than disease classification.

Across image-based studies, reported diagnostic performance varied substantially depending on the task definition, image generation method, and annotation strategy. Sample sizes were generally smaller than those in raw signal-based studies, and most investigations were single-centre with internal validation only. Differences in image preprocessing, labeling granularity, and evaluation workflows limited direct comparison across studies, and external validation was uncommon.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the QUADAS-AI framework across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Overall, high or unclear risk of bias was common among the included studies, with relatively few studies judged as low risk across all domains (Fig. 2A and B).

In the patient selection domain, the majority of studies were judged to be at high risk of bias, primarily due to retrospective study designs, non-consecutive or convenience sampling, and restricted or highly selected study populations. Only a small proportion of studies reported prospective enrollment or clearly described inclusion of consecutive patients, resulting in a high prevalence of selection-related concerns.

For the index test domain, high risk of bias was most frequently observed. Most studies did not clearly prespecify model decision thresholds, and many lacked adequate separation between training, validation, and testing datasets. In several studies, model development and performance evaluation were performed on overlapping datasets or without sufficient detail regarding internal validation procedures, leading to concerns regarding overfitting and optimistic performance estimates.

Regarding the reference standard, most studies were judged as having unclear risk of bias. Reference standards were typically based on expert interpretation of urodynamic studies or established diagnostic criteria, such as pressure–flow nomograms, with inconsistent reporting of blinding between the index test and reference standard. Only a minority of studies explicitly stated that reference standard assessment was conducted independently of AI model outputs.

In the flow and timing domain, most studies were assessed as having unclear risk of bias. While complete case inclusion was often implied, details regarding patient exclusions, handling of missing data, and timing between index tests and reference standard assessments were frequently insufficiently reported. As a result, the potential impact of incomplete data flow or selective exclusions could not be fully assessed.

Domain-level distributions of risk-of-bias judgments are summarized in Fig. 2A and B, highlighting that concerns were most prominent in the patient selection and index test domains, whereas low risk of bias was least frequently observed across all domains.

Discussion

In this systematic review, we synthesised the current evidence on artificial intelligence applications across the urodynamic assessment spectrum, encompassing uroflowmetry and invasive urodynamic studies. The results demonstrate that AI-based approaches have been applied to a wide range of diagnostic tasks and data representations, with many studies reporting high discriminative performance. However, substantial heterogeneity in study design, data representation, validation strategies, and methodological quality limits the interpretability and clinical generalisability of these findings [16, 24, 27, 29]. Our findings complement prior broader AI-in-urology reviews by specifically focusing on urodynamic data modalities, methodological quality, and translational readiness.

Beyond cataloguing performance, an important insight from this review is that different data representations may imply different translational roles for AI in urodynamics. Unsupervised machine-learning approaches have also been explored in urodynamics. For example, van Dort et al. (2025) applied clustering analysis to pressure–flow study patterns and identified previously unrecognised subtypes associated with detrusor contraction and prostate characteristics, suggesting a potential future role for AI in phenotype discovery rather than solely diagnostic prediction [30]. Parameter-based approaches may support triage or simplified prediction, raw signal models may support automated pattern recognition, and image-based models may enable scalable interpretation support. Viewed in this way, current studies suggest emerging decision-support use cases, although evidence remains insufficient for deployment.

A central observation of this review is that reported performance varies systematically according to the representation of urodynamic data used as model input. Parameter-based models, which rely on engineered features derived from conventional urodynamic indices, generally achieved modest to good performance, particularly for binary classification tasks [17]. In contrast, raw signal–based models more frequently reported high discriminative performance, with several studies achieving AUC values exceeding 0.85 and, in some cases, above 0.90 [22, 23, 27]. Image-based models demonstrated feasibility for automated classification of urodynamic patterns, although reported performance and study design varied widely [29]. These findings suggest that AI models capable of learning directly from physiological signals or visual patterns may capture information beyond conventional summary parameters; however, differences in study populations, reference standards, and validation approaches preclude direct comparison across categories. One possible explanation for the comparatively lower performance of parameter-based models is that handcrafted urodynamic indices may compress complex physiological information and may fail to preserve temporal or spatial patterns contained in raw signals or trace images. In contrast, raw signal– and image-based approaches may capture richer representations of pressure dynamics and waveform morphology, potentially supporting improved pattern recognition.

Despite encouraging performance metrics, the methodological quality of the available evidence remains a major concern. Risk-of-bias assessment using QUADAS-AI revealed frequent limitations across all domains, particularly in patient selection and index test evaluation [31]. Most studies were retrospective and single-centre, with non-consecutive sampling and limited transparency in model development. Validation was predominantly internal, and independent external or temporal validation was uncommon. As a result, reported performance metrics may overestimate true diagnostic accuracy when applied to new populations or clinical settings.

From a machine-learning perspective, several technical patterns may help explain the observed variation in performance across studies. Models trained on raw urodynamic signals or visual representations can directly learn temporal and morphological characteristics that may be compressed or lost when data are reduced to predefined parameters. Convolutional architectures are well-suited for recognising local spatial or temporal patterns in curve or image representations, whereas transformer-based models may preserve longer-range temporal relationships in multichannel urodynamic recordings through attention mechanisms [3].

However, attribution of performance differences to specific architectures remains challenging. Variability in preprocessing pipelines, annotation granularity, class-imbalance handling, and validation design likely contributes substantially to reported performance. In particular, inconsistent separation of training and evaluation data and post hoc threshold optimisation may result in optimistic performance estimates [32]. These technical considerations highlight the need for more transparent reporting and standardised evaluation frameworks in future AI-based urodynamic research.

Importantly, many studies relied on expert-defined reference standards or established diagnostic indices as ground truth, reflecting current clinical practice in urodynamics [16]. While this approach is pragmatic, it also introduces circularity, as AI models are trained to reproduce existing interpretation patterns rather than independently validated physiological truth [25]. This reliance on expert annotation highlights a fundamental challenge in AI-assisted urodynamics: improvements in model performance do not necessarily translate into improved diagnostic validity or clinical decision-making [33].

From a clinical perspective, the diversity of AI applications identified in this review underscores both the potential and the current limitations of AI in urodynamics. High reported performance in controlled research settings suggests that AI may support standardisation, reduce interobserver variability, and assist interpretation in selected tasks. However, the absence of robust external validation, limited reporting of workflow integration, and lack of prospective clinical evaluation restrict conclusions regarding real-world clinical readiness. At present, AI in urodynamics should be viewed primarily as an adjunctive research tool rather than a mature clinical diagnostic solution.

This review has several strengths. We applied a structured, data-representation–based framework to categorise AI approaches, enabling comparison across heterogeneous studies. In addition, use of the QUADAS-AI tool allowed systematic appraisal of methodological quality tailored to AI-based diagnostic research. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures precluded quantitative meta-analysis. Because included studies addressed distinct conditions (e.g., DO, DU, BOO), reported performance metrics should not be interpreted as directly comparable across tasks. Second, restriction to English-language publications may have led to the omission of relevant studies. Finally, rapid evolution of AI methodologies means that newer approaches may not yet be fully represented in the published literature. Although this review applied systematic methods for study identification, selection, extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment, the evidence base was too heterogeneous to support quantitative synthesis; therefore, the review should be interpreted as a systematic review with narrative synthesis. The included studies also varied substantially in patient populations and technical acquisition protocols. Furthermore, reporting of catheter systems and other procedural details was inconsistent across studies and could not be systematically assessed, representing an additional limitation of the evidence base. Reporting of preprocessing quality assessment and exclusion of low-quality traces was also inconsistent across studies, representing an additional source of uncertainty and underscoring the need for improved reporting standards in future AI studies.

A key finding of this review is that apparently strong diagnostic performance was often reported despite important methodological constraints. Most studies relied on retrospective single-centre development cohorts and internal validation, while independent external validation remained uncommon. Accordingly, reported performance metrics should be interpreted cautiously and not assumed to represent clinical readiness. Future studies should align more closely with contemporary AI evaluation principles, including transparent dataset reporting, rigorous separation of development and evaluation data, and external validation. Given the rapid evolution of AI methodologies, some newer approaches may emerge beyond the search date despite efforts to provide a contemporary synthesis.

An important issue highlighted is that artificial intelligence for urodynamic interpretation cannot be considered independently of signal quality. Most published studies focused on diagnostic classification tasks, whereas comparatively few addressed automated identification of poor-quality traces, technical artifacts, or technically inadequate studies before interpretation. This represents an important translational gap, as artifact-prone or low-quality data may introduce bias into both model training and inference. Future AI applications in urodynamics should therefore incorporate automated quality-control assessment as part of clinically credible decision-support pipelines.

Future research in this field should prioritise prospective study designs, transparent reporting of model development, and rigorous external validation using independent cohorts. Standardisation of outcome definitions and reporting metrics would facilitate comparison across studies and improve evidence synthesis. Moreover, evaluation of AI models within clinical workflows, including assessment of usability, interpretability, and impact on decision-making, will be essential before widespread clinical adoption can be considered.

Although current evidence does not support routine clinical implementation, AI may have future roles in standardising urodynamic interpretation, supporting training, reducing interobserver variability, and potentially assisting decision support in specialised settings. These potential clinical applications represent an important rationale for continued rigorous evaluation.

Beyond diagnostic classification, future research may increasingly focus on artificial intelligence applications for automated quality assurance in urodynamics, including artifact detection, signal-quality assessment, and identification of technically inadequate studies [34]. Such developments may improve both reliability of clinical interpretation and robustness of AI training datasets. Potential future decision-support applications may include triage support from uroflowmetry, real-time signal-quality control during testing, and automated recognition of characteristic urodynamic patterns. However, before such tools could credibly influence clinical decisions, evidence from multicentre external validation, transparent reporting, prospective evaluation, and demonstration of clinical utility would likely be required.

In conclusion, artificial intelligence has been widely applied to urodynamic data with generally high reported diagnostic performance, particularly in studies using raw signal–based approaches. Nevertheless, methodological limitations and high risk of bias are prevalent, and current evidence does not yet support routine clinical implementation. Addressing these limitations through robust study design and validation will be critical to realising the potential of AI-assisted urodynamic interpretation.

Conclusion

This systematic review shows that artificial intelligence has been widely applied to urodynamic assessment, including uroflowmetry and invasive urodynamic studies, with many studies reporting generally favorable diagnostic performance. Approaches using raw physiological signals or image-based representations frequently achieved high discriminative metrics.

However, the current evidence is limited by substantial methodological heterogeneity and frequent risk of bias, with most studies relying on retrospective, single-centre datasets and limited validation. As a result, high reported performance does not yet support routine clinical implementation.

Further prospective studies with rigorous external validation and standardised reporting are required to define the clinical role of AI-assisted urodynamic interpretation.
