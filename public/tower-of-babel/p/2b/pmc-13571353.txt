Mechanisms of Exertional Presyncope/Syncope in Hemodynamically Significant Myocardial Bridging With Concomitant Coronary Vasospasm

In patients with exertional presyncope/syncope, comprehensive invasive physiological and vasomotor assessment unmasked hemodynamically significant myocardial bridging and concomitant epicardial coronary spasm, enabling appropriate diagnosis and successful treatment with diltiazem. CTA = computed tomography angiography; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MB = myocardial bridging; Pd/Pa = distal coronary pressure-to-aortic pressure ratio; RFR, resting full-cycle ratio.

Myocardial bridging (MB) has traditionally been regarded as a benign congenital coronary anomaly; however, accumulating evidence suggests that MB may contribute to myocardial ischemia through multiple mechanisms, including impaired diastolic coronary perfusion and coronary vasomotor dysfunction. In some patients, these pathophysiological abnormalities may manifest not only as anginal symptoms but also as exertional presyncope or syncope, potentially reflecting transient severe myocardial ischemia and hemodynamic compromise. Furthermore, MB is increasingly recognized to be associated with epicardial coronary spasm, which may further exacerbate ischemia and increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular events. We report 2 cases of hemodynamically significant MB associated with concomitant coronary vasospasm, presenting with exertional presyncope or syncope, in which comprehensive invasive physiological and vasomotor assessments were instrumental in elucidating the underlying mechanisms.

Case 1

A 61-year-old man had experienced episodic chest discomfort over the preceding 3 years, occasionally accompanied by exertional presyncope. These presyncopal episodes typically resolved spontaneously within a few minutes. He initially sought evaluation at a local clinic, where ambulatory Holter electrocardiographic monitoring revealed no significant abnormalities to account for his symptoms. More recently, during a day trip, he developed presyncope twice on the same day—once while ascending approximately 200 steps to a shrine and again during descent. This recurrence prompted him to pursue further comprehensive medical evaluation at our institution.

His past medical history was largely unremarkable, apart from hyperuricemia managed with febuxostat 20 mg daily. He reported habitual alcohol consumption of approximately 75 g of ethanol per day. He had a prior smoking history of 20 cigarettes per day but had ceased smoking 11 years earlier. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated a low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level of 144 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c of 5.5%, brain natriuretic peptide of 16.3 pg/mL, and high-sensitivity troponin T of 0. 010 ng/mL. Chest radiography (Figure 1A) and resting electrocardiography (ECG) (Figure 1B) were unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function, with mild-to-moderate aortic regurgitation in the context of a mildly dilated ascending aorta. Carotid duplex ultrasonography showed only mild intimal thickening without hemodynamically significant stenosis. Given his exertional chest discomfort, coronary computed tomography angiography was performed, demonstrating moderate stenosis with calcified plaque in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD), as well as MB in the mid-LAD (Figure 2).

To establish a definitive diagnosis for his chest symptoms and presyncopal episodes, the patient underwent coronary angiography with a comprehensive invasive physiological assessment. Initial angiography of the LAD, performed without intracoronary nitroglycerin, demonstrated only mild proximal stenosis, and dynamic compression of the LAD was not evident (Figure 3A, Video 1). Acetylcholine provocation testing was subsequently performed. Intracoronary administration of 50 μg acetylcholine into the right coronary artery induced approximately 90% stenosis in the distal segment without accompanying ischemic electrocardiographic changes or chest symptoms. In contrast, administration of 50 μg acetylcholine into the left coronary artery provoked diffuse 90% stenosis in the distal LAD, accompanied by attenuation of the precordial T waves and chest pain. Although the ECG changes did not fulfill the Coronary Vasomotion Disorders International Study Group ECG criteria for ischemia, the combination of angiographically documented epicardial spasm and symptom reproduction supported a diagnosis of epicardial coronary artery spasm (Figures 3B and 4, Video 2). Following intracoronary administration of 200 μg nitroglycerin and 2 mg nicorandil, the epicardial spasm resolved, unmasking dynamic systolic compression attributable to MB in the LAD (Figure 3C, Video 3). Coronary microvascular function was then assessed using a Pressure Wire X and CoroFlow system (Abbott), with maximal hyperemia induced by 2 mg of intracoronary nicorandil, demonstrating a preserved coronary flow reserve of 2.8 and a borderline elevated index of microvascular resistance of 25 in the LAD (Figure 5A). Finally, dobutamine stress testing with measurement of the resting full-cycle ratio (RFR) was performed. The RFR was 0.93 at baseline and decreased to 0.90 and 0.75 at dobutamine infusion rates of 10 and 20 μg/kg/min, respectively (Figure 5B). Notably, the distal coronary pressure-to-aortic pressure ratio (Pd/Pa) declined from 84/89 at baseline to 45/52 at 20 μg/kg/min of dobutamine. At peak stress, the patient developed chest discomfort and presyncope, closely resembling his prior symptoms. Coronary angiography performed shortly after cessation of dobutamine (approximately 5 minutes) demonstrated persistent and pronounced compression at the site of MB during both systole and diastole (Figure 3D, Video 4).

Collectively, these findings supported a diagnosis of hemodynamically significant MB causing ischemic symptoms and presyncope, in the setting of concomitant epicardial coronary spasm. Treatment with diltiazem 200 mg daily was initiated, resulting in complete resolution of exertional and syncopal symptoms.

Case 2

A 66-year-old man with a history of hypertension, managed with candesartan 8 mg, amlodipine 2.5 mg, and indapamide 2 mg daily, presented to the emergency department following an episode of transient loss of consciousness that occurred after rapidly ascending stairs to catch a train. At the time of the event, he had consumed alcohol; however, the quantity was consistent with his habitual intake of approximately 50 g of ethanol per day. This represented his first documented syncopal episode. He also reported mild exertional dyspnea. His smoking history included 30 cigarettes per day between the ages of 40 and 43 years, and he had since quit. The initial evaluation, including electrocardiography and chest radiography, was unremarkable. Laboratory testing revealed no elevation in cardiac biomarkers (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, 109 mg/dL; hemoglobin A1c, 6.1%; brain natriuretic peptide, 9.9 pg/mL; high-sensitivity troponin T, 0.010 ng/mL). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function without significant valvular abnormalities or left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Ambulatory Holter electrocardiographic monitoring was subsequently performed and revealed episodes of horizontal ST-segment elevation during the night. Coronary computed tomography angiography for anatomical assessment demonstrated a relatively long myocardial bridge in the mid-LAD (Figure 6).

Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography in Case 2

Coronary computed tomography angiography demonstrating a long myocardial bridge in the mid–left anterior descending artery (white arrow).

Invasive coronary angiography with comprehensive physiological assessment was then undertaken. Baseline angiography, performed without intracoronary nitroglycerin, showed no obstructive coronary artery disease, and systolic compression of the mid-LAD was not evident (Figure 7A, Video 5). Acetylcholine provocation testing (100 μg administered into the left coronary artery) induced subtotal occlusion of the mid-to-distal LAD, accompanied by chest pain and ST-segment elevation in the precordial leads (Figures 7B and 8, Video 6); these findings were promptly reversed by intracoronary nitroglycerin, consistent with a diagnosis of epicardial coronary spasm (Figure 7C, Video 7). Assessment of coronary microvascular function with maximal hyperemia induced by 2 mg of intracoronary nicorandil demonstrated preserved indices (coronary flow reserve, 5.4; index of microcirculatory resistance, 13) (Figure 9A). Dobutamine stress testing with RFR measurement revealed a decrease in RFR to 0.76 at a dobutamine infusion rate of 20 μg/kg/min (Figure 9B). Notably, this hemodynamic deterioration was accompanied by a marked decline in systemic and coronary blood pressure (Pd/Pa 52/53), temporally associated with the onset of presyncopal symptoms.

(C) Intracoronary nitroglycerin completely relieved the coronary spasm.

Collectively, these findings supported the diagnosis of hemodynamically significant MB causing ischemic symptoms and presyncope, in the setting of concomitant epicardial coronary spasm. Therapeutically, amlodipine was replaced with benidipine 4 mg, diltiazem 200 mg was initiated, and indapamide was discontinued. Following these adjustments, the patient's symptoms resolved.

Discussion

MB, characterized by an intramyocardial course of a coronary arterial segment, has traditionally been regarded as a benign anatomical variant. However, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that MB is frequently associated with coronary vasomotor abnormalities, particularly coronary vasospasm, and is increasingly recognized as an important substrate for ischemia with nonobstructive coronary artery disease.1, 2, 3 Moreover, the coexistence of MB and coronary vasospasm has been associated with an increased incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events, including malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.1,4 Both of our patients exhibited concomitant coronary vasospasm, consistent with previous reports demonstrating that MB is associated with an approximately 3.5-fold increased risk of coronary vasospasm.5 Therefore, careful evaluation for coexisting vasospastic disorders is essential in patients with MB, particularly in those with severe bridging associated with physiologically significant myocardial ischemia. The pathophysiological significance of MB predominantly relates to impairment of diastolic coronary augmentation rather than systolic flow limitation.6 In case 1, postdobutamine stress angiography successfully demonstrated persistent vessel compression extending into diastole, indicative of delayed relaxation of the bridged segment. This phenomenon creates a substantial diastolic impediment to coronary flow at a time when coronary flow augmentation is maximal, especially within the left coronary circulation. Consequently, the hemodynamic consequences of MB may become particularly pronounced under conditions of tachycardia, in which shortening of diastolic duration further compromises coronary perfusion. From a therapeutic perspective, beta-blockers have generally been considered first-line agents because they reduce heart rate and myocardial contractility, thereby attenuating systolic compression of the bridged segment. Nevertheless, given the substantial prevalence of concomitant coronary vasospasm in patients with MB, exclusion of vasospastic angina before initiation of beta-blocker therapy may be clinically important, as beta-blockers can potentially exacerbate coronary vasoconstriction. In both of our cases, coronary vasospasm was confirmed; therefore, beta-blockers were avoided, and diltiazem was selected instead. Owing to its combined negative chronotropic, negative inotropic, and anti-vasospastic properties, diltiazem led to marked symptomatic improvement in both patients.

Beyond demonstrating the pathophysiological significance of MB, these cases also provide important diagnostic insights. Because MB is frequently detected incidentally on coronary computed tomography angiography or coronary angiography, distinguishing a benign anatomical finding from a clinically significant lesion remains challenging. In both patients, several clinical features raised suspicion that MB was contributing to symptom development: symptoms were reproducibly triggered by physical exertion, no alternative explanation for syncope or presyncope was identified despite comprehensive evaluation, and invasive physiological testing demonstrated objective evidence of stress-induced ischemia. These findings suggest that patients with exertional chest symptoms, presyncope, or syncope in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease may benefit from further physiological and vasomotor assessment when MB is identified. Physical activity itself may contribute to symptom precipitation in susceptible patients with MB. Exercise increases heart rate and myocardial contractility, resulting in greater systolic compression and delayed diastolic relaxation of the bridged segment, thereby exacerbating impairment of coronary perfusion. Furthermore, our case 2 had consumed alcohol before symptom onset. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established, alcohol may have amplified the hemodynamic consequences of MB through increases in heart rate and alterations in coronary vascular tone. Vasodilation of the coronary segments proximal and distal to the bridged artery may theoretically accentuate the pressure gradient across the tunneled segment and increase dynamic compression. In addition, alcohol is a recognized trigger of coronary vasospasm, which may be particularly relevant given the concomitant epicardial coronary spasm documented in both patients. Therefore, physical exertion and alcohol consumption may have acted synergistically to precipitate ischemia and hemodynamic compromise in these cases.

A common physiological abnormality in both cases was unmasked during dobutamine stress testing, which pharmacologically simulated exercise conditions in the catheterization laboratory and thereby amplified the hemodynamic impact of MB. First, dobutamine stress provoked a marked decline in diastolic coronary pressure distal to the bridged segment. Because systolic compression caused by MB may paradoxically increase distal intracoronary systolic pressure, the physiological severity of MB is more accurately assessed using diastolic physiological indices, such as diastolic fractional flow reserve or instantaneous wave-free ratio.7,8 RFR, which represents the lowest Pd/Pa value across the entire cardiac cycle, also has emerged as an alternative recommended index for physiological assessment.9 In both patients, dobutamine stress induced an RFR value ≤0.76, fulfilling the established physiological criteria for hemodynamically significant MB.10 Second, both cases demonstrated a substantial decline in systemic blood pressure during dobutamine stress. Although significant myocardial ischemia attributable to MB was clearly provoked, this ischemic response was accompanied by a pronounced reduction in both systemic and distal coronary perfusion pressure. This hemodynamic response was somewhat unexpected and could not be further characterized by simultaneous duplex echocardiographic assessment during stress testing. Nevertheless, both patients developed presyncopal symptoms temporally associated with the dobutamine-induced hypotensive response, closely reproducing their previous exertional episodes. Furthermore, these symptoms resolved following initiation of MB-targeted therapy with diltiazem. Collectively, these findings suggest that exercise-induced presyncope associated with physiologically significant MB may be reproducibly provoked under pharmacological stress conditions in the catheterization laboratory.

Conclusions

These cases highlight that physiologically significant MB may manifest not only as exertional ischemic symptoms but also as syncope or presyncope secondary to stress-induced hemodynamic compromise. Comprehensive invasive physiological assessment, incorporating both intracoronary acetylcholine provocation testing and dobutamine stress evaluation, may therefore represent a valuable strategy for unmasking the concealed pathophysiological impact of MB.
