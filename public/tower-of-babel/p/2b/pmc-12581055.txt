US policy changes denying children a healthy climate and a healthy future
Authors: Emma R Rashes, Robert Gregory Cook, Donna Koller, Jeffrey Goldhagen, Michael Weitzman
Published: 2025

Introduction

Children and young people are acutely aware of the effects of the climate crisis on their health and well-being.1 2 In a survey of 10 000 adolescents internationally, over 50% reported feeling sad, anxious, angry, powerless, helpless and/or guilty about the climate crisis, and more than 45% reported feelings about climate change negatively impacting their lives daily.1 A recent study noted a 15-year-old saying, ‘Older people will die of old age, I’ll die of climate change’.3 Some children and adolescents are actively advocating for climate action, including through protests.3 Residing in perilous environments ravaged by flooding, drought or poor air quality can jeopardise children’s ability to enjoy and exercise their right to an adequate standard of living. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) stipulates that States must provide environmental conditions that allow children to thrive and grow.4 As an international human rights treaty, these rights are invariable and apply to all children regardless of background or circumstance. In the case of the climate crisis, relevant rights include Article 4 (government responsibilities to ensure children’s health), Article 6 (life, survival and development) and Article 27 (adequate standard of living).

The purpose of this paper is to highlight several recent changes to vital US climate policies and programmes that will likely have significant effects on children’s health and well-being. Since the first day the current administration took office, the US has undertaken a broad rollback of climate safeguards. Changes in the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and other organisations eliminate power-plant emissions limits, vehicle standards and methane regulations, among other important regulatory standards.5 Additional proposed policy changes include removing tax incentives that encourage purchasing and using electric vehicles and solar panels. The US has been among the world’s largest historical and current contributors to climate change, responsible for approximately a quarter of global cumulative carbon dioxide emissions from 1850 to 2019, and in 2020, it was the second-leading contributor to greenhouse gas emissions. As such, the US bears a primary responsibility for mitigating the damage it has caused to the environment and human health.6 7

The EPA has consistently highlighted the amplified impact of climate change on children.8 Children face unique vulnerabilities: their developing anatomy, physiology and behaviours expose them disproportionately to environmental threats, which in turn increase risks of neurodevelopmental disorders, ingestion of toxins, malnutrition, heat-related illness, asthma and diseases later in life, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. This is particularly the case for young children who are most susceptible because of critical periods in brain, physical and physiological growth and development, proximity to the ground and increased hand-to-mouth behaviours.9 The convergence of aggressive policy rollbacks at the EPA, diminished federal research investment and expanded fossil fuel development not only aggravates environmental hazards but also compromises scientific capacity to better understand, monitor, prevent and address climate change, escalating risks to child health on a global scale.

Air quality

Under the current administration’s EPA, sweeping cuts of critical air-quality safeguards have been initiated, including the formal reconsideration of the ‘Clean Power Plan V.2.0’, which aimed to limit greenhouse gas emissions from coal and gas-fired power plants.10 The agency also moved to dismantle these and other core protections on the grounds that emissions from these sources do not significantly harm public health.5 These measures directly undermine the EPA’s longstanding Office of Children’s Health Protection mandate, which requires agency actions to prioritise and mitigate risks to children’s health.8 Poor air quality has numerous impacts on fetal and child development, including immune dysregulation, altered gene expression, preterm birth, diminished lung function and endocrine dysfunction.11 Increased air pollution limits children’s ability to play, both their right and primary means of enjoyment, development and connection.12 Ample evidence, including much derived from the EPA and other government-funded research, has demonstrated that poorer air quality exacerbates childhood asthma, impairs respiratory and neurodevelopment, and hampers early childhood growth, highlighting serious health consequences for children on a global scale.

Fossil fuel combustion

Under the current administration, the US aims to aggressively expand fossil fuel extraction through a series of executive orders that declare a ‘national energy emergency’, remove regulatory barriers and establish a National Energy Dominance Council, aimed at increasing domestic oil, natural gas and coal production.13 Progress has been reversed through actions such as rolling back tax credits for renewable energy (as included in the recently passed ‘Big Beautiful Bill’), loosening regulations on carbon emissions, reducing vehicle emission standards, as well as withdrawing from international agreements like the Paris Agreement on Climate Change; directly conflicting with scientific consensus on the health toll of fossil fuel combustion.10 14 15 Children, particularly those in low-income communities, are subject to a disproportionate burden of disease, ranging from respiratory illness and neurodevelopmental delays to heat-related disease, linked to both air pollution and climate change driven by fossil fuel use.16 Low-income communities, most impacted by the climate crisis, are often those that contribute least to the problem.17 The acceleration of fossil fuel projects increases emissions of particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide and other toxic pollutants. These emissions impair lung development, exacerbate asthma, contribute to preterm birth and reduce cognitive outcomes in children worldwide.18 By increasing fossil fuel combustion and delaying the transition to cleaner energy, current US policy will only exacerbate this global childhood health crisis.

Climate-sensitive infectious diseases

Rising global temperatures are reshaping the distribution and intensity of vector-borne diseases, which particularly endanger children. As climate change drives warmer and wetter conditions across many regions, disease vectors are expanding into new geographic zones, increasing the incidence of illnesses such as dengue, malaria, Zika, Lyme disease and West Nile virus in previously unaffected areas.19 Children are especially at risk due to their developing immune systems and higher likelihood of outdoor exposure. The American Academy of Paediatrics projects that climate change may put an additional one billion people, many of them children, at risk for diseases transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes within this century.20 We are seeing similar trends even just within the US, as the rising incidence of Valley Fever in the southwestern USA has been linked to warmer, drier conditions that support the pathogen’s expansion into new environments.21 In Africa, climate change has contributed to the resurgence and intensification of malaria, cholera and dengue outbreaks, disproportionately affecting children and overwhelming healthcare infrastructure.22 These global increases in infection rates underscore the urgent need to integrate child-centred health protections into climate policy.

Research funding cuts

The current administration plans to restructure the US$40 million Climate Change and Health Initiative, initially instituted to amplify climate change research. These NIH funding cuts will restrict research on a broad range of climate change-related topics, such as extreme weather, and physical and mental health impacts of climate change, such as ‘climate anxiety’.23 In the US and worldwide, climate anxiety is a rampant and rising problem among youth. As best shown in studies by Hickman et al and Lewandowski et al, more than half of youth aged 18–25 reported being ‘very’ or ‘extremely’ worried by climate change.1 2 The majority of youth in both of these studies expressed their belief that governments should do more to combat climate change, yet limiting research funding prevents us from further understanding the mental health challenges of and limits our ability to support these children. Additionally, all NIH grants involving collaboration with foreign partners are being terminated, resulting in a loss of approximately US$500 million in foreign research. Though only a small proportion of the NIH’s research is climate change-related, this is a huge loss.24 Limiting projects that cannot continue without US support denies the opportunity to learn about and resolve many issues affecting children worldwide. In addition, the current administration has dismissed employees and cut funding for the National Climate Assessment.25 This crucial survey, which reports on the effects of climate change on human health, the US economy and water resources, ensures that the nation is aware of the effects of climate change. Such information is imperative to ensure that health professionals and families can prepare to confront the effects of climate change. By restricting this survey, the nation is left without data that can mitigate future risks.

Conclusion

Climate change poses a critical and unequal threat to children’s health worldwide, especially young, vulnerable and underserved populations. As highlighted by UNICEF’s Children’s Climate Risk Index, nearly one billion children face extreme climate-related risks, undermining rights protected under the UNCRC.17 To safeguard the health of our children worldwide, it is essential to restore environmental protections and invest in climate and health research. Progress has already been too slow, and if the US backtracks on climate change initiatives now, it will only exacerbate and accelerate the global risks to children’s health and well-being. Child health advocates must champion the right of children to live in healthy and safe environments that give them the optimal chance of reaching their fullest potential. We owe this to our children as the future of humankind.
