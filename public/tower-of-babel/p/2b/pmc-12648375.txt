Forest Carbon Dynamics as a Constraint on Future Land‐Use and Land‐Cover Change
Authors: Wenqiang Zhang, Anping Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate projections of land use and land cover change (LULCC) are crucial for understanding global change and guiding climate mitigation. Most projections emphasize socioeconomic and climatic drivers but overlook feedbacks from forest carbon dynamics. Luo et al. (2025) address this gap by coupling a terrestrial biosphere model (CLASSIC) with an integrated assessment model (GCAM) to assess how forest carbon dynamics shapes future LULCC. Their results show that climate‐, CO2−, and policy‐driven carbon dynamics strongly influence land allocation, reducing managed‐forest expansion and moderating losses of unmanaged lands.

Land use and land cover change (LULCC) represents one of the most consequential dimensions of global environmental change, with far‐reaching impacts on the carbon cycle, biodiversity, ecosystem services, and the climate system. Yet projecting future LULCC remains a daunting challenge (Friedlingstein et al. 2025) as land‐use decisions are co‐determined by both biophysical and socioeconomic constraints, often involving complex trade‐offs between competing demands (Lee et al. 2023; Winkler et al. 2021). Importantly, while land systems are increasingly positioned as key instruments for climate change mitigation through enhanced terrestrial carbon sequestration, most LULCC projections make a critical simplification by treating the terrestrial carbon sink as static. Capturing the dynamic feedbacks between climate and land carbon is therefore essential for integrating climate goals into land‐use planning (Meyfroidt et al. 2022).

Against this backdrop, Luo et al. (2025) made an important conceptual and methodological advance by directly linking forest carbon dynamics with future land‐use trajectories (Figure 1). Their modeling framework, published recently in Global Change Biology, bridges the strengths of process‐based terrestrial biosphere models (TBMs) and integrated assessment models (IAMs) by coupling the Canadian Land Surface Scheme–Canadian Terrestrial Ecosystem Model (CLASSIC) with the Global Change Assessment Model (GCAM). This integration allows ecological processes that regulate forest carbon dynamics to influence the socioeconomic decisions that drive land allocation. Using meteorological forcing data from two Earth system models (ESMs) and three Shared Socio‐economic Pathways—Representative Concentration Pathways scenario combinations (SSP1‐2.6, SSP3‐7.0, and SSP5‐8.5), Luo et al. (2025) demonstrated that incorporating dynamic forest carbon feedbacks can substantially alter projected land‐use outcomes, reducing the expansion of managed forests, moderating losses of unmanaged lands, and reshaping cropland distribution.

The diagram shows that global change drivers influence biospheric carbon dynamics, which, through land‐use competition, reshape future LULCC trajectories. Feedbacks among climate, CO2 fertilization, and soil carbon alter vegetation productivity and land allocation, informing policy responses such as carbon pricing, reforestation incentives, and conservation strategies.

The key novelty of this study lies not merely in the technical coupling of two established models but in the reversal of perspective it introduces: rather than asking how land‐use change affects the carbon cycle, Luo et al. (2025) asked how the carbon cycle, represented by forest carbon dynamics, constrains future land‐use change. This inversion marks a conceptual advance, positioning carbon dynamics as an active agent rather than a passive outcome in integrated assessment modeling. Most previous studies have treated terrestrial carbon as a response variable within IAMs. Luo et al. (2025) instead embedded it as a feedback mechanism that can reshape the trajectory of land‐use transitions. This strategy builds upon and extends the work of Thornton et al. (2017), who demonstrated the importance of coupling biospheric feedbacks into socioeconomic projections. Thornton and colleagues showed that omitting these feedbacks could lead to inconsistencies between ESMs and energy–economic models, as the latter typically impose human‐system forcings without accounting for climate‐driven changes in land productivity. Luo et al. (2025) advanced this line of research by explicitly incorporating the effects of global change on forest productivity. By coupling a detailed TBM (CLASSIC) with a widely used IAM (GCAM), they provided an operational framework for assessing how biophysical carbon feedbacks shape future LULCC trajectories under various socioeconomic and climate scenarios. Their study thus transforms the insight that biospheric changes influence human land‐use and energy systems from theoretical principle to testable, scenario‐based modeling practice.

The coupled framework used by Luo et al. (2025) recognizes that forest carbon dynamics respond to global change factors including rising CO2 concentrations, climate variability, drought stress, disturbance, and management practices (Pan et al. 2024). All of these factors affect vegetation productivity and, in turn, the land area required to meet demands for food, timber, and energy (Mo et al. 2023). By explicitly linking these processes to land‐use decisions, Luo et al. (2025) revealed how biospheric carbon feedbacks act as a constraint on future land allocation. Increases in forest productivity can reduce the land required for timber production, freeing space for cropland or conservation. Conversely, declines in carbon sequestration under climate stress can necessitate larger forest areas to achieve mitigation goals, limiting land available for other uses. In both cases, carbon dynamics mediate competition among land‐use types, altering the economic and environmental trade‐offs that shape global land trajectories.

Luo et al. (2025)'s simulations reveal consistent and physically plausible patterns. From 2015 to 2100, vegetation carbon density increased across most land‐cover types, while soil carbon density declined slightly, likely due to enhanced soil respiration (Nottingham et al. 2020). Across all scenarios, vegetation and soil carbon respond most strongly to CO2 fertilization, while the influence of climate change varies regionally and nitrogen deposition has only minor effects. Incorporating these carbon dynamics into land‐use projections reshapes outcomes across all SSP–RCP combinations. Under SSP5‐8.5, vegetation and soil carbon increase substantially, with CO2 fertilization identified as the dominant driver.

Integrating CLASSIC and GCAM allows feedbacks between ecosystem processes and policy‐driven land allocation to emerge dynamically from the model structure rather than being imposed externally. The consistency of results across two ESM forcings reinforces the robustness of their conclusions and demonstrates that the framework captures key feedbacks between the biosphere and human systems. The broader significance of this study lies in how it reframes the role of land in climate mitigation. Land‐based strategies such as afforestation, reforestation, and bioenergy expansion are central to achieving global net‐zero targets (Meyfroidt et al. 2022). Yet the potential of these strategies depends on how ecosystems respond to global change. If forest growth saturates or carbon losses accelerate under warming, the available mitigation potential could decline sharply. Luo et al. (2025)'s results highlight that the land sector's contribution to climate goals cannot be treated as an external lever; it is an emergent property of coupled ecological and socioeconomic feedbacks.

While the framework proposed by Luo et al. (2025) offers significant advantages and establishes an important foundation for understanding interactions between forest carbon and land‐use change, its findings partly depend on the internal structure and assumptions of GCAM, and different IAMs may produce varying feedback outcomes (Mishra et al. 2021). At regional scales, key ecological processes (e.g., soil carbon turnover, disturbance dynamics, and forest recovery) are represented in simplified form, limiting spatial resolution and ecological realism. This simplification becomes particularly critical when addressing which global change drivers dominate the feedbacks between forest carbon and land use. Moreover, the interactions between biophysical feedbacks and policy mechanisms—such as carbon pricing, conservation incentives, or bioenergy mandates—remain insufficiently explored, as the forest carbon trajectories simulated by CLASSIC are not explicitly constrained by mitigation targets in the current configuration. Future studies should aim to implement goal‐oriented feedbacks in which specific forest carbon density or sequestration targets dynamically influence land‐use pathways, thereby linking ecological processes and policy objectives more directly. Addressing these challenges will require closer collaboration between the biosphere and integrated assessment modeling communities, supported by observational constraints from satellites, flux towers, and field networks. Extending this coupled approach across multiple IAM frameworks, calibrating against observational benchmarks, and explicitly representing regional disturbance regimes will collectively enhance model predictive accuracy and policy relevance. Ultimately, achieving reliable projections of future LULCC will depend on fully coupling ecological, climatic, and socioeconomic processes within an integrated modeling framework.

In summary, Luo et al. (2025) made a major advance by quantifying how forest carbon dynamics shape future land‐use trajectories. Their study demonstrates that future LULCC is not determined solely by socioeconomic and climate drivers but also by the responsiveness of ecosystems. This insight that carbon dynamics act as a “hidden driver” of land‐use change represents an important step forward in how we conceptualize and model coupled human–Earth systems. By bridging process‐based terrestrial biosphere modeling with IAM scenario analysis, Luo et al. (2025) provided a coherent and policy‐relevant framework that directly supports global climate commitments under the Paris Agreement, particularly the forestry and land‐based targets embedded in Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). The framework offers a quantitative foundation for assessing how land‐based mitigation measures, e.g., afforestation, reforestation, and bioenergy expansion, interact with biophysical feedbacks to influence carbon sequestration potential and land‐use outcomes. These insights are vital for translating scientific understanding into actionable policies and for evaluating the feasibility of national and global net‐zero pathways. Achieving global climate goals ultimately depends not only on socioeconomic pathways or technological innovation but also on the adaptive capacity of the biosphere. Explicitly representing biospheric feedbacks in policy‐linked modeling frameworks is therefore essential for developing robust strategies for climate mitigation, sustainable land management, and long‐term stewardship of the terrestrial biosphere in a rapidly changing world.
