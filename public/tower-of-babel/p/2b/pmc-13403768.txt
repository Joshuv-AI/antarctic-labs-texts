Cardiology care team and vaccines

Patients with heart disease face a higher risk of hospitalization and death after respiratory virus infection compared with the general population. Uptake of vaccinations is low, with data suggesting that medical specialists are less likely than primary care clinicians to inform patients on the need for vaccines.1 However, recommendations for vaccines from health care providers are strongly associated with patients obtaining vaccination, and the cardiologist may be particularly effective in educating patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) on the benefits of vaccination.2

Vaccinations are imperative for care optimization of the patient with CVD and should be thought of as a core component for heart-healthy living.3 The cardiology care team should be equipped to provide vaccine education, fostering conversations to support this component of preventive cardiovascular care. Effective conversations allow for discussing the need for vaccination, including the ability of vaccines to mitigate CVD morbidity and mortality. Shared decision-making in which the patient’s views are central is important for a successful conversation. Assuming that vaccinations should be provided by primary care will lead to missed opportunities to vaccinate the CVD patient population, as in many cases the CVD patient mainly interacts with their cardiac care provider. Many patients with CVD will see their cardiology care provider first for initial post-hospital follow-up or postprocedural visits (where they may be newly diagnosed with CVD) or are followed up more closely in the outpatient setting in such disease states as heart failure/heart transplantation. If the CVD provider describes the use of vaccines as a way to optimize CVD risk, patients may be more willing to be vaccinated.2

Vaccine need and access should be part of the patient shared decision-making discussion with the CVD care provider. Clinicians should make themselves aware of vaccine timelines and how vaccines are distributed in their clinical setting. Patients with CVD will touch different access points of the health care system (eg, outpatient clinics, emergency departments, inpatient settings and procedural areas) but almost all will have an encounter with a cardiology care provider. Cardiology care team colleagues should take this opportunity to convey the importance of particular vaccines and their recommended frequency. Reserving a few moments to provide a clear, strong recommendation for vaccination may persuade most patients to get vaccinated.2 Framing vaccination as part of heart-healthy living can help connect the importance to preventing heart disease.3

Providing educational materials to patients with vaccine information targeting common questions can also help the cardiology care team convey important points. Once patients have decided to incorporate vaccination into their heart-healthy living plan, cardiology care team providers should be ready to provide vaccinations or refer patients to a provider or location that has vaccines. If patients are referred out, follow-up should be available to ensure that patients received a vaccine.4

Another support to the cardiology care team focusing on vaccination can be a vaccine champion: an individual who can prioritize efforts for vaccination in clinical care. Vaccine champions can support vaccine assessments and education during visits, look for opportunities to raise vaccination awareness, and be a clinical support point person for vaccine information, especially in a busy clinical setting.4

Vaccine hesitancy

For those individuals with vaccine hesitancy, explaining in clear language how vaccines work is warranted. Clinicians should actively listen to patients who are unsure about a vaccine and give patients an opportunity to voice concerns. Providing education, building trust, and offering reassurance can move patients from being hesitant to deciding to get vaccinated if they feel more informed and involved. Clinicians should be prepared to answer questions about the effectiveness, necessity, and safety of adult vaccines.2

Patients trust and listen to their health care teams. For most patients, a clear recommendation for a vaccine is often what ultimately motivates the patient to get vaccinated.•Discuss the risks and benefits of recommended vaccines and the safety and benefits of the vaccines in preventing serious illness and complications.•Communicate heart benefits.
