Methodological quality of nursing guidelines in China: an AGREE II and RIGHT appraisal (2023–2024)
Authors: Maozheng Xuan, Daoxin Wang, Taibing Fan, Wenli Zhao, Weijie Liang, Nan Liu, Huan Li, Rui Hou, Xing Ming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the methodological and reporting quality of clinical guidelines and expert consensus statements in the Chinese nursing field published in journals from 2023 to 2024.MethodsWe searched databases including CNKI, WanFang Data, SinoMed/CBM, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, supplemented by searches of guideline repositories (e.g., GIN) and academic organization websites. The search period spanned from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024. Two researchers independently screened the literature, extracted data, and assessed quality using AGREE II and RIGHT tools.ResultsA total of 28 guidelines and consensus statements were included. The mean standardized percentages for AGREE II domains were: Scope and Purpose, 46.74%; Stakeholder, Involvement 32.13%; Rigor of Development, 30.64%; Clarity of Presentation, 39.22%; Applicability, 29.42%; and Editorial Independence, 42.77%. RIGHT reporting rates were highest for items 1a, 1b, 3, 4, 18a, and 19a 28/28 (100%). Although the small number of guidelines (n = 5) limits inferential strength, descriptive comparisons suggested consistently higher scores in guidelines than in consensus statements across all AGREE II domains and in total RIGHT score (P < 0.05), and these findings should be regarded as exploratory. Evidence-based documents outperformed expert opinion-based documents (P < 0.05). Team size was positively correlated with the RIGHT score (P < 0.05).ConclusionCurrent guidelines and consensus statements in the domestic nursing field exhibit significant shortcomings in methodological rigor, reporting completeness, and clinical applicability. Future development processes should strictly adhere to AGREE II and RIGHT standards, strengthen evidence-based methodology, emphasize multidisciplinary collaboration and patient involvement, and enhance reporting transparency and clinical translation support.Keywords: Evidence-based nursing, Clinical guidelines, Expert consensus, Quality assessment

Introduction

Clinical practice guidelines and expert consensus statements serve as crucial guiding documents for nursing practice, playing a vital role in enhancing the quality of clinical care and patient safety. With the rapid development of nursing science in China, relevant academic organizations and journals have recently published a substantial number of such documents aimed at promoting the standardization and scientization [1]. However, these guidelines and consensus statements vary in their development methods, evidence integration, and reporting standards. Their inconsistent quality may affect the reliability and applicability of the recommendations [2]. High-quality guidelines can effectively inform clinical decision-making and optimize nursing workflows; conversely, documents with methodological flaws or incomplete reporting may misguide practice and even lead to resource waste [3]. Therefore, a systematic and objective quality assessment of published guidelines and consensus statements in the domestic nursing field is warranted.

Several previous studies have appraised the methodological or reporting quality of Chinese clinical practice guidelines using AGREE II or RIGHT in fields such as rehabilitation, oncology, and traditional Chinese medicine [4, 5], and the combined use of these two tools has become a standard approach in guideline appraisal across various clinical domains. However, existing nursing‑focused appraisals in China have typically covered broad time spans and have rarely distinguished between formal clinical guidelines and expert consensus documents, leaving critical nuances unexamined [6]. Given that consensus statements constitute the majority (82%) of nursing guidance documents in China, their comparative quality against formal guidelines is clinically relevant but remains unexplored.

Therefore, the novelty of this study rests entirely on the combination of the subject domain (Chinese nursing), document type (guidelines and consensus statements), and time period (2023–2024). Specifically, this combination addresses three distinct evidence gaps that cannot be inferred from previous appraisals in other specialties or broader timeframes. First, the focused 2023–2024 window provides a timely and undiluted baseline, avoiding the confounding effects of older, potentially outdated methodologies. Second, the nursing‑specific scope with separate subgroup analysis of guidelines versus consensus statements offers the first comparative quality profile within this discipline, which is essential for developing targeted improvement strategies for each document type. Third, the simultaneous application of AGREE II and RIGHT — while methodologically standard — ensures a comprehensive and reproducible benchmarking framework, allowing direct comparability with future studies in Chinese nursing and beyond. Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate the methodological and reporting quality of Chinese nursing guidelines and consensus statements published between 2023 and 2024, identify specific shortcomings, and provide actionable recommendations for future development and standardized reporting.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Published clinical practice guidelines or expert consensus statements. (2) Publication date between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2024. (3) Formally published in academic journals within mainland China (excluding online pre-publications). (4) Topics belong to the nursing field, or are led by nursing academic organizations, with content directly guiding nursing practice. (5) If updated versions of the same guideline or consensus were published within this period, only the latest version was included.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Non-Chinese literature. (2) Interpretations, translations, commentaries, application effect evaluations, introductory articles, or errata related to guidelines/consensus. (3) Full text unavailable, or key information (e.g., developing institution, publication date) missing. (4) Duplicate publications of the same guideline/consensus in different journals; only the first publication or the version with the most complete information was retained. (5) Publications only in conference abstract form, not formally peer-reviewed and published in a journal.

Literature search strategy

Computerized searches were conducted in Chinese and English databases. The Chinese databases included CNKI, WanFang Data, and SinoMed/CBM. The English databases included PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science Core Collection.Supplementary searches: International guideline repositories (e.g., Guidelines International Network, GIN) and websites of relevant domestic academic organizations (e.g., the Chinese Nursing Association) were also searched to supplement potentially missed literature. The search period was from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024 (inclusive). A snowball search was performed by reviewing the references of included studies to avoid omissions. Searches combined subject headings and free-text terms based on three conceptual groups:

Concept Group 1 (Nursing/Field): “护理(nursing)”, “护士(nurse)”, “护理实践(nursing practice)”, “nursing”, “nurse-led”, “nursing care”.

Concept Group 1 (Nursing/Field): “护理(nursing)”, “护士(nurse)”, “护理实践(nursing practice)”, “nursing”, “nurse-led”, “nursing care”.

Concept Group 2 (Document Type): “指南(guideline)”, “共识(consensus)”, “规范(standard)”, “推荐(recommendation)”, “实践指引(practice guidance)”, “guideline”, “consensus”, “recommendation”, “practice guideline”, “expert consensus”.

Concept Group 3 (Limiter - China/Journal): “中国(China)”, “国内(domestic)”, “China”, “Chinese”.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two researchers independently performed literature screening and data extraction, with subsequent cross-checking.

Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher. If data were missing during extraction, attempts were made to contact the authors for supplementation. During screening, titles and abstracts were first reviewed to exclude clearly irrelevant literature, followed by full-text review for final inclusion decisions.

Extracted data primarily included: (1) Basic information of included guidelines/consensus (title, journal, publication date, developing institution, development method). (2) Key elements for methodological quality assessment. (3) Key elements for reporting quality assessment.

Quality assessment of included studies

Four trained postgraduate researchers in nursing and medical fields assessed the methodological quality and reporting quality of included documents using the AGREE II and RIGHT tools, respectively. The researchers had a consistent understanding of each item and used identical assessment criteria. The AGREE II tool comprises 6 domains and 23 items. Each item is scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The standardized percentage for each domain was calculated as: (Obtained Score - Minimum Possible Score) / (Maximum Possible Score - Minimum Possible Score) × 100%. The RIGHT checklist includes 7 domains and 22 items. Items were scored as “reported” (1 point), “partially reported” (0.5 points), or “not reported” (0 points). A total score for each guideline was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed using Excel software. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparing measurement data. The significance level for all hypothesis tests was set at α = 0.05. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using SPSS 26.0 to assess inter-rater agreement among the four researchers for AGREE II items. ICC values range from 0 to 1, with ICC < 0.40 indicating poor agreement, 0.40–0.75 indicating fair agreement, and ICC > 0.75 indicating good agreement. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.To explore the relationship between team size and guideline quality, we categorized documents into “larger” (≥ 18 developers) and “smaller” (< 18) groups. This cutoff was chosen empirically based on the WHO-recommended range of 10–20 members was used only as contextual reference rather than a strict analytical boundary [7]. We acknowledge that this empirical cutoff is a study limitation, and the results of this subgroup analysis should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical considerations

The relatively small number of included documents and, in particular, the small number of formal guidelines in the subgroup analysis represent a methodological limitation. The Mann-Whitney U test was selected as a nonparametric alternative to the independent t‑test given the non‑normal distribution of the AGREE II domain scores and RIGHT total scores. However, when the total sample size is small, nonparametric tests have reduced statistical power, and the reliability of p-values may be compromised. In particular, for subgroup comparisons involving a group as small as n = 5, the quantitative p-values should be interpreted with caution, and the results are best viewed as descriptive rather than confirmatory. No formal sample size calculation was performed prior to the study, as this was a comprehensive systematic appraisal of all eligible documents published in the target timeframe rather than a hypothesis‑driven inferential study.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated 28 clinical guidelines and expert consensuses in nursing, published in domestic journals between 2023 and 2024. Topics encompassed specialties such as stroke thrombolysis, pressure injury prevention, and diabetes management. Methodological quality and reporting completeness were assessed using the AGREE II and RIGHT instruments.

Methodological quality characteristics and major deficiencies

AGREE II assessment results indicated that the included guidelines performed relatively well in the Scope and Purpose domain, typically clearly outlining their objectives and target populations [30]. For instance, Han et al. precisely defined their target group and assessment focus [8].However, other key methodological domains showed notable weaknesses. Stakeholder Involvement scored low, with over 75% of documents failing to specify the incorporation of patient or public input. Rigor of Development received the lowest scores, as over 40% did not clearly articulate the benefit-risk trade-offs of recommendations. Applicability was also inadequate, with most guidelines lacking discussion on implementation barriers or monitoring indicators. In contrast, Editorial Independence performed relatively well. For example, Chang et al. explicitly stated the absence of commercial influence and fully disclosed conflicts of interest, supporting content objectivity [1].

Reporting completeness and identified deficiencies

RIGHT appraisal showed relatively complete reporting for basic information and some declarative items. For example, Xing et al. clearly defined the target population and fully disclosed funding and conflicts of interest, ensuring transparency [3]. However, major gaps existed in reporting scientific rigor and procedural transparency: over 70% of documents inadequately described evidence retrieval and selection; over 80% failed to clarify how evidence informed recommendations or to provide scenario-specific guidance; and > 90% omitted details on external review or update plans. These omissions compromise the recommendations’ credibility and timeliness [33].

Quality disparities across document types

Subgroup analysis revealed that guidelines scored significantly higher than consensus documents across all AGREE II domains and in total RIGHT scores (P < 0.05), with consistently large differences in median scores. The p-values from the Mann‑Whitney U test were below 0.05 for all comparisons, suggesting that the observed differences are unlikely to be due to chance alone; however, given the small number of guidelines (n = 5), these results should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The higher quality of guidelines may be tentatively attributed to their more structured development processes. Similarly, evidence - based documents tended to show superior methodological and reporting quality compared to expert opinion‑based ones (P < 0.05), highlighting the potential value of systematic evidence synthesis [5, 34]. Nevertheless, the limited sample size precludes strong causal inferences, and these findings require confirmation in larger studies.Given the clinical heterogeneity of the included documents, these aggregate findings should be viewed as reflecting broad patterns rather than definitive quality benchmarks for all nursing guidelines in China. Specific clinical areas may require separate evaluations.

To contextualize our findings within the broader literature, we compared our AGREE II domain scores with those reported in other guideline appraisal studies. A large-scale evaluation of 573 Chinese clinical practice guidelines published between 2014 and 2018 reported a mean overall methodological quality of only 19.4%, with “Applicability” scoring 14.2% and “Rigor of Development” 11.7% [35]. Our nursing-specific findings – Applicability 29.42% and Rigor of Development 30.64% – suggest that guideline quality in China has improved over time.An international comparison of obstructive sleep apnea guidelines found that Asian guidelines scored significantly lower than European and American guidelines in the “Rigor of Development” domain and overall quality [36]. This finding is consistent with our observation that “Rigor of Development” remains one of the lowest-scoring domains in Chinese nursing guidelines, highlighting the need for methodological capacity building in Asia, including China.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its specific and timely scope. We systematically appraised nursing‑specific guidelines and expert consensus statements published in mainland China during the most recent two‑year period (2023–2024), a combination of subject domain, document type, and time window that has not been examined together in previous research. This focused scope provides three distinct advantages: it offers a contemporary baseline for quality benchmarking that avoids the confounding effects of older methodologies; it enables the first separate comparison between clinical guidelines and consensus statements within the Chinese nursing field, which is clinically meaningful given that consensus documents constitute the majority (82%) of nursing guidance in China; and it ensures that our findings reflect the current state of guideline development, informing timely and actionable quality improvement strategies. To ensure comprehensive appraisal, we employed both AGREE II and RIGHT, which together capture methodological rigor and reporting completeness within a single evaluative framework [34, 35].Furthermore, due to the small sample size, subgroup comparisons involving such small cells may be underpowered. Small samples provide limited information for reliable statistical inference, especially regarding distributional assumptions and effect size estimation. Additionally, the ≥ 18 cutoff used for team size subgroup analysis was determined empirically based on our sample distribution rather than derived from a validated external standard. While the WHO-recommended range of 10–20 members [7] was consulted for contextual reference, this empirical threshold represents a limitation of our subgroup analysis, and findings related to team size should be interpreted as exploratory.Therefore, the findings of subgroup analyses are best considered as exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. We have deliberately avoided strong causal claims based on these comparisons and have framed our conclusions accordingly.We also considered whether a bootstrap or sensitivity analysis might strengthen the subgroup comparisons. However, bootstrapping would generate unstable estimates and is not methodologically justified. Future studies with larger numbers of nursing guidelines could usefully employ resampling methods to confirm the robustness of subgroup differences.Finally, the 28 documents cover diverse clinical topics, subspecialty subgroup analyses were not feasible due to small sample sizes. Descriptively, guidelines on pressure injury prevention and stroke thrombolysis tended to score higher, whereas those on rare or highly specialized procedures often scored lower. These observations require confirmation in future studies.

Our AGREE II assessment found that the Applicability domain scored only 29.42% (second lowest). We identified three structural reasons. First, the domain demands a rare combination of evidence synthesis and clinical implementation expertise. Second, nursing guidelines are often developed by small, homogeneous groups without patient or implementation experts − 78.57% (22/28) omitted patient perspectives. Third, the lack of patient involvement directly undermines applicability, as patient-centered strategies require understanding patient values and constraints. Without patient input, even evidence-based recommendations may misalign with patient realities, reducing uptake. Addressing these barriers requires policy-level changes, including patient engagement mandates and implementation toolkits.

Four independent reviewers achieved high inter-rater reliability (ICC), supporting result robustness [37, 38]. However, some subjectivity persists, and the exclusion of non-journal or earlier literature may affect the generalizability of the conclusions.

Implications and recommendations

Based on our AGREE II and RIGHT findings, we propose three concrete recommendations for Chinese nursing.First, nursing journals should mandate the RIGHT checklist for all guidelines and consensus statements.Critical items were nearly always missing: external review (92.85%), updating (89.28%), implementation tools (85.71%), resource implications (96.42%), and implementation considerations (82.14%). A mandatory RIGHT requirement would directly improve reporting transparency without additional research.Second, the Chinese Nursing Association should establish a nursing-specific guideline registry and a mandatory methodology training program.Only 32.13% of documents adequately addressed stakeholder involvement, and only 30.64% met rigor standards. The Association should: (a) create a registry modeled on international platforms to promote prospective registration and transparency [39]; and (b) require training in systematic review methods, GRADE, and conflict-of-interest management for all endorsed guidelines [40]. Third, the Association should develop a standardized “Implementation Toolkit” template. Applicability scored 29.42%, and implementation-related RIGHT items were critically under-reported. A simple one- to two-page supplementary template should require developers to state: (a) potential barriers to implementation; (b) facilitators (e.g., existing protocols, leadership support, training materials); (c) a plain-language summary for patients and families; and (d) suggested audit criteria for measuring adherence [40].

Conclusion

In summary, current nursing guidelines and consensuses in China show deficiencies in methodological rigor, reporting, and clinical applicability. Future efforts should adopt international standards, include patients and multidisciplinary professionals, follow evidence-based processes, and consider implementation feasibility. This will support the development of scientifically sound, clinically relevant documents to standardize and improve nursing practice.
