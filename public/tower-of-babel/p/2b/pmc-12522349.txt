Association of metabolic syndrome and prognosis in elderly patients with community-acquired pneumonia
Authors: Xiaofeng Tang, Li Zhang, Ruijuan Wu, Jie Liu, Yongfeng Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the association between metabolic syndrome (MetS) and clinical outcomes in elderly patients hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 712 elderly patients (aged ≥ 65 years) admitted with CAP to a tertiary hospital between 2018 and 2022. Patients were stratified into two groups based on MetS status (MetS group vs. non-MetS group). The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline characteristics. Multivariate Cox regression and logistic regression analyses were performed to assess associations, adjusting for confounders.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 336 patients (168 MetS and 168 non-MetS) were included in the final analysis. The MetS group had a significantly higher 90-day mortality rate compared to the non-MetS group (29.8% vs. 11.9%, p < 0.01). Multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that MetS was independently associated with a 1.98-fold increased risk of mortality (95% CI: 1.08–3.63; p = 0.03). A clear dose-response relationship was observed, with each additional MetS component increasing mortality risk by 1.46-fold (95% CI: 1.11–2.16; p < 0.01).ConclusionMetS is an independent predictor of worse short-term outcomes in elderly CAP patients, including higher mortality and longer hospital stays. Early recognition and management of MetS may improve prognosis in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03914-4.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Pneumonia, Mortality, Elderly, Prognosis

Background

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly among older adults [1]. Globally, CAP affects approximately 5 to 11 out of every 1,000 adults annually, with the incidence rising significantly with age [2]. The growing aging population and the high burden of chronic comorbidities further compound the clinical and public health challenges of managing CAP in older adults.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of interconnected cardiometabolic risk factors, including abdominal obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance [3]. Its prevalence has increased dramatically over the past decades, especially in older populations. Data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) indicate that approximately 33.38% of Chinese adults meet the criteria for MetS, and prevalence increases with age, reflecting a substantial proportion of elderly individuals at increased risk for poor health outcomes [4].

Emerging evidence suggests that MetS may adversely affect the prognosis of acute infections [5, 6]. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms—such as chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, impaired innate immunity, and altered metabolic homeostasis—may contribute to increased susceptibility to infections and worsened outcomes [7, 8]. However, despite these potential links, the relationship between MetS and short-term prognosis in elderly patients hospitalized with CAP remains understudied and poorly understood.

Understanding the prognostic impact of MetS in this population is critical for early risk stratification and personalized management strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between MetS and short-term clinical outcomes in elderly patients hospitalized with CAP. We hypothesized that the presence of MetS would be independently associated with increased short-term mortality in this population. The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay and 30-day all-cause mortality. These outcomes were chosen to reflect both survival and short-term clinical burden in elderly CAP patients with and without MetS.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Huanggang Central Hospital, a tertiary care hospital. We included elderly patients aged ≥ 65 years who were admitted with a primary diagnosis of CAP between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2022. CAP was diagnosed according to standardized clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria, including the presence of new pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging, in combination with symptoms such as cough, sputum production, fever, or dyspnea, and no hospitalization within the prior 14 days. All diagnoses were retrospectively confirmed by two attending physicians, who independently reviewed medical records, radiographic reports, and laboratory data. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and standardized protocols were followed to ensure consistency. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) hospital-acquired pneumonia, (2) immunosuppressive conditions (e.g., HIV/AIDS, organ transplantation, chemotherapy within 6 months), (3) missing key clinical data, or (4) terminal illness with an expected survival of < 30 days due to non-pulmonary causes.

Definition of metabolic syndrome

Central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in male or ≥ 80 cm in female) is one of the diagnostic criteria of MetS outlined by the Chinese Diabetes Society (CDS) [9]. The International Diabetes Federation definition suggests that If BMI is > 30 kg/m², central obesity can be assumed and waist circumference does not need to be measured [10]. In this study we used Asian cutoff for obesity that is, ≥ 25 kg/m2 11. The MetS was defined as the presence of 3 of the following 5 criteria according to previous studies [11, 12]: (1) Obesity: BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2; (2) Hyperglycemia: FPG ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or 2-h PG ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, or known diabetes; (3) Hypertension: BP ≥ 130/85 mmHg or on treatment; (4) TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L; (5) HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L. Based on these criteria, patients were stratified into two groups: those with MetS and those without, at the time of CAP diagnosis.

Data collection and outcomes

Demographic data, comorbidities, chest X-ray, laboratory results, pathogens, and pneumonia severity index (PSI) scores were extracted from electronic medical records.

The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality rate, with survival data obtained from medical records or, when necessary, through telephone follow-ups. It was selected based on its widespread use in pneumonia outcome research and its relevance to capturing both short-term complications and post-discharge mortality risks in elderly patients. This timeframe allows for assessment of the cumulative impact of metabolic dysregulation on recovery and survival. Secondary outcomes, including 30-day mortality and hospital length of stay, were chosen to reflect early clinical deterioration and healthcare burden, respectively. These outcomes were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and previous studies on pneumonia and metabolic comorbidities [13, 14].

Statistical analysis

The overall completeness of the dataset was high, with missing data < 5% for all variables. Missing data for covariates were handled using multiple imputations with chained equations (MICE), under the assumption of missing at random (MAR).

To minimize potential bias arising from baseline differences between the MetS and non-MetS groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was applied. Propensity scores were estimated through logistic regression, incorporating covariates such as demographic data, comorbidities, chest X-ray, laboratory results, pathogens, and PSI scores. A 1:1 matching was conducted between MetS and non-MetS patients using a nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement, with a caliper width set to 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. To assess the balance of covariates after matching, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for all baseline characteristics.

After PSM, 336 patients (168 matched pairs) were included in the final analysis. Assuming that about 20% of participants would die by the time of the primary analysis (with a two-sided type I error rate of 5%) and a 10% non-response and attrition rate, a power of > 90% was provided to detect a hazard ratio of 2.0. This HR was chosen based on our exploratory data and supported by findings from a previous study by Liu et al. [15] which reported a similar HR for mortality risk in elderly pneumonia patients with diabetes mellitus.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using Student’s t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Kaplan-Meier curve and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used to analyze time-to-event data (90-day mortality). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using time-dependent covariates and log(-log[survival]) plots, which confirmed that the assumption was not violated. To enhance the robustness of findings, the subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the association of MetS status with 90-day mortality in patients stratified by age, gender and PSI. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. A completed STROBE checklist has been provided as a supplementary Table 1.

All statistical analyses were performed with the SPSS statistical software package (SPSS version 22.0 for Windows, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Results

A total of 853 patients diagnosed with CAP were initially enrolled in this study. After excluding 141 cases due to immunosuppressive conditions (n = 21), terminal illness (n = 26) and missing key clinical data (n = 94), the final analysis included 712 participants. Among them, 285 (40.0%) were identified as having MetS. PSM yielded 168 matched pairs, resulting in 336 patients for subsequent comparisons (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 1, all SMDs after matching were less than 0.1, indicating adequate balance across matched groups and suggesting that residual confounding was minimized. However, significant differences were observed in MetS components and medications between the two groups (Table 2 all p < 0.05). These findings confirm the successful matching of the cohorts while highlighting the distinct metabolic profiles of the MetS group.

The 90-day mortality rate was 29.8% in the MetS group and 11.9% in the non-MetS group (p < 0.01). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of elderly patients hospitalized with CAP, we found that MetS was significantly associated with increased short-term mortality. Specifically, patients with MetS had a 1.98-fold higher risk of 90-day all-cause mortality compared to those without MetS, even after multivariate adjustment. Furthermore, we observed a dose-response relationship, whereby an increasing number of MetS components correlated with progressively higher mortality risk. These findings suggest that MetS not only identifies a subgroup of patients with distinct metabolic and inflammatory profiles but also serves as an independent prognostic factor in elderly patients with CAP.

Our results are consistent with and extend prior research linking metabolic abnormalities to adverse outcomes in acute infections. In a recent study by Xu et al. [6] metabolic syndrome was significantly associated with increased 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), with a hazard ratio of 1.62 for 90-day mortality after multivariable adjustment. Moreover, that study also identified a dose-response relationship: mortality increased progressively with the number of MetS criteria met. These findings mirror the dose-dependent trend observed in our study among elderly patients hospitalized with CAP, supporting the notion that the burden of metabolic derangements—regardless of the specific acute respiratory condition—can worsen clinical outcomes. Additionally, the negative prognostic influence of MetS appears to extend across different respiratory infections, as shown in a multicenter study by Wang et al. [12] on patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). In that study, 19.3% of COVID-19 patients had MetS, and these individuals were significantly more likely to develop severe or critical illness (33.3% vs. 6.4% and 4.4% vs. 0.5%, respectively), with MetS being an independent risk factor for severe disease (OR = 7.668, 95% CI: 3.062–19.201). At 28-day follow-up, a substantially higher proportion of MetS patients showed residual bilateral pneumonia, suggesting that MetS may contribute not only to acute deterioration but also to delayed pulmonary recovery. The findings from COVID-19 and ARDS cohorts support the broader hypothesis that MetS predisposes elderly patients to more severe pulmonary complications and worse long-term outcomes regardless of the etiology of the infection. Compared with these previous studies, our investigation adds novel insights by focusing specifically on elderly CAP patients, showing that MetS predicts mortality. Collectively, these studies highlight the systemic vulnerability conferred by MetS and suggest a need for more personalized monitoring and intervention strategies for elderly patients with pulmonary infections and metabolic abnormalities.

Several mechanisms may underlie the association between MetS and poor outcomes in pneumonia. Chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, a hallmark of MetS, can impair both innate and adaptive immune responses, potentially reducing the host’s ability to clear pathogens [16]. Insulin resistance and hyperglycemia may disrupt neutrophil function and increase the risk of secondary infections [17, 18]. Moreover, endothelial dysfunction, commonly present in patients with hypertension and dyslipidemia, may compromise pulmonary microcirculation and gas exchange [19, 20]. These mechanisms, when combined, can amplify the severity of pneumonia and hinder recovery, particularly in elderly patients with reduced physiological reserves.

Our finding of a dose-response relationship between the number of MetS components and mortality is particularly noteworthy. Similar observations have been made in other respiratory research [6, 12] where incremental increases in MetS features correlate with greater risk of adverse outcomes. In the context of pneumonia, this implies that even the partial presence of MetS may confer elevated risk and underscores the potential utility of using the number of MetS components as a prognostic stratifier in clinical practice.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective design may introduce information and selection bias, although we attempted to mitigate this through strict inclusion criteria and PSM. Second, our study was conducted at a single tertiary care center in China, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Differences in geographic location, ethnic background, and healthcare practices may affect the prevalence and clinical impact of metabolic syndrome, as well as pneumonia management strategies. Therefore, caution is warranted when applying these results to broader or more diverse populations. Third, some unmeasured confounding factors, such as physical activity levels, nutritional status, or medication adherence, were not accounted for. Additionally, although BMI is a widely used proxy for obesity, it may not accurately reflect body fat distribution or muscle mass in elderly individuals. Given the retrospective design of our study, we were unable to include alternative measures such as waist circumference or body composition indices, which may better capture obesity-related risk in this population. Fourth, although we collected pathogen data, we did not analyze whether specific causative organisms differed between groups. Certain pathogens, such as multidrug-resistant bacteria, might be more prevalent in patients with MetS and could partly contribute to the observed differences in outcomes. Future studies should explore this potential association in more depth. Fifth, information on frequent outpatient visits, recent antibiotic use, or prior healthcare exposure was not available. These factors may increase the risk of infection with resistant organisms and contribute to worse prognosis, representing potential unmeasured confounders in our study. Finally, we did not evaluate long-term functional outcomes, which are particularly relevant in elderly populations.

In conclusion, metabolic syndrome is independently associated with increased short-term mortality and longer hospital stays in elderly patients with CAP. These findings suggest that MetS may serve as a useful marker for early risk stratification. Future prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate our findings and explore whether targeted metabolic interventions can improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
