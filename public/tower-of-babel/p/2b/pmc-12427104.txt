The effect of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi on the growth of wheat seedlings with contrasting phosphorus use efficiencies under low phosphorus stress
Authors: Qian Sun, Juncheng Wang, Hong Zhang, Lirong Yao, Erjing Si, Baochun Li, Yaxiong Meng, Chan Wang, Ke Yang, Xunwu Shang, Xinyang Xie, Huajun Wang, Xiaole Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
HighlightsAMF promotes phosphorus uptake in inefficient wheat varieties, resulting in significant enhancement of their phosphorus utilization efficiency; Inoculation of AMF can enhance the stem strength of wheat varieties with low phosphorus utilization efficiency and enhance the uprightness of wheat plants, which may provide new ideas for research on the resistance of wheat cultivation to collapse. Introduction

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are widely distributed in soil and can form mutualistic symbiosis with the roots of over 80% of terrestrial vascular plants [1], playing a crucial role in plant nutrient acquisition from soil [2, 3]. The establishment of this mycorrhizal symbiosis requires signal exchange, with strigolactones (SLs) secreted by plant roots serving as essential signaling molecules [4, 5]. These SLs can stimulate AMF spore germination and enhance branching [6, 7]. When AMF perceive SLs secreted by crop roots under low-phosphorus (P) stress [8], they activate symbiosis-related signaling pathways [9, 10].

As a globally important cereal crop, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is highly susceptible to phosphorus deficiency, which severely constrains its productivity. The inoculation of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) has been shown to effectively counteract phosphorus limitation effects on wheat growth. After AMF colonizes the roots of host plants, it forms vesicles and mycelium in the root cortex cells, which increases the contact area between the crop root system and the soil, expands the absorption range, improves the crop’s uptake of nitrogen, phosphorus and other nutrients in the soil, and promotes the accumulation of nutrients [11, 12], and at the same time, the plant can also provide the fungus with a carbon source and energy to achieve a mutually beneficial symbiosis [14]. This type of nutrient exchange plays a key role in soil nutrient cycling and plant health. It has been shown that AM fungi take up organic phosphate from the surrounding environment through phosphate transporter proteins located in the ectomycelium, which are then integrated into the vesicles of the ectomycelium of the plant root system, and then transferred through the vesicles to the endomycelium for plant uptake and utilization [13]. This phosphorus uptake process relies on phosphorus transporter proteins specifically localized in the plexiform arbuscular membrane (PAM), the transcriptional expression of which is induced by mycorrhizal symbiosis, as in potato (StPT3) (Solanum tuberosum) [14], sabal bean (MtPT4) (Medicago truncatula) [15], and rice (OsPT11) (Oryza sativa) [16]. In other words, the plant root system sensed phosphorus stress, which promoted the occurrence of mycorrhizal symbiosis, and the external mycelium colonized to the crop root system further mobilized the expression of phosphorus transport proteins, thus promoting the decomposition of immobilized phosphorus in the soil by mycorrhizae, which in turn increased the uptake and utilization of phosphorus by the crop. It has also been shown that after the establishment of mycorrhizal symbiosis, mycorrhizal symbionts can activate soil organic phosphorus through their own secretion or by inducing the root system of the host plant to secrete organic acids and acid phosphatase (APC) [17, 18], so as to ameliorate the loss brought about by soil phosphorus depletion [19], and thus to effectively resist the low phosphorus stress. Zuccaro et al. showed that the mycorrhizal fungus Serendipita indica-associated ACPase enzyme is activated in phosphorus deficient environments [20], whereas acid phosphatases alleviate low phosphorus stress by hydrolyzing organophosphorus compounds to release plant-available inorganic phosphorus (Pi). For example, wheat inter-root soil acid phosphatase activity was directly proportional to the rate of AMF colonization [21], the P content of AMF-inoculated maize roots increased by 3.29-fold [22], and the P concentration of AMF-inoculated walnut leaves increased by 21.0-26.2% compared with that of non-AMF-inoculated walnuts at low P levels [23], and inoculation with AMF increased aboveground biomass of soybean by 16.5% and also increased phosphorus content [24]. It can be seen that through the mycorrhizal symbiosis pathway, the P uptake and transport efficiency is higher, and the excessive consumption of inter-root P is also reduced, which not only alleviates the negative impact of low phosphorus on the growth of crops, but also reduces the phosphorus fertilizer application during the production of farmland, so as to achieve environmental protection and resource conservation while ensuring the normal conditions for the growth and development of crops. Emerging research indicates that AMF inoculation significantly influences root hair morphology, particularly length and surface area, across diverse soil phosphorus levels [25]. Mechanistic studies have linked these morphological adaptations to auxin (IAA) accumulation in root tissues [26, 27]. Such AMF-mediated architectural modifications appear critical for maintaining nutrient uptake and water balance in host plants facing abiotic stresses. Collectively, these findings provide both a theoretical basis and practical approaches for improving wheat production through AMF application.

Currently, no comprehensive study has examined AMF’s effects on wheat varieties with contrasting phosphorus use efficiencies. This knowledge gap is particularly significant under varying phosphorus concentrations, which directly affect AMF symbiosis efficiency. This study investigated morphological, physiological changes and gene expression levels during the growth and development of wheat varieties with different phosphorus concentrations and phosphorus utilization efficiencies in symbiosis with AMF in order to clearly analyze the effects of mycorrhizal symbiosis on the growth and development of wheat varieties with different phosphorus utilization efficiencies, to screen out star genes associated with low-phosphorus stress in the context of mycorrhizal symbiosis, and to define the metabolic pathways of the genes concerned so as to lay the foundation for subsequent studies on the molecular mechanisms of genes. This will lay the foundation for subsequent research on the molecular mechanism of genes.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

This experiment adopted a pot culture method using two wheat varieties provided by the Cereal Crop Genetics and Breeding Laboratory of Gansu Agricultural University: the phosphorus-efficient cultivar SW2 and the phosphorus-inefficient cultivar SW14. Under low phosphorus conditions, the phosphorus-efficient wheat variety SW2 had a greater adaptive capacity, as evidenced by thicker root and stem diameters, shorter root lengths, and less anthocyanin accumulation at the base of the stem compared to the phosphorus-inefficient wheat variety SW14. The tested inoculum was Rhizophagus intraradices (strain code: BGC BJ09), obtained from the Institute of Plant Nutrition and Resources, Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (available at http://www.yzs.baafs.net.cn/). The inoculum was propagated using clover (Trifolium sp.) as the host plant, following the cultivation method described by Selvakumar et al. [28]. After three months of pot culture, the inoculum was harvested, which included all substrate, root fragments, spores, and hyphae from the culture containers. The harvested inoculum was stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Experimental design

Treatments

The study employed two wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) genotypes with contrasting phosphorus-use efficiencies: the phosphorus-efficient genotype SW2 and the phosphorus-inefficient genotype SW14. Two phosphorus (P) regimes were established: Low phosphorus (LP): 0.05 mmol/L available P; normal phosphorus (NP): 0.5 mmol/L available P. Mycorrhizal inoculation included AMF treatment: plants were inoculated with Rhizophagus intraradices (formerly Glomus intraradices) strain BGC BJ09; control (CK): not inoculated with AMF. Nutrient Solution Preparation: Phosphorus concentrations were adjusted using KH₂PO₄, with K₂SO₄ supplementation in LP treatments to maintain potassium (K) homeostasis. Experimental Design: 2 phosphorus levels * 2 AMF levels * 2 varieties, for a total of 8 treatments, each of which was set up in replicates of 3 pots with 5 plants planted in each pot to ensure that there was a sufficient amount of material to complete this experimental design. All three replications of the experimental sampling were randomly selected from one of the three pots, rather than from the same pot.

Plant cultivation protocol

Wheat seeds with uniform size and fullness were selected, sterilized by 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 5 min, and rinsed three times with distilled water. The sterilized seeds were placed on moist filter paper in germination dishes for 20 h until radicle emerged. Vermiculite and sand were mixed at a 2:1 ratio (333 g vermiculite: 167 g sand) by autoclaving under different treatments, soil samples and strains were mixed in pots (the amount of fungus used in each pot was 30 g, i.e., the amount of inoculum for each wheat plant was 6 g), and watered with appropriate amount of sterile water. And then the mixture was slightly dried before the germination of wheat seeds in the pots. One week after the growth of the beginning of the phosphorus stress treatment. The capacity of the pots was 500 ml, and each pot should be watered with 26 ml of nutrient solution every time, every 3d. The growth conditions were 22 °C, 16 h of light/8 h of darkness, and the samples were sampled and analyzed when they reached 30 d of growth.

Test methods

Phenotypic determination

The roots were washed and kept intact, and their plant height, root length, stem thickness (diameter of the 2nd-3rd internode at the base), leaf width, fresh weight, and dry weight were measured. For each treatment, three biological replicates were analyzed, with data collected from three individual wheat plants per treatment batch. Then the intact single plants were selected under different treatments, the aboveground and underground parts were separated, the fresh weight was measured with electronic scale, and the data were recorded. The fresh weight samples were put in Kraft paper bags, then dried in an oven at 108 °C for 12 h, and at 80 °C for 24-48 h to a constant weight which was measured with an electronic scale, and the data were recorded. At 30 d of inoculation, the seedlings at different treatment levels were harvested. Roots were rinsed with tap water and scanned using an EPSON Expression 12000XL Root Scanner, and root morphology parameters obtained during the scanning process were analyzed, including total root length and total surface area.

Mycorrhizal fixation

With the treatment method of Phillips [29], wheat seedling roots under different varieties of mycorrhizal treatment were selected, and small segments of 3–5 mm were chosen from the root tip of the main root upward and immediately put into the formaldehyde-acetic acid-ethanol fixative (FAA) in the refrigerator at 4 °C after sampling for 24 h. Root morphology and root hair development under mycorrhizal symbiosis were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM model number: S-3400 N) following standard sample preparation, including alcohol dehydration, vacuum gold coating, and critical-point drying. The lateral roots of wheat roots inoculated with AMF were intercepted 1–2 cm and immediately put into FAA at 4 °C for 24 h. Samples from 9 to 12 replicates per treatment were taken for reserve (only a sample size of 3–5 replicates was used in the actual experiment, and after the samples were taken out from FAA, the fixative on the surface of the roots was washed away with running water and then stained with the trypan blue staining method [30], and the staining time was controlled at 10–15 min to ensure that the staining solution fully penetrated into the sample tissues. A Leica optical microscope was used to observe the root mycelium colonization of the stained samples.

Determination of hormone content

Wheat plants treated for 30 days were sampled under different treatments and different tissue parts (roots and leaves), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in the refrigerator at −80 °C for measurement. The IAA content in roots and leaves and SL content in roots were determined by enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA), and the absorbance value was measured at 425 nm, which was commissioned to be determined by Shanghai JW Biotechnology Co.

Paraffin sections

Wheat seedlings grown for 30 d under different treatments were selected, 3–6 replicates were selected for each treatment, and the parts for sampling were kept consistent, put into the FAA in the refrigerator at 4 °C for 24 h. The residual fixative on the surface of the samples was washed off with distilled water and then dehydrated by alcohol gradient. The samples were transparent by using anhydrous ethanol and xylene. The transparent samples were embedded in wax using the paraffin embedding machine and the slices were trimmed, and then dewaxed, rehydrated and stained. Finally, the samples were sealed with neutral gum and observed with a forward and inverted integrated fluorescence microscope.

Determination of acid phosphatase activity and P content

Wheat plants treated for 30 d were sampled under different treatments and different tissue parts (roots and leaves), quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in a −80 °C refrigerator to be measured. The acid phosphatase (APC) activity test kit was purchased from Beijing Box Biotechnology Co. According to the instruction manual for specific operation steps, phosphorus content was determined according to the molybdenum blue method [31] with modifications. Briefly, wheat samples from different treatments were dried at 65 °C for 72 h to constant weight. Three biological replicates (0.04 g dry weight each) of roots, stems, and leaves from each treatment were nitrified in concentrated H₂SO₄-H₂O₂ (5:1, v/v) at 320 °C, respectively. Absorbance was measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (at 660 nm).

Transcriptome sequencing

A symbiotic system of wheat roots with different phosphorus utilization efficiencies and mycorrhiza from branching was established under normal phosphorus and low phosphorus conditions, and 24 samples (8 treatments and 3 replicates) of roots sampled from different treatments were subjected to RNA-seq sequencing when incubated for 30d. The treatments were named A (SW2-NP), A1 (SW2-NP-AMF), B (SW2-LP), B1 (SW2-LP-AMF), C (SW14-NP), C1 (SW14-NP-AMF), D (SW14-LP), and D1 (SW14-LP-AMF). The datasets analysed during the current study are available in the NCBI repository, PRJNA1276627。.

Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (qPCR) validation

Wheat roots and leaves sampled at 30 d of growth were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a −80 °C refrigerator. For transcriptome sequencing and qPCR validation. Eight genes highly expressed in the SW14 material under LP + AMF treatment were selected for qPCR validation based on transcriptome screening. The internal reference gene was selected as Triticum aestivum Actin, and the primer design was based on the CDS region of the gene, and the design of primer sequences is shown in Annex 1.

Methods of analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to assess the effect of AMF inoculation (Factor 1) and varieties with different phosphorus-phosphorus use efficiency in wheat (Factor 2). Degrees of freedom (df) are reported for each F-statistic. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All raw data and analyzed files are available in the Supplementary Material”.

Results and discussion

Effect of 30-day AMF inoculation on wheat phenotype

The experimental results showed that at low phosphorus concentration, the plant height, root length, leaf width, and stem thickness of phosphorus-inefficient wheat SW14 after symbiosis with AMF were significantly different from CK (without AMF) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A-D), in which the plant height, leaf width, and stem thickness were significantly (P < 0.05) higher than those of CK, and the root length was significantly (P < 0.05) lower than that of CK., which is consistent with the results of the study of sorghum [32] inoculated with AMF after inoculation with AMF, which is consistent with the findings of the study. However, in phosphorus-efficient material SW2, only the plant height and leaf width were significantly (P < 0.05) better than those of the CK treatment, and the rest did not have any significant differences, which was related to the material’s own absorption and utilization of nutrients.

Mycorrhizal colonization

Trypan blue staining revealed successful AMF colonization in both SW2 and SW14 wheat roots, with distinct mycorrhizal structures, including intraradical hyphae and vesicles, observed within root tissues (Fig. 2a-e). Abundant extraradical hyphae were clearly visible on root surfaces. Notably, the phosphorus-inefficient genotype SW14 exhibited more extensive hyphal networks under low-phosphorus (LP) stress compared to SW2. This divergence may arise from genotypic differences-SW2, a phosphorus-efficient cultivar, activates intrinsic low-P stress adaptations (e.g., PHT1 transporter upregulation), which when sensed by AMF, attenuates symbiosis, resulting in restrained hyphal expansion and limited vesicle formation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations revealed distinct differences in root colonization patterns between the two wheat genotypes under phosphorus-deficient conditions. Compared with SW2, the roots of SW14 inoculated with AMF under low phosphorus stress had more mycelial colonization, more vigorous growth of mycelium and root hairs at the root tip, and extremely dense mycelial distribution (Fig. 2f-m). This was due to the fact that under low phosphorus treatment, the ability of SW14 to adapt to adversity stress was much weaker than that of SW2, and AMF sensed low phosphorus in the root system, accelerating the formation of vesicles from the branching vesicles in the root epidermal cells [28].

Effect of 30-day AMF inoculation on endogenous hormones in wheat

AMF promotes root development in plants. Studies have demonstrated that AMF colonization enhances tomato root and stem growth through the production of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) [37] and there are also studies that have clearly proposed that mycorrhizal symbiosis can significantly increase the accumulation of IAA [38]. Through this experimental study, it was found that the IAA content in the roots after AMF inoculation increased significantly in both varieties at both normal and low phosphorus concentrations, and reached a highly significant level (P < 0.01) in SW14 (e.g., Fig. 3A), and SW14-NP + AMF increased by 0.45-fold compared with the SW14-NP treatment, and SW14-LP + AMF increased by 1.05-fold compared with the SW14-LP treatment. LP treatment by 1.05-fold; while the IAA content in leaves was significantly higher than CK only under LP (e.g., Fig. 3B), in which SW14-LP + AMF increased the IAA content in leaves by 106% compared with SW14-LP treatment to a highly significant level (P < 0.01). Some studies found that inoculation with AMF caused significant accumulation of growth hormone (IAA) in seedling wheat and promoted growth changes in the wheat root system [27, 38, 39], which is consistent with the results of this study. Overall, the effect of AMF inoculation on IAA synthesis showed differences in wheat varieties with different phosphorus utilization efficiencies and in different tissue sites, and AMF inoculation promoted IAA synthesis more in phosphorus-inefficient varieties. Studies have shown that ethylene and growth hormone can jointly promote fiber SCW deposition, but this experimental study found no significant change in ethylene content after inoculation with AMF compared with the control.

Analysis of IAA and SL content in wheat cultivars with different phosphorus utilization efficiency inoculated with AMF under low phosphorus stress

Strigolactones (SLs) is carotenoid derivatives of plant roots that promote symbiosis between plants and soil microorganisms. It has been shown that SL exudation in roots promotes mycorrhizal elongation and branching of AMF [40]. It is an essential phytohormone for plants to cope with phosphorus deficiency [41], and at the same time, phosphorus deficiency is one of the important signals for SL biosynthesis [42]. The results of the present study showed that SL was significantly accumulated in wheat after symbiosis with AMF, and the accumulation was especially higher under low phosphorus stress (e.g., Fig. 3C), and Akiyama, K, Boyno G, et al. [43, 44] also suggested that while AMF responds to SL signals released by the plant and establishes a symbiotic relationship with the host plant, the plant is also able to enhance signals to synthesize SL in the presence of AMF, which is consistent with the results of the present study. results of this study. This suggests that inoculation with AMF can promote the synthesis and expression of SL in wheat roots, and at the same time enhance the establishment of a symbiotic relationship between the mycelium and the wheat root system, realizing the reciprocal symbiosis between wheat and the mycorrhizal fungi from the branching mycorrhizal fungi.

It is worth noting that in this study, we found that the trends of SL and IAA contents were almost the same after AMF inoculation, and Hayward et al. also found that IAA and SL interacted in a specific pathway [45], and this interaction could affect root architecture, lateral root development and mycorrhizal colonization, thus improving nutrient uptake and plant adaptability [46, 47], which will be further discussed in the following study. This phenomenon will be further discussed in the following studies.

Effect of 30-day AMF inoculation on stem structure of wheat

Following the results of the analysis of hormone levels, a closer study of the stem structure was carried out, and it was described in a related article that the inoculation with AMF not only increased the stomatal conductance of alfalfa under salt stress but also improved its utilization of CO₂ [48]. Therefore, it is speculated that the plants inoculated with AMF should exhibit more developed transport tissues. Through the observation of stem anatomical structure, it was found that the stem structure of wheat in the two treatments, SW14-NP + AMF and SW14-LP + AMF, was significantly thickened compared with that of the non-inoculated with AMF, that the number of transport tissues also increased significantly, that the thickness of the mechanical tissues of the stem wall increased significantly, and the epidermal cell wall was thickened, the number of layers increased, the thickness was large and more lignified and fibrous, and the number and area of vascular bundles in the stalks significantly increased and enlarged (Fig. 4). To further verify this phenomenon, wheat stem lignin and cellulose contents were determined and found to be highly significant (P less than 0.01) higher than CK in each treatment after inoculation with AMF.This phenomenon can be further verified at the transcriptome level in conjunction with the GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment results below. In contrast, Mendoza-Soto A B et al. also found that AMF could promote cell wall thickening by up-regulating the expression of cellulose synthase (CesA) and lignin synthesis genes (PAL, 4CL) [49], a result that is consistent with the results of the present study both at the hormonal level and at the molecular level. However, the material specificity resulted in no significant changes in the stems of phosphorus-efficient wheat SW2 (Fig. 5A, B), suggesting that, in terms of stem structure optimization, wheat varieties with inherently superior conditions may not produce extremely significant changes to adapt to the adversity stress after inoculation with AMF, which has a certain degree of resistance on its own; whereas phosphorusine-inefficient varieties possessed a more developed transport tissue and a more abundant stems to cope with the adversity stress, while phosphorus-inefficient varieties have more developed transport tissues and fuller stems after AMF inoculation.

Effect of AMF inoculation on lignin, cellulose, and silicon content of wheat under different phosphorus concentration treatments

By observing the morphology of plants inoculated with AMF and without AMF at 30 d of treatment, it was found that the two wheat varieties inoculated with AMF were greatly improved morphologically, not only the leaves were more abundant, but also the uprightness was obviously improved, especially the two wheat varieties under low phosphorus stress, which was especially obvious, and this phenomenon was attributed to the fact that after inoculation with AMF, the nutrient absorption and utilization by the root system was increased, and the nutrient exchange of the plant was promoted, thus the stem strength of wheat inoculated with AMF was greatly improved, which was originally due to the accumulation of lignin and cellulose. This phenomenon is due to the inoculation of AMF, increase the absorption and utilization of nutrients in the root system, promote the exchange of nutrients in the plant root system, lignin and cellulose accumulation, so as to strengthen the stem strength of AMF-inoculated wheat, greatly improving the original lack of nutrients due to the soft collapse of plant stems and leaves phenomenon, thus showing a stronger uprightness (Fig. 6).

Effect of 30-day AMF inoculation on APC content in wheat

Acid phosphatase (APC) is an adaptation-inducing enzyme, and mycorrhizal symbiosis can help host plant roots adapt to low phosphorus environments by enhancing the secretion of acid phosphatase from the root system [50], and promote phosphorus uptake by wheat roots [51]. In this study, AMF inoculation significantly increased APC accumulation in the phosphorus-inefficient cultivar SW14, with root and leaf APC content rising by 22.8% and 25.2%, respectively (P < 0.01), under low-phosphorus conditions (Fig. 7A, B). The strong adaptation of the phosphorus-efficient material SW2 itself to low phosphorus stress resulted in the rise of APC content after inoculation with AMF not reaching significant levels. This indicates that when wheat itself is in poor developmental condition and the soil is P-deficient, inoculation with AMF can effectively promote the secretion of APC by the root system, accelerate the decomposition and release of soil organic phosphorus and other organic phosphorus, and improve the efficiency of phosphorus utilization by wheat, thus promoting the growth and development of wheat. Some studies have shown that colonization of AM fungi enables host plants to effectively take up inorganic nutrients, especially phosphorus, from the soil [52], which is consistent with the results of this study.

Effect of 30-day AMF inoculation on P content of wheat roots, stems, and leaves

Mycorrhizal symbiosis can significantly increase host plant root vigor and promote nutrient accumulation [53], and the organic acids and phosphatases secreted by AMF can activate a portion of the insoluble phosphate fixed in the soil, leading to a better uptake of soil phosphorus by plants [54]. It was found that the P content of roots, stems, and leaves of the phosphorus-inefficient material SW14 inoculated with AMF under low phosphorus stress increased extremely significantly (P < 0.01), by 113.55%, 75.11%, and 48.64%, respectively, compared with CK. In the phosphorus-efficient material SW2, only the treatment SW2-LP + AMF in roots increased the P content by 29.24%, and the increase in the rest of the parts did not reach a significant level. Overall, the P content of wheat inoculated with AMF was always higher than that of treatments without AMF (Fig. 7C-E), which indicated that inoculation with AMF could promote the absorption and accumulation of phosphorus in wheat.

Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis drives the synthesis of lignin monomers (e.g., pinoresinol, mustardiol) and promotes cell wall lignification, which significantly enhances the mechanical strength of stalks. Activation of fructose and mannose metabolism provides energy and carbon skeleton support for phosphorus uptake. The MAPK signaling pathway—plants might coordinate the synergistic effect of phosphorus uptake and culm development by integrating mycorrhizal symbiotic signaling and stress response. Under the joint action of these pathways, it not only effectively promotes phosphorus transport and uptake, but also helps to enhance the adaptive capacity of wheat in adversity.

Conclusion

Compared with uninoculated, low-phosphorus-stressed wheat, AMF inoculation significantly improved the growth and dry matter accumulation of wheat, enhanced IAA, SL, and APC activities, and increased phosphorus accumulation in wheat; after AMF inoculation, the anatomical map of wheat stem structure revealed that the phosphorus-inefficient wheat SW14 showed well-developed conductive tissues and fuller pith lumen in the stem, and that the lignin and cellulose activities were also significantly enhanced. TTranscriptome analysis revealed that the pathway “Cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis” was significantly enriched in the AMF-inoculated treatments, indicating that AMF inoculation activates biosynthetic pathways for cell wall constituents (e.g., cellulose and lignin) directly within the cell wall. This suggested that AMF inoculation could activate the pathway related to cell wall component (e.g., cellulose, lignin) synthesis and thus directly enhance the culm structure, but a clearer mechanism of action needs to be further investigated.

Generally, AMF inoculation exerted a positive effect on wheat growth under both normal and low phosphorus (P) conditions. However, this positive impact varied among wheat varieties with differing P utilization efficiencies. Specifically, the promotion effect was more pronounced in P-inefficient varieties compared to their P-efficient counterparts, where the growth enhancement was minimal and statistically insignificant. This disparity was closely associated with the intrinsic defense mechanisms of the plant materials against adversity stress. Notably, AMF inoculation significantly boosted wheat growth and nutrient accumulation under low P stress. These findings suggested that upon establishing mycorrhizal symbiosis, AMF could detect low P stress in the wheat root system and facilitate better P absorption in P-inefficient wheat varieties. Additionally, mycorrhizal symbiosis triggered substantial hormonal changes within the plant, activating multiple pathways related to stress responses, such as those involved in P stress mitigation and stem strength enhancement, thereby aiding plants in coping more effectively with adverse conditions. At the same time, clumping mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) is also considered a safe, economic and environmentally friendly method for relieving crop growth pressure; from an ecological point of view, on the one hand, mycorrhizal symbiosis reduces phosphorus fertilizer application, lowers phosphorus fertilizer dosage, and saves phosphorus resource consumption, and on the other hand, it is simple to propagate and saves costs; in the long run, this mycorrhizal symbiosis production method can bring low-phosphorus soil increased production and income, which is worth studying in depth. Therefore, the application of AMF can not only reduce the input of fertilizer in crop production, but also improve the soil environment, promote the growth and development of crops, and provide a new idea of environmental protection for the cultivation of high-yielding and stable food crops.

Shortcomings and prospects

This experiment only investigated the physiological and biochemical changes and the molecular-level regulation of wheat when inoculated with AMF for 30 d. The time point of the experiment was too single to clarify the specific trend of the effect of AMF on the growth of wheat. According to the results of the current study, the inoculation of AMF not only improved the ability of phosphorus inefficient wheat materials to cope with adversity stress, but also enhanced the stem strength of wheat, so we hypothesized that the inoculation of AMF might enhance the resistance of wheat to failure.If the mechanism is to be clarified, further research is needed. It would be better to study the physiological and biochemical changes and molecular regulatory mechanisms of AMF inoculation during the whole reproductive period of wheat, and then to realize high-yield and high-quality breeding of wheat in an ecologically friendly way.

Regarding the research on the effect of mycorrhizal symbiosis on wheat stem strength, although there have been some explorations at home and abroad, the mechanism analysis is still in the exploratory stage, and there are still controversies about the large differences in the effects of different strains of bacteria, and the conclusion of universality has not yet been determined.The level of use of other fertilizers, soil type, and other external factors, etc. can affect the benefits of AMF use, and a large number of studies are needed for the future to analyze these issues.
