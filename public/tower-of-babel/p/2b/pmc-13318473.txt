Flow Diversion in Fetal-Type Posterior Communicating Artery Aneurysms: Anatomical Challenges and Treatment Strategy
Authors: Hidehisa Nishi, Akira Ishii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fetal-type posterior communicating artery (PcomA) aneurysms remain a difficult subgroup in the flow-diverter era. In these lesions, the PcomA supplies a substantial portion of the posterior cerebral artery territory because the ipsilateral P1 segment is absent, hypoplastic, or functionally insufficient. The branch therefore functions as a major outflow channel, and persistent distal demand may maintain circulation through the aneurysm–neck complex after flow diversion.This review summarizes the anatomical basis of fetal-type PcomA aneurysms and the current evidence for clipping, coiling, intrasaccular devices, and flow diversion. Among PcomA aneurysms treated with flow diverters, non-fetal lesions generally show better angiographic outcomes than fetal-type lesions. In a recent meta-analysis, complete occlusion at final follow-up was 77% in non-fetal aneurysms versus 42% in fetal-type aneurysms. A multicenter comparative cohort similarly reported complete occlusion in 81.8% versus 43.7%, with a markedly shorter median time to occlusion in non-fetal lesions (6 vs. 51 months). Earlier fetal-type series often showed persistent aneurysm filling and low complete occlusion rates, whereas more recent selected cohorts have reported complete or near-complete occlusion in 60%–80% of cases, suggesting that flow diversion can still be effective in carefully selected anatomies and with tailored strategies.Overall, clipping and coiling remain important branch-preserving options, and intrasaccular treatment may be reasonable in selected lesions with favorable geometry. Flow diversion should be considered selectively, particularly for large, recurrent, broad-necked, or morphologically complex aneurysms, while recognizing that robust fetal circulation may delay or limit aneurysm occlusion. Current evidence remains constrained by small retrospective series, inconsistent definitions of fetal anatomy, and heterogeneous device strategies.

Introduction

Posterior communicating artery (PcomA) aneurysms are common lesions at the internal carotid artery (ICA) branch junction, but they do not represent a single anatomical entity. Some behave more like ICA sidewall aneurysms, whereas others are more appropriately understood as branch-dependent lesions. This distinction has become increasingly important in the flow-diverter era.

Flow diverters (FDs) are effective for many ICA sidewall aneurysms, but their performance is less predictable when the aneurysm neck incorporates a branch with substantial physiological demand. Fetal-type PcomA aneurysms exemplify this problem because the PcomA supplies a substantial portion of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) territory when the ipsilateral P1 segment is absent, hypoplastic, or functionally insufficient. In that setting, the PcomA functions as an important outflow pathway that must be preserved, and this branch-related demand may influence the angiographic response to reconstructive treatment.

Accordingly, fetal-type and non-fetal PcomA aneurysms should not be considered a single treatment group. This review focuses on the anatomical and hemodynamic features that distinguish fetal-type lesions and on how these features influence the relative roles and limitations of current treatment strategies, particularly flow diversion.

Literature Search and Scope of Review

This narrative review was based on a targeted literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE for articles published up to March 2026. Search terms included “posterior communicating artery aneurysm,” “PcomA aneurysm,” “fetal posterior cerebral artery,” “fetal-type posterior communicating artery,” “flow diverter,” “Pipeline embolization device” (Medtronic Neurovascular, Irvine, CA, USA), “coiling,” “stent-assisted coiling,” “WEB” (Woven EndoBridge [WEB] Aneurysm Embolization System; Terumo Neuro, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA), and “intrasaccular device.” Additional relevant articles were identified from the reference lists of selected publications. We included clinical series, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, technical reports, and hemodynamic studies that addressed the anatomy, treatment strategy, or angiographic outcomes of PcomA aneurysms, with particular attention to fetal-type configurations. Because this was a narrative review, no formal systematic review protocol, quantitative meta-analysis, or risk-of-bias assessment was performed.

Anatomy and Hemodynamic Peculiarities of Fetal-Type PcomA Aneurysms

PcomA anatomy exists along a developmental spectrum, and this spectrum directly influences aneurysm behavior at the PcomA origin. Broadly, PCA circulation can be classified as adult type, in which the P1 segment is larger than the PcomA (P1 >PcomA); transitional or intermediate type, in which the vessels are similar in caliber (P1 ≈ PcomA); and fetal type, in which the PcomA is larger than the P1 segment (P1 <PcomA) (Fig. 1).1) This classification reflects the relative contribution of carotid and vertebrobasilar inflow to PCA perfusion and should be understood not only as a morphological distinction but also as the developmental result of how posterior circulation supply matures.

Spectrum of PcomA configurations relevant to PcomA aneurysms. Schematic illustration of adult, transitional/intermediate, and fetal-type PcomA configurations. In the adult type, the P1 segment is larger than the PcomA; in the transitional/intermediate type, P1 and PcomA are of similar caliber; and in the fetal type, the PcomA is larger than and functionally dominant over the ipsilateral P1 segment. When the ipsilateral P1 segment is absent, this configuration may be referred to as a true fetal type.

PcomA, posterior communicating artery

From an embryological perspective, the PCA territory is initially supplied mainly by the internal carotid system through the primitive PcomA, and with maturation, the vertebrobasilar system and the P1 segment enlarge to assume a greater role in PCA perfusion.1) Adult-type anatomy therefore represents the usual shift toward vertebrobasilar dominance, whereas fetal-type anatomy reflects persistence of carotid dominance due to incomplete development of the P1 segment. Transitional forms lie between these 2 extremes. In addition to its hemodynamic relevance, fetal-type anatomy has also been reported to be associated with rupture in PcomA aneurysms.2)

The literature, however, does not apply a single uniform definition of fetal-type anatomy. As shown in Table 1, some studies reserve the term for cases with an absent P1 segment, whereas others include lesions with a hypoplastic but visible P1. Transitional, partial fetal, and full fetal patterns are also used inconsistently. This matters because apparently similar case series may in fact include lesions with different hemodynamic dependence on the PcomA. In this review, fetal-type PcomA anatomy is defined as a configuration in which the PcomA is larger than the ipsilateral P1 segment and provides dominant or major supply to the ipsilateral PCA territory. This includes both true or full fetal-type anatomy, in which the P1 segment is absent, and partial fetal-type anatomy, in which the P1 segment is hypoplastic but visible. Transitional anatomy, in which the P1 segment and PcomA are of similar caliber, is not considered fetal-type in our framework, although some cited studies included transitional cases within their fetal-type definitions; such differences are explicitly noted when interpreting individual studies.

In adult-type anatomy, the PcomA mainly functions as a communicating vessel, and the PCA territory is supplied predominantly through the basilar apex and P1 segment. Recent patient-specific computational modeling further suggests that increasing fetal dominance shifts PCA perfusion progressively toward the carotid system and alters local hemodynamic indices in the PcomA-related circulation, supporting the view that fetal-type anatomy is not merely a morphological variant but a functionally distinct flow configuration.3) In fetal-type anatomy, the PcomA becomes a major distal outflow channel. The aneurysm, ICA, and PcomA should therefore be regarded as a coupled anatomical and hemodynamic unit rather than as a simple ICA sidewall lesion.

The therapeutic implication is direct: in fetal-type anatomy, the PcomA serves as a major distal outflow channel, and persistent branch demand may maintain circulation through the aneurysm–neck complex after FD placement. Fetal-type PcomA aneurysms therefore occupy an intermediate position between simple sidewall aneurysms and branch-incorporated lesions, which may partly explain their less predictable angiographic response to reconstructive treatment.

Current Treatment Modalities and Clinical Evidence for PcomA Aneurysms

Management of PcomA aneurysms remains heterogeneous because anatomical configuration, branch incorporation, and hemodynamic dependence vary substantially among lesions. The core goals are durable aneurysm exclusion, preservation of important branch flow, and low treatment-related morbidity; no single modality is optimal in all cases.

Microsurgical clipping remains an important option when immediate and durable aneurysm exclusion is prioritized, because it can provide reliable and definitive neck closure without relying on delayed endothelial remodeling. In fetal-type PcomA aneurysms, however, this strength must be balanced against the technical complexity of preserving a hemodynamically important branch. The available surgical literature suggests that clipping offers excellent anatomic efficacy, with aneurysm obliteration achieved in most or all reported cases, but at the cost of non-negligible procedural morbidity. In an early series of 24 clipped aneurysms arising at the origin of a fetal PCA (fPCA), intraoperative rupture occurred in 17%, and postoperative angiography demonstrated fPCA occlusion in 1 patient (3%), resulting in an occipital infarct.4) In a later 74-patient clipping series, complete aneurysm obliteration was achieved in all cases, but postoperative complications were observed in 32.4%, although 85.1% still had a favorable 3-month outcome.5) Similarly, in a 119-patient lateral supraorbital series, clipping was technically successful in all patients, yet perioperative complications occurred in 41 cases (34.4%), including 5 cases of postoperative infarction, and only 89 (74.8%) had a good prognosis at follow-up.6) Taken together, these data suggest that clipping can achieve dependable aneurysm closure in fetal-type lesions, but this benefit must be weighed against a complication profile that is not negligible.

Coiling for fetal-type PcomA aneurysms is technically feasible and generally safe, but angiographic durability remains a limitation, particularly in wide-neck or branch-incorporated lesions. In an early series of 9 fetal-type aneurysms, fPCA patency was preserved in all cases, complete or near-complete occlusion was achieved in 88.9% (8/9), and procedure-related morbidity and mortality were both 0%.7) In a larger cohort of 480 coiled PcomA aneurysms, the overall recanalization rate was 33.1%, with a higher crude rate in fetal-type than non-fetal lesions (37.8% vs. 26.9%), although this difference was no longer significant after matched analysis.8) Similarly, recurrence occurred in 30.9% (53/171) of coiled PcomA aneurysms, with larger maximum diameter, incomplete initial occlusion, and the absence of adjunctive techniques (stent or balloon) emerging as major predictors of failure.9) Adjunctive techniques may improve durability: in 19 unruptured dome-origin fetal-type lesions, stenting into the fPCA improved occlusion and 1-year recanalization-free survival without symptomatic complications, and in a small λ-stenting series, complete occlusion was achieved in 75.0% with no perioperative complications or reinterventions.10,11) Overall, these data suggest that coiling is a reasonable branch-preserving option for fetal-type PcomA aneurysms, but simple coiling is often limited by recurrence, and adjunctive stent- or balloon-assisted techniques may be preferable in complex anatomy.

Intrasaccular devices have emerged as another option for selected PcomA aneurysms, especially when aneurysm geometry is favorable and branch preservation is critical. Conceptually, they are attractive because they may allow aneurysm exclusion without parent-vessel reconstruction or reliance on branch flow remodeling. This potential advantage may be particularly relevant when preservation of PcomA flow is important. Clinical evidence, however, remains limited and should be interpreted cautiously, because most available series are small and anatomically selected. In an early dedicated PcomA WEB series of 15 aneuysms, including 10 ruptured lesions, no intraoperative rupture occurred, only 1 thromboembolic event was observed, and at a mean angiographic follow-up of 13 months, complete and adequate occlusion were achieved in 54% and 72% of cases, respectively; 2 retreatments were required, but no procedure-related morbidity or mortality was reported.12) More recently, in a WEB-first strategy cohort of 46 PcomA aneurysms, including 14 fetal-type variants, complete occlusion among WEB-treated lesions was achieved in 85.7% (6/7) of fetal-type aneurysms and 50.0% (7/14) of non-fetal aneurysms, while adequate occlusion was 85.7% in both groups.13) These results suggest that intrasaccular treatment can be safe and reasonably effective in selected PcomA aneurysms, including some fetal-type lesions. However, the current evidence remains too sparse to define its role with confidence in fetal-type aneurysms specifically, and the apparent favorable results likely reflect strong anatomical selection. In addition, applicability is highly anatomy-dependent: lesions with marked branch incorporation, elongated morphology, or unfavorable neck geometry may not be suitable for WEB-based treatment.

FD expands the treatment spectrum by offering parent-vessel reconstruction, especially for large, recurrent, broad-necked, or complex lesions in which coiling alone is unlikely to be durable.14) At the PcomA origin, however, FD outcomes are strongly influenced by branch-related flow demand. For this reason, results should not be interpreted without distinguishing non-fetal from fetal configurations. That distinction is central to the next section.

Flow Diversion Outcomes in PcomA Aneurysms: Differences between Non-Fetal and Fetal Configurations

FD has become established for many ICA aneurysms, but outcomes at the PcomA origin are more heterogeneous than at other sidewall locations because PcomA aneurysms do not represent a single anatomical entity. Posterior circulation configuration, especially the relative contribution of the P1 segment and the PcomA to PCA perfusion, appears to be a major determinant of angiographic response; accordingly, cohort-level FD results should be interpreted cautiously unless fetal and non-fetal lesions are clearly distinguished. Interpretation of the literature is further complicated by inconsistent definitions of fetal-type anatomy, heterogeneity in aneurysm size, branch incorporation, device platform, adjunctive coiling, and follow-up duration, as well as variable reporting of angiographic outcomes and branch patency.15) Despite these limitations, a broad pattern is reproducible: non-fetal PcomA aneurysms generally show better and more predictable occlusion after FD than fetal-type aneurysms, indicating that posterior circulation anatomy is a key determinant of reconstructive success at the PcomA origin (Table 2).

FD outcomes in non-fetal PcomA aneurysms

In non-fetal PcomA aneurysms, FD outcomes are generally closer to those achieved in other ICA sidewall aneurysms than to those seen in fetal-type lesions. In a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 403 PcomA aneurysms, the complete occlusion rate at final follow-up was 77% in the non-fetal subgroup, compared with 42% in fetal-type aneurysms, supporting the greater efficacy of FD treatment in non-fetal anatomy.15) In a multicenter comparative series, complete occlusion was achieved in 81.8% of non-fetal aneurysms versus 43.7% of fetal-type aneurysms, and the median time to occlusion was substantially shorter in non-fetal lesions (6 vs. 51 months).16) In the largest dedicated non-fetal series, 3–9-month angiography demonstrated complete occlusion in 32 of 47 aneurysms and near-complete occlusion in 11 of 47, yielding adequate occlusion in 43 of 47 lesions (91.5%); only 1 intraprocedural rupture occurred, and median functional outcome remained excellent (median modified Rankin Scale 0 at follow-up).17) These data support the biological premise that, when the PcomA does not provide dominant supply to the PCA territory, FD-induced flow remodeling is less opposed by persistent distal branch demand and more often results in progressive thrombosis and delayed aneurysm closure.

It is also important to recognize that outcomes are not uniform even within the non-fetal subgroup. In the same non-fetal series, 29 of 46 initially patent PcomAs remained patent at 6 months, whereas others showed progressive flow reduction or occlusion over time.17) This type of branch remodeling is often clinically silent when collateral supply is sufficient: in a dedicated PED (Medtronic Neurovascular) series of PcomA aneurysms with 6-month angiographic follow-up, the PcomA was patent in 23.3%, had diminished flow in 23.3%, and was occluded in 53.3%, yet no patient developed symptoms attributable to PcomA occlusion; similarly, an earlier series in patients with a preserved P1 segment found no new neurological deficits despite diminished or occluded PcomA flow at follow-up.18,19) More broadly, incorporated-branch anatomy appears to be an important determinant of success: in a recent registry subanalysis of intracranial aneurysms with incorporated branches, complete occlusion was 92.9% when the branch originated from the neck versus 60.8% when it originated from the sac.20) Thus, non-fetal PcomA aneurysms with sidewall-like or neck-branch morphology appear to be the most favorable setting for FD-based reconstruction, whereas larger sacs and aneurysms with dome-branch incorporation have been associated with incomplete occlusion or persistence after flow diversion.20–22) Recurrent aneurysms, particularly those with pre-existing stents, may also be less favorable because prior implants can interfere with flow-diverter apposition and endothelialization.23)

FD outcomes in fetal-type PcomA aneurysms

The situation is different in fetal-type aneurysms. Earlier studies and pooled analyses generally suggested lower and slower occlusion in fetal-type lesions, whereas some recently selected cohorts have reported more favorable results. In comparative FD studies, fetal-type PcomA aneurysms showed lower complete occlusion rates than non-fetal lesions, both in a 2023 meta-analysis (42% vs. 77%) and in a multi-institutional cohort (43.7% vs. 81.8%), with a longer median time to occlusion in the latter study (51 vs. 6 months).15,16) Earlier series were even less favorable: Wallace et al. reported complete occlusion in only 17% at 5–7 months and 33% overall after retreatment; Roy et al. found complete occlusion in only 1 of 9 fetal-configuration aneurysms (11%), with 6 of 9 remaining incompletely occluded; and Ten Brinck et al. observed 0 of 3 occlusions in their own cohort while reporting a pooled complete occlusion rate of only 31.4% (16/51) across the available literature.24–26) Collectively, these data indicate that aneurysm closure after FD in fetal-type anatomy is not only less frequent, but often substantially slower and less predictable.

Because the definition of fetal-type PcomA varies across studies, outcomes in true fetal-type anatomy with an absent ipsilateral P1 segment should be interpreted separately from broader fetal-type cohorts that include hypoplastic P1 segments. This pattern is mechanistically plausible. In fetal-type anatomy, the PcomA remains a major or obligatory route to the distal PCA territory because the ipsilateral P1 segment is absent, hypoplastic, or functionally insufficient. Persistent physiological demand across this branch may therefore maintain flow through the aneurysm–neck complex even after technically satisfactory FD placement.27) In that setting, the device may reduce inflow without suppressing it sufficiently to induce timely and durable thrombosis. In practical terms, the very branch that must be preserved may also be the factor that limits reconstructive efficacy. This creates a fundamental therapeutic tension: successful preservation of PcomA flow may indicate continued physiological demand across the branch, but the same persistent flow can delay or prevent complete aneurysm thrombosis after FD. Published data remain limited and are derived mainly from small retrospective series, but the consistency of persistent filling and lower occlusion rates across multiple cohorts suggests that branch patency in fetal-type lesions is often a marker of ongoing hemodynamic demand rather than a benign angiographic finding.

More recent reports, however, suggest that fetal configuration should not be viewed as uniformly untreatable by FD. In a selected series of 16 complex fetal-type aneurysms treated with a single flow-diverting stent deliberately expanded across the neck, Baranoski et al. reported complete obliteration in 62.5% and favorable angiographic results in 75%, with no retreatment and no posterior circulation ischemia.28) Likewise, Han et al. reported >90% occlusion in 75% (12/16) of followed fetal-variant aneurysms at 1 year, with 2 minor in-stent thrombosis-related strokes but no permanent deficit; distal PED tip placement in the proximal M1 segment was associated with occlusion.29) In a multicenter case series of PED-treated saccular PcomA aneurysms, Enriquez-Marulanda et al. reported acceptable occlusion rates overall and did not find fetal PcomA, branch origin pattern, or branch patency at follow-up to significantly affect aneurysm occlusion.30) A propensity score–weighted cohort study also found that fetal PcomA anatomy was not significantly associated with incomplete occlusion, unfavorable functional outcome, or complications after PED treatment, suggesting that the adverse effect of fetal configuration may be less uniform than earlier series implied.31) These later data are encouraging, but they remain retrospective, single center, and likely reflect careful anatomical selection and specific deployment strategy rather than broadly reproducible results.

These limitations have led some operators to consider adjunctive strategies, especially in large or broad-necked aneurysms with marked incorporation of the PcomA origin. In such lesions, FD plus coiling or more aggressive reconstructive constructs may be conceptually more attractive than FD alone because they may further reduce residual sac flow while maintaining branch perfusion.32) Consistent with that concept, Sakamoto et al. recently reported complete or near-complete occlusion in all 5 cases treated with overlapping FD plus coiling, with no ischemic or hemorrhagic complications during a mean 17.2-month follow-up and preservation of antegrade PcomA patency in 4 of 5 cases.33) Nevertheless, this evidence remains preliminary and insufficient to establish superiority over FD alone.

The discrepancy between unfavorable earlier series and more favorable recent reports is likely multifactorial. First, the anatomical definition of fetal-type circulation varies across studies; cohorts limited to absent P1 segments may represent a more hemodynamically dependent subgroup than cohorts including hypoplastic but patent P1 segments.1,10,25,27) Second, case selection may have differed across studies. Less favorable candidates for FD may include aneurysms with anatomically absent P1 (true fetal type), marked branch incorporation, large or recurrence after previous treatment.7,20,22,27,30) In contrast, lesions with a hypoplastic but patent P1 segment (partial fetal type) may be more likely to allow gradual redistribution of PCA inflow from the carotid system through the PcomA toward the vertebrobasilar system through the P1 segment; the process may be regarded as collateral or hemodynamic remodeling after FD placement. Baranoski et al. observed increased P1 flow in all cases with decreased PcomA flow after FD placement, and Sakamoto et al. similarly reported reduction of PcomA diameter with compensatory enlargement of the P1 segment after overlapping FD with coiling.28,33) However, such remodeling should not be assumed in true fetal-type anatomy with an anatomically absent P1 segment. Because the proportions of true fetal and partial fetal configurations have been infrequently reported in previous studies, it remains difficult to determine whether differences in reported outcomes reflect differences in underlying anatomy and patient selection. Third, technical strategy is not uniform. Baranoski et al. used a single Pipeline device selected slightly smaller than the maximal diameter of the larger landing zone, usually by 0.25 mm, to facilitate full device expansion and increase local metal coverage at the neck; this strategy was associated with complete obliteration in 62.5% and favorable angiographic results in 75% of complex fetal-type cases.28) Similarly, Han et al. reported that distal PED tip placement in the proximal M1 segment was associated with complete occlusion, probably because M1 anchoring reduces foreshortening or migration at the acute ICA–PcomA angle and improves neck coverage; however, this strategy also increases the extent of jailed branches and should not be regarded as risk-free.29) Overlapping FDs increase local metal coverage at the neck and may reduce intra-aneurysmal velocity, whereas adjunctive coiling decreases residual sac volume and may accelerate thrombosis.33) Nevertheless, these approaches should be interpreted as selective strategies rather than proven standard techniques: overlapping devices and adjunctive coiling may increase thromboembolic risk, complicate future sac access, and require meticulous confirmation of wall apposition and branch preservation. Thus, available evidence suggests that deliberate neck expansion, stable distal anchoring, overlapping FD, and adjunctive coiling can contribute to favorable outcomes in selected anatomies, but their benefit remains unproven in comparative studies and should be balanced against added procedural complexity. Fourth, angiographic endpoints and follow-up intervals differ across studies. Complete occlusion, near-complete occlusion, and “adequate” occlusion should not be interpreted equivalently, because composite favorable endpoints may overestimate FD efficacy.28) In addition, early follow-up may underestimate delayed FD-related thrombosis, whereas persistent PcomA/fPCA demand may delay or prevent complete sac occlusion in fetal-type lesions.16) Therefore, recent favorable results should be interpreted as evidence that FD can be effective in carefully selected fetal-type aneurysms with optimized strategy, rather than as proof that fetal-type anatomy no longer affects treatment response.

From a clinical standpoint, fetal-type PcomA aneurysms continue to pose a fundamental tension between aneurysm exclusion and branch preservation: constructs conservative enough to protect a functionally important PcomA may leave enough residual flow to prevent complete aneurysm closure, whereas more aggressive flow-modifying strategies may improve occlusion at the cost of greater uncertainty regarding branch safety. This unresolved trade-off is one of the main reasons FD in fetal-type lesions remains controversial even in experienced centers.

Why do FD outcomes differ between non-fetal and fetal configurations?

The difference between non-fetal and fetal lesions is best understood as a difference in hemodynamic environment. In non-fetal anatomy, the PcomA usually functions as a secondary communicating channel, and PCA perfusion is supplied predominantly through the vertebrobasilar system via the P1 segment. FD-induced flow remodeling can therefore reduce aneurysmal inflow without being strongly counteracted by persistent distal branch demand.27)

In fetal-type anatomy, by contrast, the PcomA often serves as a major or obligatory outflow pathway to the distal PCA territory. When the aneurysm neck incorporates this branch, flow across the aneurysm–neck–PcomA complex may persist even after technically satisfactory FD deployment.34) This persistent outflow demand provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for slower thrombosis, more frequent incomplete occlusion, and prolonged angiographic persistence in fetal-type lesions.

This hemodynamic effect is likely modulated by several anatomical factors, including the degree of fetal dominance, actual P1 caliber, extent of branch incorporation, aneurysm size and neck width, and the flow-modifying effect of the device construct or adjunctive coiling.35) Accordingly, the difference between non-fetal and fetal configurations should be understood not as a purely binary phenomenon, but as a spectrum of branch dependence and residual outflow demand.

Practical Treatment Selection in Fetal-Type PcomA Aneurysms

Evidence-based considerations for treatment selection

Treatment selection for fetal-type PcomA aneurysms should be individualized because both aneurysm morphology and branch physiology influence treatment response. The currently available evidence is limited and derives mainly from small retrospective series with heterogeneous anatomical definitions, treatment strategies, and follow-up intervals. Overall, current data support coiling as an established branch-preserving option, suggest a selective role for intrasaccular treatment in favorable anatomies, and indicate that FD may still be useful when durable endosaccular treatment appears difficult, albeit with a less predictable angiographic response than in non-fetal lesions. Treatment selection should therefore consider branch incorporation, relative P1/PcomA development, anticipated durability, and procedural risk rather than aneurysm size alone.

Practical positioning of current treatment options

Within these limitations, treatment selection should be framed as an individualized, anatomy-driven decision (Fig. 2). Very small fetal-type aneurysms should not be treated solely on the basis of size; treatment should be justified by rupture status, symptoms, documented growth, irregular morphology, or other patient-specific risk factors. When treatment is selected, coiling remains an important branch-preserving option if satisfactory aneurysm exclusion can be achieved without compromising the PcomA origin. In selected lesions with favorable neck configuration and sac geometry, intrasaccular treatment may be considered. FD-based reconstruction may be considered more strongly for large, recurrent, broad-necked, or morphologically complex aneurysms in which coiling alone is unlikely to provide durable occlusion and intrasaccular treatment appears anatomically unsuitable. However, this approach should be individualized according to P1 development, degree of fetal dominance, branch incorporation, landing-zone quality, prior treatment history, antiplatelet considerations, and local expertise. Follow-up and retreatment considerations

Persistent filling or recurrence should be anticipated when selecting the initial treatment strategy, particularly in fetal-type PcomA aneurysms with large sacs, broad necks, or dome-branch incorporation.20–22) A key practical limitation of FD-based treatment is that once a FD has been deployed across the aneurysm neck, subsequent access to the aneurysm sac may become difficult, especially after endothelialization or overlapping FD placement. Therefore, in lesions judged to have a high risk of persistent filling, the need for future sac access should be considered before the initial procedure, and adjunctive coiling or an alternative branch-preserving strategy may be preferable in selected cases.

After FD, small residual filling may be observed if it is stable or progressively decreasing and if preservation of a functionally important PcomA remains necessary. However, persistent substantial filling, aneurysm enlargement, or recurrent filling after initial improvement should prompt reassessment of P1 development, PcomA dominance, branch incorporation, and device apposition. Retreatment after FD may include additional FD placement when inadequate neck coverage or device apposition is suspected. Adjunctive coiling of the residual sac can be considered only when safe access is feasible, either through the FD interstices or, in selected non-fetal configurations with a sufficiently developed P1 segment, via a retrograde P1–PcomA route; however, these approaches are technically constrained and must avoid compromise of the PcomA or its perforators.

After recurrence following stent-assisted coiling or branch-protective stenting, additional coiling may be considered when catheterization of the residual sac through or around the existing stent is feasible. FD-based reconstruction can also be considered for broad-necked or repeatedly recurrent remnants, but prior stents may interfere with flow-diverter apposition and endothelialization; therefore, the feasibility of FD should be assessed in relation to the existing construct, landing-zone quality, antiplatelet tolerance, and the need to preserve the dominant PcomA/fPCA.

Overall, fetal-type PcomA aneurysms should not be approached as routine ICA sidewall aneurysms. FD may still have an important role, but it is best viewed as a selective reconstructive option within a broader anatomy-driven treatment strategy rather than as a default solution.

Limitations of the Current Evidence and Future Directions

The current evidence has several important limitations. First, definitions of fetal-type PcomA anatomy are not consistent across studies. Some authors restrict the term to cases with an absent P1 segment, whereas others include hypoplastic but visible P1 segments. Because treatment response is likely influenced by the degree of branch dependence, future studies should report P1 development, relative PcomA dominance, and the extent of branch incorporation more clearly.

Second, most available data are derived from small retrospective series with heterogeneous aneurysm morphology, treatment modality, device platform, deployment strategy, adjunctive techniques, and follow-up duration. These factors introduce substantial selection bias and make direct comparison among coiling, intrasaccular treatment, FD alone, and FD with adjunctive techniques difficult.

Third, angiographic endpoints are not standardized. Complete occlusion, near-complete occlusion, adequate occlusion, and >90% obliteration are used differently across studies, and denominators are often limited to patients with available imaging follow-up. This heterogeneity may partly explain discrepancies between earlier unfavorable reports and more recent favorable series. Future studies should use consistent angiographic endpoints and should separately report aneurysm occlusion, PcomA patency, P1 remodeling, ischemic events, and retreatment.

Finally, predictors of successful occlusion after FD remain insufficiently defined. Although fetal-type configuration is associated with lower or slower occlusion in many reports, outcome is probably determined by multiple interacting factors, including actual P1 caliber, degree of fetal dominance, aneurysm size, neck width, branch incorporation, device apposition, landing-zone selection, and adjunctive coiling. Prospective multicenter registries with standardized anatomical definitions, longer imaging follow-up, and detailed reporting of branch-related hemodynamics are needed to refine treatment selection and define realistic expectations for aneurysm occlusion, branch preservation, and retreatment.

Conclusion

Fetal-type PcomA aneurysms are a distinct subgroup in which treatment response is shaped by both aneurysm morphology and branch physiology. Their central therapeutic challenge is the tension between preserving a functionally important PcomA and achieving durable aneurysm occlusion. The same persistent branch demand that protects PCA perfusion may also maintain flow through the aneurysm–neck complex and delay or limit aneurysm thrombosis after FD. Therefore, FD should be positioned as a selective reconstructive option within a broader anatomy-driven treatment strategy rather than as a default solution. Treatment selection should account for P1 development, degree of PcomA dominance, branch incorporation, aneurysm size and morphology, feasibility of branch-preserving alternatives, and the potential need for retreatment. More consistent anatomical definitions and larger multicenter studies are needed to refine treatment selection and outcome prediction.
