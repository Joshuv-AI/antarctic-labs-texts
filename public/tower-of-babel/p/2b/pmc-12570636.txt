Optimization of carbon dioxide insufflation strategies in minimally invasive cardiac surgery: the efficacy of continuous insufflation protocols with left ventricular venting
Authors: Zhibin Hu, Wenshuai Mao, Lijun Guo, Zhiwei Liu, Xujie Hu, Shuwei Wang, Bing Zhou, Yong Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAlthough CO₂ insufflation play an established role in open-heart surgery, its optimal application in minimally invasive cardiac procedures remains unclear. This study systematically compared continuous versus single CO₂ insufflation protocols during minimally invasive modified morrow procedures, and assessed the influence of left heart venting modality on microbubble clearance efficacy.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 351 patients undergoing minimally invasive modified morrow procedures (2023–2024). Participants were stratified into two groups: Continuous Insufflation Group (CIG, n = 228; 5 L/min CO₂ throughout cardiopulmonary bypass [CPB]) and Single Insufflation Group (SIG, n = 123; 5 L/min CO₂ 5-min pre-aortotomy closure to 5-min post-unclamping). Microbubble dynamics were quantitatively classified into four grades (0-III) using standardized intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify predictors of microbubble formation and clinical outcomes.ResultsThe CIG exhibited a statistically significant reduction in overall microbubble incidence compared to SIG (13.60% vs. 36.59%, p < 0.05), particularly in attenuating Grade I-II microbubbles (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified CO₂ insufflation strategy (odds ratio [OR] = 3.694, p = 0.005), left heart venting modality (OR = 32.527, p < 0.001), and CPB time (OR = 1.017, p = 0.017) as independent predictors. Importantly, the combination of continuous insufflation with left ventricular venting was associated with a 93.3% reduction in Grade I microbubbles (OR = 0.067, p = 0.001). Clinically, the CIG exhibited a lower incidence of perioperative arrhythmias (24.12% vs. 36.59%, p = 0.009), despite slightly longer hospitalization (9.80 ± 4.42d vs. 8.88 ± 3.26d, p < 0.05). Neurological outcomes (4.82% vs. 3.25%, p = 0.483) and ICU stay duration (1.59 ± 1.69d vs. 1.74 ± 2.14d, p = 0.215) were comparable between groups.ConclusionIn the context of the minimally invasive modified morrow procedure, continuous CO₂ insufflation significantly reduces microbubble formation and perioperative arrhythmias. Left ventricular venting modality is a key determinant of de-airing efficacy. Protocol optimization should prioritize continuous CO₂ insufflation in combination with left ventricular venting systems to enhance procedural safety.

Introduction

Thoracoscopic-assisted minimally invasive cardiac surgery has emerged as a pivotal surgical alternative in contemporary cardiac practice, owing to its well-documented advantages, including reduced trauma, minimized blood loss, and accelerated postoperative recovery [1, 2]. This approach not only decreases postoperative pain and hospitalization duration but also demonstrates lower rates of surgical complications compared to conventional sternotomy [3, 4].

However, the constrained surgical field associated with minimally invasive approaches via limited incisions presents substantial technical challenges to conventional sequential de-airing techniques [5]. Of particular clinical relevance, persistent gaseous microemboli during cardiac procedures are strongly correlated with postoperative neurological dysfunction (PND) [6], underscoring the imperative for effective de-airing strategies in minimally invasive cardiac surgery.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂), leveraging its superior density and high blood solubility (25-fold greater than air), has been extensively adopted as an efficient de-airing agent in open-heart procedures, particularly in minimally invasive settings [7, 8]. Our prior investigations have further validated the enhanced microbubble clearance efficiency and antiarrhythmic benefits of continuous CO₂ insufflation in minimally invasive mitral valve surgery [9]. Nevertheless, improper management of continuous CO₂ insufflation may precipitate intraoperative hypercapnia secondary to elevated partial pressure of carbon dioxide [10], while the interference from concurrent suction on CO₂ displacement efficiency remains a non-negligible concern [7]. Importantly, Benedetto U concluded that continuous CO₂ insufflation failed to demonstrate significant protective effects on clinical outcomes, particularly regarding PND, with insufficient evidence currently justifying its routine clinical implementation [11, 12].

Against this contentious backdrop, we conducted a comparative analysis of two distinct CO₂ insufflation strategies (continuous versus single insufflation) combined with varied left heart venting modalities in patients undergoing minimally invasive Modified Morrow procedure. All procedures were performed via thoracoscopic-assisted right infra-axillary approach. The assessed parameters included: intraoperative microbubble signals graded by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), incidence of PND, perioperative arrhythmias, intensive care unit (ICU) stay and postoperative hospitalization.

A total of 351 patients met the inclusion criteria and were stratified into two cohorts based on intraoperative CO₂ insufflation protocols: (1) Continuous Insufflation Group (CIG, n = 228): patients received continuous CO₂ insufflation at 5 L/min, initiated at the start of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and maintained until its termination. (2) Single Insufflation Group (SIG, n = 123): patients received CO₂ insufflation at 5 L/min starting 5 min before aortotomy closure and continued until 5 min after unclamping. Additionally, patients were categorized based on the left heart venting modality into: (1) left ventricular venting group (n = 298): active left ventricular venting via a dedicated vent using a ventricular venting cannula. (2)Left atrial venting group (n = 53): active venting using an intracardiac suction catheter.

This study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board(IRB) of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No.: IRB-2023–040). All procedures strictly adhering to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was waived by the IRB given the retrospective observational nature of this study.

Surgical and cardiopulmonary bypass protocols

All procedures were conducted under standardized total intravenous anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Patients were placed in a supine position with 30 ° right lateral elevation achieved by thoracic padding. Peripheral CPB was established via femoral arterial and venous cannulation. A 4-cm longitudinal incision was created along the right anterior axillary line, followed by entry into the thoracic cavity through the third intercostal space. The transaortic septal myectomy technique previously described in our institutional protocols [13], was performed under direct visualization via aortotomy. Myocardial protection was achieved using modified del Nido cardioplegia solution, specifically employing a 4:1 blood-to-cardioplegia ratio mixture. This protocol enhances myocardial perfusion while mitigating hemodilution-induced myocardial edema [14]. Following adequate septal resection, the aortotomy was closed using a two-layer running polypropylene suture (4–0 Prolene, Ethicon). Prior to aortic unclamping, temporary epicardial pacing leads were routinely implanted. Cardiopulmonary bypass was systematically weaned after hemodynamic stability was achieved post-unclamping.

Left heart venting refers to the active gas/blood removal through a cannula connected to the CPB system. Left heart venting was achieved using one of two approaches: (1) Intracardiac suction catheter (DLP 20Fr, CN:12,012, Medtronic,USA), positioned in the left atrium via the right superior pulmonary vein (2) Ventricular venting cannula (DLP13Fr, CN:12,001, Medtronic,USA), advanced through the mitral orifice into the left ventricular apex.

During CPB, oxygenation parameters were strictly controlled, with partial pressure of oxygen maintained at 150–250 mmHg and mean arterial pressure controlled within 50–80 mmHg. The partial pressure of CO₂ was titrated to 40–45 mmHg using sweep flow modulation via a precision gas blender.

Comprehensive TEE evaluation was performed pre- and post-CPB termination to assess cardiac function and evaluate the residual microbubble burden.

All procedures were performed by a dedicated multidisciplinary team comprising: a single attending surgeon (Dr. CUI) who performed all operations; three board-certified perfusionists rotating per institutional protocol; and four senior cardiac anesthesiologists, each with ≥ 5 years of dedicated MICS experience, managing anesthesia for all cases. This stability minimized performance variability.

CO₂ insufflation protocol and cardiac de-airing procedure

Following thoracotomy, a 12-Fr CO₂ insufflation catheter (Hangzhou Gaoling Medical Devices) was positioned at the cephalad aspect of the thoracic cavity through the primary operative port, delivering CO₂ at a flow rate of 5 L/min [9]. All patients underwent a standardized four-step de-airing protocol, consisting of: (1) 15° Trendelenburg positioning; (2) Ventricular venting interruption; (3) Ventilator-synchronized pulmonary re-expansion; and (4) Hemodynamic-guided right atrial pressurization.

Real-time monitoring of CO₂ concentration was performed using a custom-designed optical sensor, developed by the College of Biomedical Engineering and Instrument Science, Zhejiang University. The sensor was positioned 15 mm distal to the thoracic incision, enabling quantitative recording of air displacement efficiency, calculated as ([CO₂]/[ambient air]) × 100%.

Intraoperative microbubble burden was quantified using TEE to monitor ascending aortic and intracardiac signals. Continuous TEE surveillance was performed for 10 min following the onset of cardiac ejection. All assessments were conducted by an independent blinded echocardiographer following a standardized protocol.

Primary endpoints

PND encompassed stroke (validated by emergent cranial computed tomography), coma, agitation (richmond agitation-sedation scale score ≥ + 2), delirium (assessed via confusion assessment method for ICU), or seizure activity (documented on continuous electroencephalography). Perioperative arrhythmias were strictly defined as sustained (> 30 s) tachyarrhythmias (atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response > 110 bpm, ventricular tachycardia > 100 bpm) or ischemic electrocardiographic changes (ST-segment elevation/depression > 1 mm in two contiguous leads) occurring within the initial 10-min postoperative window following aortic cross-clamp removal.

Secondary endpoints

Healthcare utilization was evaluated through two key metrics: (1) ICU stay duration: defined as the ICU admission-to-discharge readiness interval (days); and (2) Postoperative hospitalization: calculated as surgery-to-discharge interval contingent on institutional recovery benchmarks (days).

This tiered endpoint structure aligns with STROBE guidelines for surgical outcome studies, prioritizing clinically meaningful endpoints while also capturing system-level efficiency parameters.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared via independent t-test (with Levene's variance assessment); non-normal variables were expressed as median (IQR) and compared using Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts (%) and analyzed by χ2 test or Fisher's exact test when appropriate (i.e., for low-frequency cells).

Predictors of microbubble signals were first identified through univariable logistic regression (screening threshold p < 0.05), followed by multivariable logistic regression with forward elimination (retention p < 0.05). All tests were two-tailed, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significance. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for logistic models.

Results

Echocardiographic outcomes of minimally invasive modified morrow procedure

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a significant reduction in maximal septal thickness from 20.28 ± 4.54 mm preoperatively to 14.65 ± 2.73 mm postoperatively (95% CI 4.74–6.51; p < 0.001), with all patients achieving clinically satisfactory left ventricular outflow tract gradients. No significant intergroup differences were observed between groups. (p > 0.05).

Gas clearance efficiency

Both groups maintained ≥ 99% CO₂ concentration at all monitored anatomical sites during the intervention period under 5 L/min insufflation without the need for supplemental suction.

Perioperative outcomes

Both groups demonstrated comparable outcomes in myocardial performance metrics (spontaneous sinus rhythm recovery rate, postoperative left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]), major complications (postoperative atrial fibrillation, conduction block, pacemaker implantation), and inflammatory markers (leukocyte count) (p > 0.05 for all). However, the SIG exhibited significantly lower peak cardiac troponin I (cTnI) levels compared to the CIG (p < 0.05). Importantly, the SIG achieved shorter durations of CPB, ACC time, and postoperative hospitalization (p < 0.05 for all comparisons).

A significant divergence in ventricular venting strategies was observed between groups, with a higher proportion of patients in the SIG receiving left atrial venting compared to the CIG (43.09% vs 14.04% left atrial venting; p < 0.05). All patients were discharged with neurologic stability except for one death due to intracerebral hemorrhage (0.28%).

Discussion

This comparative study elucidates key determinants of microbubble prevention during the minimally invasive modified morrow procedure. The continuous CO₂ insufflation protocol demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing microbubble formation compared to single insufflation (13.60% vs. 36.59%, p < 0.05), particularly for Grade I-II events. The benefit is attributable to the unique biophysical properties of CO₂—its high density (1.98 g/L) and elevated blood solubility (25-fold greater than air)—which establish a stable gas barrier that facilitates continuous air displacement [16]. The right infra-axillary minimally invasive approach further enhanced gas exchange dynamics by creating a semi-closed thoracic cavity model. Importantly, left ventricular venting modality emerged as the dominant predictor of de-airing efficiency (OR = 32.527; p < 0.001). Ventricular venting cannulas, positioned transapically through the mitral orifice into the left ventricle apex, enabled dual-phase microbubble clearance: pre-aortic closure ventricular air displacement and post-reperfusion turbulent flow evacuation, outperforming passive atrial suction catheters. The synergistic interaction between continuous CO₂ insufflation and left ventricular venting yielded a 93.3% risk reduction for Grade I microbubbles (OR = 0.067, p = 0.001), underscoring the combined efficacy of gas-phase and mechanical interventions in optimizing de-airing. Although elevated postoperative cTnI levels were observed in the CIG (24.1 vs. 13.5 μg/L), this likely reflected prolonged myocardial ischemia due to extended aortic cross-clamp (74.83 vs. 62.41 min) and CPB durations (115.77 vs. 99.50 min), rather than direct CO₂ toxicity, as evidenced by comparable LVEF recovery [17].

The present findings complement the seminal work by Weltert LP et al. [18, 19] in minimally invasive mitral valve surgery, confirming the universal air-clearance superiority of continuous CO₂ insufflation while necessitating procedure-specific protocol adaptations. Particularly in the context of the morrow procedure, optimal outcomes require strategic modifications such as ventricular venting modality selection. Crucially, our flow dynamics investigations demonstrated that CO₂ flow rates exceeding 5 L/min achieve over 99% gas-phase purity at all monitored sites within 5 min, effectively reducing residual air content below 1% [9]. Notably, the present findings further elucidate the anatomical superiority of ventricular venting cannula positioning—apically positioned via trans-mitral access—as the cornerstone of de-airing efficiency. The mechanism hinges on direct left ventricle cavity evacuation during both aortic closure (preventing air entrapment) and post-reperfusion (eliminating turbulence-derived microbubbles), providing reliable evidence for instrument selection in minimally invasive cardiac surgery. Consequently, ventricular venting cannulas are strongly recommended as the primary drainage strategy, particularly in procedures requiring deep intracavitary manipulation such as the Morrow procedure. Despite significant microbubble reduction, the incidence of neurological complication remained similar between groups (4.82% vs. 3.25%, p = 0.483), potentially due to the predominance of low-risk Grade I microbubbles and underpowered detection of rare events [20, 21]. Importantly, microbubble formation demonstrates a robust association with perioperative arrhythmias (OR = 70.871, p < 0.001), which may be mediated through two mechanisms: (1) coronary microemboli-induced myocardial ischemia following cardiac reperfusion, triggering electrophysiological instability; (2) direct disruption of endocardial action potential propagation by intracardiac bubbles, facilitating reentrant arrhythmias. This phenomenon may parallel the electrophysiological effects of epicardial adipose tissue secretomes, which alter intercellular electrical coupling to modulate myocardial excitability [22, 23].

Study limitations include the inherent selection bias associated with its retrospective design and operator-dependent variability in TEE grading. These limitations underscore the need for future multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) employing factorial designs to isolate the effects of individual interventions. Emerging solutions involve intelligent CO₂ delivery systems equipped with real-time gas sensors for dynamic flow regulation (3–8 L/min), as well as integrated neuroimaging-biomarker protocols—such as diffusion-weighted MRI combined with serum neurofilament light chain (NfL)—to quantify subclinical cerebral microembolism.

Conclusion

Continuous CO₂ insufflation demonstrates significant efficacy in reducing intracardiac microbubble formation, a protective effect directly mediated by its high density (1.98 g/L) and exceptional blood solubility (25-fold higher than air), which collectively establish a stable gas barrier displacing residual air. Complementing this, ventricular venting cannulas—strategically positioned at the left ventricular apex via trans-mitral access—achieve superior de-airing efficiency through direct evacuation of microbubbles generated during cardiac reperfusion. So, the combined application of continuous CO₂insufflation with ventricular venting may constitute the optimal de-airing management strategy for minimally invasive modified morrow procedure.
