Investigating geographic tongue severity: a cross-sectional study on symptoms and potential risk factors
Authors: Bilgün Çeti̇n, Derya İçöz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeographic tongue (GT) is a benign, recurrent inflammatory condition marked by atrophic and erythematous lesions, often asymptomatic but sometimes causing discomfort. This study evaluated some potential etiological and risk factors influencing Geographic Tongue Area and Severity Index (GTASI) and the relationship between GTASI scores and symptoms.MethodsA total of 163 GT patients (100 females [61.35%], 63 males [38.65%]) were assessed using the GTASI, which incorporates erythema, white halo, and fissured tongue (FT) scores. Statistical analyses investigated relationships between GTASI, symptoms, family history, tobacco using, allergy or atopy and medication, Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, while numerical variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsWhile GT severity showed no significant relationship with gender (p = 0.694), smoking (p = 0.907), allergic conditions (p = 507), medication (p = 0.364) or family history (p = 591). Among participants, 106 (65%) reported symptoms. A total of 137 patients (84%) had at least one fissured tongue lesion associated with GT. Higher FT scores were linked to an increased likelihood of symptoms (p = 0.048). Conversely, erythema scores were not associated with symptoms (p = 0.943).ConclusionThe study did not identify risk factors influencing overall GTASI scores. However, symptom presence is significantly associated with fissured tongue rather than GT severity suggesting that clinicians should focus on fissured tongue when evaluating symptomatic patients. Background

Benign migratory glossitis, commonly known as geographic tongue (GT), was first described by Rayer in 1831. GT is a chronic, benign, recurrent inflammatory disorder that primarily affects the tongue, and its exact etiology remains unclear [1]. Its incidence has been reported to range between 1 and 5%, with higher prevalence among individuals under 30 years of age [2]. While some studies have reported no significant gender difference, many suggest a greater occurrence in females [3, 4]. Although rare, GT can also affect the labial or buccal mucosa, hard palate, lips, and floor of the mouth [1, 5].

GT is characterized by epithelial atrophy, especially of the filiform papillae, and clinically manifests as erythematous areas surrounded by a white halo. These lesions vary in shape and size, most often on the dorsal and lateral surfaces of the tongue, and may regress and reappear over time [3, 4]. Although often asymptomatic, some patients report burning, pain, or taste disturbances, particularly with acidic or spicy foods [1].

Several systemic and local factors have been suggested in the etiology of GT, including smoking, systemic diseases, allergies, and fungal infections [6]. Associations with conditions such as hay fever, allergic rhinitis, asthma, eczema, and psoriasis have also been described [1, 7]. In addition, familial clustering suggests a genetic predisposition, as GT is more prevalent in the parents and siblings of affected individuals than in the general population [4].

Despite these associations, there is still limited evidence on which factors influence the severity of GT. To address this gap, the present study aims to investigate potential etiological and risk factors that may affect GT severity and to assess whether GT severity correlates with patients’ symptomatic status.

To enable objective assessment, Picciani et al. recently introduced the Geographic Tongue Area and Severity Index (GTASI), adapted from the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), which has long been used to monitor disease activity in psoriasis [3, 8]. GTASI has been shown to be reliable and reproducible for grading GT severity [9]. However, its clinical utility in identifying risk factors or guiding management remains unexplored. This study therefore also contributes to clarifying the practical value of GTASI in research and clinical practice.

Methods

This study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Selçuk University under approval number 2023/33.

Study design

First, among patients aged 18 to 90 years who presented for examination at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Faculty of Dentistry, Selçuk University, 163 participants with GT were selected during the examination of the oral mucosa. The age range was determined to include adult patients, who routinely constitute the patient population of our clinic and are capable of providing informed consent.The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) the lesion had to be clearly visible during clinical examination, (2) participants had to be free from any other oral mucosal pathology that could potentially confound the diagnosis of GT, (3) there must be no condition that would impede the full visibility of the tongue surface during photography, such as ankyloglossia or other anatomical anomalies, (4) the lesion was identified as GT based on specific clinical features, including irregularly bordered erythematous areas with epithelial atrophy, primarily affecting filiform papillae, and the diagnosis was confirmed by two experienced oral medicine specialists, (5) the lesion should demonstrate migratory behavior, including periods of disappearance and reappearance, which is characteristic of GT [4].

Five distinct photographs were taken from these patients, allowing observation of all anatomical regions of the tongue (including the tongue apex, two lateral margins, lingual belly, and dorsal surface). Each photograph was obtained using a single, state-of-the-art smartphone (Iphone 14pro, Martinique, France) utilizing its 48MP main camera sensor with 1x zoom, with patients seated on the same dental unit for every session, under a combination of natural and ambient fluorescent lighting. All photographic parameters (e.g., focus, zoom level) were standardized, and both the device flash and the dental unit’s reflector were turned off. Photographs were consistently taken by the same observer.

Additionally, a nine-item clinical and demographic information form assessing the patients’ systemic health status, symptomatology, and family history was completed by the participants. This form was specifically designed for this study and was administered to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Subsequently, the photographs were evaluated separately by two experienced observers in oral diagnosis and maxillofacial radiology. To assess inter-rater reliability, approximately 30% of the participants (n = 49) were randomly selected and their photographs were independently evaluated by both observers. Cohen’s kappa analysis demonstrated good to excellent agreement which demonstrated good to excellent agreement for all assessed parameters: dorsal tongue – erythema (κ = 0.829), white halo (κ = 0.868), fissures (κ = 0.830); tongue margins – erythema (κ = 0.864), white halo (κ = 0.851), fissures (κ = 0.939); tongue belly – erythema (κ = 0.907), white halo (κ = 0.890), fissures (κ = 0.917); tongue apex – erythema (κ = 0.814), white halo (κ = 0.748), fissures (κ = 0.820). For data with discrepancies between the observers, consensus was reached, and the final joint decision was recorded. The photographs were initially assessed based on the severity of the lesions in the corresponding area, focusing on the most severe aspect of the lesions, including erythema, white halo, and fissured tongue (FT)(Fig. 1) [3].

Examples of GT tongue categorized according to GTASI (A: Mild, B: Moderate, C: Severe, white arrow: Lesion-associated fissured tongue, hollow arrows: white halo, elliptical shapes: Erythema)

Region-based analysis of the tongue and GTASI scoring

In the second phase, the tongue was divided into four regions: dorsum, lateral margins, belly, and apex (Fig. 2). These regions represented 40%, 30%, 20%, and 10% of the total tongue area, respectively. The following scores were used to indicate lesion distribution in these four regions: 0 = no involvement; 1 = < 10%; 2 = 10% < 30%; 3 = 30% < 50%; 4 = 50% < 70%; 5 = 70% < 90%; 6 = 90% − 100% [3].

Regions of the tongue surface. (The tongue is divided into four regions: A Apex, M Margins-left and right, B Belly, and D Dorsum)

The severity of GT was assessed based on three main characteristics of the lesions: erythema (E), white halo (H), and the presence of FT. Erythema and white halo were rated on a scale from 0 to 4, where 0 represents the complete absence of lesions on the tongue surface and 4 indicates maximum involvement.

For erythema:

0 = no erythema,

1 = mild erythema,

2 = moderate erythema,

3 = pronounced erythema,

4 = severe erythema.

A similar method was used for the white halo:

0 = no halo,

1 = thin white halo,

2 = moderately thick white halo,

3 = thick white halo,

4 = very thick white halo.

The assessment of FT will consider the presence or absence of grooves or cracks in the affected areas:

0 = no cracks in the lesions,

1 = presence of cracks in these areas.

To classify the severity of the lesions on the tongue, all obtained values for each anatomical region were summed and multiplied by the area percentage of that region to obtain a final value. An example formula for the dorsal surface of the tongue is as follows [3]:

GTASID = 0.4(FTD + ED + WHDD) × AD (FTD: fissured tongue on dorsum, ED: erythema on dorsum, WHD: white halo on dorsum.

The other regions were calculated in the same manner using their respective area percentages, and the results were summed to calculate the final index [3]:

GTASI = GTASID + GTASIB + GTASIM + GTASIM (D: dorsum, B: belly: M: lateral margins, A: apex).

The severity index ranges from 0 to 54, where 0 indicates the complete absence of GT lesions and 54 represents the highest degree of severity [3]. According to the criteria defined by Schmitt and Wozel [8] for the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), scores between 1 and 7 are considered mild, scores between 7 and 12 are categorized as moderate, and scores greater than 12 are regarded as severe (Fig. 1). This index, originally designed for psoriasis, has been adapted with minor modifications for GT lesions by Picciani et al. [3]. In our study, this adapted index was utilized to calculate the severity of GT.

Finally, the regional erythema and FT (fissured tongue) scores were summed to calculate the erythema score (ES) and FT score (FTS) for each subjects. These scores were used to determine the relationship between the presence of symptoms in participants and the FT score, erythema score, and GTASI. The scores were calculated using the following formula:

FTS = FTD+ FTB+FTM+FTA (D: dorsum, B: belly: M: lateral margins, A: apex).

ES = ED+EB+EM+EA (D: dorsum, B: belly: M: lateral margins, A: apex).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS v.22 (Armonk, NY, USA) The relationship between categorical data, classified according to the calculated GTASI, and etiological factors, symptoms, smoking habit and gender was tested using chi-square analysis. Additionally, distributional and frequencies were calculated using descriptive statistics. Normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which revealed that the assumption of normality was not met (p < 0.001). Accordingly, the Mann–Whitney U test was performed to determine whether there was a difference in fissure and erythema scores based on the presence of symptoms. The level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The mean age of the participants in the study was 33.14 ± 13.964 years, ranging from 16 to 82 years. Of the 163 participants with GT, 100 (61.35%) were female, and 63 (38.65%) were male. No significant difference in the GTASI was observed between genders (p = 0.694) (Table 1).

Participants with symptoms most commonly suffered from a burning sensation provoked by spicy, hot, and acidic foods, reported by 90 participants (55.2%). Only two participants experienced persistent and constant pain on the tongue, and both were individuals with GT categorized as ‘moderate’ and ‘severe’. However, only one participant (0.6%) reported a taste disorder perception alone, who was categorized as ‘mild’. Furthermore, eight participants (4.9%) suffered from both persistent and provoked pain, four experienced both provoked pain and taste disorder, and one participant reported all three symptoms simultaneously (Table 2). In addition, fissured tongue was present in 84% of the participants, and 67.2% of these individuals reported symptoms.

No significant difference was observed between the presence and absence of symptoms across GTASI categories (p = 0.604) (Table 2). However, FT scores were significantly different in patients with symptoms (p = 0.048), indicating that higher FT scores increase the likelihood of symptom occurrence. On the other hand, erythema scores were not significantly associated with the presence of symptoms (p = 0.943) (Table 3). These findings indicate that the symptoms reported by participants were not associated with the severity of GT or the extent of erythema scores on the tongue, but rather with FT scores. The presence of FT within GT lesions and its occurrence across multiple regions of the tongue, leading to higher FT scores, appears to be a potential factor influencing the severity of symptoms experienced by individuals.

When participants were asked whether the lesion on their tongue was also present in their family members, 55 (33.7%) participants reported at least one family member also had GT. However, the majority stated that they were unsure (71 participants, 43.5%). Despite 37 participants reporting that none of their close family members had GT, no significant association was found between family history and the GTASI score (p = 0.591). (Table 1).

The majority of participants reported that they did not smoke (132 participants, 81.0%). Although 31 individuals (19.0%) stated that they were regular smokers, surprisingly, no significant difference was observed in the GTASI between smokers and non-smokers (p = 0.907). Among both smokers and non-smokers, the most frequently observed GTASI category was ‘moderate’ (Table 1).

When participants were asked about their medical history regarding allergies and medication use, 44 (27.0%) reported having at least one allergic condition, and 47 (28.8%) reported use of at least one medication regularly. However, the majority stated that they had no allergies (73.0%) and did not use any medications (71.1%). And finally, the presence of allergies or regular medication use was not found to have a significant impact on the GTASI(p = 0.507, p = 0.364) (Table 1). Notably, although participants were asked about systemic inflammatory conditions, such as psoriasis or autoimmune disorders, none reported having these conditions.

Discussion

GT is a phenomenon characterized by map-like lesions on the tongue surface, typically associated with the atrophy of filiform papillae and an idiopathic inflammatory process that appears to migrate over time. GT has been reported to be more prevalent in younger individuals [3, 10]. Consistent with previous findings in this study, the majority of individuals with GT in this study were under the age of 30. Specifically, 49.07% of participants were under 30 years old, 36.8% were between 30 and 50 years old, and 14.13% were over 50 years old, with only 4.9% being over 60. In this study, we investigated whether GTASI scores differed across age groups. Although the prevalence of GT varied among the age groups, no statistically significant difference was observed in GTASI scores.

Zang et al. [10] reported that 58.5% of the 123 individuals with GT in their study, which evaluated potential risk factors for GT in the Chinese population, were females. This finding aligns with the results of our study, where 61.35% of individuals with GT were female, suggesting that GT is more frequently observed in females. However, no significant difference was observed in GTASI scores between genders. This indicates that although GT is more commonly observed in females [11], gender has no significant effect on the GTASI. Moreover in both sexes, the most commonly observed GT severity was “moderate,” with rates of 49.0% and 42.9%, respectively. The observed null findings may be influenced by the relative homogeneity of the study population, the geographically limited sample, or the sample size, which could limit variability and the ability to detect subtle associations.

The predisposing and risk factors of GT remain uncertain. Various studies have evaluated several potential risk factors including allergic or atopic conditions, genetic predisposition, psoriasis, burning mouth syndrome, certain systemic diseases, genders and microorganisms [6, 7, 10–15]. In this study, the potential influence of some of these risk factors on the GTASI was investigated. The factors investigated included allergic asthma, allergic rhinitis, hay fever, drug or food allergies, eczema, and psoriasis. A total of 44 participants (27.0%) reported having been diagnosed with at least one of these conditions. Among them, 13 (29.5%) individuals reported allergic asthma, 11(25.0%) had allergic rhinitis, 9 (20.4%) had hay fever, 7 (15.9%) had drug allergies, 9 (20.4%) had eczema, and 2 (4.5%) mentioned other allergic conditions (e.g., animal dander, food allergies). The majority of participants (73.0%) reported that they had not been diagnosed with or experienced any complaints related to these conditions. When compared based on GTASI, no significant association was found between participants suffering from allergic or atopic conditions and those without any allergic conditions.

Several studies have investigated the relationship between allergy or atopy and GT [7, 10, 12, 16]. In their study evaluating the risk factors of GT, Zang et al. [10] found no significant difference between individuals with and without allergies. Similarly, Dick et al. [7], in their study examining the association between atopy and GT, reported no difference compared to the control group and concluded that it would be inaccurate to suggest that atopy or allergy is the oral manifestation of GT. On the other hand, Goregen et al. [12] found that individuals predisposed to allergies had a higher likelihood of developing GT compared to control subjects and suggested that allergy might be a potential risk factor. Similarly, Honarmand et al. [16] identified a correlation between GT and a history of allergy in their study.

In allergic individuals, an increase in the normal inflammatory response can be observed, which may lead to a more severe progression of inflammatory diseases [17]. Since GT is a condition involving inflammatory processes [10], we anticipated that individuals with a history of allergy or atopy would exhibit significantly altered GTASI scores, reflecting a more severe manifestation of GT. However, we found no significant difference in GTASI scores between individuals with and without allergies. Additionally, acute systemic diseases may transiently affect tongue morphology. For instance, SARS-CoV-2 infection has been reported to be associated with various oral manifestations [18], including xerostomia [19], aphthous-like ulcers [20], candidiasis, and depapillated tongue [21]. Furthermore, specific evidence suggests that immune alterations and pharmacotherapy related to SARS-CoV-2 may also be linked to GT and FT [18]. These observations indicate that even temporary systemic inflammatory conditions may contribute to the expression of GT.

Various studies have investigated the relationship between psoriasis and GT, with some suggesting a potential association, even proposing that GT could be an oral manifestation of psoriasis, however others have reported no such relationship [22–26]. However, in our study, none of the 163 participants with GT had been diagnosed with psoriasis. A broader sample size might have included patients diagnosed with psoriasis, allowing for an investigation into whether psoriasis has any impact on the GTASI. However, this was not possible to evaluate within the scope of our study.

In the current study, 28.8% of the participants reported using at least one medication regularly. Among these, the most common were antihypertensive drugs, with 11.0% of participants using them. This was followed by thyroid hormone medications (6.1%), antidiabetic drugs (4.9%), and antihistamines (2.4%). Additionally, 7.9% of participants reported using other medications, such as iron supplements, vitamins, gastric antacids and proton pump inhibitors. In the study by Dafar et al. [6] which investigated the risk factors for GT, patients diagnosed with GT at two different times and by different professionals were compared with a control group. They reported that the incidence of patients using regular medication was higher in the GT group compared to the control group, with a significant increase specifically observed in the use of antihypertensive drugs among a subset of GT patients. On the other hand, Honarmand et al. [16] reported that there was no difference in the prevalence of GT between patients who used medication regularly and those who did not use any medication. In line with the objective of the study, when examining the effect of medications on the GTASI, whether grouped by individual medication or simply by the presence or absence of medication use, we found that regular medication use did not have a significant effect on the GTASI.

Another risk factor investigated in several studies is smoking, which has been shown to be associated with a reduction in the incidence of GT, according to the findings of many studies [1, 6, 16, 26]. Similarly, in this study, 81.1% of the participants were non-smokers. Since our study lacks a control group, it is not possible to conclude that the prevalence of GT is lower in smokers. Based on the literature, we hypothesized that the lower prevalence of GT in smokers could inversely affect the severity of the condition. We developed this hypothesis based on studies reporting that smoking may have a protective effect on the development of geographic tongue, likely due to its mucosal effects [16]. Therefore, we compared the GTASI index scores of smokers and non-smokers with geographic tongue. However, no significant difference was observed between the GTASI scores of smokers and non-smokers. In smokers, the “moderate” GT category was also the most common, accounting for 45.1% of smoker cases.

When it comes to genetic transmission, 33.7% of the participants (55 individuals) in this study reported the presence of GT lesions in at least one first-degree relative. A research by Redman et al. [5] at the University of Minnesota on students with GT analyzed family history and revealed that the prevalence of first-degree relatives with GT was significantly higher than the control group. In this study, no significant difference was found in the severity of GT between participants with a family history of the condition and those without. However, a significant proportion of participants (43.5%) stated that they were unaware of whether GT was present in their family. Therefore, it cannot be overlooked that studies evaluating family members collectively might reveal a difference.

GT is mostly observed as asymptomatic. However, many patients report symptoms such as sensitivity, pain, or burning, particularly provoked by acidic, spicy, or hot foods and beverages [4, 9, 11, 13, 14]. In addition, although less frequently, persistent burning or pain, taste disturbances, or a combination of these symptoms have also been reported [1, 11]. In our study, unlike previous findings, the majority of participants (55.2%) reported experiencing sensitivity or burning sensations provoked by various foods and beverages. This high rate of symptomatic cases may be related to the high prevalence of fissured tongue in our study population, as 84% of participants presented with fissured tongue and 67.2% of them reported symptoms. Meanwhile, 34.9% of participants reported no symptoms, 4.9% reported both provoked and persistent burning 1.2% experienced persistent pain or burning, and 2.4% experienced provoked pain accompanied by taste disturbances. Additionally, 1 participant (0.6%) reported only taste disturbances, while another participant (0.6%) experienced all three symptoms simultaneously.

When investigating whether the severity of GT leads to more symptoms, we found no significant relationship between GT severity and symptoms (p = 0.604). While 67.7% of participants in the ‘mild’ category reported symptoms, 30.3% of those in the ‘severe’ category reported no symptoms at all. Regarding the type of symptoms, due to the insufficient amount of data, symptoms were only assessed as ‘present’ or ‘absent’. Regezi et al. [27] reported that the presence of symptoms GT is more pronounced in individuals with fissured tongue and candida infection. In this study, while investigating the difference in symptoms between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, erythema and FT scores obtained during the calculation of the GTASI index were analyzed. No significant relationship was found between erythema scores and symptoms (p = 0.934), but it was determined that individuals with higher FT scores were more likely to experience symptoms (p = 0.48) (Table 3). However, the effect size for FT scores was small (r = 0.155, 95% CI: 0.003–0.303), indicating that while the association is statistically significant, its clinical impact may be limited. Future studies with larger sample sizes or longitudinal designs could help clarify whether these small differences translate into meaningful clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, this suggests that GT does not produce symptoms on its own, but rather in the presence of FT. Fissure scores reflect the presence of GT-associated FT across the four regions of the tongue; as the score increases, the number of GT-associated fissures on the tongue surface also increases, with the maximum score being 4. In this context, the possible mechanism underlying the association between FT and symptoms can be interpreted as follows: as the number of fissures increases, these regions are more susceptible to mechanical trauma, which may expose nerve endings and contribute to heightened sensitivity to pain, acidic, or spicy foods and beverages. In addition, fissures can retain food debris and microorganisms, which may enhance local irritation and trigger an inflammatory response, potentially contributing to pain, burning sensation, or discomfort. Furthermore, an increased number and depth of fissures may promote the colonization of Candida or other superficial infections, which could explain the relationship between FT and symptoms. Similarly, Dafer et al. reported that the presence of FT in patients with GT increased the likelihood of experiencing symptoms, which is consistent with the findings of the present study [6]. The higher frequency of symptoms observed in individuals with FT may be partly attributable to the presence of fungal infections. In the study conducted by Dudko et al. [28], 69% of patients with a detected fungal infection of the tongue were individuals with fissured tongue. Furthermore, analysis of symptoms according to the presence or absence of fungal infection revealed that idiopathic pain and burning were reported significantly more often by patients with fungal involvement of the tongue. The most frequently detected fungal species was reported to be C. albicans. Whether symptomatic GT is associated with Candida infection in fissured tongues could be further clarified through additional research.

Histopathological examination of the erythematous areas of GT indicates that the red appearance of these regions is primarily due to a mononuclear subepithelial infiltrate, predominantly composed of CD4 + T-lymphocytes, along with vascular ectasia [1]. In this study, erythema scores are not related to degrees of epithelial desquamation; rather, the degree of redness appears to depend on the severity of vascular inflammation and subsequent dilation, which by itself may not be sufficient to trigger symptomatic responses. This observation may explain why the presence of erythema alone was not significantly associated with symptom scores in the study cohort.

These findings carry important clinical implications. Since our results indicated that symptoms appeared to be more closely associated with the presence of fissured tongue rather than with the severity of GT, clinicians should carefully screen for FT when evaluating patients with GT. Identifying FT may help predict which individuals are more likely to experience discomfort, thereby guiding decisions regarding the need for closer follow-up, symptom management strategies, and patient counseling. Thus, incorporating FT assessment into routine clinical examination could enhance the overall management of symptomatic GT patients.

GT generally does not require treatment [29]. In symptomatic cases, dietary modification and the adoption of tongue-brushing habits are recommended. In addition, various symptomatic treatment approaches have been reported in the literature, including the use of rinses, acetaminophen, mouthwashes with topical anesthetic agents, antihistamines, anxiolytics, and corticosteroids [29, 30]. For this purpose in this study, symptomatic treatment (topical steroids and antihistamines) was recommended for patients with some symptomatic GT.

There are very few studies evaluating the severity of GT [3, 6, 31]. Dafar et al. [6] determined the severity of GT based on the number of lesions observed on the tongue, categorizing it as follows: “mild” for a single lesion, “moderate” for 2–5 lesions, and “severe” for 6 or more lesions. Although different methods have been used to assess GT severity, consistent with the findings of this study, the severity of GT was most frequently reported in the ‘moderate’ category, followed by ‘severe’, with ‘mild’ being the least reported.

On the other hand, Picianni et al. [3] developed the GTASI index by assessing scores with three observers on 100 participants diagnosed with GT. For all three observers, the most frequently observed GTASI category in these participants was ‘mild’. Following that, ‘severe’ was reported, with the least frequent category being ‘moderate’. In our study, however, the ‘moderate’ category was identified in the majority, accounting for 46.6% of the 163 participants. The difference in findings between our study and Picianni et al.‘s study may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, our study included a larger sample size (163 participants) compared to their 100 participants, which may better reflect the distribution of GT severity. Additionally, demographic factors such as age and geographic location, as well as differences in observer experience, could have contributed to the discrepancy in GTASI categories observed. Finally, the higher prevalence of the ‘moderate’ category in our study may suggest that more advanced cases of GT were more readily identified. However, we believe that slight variations in the calculation of the developed index would not significantly impact the final score or the resulting category. In this regard, Picianni et al. [9] in another study, evaluated the applicability of the GTASI among different healthcare professionals, including dentists, oral diagnosis specialists, and dermatologists. As a result, they reported a high level of agreement among these professionals and concluded that the index demonstrated successful reproducibility in clinical practice. Furthermore, in a study by Dick et al. [31], which evaluated the correlation between GTASI and histopathological findings, a significant association was observed between the severity of GT and its histopathological appearance. Notably, the “mild” category showed a significant difference compared to the “moderate” and “severe” categories [31]. This finding demonstrates that the GTASI, as defined by Pacianni et al. [3], provides relatively objective criteria for assessing the severity of GT and is reproducible. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to support this finding.

No significant association was found between individuals with these risk factors and those without them (p > 0.05). This suggests that etiological factors do not account for the extensive involvement and severity of GT observed in some individuals.

This study has several limitations: the data regarding patients’ allergic-atopic conditions, family histories, and symptoms were based on self-reports collected via a clinical and demographic information form. The use of more objective measures, such as specific diagnostic tests, could yield more reliable results. Additionally, other potential risk factors, such as microbial status, dietary habits, levels of emotional stress, and psychosocial factors, were not evaluated, which might limit the understanding of GT severity. Moreover, since no patients with a diagnosis of psoriasis were included in the sample, it was not possible to investigate the potential impact of psoriasis on GT severity. Although the photographs were taken following a standardized protocol, the distance and angle of imaging may have obscured certain key features and potentially influenced the evaluator. This represents a limitation of the study, and scoring directly at the patient’s chairside could provide a more accurate assessment in future research. Furthermore, although inter-rater reliability for GTASI scoring was assessed, intra-rater reliability was not evaluated, which should be considered a limitation as it may have introduced potential variability in scoring consistency. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design of the study, which allows for identifying associations but does not establish causality, and the lack of longitudinal follow-up prevents the assessment of progression or remission of lesions over time. As such, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the absence of microbiological or histological confirmation may have led to possible misclassification of lesions with similar appearances, such as candidiasis. Most importantly, the effect size and statistical power could not be adequately achieved due to the limited number of GT patients included in the study. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes are essential to confirm these findings and to provide more robust evidence regarding the factors influencing GT severity.

Conclusions

In this study, we did not identify any potential risk or etiological factors influencing GTASI scores. However, the question of why some individuals experience more severe and widespread geographic tongue, while others present with milder and more localized lesions, remains worth investigating. To answer this, future studies should be designed to include additional risk factors, such as level of emotional stress or dietary habits.

Finally, the results indicate that the presence of symptoms is associated not with the presence or severity of GT but rather with the presence and extent of fissured tongue. Future research should focus on larger and more diverse samples to identify factors influencing the variety and intensity of symptoms. Future research should focus on larger and more diverse samples, incorporate prospective study designs, microbiological profiling, and broader lifestyle factors, to better understand the variety, intensity, and etiology of symptoms.
