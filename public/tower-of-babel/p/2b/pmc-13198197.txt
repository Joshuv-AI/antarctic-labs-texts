Mediating effects of physical activity and vitamin D on the association between cognition and dysmobility syndrome in Korean older women
Authors: Minjun Kim, Soo Hyun Park, Inhwan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the associations between cognitive function, physical activity, and vitamin D with dysmobility syndrome (DMS), and to assess the mediation effects of physical activity and vitamin D.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 181 community-dwelling older women. Cognitive function was assessed using the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination. Physical activity was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and categorized as active or inactive. Serum vitamin D was analyzed and classified as either ‘≥20 ng/mL’ or ‘<20 ng/mL’. DMS was defined as meeting at least three of six clinical criteria, including low muscle mass, high body fat, osteoporosis, slow gait speed, low hand-grip strength and history of fall.ResultsMediation analysis revealed mediation effects for both physical activity (Indirect effect = −0.0091) and vitamin D (Indirect effect = −0.0066) on the relationship between cognitive function and DMS through independent paths. Additionally, the sequential path of physical activity → vitamin D showed partial mediation (Indirect effect = −0.0013).ConclusionsThis study suggested that regular physical activity and adequate vitamin D levels can contribute to the prevention and management of cognitive impairment-related DMS in older Korean women.

Introduction

Aging causes negative changes in body composition, including decreased muscle and bone mass, increased body fat, and a decline in physical function, such as muscle function, gait, and balance (Westbury et al., 2020). The rate of aging varies based on health status, lifestyle, and environmental factors, all of which impact the ability to perform activities of daily living and influence independence (Zaninotto, Head & Steptoe, 2020; Abud et al., 2022). Dysmobility syndrome (DMS), proposed by Binkley, Krueger & Buehring (2013) represents a cluster of age-related changes, including muscle loss, osteoporosis, reduced gait speed, muscle weakness, and falls. Recent studies have expanded the concept, linking DMS not only to these physical changes but also to diseases common in older adults, such as metabolic syndrome and osteoporotic fractures, which increase mortality risk. Notably, DMS is more prevalent in elderly women than in elderly men, and its occurrence is more strongly associated with increased mortality risk in elderly women (Looker, 2015). Given the projected rise in aging populations, effective prevention and management strategies must be developed at national and societal levels (Lee et al., 2017; Buehring et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2022).

Cognitive impairment, a subtype of mental changes that naturally accompany aging, varies in rate and extent based on an individual’s health, lifestyle, and environment (Deary et al., 2009). In older adults, cognitive impairment can impair daily living, reduce physical activity, and trigger a negative cycle that accelerates changes in body composition and physical function (Greiner, Snowdon & Schmitt, 1996; Millán-Calenti et al., 2012). For instance, a recent longitudinal study found that cognitive impairment in elderly women was significantly associated with bone loss and increased fracture risk (Bliuc et al., 2021), while another identified cognitive impairment as an independent predictor of sarcopenia onset (Cipolli et al., 2023). Additional research has linked cognitive impairment to decreased grip strength, slower gait speed, and higher fall risk (Montero-Odasso & Speechley, 2018; Peel et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2022). These findings suggest that cognitive impairment extends beyond mental changes, impacting body composition and physical function, and may independently contribute to DMS.

Regular physical activity in older adults prevents key components of DMS, such as sarcopenia and osteoporosis, and improves physical functions like gait speed, muscle strength, and balance (Yorston, Kolt & Rosenkranz, 2012; Renner et al., 2022; Seo & Lee, 2022). It also helps prevent cognitive decline by promoting neurogenesis and neuroplasticity in the hippocampus (Ma et al., 2017). Vitamin D, essential for calcium homeostasis and bone metabolism, plays a key role in preventing osteoporosis and sarcopenia. By promoting muscle conservation, it reduces physical dysfunction and fall risk (Hamilton, 2010; Meehan & Penckofer, 2014; Halfon, Phan & Teta, 2015). Additionally, vitamin D supports cognitive function through its regulation of inflammatory response, antioxidative activity, and neuroprotective effects in the central nervous system (Briones & Darwish, 2012; Mokhtari-Zaer et al., 2020; Gáll & Székely, 2021). Previous studies indicate that physical activity and vitamin D in old age are independent factors closely associated with both cognitive impairment and DMS. We hypothesize that the relationship between cognitive impairment and DMS is influenced by physical activity and vitamin D levels. However, most previous studies have focused on isolated associations between these variables, with a lack of research on their interactions.

Therefore, this study aims to explore the interactions between cognitive function, physical activity, and vitamin D in relation to DMS in community-dwelling elderly women in South Korea. Specifically, we seek to investigate whether physical activity and vitamin D mediate the relationship between cognitive function and DMS, providing data for developing public health strategies to promote healthy aging and improve quality of life.

Materials & Methods

Study participants

The study initially included 238 elderly women (65 years or older) who use elderly welfare facilities in S—City, Gyeonggi-do, and lead normal lifestyles. Data from 57 participants were excluded due to missing data on body composition (n = 5), bone density (n = 23), grip strength (n = 14), or gait analysis (n = 15). The remaining 181 individuals were included in the analysis. To assess the potential for selection bias, baseline characteristics, including age, DMS parameters, and health-related parameters, were compared between the included and excluded participants. No significant differences were observed across these key variables (all p > 0.05). Before the study began, participants were informed of its purpose, methods, and procedures, and only those who voluntarily consented were included. This study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board of Changwon National University (IRB-7001066-202506-HR-041). All participants provided written informed consent before participation. The study adhered to the recommendations for the conduct, reporting, editing and publication of scholarly work in medical journals (ICMJE guidelines). Only biological sex was considered, as all participants were community-dwelling older women. No human organs or tissues were used in this study.

Body composition and blood pressure

Height was measured using an automatic stadiometer (DS-102; Jenix, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Body weight, body composition (fat and muscle), and bone density were assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (Lunar Prodigy; GE Medical Systems Lunar, WI, USA), after participants removed metal objects and wore a gown. Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured at the femur, femur neck, and lumbar spine (L1–L4). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2), and the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) was calculated as appendicular skeletal muscle mass (kg)/height (m2). After a sufficient rest period, blood pressure was measured twice using an automated device (FG-500R; Jawon Medical, Seoul, Republic of Korea), with the mean value recorded.

Hand-grip strength and gait measurement

Hand-grip strength was measured twice for each hand using a dynamometer while participants stood upright, with the highest value recorded. Gait speed was assessed over a 10 m course using a gait analysis system (Optogait, Microgait, Bolzano, Italy). The initial 2.5 m for acceleration and the final 2.5 m for deceleration were excluded, and data from the middle 5 m were analyzed (Lee, 2017). Both hand-grip strength and gait speed were measured following standardized protocols, as described previously (Roberts et al., 2011; Lindemann et al., 2008).

Cognitive function measurement and classification

Cognitive impairment was defined using the age- and education-adjusted Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)—whose reliability and validity have been validated in Korean older adults (Han et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2009)—rather than a single fixed threshold. Based on the normative data for the Korean elderly population, specific cut-off values were applied across 16 subgroups categorized by age (65–69, 70–74, 75–79, and ≥ 80 years) and education (0–3, 4–6, 7–12, and ≥ 13 years). The thresholds for cognitive impairment ranged from 16–19 for the 0–3 years of education group, 20–23 for 4–6 years, 24–25 for 7–12 years, and 26–27 for ≥ 13 years, depending on the specific age category. This method ensures that the assessment was formally adjusted for both aging and educational background.

An unauthorized version of the Korean MMSE was used by the study team without permission, however this has now been rectified with PAR. The MMSE is a copyrighted instrument and may not be used or reproduced in whole or in part, in any form or language, or by any means without written permission of PAR (http://www.parinc.com/).

Physical activity measurement and classification

Physical activity was assessed using the Korean version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which has been validated in Korean older adults (Chun, 2012). Weekly activity levels were calculated using metabolic equivalent of task (MET)-min/week. Participants with activity levels below 600 MET-min/week were classified as “inactive” while those with levels of 600 MET-min/week or higher were classified as “active” (Chun, 2012; Poggio et al., 2016).

Vitamin D measurement and classification

After a 10-hour fast, 10 mL of blood was collected from the brachial vein using a disposable syringe following a standardized clinical protocol. The sample was placed in a serum-separating tube and centrifuged at 3,000 RPM for 10 min to separate the serum. Vitamin D levels were analyzed using an Advia analytical device (Siemens, Forchheim, Germany) validated for serum 25(OH)D measurement and reagents from the same company, measuring serum 25(OH)D concentration using chemiluminescent immunoassay (Chen et al., 2012). Vitamin D deficiency was defined as a serum level <20 ng/mL (Holick, 2009).

DMS measurement and classification

DMS is defined by the following criteria: high body fat percentage (≥40%), osteoporosis (T-score ≤−2.5 at the femur, femoral neck, or lumbar spine) (Kanis, 1994), low muscle mass (ASMI ≤ 5.4 kg/m2), slow gait speed (≤1.0 m/s), low hand-grip strength (<18 kg), and a history of falls within the past year (Chen et al., 2014). Participants meeting fewer than three criteria were classified as normal, while those meeting three or more criteria were classified as having DMS (Binkley, Krueger & Lewis, 2013).

Covariates

The covariates in this study included age, age at menopause, education, marital status, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors. Education was categorized as lower than elementary school, middle or high school, and college or higher. Marital status was classified as married or widowed/divorced. Smoking was defined as a history of smoking at least five packs or current smoking (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 1994). Heavy alcohol was defined as drinking two or more times per week (Rantaköme et al., 2014). CVD risk factors were assessed based on the American college of sports medicine pre-participation risk assessment (Thompson et al., 2013).

Statistics

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage (%). Independent t-test compared continuous variables based on the presence or absence of DMS, cognitive impairment, physical activity levels, and vitamin D levels. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Binomial logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratio (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the risk of DMS based on cognitive impairment, physical activity, and vitamin D levels. Mediation analysis was conducted with the process macro (Model 6) using 5,000 bootstrap samples to assess the mediating effects of physical activity and vitamin D levels on the relationship between cognitive function and DMS. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for PC (version 28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with the Process macro by Andrew F. Hayes for mediation analysis. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Comparison of variables according to physical activity and vitamin D levels

Table 3 presents a comparison of variables based on physical activity and vitamin D levels. ORs for DMS based on cognitive impairment, physical activity, and vitamin D levels

Table 4 presents the ORs for DMS in relation to cognitive impairment, physical activity, and vitamin D levels. Regarding cognitive impairment, the case group exhibited a significantly higher risk of DMS than the control group (OR = 1, reference). In Model 1, the OR was 3.162 (95% CI [1.544–6.475], p = 0.002). After adjusting for age in Model 2, the OR was 2.663 (95% CI [1.267–5.600], p = 0.010), and after further adjustments for sociodemographic and health-related factors in Model 3, the OR was 2.480 (95% CI [1.092–5.632], p = 0.030).

Analysis of the mediation effects of physical activity and vitamin D on the relationship between cognitive function and DMS

Table 5 and Fig. 1 illustrate the mediation effects of physical activity and vitamin D on the relationship between cognitive function and DMS. After adjusting for covariates, cognitive function exhibited direct effects on DMS (path c’, β = −0.1057, p < 0.001). Furthermore, cognitive function positively correlated with both physical activity (path a1, β = 31.8647, p = 0.032) and vitamin D (path a2, β = 0.3642, p = 0.049). In turn, these variables negatively correlated with DMS (path b1, β = −0.0003, p = 0.008; path b2, β = −0.0180, p = 0.037).

The direct effect is the coefficient c’. The total effect (c) is equal to the sum of the direct and indirect.

Mediation analysis.

Path coefficients of cognitive function on dysmobility syndrome through physical activity and vitamin D.

Bootstrapping was used to analyze the mediation effects of physical activity and vitamin D on the relationship between cognitive function and DMS. The 95% CI, which did not include 0, indicated that both physical activity and vitamin D mediated the association between cognitive function and DMS. The total mediation effects of physical activity and vitamin D (95% CI [−0.0334 to −0.0063]) accounted for 16.1% of the total effects of cognitive function on DMS. Specifically, the independent mediation effects of physical activity (95% CI [−0.0235 to −0.0009]) and vitamin D (95% CI [−0.0156 to −0.0007]) contributed 8.6% and 6.2%, respectively, while the mediation effect of the physical activity → vitamin D path (95% CI [−0.0036 to −0.0002]) explained 1.3% of the total effect. These results suggest that cognitive function directly affects DMS while exerting indirect effects via physical activity and vitamin D.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the mediation between cognitive function, physical activity, and vitamin D in relation to DMS in community-dwelling elderly women in South Korea. The cognitive impairment, physical inactivity, and vitamin D deficiency groups all exhibited a significantly higher risk of DMS than their respective control groups. Additionally, physical activity and vitamin D partially mediated the relationship between cognitive function and DMS, through both independent and combined sequential paths.

Cognitive impairment, characterized by a decline in memory, attention, verbal ability, and spatiotemporal skills, is prevalent among older adults, affecting approximately 19% of the elderly population globally (Pais et al., 2020). This condition not only impairs daily activities but also leads to negative physical changes. Studies have shown that cognitive impairment shares risk factors with sarcopenia, alcohol consumption, smoking, hypertension, and a sedentary lifestyle. These shared risk factors suggest common pathophysiological mechanisms, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance (Basile & Sardella, 2021). Furthermore, a reduction in osteocalcin, a marker of bone loss, has been linked to cognitive impairment (Obri et al., 2018). Evidence suggests that cognitive dysfunction is closely linked to osteoporosis (Li et al., 2016). Additionally, cognitive impairment is significantly associated with other physical impairments such as slow gait speed, weak grip strength, and increased fall risk (Montero-Odasso & Speechley, 2018; Peel et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2022). These findings indicate that cognitive impairment is closely related to risk factors for DMS and may serve as an independent risk factor. Our results support this, as the cognitive impairment group exhibited a significantly higher relative risk of DMS than its corresponding control group. Thus, cognitive dysfunction in old age appears to be an independent risk factor for DMS. However, due to the cross-sectional design of our study, the possibility of a bidirectional relationship between cognitive impairment and DMS cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

We also analyzed the relative risk of DMS based on physical activity and vitamin D status. The inactive group and the vitamin D < 20 ng/mL group had significantly higher risks of DMS compared to the active group and the vitamin D ≥ 20 ng/mL group, respectively. These findings align with two studies on elderly women, where low physical activity and lack of recreational physical activity were associated with increased risk of DMS and osteo-sarcopenic obesity (a physical phenotype combining osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and obesity) (Dos Santos et al., 2019; Dos Santos & Gobbo, 2020). Regarding vitamin D levels, our results are consistent with those of a study by Jia et al. (2022), which found that vitamin D deficiency significantly increased the risk of sarcopenic obesity in middle-aged and elderly Asians, as well as another by Mendes et al. (2018), which reported negative correlations between vitamin D and physical dysfunction indices (such as gait speed and grip strength) in elderly Europeans. The associations observed in this study may be explained by several underlying biological mechanisms. Physical activity induces mechanical loading on bone and muscle, which plays a crucial role in musculoskeletal health by regulating bone remodeling processes (Cariati et al., 2021). Mechanical stress modulates osteocyte-derived sclerostin, thereby affecting bone resorption and formation (Cariati et al., 2021; Sapir-Koren & Livshits, 2014). In addition, physical activity promotes skeletal muscle hypertrophy by increasing protein synthesis via the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway (Goodman, Hornberger & Robling, 2015). Vitamin D contributes to these processes by regulating insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells and enhancing glucose transport in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, thereby improving insulin sensitivity and influencing body composition (Szymczak-Pajor, Drzewoski & Śliwińska, 2020). Vitamin D is involved in calcium–phosphate homeostasis and the regulation of myogenic factors, contributing to bone remodeling as well as skeletal muscle cell proliferation, differentiation, and protein synthesis (Gunton et al., 2015; Mason et al., 2020; Montenegro et al., 2019). It also plays a role in energy homeostasis and supports muscle function and recovery, partly through interactions with parathyroid hormone (Mason et al., 2020). Overall, the present results extend the literature, highlighting the roles of physical activity and vitamin D levels in body composition changes and physical dysfunction in older adults. Specifically, the finding that both physical inactivity and vitamin D deficiency serve as independent risk factors for DMS suggests that this syndrome is not merely an inevitable part of biological aging, but a condition that may be influenced by modifiable lifestyle factors. This offers a perspective that DMS could be a preventable and manageable health status through proactive physical activity and vitamin D interventions. Ultimately, these findings emphasize the importance of maintaining adequate vitamin D levels and engaging in regular physical activity as an integrated approach to manage musculoskeletal health and promote healthy aging.

Physical activity and vitamin D partially mediated the association between cognitive function and DMS, both independently and through a combined sequential path. This mediating mechanism can be explained by the established relationship between physical activity and vitamin D. Physical activity promotes vitamin D synthesis by increasing exposure to ultraviolet radiation during outdoor activities (Kluczynski et al., 2011). In addition, independent of outdoor exposure, physical activity influences serum phosphate levels (Maïmoun & Sultan, 2009), which in turn regulate vitamin D through negative feedback mechanisms (Fukumoto, 2014). Physical activity may also promote vitamin D activation by decreasing ionized calcium levels, thereby stimulating parathyroid hormone secretion (Maïmoun & Sultan, 2009). These mechanisms suggest that physical activity influences vitamin D status and that both factors may jointly mediate the relationship between cognitive function and DMS. Consistent with this interpretation, Yao et al. (2023) found that physical activity reduced the risk of sarcopenia associated with cognitive dysfunction among Chinese individuals aged 60 years or older, Puente-González et al. (2021) observed that a 6-month exercise intervention improved bone density, balance, gait, and reduced fall risk in Alzheimer’s patients. Lee et al. (2020) reported significantly low vitamin D levels in Alzheimer’s patients with sarcopenia, and Peng et al. (2016) found that vitamin D was a key factor linking cognitive function and osteoporosis in elderly women. In summary, these findings highlight the importance of integrated strategies focused on promoting regular physical activity and maintaining adequate vitamin D levels to support musculoskeletal health and prevent physical dysfunction in older adults. Unlike previous studies that focused on the effects of physical activity and vitamin D on physical function separately, this study demonstrates their interrelated partial mediation effects, offering new insights for designing strategies to promote healthy aging and physical function changes. Specifically, although the sequential mediation effect (physical activity → vitamin D) in our study accounted for a relatively small proportion (1.3%) of the total effect, its statistical significance provides empirical evidence that these factors are part of an interrelated pathway. The total indirect effect (16.2%) suggests that while a meaningful portion of the association between cognitive function and DMS is explained by these variables, a larger part of the effect may be attributed to the direct influence of cognitive status or other unobserved factors. However, considering the substantial independent risk posed by vitamin D deficiency (OR = 4.071) shown in our results, even a small mediation through this pathway possesses practical significance for early intervention. These findings advance the current understanding of DMS by suggesting that its prevention requires an integrated strategy rather than focusing on a single risk factor. However, since this was a cross-sectional study, longitudinal research is needed to confirm causal relationships.

This study had certain limitations. First, the findings are limited to community-dwelling elderly women in South Korea, making it difficult to generalize to other ethnicities, elderly men, or all elderly women in South Korea. Second, the study did not investigate factors that may influence vitamin D levels, such as season, outdoor exposure, or use of health supplements. Further research should consider these factors. Third, the absence of data on medications and metabolic risk factors—which could impact DMS and cognitive impairment—allows for potential unobserved confounders. Fourth, due to the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be made. Fifth, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be excluded; specifically, the decline in physical function caused by DMS may restrict outdoor activities, directly leading to reduced sunlight exposure and vitamin D synthesis, which in turn could exacerbate cognitive impairment. Sixth, while physical activity was quantified using MET values from the IPAQ, the lack of specific data on resistance training precluded us from analyzing the distinct effects of different exercise modalities. Therefore, longitudinal studies addressing these limitations—including more granular assessments of both aerobic and resistance exercises to provide specific clinical guidance—are necessary to clarify the complex roles of physical activity and vitamin D in the relationship between cognitive function and DMS.

Conclusions

We analyzed the effects of cognitive function, physical activity, and vitamin D on DMS in elderly women living in the community. Our findings suggest that these factors the risk of DMS both independently and in combination. Specifically, a potential sequential mediation effect through the physical activity → vitamin D pathway was observed. While the effect size was modest, these results provide preliminary empirical support for developing integrated strategies to prevent declines in physical function and composition in older adults.
