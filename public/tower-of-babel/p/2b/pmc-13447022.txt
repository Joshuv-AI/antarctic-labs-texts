Isolated Maxillary Extracranial Metastasis of a Suprasellar Germinoma
Authors: Jun Hyun Lee, Hyeon Jin Park, Sang-Hoon Shin, Joo-Young Kim, So-Hyun Ji, Nayoung Han, Ji-Man Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracranial germinomas are exquisitely radiosensitive, and a tissue diagnosis obtained by minimal biopsy followed by radiotherapy (with or without chemotherapy) provides excellent long-term control; aggressive resection adds morbidity without oncological benefit. We report a 20-year-old man whose sellar and suprasellar mass was preoperatively interpreted as a pituitary adenoma and was treated by aggressive endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal resection after an intraoperative frozen section suggested adenoma; final histology demonstrated germinoma. The postoperative course was complicated by a persistent skull-base cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus meningitis/ventriculitis, and CSF dissemination documented by repeatedly positive CSF cytology and elevated CSF β-human chorionic gonadotropin. The initial skull-base reconstruction failed, and the resulting persistent CSF rhinorrhea required revision dural repair on postoperative day (POD) 29; proton craniospinal irradiation was delivered from POD 52. The patient achieved remission but, approximately 21 months after completing radiotherapy, developed a destructive germinoma lesion in the midline maxilla, with no recurrence at the primary site and no leptomeningeal disease at that time. In light of the anterior maxillary location and the relatively long interval after radiotherapy, we interpret the lesion as an isolated extracranial metastasis rather than a proven operative-tract event. The principal lesson is that accurate initial diagnosis and approach selection—measurement of tumor markers and minimal or transventricular biopsy in preference to resection—remain essential in young patients with sellar/suprasellar masses in whom germinoma is a diagnostic possibility.Keywords: Germinoma, Pituitary neoplasms, Neoplasm metastasis, Maxillary neoplasms, Craniospinal irradiation

INTRODUCTION

Intracranial germinomas are rare malignant germ-cell tumors that arise in midline structures—most often the pineal and suprasellar regions—and predominantly affect children and young adults, with a well-recognized higher incidence in East Asian populations [1,2,3]. Suprasellar germinomas typically present with central diabetes insipidus (DI), visual disturbance, and anterior hypopituitarism because of their location adjacent to the hypothalamic–pituitary axis [1,2]. Because germinomas are exquisitely sensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, combined-modality treatment achieves long-term disease control and survival exceeding 90% [2,4]; consequently, the goal of surgery is to secure a histological diagnosis with minimal intervention rather than cytoreduction, and radical resection increases morbidity without improving outcome [5,6].

In practice, the diagnosis is frequently delayed because the imaging appearance can mimic a pituitary macroadenoma [7,8]. Several clues should nonetheless raise suspicion of germinoma in a young patient with a sellar/suprasellar mass: young age and male sex in an East Asian population; the presence of central DI, which is characteristic of germinoma but uncommon in pituitary adenoma and may be clinically silent when a patient compensates by drinking [9]; loss of the normal posterior-pituitary “bright spot” on non-contrast sagittal T1-weighted images, whose absence supports central DI and a neurohypophyseal germinoma [9]; a synchronous enhancing pineal lesion indicating bifocal germinoma; and elevation of serum and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG). Recognition of these features permits a markers-and-biopsy-first strategy and avoids unnecessary violation of a skull-base corridor.

Tract metastasis after germinoma surgery is rare but has been reported along endoscopic third-ventriculostomy and ventricular-catheter tracts, typically manifesting from months to several years after the index procedure [10,11]. In contrast, isolated extracranial metastasis involving the maxilla after treatment of a suprasellar germinoma is exceptional. We report such a case after aggressive transsphenoidal resection complicated by a persistent CSF leak and infection. However, given the anterior maxillary location of the lesion and the 21-month interval after completion of radiotherapy, we interpret the maxillary lesion as an isolated extracranial metastasis rather than a proven surgical-tract event, and use the case to emphasize the importance of initial diagnostic accuracy and approach selection.

CASE REPORT

A 20-year-old man presented with progressively worsening visual field defects, dizziness, and headache. He had no documented preoperative DI; central DI developed only postoperatively (the first dose of vasopressin was given on the evening of surgery), consistent with operative manipulation of the infundibulum. Contrast-enhanced brain MRI demonstrated a 2.7×1.8×4.3 cm sellar and suprasellar mass extending into the third ventricle, displacing the optic chiasm anterosuperiorly, abutting—but not encasing—the cavernous segments of both internal carotid arteries, and producing mild obstructive hydrocephalus of both lateral ventricles (Fig. 1A); on FLAIR (fluid-attenuated inversion recovery) the mass was iso- to slightly hypointense. Dedicated review of the initial study showed a preserved posterior-pituitary bright spot and no pineal lesion, and the mass was interpreted as a probable pituitary macroadenoma. Preoperative serum and CSF tumor markers were not obtained, and germinoma was not raised in the preoperative differential—points we revisit in the Discussion.

The patient underwent a binostril endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal operation. A right-sided nasoseptal flap (approximately 7 cm long with a 2 cm pedicle) was raised, and the septal cartilage and vomer were removed to widen the midline corridor. After exposure of a thin sellar floor with focal bony dehiscence, a transsellar pituitary-capsule biopsy was performed; both the surgeon’s intraoperative impression and the frozen-section report were “compatible with pituitary adenoma,” although the frozen specimen was explicitly noted to show prominent pleomorphism and abundant mitoses. On that basis the dural opening was extended and an aggressive resection was undertaken. During supradiaphragmatic debulking the third ventricle was opened widely and CSF was spilled into the operative field; the resection was then extended anteriorly through the planum sphenoidale to remove the subfrontal component, leaving only a small chiasm-adherent portion. Two permanent specimens were submitted—the main mass and the pituitary stalk, identified as the tumor origin. The skull base was reconstructed with a fascia lata graft (placed in inlay–onlay fashion) reinforced with a dural sealant, followed by septal silastic splints and nasal packing. Immediate postoperative CT showed marked pneumocephalus in the resection cavity and bifrontal regions, reflecting the wide intradural communication created at surgery.

Final histopathology demonstrated germinoma: large round-to-polygonal cells with clear cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli in a lymphocyte-rich background, immunoreactive for c-kit (CD117), placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP), and SALL4, with focal β-hCG positivity and a Ki-67 labeling index exceeding 90%. Postoperative tumor markers were serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) 2.5 ng/mL and β-hCG 4.7 mIU/mL, with CSF AFP <0.4 ng/mL and an elevated CSF β-hCG of 40.4 mIU/mL. The normal AFP argued against a yolk-sac component, and the mildly elevated β-hCG together with focal β-hCG immunostaining was consistent with a germinoma containing syncytiotrophoblastic giant cells.

On postoperative day (POD) 17 the patient developed high fever (39.1℃) and altered mental status; CSF showed a white-cell count of 13,920/mm3 and cultures grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), confirming meningitis and ventriculitis. He was transferred to our institution on POD 22. MRI on POD 26 showed residual enhancing tumor in the right sellar/suprasellar region and thick ependymal/subependymal enhancement consistent with ventriculitis (Fig. 1B and C).

Despite this reconstruction, persistent CSF rhinorrhea developed and did not resolve with repeated lumbar drainage, requiring a revision dural repair on POD 29, after which the leak resolved with no further rhinorrhea documented. The persistent fistula reflected failure of the skull-base reconstruction rather than the extent of tumor removal itself. Progressive hydrocephalus required several external ventricular drain (EVD) placements and revisions between POD 31 and POD 67, and on POD 44 the patient developed septic shock from systemic MRSA dissemination.

Serial CSF cytology obtained during EVD management repeatedly demonstrated malignant germinoma cells, confirming CSF dissemination. Proton craniospinal irradiation was therefore delivered from POD 52 to POD 86 with the EVD in place, with prescribed doses of 25.2 Gy to the craniospinal axis, 30.6 Gy to the whole ventricles, and a 39.6 Gy boost to the primary tumor bed. CSF cytology converted to negative near the completion of irradiation. The EVD was removed on POD 88, and a ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed on POD 92 for definitive management of hydrocephalus.

Approximately 21 months after completing radiotherapy, the patient presented with upper-jaw pain and swelling. MRI showed a new 4.3×2.7 cm enhancing mass centered on the midline maxilla, invading the nasal cavity and left maxillary sinus (Fig. 2A); CT demonstrated permeative osteolysis indicating an aggressive lesion (Fig. 2B); and PET-CT (positron emission tomography–computed tomography) showed intense hypermetabolism (maximum standardized uptake value 13.3) (Fig. 2C). Biopsy confirmed recurrent germinoma with an immunophenotype identical to the primary tumor (Fig. 3). At recurrence, tumor markers were serum AFP 3.2 ng/mL, serum β-hCG 6.8 mIU/mL, CSF AFP <0.4 ng/mL, and CSF β-hCG <3 mIU/mL. Importantly, there was no enhancement at the primary sellar/suprasellar site and no leptomeningeal disease, consistent with an isolated extracranial metastasis rather than local or craniospinal relapse.

The patient received six cycles of systemic chemotherapy (modified KSPNO-G081 protocol) followed by focal proton radiotherapy to the maxilla (30.6 Gy in 17 fractions). At the most recent follow-up, approximately five years after completion of salvage therapy, he remained in remission, with stable post-treatment changes on the most recent surveillance MRI; subtle leptomeningeal enhancement seen on follow-up imaging was interpreted as a treatment-related change rather than recurrent disease.

DISCUSSION

The most important lesson of this case concerns initial diagnosis rather than surgical technique. In a young—particularly East Asian—patient with a sellar/suprasellar mass, germinoma should rank high in the differential, and a pituitary macroadenoma with marked suprasellar extension is comparatively unlikely at this age [7,8]. Most of the complications that followed were downstream of the decision to proceed to resection. Had germinoma been suspected preoperatively, the appropriate pathway would have been measurement of serum and CSF AFP and β-hCG followed by minimal biopsy for histological confirmation; because the tumor reached the third ventricle and produced obstructive hydrocephalus, an endoscopic transventricular biopsy combined with internal CSF diversion (endoscopic third ventriculostomy or septostomy) could have provided tissue and treated the hydrocephalus in a single procedure, without opening the transsphenoidal corridor or creating a skull-base defect [5,6,12]. In our patient this opportunity was missed: tumor markers were not obtained, and although the frozen section was read as compatible with adenoma, it was explicitly noted to show prominent pleomorphism and abundant mitoses—atypical features for a benign adenoma that should themselves have prompted reconsideration of the diagnosis and measurement of markers before any radical resection.

Several clues available before and during surgery should have prompted this strategy. Central DI is characteristic of suprasellar germinoma and uncommon in adenoma and may be clinically silent when a patient compensates by drinking; when present it is a strong pointer to germinoma [9]. On imaging, loss of the normal posterior-pituitary bright spot on sagittal non-contrast T1-weighted images and any subtle enhancing pineal lesion (indicating bifocal disease) likewise favor germinoma [9]. In our patient, however, these pointers were absent—DI was absent preoperatively and emerged only after surgery, the posterior-pituitary bright spot was preserved, and no pineal lesion was present—so none signaled germinoma, and together they fostered the impression of adenoma. This illustrates an important caveat: although these features strongly suggest germinoma when present, their absence does not exclude it, and age, sex, ethnicity, and tumor-marker testing remain decisive. Tumor markers should be obtained before any operative intervention; here they were available only postoperatively, removing an opportunity for preoperative diagnosis.

We are careful not to overstate the causal role of the extent of resection. The central nervous system infection in this patient was a consequence of skull-base reconstruction failure with a persistent CSF fistula—the portal through which MRSA reached the subarachnoid space—rather than of tumor removal as such; and CSF dissemination is an intrinsic biological property of germinoma, here evidenced by repeatedly positive CSF cytology and elevated CSF β-hCG, not an artifact created by surgery. What the aggressive resection did contribute was avoidable morbidity: a large skull-base defect, persistent CSF leakage, meningitis/ventriculitis, repeated CSF-diversion procedures, and delayed initiation of definitive oncologic therapy. These events may have created a biologically permissive context, but they do not establish a direct route of spread to the maxilla.

An important limitation of any operative-tract explanation is the anatomical relationship between the recurrence site and the prior endonasal approach. The recurrent lesion was centered in the anterior midline maxilla, whereas the most anterior aspect of the maxillary bone is not directly exposed during a standard endonasal transsphenoidal approach. Although nasal septal and sphenoidal structures were manipulated during the operation, direct exposure of the anterior maxillary bone to the tumor-bearing operative field cannot be asserted. Therefore, the anatomical correspondence between the surgical pathway and the recurrence site is insufficient to support a definite operative-tract origin.

The time course also supports a more conservative interpretation. The maxillary lesion was detected approximately 21 months after completion of radiotherapy, an interval that is longer than would be expected for a direct operative-route event in view of the typical radiosensitivity and growth behavior of germinoma. At the time of maxillary recurrence, there was no recurrence at the primary sellar/suprasellar site, no leptomeningeal disease, and no elevation of CSF β-hCG. Taken together, these findings favor the diagnosis of an isolated extracranial metastasis rather than proven spread along the previous surgical route.

The exact route of extracranial spread remains uncertain. The history of cytology-positive CSF dissemination and persistent postoperative CSF rhinorrhea raises the possibility that malignant cells reached extracranial tissues, but neither factor proves direct tumor deposition in the anterior maxilla. Hematogenous or lymphatic spread, microscopic extracranial disease below the threshold of initial detection, or another unrecognized route cannot be excluded. We therefore describe the recurrence as an isolated maxillary extracranial metastasis and avoid assigning a definitive mechanism.

Our experience reinforces several key principles: 1) the most effective prevention of avoidable morbidity is accurate initial diagnosis and approach selection, with tumor markers plus minimal or transventricular biopsy preferred to resection whenever germinoma is plausible; 2) when germinoma is recognized intraoperatively—or when a frozen section is equivocal in a young patient—further resection should be abandoned in favor of adjuvant therapy; 3) skull-base CSF leaks should be repaired promptly to prevent infectious complications and treatment delays; and 4) follow-up should include attention to unexplained extracranial symptoms, because rare isolated extracranial metastasis may occur even when the primary intracranial site and leptomeningeal spaces remain controlled.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that isolated extracranial metastasis to the maxilla can occur after treatment of a suprasellar germinoma, even in the absence of recurrence at the primary intracranial site or leptomeningeal relapse at the time of detection. Because the most anterior maxillary bone is not directly exposed during an endonasal transsphenoidal approach and because the lesion appeared 21 months after radiotherapy, the recurrence should not be regarded as a proven surgical-tract event. The case primarily emphasizes the importance of accurate initial diagnosis: in a young patient with a sellar/suprasellar mass, germinoma should be considered, serum and CSF tumor markers should be obtained, and minimal or transventricular biopsy should be preferred over radical resection whenever feasible.
