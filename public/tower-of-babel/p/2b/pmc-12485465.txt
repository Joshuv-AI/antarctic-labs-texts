Authors’ response to comments on “Efficacy and safety of low-dose naltrexone for the management of fibromyalgia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with trial sequential analysis”
Authors: Timotius Ivan Hariyanto, Akhil Deepak Vatvani, Pratik Patel, Theo Audi Yanto
Published: 2025

TO THE EDITOR

We appreciate the interest shown in our recently published article, “Efficacy and safety of low-dose naltrexone for the management of fibromyalgia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with trial sequential analysis,” in the Korean Journal of Pain [1]. We are grateful to the authors of the letter for their thoughtful comments and would like to take this opportunity to address their points in this correspondence.

As highlighted in their letter, the decision to use either fixed-effects or random-effects models in meta-analytical research remains a topic of ongoing discussion. Currently, there is no universally accepted guideline or standardized approach for determining the most appropriate model across different contexts. Consequently, model selection is not inherently correct or incorrect but depends on the specific analytical assumptions and goals of the study.

In their correspondence, the authors advocate for selecting meta-analytic models based on the I² statistic. Specifically, they suggest using a fixed-effect model when I² is 50% or lower and the heterogeneity test yields a non-significant result (P ≥ 0.05), and a random-effects model when I² exceeds 50% with a significant P value (P < 0.05). However, Borenstein et al. [2] have cautioned against this practice for several reasons. First, relying on a non-significant test for heterogeneity to justify a common effect size can be misleading, as such tests often lack statistical power and may fail to detect real heterogeneity, especially with small sample sizes or limited numbers of studies [2]. Second, this approach inherently assumes the fixed-effect model as the default, which may not be appropriate [2]. The random-effects model is generally more flexible, as it accounts for both within- and between-study variability [2]. In fact, the fixed-effect model can be considered a special case of the random-effects model, where between-study variance is assumed to be zero—a restriction that is often unsubstantiated and may not reflect the true data structure [2]. Third, choosing a random-effects model carries minimal risk [2]. Its weighting incorporates both within-study variance (V) and between-study variance (T²), and when T² is negligible, the estimates produced by both models are identical [2]. This was evident in our analysis of pain score outcomes. With an I² value of 33%, applying the random-effects model produced a pooled mean difference of –0.86 (95% CI: –1.20 to –0.51; P < 0.00001), as shown in Fig. 1A. Using the fixed-effect model for the same outcome yielded a nearly identical result: a mean difference of –0.86 (95% CI: –1.12 to –0.60; P < 0.00001), illustrated in Fig. 1B.

Forest plot that demonstrates the change in the pain scores in fibromyalgia patients who received low-dose naltrexone (LDN) when compared to those who received placebo by using random-effects (A) and fixed-effect (B) models. SD: standard deviation, CI: confidence intervals.

Model selection in meta-analysis should primarily be guided by how well a given model represents the underlying variability in effect sizes and incorporates the appropriate sources of error [2,3]. While meta-analytical techniques aim to quantify effects statistically, they inevitably involve uncertainties that may not be fully captured by numerical synthesis [2,3]. Therefore, it is prudent to apply analytical methods that are conservative in their assumptions and less prone to overstating precision [2,3]. In most cases—especially when studies are drawn from diverse published sources—the random-effects model tends to offer a more appropriate representation of the data structure [2,3].

In our study, we adopted the random-effects model as the default approach based on the expectation that clinical and methodological differences would exist across studies [4–7]. Variability in participant characteristics and how the interventions were delivered likely contributed to heterogeneity in effect estimates. This is supported by Table 1 in our article, which highlights variations in the duration of low-dose naltrexone treatment, length of follow-up, concurrent pain medication regimens, as well as baseline participant features such as age and body mass index. These factors are all plausible contributors to differences in treatment response and thus justify the use of a random-effects framework.

We acknowledge the concern raised about the phrasing “random-effect model” instead of the more accurate term “random-effects model” and regret this oversight. Nonetheless, we consider this a minor typographical issue that does not affect the validity or reliability of the results presented in our study.
