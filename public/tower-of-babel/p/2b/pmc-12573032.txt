Prioritizing Data Quality in Machine Learning for Thermophysical Property Prediction: A Case Study on Normal Boiling Points of Organic Compounds
Authors: Frank T. Mtetwa, Neil F. Giles, W.Vincent Wilding, Thomas A. Knotts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of the normal boiling point (NBP) of organic compounds is essential for the design and optimization of chemical processes. This is especially true for modern practice since experimental measurements of many compounds of contemporary importance are difficult to perform due to cost, purity, and stability issues. Traditional methods, such as group contribution (GC) and quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) approaches, are limited by inflexibility and diminishing accuracy in these situations. Machine learning (ML) approaches have shown promise but are plagued by two weaknesses for the nonspecialist: ease of use (especially compared to GC and QSPR) and proven accuracy for compounds outside the training set. The large effort required to use an ML model of others, let alone develop a new model, is the source of the first problem. The second often arises due to the data-intensive nature of ML. In particular, the origin and reliability of the data used to train a model has to date been of secondary importance compared to finding the optimal ML techniques to use for a given task. This study addresses both these issues. First, we explore the effectiveness of ML models trained on two data sets: a smaller, rigorously curated experimental set from the DIPPR 801 database and a larger, publicly available but uncurated data set. Four distinct molecular featurization methods were evaluated, including RDKit descriptors, Joback groups, and two graph-based representations using graph neural networks (GNNs). Models were implemented using LightGBM and PyTorch, and performance was assessed through a 10-fold cross-validation across four evaluation frameworks. Despite conventional expectations that larger data sets yield superior models, results consistently showed that the curated data set of fewer but certifiably accurate values outperformed the uncurated one in terms of accuracy, bias reduction, and generalization. These findings emphasize the importance of data quality over quantity and advocate for a data-centric approach in ML applications for chemical property prediction. A user-friendly web application is also provided to facilitate access to the best-trained model for the nonspecialist, where all that is needed is a SMILES formula (a single and simple text string that is widely available) for the molecule.

Introduction

Accurate prediction of the NBP of chemical compounds is fundamental to the design and optimization of industrial processes, particularly those involving phase changes, such as distillation and evaporation. NBP is also a critical thermophysical property that influences a wide range of engineering and scientific applications, including material design, thermal system development, and the analysis of transport phenomena. , Understanding and predicting NBP is essential not only for the effective operation of these processes but also for the estimation of other key properties, such as critical temperature, vapor pressure, and liquid density, which are often derived from NBP. , However, traditional methods for predicting NBP, such as QSPR approaches and GC methods, suffer from three key weaknesses: (1) they are hard to automate (especially for complex formulations with many group corrections and their accompanying ambiguity), (2) they struggle to capture the full complexity of molecular interactions, and (3) they seem to have reached a plateau in accuracy where refinement is no longer leading to better performance. The first two become important for the multifunctional and associating compounds, which are of contemporary importance for greener chemistries. The relevance of the third is problematic, as errors in NBP are magnified when predicted NBP values are used to obtain estimates of other properties. In short, better methods for predicting NBP are always welcome in the thermophysical properties community. ,

GC methods, mentioned above, have been workhorse techniques for property prediction for decades. The approaches predict compound properties based on the sum of contributions from individual chemical groups found in a particular molecule. Advanced GC methods, such as the one by Nannoolal et al., attempt to incorporate interaction terms between groups to move beyond simple additive assumptions. Despite these efforts, GC methods still rely heavily on empirical data and assume consistent group contributions across different compounds. These lead to significant prediction errors, especially in complex molecules. Several GC methods, such as those by Joback, Constantinou and Gani, Stein and Brown, Nannoolal, and Emami, have been proposed for NBP prediction. A comprehensive analysis by Elliot et al. revealed that these methods often produce high errors for associating compounds as well as polar compounds, highlighting the need for more sophisticated approaches. QSPR models depend heavily on the selection of molecular descriptors and are sensitive to issues such as multicollinearity and insufficient variance among the descriptors. Moreover, they are usually accurate only for the family of chemicals for which they were created.

In recent years, ML approaches have emerged as promising alternatives for molecular property prediction tasks with the potential to overcome each of the weaknesses outlined above. These methods can model complex, nonlinear relationships without relying on rigid mathematical forms, and they have demonstrated superior performance on various prediction tasks, including NBP estimation. − However, ML approaches also have deficiencies. This work concerns two. The first is that it has proven very difficult for groups to replicate the methods proposed by others. This is particularly problematic for frontline engineers, who often lack the time, resources, and/or specialized training required to develop ML techniques or implement those found in the literature. (Replicating ML results among different groups is difficult even for specialists in the field). Second, the focus of ML researchers to date has been on the development of novel ML algorithms, and relatively little effort has gone into examining how the accuracy of the underlying data affects the accuracy of resulting predictions (more on this later). To summarize, the widespread use of ML for thermophysical property predictions is hindered by two factors. First, an easy interface for the lay engineer to use the work of others, and second, data curation.

It would seem that the former should be rather easy to fix. Some groups have posted codes toward this end, but updates to the open-source software on which these are built render these unusable after just a year or two. As such, even though numerous models have been published, fewif anyare available through a user-friendly online interface.

Concerning the weakness in data curation, inherent to any ML approach for molecular systems is the identification of how to distinguish one molecule from another. The QSPR community has been at this for years, and most ML papers to date have focused on different ways to combine the thousands of molecular descriptors (e.g., molecular weight, atom types composing the molecule, dipole moment, number of double bonds, branching, polarity, etc.) that have been around for decades and translate these into machine learning numerical inputs (featurization). Determining which features describing chemicals are best correlated to thermophysical properties has become a sort of arms race that has defined the field. What has been neglected is the effect of data quality on the resulting models.

Focusing on data quality does not imply that featurization is unimportant. On the contrary, determining how to represent one molecule from another plays a critical role in determining both the accuracy and transferability of the resulting ML model. Transferability refers to the ability of the model to make accurate predictions of compounds that were not part of the training set. This work incorporates the best featurization practices that have been published in this regard, while addressing the importance of data curation and providing a user-friendly interface for the best model.

It has been known for decades that the performance of any prediction method is fundamentally tied to the data on which it is trained. Incomplete, noisy, or poorly integrated data sets not only limit the potential accuracy of predictions but also reduce the model’s ability to generalize effectively across a broad range of applications. Yet this lesson is not always applied in ML approaches because they are data-intensive. In fact, once a user learns ML basics and algorithms, the hardest part of creating an ML model is obtaining data with which to perform training. Commonly, practitioners scrape values indiscriminately from any available electronic source, giving little thought to the quality of the data or whether it comes from experimental measurements or predictive estimates and often without assessing its reasonableness. Such an approach can be justified when the focus is indeed on optimizing the protocols, but we will show that data curation becomes crucial once the focus shifts to making a prediction technique sufficiently accurate for design purposes.

Some have argued that the size of the data set is more important than the quality, and this seems to be the opinion that has won out, to date, with thermophysical property prediction using ML. As an example, the data set from the publicly available SPEED database reports values for 25 pure-component properties, which, according to the publication, are “based on experimental data.” Its NBP subset consists of 5277 entries. This number seems unreasonable, however, as Elliot et al. found only 4467 high-quality experimental values for NBP when they did a rigorous analysis. This suggests that the validity of any freely available set with a larger number should be viewed with caution. Moreover, some of the values in the SPEED set are clearly incorrect. For example, 4-phenyldiazenylbenzene-1,3-diamine is said to have an NBP of 2535.15 K. For this relatively small compound, this value is questionable, as the chemical would likely decompose or combust at a much lower temperature, and thus the number cannot be considered experimental or reliable. Another example of unreliable data in this set is the NBP of chlorine, which is stated to be 993.15 Kfar above the 239 K reported by multiple sources. −

In short, this work tests the hypothesis that training and validating an ML model on a smaller, rigorously curated data set yields more accurate predictions than using a larger collection of unknown origin or reliability. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We begin by explaining the source of the data and the ML methods used to test the hypothesis. We then present the results and discuss their implications. The analysis suggests that the model prepared using a small but accurate data set predicts better than other approaches, and we publish the best method in a simple web interface so others may validate our results and use our model for research and design.

Because the main purpose of this paper is to make an ML model for NBP prediction accessible to scientists and engineers without training in ML jargon and techniques, the paper is written to avoid specialized vocabulary when possible. Emphasis is also given to the repetition of key concepts and vocabulary throughout the narrative. Due to the need for scientific rigor in reporting, some details presented below will be difficult for the nonspecialist to understand. But jargon-heavy sections may largely be skipped or ignored by the ML novice without compromising understanding of the concepts. For such readers, it is sufficient to understand that we are using generally accepted ML best practices. The details are presented so that this claim can be evaluated by those trained in ML techniques.

Data and Methods

Data Preparation

The “high-quality” data set is drawn from the DIPPR 801 database , an industrial standard whose NBP values undergo a thorough evaluation. This evaluation process uses a systems-level approach to select accepted values for each of the properties in the database. By this is meant that multiple dozens of rigorous and qualitative checks are performed for data validation. For NBP specifically, the accuracy of the value is ascertained by ensuring it and the associated vapor pressure curve agree rigorously with several other properties, including critical temperature, critical pressure, ideal gas heat capacity, liquid heat capacity, liquid density, and heat of vaporization. , Only organic compounds with accepted experimental values for NBP in the database were selected for this work. This gave 1632 compounds covering 76 chemical families and will be referred to as the DIPPR data set.

The other data set used in this work was created from the publicly available SPEED database. As mentioned in the introduction, this set of values has known inaccuracies and does not distinguish between experimental and predicted points but has been used previously to create ML models for NBP. All inorganic compounds, as well as duplicated data, were removed from the SPEED compilation, and 5175 compounds were left. This set is called the SPEED data set hereafter.

Figure illustrates the distribution of NBP values in the DIPPR data set, and Figure is for the SPEED data set. Note that the range of values is roughly equal between the two sets. This is important when parsing the data into different sets for training, validation, and testing ML models.

To give an unbiased estimate of the performance of ML models trained on the DIPPR and SPEED sets, a test set of 376 random compounds, which overlapped both the DIPPR and SPEED data sets, was created. Accurate, reliable, experimental values for NBP were available in the literature for each of these chemicals. These 376 compounds were not used in any way to train or validate any ML model shown later for either the DIPPR or SPEED databases. They were held in reserve to test the accuracy of models trained on the remaining data in each set. When these 376 compounds were removed from both data sets (the same 376 compounds in each case), the final training sets were composed of 1259 and 4799 compounds for the DIPPR and SPEED versions, respectively.

Feature Selection and Engineering

ML algorithms work only with numerical inputs for efficient computations. Other types of data, such as strings (text), pictures, diagrams, etc., must be converted into numerical representations before being provided to the algorithm, and the output must then be transformed into forms other than numerical if desired. The selection, extraction, and transformation of the features or inputs (numerical descriptions of the molecules in this work) and labels or targets (values for NBP in this work) must be numerical. The latter is naturally satisfied for property prediction, but many options exist for the former. This is a crucial step, as the quality and relevance of the features have a significant impact on the performance of the model. , Therefore, it is important to carefully choose and engineer features that capture relevant chemical information from the data and remove noise or irrelevant information.

All features were generated from a molecule’s simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) formula because of their ubiquity and ease of use. SMILES is a string notation system that represents the chemical structure of a molecule in a compact way. They have proven useful in computational chemistry, molecular simulations, and ML for decades because they may be standardized, are easy to understand, can be generated by hand relatively easily, and can be generated automatically from 2D representations of molecular structures using open-source as well as commercial software. −

As mentioned above, feature selection can greatly affect the performance of an ML model, and there are many possible feature sets available for chemicals. One of the goals of the ML field, with regard to molecules, is to find the optimal way to describe a molecule (if there is one). To this end, four different approaches for feature selection were tested and supplied to the ML algorithms. The methods used were one based on RDKit molecular descriptors another on Joback functional groups and two on molecular graphs. ,

RDKit Descriptors

RDKit is an open-source cheminformatics software library that calculates 217 molecular 2D descriptors with only a SMILES string as the input. It is frequently used in ML publications because it is freely available, and its Python implementation is easy to use, even though it produces relatively few descriptors. ,− The full list of descriptors is available in the literature, but examples include molecular weight, number of heavy atoms, and topological polar surface area.

The molecular descriptors can range over orders of magnitude in value. Features with large numerical variations have been shown to produce inferior ML models , so all the features were scaled using a standard scaler. This technique involves normalizing or standardizing the input features of the data set and ensures that no single feature dominates the learning process or has undue influence on the model. The standardization is done according to 1 xscaled=xi−x̅σ

Here, x i represents individual descriptor values, x̅ is the mean of the descriptor values, and σ is the standard deviation.

When ML first became widespread, it was thought that these techniques might be an answer to many problems because they can handle so many inputs. However, research − has shown that reducing the features to focus only on those most correlated with the target and least correlated with each other makes more transferable models. Thus, the 217 RDKit descriptors were evaluated to create a reduced feature set.

One of the goals of feature reduction is to reduce the multicollinearity among features. In layman’s terms, for the present case, two features are collinear if they correspond to the same physical feature of the molecule. For example, if a model for the NBP of n-alkanes was being created, two features that would be collinear are (1) the number of carbons in the molecule and (2) the molecular weight of the molecule. One of these would be eliminated through feature reduction to produce a better model.

Feature reduction may be performed by using several numerical techniques. This study used a two-step process. The first step involved calculating the pairwise Spearman correlation coefficient (SCC), defined as 2 SCC=∑i=1n(R(xi)−R̅x)(R(yi)−R̅y)∑i=1n(R(xi)−R̅x)2∑i=1n(R(yi)−R̅y)2 where R(x i ) and R(yi) are the individual ranks of data points for the molecular descriptors and NBP, respectively, and R̅x and R̅y are the mean ranks. (The rank of a value is its position when the data are sorted in ascending order.) The SCC was calculated among all pairs of features to identify highly correlated pairs, defined as those with an SCC greater than 0.9. Only one feature from each correlated pair was retained to train the model. This was decided by determining the SCC between each feature in the pair and the target variable (NBP) and removing the feature with the lower correlation. This approach retains only the most informative features.

The second step of feature reduction is a process called variance thresholding. Low-variance features are undesirable because they cannot distinguish one molecule from another, resulting in minimal contribution to predictive power. Including them can also introduce noise into the model. Variance thresholding was done using the open-source software scikit-learn, and only features with variances greater than 0.1 were retained. This method ensured that only features with a sufficient variance were preserved. The combined, two-step feature reduction approach lowered the number of RDKit features from 217 to 80. The identity of the 80 RDKit descriptors comprising the second feature set is found in the Supporting Information.

Joback Groups

The second approach to create features for the model was to describe each molecule as a set of functional groups following the method of Joback. For example, hexane is composed of four >CH2 and two −CH3 groups, while propanal has one −CH3, one >CH2, and one <OCH group. This approach was included because the Joback groups are ubiquitous in the property prediction field. Thermo, an open-source software for the calculation of temperature- and pressure-dependent chemical properties (both thermodynamic and transport), was used to determine the number of each type of the 41 Joback groups comprising each molecule based on its SMILES string.

Molecular Graphs

The final feature set tested was the molecular graph approach inspired by the work of Yang et al. Graph-based descriptors are so named because they may be pictured as dots (termed nodes) connected by lines (termed edges). The idea is to show the interconnectedness between the information. Molecular graphs apply the concept to chemistry and are mathematical representations of molecules where the graph is constructed by representing atoms as nodes and chemical bonds between atoms as edges. This provides a way to model and analyze molecular structures using graph theory. Unlike the previously described descriptor-based featurization methods, where the ML algorithm was provided with multiple features, molecular graphs involve transforming these features into a single form that is directly interpreted by the ML algorithm. More specifically, the molecular graph serves as the input to the ML algorithm rather than a list of several separate descriptors. This has been shown to improve the performance of ML algorithms. ,

In the implementation of molecular graphs described here, common chemical descriptors, such as atom type and bond count, are used to create nodes (atoms) and edges (bonds); however, these descriptors are first transformed into a single vector for each compound, which is then provided to the ML algorithm. Data Set Splitting and Cross-Validation

In ML, the process of data set splitting is crucial for training and evaluating model performance effectively. Typically, data are divided into distinct subsets, including a training set, a validation set, and a test set, each serving a specific purpose in model development. As explained in Section , splitting of the data set into training and test sets, the latter of which was composed of 376 compounds and was never used in any model development but only to test the models, produced a DIPPR training set of 1259 compounds and a SPEED training set of 4799 compounds.

Models were developed using the common practice of 10-fold cross-validation performed independently on each training subset (1259 DIPPR vs 4799 SPEED) and each featurization approach. In this procedure, the training set data are partitioned into ten stratified folds or parts, each preserving the target variable distribution (see Figures and ). The model is iteratively trained on nine of the folds and validated on the remaining one.

Validation is the phase of model development in which hyperparameters are tuned by using cross-validation. In 10-fold cross-validation, the model is trained on nine of the folds and validated on the remaining one, and then this process is repeated ten times so that each fold serves exactly once as the validation set. The goal of 10-fold cross-validation is to quantify performance and guard against overfitting to any subset of the training data, thereby improving the model’s transferability to unseen compounds. Cross-validation can be thought of as an optimization stage where some of the data in the training set are held back initially when the model is created and then used to evaluate and refine the model. The test set, the 376 compounds removed from both training sets, is never used to train or validate the model. Rather, after all model parameters are fixed, the model is used to predict the NBP values for the compounds in the test set to assess the transferability of a model.

As is standard practice, performance metrics (described later) were averaged across the results of the 10 folds to compare the performance of the model on the data used (DIPPR or SPEED) for training/validating the models. Uncertainty in the metrics was quantified using the standard error of the mean, defined by 3 SEM=σn where σ is the standard deviation of the n values of the error metric, and n is the number of cross-validation folds. As mentioned above, n = 10 is used in this work.

Model Architectures

The ML frameworks examined in this work included Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) and PyTorch based architectures. Each of the three feature selection approaches detailed in Section (RDKit, Joback Groups, and Molecular Graphs) was evaluated across these frameworks. LightGBM achieved the highest performance using the RDKit 80 descriptors, and a PyTorch standard network produced the best results for the Joback group descriptors.

Graph-based descriptors may be used as features in any ML algorithm, but PyTorch has a specialized extension (PyTorch Geometric) for handling graph-structured data. Within this extension are two algorithm options: graph convolutional networks (GCNs) and message passing neural networks , (MPNNs), which are both flavors of GNN methods. GCNs operate by aggregating node features from neighboring nodes using shared linear transformations. MPNNs extend this concept through a more flexible architecture that separates the learning process into message passing, node updating, and readout phases. This general formulation allows for the integration of edge features and customized aggregation functions. We tested both of these approaches on the molecular graph feature set. In summary, the results of four ML algorithms are presented below: RDKit 80 implemented in LightGBM, Joback in PyTorch, and the two molecular graphs (GCN and MPNN) in PyTorch Geometric. As mentioned above, each of the feature sets was tested using both ML algorithms (e.g., PyTorch on RDKit 80), but those not listed below produced inferior results compared to those listed.

The hyperparameters for all the methods were optimized using Optuna with the tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) algorithm. The search space included the learning rate, regularization strength, and model-specific architecture parameters. This method provided an effective balance between exploration and exploitation, ensuring reproducibility and consistency across all experiments. Hyperparameter ranges are listed in Tables and .

Results and Discussion

In this section, we present and analyze the performance of the models developed for predicting NBP. As is common, we list the performance of the model to fit the training and validation subsets (data from the 1259 DIPPR and 4799 SPEED training sets, which were each split into 10-fold cross-validation) and the 376 compounds in the test set, which were never used in any way to determine model parameters. Results are presented in both tabular and graphical form. The former lists the metrics defined in Section for training and validation subsets for the 1259 DIPPR and 4799 SPEED databases, while the latter are parity plots used to show the performance of the models on the 376 test set compounds. The figures also list the MAPE and MAE for each model on the test set. We split the presentation of the results into tables and figures to emphasize the type of data being shown. Compounds in the tabular results were used in the creation of the model. Compounds in the graphical results were never used in any form to create the model and serve to test the transferability of a model.

Table shows the statistical metrics for training and validation for each feature approach on the DIPPR data set, and Table shows results for the SPEED data set. As explained above, the errors found in the tables were obtained from the 10-fold cross-validation as described in Section . Notice that the Joback groups performed poorly on both the training and validation metrics on both the DIPPR and SPEED data sets. This is slightly surprising since group contribution methods have been the mainstay of property prediction for the last few decades, and they are thought to encode significant chemical information into the approach. It appears, however, that the single-valued groups struggle to capture the complexities introduced by environments. For example, a >CH2 group in an alkane may not contribute to the physical phenomena giving rise to NBP as the same group would in a molecule with many functional groups. The RDKit feature set and the two molecular graph sets, GCN and MPNN, appear better at capturing these complexities.

Notice the general trend of models created using the 1259-compound DIPPR training set outperforming those created using the 4799-compound SPEED training set. Specifically, for the RDKit descriptors, the parity plot for the DIPPR data set (Figure a) shows a better fit compared to that of the SPEED data set (Figure a), the latter of which has visible outliers throughout. Numerically, the DIPPR MAPE is 3.4% vs the SPEED value of 4.5%.

For Joback group descriptors (panel (b) in each figure), models trained on both databases display some biased behavior in different regions. In particular, the DIPPR data set model (Figure b) produced several outliers for compounds with NBPs above 550 K, which is consistent with the fact that it relies on additive group contributions that may not account for steric effects, intramolecular interactions, or conformational flexibility in larger or more complex molecules. The Joback SPEED results (Figure b), also based on this model, consistently overpredicted the NBP for compounds with experimental values less than 400 K and underpredicted for compounds with experimental values above 600 K.

Finally, for both GNN methods (GCN and MPNN), the DIPPR data set models (Figure c,d) produced better results than the SPEED data set models (Figure c,d). Moreover, very little bias was found in these graph-based approaches.

To assess transferability (the ability of the model to accurately predict the NBP for compounds not used in its training) across the different featurization methods for the two data sets, we computed the difference between the MAPEs of the training and test sets. Specifically, we defined 7 ΔMAPE=MAPEtrain−MAPEtest

Negative values of ΔMAPE mean the model performs more poorly on compounds it has never seen before. Thus, the lower (more negative) the value, the poorer the transferability of the model due to overfitting the training set. Models that predict well for compounds outside the training set will have values of ΔMAPE close (or closer) to zero.

Figure reports the ΔMAPE values for each featurization method across the DIPPR and SPEED data sets. In each case, models trained on the DIPPR data set produce ΔMAPE values that are closer to zero than their SPEED counterparts. This is particularly true for Joback (0.19), GCN (−0.01), and MPNN (−0.65). The slightly positive ΔMAPE for the DIPPR/Joback model can occur for models with particularly good transferability, and such has been observed by Babaei et al. for thermophysical property prediction situations.

The results of Figure deserve additional discussion because they suggest a complex relationship between feature selection and data quality. Notice how the difference between the DIPPR and SPEED database ΔMAPE results for Joback is very large (2.29), while the next closest is 1.26 (MPNN). Simpler, rule-based descriptors such as Joback groups rely on a limited set of additive contributions to represent the molecule. Thus, when errors are encountered, relationships between groups and NBP resulting from “good” data are compromised in an attempt to accommodate the “bad” data. In contrast, richer featurizations such as RDKit descriptors or graph-based embeddings (GCN and MPNN) distribute chemical information across many correlated features or directly encode molecular topology. In other words, the amount of information used to identify one molecule from another is greater. This provides some robustness against random noise. Unfortunately, this very flexibility also makes them vulnerable to overfitting and systematic errors. Notice how graph-based models trained on SPEED exhibit larger ΔMAPE values than their DIPPR-trained counterparts, indicating overfitting of spurious patterns present in the noisy data. Overall, errors or inconsistencies in the SPEED data set disproportionately degrade the accuracy of less complex or smaller featurization schemes, but they also reduce transferability in complex and feature-rich approaches. It is a subtle relationship: more sophisticated featurizations can extract greater value from curated data sets, but they are more easily misled when data quality is poor.

Analysis of Hypothesis

The hypothesis of this work was that training and validating an ML model on a smaller, rigorously curated data set yields more accurate predictions than using a larger collection of values of unknown origin or reliability. The results presented support this contention despite the prevailing assumption in ML that larger data sets, even if flawed, lead to improved model performance because the amount of data will overcome deficiencies in their accuracy. We present two pieces of evidence to support this claim.

Starting with the validation results in Tables and , the MAPEs for the DIPPR models were lower than those prepared from the SPEED database in all cases except the Joback featurization scheme. Comparing the last line in these tables, the DIPPR/SPEED MAPE validation values are 2.85 vs 4.54, 6.38 vs 5.71, 4.02 vs 4.12, and 3.69 vs 3.92, where each of the differences is deemed significant based on the uncertainties (see eq ). As mentioned, the Joback featurization scheme is the exception to the trend, but this approach performed significantly worse overall than the other models. In the three best models, we see that the DIPPR MAPEs are superior.

The most rigorous test of the hypothesis is how the models perform on the test set dataNBP values for compounds never used in the training or validation of the model. These results are quantified in the MAPEs of Figures and and the results in Figure . For the former, in each case shown in Figures and , the DIPPR model performance on the test set is superior to the corresponding SPEED result. Specifically, the DIPPR vs SPEED MAPEs on the test sets are 3.4% vs 4.5%, 5.2% vs 7.1%, 3.3% vs 4.7%, and 3.2% vs 4.1% for the RDKit, Joback, GCN, and MPNN models. Also, in each case in Figure , the magnitude of the SPEED ΔMAPE is larger, and in most cases much larger, than the corresponding DIPPR value.

An important question to address is why models trained on the SPEED data set consistently underperform despite having access to nearly four times as many compounds. One explanation is that incorrect or inconsistent entries in the SPEED set act as “negative leverage points.” Because ML algorithms minimize error across all data, even a relatively small number of systematically erroneous values can pull the regression surface away from the true physical relationship, degrading the overall model accuracy. A second mechanism is that the higher noise level in the SPEED data set obscures subtle yet physically meaningful trends, such as steric contributions, intramolecular interactions, or functional-group-specific effects, that remain detectable in the rigorously evaluated DIPPR data set. Thus, the DIPPR-trained models benefit not only from reduced bias but also from clearer signal-to-noise ratios, enabling them to capture thermodynamically consistent patterns more effectively.

Considering the consistent advantage observed for models created using the smaller but curated DIPPR database over the larger SPEED database of unknown origin, it is reasonable to deem the hypothesis of this work to be valid. This outcome emphasizes the pivotal role of data quality over quantity and suggests that investing in data validation will yield better models than simply increasing the number of data points available to train the model.

Model Accessibility

In addition to testing the core hypothesis, this work also aimed to develop a machine learning model that is accessible to users without expertise in ML methods. To that end, a user-friendly web application was created for the two best-performing modelsGCN and MPNN. The interface requires only a SMILES string (or a file containing one SMILES string per line) as the input. The application is available at https://github.com/thomasknotts/GNN-NBP.

Conclusion

The purpose of this work was to test whether a large database of property values of unknown origin and accuracy produces better ML models than a smaller database of high-quality data. These findings carry implications not only for model development but also for data set construction and curation strategies in resource-constrained domains such as chemistry and materials science. Ultimately, this work contributes to the growing body of evidence that smarter data, not just more data, are essential for building robust and trustworthy ML models. The results indicate that the latter produces models that are better able to predict the NBP for compounds not used in training (the true prediction case). This was true regardless of the featurization scheme used to describe the molecules or the ML algorithm employed. These results suggest that more emphasis should be placed on refining the data quality rather than solely improving model architectures. Although this study contrasts data sets at two extremeshighly curated vs largely uncuratedpractitioners often encounter mixtures of both. A natural question is where the balance lies between data quality and data quantity. We hypothesize that adding moderate amounts of noisy data to a curated core could initially improve performance by broadening chemical diversity, but beyond a certain threshold, the accumulation of systematic errors would outweigh the benefits, degrading generalization. Determining this crossover point is a critical direction for future work in the field and will require controlled experiments in which curated data are incrementally mixed with noisy values. Until such studies can be done, our results strongly suggest that curation should remain the first priority when building ML-ready data sets for thermophysical property prediction.
