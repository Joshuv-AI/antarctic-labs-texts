An oxygen-permeable, stirrer-pump-integrated liver–heart microphysiological system enables time-resolved analysis in PK–PD relationships of metabolism-dependent cardiotoxicity
Authors: Shinichiro Horiuchi, Kiyoshi Oka, Kenta Shinha, Riko Jinno, Miku Kochi, Nanae Koda, Yusuke Masuo, Hiroshi Kimura, Yukio Kato, Daiju Yamazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Tubeless liver-heart MPS enables dynamic analysis of PK-PD relationships.•Bottom-up oxygenation sustains robust primary human hepatocyte function.•Hepatic metabolism progressively attenuates terfenadine cardiotoxicity.•Metabolite formation tightly correlates with cardiac functional recovery.•Practical platform bridges in vitro findings to clinical risk prediction.

Introduction

Animal experiments often have limitations in predicting drug toxicity because of differences in species, particularly in the expression and catalytic activities of hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes (Martignoni et al., 2006). Owing to this inherent limitation in preclinical animal testing, several approved drugs have later been withdrawn from the market for causing severe, unexpected, human-specific cardiotoxicity associated with altered hepatic metabolism and drug–drug interactions (Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences, 2005). These failures underscore the critical need for alternative human-relevant methods to predict metabolism-dependent toxicity. Consequently, recent regulatory changes—including the FDA’s, 2026 draft guidance on validating new approach methodologies (NAMs) as alternatives to animal testing—have accelerated the transition toward human-relevant approaches (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2026). Therefore, the development of reliable in vitro platforms using human-derived cells is essential to improve the predictability of drug safety (Ando et al., 2017; Yamazaki et al., 2018).

Despite the increasing use of human-derived cells, conventional cardiomyocyte-only assay systems inherently lack metabolic capacity and therefore frequently fail to detect metabolism-dependent cardiotoxicity. The absence of robust hepatic metabolic activity in standalone cardiomyocyte assays has been increasingly recognized as a critical bottleneck in predictive toxicology frameworks (Yamazaki, 2023; Zushin et al., 2023). A representative example is terfenadine, which blocks the human ether-à-go-go-related gene (hERG) channel and induces potentially fatal arrhythmias in its parent form (Roy et al., 1996; Roden, 2004). Conventional regulatory reliance on hERG inhibition assays has often led to false-positive predictions of proarrhythmic risk, prompting initiatives such as the comprehensive in vitro Proarrhythmia Assay (CiPA) to improve testing paradigms (Gintant et al., 2016; Blinova et al., 2018). Although these initiatives successfully integrate multichannel pharmacology and in silico modeling, their core in vitro assays primarily rely on isolated ion channels or cardiomyocyte-only models that lack robust hepatic metabolic capacity. Consequently, several implementations of these frameworks do not explicitly incorporate dynamic metabolic clearance. Terfenadine is extensively metabolized in the liver by CYP3A4, and its metabolites are further converted to fexofenadine (Yun et al., 1993; Ling et al., 1995), which exhibits markedly reduced hERG channel inhibition (Roy et al., 1996). However, cardiomyocyte-only assays tend to overestimate terfenadine toxicity because of the absence of hepatic clearance (Blinova et al., 2018; McAleer et al., 2019). These findings underscore the need for integrated multiorgan systems that recapitulate both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics to improve predictive modeling (McAleer et al., 2019; Marx et al., 2020). Furthermore, three-dimensional (3D) human iPS cell-derived engineered heart tissues (hiPSC-EHTs) offer substantial advantages over conventional 2D monocultures by promoting structural and functional maturation toward adult-like phenotypes (Feric and Radisic, 2016; Mannhardt et al., 2016; Ronaldson-Bouchard et al., 2018).

Existing heart–liver microphysiological systems (MPS) designed to simultaneously evaluate parent drugs and metabolites have several technical and practical challenges (Ferrari and Rasponi, 2021). Many existing multi-organ platforms face issues such as failing to maintain sufficient metabolic activity in hepatocytes due to oxygen supply limitations or relying on complex perfusion systems (McAleer et al., 2019; Lee-Montiel et al., 2021; Low et al., 2021; Ingber, 2022). At present, few systems exist that can simultaneously achieve adequate hepatic metabolic activity, time-resolved functional readouts, and operational simplicity suitable for routine pharmacological screening. In particular, insufficient oxygenation leads to a rapid decline in CYP450 activity in primary hepatocytes, thereby limiting their applicability in routine pharmacological testing (Nishikawa et al., 2022; Takemura et al., 2023).

To address these challenges, we constructed an assay system using the BioStellar™ Plate (Shinha et al., 2021), which is a microfluidic device that integrates a stirrer to eliminate external tubing (Fig. 1). To sustain robust hepatic metabolism, we incorporated an oxygen-permeable InnoCell™ (Takemura et al., 2023). Our previous study confirmed that this oxygen-permeable membrane significantly enhances hepatic CYP activities in the optimized culture medium for liver-heart co-culture (Horiuchi et al., 2024). Beyond maintaining cell viability, our primary objective was to apply this oxygen-optimized, tubeless platform to resolve the complex pharmacological dynamics of metabolism-dependent cardiotoxicity. In this study, we demonstrate that an integrated co-culture of hiPSC-EHTs and cryopreserved primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) successfully captured the time-resolved pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic (PK–PD) relationships of terfenadine. This approach enabled the dynamic evaluation of a reversible pharmacological mechanism—specifically, the metabolism-driven restoration of contractile function—which gets inherently missed in conventional standalone assays. Materials and methods

Commercially available cell lines, primary cells, and human-derived cells were used in this study, and all procedures followed the ethics guidelines of the National Institute of Health Sciences.

Culture medium

The culture media used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Hepatocyte Maintenance Medium, comprising William's E Medium supplemented with the CM4000-Cell Maintenance Supplement Pack (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), but without dexamethasone (HM Dex(-) medium), was used for the co-culture of PHHs and hiPSC-EHTs. This medium formulation was previously optimized to maintain the functionality of both cell types in coculture (Horiuchi et al., 2024).

Fabrication of human iPS cell-derived engineered heart tissue

hiPSC-EHTs were fabricated using iCell Cardiomyocyte 2 (FUJIFILM Cellular Dynamics, Inc., Madison, WI, USA) and HS27a cells, a bone marrow stromal cell line (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) (Roecklein and Torok-Storb, 1995). HS27a cells were cultured in gelatin-coated 100-mm culture dishes in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva, Tokyo, Japan), GlutaMAX (Gibco), MEM non-essential amino acids (Gibco), and a penicillin-streptomycin solution (Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan). Cells were passaged once per week.

Agarose molds were prepared by adding 2 mL of 2% agarose in Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to each well of a 24-well plate and inserting spacers (C0002; DiNABIOS, Zurich, Switzerland). Once the agarose solidified, the spacers were removed and PDMS racks (C0001, DiNABIOS) were placed into each mold (Hansen et al., 2010; Stoehr et al., 2014). HS27a cells were harvested from 100-mm culture dishes via trypsinization. The iCell cardiomyocyte 2 (FUJIFILM Cellular Dynamics, Inc., Madison, WI, USA) was thawed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HS27a cells and iCell Cardiomyocytes 2 were mixed at a 1:10 ratio and centrifuged ( 200 × g, 5 min, 23 °C). The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in iCell cardiomyocyte maintenance medium (FUJIFILM Cellular Dynamics, Inc.) containing a final concentration of 5 mg/mL fibrinogen (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 3 U/mL thrombin (Sigma-Aldrich). Each 100 µL of this suspension contained 5.0 × 104 HS27a cells and 5.0 × 105 iCell Cardiomyocytes 2. The suspension was poured into agarose molds and the constructs were incubated for 90 min at 37 °C under 5% CO2 to allow fibrinogen polymerization. The resulting hiPSC-EHTs were transferred to 24-well plates containing 2 mL iCell Cardiomyocyte Maintenance Medium per well. hiPSC-EHTs were cultured in iCell cardiomyocyte maintenance medium for three weeks to allow maturation (Mannhardt et al., 2016; Karbassi et al., 2020), followed by cultivation in EHT medium for over one week (Leonard et al., 2018).

MPS for co-culture

The BioStellar™ Plate (Sumitomo Bakelite, Tokyo, Japan) features a modified polystyrene (PS) bottom equipped with a highly oxygen-permeable InnoCell™ (Mitsui Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) designed to enhance hepatocyte function by optimizing the oxygen supply. A dedicated stirrer base (Microfluidic Systems Laboratory, Tokyo, Japan) was used to drive the integrated stirrer using a BioStellar™ Plate. This base consisted of an MFS-BSDOCK containing a driving magnet and an MFS-BSCNT unit to control the rotation speed.

Cryopreserved primary human hepatocyte culture

The BioStellar™ Plate was coated with Corning® Collagen I rat tail solution (50 µg/mL; Corning, Corning, NY, USA) for 5 h and subsequently washed twice with PBS. Individual cryopreserved primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) (Lot #3-51) were obtained from XenoTech (Lenexa, KS, USA). Cells were thawed using OptiThaw (XenoTech) and resuspended at 7.2 × 105 cells/mL using OptiPlate (XenoTech). Subsequently, 500 µL of the cell suspension was seeded into the BioStellar™ Plate. After 5 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with 500 µL of HM medium per well. The medium was exchanged again with 500 µL of fresh HM medium on the following day (Day 1).

Cardiotoxicity evaluation of cumulative terfenadine administration in human iPS cell-derived engineered heart tissue monoculture

To evaluate the cardiotoxicity of terfenadine in a monoculture, hiPSC-EHTs were pre-cultured in 2 mL of HM Dex(-) medium in a 24-well plate for 3 h. Following pre-culture, terfenadine was administered cumulatively to achieve final concentrations ranging from 0.01 µM to 10 µM. A 10-second contraction video was recorded after a 15-minute exposure at each concentration.

Metabolism-dependent cardiotoxicity evaluation of terfenadine

A schematic overview of the experimental workflow for the cardiotoxicity evaluation of terfenadine in the co-culture is shown in Fig. 2. Tissue contraction was subsequently recorded via video imaging for 10 s at 4, 24, and 48 h post exposure. Evaluation of contractile properties in human iPS cell-derived engineered heart tissue

The contractile motion of hiPSC-EHTs was recorded for 10 s in a plate incubator TP10- LSRMPS (LCI, Seoul, South Korea) using a CKX41 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a high-speed HAS-U2 camera (Detect, Tokyo, Japan) at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, a frame rate of 60 fps, and a shutter speed of 1/700 s. The recorded videos were analyzed using the SI8000 software (Lpixel Inc., Tokyo, Japan) to extract functional parameters, including contractile amplitude (delta object length), contraction velocity, and relaxation velocity.

RNA isolation and real-time polymerase chain reaction

Cultured cells and tissues were washed twice with PBS. Prior to RNA isolation, hiPSC-EHTs were treated with proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at a final concentration of 0.22 mg/mL for 10 min at 55 °C. Total RNA was then extracted using the RNeasy® Total RNA Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels in PHHs and hiPSC-EHTs were quantified using a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). CYP3A4 expression was evaluated using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with primer and probe sets (CYP3A4: Hs00430021_m1). CYP2J2 expression was evaluated using the THUNDERBIRD® Next SYBR® qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) with the primers Synthesized by Life Technology Japan (Tokyo, Japan) (F: TGCTGTCATCCATGAGGTGCAG, R: CGCCGTCAAATTGGTCAGGATC). Pooled human liver RNA (BioChain Institute, Inc., Newark, CA, USA) served as a universal standard to generate calibration curves for both cell types, enabling a direct quantitative comparison of expression levels between hepatic and cardiac tissues. Relative expression levels were calculated based on these standard curve equations, with the expression level of the standard set to one.

Evaluation of terfenadine adsorption to PDMS racks

Terfenadine adsorption onto the PDMS racks used for hiPSC-EHT culture was evaluated under cell-free conditions. HM Dex(-) medium containing 10 µM terfenadine (corresponding to the maximum concentration evaluated in the cumulative administration assay) was prepared, and 2 mL of this medium was dispensed into each well of a 24-well plate. The PDMS racks were placed in these wells and incubated for 48 h. The control wells contained identical medium but lacked PDMS racks. Aliquots (50 µL) of the medium were collected at 0, 4, 24, and 48 h for subsequent liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis.

LC–MS/MS analysis

Methanol containing verapamil as an internal standard was added to each sample, followed by vigorous vortex mixing and centrifugation twice at 21,500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were analyzed using LC–MS/MS consisting of a Nexera X2 coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (LCMS-8050; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a COSMOSIL 3C18-MS-III S-3 column (2.0 × 50 mm, 3.0 µm; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) maintained at 50 °C with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient elution program was as follows: 95% A from 0 to 0.3 min, 95%–50% A from 0.3 to 3.3 min, 50%–2% A from 3.3 to 3.5 min, 2% A from 3.5 to 4.5 min, 2%–95% A from 4.5 to 4.6 min, and 95% A from 4.6 to 5.5 min. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in the positive ion mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The MRM transitions for terfenadine and fexofenadine were m/z 472.2 > 436.3 and 502.3 > 466.3, respectively. Data analysis was conducted using LabSolutions software (Shimadzu), and quantification was performed based on peak height ratios relative to the internal standard. The method was linear over the range of 0.3–30 fmol/injection for both terfenadine and fexofenadine.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as the average ± standard error (SE). Independent biological replicates (n = 3 or 4, each representing a distinct tissue construct) were used for all experimental groups. All statistical analyses were performed using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, followed by an F-test to assess the equality of variances. One-way analysis of variance was performed for multiple comparisons among three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test to determine statistical significance. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Determination of terfenadine exposure concentration

A cumulative terfenadine administration test in hiPSC-EHT monocultures determined the optimal exposure concentrations for metabolism-dependent cardiotoxicity evaluation. The contractile amplitude of hiPSC-EHTs decreased rapidly and dose-dependently between 0.3 and 3 µM (Fig. 3). Relative to pre-exposure baseline levels, the contractile amplitude declined to 40% at 1 µM and 13% at 3 µM. Furthermore, terfenadine concentrations of 1 µM and higher induced distinct arrhythmic beating patterns. We hypothesized that metabolism-induced functional recovery would become most apparent under conditions that cause a precipitous decline in contractility and the onset of arrhythmia. Time-dependent functional recovery in liver–heart co-cultures

Representative contractile waveforms and motion for all experimental groups are shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Videos S1–S4. Functional monitoring at 4 h post-exposure revealed that all experimental groups, except for co-cultures exposed to 1 µM terfenadine, fell into a severe fibrillation-like state (Fig. 4, Supplementary Videos S1, S3 and S4). In contrast, the 1 µM terfenadine co-cultures maintained feeble contractions but clearly exhibited an arrhythmic pattern (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Video S2). Co-cultures at this concentration also exhibited severely reduced contractility, dropping to approximately 6% of the pre-exposure baseline (Fig. 5A). Contractile function at 24 h under the 1 µM exposure remained at approximately 8% of the baseline in hiPSC-EHT monocultures, whereas co-cultures demonstrated a substantial recovery to approximately 70% (Fig. 4, Fig. 5A). Conversely, both monocultures and co-cultures exposed to 3 µM terfenadine maintained a fibrillation-like state at this 24-hour time point (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Videos S3 and S4). Functional evaluation at 48 h for the 1 µM exposure group showed only 30% recovery in hiPSC-EHT monocultures, whereas co-cultures fully recovered to pre-exposure baseline levels (Fig. 4, Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, hiPSC-EHT monocultures exposed to 3 µM terfenadine remained in a fibrillation-like state at 48 h (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Video S3). Co-cultures under the same 3 µM exposure achieved a partial functional recovery to 30% of baseline, although the tissues exhibited irregular beating periods and contractile amplitudes (Figs. 4B and 5A, and Supplementary Video S4). Pharmacokinetic profile of terfenadine and fexofenadine

LC–MS/MS analysis demonstrated a time-dependent decrease in terfenadine concentration across all experimental conditions, irrespective of the initial dose (Fig. 5B). Terfenadine levels in co-cultures exposed to 1 µM terfenadine fell below the detection limit by 24 h. At this concentration, the corresponding PHH monocultures reached undetectable levels after 48 h. The 3 µM exposure condition exhibited a progressive reduction in terfenadine concentration, with the most rapid decline observed in co-cultures, followed by PHH monocultures, and finally hiPSC-EHT monocultures. Conversely, fexofenadine formation was prominent exclusively in PHH-containing conditions, increasing time-dependent manner (Fig. 5C). Co-cultures displayed a trend toward higher fexofenadine production than the PHH monocultures, although the difference was not statistically significant. hiPSC-EHT monocultures also exhibited a time-dependent increase in fexofenadine levels; however, these levels remained negligible compared to those generated in the presence of PHHs.

Gene expression of metabolic enzymes

Gene expression analysis confirmed the presence of CYP3A4, the primary metabolic enzyme for terfenadine (Yun et al., 1993), in PHHs at high levels comparable to the pooled human liver RNA standard (relative expression ≈ 1) (Fig. 6). In contrast, CYP3A4 expression in hiPSC-EHTs was below the detection limit. Co-cultured PHHs showed a trend toward higher CYP3A4 expression than monocultures, although this was not statistically significant. The expression of CYP2J2, a cardiac-expressed enzyme also implicated in terfenadine metabolism (Evangelista et al., 2013), was confirmed in both PHHs and hiPSC-EHTs. Evaluation of terfenadine adsorption to PDMS material

Cell-free evaluation of terfenadine adsorption revealed a substantial decrease in drug concentration in the presence of PDMS racks compared to the rack-free control conditions (Fig. 7). Terfenadine concentrations in the PDMS rack condition declined to 51% of the rack-free control after 24 h and further dropped to 31% after 48 h. Furthermore, the introduction of the PDMS racks resulted in a statistically significant reduction in terfenadine levels at both 24 and 48 h relative to the initial baseline concentration. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that integration of hiPSC-EHTs with PHHs within a pump-integrated MPS device enables attenuation of terfenadine-induced contractile dysfunction via metabolic clearance (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). This platform combines tubeless circulation, oxygen-sustained hepatocyte function, and time-resolved PK–PD coupling, enabling an evaluation approach that is otherwise difficult to achieve with conventional cardiomyocyte-only assays or static co-culture systems. LC–MS/MS analysis confirmed a decrease in terfenadine concentration, accompanied by a simultaneous increase in fexofenadine concentrations (Fig. 5). Notably, substantial depletion of terfenadine with marked fexofenadine formation was observed in the co-culture system, whereas fexofenadine production was nearly undetectable in the hiPSC-EHT monocultures.

As this study is positioned as a proof-of-concept, supratherapeutic concentrations (1–3 µM) were intentionally selected to generate a sufficiently robust pharmacodynamic signal in which metabolism-mediated functional recovery could be clearly detected and distinguished from background variability (Lalonde et al., 1996). This approach is consistent with standard practice in proof-of-concept MPS studies, where establishing clear mechanistic signals takes precedence over strict clinical concentration matching. Furthermore, the substantial PDMS adsorption and inherent dilution effects within the system reduce the actual exposure concentrations, bringing the effective free drug levels closer to the pharmacological range. Establishing this clear proof-of-concept signal is a necessary prerequisite. Future studies utilizing optimized low-adsorption platforms will then enable subsequent evaluations at clinically relevant concentrations. At supratherapeutic concentrations, terfenadine acts as a potent multichannel blocker that inhibits L-type calcium channels in addition to the hERG channel (Mirams et al., 2011). As hiPSC-CM excitation–contraction coupling relies heavily on calcium influx through L-type calcium channels (Karbassi et al., 2020), the marked contractile reduction in hiPSC-EHT monocultures is likely primarily mediated by L-type calcium channel blockade. In contrast, fexofenadine exhibits markedly reduced multichannel blockade (Roy et al., 1996; Katchman et al., 2006) and lacks L-type calcium channel inhibition. Therefore, the progressive restoration of contractility observed in the co-culture system—recovering fully at 1 µM and partially at 3 µM (Fig. 4, Fig. 5)—likely reflects a reversible mechanism: release from this transient multi-channel inhibition, particularly the L-type calcium channel blockade, driven by hepatic metabolic clearance. This finding is consistent with a previous report demonstrating that terfenadine-induced cardiac arrest at 1 µM is reversible in hiPSC-cardiomyocytes (Guo et al., 2011). The partial recovery at 3 µM likely reflects the longer time required to metabolically clear the higher initial substrate load. Furthermore, the arrhythmias observed during the contractile recovery process at 3 µM are likely attributable to the unmasking of hERG inhibition following the partial release from L-type calcium channel blockade, consistent with the biphasic phenomenon reported by Guo et al. (2011). Contractile motion analysis provides a functional readout that integrates both mechanical and electrophysiological responses, as the arrhythmic beating patterns observed in this study reflect the underlying ion channel disturbances. Future studies incorporating field potential duration measurements or calcium transient analysis will provide complementary electrophysiological endpoints.

Interestingly, functional monitoring revealed a distinct temporal delay between rapid terfenadine clearance and full recovery of contractile function. Specifically, under the 1 µM terfenadine co-culture condition, the concentration of the parent compound decreased below the detection limit by 24 h, yet contractile function had not fully normalized. Terfenadine is known to dissociate slowly from histamine H1 receptors (Simons, 2004). Similarly, slow dissociation kinetics at the hERG channel result from drug “trapping” within the channel cavity, leading to an exceptionally slow washout and prolonged blockade even after extracellular free drug concentrations decline (Mitcheson et al., 2000; Kamiya et al., 2008). This delayed functional recovery is highly consistent with slow collective offset of the multichannel blockade. These findings highlight the utility of our integrated platform in capturing complex, non-equilibrium PK–PD relationships that may reflect the underlying drug–target binding kinetics. In this study, PK–PD refers to the temporal association between drug/metabolite concentrations and functional cardiac responses, rather than fully quantitative PK–PD modeling. The synchronized temporal acquisition of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data provides a foundation for future compartmental modeling.

As a proof-of-concept, this platform enables simultaneous evaluation of drug metabolism (PK) and functional cardiac response (PD). The temporal decrease in terfenadine concentration closely paralleled the recovery of contractile function, supporting a temporal association consistent with the PK–PD relationship (Fig. 5). The temporal association between fexofenadine formation and the recovery of contractile function establishes a mechanistically plausible PK–PD correlation. Such an integrated analysis is difficult to achieve using conventional single-organ or endpoint-based assays. The tight temporal alignment between metabolite formation and functional recovery, together with the absence of these effects under monoculture conditions, strongly supports hepatic metabolism as the primary contributing driver (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). However, direct mechanistic proof requires functional inhibition studies. In functionally coupled liver-heart systems, utilizing standard CYP3A4 inhibitors like ketoconazole or itraconazole presents a challenge due to their known direct off-target effects on cardiomyocyte electrophysiology and contractility. For example, ketoconazole acts as a multi-ion channel blocker that inhibits L-type Ca2+ channels (Sung et al., 2012), and itraconazole exhibits strong negative inotropic effects that directly decrease myocardial contractility (Qu et al., 2013; Cleary et al., 2013). Therefore, future investigations must establish protocols using alternative, non-cardiotoxic CYP3A4 inhibitors to demonstrate this causal relationship without confounding the functional readouts. The current dataset provides a foundational basis for future quantitative compartmental modeling, which will require the complete elimination of PDMS adsorption and the optimization of the liquid-to-cell ratio.

Gene expression analysis further supported this localized metabolic conversion. CYP3A4 expression was restricted to PHHs, whereas CYP2J2 was detected in both the cell types (Fig. 6). However, negligible metabolite formation in hiPSC-EHT monocultures suggests that cardiac CYP2J2 alone is insufficient for effective terfenadine clearance, although its minor contribution cannot be completely excluded. Previous studies have demonstrated that while CYP2J2 can metabolize terfenadine (Evangelista et al., 2013), its contribution to the overall clearance is minor compared to that of CYP3A4, which serves as the predominant metabolic pathway (Ling et al., 1995). Consequently, the present findings suggest that CYP3A4 acts as the primary metabolic enzyme for terfenadine in this system. As previously demonstrated, bottom-up oxygen supply via an oxygen-permeable membrane prevents hypoxia and sustains robust hepatic CYP3A4 activity (Horiuchi et al., 2024). This optimized oxygenation presumably enhanced the CYP3A4-mediated metabolism of terfenadine, thereby contributing to the functional attenuation of cardiotoxicity observed in the co-culture system. These results were consistent with the established metabolic pathways of terfenadine, confirming that hepatic metabolism was the dominant detoxification pathway (Yun et al., 1993). Subsequent evaluations employing direct measurements of CYP3A4 enzymatic activity using standard substrates will further confirm the correlation between gene expression and functional metabolic capacity within this system.

Conventional conditioned medium (CM) transfer methods inherently suffer from temporal disconnects and limit their ability to capture bidirectional organ-organ interactions (Esch et al., 2015; Marx et al., 2016). Our co-culture platform addresses these fundamental limitations and provides insights into potential inter-organ crosstalk. We confirmed a significant upregulation of CYP2J2 expression in hiPSC-EHTs during co-culture (Fig. 6). Concurrently, in the hepatic compartment, we observed a trend toward enhanced CYP3A4 expression in co-cultured PHHs compared to that in monocultures, which was logically accompanied by a corresponding trend toward increased fexofenadine production (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The trend of increased CYP3A4 expression in the co-culture condition represents a preliminary observation that requires future statistical validation. Independent biological replicates of n = 3 or 4 were utilized due to the throughput constraints inherent to complex microphysiological systems; however, increasing the sample size in future iterations will be necessary to confirm these potential inter-organ crosstalk mechanisms. Finally, although CYP2J2 plays a protective role in cardiovascular physiology (Aliwarga et al., 2018), its precise functional contribution to terfenadine clearance in this specific coculture system remains unclear.

Previous studies have demonstrated the immense potential of multi-organ microphysiological systems to define temporal PK–PD relationships (McAleer et al., 2019; Ferrari and Rasponi, 2021; Lee-Montiel et al., 2021). However, their sophisticated platforms inherently rely on external pumping mechanisms and complex microfluidic circuits, which pose significant challenges for routine high-throughput implementation. In addition to their biological relevance, a major bottleneck in the routine industrial adoption of conventional perfusion-based MPS is their operational complexity. Many existing multiorgan platforms require external pneumatic pumps, complex fluidic tubing, and intricate bubble-trapping systems (Low et al., 2021; Ingber, 2022). Such external instrumentation not only reduces experimental throughput but also increases the risk of contamination, fluid leakage, bubble entrapment, and overall system failure. The present platform addresses these critical engineering challenges by utilizing a BioStellar™ Plate that integrates microfluidic channels and a magnetic stirrer directly into the plate architecture. This stirrer-pump-integrated design eliminates the need for external fluidic circuits while maintaining continuous fluid circulation between the interconnected tissue compartments. Combined with enhanced bottom-up oxygenation, this straightforward architecture successfully translated sustained hepatic metabolic capacity into dynamic PK–PD relationships. By achieving this level of operational simplicity without compromising the microphysiological functionality, this system provides a highly practical and scalable framework for routine preclinical safety evaluation.

Despite these advantages, this study had several limitations. Significant nonspecific adsorption of hydrophobic compounds onto the PDMS components was observed, which is consistent with previous reports (Shirure and George, 2017; McAleer et al., 2019). Whereas the BioStellar™ Plate and its oxygen-permeable membrane consisted of low-adsorption materials, the supporting racks for the 3D hiPSC-EHTs were made of PDMS. As PDMS adsorption is driven primarily by the physicochemical properties of terfenadine and the material itself, this nonspecific passive adsorption is expected to act as a constant background factor in all plate configurations utilizing PDMS components, including cell-free controls and hiPSC-EHT monocultures. Critically, functional recovery was observed exclusively under co-culture conditions, and the sole variable differentiating the recovering tissues from the non-recovering monocultures was the presence of metabolically active PHHs.

A comparison of the three experimental configurations, particularly the rack-free PHH monoculture, enabled a clear distinction between active metabolic clearance and passive material loss. Importantly, the pharmacokinetic profiles clearly demonstrate that hepatic metabolism is the predominant driver of terfenadine clearance. Specifically, terfenadine concentrations declined substantially and more rapidly in PHH monocultures, which completely lacked highly adsorptive PDMS racks, compared to hiPSC-EHT monocultures containing racks (Fig. 5B). hiPSC-EHT monocultures, which completely lacked CYP3A4 expression (Fig. 6), generated negligible amounts of fexofenadine. In contrast, PHH-containing conditions expressing robust CYP3A4 resulted in a marked time-dependent increase in fexofenadine formation (Fig. 5C). These findings strongly indicate that CYP3A4-mediated hepatic metabolism significantly drives terfenadine depletion in the co-culture system. This robust chronological and spatial divergence provides compelling evidence that active hepatic metabolism, rather than simple passive adsorption, is the primary driver of the observed functional detoxification.

Based on the cell-free adsorption data, the actual free drug concentration to which hiPSC-EHTs were exposed is estimated to be approximately 30–50% of the nominal concentration. We acknowledge that this substantial passive adsorption complicates the absolute quantitative interpretation of the pharmacokinetic profile. However, because both the monoculture and co-culture systems utilized identical device configurations, the background PDMS adsorption kinetics are equivalent. Consequently, the distinct functional recovery and altered pharmacokinetic profiles observed exclusively in the co-culture condition can be attributed to the metabolic activity of the PHHs, independent of the baseline passive adsorption. Although fully quantitative PK-PD modeling requires further optimization of the liquid-to-cell ratio, our empirical findings establish a vital foundational dataset for future in silico integration.

Achieving fully quantitative in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) requires overcoming several inherent limitations of the current system. Precise quantitative evaluation necessitates the complete substitution of PDMS with low-adsorption materials to minimize nonspecific binding. Recent findings by Fujiwara et al. (2025) demonstrated that the transition from conventional silicone to low-adsorption cardiac devices increased the apparent cardiotoxicity of doxorubicin. Furthermore, the relatively large medium volume currently required for operation results in a non-physiological liquid-to-cell (LoC) ratio, which inherently dilutes metabolites and potentially limits the sensitivity of detecting reactive intermediates. Future optimizations must rigorously address this LoC ratio (scaling) along with the fine-tuning of continuous mixing kinetics. These progressive optimizations are essential for advancing this semiquantitative evaluation platform toward a fully quantitative, mathematically modeled IVIVE framework that can potentially be integrated with in silico physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling. Several translational milestones must be met to achieve routine industrial adoption. These include the simplification of operational protocols, establishment of standard operating procedures (SOPs), and rigorous interlaboratory validation to ensure reproducibility. While terfenadine represents a well-characterized model compound for this purpose, future studies must evaluate additional metabolism-dependent compounds, including bioactivation-based toxicants and prodrugs, to fully establish the platform's broader utility. Regarding the context of use (CoU), this system is not intended for primary high-throughput screening but rather serves as a tier-2 mechanistic evaluation platform. While replacing animal models entirely requires further in vivo correlation, the current system acts as a crucial complementary tool. It effectively bridges the translational gap by clarifying discrepancies between conventional static in vitro results and anticipated human drug responses. Ultimately, this system provides a foundational framework for evaluating dynamic shifts in dose-response relationships mediated by human-specific metabolism, thereby overcoming the inter-species differences inherent in animal models.

We plan to conduct further investigations to address these specific validation steps. In this study, the successful capture of metabolism-dependent functional recovery highlights the practical utility of this oxygen-optimized stirrer-pump-integrated platform. By sustaining the necessary hepatic metabolic capacity without relying on complex external perfusion, this accessible system provides a tangible step toward bridging in vitro findings and clinical risk prediction in human-relevant safety pharmacology. Consequently, this approach directly contributes to the ongoing regulatory paradigm shift toward the integration of new approach methodologies (NAMs), as emphasized in recent FDA guidelines for drug development.
