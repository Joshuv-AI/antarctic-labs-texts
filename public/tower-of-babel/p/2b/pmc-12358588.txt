Nanoparticle-driven growth enhances mechanical properties of flax stems
Authors: Wencai Li, Xhulja Biraku, Erik Nielsen, Alan Taub, Mihaela Banu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While nanoparticle uptake by plants has primarily been studied for environmental remediation, its purposeful use to enhance plant structural and mechanical properties remains unexplored. We hypothesized that cellulose nanofibers (CNFs), introduced into the growth medium, could be absorbed by flax stems, reinforcing their cell walls formation and, as a consequence, improve the mechanical performance. Thus, in this paper it is proved that flax plants treated with a 0.2% w/v CNF solution after root excision showed increased stem diameter, reduced pith size, and significantly accelerated root regeneration (~ 7 cm in 20 days) compared to controls treated with autoclaved deionized water. Analyses of the CNF-treated stems showed an increase in the mechanical performance of the stems revealing up to 50% increase in energy to fracture, 22% increase in Young’s modulus, and approximate 33% improvement in stiffness at a reduced density compared to the non-treated stems. To explain these effects, morphology analysis of stems was conducted. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) revealed enhanced hydrogen bonding, cellulose crystallinity, and thermal stability. This study developed a novel in vivo approach by incorporating CNFs into plants during early growth to trigger structural reinforcement, which in turn improves mechanical performance.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is an advanced emerging field of science that deals with alternations of matter at a specific size of 1–100 nm, called nanoparticles, which are increasingly used in energy, environmental engineering, biomedicine, and consumer products due to their unique properties such as high surface area to volume ratio, tiny size, stability, ease of surface alteration, biocompatibility, porosity characteristics, and dispersibility1–3. In recent years, their use has extended to plant science. For example, ferrous nanoparticles have been applied to deplete heavy metals during soil remediation4 and have been shown to be effective, eco-friendly fertilizers, functioning to synchronize plant nutrient uptake while limiting groundwater contamination by supplying nutrients in an immobilized form that remains available for the growing plants5. Nanopesticides have also significantly increased pesticidal efficacy and decreased toxicity, contributing towards sustainable agriculture and global food security3. In plant genetic engineering, nanoparticles have been used to deliver small-interfering RNA to plant leaves like vehicles6 and addressed some of the most critical challenges of CRISPR genome editing in plants7. The uptake, translocation, and accumulation of engineered nanoparticles in plants have been intensively studied in the past decade8. Previous research has provided a comprehensive knowledge of nanoparticle uptake via foliar spray, branch injection, root exposure, and seed priming9. Others have focused on nanoparticles to suppress pathogen infection (fungal, viral, bacterial) through enhanced nutrition. For example, silicon nanoparticles work as an ideal candidate for developing sustainable agriculture since they are biocompatible, biodegradable, and non-toxic10. Nanoparticles may also help alleviate plant abiotic stress such as drought, salinity11 and temperature12 which lead to the reduction of crop yield due to plant growth retardation. In addition, nanoparticles can be used for crop biofortification, which will be important as agriculture moves to more marginal lands13. Sundaria et al.14 demonstrated that iron oxide nanoparticles offered a pronounced increase in germination percentage and shoot length and triggered iron acquisition and accumulation in wheat grains. Similar to iron, zinc oxide nanoparticles significantly increased the shoot length, root length, biomass, leaf area, chlorophyll content, and photosynthesis characteristics of tomato plants using foliar spray. As a result, the tomato yield was improved15. However, most existing studies have focused on ecological, physiological, or biochemical aspects. The purposeful uptake of nanoparticles to improve plant structural integrity and mechanical performance has received little attention.

To explore this, we turn our attention to flax (Linum usitatissimum L.), a wieldy cultivated plant known for its bast fibers due to its outstanding specific mechanical properties, comparable to those of glass fiber16. Flax stems, with their intricate structural organization comprising vascular bundles, cell walls, and a central pith, present unique mechanical properties that are essential for various bioengineering and industrial applications. These mechanical properties, including stiffness and tensile strength, depend heavily on the composition and arrangement of cell walls, which primarily consist of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin17. However, differences in cell maturity, biochemical composition distribution, and stem geometry due to variable environmental conditions and its impacts on cell wall deposition16 can lead to inconsistent mechanical performance, posing challenges for the use of flax fibers in high-performance applications. Recent advancements in nanotechnology provide a promising approach to addressing these challenges. One class of nanomaterials, cellulose nanofibers (CNFs), which are derived from plant cell wall material through successive chemical extraction and mechanical milling processes that break down cellulose into nanoscale fibers (Fig. 1)18 and can selectively enrich load-bearing crystalline cellulose fractions that generally are highly uniform in comparison to their originating cell wall materials19. This post-processing results in nanoparticles with defined size, shape, and chemical compositions, which are made of repeating sugar-based units connected by strong β-glycosidic bonds That particle and profile can be controlled to a higher degree20 offering a potential for precise interactions with plant tissues and modifying the variability of fiber characteristics caused by different environmental growth conditions. Moreover, CNFs, known for their exceptional mechanical strength, biodegradability, and biocompatibility, offer a unique opportunity for reinforcing plant structures. Therefore, we envision developing a closed loop, extracting CNFs from our cultivated flax and reintegrating them into our plants, to achieve self-sufficiency (Fig. 1). Studies have demonstrated that nanoparticles can be absorbed, transported, and integrated into plant tissues, enhancing physiological and biochemical characteristics. For example, they can improve stress tolerance and nutritional quality of crops while enabling targeted delivery within plant systems13​. Despite these advances, the purposeful integration of nanoparticles, particularly CNFs, into plant stems during growth to enhance structural and mechanical performance remains largely unexplored.Fig. 1Process of CNF extraction from plants. Building on these insights, we hypothesize that CNFs, when introduced into the growth medium, adsorbed, and integrated into flax stem cell walls, can serve as reinforcing agents that enhance structural integrity and mechanical properties like their reinforcing effects in polymer nanocomposites due to their strong fiber-matrix interfacial bonding and large aspect ratio21. This reinforcement mechanism is anticipated to mitigate the natural variability in stem morphology, resulting in more mechanically enhanced plants. The uptake of nanoparticles into plant tissues, whether through foliar sprays, root absorption, or seed priming, offers a well-documented pathway for their effective integration​. The objectives of this study are twofold: (1) to evaluate whether CNFs can effectively enhance plant growth and development and (2) to quantify the resulting improvements in mechanical properties, such as Young’s modulus and stiffness.

In this article, we demonstrated the potential of cellulose nanofiber (CNFs) to enhance the growth and development of flax through purposeful uptake during growth. Stem variability reduction and reinforcement mechanisms were also discussed. We assessed the potential of the in vivo CNF treatments to improve the mechanical performance of flax stems and established chemical structure and thermal property basis for mechanical property enhancement.

Enhanced shoot-borne root initiation and stem architecture via CNF uptake

Plant organs are typically protected by cell walls and tissues (a waxy cuticle layer), which, notwithstanding their sealing capability, may be penetrated by nanoparticles smaller than 5–20 nm in diameter6. After penetrating into the surface of plants, nanoparticles can translocate through apoplastic route, i.e. through extracellular spaces such as cell walls and longitudinal channels in the cell walls without penetrating any cell walls. On the other hand, the symplastic route requires the nanoparticles to cross the cell membrane by fluid-phase endocytic, or non-endocytic pathways and move from cell to cell through plasmodesmata8 (Fig. 2a–c), ultimately facilitating upward movement within the plant22. Earlier studies of the uptake and movement of nanoparticles in plants showed that these frequently accumulated and aggregated primarily in root tip tissues8. However, most of these focused on nanoparticles that were non-biological in origin, such as SiO223, or nano-gold24. We were curious whether the fact that CNFs themselves generated from plant cell wall components might alter how this material might affect flax growth. We tested whether the expression of the enhancement was that much in one dicotyledonous, flax (Linum usitatissimum). Flax seeds were germinated on soil and then transplanted to 50 mL tubes in CNF solutions (Fig. 2d–f). To follow the strategy of root development of tomato as roots continuously initiated from the internodes25 flax stems were cut from the main root system to expose the vasculature directly to the extracellular aqueous environment. This might therefore overcome the size-exclusion barriers posed by cell walls present in root meristematic and root epidermal tissues. All stems were cut directly above the first node (Fig. 3a).

To test whether CNF uptake impacts plant growth and assess the difference clearly and quantitatively, we conducted continuous monitoring for a 20-day period, comparing plant growth in CNF solution with control (autoclaved deionized water) (Fig. 2g,h). Plant height was recorded. The results demonstrated a significant increase in the height of the CNF-treated plants compared to controls, with an average height of 16.45 ± 0.78 cm for treated samples versus 14.62 ± 1.16 cm for controls (Fig. 3c), 17 technical replicates done for each condition. This corresponds to an approximate 13% increase in the height. One representative image captured at the end of 20 days (Fig. 3b) visually highlights this difference. This observed enhancement in height suggests that incubation with CNF may play an important role in promoting elongation in these excised stem sections, making us curious about their contribution to enhancing plant architecture. To further investigate the effects of CNFs on plant growth and development, we analyzed its vegetative apical meristems. In stem cuttings incubated with water alone, apical structures were poorly developed, and displayed significant chlorosis or senescence compared to stem cuttings incubated in CNF containing media (Fig. 3b). While the CNF-treated samples maintained relatively healthy vegetative apical meristems. This diffidence suggests that CNF treatment delays the senescence process, potentially contributing to extending reproductive activity. To support this observation, Fig. 3d and e shows a short treatment period of 8 days. The leaves of the controls had begun transitioning to a yellow-green hue (Fig. 3d). In contrast, leaves from the CNF-treated samples (Fig. 3e) retained a vibrant green color. Although the precise mechanism remains to be uncertain, taken together, these results indicated that growth and development of cut flax stems were supported by incubation in CNF-containing media.

In addition to supporting increased arial growth and development, we also observed enhanced formation of adventitious roots at the bases of flax plants grown in the presence of CNF. While basal plants sections incubated in water alone displayed little or no adventitious roots (Fig. 3f), plants incubated in CNF-containing water often showed numerous adventitious roots (Fig. 3g). Quantitative analysis after 20 days further underscored the dramatic difference in root development between two groups. 17 individual plants per treatment condition were measured here to ensure biological variability. As shown in Fig. 3h, the maximum root length in control samples averaged 0.50 cm, compared to 7.00 cm in CNF-treated samples, resulting in an up to 14-fold increase. This sharp contrast is visually reinforced by the accompanying photographs in Fig. 3h. These results indicate that in addition to supporting arial growth and development, incubation with CNF also promoted formation and development of adventitious roots in flax stem cuttings.

Given the increased growth and stimulation of adventitious root formation we observed when flax cuttings were incubated with CNF, we were curious how this might impact stem tissue development, in particular the formation of the bast fiber stem elements. To accomplish this, we first generated transverse cross-sections of CNF-treated and control flax stems under a microscope (Fig. 2i). The outer diameter (D) and pith sizes were measured using ImageJ software for precise quantification (Fig. 3i) for 8-day-old samples. Our observations revealed that CNF treatment led to a partial increase (6%) in the outer diameter of the stems (Fig. 3j), coupled with a reduction of approximately 10% in pith size (Fig. 3k). These structural changes suggested that CNF integration during plant growth reinforces the stem architecture, further supporting our hypothesis that enhancing the structural properties of flax stems is achievable through the in vivo nanofiber incorporation. Taken together, the findings in this section demonstrate that CNF uptake not only promotes significant shoot-borne root generation but also strengthens the structural integrity of flax stems, suggesting potential improvements in the mechanical performance of the stems.

Mechanical property enhancements via in vivo CNF incorporation

To evaluate whether the above morphological changes translate into measurable mechanical reinforcement, we next conducted a series of tests to quantify the tensile and compressive properties of flax stem following CNF uptake. Tensile test specimens were prepared (Fig. 4a–c), where epoxy tabs were added to both ends of the stems. These tabs encapsulated the stem and ensured secure attachment during testing, protecting thin and non-stiff stems at the grips and enabling load transfer from the grips to the stems. To prevent slippage at the grips, coarse sandpaper was used to roughen the surface of epoxy tabs. Tensile and compression testing were carried out (Fig. 4d,e) on the stems, respectively. Under axial loading, only the cross-section area of the stem was required for mechanical calculation, which was determined using an optical microscope. Given that the pith is hollow (Fig. 4d magnified image) and doesn’t contribute to mechanical performance of stems, its area was subtracted from the total cross-section to determine the effective load-bearing area, ensuring accurate calculations of mechanical properties.

The tensile stress-strain behavior of the control and CNF-treated flax stems revealed significant differences in their failure mechanisms (Fig. 4f). The control samples exhibited a sharp and abrupt stress drop upon reaching the maximum tensile loading (blue and green curves, Fig. 4f). This brittle failure behavior indicates a simultaneous collapse of all structural components of the stem, including the epidermis, vascular bundles, and pith (Fig. 4g). In contrast, CNF-treated stems demonstrated a progressive failure pattern, characterized by three distinct stress-drop phases (Fig. 4f). The damage initiation occurring at phase ①, likely corresponding to the delamination of the epidermis and its partial separation from the underlying cortical tissue due to the increasing tensile loading. This process is further supported by Fig. 4h, where the detachment of the outer layer is evident. As the loading continues, further delamination and microcrack accumulation occur leading to damage propagation into vascular bundles (phase ②), as suggested by the observed sharp stress drops in the stress-strain curve shown in Fig. 4f accompanied by real-time observations of the testing process. Finally, the third phase stopped at the complete fracture of the core tissues, including pith and vascular bundles of stem (Fig. 4h). The staged failure in CNF-treated stems likely tells us that incubation with media containing CNFs potentially improves the adhesion between epidermis, cortex, and vascular bundles, which enables more efficient load transfer and delays crack propagation. Additionally, CNFs may also act as toughening agents which bridge microcracks and distribute stresses more uniformly, which is analogous to the mechanism on carbon nanotube (CNT)/Al2O3 hybrids in glass fiber/epoxy composites, where hybrids dispersed between adjacent fibers serve as bridges to interconnect the neighboring fibers and slow down the growth of microcracks26. Similarly, multi-walled carbon nanotubes also interact with the polymer matrix through linear contact due to their high aspect ratio27. While direct evidence in plant tissues is limited, CNFs may function analogously since their similar structures allowing CNF-treated stems to sustain higher loads with a larger extension range (Fig. 4f). However, control stems lack such reinforcing interactions, leading to stress concentration. As a result, an abrupt failure happens. CNFs treatment resulted in staged failure process in flax stems that highlights their enhanced resilience, which indicated that the improved growth and development in Sect. 0 also resulted in altered physical properties of the load-bearing elements in these plant tissues.

Following the stress-strain curve analysis, we further assess their mechanical properties by comparing the Young’s modulus and stiffness under tensile and axial compressive loading (Fig. 4i,j), respectively. The former was calculated from the linear portion of the stress-strain curve (Fig. 4f), while the stiffness was obtained by the ratio of force to displacement. Given the inherent variability in plants, we employed Weibull analysis to statistically model these mechanical properties for 17 control and 17 CNF-treated stems. This method ideally can provide us with a robust estimate of central tendencies. In the tensile test, CNF-treated stems exhibited approximate 7% increases in Young’s modulus and an 8% improvement in stiffness (inserted table in Fig. 4i). The improvements were more pronounced in compression test, where Young’s modulus increased by 22% (from 110.74 to 135.27 MPa) and stiffness improved by 33% (from 9.42 to 12.54 N/mm) after incubation with media containing CNFs (inserted table in Fig. 4j). These significant improvements in compressive properties highlight the role of CNFs in enhancing the load-bearing capacity of stems. CNFs likely reinforce the interactions between the vascular bundles and pith, preventing or delaying localized bucking and deformation under axial loading, which is in line with our previous observations under tension.

To further visualize the differences in mechanical properties, we generated a radar chart comparing stiffness, Young’s modulus, strength, energy, and ultimate load for the compression test (Fig. 4l), which effectively highlights the multidimensional improvement in mechanical performance by CNF treatment and offers a clear comparative perspective between the control and CNF-treated samples. Furthermore, density, a crucial indicator of compositional changes in biological systems, was measured. The results were illustrated using a violin plot (Fig. 4k) to better show the distribution and variability of our data. The density of CNF-treated stems is marginally lower than that of control samples (approximately 10%), which contrasts with conventional studies reporting a positive correlation between density and mechanical properties in biology-based system28. This reduction in density could reflect microstructural changes, such as improved porosity (the density of a plant stem increases with the solid fraction) or optimized tissue composition (i.e. cellulose content and cellulose crystallinity)29 without compromising mechanical performance. Such a phenomenon may point toward the development of a lightweight and high-performance bio-material system.

Our results indicate that incubation with CNFs improves both tensile and compressive mechanical properties, while simultaneously reducing density. Taken together with Sect. 0, CNF integration not only reinforces the structural architecture but also introduces a lightweight reinforcement strategy.

Structural and chemical evidence for CNF integration in plant cell walls

To establish the variations introduced by CNF treatments on the chemical structure of the flax stem and provide a chemical basis for the enhanced mechanical properties observed in the preceding section, we performed Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) on both horizontal and vertical cross-sections of the control and CNT-treated stems (Fig. 5a,b). A broader band corresponding to OH-stretching in the region of 3650 –3000 cm− 1 was observed in both spectra, which is attributed to intramolecular hydrogen bonding in cellulose II30. CNF-treated samples displayed increased intensity in this band, indicating a stronger hydrogen bonding network within the cellulose structure. This enhancement in hydrogen bonding likely prompts closer molecular packing and better cohesion among cellulose chains, thereby facilitating more effective load transfer across the matrix, which have been previously reported in cellulose nanomaterials20. Near 2916 cm− 1 was the stretching vibration peak of C-H, representing the structural aliphatic groups in lignin31 showed increased absorption intensity in CNF-treated samples and no equivalent peak was displayed at 2964 cm− 1 in the spectra of control samples, suggesting potential structural modifications within the stems. Since there is little to no lignin present in CNFs, this increase suggests that CNFs are inducing changes in the stem biomass as well. This effect could show that CNFs may reinforce these stem segments through two distinct ways: (1) uptake and structural reinforcement directly due to the biophysical properties of the CNF particles, and (2) indirect structural reinforcement due to in vivo adaptation of plant cell walls by incubation with CNFs. Moreover, the peaks derived from skeletal vibration of the aromatic group in lignin (1414 cm− 1) and C = O stretching in the G unit of lignin (1264 cm− 1) for control samples exhibited less intensity compared to CNF-treated samples. This could be ascribed to more lignin induced by the CNF uptake32. A similar trend was observed at 782 cm− 1, corresponding to C-H deformation vibrations30 further supporting the densification of the lignin network. This enhanced our understanding of the improved mechanical properties since lignin is a primary natural matrix component in flax plants, which to some extent influenced stiffness.

Likewise, hemicellulose, a mixture of a number of different polysaccharide networks and another critical natural matrix component, also exhibited structural changes. A weak band appeared around 1736 cm− 1 in all samples corresponded to C = O stretching in acetyl and uronic ester groups from hemicellulose or ester linkage of carboxylic group of the ferulic and p-coumaric acids of lignin33,34. Additionally, a drastic change in intensity at 1046 cm− 1 was observed, associated with C-O-C stretching vibrations in xylan-rich hemicellulose30. Since only xylan and arabinoxylan are thought to directly interact with lignin, the sharper and more intense absorbance peak of CNF-treated stems (Fig. 5a), indicating that CNF uptake may induce structural rearrangement within xylan. Given the role of hemicellulose in providing elasticity and distributing load across the lignocellulosic network, these changes likely contribute to the superior mechanical performance of the CNF-treated samples. Interestingly, the peak at 1616–1605 cm− 1 in all spectrums corresponds to the O-H bending of absorbed water. Although the FTIR samples were dried, the water adsorbed in the cellulose molecules was difficult to remove completely due to the cellulose-water interaction35. These shifts suggest that CNF treatment may affect how cellulose, lignin, and hemicellulos are organized within the cell wall, possibly leading to a more coordinated and structured network that contributes to improved mechanical behavior.

Additionally, to further investigate the structural modifications in the treated samples, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was conducted (Fig. 5c). The onset temperature of the phase transition increased from 166.06 °C in the control samples (Fig. 5d blue dashed frame) to 168.04 °C after CNF treatment (Fig. 5d red dashed frame), showing a slight increase in thermal stability due to incubation with CNF. This shift may suggest that CNFs enhance the intermolecular interactions within the lignocellulosic matrix, leading to a more thermally stable structure. After CNF treatment, the degradation temperature was increased to 168.42 °C. The degradation enthalpy of the CNF-treated sample is 11.88 J/g, which was higher than that of controls (8.01 J/g), indicating an increase of degree of crystallinity in CNF-treated samples (Fig. 5d). This is consistent with the FTIR findings, where stronger hydrogen bonding was observed likely restricting cellulose chain mobility and prompting tighter molecular packing. These changes support the hypothesis that CNFs are integrated into the cell wall during stem growth. It is also worth noting that multiple endothermic peaks appear in the CNF-treated samples showing two different crystal morphologies (Fig. 5d). The peak ① (lower melting peak) may be attributed to a localized molecular rearrangement potentially linked to the row nucleated shish kebab structure, where none of the crystals are perfect, melting more easily. However, there may be spherulite structures at peak ②, where all the crystallites are roughly symmetric and highly ordered, so it is going to melt at a higher temperature36. These results are in line with Ref.37, who reported that the added CNFs can act as crystal nucleation agents to promote the formation of ordered structures in poly (lactic acid) matrix, which may contribute to the additional thermal behavior in the DSC curve of CNF-treated stems. The increase in crystallinity improved molecular packing and load transfer efficiency38 which is in line with our improved mechanical performance, also supported by the improved thermal stability.

These improvements in mechanical performance offer the potential to strengthen flax-based composites for applications in automative, aerospace, and construction sectors. This contributes to the advancement of lightweight, low-cost, and environmentally friendly bio-composites, aligning with the broader direction of nano-enhanced green composite development39.

Conclusions

This study presents a cost-effective and sustainable in vivo treatment technique using CNFs to enhance plant development and mechanical performance in cut flax stem sections. By introducing low concentrations of CNFs during the growth process, we observed significant improvements in plant health, shoot-borne root initiation, and structural reinforcement of flax stems. More importantly, we successfully enhanced the mechanical properties of flax stems through modifications in plant morphology and cellular structures.

Compared with control samples grown in autoclaved distilled water, CNFs caused an approximate 13% increase in height (from 14.62 ± 1.16 cm to 16.45 ± 0.78 cm) within 20 days and a delay in senescence, characterized by a healthier vegetative apical meristem and vibrant green color. More notably, CNF treatment induced a dramatic increase in shoot-borne root growth, with a maximum root length of 7.00 cm, compared to only 0.5 cm in control samples. Structural enhancement was evident at the macroscale, as incubation with CNF resulted in an increase in stem outer diameter and partial closure of the pith. These morphological changes contributed to improved mechanical behavior, shifting failure mode from a brittle fracture to a more progressive failure mechanism that enabled better load distribution and crack propagation resistance. Furthermore, under tensile loading, CNF-treated stems demonstrated a 7% increase in Young’s modulus and 8% improvement in stiffness. The effects were even pronounced under compression, where Young’s modulus increased by 22% (from 110.74 MPa to 135.27 MPa) and stiffness improved by 33% (from 9.42 N/mm to 12.54 N/mm). Contrary to conventional positive correlation between density and mechanical properties in biological materials, CNF-treated stems exhibited slightly lower density than control samples, suggesting a potential for developing lightweight yet high-performance bio-based materials. Moreover, FTIR spectra revealed increased hydrogen bonding in cellulose and modifications in lignin and hemicellulose structures, supporting a more cohesive plant matrix. And the enhanced crystallinity and thermal stability further confirmed our hypothesis that CNFs can effectively improve the mechanical properties of flax stems.

The long-term uptake and integration of nanoparticles into plant structures is attractive. Future research should extend the investigation to a fully maturity stage (120 days after sowing) to assess the durability of CNF-induced enhancements over the entire growth cycle of flax plants. Additionally, the precise mechanisms over CNF uptake and translocation in flax plants remain unclear. To better understand how CNFs integrate with the cell wall and their localization over time, radiolabeling techniques (e.g.3, H-labeling CNFs) will be applied to track CNF distribution and interactions within plant tissues. On the other hand, one limitation of our current study lies in the challenges associated with accurate extension measurement in thin and flexible stems. The use of conventional clip-on extensometers is infeasible due to their fragility. However, in the absence of these devices, sample slippage within the grips can potentially skew the extension reading. Therefore, non-contact methods such as digital image processing or laser extensometers should be explored in our future work to improve the accuracy of strain measurement.

While the current approach is effective for controlled conditions and commercial CNF products (Valida S231C from Sappi) are relatively low cost (< $30/kg), the real-world application of CNF treatment on a large-scale field production would require additional development. The greenhouse-grown or high-value fiber crops, where controlled irrigation or hydroponic systems are already in place would be feasible for integrating CNF suspension into existing watering systems without extensive equipment modification. However, future work should develop dry or concentrated CNF formulations and assess their performance under variable soil environments and for the whole plant system to improve ecological validity toward scalable industrial practice.

Methods

Preparation and application of CNF treatments for in vivo plant treatments

Flax seeds (Linum usitatissimum L., variety Agatha), sourced from Willamette Valley, Oregon, were subjected to a two-step sterilization process to ensure the removal of surface contaminants and pathogens. First, the seeds were treated with 70% ethanol to disrupt the lipid membranes of potential microorganisms and denature proteins, effectively killing bacteria and fungi on the seed surface. This was followed by a solution containing 20% bleach (sodium hypochlorite) and Tween-20, which acted as a surfactant to enhance the penetration of the bleach, ensuring the oxidation and elimination of any remaining microbial contaminants40. After sterilization, the seeds were thoroughly rinsed five times with autoclaved deionized water to remove residual chemicals. The sterilized seeds were stored at 4 °C to synchronize germination41. The seeds were then sown in gardening trays filled with Arabidopsis soil (Sun Gro Horticulture, Agawam, MA, USA) and cultivated in a growth chamber (Flex™ FXC-9, BioChambers, Winnipeg, Canada) set to a controlled temperature of 20 °C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod, following established protocols. After 45 days, plants of uniform height were selected, and roots and lateral branches were carefully excised using a sterile razor blade, leaving only the main stems for subsequent experimentation. A schematic illustration of the flax plant preparation and treatment process is shown in Fig. 2d–f.

A 0.2% (w/v) cellulose nanofiber (CNF) suspension was selected and prepared based on prior work conducted within our group42 which evaluated a range of concentration and identified 0.2% w/v as the most effective and reliable concentration. CNF paste (Valida S231C; 8 ± 0.5% cellulose, Sappi, Maastricht, Netherlands) was weighed (0.1 g) and dispersed in 50 mL of autoclaved deionized water. The solution was stirred until fully homogenized and further sterilized by UV irradiation for 3 min to minimize microbial contamination. Control samples were prepared with sterilized deionized water. To ensure sterility during sample preparation, all procedures were conducted under a laminar flow hood (Envirco Corp HORIZ Flow Bench, USA). Then each flax was individually immersed in a centrifuge tube containing either the CNF suspension or the control solution. Samples were then returned to the growth chamber under identical environmental conditions for eight days. During this treatment period, root formation and plant height were monitored. Following the treatment, the plants were removed from the solutions, dried using anhydrous calcium sulfate for 10 days (W. A. Hammond DRIERITE Co. LTD, OH, USA), and subjected to oven drying for 30 min before further analyses.

Standardized mechanical testing for evaluating tensile and compressive properties

Precision preparation of flax stem specimens for reliable mechanical assessment

Specimens were cut using a hot wire cutter (THERMOCUT 115/E, PROXXON Inc., Hickory, NC, USA) to achieve clean, flat end faces without deforming the stem cross-sections or causing significant tissue damage. For axial compression tests, 17 groups of stem segments, each 5 mm in length, were prepared between two nodes to ensure consistency in geometry and mechanical properties. The preparation process for tensile test specimens is illustrated in Fig. 4a–c, To prevent slippage or crushing by the testing machine grips, epoxy end tabs were created at both ends of the 65 mm long stems. Loctite EA 9430 resin and hardener were mixed at a ratio of 100:23 and degassed in a vacuum oven (Lindberg/Blue M™, VO1218A, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 30 °C for 15 min. The ends of the stems were trimmed and roughened using P400 carbide sandpaper to enhance bonding. Customized silicone molds, pre-coated with a release agent (Berg Engineering & Sales Company, Inc., IL, USA), were used to cast the epoxy around the ends of the stems. The specimens were cured at room temperature overnight, and the epoxy tabs were polished to ensure uniform width and thickness.

Axial compression testing: probing the longitudinal strength and stability of flax stems

Axial compression experiments were performed using a TA.XTPlus Texture Analyzer (Texture Technologies Corp., Brewster, NY, USA) equipped with a 294 N load cell. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, a 5 mm stem segment was carefully positioned between parallel plates designed with grooves to ensure precise alignment along the longitudinal axis, thereby minimizing eccentric loading effects. The stems were compressed at a constant displacement rate of 0.02 mm/s until structural failure occurred. Force-displacement data were recorded for mechanical property analysis, including compressive strength and stiffness. Due to the significant structural contribution of the pith, the cross-sectional area (A) of the stem was calculated as a hollow ring, as shown in the cross-sectional schematic in Fig. 4d (magnified image). This approach accounts for the distinct inner and outer structural contributions of the plant stem.

Tensile testing of flax stems: quantifying the reinforcement effects of CNF treatment

Tensile experiments were conducted to assess the mechanical behavior of plant stems under unidirectional loading along their longitudinal axis. The tests employed an Instron 3345 universal testing machine (Instron, Norwood, MA, USA) equipped with a 5 kN load cell. To enhance geometric uniformity, all nodes were removed from the stem specimens. As shown in Fig. 4b, a 65 mm stem segment with epoxy-reinforced end tabs was securely clamped to ensure proper axial loading. The tensile tests followed ASTM D638 standards and were carried out at a constant crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until the specimens were fractured. Force-displacement data were recorded using Instron Bluehill 2 software for subsequent mechanical property analysis, including tensile strength and Young’s modulus. A total of 17 group-specimens, comprising both cellulose nanofiber (CNF)-treated and untreated samples, were evaluated to investigate the effects of CNF treatment on the mechanical properties of the stems.

Structural and thermal characterization techniques

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed to detect the changes in the chemical structures of the flax stems after CNF treatment. A Jasco FTIR-4X spectrometer was used, and data was processed using Spectra software. Measurements were conducted over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4.0 cm−1, using 16 scans per sample. Comparative analysis of the spectra from untreated and CNF-treated samples focused on identifying functional group modifications and potential chemical bonding associated with the CNF treatment.

Thermal properties, including thermal stability and crystallinity, were evaluated using a Differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) (Discovery 2500, TA Instruments, DE, USA) to identify differences between untreated and CNF-treated samples. DSC scans were conducted in the temperature range from 25 to 200 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The crystallization and melting behavior were recorded in a nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate of 50 mL/min.

The outer and inner diameters of the flax stems were measured using images captured with an Olympus GX51 Inverted microscope. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the cross-sectional areas of the stems. Measurements were conducted on both untreated and CNF-treated samples to evaluate any dimensional changes induced by the treatment. The density of flax stems was determined using a Cubis® II analytical balance (Sartorius) with YDK03 Density Determination Kit for both untreated and CNF-treated samples to understand the impact of CNF treatment on material performance.
