Machine learning-based identification of abnormal functional connectivity in obesity across different metabolic states
Authors: Yuan Yue, Patrick Manning, Dirk De Ridder, Matthew Hall, Divya Bharatkumar Adhia, Samantha Ross, Daniel Alencar da Costa, Jeremiah D. Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a major health concern linked to chronic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease. However, most neurological studies have focused on specific metabolic states, limiting understanding of how brain function changes from fasting to satiety. Furthermore, hypothesis-driven approaches may introduce bias and fail to capture complex neural interactions. This study aimed to identify brain connectivity patterns associated with obesity across different metabolic states using a data-driven approach.MethodsElectroencephalography data were collected from 30 women with obesity and 30 women without obesity over a four-hour period encompassing fasting and post-meal states. All subjects were aged 20 to 65 years. Functional connectivity was calculated from source-localized signals, and a machine learning framework incorporating a feature selection method was applied to identify the most discriminative connectivity features between groups.ResultsHere we show that six connectivity features classify obesity with 95% accuracy across metabolic states. Reduced connectivity are observed within food-reward processing regions in the obese group, with the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex emerging as a central hub. This pattern reflects a persistent alteration in energy prediction and craving regulation that is independent of metabolic state.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that disrupted brain connectivity is a fundamental characteristic of obesity. The results highlight the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex as a key region underlying maladaptive reward processing and suggest that targeting this area through neuromodulation therapies may offer a promising intervention for obesity treatment.

Introduction

Obesity is an epidemic global health problem with an estimated 13% of adults and 6% of children classified as obese1. Prevalence rates are even higher in developed countries, reaching ~25% among adults. Obesity is associated with an increased risk of chronic disease including Type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and certain cancers2 and it is regarded as a complex disorder driven by genetic and epigenetic factors interacting with an obesogenic environment3. The interaction between an individual and the environment involves the concerted activity of the nervous, endocrine and immunological system4,5. An understanding of these pathogenic mechanisms is critical for developing appropriate interventions to prevent and treat obesity.

One of the components of the immune-endocrine-nervous system interactions involved in the development of obesity relates to the underlying neurobiological mechanisms that control feeding behaviour6,7. Extensive neuroimaging research has compared the differences in the brains of obese and non-obese individuals8. These studies have examined various brain characteristics, such as functional connectivity (FC)8, structural changes8, and blood flow variations9, to understand the impact of obesity on brain function and structure and its subsequent effect on behaviour and cognition. Among these characteristics, FC has emerged as a particularly well-explored area. FC refers to the statistical dependencies between different brain regions, reflecting how these regions communicate with each other. It can be measured using various neuroimaging techniques, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG), and provides insights into how different parts of the brain interact during various physiological or mental states10.

Several studies have found differences in FC patterns associated with reward processing, self-regulation, and emotional responses in obese individuals compared to healthy non-obese individuals10–13. For example, decreased FC between the prefrontal cortex and the striatum, which is crucial for inhibiting food-related cues, and increased FC between the hypothalamus and regions involved in emotional processing, such as the amygdala, have also been reported14.

In addition, research has suggested a strong bidirectional association between obesity and psychological distress. For example, the presence of depression can predict the development of obesity and vice versa15. This relationship extends to other psychiatric comorbidities, including anxiety disorders16 and binge eating disorder17, which are frequently observed in individuals with obesity. Shared biological mechanisms, such as dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis18 and chronic low-grade inflammation19, may underlie this bidirectional link. Furthermore, from a Bayesian brain theory perspective20, which suggests that the brain functions as a probabilistic inference machine integrating sensory inputs with prior beliefs to update predictions, obesity is linked to insufficient behavioural adaptation, particularly in response to negative prediction errors21.

Furthermore, studies have found that patterns of FC are modulated by hunger and satiation, or by the presence of food cues10. For instance, during hunger, obese individuals exhibit heightened FC between the hypothalamus and the striatum, indicating increased sensitivity to food cues14. Additionally, alterations in FC between the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala suggest heightened emotional and impulsive responses22. In contrast, during satiety, these connections may shift, reducing sensitivity to food cues and enhancing self-regulation23.

While various brain activity patterns associated with obesity have been identified, most existing studies focus on a single specific metabolic state, such as fasting or peak satiety, which can limit the overall understanding of obesity10. Although a few studies have explored intrinsic obesity-related brain activities by examining brain function at one specific time point during the preprandial state and another distinct time point during the postprandial state24–26, these investigations remain limited. This is because brain activity patterns can fluctuate due to the different metabolic states in the postprandial period, underscoring the necessity for studies incorporating multiple time points to gain a holistic understanding of obesity.

Our study aims to address this limitation by investigating brain activity associated with obesity, specifically focusing on EEG-based FC across different metabolic states (including fasting and multiple postprandial states) using a data-driven machine learning (ML) approach. This contrasts with previous studies, which looked at brain correlates based on a prior hypothesis6,8,10. Specifically, ML does not rely on predefined brain regions, thus reducing bias and enabling the discovery of complex interactions between brain regions that traditional statistical methods, such as significance tests and regression, may not uncover. Additionally, EEG is particularly well-suited for ML analysis due to its ability to generate extensive datasets through repeated measurements over time27. To our best knowledge, our study is the first to uncover obesity-related FC patterns by analysing both fasting state data and data across multiple postprandial metabolic states. It is also the first to employ an ML approach to identify EEG-based obesity-related brain activity patterns. While two previous studies have applied ML to identify connectivity patterns related to the obesity phenotype28,29, they did not account for metabolic-induced variations and relied on fMRI data. As a result, the effects of food intake and the temporal dynamics of connectivity, which EEG uniquely captures, were not considered in those studies.

Our results show that a data-driven ML approach can classify obesity with a high accuracy of 95% using only six FC features across different metabolic states. Obesity is linked to significantly reduced connectivity between brain regions involved in craving and satiety processing. Notably, the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), a key region in food craving, emerges as a central node in this network, showing a persistent lack of adaptive connectivity in response to food intake. These findings highlight the dACC’s crucial role in disrupted reward regulation and suggest that obesity is associated with stable alterations in brain connectivity that are independent of metabolic state.

Methods

The main steps of the proposed method in this study are demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Experimental design

The study recruited 30 female non-obese subjects and 30 age-matched female subjects with obesity, aged between 20 and 65 years. Inclusion criteria required a Body Mass Index (BMI) greater than 30 for the obese group and less than 25 for the non-obese group. Exclusion criteria included comorbidities associated with obesity (diabetes and obstructive sleep apnoea, pregnancy, the use of centrally acting medications, significant weight changes in the past six months, and a history of head injury or neurological/psychiatric disorders). The study was conducted following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Southern Health and Disabilities Ethics Committee (Ref15:/STH/68). All subjects provided written informed consent and attended the research facility following an overnight fast.

Anthropometric data, including age, height, weight, blood pressure, resting heart rate, and waist circumference, were directly measured from the subjects. BMI, resting energy expenditure (REE), body fat percentage (using the BC-418 Segmental Body Composition Analyzer), fat mass, and fat-free mass were further computed.

Subjects with obesity completed the protocol at three stages: baseline (BL), post-weight loss (PWL) after a 4-week very low-calorie diet (>5% weight loss), and 3 months post-weight loss (3MON) after resuming normal diets, while remaining obese at PWL and 3MON. This design aimed to capture physiological and neural adaptations during and after weight loss in obese individuals. Non-obese subjects completed the protocol once at BL.

Five minutes of EEG recordings were made, initially sampled at 1000 Hz and further downsampled to 128 Hz30 to reduce computational load while preserving relevant neural information. The recordings were conducted over a 4-h periprandial window to capture variations induced by changes in the metabolic state (i.e., transitions between fasting, food intake, and postprandial digestion). Initial data were recorded at the fasting state (T0), with subsequent recordings made at 15 min (T15), 30 min (T30), 45 min (T45), 60 min (T60), 90 min (T90), 120 min (T120), 180 min (T180), and 240 min (T240) after the initial recording. Subjects were given half of a liquid meal after the T0 recording and the remaining half after the T15 recording. Dividing the meal had no significant effect on hormonal, metabolic, or appetite regulation31, and it improved subject comfort with meal ingestion.

The liquid meal used in this study was Ensure Plus (1.5 kcal/mL), consisting of 16.7% protein, 53.8% carbohydrate, and 29.5% fat. We decided on average 600 kcal would be administered, aligning with relevant studies using 700 kcal32,33. The final meal volume was customized for each subject and calculated as follows: 600 kcal Average REE−1× 1.5 kcal/mL−1× Subject’s REE.

Blood samples were taken by venepuncture at T0, T45, T120, and T240 into 5 mL dipotassium EDTA tubes for glucose and insulin measurements. The tubes were immediately placed on ice and centrifuged at low speed (1500 × g) for 15 min at 4 °C within 30 min. Aliquots were taken from these tubes within 15–30 min and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Questionnaires related to eating behaviours (Intuitive Eating, Mindful Eating, Dutch Eating Behaviour Questionnaire [DEBQ]-Restrained, DEBQ-External, DEBQ-Emotional, Yale Food Addiction Scale [YFAS], Binge Eating) were completed by all subjects at the BL stage. The original questions for these are provided in the Supplementary Tables 2–6.

Data preprocessing, source localization algorithm and functional connectivity computation

EEG data were segmented into epochs of 2560 time frames, corresponding to a total duration of 20 s. To minimize spectral leakage and improve frequency resolution, a Hann window was applied to the data prior to Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) computation. The Hann window was corrected to account for the reduction in amplitude caused by windowing, ensuring accurate estimation of spectral power34.

We used the Exact Low-Resolution Brain Electromagnetic Tomography (eLORETA) method, implemented in the LORETA-KEY software package, for EEG source localization. The eLORETA methods has been validated in prior studies using fMRI35, structural MRI36, PET37, and intracranial EEG38. The head model and electrode coordinates were based on the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152) average MRI brain map39. The solution space encompassed 6239 voxels with a spatial resolution of 5 mm3, confined to the cortical grey matter.

FFT was then used to decompose the data into frequency components. Lagged coherence was then computed across five standard frequency bands: delta (0.1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–45 Hz)40. Lagged coherence was selected as the FC metric based on prior evidence demonstrating its suitability for experimental EEG data41,42. This measure quantifies the consistency of non-zero phase lag relationships between signals over time using Fourier-based techniques while excluding instantaneous (zero-lag) interactions. For detailed computational methods, refer to ref. 43.

The analysis was conducted across all pairs of 88 Regions of Interest (ROIs), which included an expansion of the 84 ROIs defined by the LORETA-KEY package. Due to the established relevance of the anterior cingulate gyrus in obesity10,27,44, we increased its structural resolution by subdividing it into pregenual, subgenual, and dorsal subregions for both hemispheres, yielding four additional areas. A 3D map (axial, sagittal and coronal planes) of the centroids of all ROIs has been provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

The resulting lagged coherence values were averaged within each frequency band to produce a single connectivity measure per band.

Model training and testing

As outlined in Section Experimental Design, experimental data were collected from 30 subjects with obesity at three stages (BL, PWL, and 3MON) and from 30 non-obese subjects only at BL, resulting in three times more samples for the obese group than the non-obese group. To prevent model bias and ensure balanced class representation during training, random oversampling was applied to duplicate minority-class (non-obese) samples within the training dataset. This approach was chosen given its proven efficiency in handling nonlinear classification under imbalanced conditions45,46.

We used a 10-fold subject-based cross-validation approach to split the dataset for training and testing47–49. This involved dividing the dataset into ten equal parts with a train-to-test ratio of 90:10, where each part was held out in turn for independent testing while the remaining nine parts were used for training. We ensured that samples from different time points (T0-T240), stages (BL, PWL, 3MON) were balanced to prevent model bias towards specific conditions. Each training set therefore consisted of 1458 samples ((27 obese subjects × 3 stages × 9 time points + 27 non-obese subjects × 3 (duplicating factor) × 9 time points)) and each independent testing fold including 108 samples (3 subjects × 3 stages × 9 time points + 3 non-obese subjects × 9 time points). Random oversampling was applied exclusively to the training data in each iteration, thereby generating ten independently oversampled training sets that ensured robustness against sampling variability.

Within the training dataset, we further applied 80:10 cross-validation. Feature selection was conducted using 80% of the data (sub-training), and model optimization was performed on the remaining 10% (subvalidation). This approach ensured that the ML model remained free from data leakage50, with the test dataset completely unseen by the model during the entire process.

Feature selection and classification

Feature selection is critical to prevent model from overfitting, particularly given the extensive set of FC features in this study. The objective of feature selection is to identify a subset of non-redundant FC features that serve as discriminators between obese and non-obese brain. These features enable the ML model to effectively differentiate between the FC patterns of obese and lean individuals.

Due to the complex interactions among these features, we implemented a two-stage feature selection process. First, we employed Random Forest (RF)-guided Dual-Layer Incremental Wrapper-Based Feature Selection (DLI-WFS)51 to identify an initial set of relevant features. This method incrementally refines feature subsets through an efficient wrapper approach that minimizes computational cost while ensuring non-redundancy and accounting for complex feature interactions. In the second stage, we further optimized the feature subset through sequential selection of features contributing to statistically significant performance improvements. This refinement step aimed to retain only the most discriminative and impactful features for clinical interpretation.

Stage 1: Initial Feature Selection Using DLI-WFS DLI-WFS begins by ranking features based on the importance scores computed by an RF classifier. The selected feature subset is initialized with the top-1-ranked feature. RF-derived feature importance scores are reliable for guiding the DLI-WFS algorithm due to RF’s ability to effectively capture interaction effects between features. For example, RF may identify that increased FC between region A and region B predicts obesity only when co-occurring with decreased connectivity in region C, highlighting an interaction between distinct neural circuits.

Next, using the initialized feature subset with the top-ranked feature, the DLI-WFS algorithm iteratively adds the next feature and evaluates performance gain (i.e. accuracy) using a wrapper algorithm. If the accuracy improves, the feature is retained; otherwise, the least important feature (based on the feature importance score computed using RF) in the current set is removed, and the process continues. This dynamic reselection ensures a non-redundant feature set optimized for classification performance. The feature selection process terminates under two conditions: (1) if the number of consecutive iterations without performance improvement exceeds a threshold, or (2) if the target number of features is reached. Both thresholds were set to 30, as validated in the original study51, to balance computational efficiency and thoroughness.

For each wrapper algorithm, these feature selection procedures are applied independently to each subtraining fold, with model performance assessed on the corresponding sub-validation fold across different feature subset sizes. Following all iterations, an integrated feature set is derived via majority voting, retaining features present in more than four of nine runs. The final feature set selected for subsequent refinement corresponds to the wrapper yielding the highest average performance across all folds.

Regarding the wrapper algorithm choice, we employed three wrapper algorithms—k-nearest neighbours (KNN), AdaBoost (ADB), and support vector machines (SVM) with a radial basis function kernel—as they were previously validated in the original DLI-WFS algorithm51. The features selected by the top-performing wrapper algorithm are passed to the next stage for further refinement.

Stage 2: Feature Refinement Although DLI-WFS selects the features that optimize performance, it does not guarantee the identification of the most minimal set of significant features. This is because the algorithm selects any feature that associates to a positive performance gain, even if the gain is not statistically significant. As a result, the selected feature subset may still include less impactful features, which could lead to unnecessary complexity without substantial benefits. Therefore, further refining the feature subset to a minimal set, retaining only those linked to statistically significant model performance gains, is essential for enhancing model interpretability, isolating key information, and mitigating overfitting risks52. To achieve this, selected features were re-ranked based on permutation importance, defined as the average reduction in accuracy after 100 random shuffles of feature values53. Starting from an empty model, features were incrementally added in descending order of importance, with performance evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation (RF classifier) within the 90% training data. Only features yielding statistically significant gains were retained. The refined feature set was then used for unbiased performance estimation on the independent test data and served as input for interpreting obesity-related brain patterns.

The algorithm of the feature selection process applied in this study is provided in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Classification RF was employed to evaluate model performance for obese versus non-obese classification on both the validation dataset (within the training data) and the independent test dataset.

As mentioned in Section Model Training and Testing, the training dataset was balanced using Random Oversampling to ensure equal representation of obese and non-obese groups. Consequently, accuracy was used as the primary metric for evaluating performance on the validation data, as the balanced dataset ensured proportional reflection of true positive and true negative rates. For the independent test dataset, which retained its imbalanced distribution, additional metrics—including precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC—were employed alongside accuracy. This comprehensive evaluation ensured reliable assessment of the model’s generalizability and mitigated bias toward the majority class.

Hyperparameter Optimization Due to computational constraints, most hyperparameters were set to their default values, as they balance efficiency and performance, ensure reproducibility, and reduce the risk of overfitting54. However, n_estimators and max_depth, the most critical hyperparameters in the RF classifier55, were optimized via randomized grid search using the subtrain–subvalidation folds. The search range for both hyperparameters spanned 25–200 at intervals of 5, resulting in converged optimal values of n_estimators = 75 and max_depth = 15 across all iterations. This tuning was critical, as the RF classifier not only performed obesity classification but also ranked feature importance in the DLI-WFS algorithm, directly influencing the wrapper-based feature selection phase and its overall effectiveness.

Analysis of selected features

To analyse the significant FC features associated with obesity (i.e., those identified after the feature refinement stage), we compared their values (i.e., FC strength) between the obese and non-obese groups, both across all time points and at each individual time point. Furthermore, to account for complex feature interactions, we also evaluated these features at each time point using classification analysis to assess how they perform under specific metabolic conditions. This approach provides a holistic view of how connectivity differs between groups and how these differences are modulated by variations in metabolic state.

Targeted gamma band dACC-involving functional connectivity analysis

Given emerging evidence implicating the dACC in reward and cognitive control processes relevant to obesity56, and considering the centrality of its gamma-band connectivity in our whole-brain findings (see Section Feature Interpretation), we conducted hypothesis-driven analyses of dACC-involving connectivity using an ML approach to further examine the activity of this area. This approach aligns with our study’s overarching data-driven framework, enabling investigation of how gamma-band dACC FC discriminates between non-obese individuals and those with obesity across two key dimensions: different metabolic states (fasting/T0, satiety/T60, and hunger/T240); and weight stages (BL, PWL, and 3MON).

Specifically, for each metabolic state, we extracted all dACC-to-87-ROI gamma-band FC values. We then performed obesity classification comparing three distinct groups: non-obese subjects at BL versus obese subjects at BL, non-obese subjects at BL versus obese subjects at PWL, and non-obese subjects at BL versus obese subjects at 3MON.

The classification employed the nested cross-validation framework introduced previously (Section Model Training and Testing), which consisted of two levels: a 10-fold subject-based outer training–test split for independent training and testing, and a 9-fold subject-based inner sub-training–validation split for feature selection optimization using DLI-WFS. We aggregated feature importance scores across all sub-training folds through majority voting and applied the most consistently selected features to the outer test fold. Final classification was performed using RF with the previously specified hyperparameters (see Section Hyperparameter Optimization) to maintain methodological consistency. We used accuracy as the final performance measure, as the number of samples per group was balanced during the training process for each classification task.

To identify robust dACC-involving FC features for interpretation, we implemented a two-stage filtering criterion: First, we retained only features demonstrating an average accuracy >0.8 across each classification comparison (BL obese vs. non-obese, PWL obese vs. non-obese, and 3MON obese vs. nonobese). Features failing to meet this accuracy criterion (<0.8) were excluded from further analysis as they likely reflected noise rather than biologically meaningful discriminative patterns. Second, from these high-accuracy classifications, we selected features that appeared in >5 of the 9 sub-training folds, thereby focusing our analysis on the most stable and consistently relevant connectivity features while avoiding overinterpretation of potentially spurious findings.

Statistics and reproducibility

Software: EEG data were acquired with a Mitsar-EEG 202 system using a 19-electrode 10–20 configuration and WinEEG software. The recorded EEG data were band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 45 Hz and imported into EureKa! Software (www.novatecheeg.com, 2001–2005) for manual artifact rejection, including eye blinks, ECG artifacts, and teeth clenching. ICoN software (www.novatecheeg.com, 2009) was used to perform independent component analysis to identify and remove artifact sources. We used the Exact Low-Resolution Brain Electromagnetic Tomography (eLORETA) method, implemented in the LORETA-KEY software package, for EEG source localization. All ML analyses were implemented using Python 3.6.7 with publicly available packages, including numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, SciPy, statistics, and matplotlib.

Statistical analyses: Statistical analyses were conducted using the stats module from the Python SciPy library. To verify whether our proposed ML model accurately classifies obesity rather than capturing spurious correlations, we performed a permutation test following established protocols57–59. In this procedure, 50% of the class labels were randomly shuffled while the feature matrix remained unchanged, effectively disrupting the true relationship between features and labels. This process was repeated 1000 times to generate a null distribution of accuracy values expected by chance. The model’s performance on the original, unshuffled data was then compared against this distribution. A significant difference indicates that the classifier identifies meaningful obesity-related connectivity patterns, confirming that its predictive accuracy is not driven by random associations. To select the appropriate significance test, we first applied the Shapiro–Wilk test to assess whether the data followed a normal distribution. Based on the data’s dependency and normality, we selected the appropriate test: a two-sided independent t-test and Cohen’s d were used to evaluate the significance and magnitude of differences in anthropometric measurements, insulin levels, and glucose levels between groups, given that the data were independent and normally distributed. A two-sided Mann–Whitney test was used to compare eating behaviour questionnaire scores between obese and non-obese groups. The paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were applied to assess whether adding features improved model performance and to evaluate model significance through permutation tests. The Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR correction) method was applied to adjust the significance threshold for multiple comparisons across all tests (except for the magnitude of differences in anthropometric measurements, insulin levels, glucose levels, and eating behaviour questionnaires).

Sample information: Details regarding sample sizes are described in Experimental Design section. All samples are considered biologically independent replicates.

Discussion

In contrast to previous hypothesis-based EEG studies60, this study applied a data-driven ML approach to explore the differences in FC between non-obese and obese individuals across different metabolic states and demonstrated that this is possible with a high degree of accuracy (Fig. 2).

This study has identified several novel and important findings. First, we demonstrated that 6 specific FC features are sufficient to accurately classify obesity, with the relationships varying depending on metabolic states and weight loss. Secondly, we have identified that the main hub in the obesity network is the dACC (Fig. 5). This finding is supported by previous functional imaging studies using EEG61,62, or fMRI63,64 as well as therapeutic studies targeting this area with non-invasive neuromodulation65. Intriguingly, we found that gamma-band dACC FC with both anterior BA22 (extending into the insula) and BA47 (lateral orbitofrontal cortex) remains unchanged after a meal (Fig. 7), in contrast to other connections. This suggests that disrupted communication between the dACC (involved in craving11) and both autonomic (insula12) and emotional (OFC) brain regions may represent the core neural dysfunction in obesity66. Furthermore, the strength of all FC features is decreased in the obese compared to non-obese individuals, suggesting that communication between the brain areas involved in craving and satiety processing is diminished (Fig. 6), resulting in a state in which energy requirements cannot be computed adequately. As a consequence, the brain may attempt to resolve the uncertainty by taking a ‘better safe than sorry’ approach and erring on the safe side, increasing food intake.

We also found that after weight loss, the FC pattern of individuals with obesity becomes more like that of non-obese individuals. This was shown by the reduction in accuracy in classifying obesity following the weight-loss period (Fig. 4). This difference is transient however and, as little as 3 months later, has returned to the pre-weight loss pattern despite weight loss being maintained. We hypothesize that this return to the pre-weight loss FC is responsible for the weight regain that is almost universal following weight loss62,67. However, the observed FC normalization during PWL could also potentially reflect the secondary effects of improved neurovascular function (e.g., altered cerebral perfusion or venous drainage following weight loss)68, or the transient changes in neurovascular coupling efficiency69. While our EEG-based methodology cannot directly distinguish these possibilities, the rapid reversion to BL FC patterns at 3MON suggests that enduring neural adaptations may outweigh transient vascular improvements. These findings underscore the importance of considering both neural and vascular factors when interpreting FC changes following weight-loss interventions.

Additionally, the above-noted resemblance with non-obese individuals seems to be especially profound at 60 min after food intake (Fig. 4), which would coincide with peak satiety. Although this may implicate hunger as a predominant factor in obesity, the persistence of significantly higher-than-chance level accuracy from 30 to 240 min postprandially does suggest that the different FC pattern may be a more general trait of obesity that cannot be fully ‘satisfied’ by eating.

From the brain network perspective, our results suggest an obesity-related network comprising brain regions previously associated with obesity10. The proposed network includes: (1) the salience network, involving the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC, BA32), the insula (medial extension of the anterior BA22), and the temporoparietal junction (TPJ, BA40); (2) regions within the somatomotor network, including BA3, BA4, and BA8; and (3) the affective network, comprising the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC, BA25), the temporopolar area (BA38), and the lateral orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, BA47). Moreover, the central executive network (CEN) is also involved, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC, BA44). Consistent with prior research10, our results suggest that obesity is associated with abnormal salience attribution to food, bodily self-processing, emotional regulation, and cognitive control—networks and functions that are expected to be involved in obesity. Notably, while the parahippocampal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) are established obesity-related regions10, they were not identified in our feature selection. This likely reflects the ML model’s exclusion of redundant features when other regions provide sufficient discriminatory power. For example, the sgACC (BA25L) and parahippocampal cortex jointly comprise an aversive-motivational network70, suggesting the retained sgACC may sufficiently represent this circuit’s role in encoding hunger as an aversive state. Similarly, the TPJ (BA40L) and PCC both contribute to self-referential processing71; the model’s selection of TPJ may have obviated PCC inclusion. These observations align with the dACC-involving connectivity analyses, which involve both parahippocampal and PCC regions in distinguishing obese from non-obese individuals.

On the other hand, the observed pattern of dACC-involving FC classification accuracy across different weight stages suggests dynamic shifts in the neural processes distinguishing obese and non-obese individuals (Table 3). At BL, dACC connectivity differentiates groups specifically during satiety, involving BA3 (somatosensory processing), BA30 (mental self-representation), BA38 (emotional regulation), BA8 (motor planning), and BA10 (predictive decision-making). This aligns with the hypothesis that obesity may involve altered integration of bodily signals, emotional responses, and predictive energy-state evaluations during satiation10. The involvement of BA10, linked to Bayesian reliability computation, further suggests an impaired resolution of satiety-related prediction errors in obesity64. Notably, these regions connecting to dACC also form a network particularly sensitive to individual differences in stress reactivity and personality traits like impulsivity72, which may modulate the observed connectivity patterns and contribute to variability in eating behaviours. The loss of high classification accuracy at PWL implies that short-term weight normalization may transiently attenuate these distinctive connectivity patterns, possibly reflecting acute metabolic or behavioural adaptations. However, the re-emergence of robust classification at 3 months—across fasting (T0), satiety (T60), and hunger (T240) states—indicates a persistent, state-independent dysregulation in obese individuals despite initial weight loss. The involvement of BA46 (working memory), BA17/20 (visual and associative processing), and BA40/7 (sensorimotor integration) during fasting, alongside BA3/30 (recurring at satiety), suggests a broader, more entrenched disruption in interoceptive, cognitive, and sensory integration that may underlie both metabolic and psychological aspects of obesity maintenance. Notably, the recruitment of BA42/36 (auditory and memory-related regions) during hunger at 3MON may reflect compensatory mechanisms or maladaptive reinforcement of food-cue associations, potentially mediated by stress-related or trait-dependent modulation of dACC function72. Together, these findings highlight the dACC’s role as a nexus sustaining obesity-related neural phenotypes, integrating not only metabolic signals but also stress responses and personality related decision-making biases. The early post-intervention changes may mask longer-term stability in this dysfunctional network engagement, which appears resistant to weight loss alone and may require targeted interventions addressing both metabolic and psychological factors.

Our findings can also be interpreted through a Bayesian brain framework, wherein the brain iteratively updates its internal representations through recurrent predictions refined by sensory evidence, progressively improving the precision of interoceptive and exteroceptive inferences73. Within this framework, hunger emerges from persistent prediction errors between anticipated and actual energy states, motivating food-seeking behaviour74, while satiety reflects the resolution of these errors through food intake75. The observed reductions in gamma-band FC between the dACC (BA32), insula (BA22), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, BA47) across metabolic all states suggest a fundamental disruption in predictive error signalling. Gamma oscillations are posited to communicate prediction errors76, with the dACC computing energy demands and behavioural adjustments62,77, the insula monitoring interoceptive hunger/satiety states78, and the OFC assigning reward values based on prior experience60. The attenuated gamma-band FC in the obesity brain network implies three interrelated deficits: rigid predictive coding where top-down expectations (e.g., habitual overeating) dominate over bottom-up interoceptive signals; blunted error signalling that reduces sensitivity to food-reward mismatches; and context-insensitive behaviour due to impaired shifting between exploitative and exploratory strategies. These findings collectively support the conceptualization of obesity as a disorder of predictive homoeostasis.

The observed delta-band FC reductions further elucidate these mechanisms (40L-4R, 25L-5R). In the Bayesian framework, delta oscillations mediate the temporal integration of top-down intentions with sensory evidence over extended timescales, therefore, delta oscillations reflect the reliability or precision of the intention79. Specifically, diminished delta coupling between the temporoparietal junction (TPJ, BA40) and superior frontal cortex (BA4) suggests compromised reliability of sustained attentional engagement, exacerbating cognitive and motor impulsivity80. Concurrently, reduced delta synchrony between the subgenual anterior cingulate (sgACC, BA25) and secondary somatosensory cortex (BA5) indicates aberrant interoceptive reward processing, potentially driving automatic overconsumption81. These delta-band disturbances reflect impaired hierarchical binding between intentional control and interoceptive valuation, further compromising adaptive decision-making in obese individuals.

Complementing these findings, reduced beta-band FC between the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC, BA44) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (BA8) reveals impaired top-down attentional control over food-related impulses. Beta oscillations in these regions typically maintain goal-directed representations while suppressing competing motivations76,82. The weakened beta coupling may specifically undermine volitional attention redirection from food cues, permitting continued reward-seeking despite intentional suppression efforts. This mirrors the gamma-band deficits, as both reflect hierarchical failures in error minimization—gamma in reward prediction and beta in inhibitory control.

Taken together, the diminished gamma, delta, and beta-band FC in obesity represent multiscale disruptions in predictive coding. Gamma-band alterations blunt reward prediction errors, perpetuating unresolved energy demands; delta-band deficits disrupt intention-action coupling, fostering impulsivity; and beta-band attenuation weakens inhibitory control, undermining dietary restraint. This tripartite oscillatory dysfunction suggests obesity is characterized by a pervasive failure in hierarchical Bayesian inference, extending beyond food-related contexts to broader decision-making domains. The persistent miscalibration of energy predictions may create a self-reinforcing cycle promoting excessive intake.

Building upon our findings of multiscale oscillatory disruptions in Bayesian predictive networks, we propose that neuromodulation therapies could specifically target: (1) the dorsolateral prefrontal-temporal parietal junction (DLPFC-TPJ) circuit to mitigate impulsive eating behaviours by enhancing delta-band synchronization of intentional control; (2) the insula-dACC axis to normalize prediction error computation during cost-benefit analysis (gamma-band); and (3) the temporal pole-sgACC-OFC-premotor network to recalibrate motivation-action coupling for feeding behaviours (beta/gamma-band). Practically, such precision neuromodulation could employ Hebbian-based protocols using transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS)11,12 or closed-loop neurofeedback66,67, with frequency-specific interventions tailored to each circuit’s oscillatory profile.

Our study has several limitations. First, EEG primarily captures cortical activity and cannot reliably detect deeper brain regions83, limiting comparisons with fMRI and PET studies. Second, the low spatial resolution of LORETA, particularly with 19-channel EEG, may introduce localization uncertainty. However, while LORETA’s resolution is inferior to fMRI and may struggle with hippocampal and fine cortical grey matter delineation84,85, its consistency with invasive intracranial recordings38 and cognitive ERPs86 supports its validity for cortical and subcortical regions, including the anterior cingulate and mesial temporal lobe87,88. Moreover, previous studies investigating EEG patterns associated with migraine89 and epilepsy90 have supported the validity of applying LORETA to 19-channel EEG recordings. Furthermore, the ML-based, data-driven approach used in this study complements LORETA’s spatial limitations by identifying reproducible and discriminative patterns across the entire dataset, rather than relying on single-subject source localization. The model’s generalizability to unseen data also helps mitigate potential inaccuracies associated with 19-channel EEG. The consistency of our findings with prior neurobiological research further supports the methodological validity of our study. Third, while ML effectively identifies FC features differentiating obesity, it does not establish causal relationships. Fourth, our study includes only female subjects to avoid sex-related confounders91, potentially limiting generalizability to males. Additionally, it should be noted that patients with significant psychiatric comorbidities—common among individuals with obesity16,18,19—were excluded from this study. Further research is therefore required to determine whether our findings are generalizable to this important patient group and to explore integrated treatment approaches that address both metabolic and mental health components to improve long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a data-driven ML approach can classify obesity with a high accuracy of 95% using 6 FC features across different metabolic states. Obesity is linked to significantly reduced connectivity between areas involved in craving and satiety. Notably, the dACC, a key region in food craving, remains a central node in this network, as its connectivity does not adapt in response to food intake. From a Bayesian brain perspective, this may indicate that the brain experiences uncertainty in predicting energy requirements, resulting in maladaptive food cravings and overeating. These findings underscore the critical role of disrupted brain connectivity in obesity and support further investigation into neuromodulation-based treatments targeting the dACC.
