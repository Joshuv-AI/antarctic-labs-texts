Clinical characteristics and surgical management of rare thoracic myelolipoma: experience from a single center with literature review
Authors: Zeguang Ye, Xi Wu, Can Fang, Mingchuang Zhu, Peng Xiong, Min Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyelolipoma is a rare benign tumor composed of mature adipose and hematopoietic tissue, most commonly occurring in the adrenal glands. Thoracic myelolipoma is extremely uncommon, particularly when originating in the lung or chest wall, and is frequently misdiagnosed due to its nonspecific clinical and radiological features. This study aimed to retrospectively characterize the clinical, imaging, pathological, and surgical features of thoracic myelolipoma and to conduct a comprehensive literature review to improve understanding of this rare entity.MethodsThirteen cases of thoracic myelolipoma diagnosed at Tongji Hospital between October 2015 and January 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. Data collected included patient demographics, clinical presentation, imaging characteristics, surgical approach, histopathology, and outcomes. Additionally, a comprehensive literature review was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, covering reports published from 1960 to 2025. Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis.ResultsThirteen patients were included (mean age: 57.2 ± 8.0 years; 6 men). Nine patients (69.2%) presented with tumors in the posterior mediastinum, and four (30.8%) had tumors in the lung. Most lesions were well-circumscribed and incidentally detected. Eleven patients (84.6%) underwent uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), and all cases were histopathologically confirmed as myelolipoma. No recurrence or metastasis was observed over a mean follow-up period of 40.5 ± 31.0 months. The literature review identified 71 additional cases with comparable clinical characteristics. Among the 33 patients with available follow-up, no recurrence or metastasis was reported.ConclusionsThoracic myelolipoma is a rare, indolent tumor that is often discovered incidentally and is generally associated with a favorable prognosis following complete surgical resection. VATS represents a minimally invasive and effective approach, offering both diagnostic and therapeutic benefits. Conservative observation may be appropriate for asymptomatic patients with characteristic imaging findings. However, surgical intervention is recommended when clinical symptoms are present or malignancy cannot be ruled out. Further large-scale studies with extended follow-up are warranted to refine management strategies.Keywords: Thoracic myelolipoma, Mediastinal myelolipoma, Pulmonary myelolipoma, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), Literature review

Introduction

Myelolipoma is a rare benign tumor composed of mature adipose tissue and hematopoietic elements [1, 2]. It most commonly arises in the adrenal glands, particularly in adults [1, 3]. However, approximately 15% of cases arise in extra-adrenal locations, and only around 3% involve the thoracic region [4, 5]. Thoracic myelolipoma typically arises in the posterior mediastinum, whereas pulmonary myelolipoma is exceedingly rare and remains poorly characterized in the literature [4, 6]. Due to its rarity, nonspecific clinical presentation, and variable imaging features, thoracic myelolipoma is frequently misdiagnosed preoperatively [7]. This retrospective study aimed to summarize the clinical, radiological, pathological, and surgical characteristics of thoracic myelolipoma cases treated at our institution, and further incorporated a comprehensive literature review to enhance understanding of this uncommon entity.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study included all patients diagnosed with thoracic myelolipoma at Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology between October 2015 and January 2025. Eligible cases were identified based on thoracic masses detected by imaging and subsequently confirmed as myelolipoma through histopathological examination. Clinical data, including demographic characteristics, presenting symptoms, imaging findings, surgical details, pathological results, and prognostic outcomes, were obtained by reviewing electronic medical records and through direct communication with patients or their family members.

All patients were followed up until August 1, 2025. During follow-up, clinical status was assessed at each visit, with particular attention to evidence of recurrence or metastasis. For patients who experienced no clinical events, follow-up continued until the date of last contact. For those with disease progression, recurrence, or death, follow-up ended at the time of the event.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IRB202505030).

Methodology of literature review

Electronic searches

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases to identify relevant studies published between January 1, 1960, and May 1, 2025. The following keywords and their combinations were used: “myelolipoma,” “pulmonary,” “mediastinal,” “rib,” and “chest wall.” All English-language case reports and case series describing thoracic myelolipoma were considered eligible and included for full-text review.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) reported at least one case of thoracic myelolipoma; (2) involved tumors located in the thoracic region, including the mediastinum, lung, rib or chest wall; (3) provided histopathological confirmation of myelolipoma.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-English language publications; (2) cases lacking essential clinical information, such as patient age, sex, tumor location, or size.

Data extraction and management

Two independent reviewers extracted data related to patient demographics, tumor location and size, imaging features, pathological findings, treatment modalities, and clinical outcomes. Additional details including author names and year of publication were also recorded. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus to minimize bias.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize clinical, radiological, pathological, and treatment-related data. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Given the small sample size and the exploratory nature of this retrospective case series, only descriptive statistics were reported, and no inferential statistical tests were performed. All data analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Results of the case series

Characteristics of the patients

Between October 2015 and January 2025, a total of 13 patients were diagnosed with thoracic myelolipoma at our institution based on thoracic masses identified by imaging and confirmed by histopathological examination. Among them, 4 patients (30.8%, 4/13) had pulmonary myelolipoma, including 1 case (7.7%, 1/13) with an endobronchial lesion (Case 1), and 9 patients (69.2%, 9/13) had posterior mediastinal myelolipoma. The cohort consisted of 6 males (46.2%, 6/13) and 7 females (53.8%, 7/13), with a mean age at diagnosis of 57.2 ± 8.0 years (range: 43–73 years).

Presenting symptoms were variable and nonspecific: cough in 1 patient (7.7%, 1/13), numbness of the right fingers in 1 (7.7%, 1/13), chest tightness in 2 (15.4%, 2/13), chest pain in 2 (15.4%, 2/13), back pain in 2 (15.4%, 2/13), and no symptoms in 5 patients (38.5%, 5/13), in whom the tumors were incidentally detected during routine imaging. Regarding hematological findings, 7 patients (53.8%, 7/13) had mild or moderate anemia, and 6 patients (46.2%, 6/13) had no anemia. Radiological imaging characteristics

Preoperative imaging evaluations included chest computed tomography (CT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Most lesions demonstrated well-defined margins. The average maximum tumor diameter was 33.8 ± 16.1 mm. Two patients (15.4%, 2/13) underwent both CT and MRI, ten patients (76.9%, 10/13) received CT only, and one patient (7.7%, 1/13) had MRI only. Among the 4 cases (30.8%, 4/13) of pulmonary myelolipoma, lesions were located in the left upper lobe, left lower lobe, right upper lobe, and right lower lobe, respectively. In the 9 mediastinal cases (69.2%, 9/13), tumors were all located between the T8–T11 vertebral levels, with 2 cases (22.2%, 2/9) involving bilateral posterior mediastinum. The majority were found in the right thoracic cavity. These cases showed variable degrees of enhancement on contrast-enhanced CT. Representative CT images of four patients with posterior mediastinal myelolipomas are illustrated in Fig. 2. Surgical procedures and pathological findings

Among the 13 patients, 11 (84.6%, 11/13) underwent uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), while 2 patients (15.4%, 2/13) required conversion to or initial open thoracotomy due to extensive pleural adhesions or challenging anatomical location. The average operative duration was 109.8 ± 37.3 min.

In the 4 patients with pulmonary myelolipoma, 2 (50.0%, 2/4) underwent segmentectomy, 1 (25.0%, 1/4) underwent wedge resection, and 1 (25.0%, 1/4) underwent lobectomy. Intraoperative frozen section analysis was performed in all 4 pulmonary cases. One case (25.0%, 1/4) was initially interpreted as a lymphohematopoietic lesion, and the other three (75.0%, 3/4) were considered benign lesions. Subsequently, final postoperative histopathological examinations confirmed the diagnosis of myelolipoma in all 13 patients. Microscopically, all tumors were composed of mature adipose tissue admixed with hematopoietic elements, including erythroid, granulocytic, and megakaryocytic lineages (Fig. 4).

Postoperative outcomes and follow-up

No major postoperative complications were observed. Only two patients (15.4%, 2/13) developed mild subcutaneous emphysema, which is a common postoperative finding in thoracic surgery. None of the patients received adjuvant therapy after surgery. As of the last follow-up, no local recurrence or distant metastasis was observed in any patient. The mean follow-up duration was 40.5 ± 31.0 months.

Results of the literature review

A total of 71 cases of thoracic myelolipoma were ultimately included in this study through a comprehensive literature search of the PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases. The detailed selection process is summarized in the flowchart (Fig. 5). Initially, 233 records were identified. After removing duplicates, 144 unique records remained. Following full-text review, 88 articles that did not report thoracic myelolipoma were excluded. From the remaining 56 articles, 82 individual cases were identified. After excluding 3 duplicate cases and 8 cases with missing essential clinical data, 71 cases were included in the final analysis.

Mediastinal myelolipomas accounted for the majority of cases (78.9%, 56/71), with the posterior mediastinum being the most common location (89.3%, 50/56), and a predominance on the right side (55.4%, 31/56). Pulmonary myelolipomas represented 15.5% of cases (15.5%, 11/71), while chest wall and rib lesions were rare (5.6%, 4/71).

The majority of cases (83.1%, 59/71) were diagnosed through surgical resection, most commonly via minimally invasive approaches such as VATS or robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) (53.5%, 38/71). Additionally, six cases (8.5%, 6/71) were diagnosed through biopsy (CT-guided, ultrasound-guided, or bronchoscopic), and another six cases (8.5%, 6/71) were identified at autopsy.

Postoperative follow-up data were available for 33 patients (46.5%, 33/71). The mean follow-up duration was 25.8 ± 23.3 months. No recurrences or metastases were reported during the follow-up period.

Discussion

Myelolipoma is a rare, benign, and non-functioning tumor that typically occurs in adults [1, 8, 9]. It was first described by Gierke in 1905 and later named by Oberling in 1929 [10, 11]. Myelolipomas are generally asymptomatic and often detected incidentally through imaging or autopsy [5, 12]. However, when sufficiently large, they may cause symptoms due to compression of adjacent structures [13, 14]. Histologically, a myelolipoma consists of mature adipose tissue intermixed with varying proportions of normal hematopoietic elements [15].

Myelolipomas are most commonly located in the adrenal glands [1, 5, 9, 12]. Extra-adrenal myelolipomas are uncommon and have been reported in the retroperitoneum, stomach, liver, presacral region, and other sites, accounting for approximately 15% of all myelolipomas [16–18]. Only about 3% occur in the thoracic cavity, with the majority arising in the posterior mediastinum [4, 5]. Our systematic review is consistent with these findings, revealing that approximately 70.4% of thoracic myelolipomas occur in the posterior mediastinum, predominantly on the right side. Additionally, 15.5% were located in the lungs, and 5.6% arose from the ribs or chest wall. Some previous studies have suggested that myelolipomas are more common in women aged 50 to 70 years, with a reported female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1 [3, 19]. However, other reports have indicated a higher incidence in males [7]. Our literature-based retrospective analysis aligns with the latter findings, showing a greater proportion of male patients, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1.4:1.

Presentation and pathogenesis

Most patients with myelolipoma are asymptomatic and non-functioning in terms of hormonal activity [7, 20]. Extra-adrenal myelolipomas are typically well-circumscribed, slow-growing masses that may cause nonspecific symptoms such as local pain or discomfort due to mechanical compression from mass effect, and in some cases, hemorrhage [9, 19, 21, 22]. According to published case reports, thoracic myelolipomas are more commonly associated with symptoms such as cough, back pain, and chest pain [23, 24].

The exact pathogenesis of myelolipoma remains incompletely understood, and several hypotheses have been proposed to explain its development. A widely accepted view is that chronic inflammation and hormonal influences play a significant role in the formation of myelolipomas [4, 6, 13]. The condition has been associated with chronic diseases such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, and endocrine disorders [5, 25]. For instance, chronic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation observed in Cushing’s disease has been implicated in the development of myelolipomas, suggesting that elevated ACTH levels may contribute to tumorigenesis by promoting the proliferation of adipose and hematopoietic tissue within the adrenal gland [26]. Additionally, in patients with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), the accumulation of ACTH and androgens may induce chronic inflammatory responses that further promote myelolipoma formation [27, 28]. Moreover, a few reports have described myelolipomas occurring in patients with chronic anemias, such as thalassemia, sickle cell disease, and hereditary spherocytosis [29–32]. It has been speculated that chronic anemia may lead to persistently elevated erythropoietin levels, which in turn stimulate hematopoietic proliferation and contribute to the development of myelolipoma [29]. Another theory posits that myelolipomas arise from metaplastic transformation of embryonic primitive mesenchymal cells or from bone marrow emboli that lodge in ectopic locations via the bloodstream [33–36]. Characteristic chromosomal abnormalities such as translocations involving 3q25 to 21p11 or 21p41 have also been reported, and may be associated with hematologic malignancies like acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndrome [5, 33, 37]. A further hypothesis suggests that the tumor mass is often adherent to vertebral structures, and that hematopoietic tissue may extend into the paravertebral space through microfractures of the vertebral body [5, 38, 39].

These four hypotheses represent the main current theories, but further research is needed to fully elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying the development of myelolipoma.

Examinations and diagnosis

Due to the lack of specific radiological features and clinical manifestations, the preoperative diagnosis of thoracic myelolipoma remains a clinical challenge [19]. Although CT and MRI are valuable tools for detecting thoracic myelolipomas, definitive diagnosis based solely on imaging remains difficult [33, 40].

CT typically reveals thoracic myelolipomas as well-defined, encapsulated masses composed of both adipose and hematopoietic tissue [41, 42]. These lesions often demonstrate heterogeneous density due to the presence of multiple tissue components. Fat typically exhibits attenuation values ranging from − 120 to − 20 hounsfield units (HU), soft tissue components range from 20 to 50 HU, and hematopoietic elements generally fall between 15 and 40 HU [33, 40, 41]. In some cases, calcifications and hemorrhagic areas can also be observed [41, 42]. Although some tumors may grow to a considerable size, they usually do not invade adjacent structures. However, as they enlarge, they may cause displacement of surrounding tissues and organs to varying degrees [22, 40].

MRI plays a valuable role in characterizing thoracic myelolipomas and differentiating their internal components [33]. These lesions typically demonstrate a well-defined margin with heterogeneous signal intensities reflecting a combination of fat and hematopoietic elements. Fatty components show high signal intensity on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences, while hematopoietic components are usually hypointense on T1-weighted images and intermediate to mildly hyperintense on T2-weighted images [19, 33, 40]. In some cases, a variegated signal pattern is evident, corresponding to the admixture of mature adipose tissue and myeloid elements. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI may reveal mild enhancement in the hematopoietic regions, improving lesion characterization [5, 22]. MRI can also aid in evaluating lesion extent and its potential involvement of adjacent structures, which is particularly important in the preoperative assessment of larger mediastinal or paraspinal tumors [33].

Histopathological evaluation remains the gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of thoracic myelolipoma [1, 4, 43, 44]. The hallmark histologic feature of myelolipoma is the admixture of mature adipose tissue and trilineage hematopoietic elements, including erythroid, granulocytic, and megakaryocytic lineages [4, 9, 13]. For lesions located in the chest wall, subpleural areas, or regions distant from major blood vessels, ultrasound- or CT-guided biopsy can be performed to achieve a definitive diagnosis [39, 40, 45, 46]. For endobronchial lesions, bronchoscopic biopsy or complete removal via bronchoscopy may be feasible approaches [4, 24]. However, percutaneous biopsy of deeply located tumors can be technically challenging due to anatomical constraints and carries potential risks such as bleeding and pneumothorax [33]. In such cases, surgical resection remains a valuable option, serving both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes [23, 33].

Management and outcomes

The management of thoracic myelolipoma is primarily individualized, guided by clinical presentation and imaging characteristics rather than tumor size alone. As most lesions are non-functional, well-encapsulated, and incidentally discovered, asymptomatic patients with radiologically typical findings may be safely observed through active surveillance [1, 4, 19]. However, surgical resection is generally recommended for patients presenting with clinical symptoms such as chest pain, dyspnea, or neurological manifestations caused by mechanical compression, or when imaging features raise suspicion of malignancy [5, 6, 23].

Surgical excision not only provides definitive diagnosis but also serves as curative treatment. The specific surgical approach should be tailored according to tumor location, size, and intra-thoracic conditions. For pulmonary myelolipomas, wedge resection may be feasible when the tumor is located peripherally. Segmentectomy can be considered for smaller, deeply situated lesions, whereas lobectomy may be required for larger tumors. Minimally invasive approaches, particularly VATS, have become the preferred method due to their safety, reduced trauma, and quicker recovery [5, 33, 40]. Nevertheless, open thoracotomy remains necessary in select cases, especially for tumors adjacent to major vessels or with extensive adhesions, as seen in one of our institutional patients who had a history of tuberculosis. Intraoperatively, mediastinal myelolipomas often appear as well-demarcated, hypervascular masses, predominantly supplied by intercostal arteries or branches of the thoracic aorta, making vascular control a key consideration during resection [6, 40].

In our institutional cohort of 13 patients, all tumors were completely resected. No adjuvant therapy was administered, and no recurrence or metastasis was observed during a mean follow-up of 40.5 ± 31.0 months. Consistently, among the 71 cases identified from our literature review, 33 had documented follow-up, with a mean duration of 25.8 ± 23.3 months, and none experienced recurrence or metastasis. These outcomes reinforce the benign nature of thoracic myelolipomas and the effectiveness of surgical resection as a definitive treatment in appropriately selected cases [4–6, 33, 40].

This study has several limitations. Owing to the extreme rarity of thoracic myelolipoma, the sample size was relatively small and the study was retrospective in nature. Although this inevitably limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings, our case series is relatively large for a single-institution study and was supplemented by a rigorously screened literature review to enhance the robustness of the data. In addition, the follow-up duration varied among patients, and only descriptive analyses were performed, precluding causal inference. Nevertheless, the present study provides valuable insights into the clinical, radiological, pathological, and surgical characteristics of this rare entity, and may serve as a useful reference for future multi-center studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up.

Conclusions

Thoracic myelolipoma is a rare benign tumor, most frequently located in the posterior mediastinum, with less common occurrences in the lung, chest wall, or ribs. Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging due to nonspecific imaging features, and definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological confirmation. Most cases are incidentally discovered and generally have a favorable prognosis following complete surgical resection. VATS is a commonly used minimally invasive approach that provides both diagnostic clarity and therapeutic benefit. While conservative observation may be appropriate for asymptomatic patients, surgical resection is recommended when symptoms are present or malignancy cannot be ruled out.
