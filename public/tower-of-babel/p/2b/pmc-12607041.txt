Study on the physicochemical properties, biological activities and metabolic profile alterations of jujube juice fermented by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum
Authors: Yuhan Zhou, Yanchen Wei, Yuxi Wang, Zichun An, Qiyu Ma, Huaxi Yi, Zhe Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study compared the fermentation of four strains of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (L. plantarum) using Xinjiang Hami jujube as raw material. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum fermentation significantly altered the physicochemical properties. All strains significantly reduced its acidity and enriched the mouthfeel of jujube juice. L. plantarum fermentation increased the total polyphenol content of jujube juice, while the contents of reducing sugar and total flavonoids showed a downward trend. All strains exhibited enhanced antioxidant capacity, with the FWDG strain showing the most significant change. Non-targeted metabolomics analysis further revealed that jujube juice of L. plantarum FWDG fermentation was enriched with amino acids, organic acids and functional active substance, including Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide(NAD), N-lactoyl-phenylalanine and dextran. This study analysed the effects of L. plantarum on the physicochemical changes and functions of jujube juice, the corresponding metabolite composition. These findings provide a foundation for the development of fermented jujube juice products with antioxidant functions.Keywords: Jujube juice, Fermentation, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Antioxidant capacity, Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Introduction

Ziziphus jujuba, commonly known as red jujube or Chinese jujube, is a plant belonging to the Rhamnaceae family (Paudel, Poudeyal, & Devkota, 2023). Jujube is rich in natural bioactive nutrients and is an important functional food ingredient with high levels of antioxidants and active compounds that benefit both digestion and sleep (Bae et al., 2023). China is the world's leading producer of jujube, accounting for over 90 % of global production. The temperate continental climate of Xinjiang region, characterised by significant temperature fluctuations and abundant sunshine, provides optimal conditions for jujube cultivation. Consequently, the quality of jujube from Xinjiang surpasses that of jujube from other regions. Although demand for jujube is high in China, direct consumption is often limited by its excessive sweetness and poor storability (Muqaddas et al., 2025). Moreover, the domestic jujube industry faces challenges such as limited scale and low added value. To enhance the economic value of jujube and its associated health benefits, there is a growing need to develop novel technologies for enriching jujube-based products.

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) was reported to enhance antioxidant properties and the flavour profile of functional beverages (Yan et al., 2023). Fermentation with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (L. plantarum) is proven to be an effective method for enhancing the nutritional value, biological activity and flavour profile of food. Currently, with the expansion of the functional food market, beverages made from fruits and vegetables fermented with L. plantarum have garnered significant attention, and jujube is gradually being used as a major ingredient in food and dietary supplements (Men et al., 2019). Fermented jujube juice exhibits considerably enhanced anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Fermentation with L. plantarum also markedly increased total polyphenol content and antioxidant activity in honeysuckle juice, improving its overall quality (Ran et al., 2024).

Fermentation with L. plantarum can considerably improve the quality and value of fruit and vegetable products. L. plantarum is a gram-positive, non-motile probiotic that is resistant to bacteriophages. L. plantarum–fermented jujube juice can restore intestinal flora balance following inflammation and alleviate colitis (Li et al., 2025). Moreover, during L. plantarum fermentation of fruit and vegetable juices, bioactive metabolites such as polyphenols and prebiotics are synthesised, and these compounds can improve digestion, boost immune system, and promote intestinal health (D'Almeida, Neta, de Andrade-Lima, & de Albuquerque, 2024). Although the effects of fermentation with L. plantarum on the quality and functionality of fruit and vegetable juices are well documented, few studies have explored the physicochemical changes and functional properties resulting from L. plantarum fermentation of Hami jujubes, and research on the promising strain remains limited.

Therefore, in this study, Hami jujubes were selected as the food matrix. To identify strains with the potential to ferment jujubes, comparisons were made across four L. plantarum fermentations in terms of viable bacterial counts, electronic tongue scores, reducing sugar content, total polyphenol content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and antioxidant indices. Additionally, non-targeted metabolomics analysis was performed to investigate the metabolites produced in jujube juices by the promising strain after fermentation. The findings will provide a theoretical basis for developing functional fermented jujube juices with enhanced antioxidant properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

Red jujubes were sourced from Hami Prefecture, Xinjiang. L. plantarum were obtained from the Functional Dairy and Probiotics Engineering Laboratory, Ocean University of China. De Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) medium was purchased from Qingdao Hope Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Pectinase and cellulase were acquired from Heshibi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Ningxia, China). The 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl radical (DPPH) radical scavenging assay kit, antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) kit, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) determination kit were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The reducing sugar reagent kit was purchased from Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, reducing sugar content detection kit, and phosphate buffer solution were acquired from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The total flavonoid kit was procured from Jiangsu Addison Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

Strains and cultures

Four strains of L. plantarum with fermentation potential, i.e., FWDG, SC-1.1, SC-1.3, and M1–12, were isolated from a previous spontaneous fermentation of jujube fruit. These experimental strains, which had been preserved in glycerol at −80 °C at the Functional Dairy and Probiotics Engineering Laboratory of Ocean University of China, were transferred to MRS liquid medium for activation and resuscitation and then incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. After centrifugation at 6000×g for 10 min at 10 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and the lower layer of L. plantarum was washed with sterile phosphate buffer and re-suspended as the inoculum to be added to jujube juice.

Preparation and fermentation of jujube juice

Hami jujube juice was prepared based on methods described in previous research, with appropriate modifications (Li et al., 2021). In brief, after removing the pits, the fruit were subjected to hot-air drying in an oven at 50 °C for 48 h. The dried fruit were then ground into a powder using a high-speed pulveriser and mixed with drinking water at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:4 (w/v). The mix was subjected to enzymatic treatment by adding a compound enzyme preparation consisting of pectinase at 2.4 g/L and cellulase at 4.8 g/L, followed by digestion in a water bath at 50 °C for 4 h. The enzyme-treated mixture was centrifuged at 6000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the precipitate was discarded and the supernatant (upper layer of clear jujube juice) was retained. The initial pH and °Brix of the obtained jujube juice were adjusted to 5.0 and 15.0°Brix using food-grade sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) and drinking water, respectively. The juice was sterilised using an autoclave (LDZX-75 KB, Shanghai Shen'an Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 121 °C for 15 min prior to fermentation, followed by cooling to room temperature. Different L. plantarum bacterial suspensions were inoculated into sterilised jujube juice at a ratio of 2:100 (v/v), followed by fermentation in a constant temperature incubator (SHP-250, Shanghai Jinghong Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 48 h to obtain fermented jujube juice. All fermentation processes were conducted independently in triplicate.

pH and viable cell count

The pH of the fermented jujube juices was measured every 12 h using a pH meter (PB-10, Sartorius, Germany). Viable L. plantarum counts during fermentation were determined based on methods described in a previous study (Roberts et al., 2018). In brief, A 0.1 mL aliquot of jujube juice was subjected to serial dilution in 0.9 mL of sterile 0.9 % (w/v) saline, yielding dilutions from 10−6 to 10−12. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of the diluted jujube juice was inoculated onto MRS agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Colonies exhibiting 30–300 colony forming units (CFU) per plate were selected and recorded. Viable cell counts were expressed as CFU/mL.

Electronic tongue

The sensory attributes of jujube juice fermented by different bacterial strains, including sourness, sweetness, bitterness, freshness, and abundance, were assessed using a taste analyser (SA402B Plus-EX, INSENT, Japan). Four samples of the fermentation broth were measured, triple-diluted with distilled water, thoroughly mixed, and subjected to taste analysis in a specialized sample cup designed for the e-tongue instrument. Each sample was analysed in triplicate.

Determination of °brix and reducing sugar content

°Brix was measured at 25 °C using a digital refractometer (SW-32D, Speedwell Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China), whereas reducing sugar content was determined using a reducing sugar content assay kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Determination of total polyphenol and total flavonoid contents

TPC was assessed using the Folin–Ciocalteu method. In brief, 1 mL of fermented jujube juice, 1 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, 3 mL of Na2CO3 solution, and 5 mL of distilled water were separately pipetted into a test tube and thoroughly mixed. The reaction mixture was vortexed and allowed to react for 5 min, followed by colour development for 30 min at room temperature in a dark environment. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 720 nm. A calibration curve was constructed using varying concentrations of gallic acid monohydrate as the standard. TFC was determined using a commercial kit in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of antioxidant capacity

Ferric reducing ability of plasma (FRAP), superoxide dismutase (SOD) content, 2,2′-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), and DPPH free radical scavenging capacity were assayed using specific kits in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

The alterations in jujube juice metabolites pre – and post-fermentation were examined via non-targeted metabolomics. Briefly, 200 μL of sample was accurately dispensed, and 800 μL of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile = 1:1 v/v) with L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL) as the internal standard was added. After vortexing for 30 s, the mixture was sonicated at 5 °C and 40 KHz for 30 min. Following a 30-min incubation at −20 °C, samples were centrifuged at 13,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was then collected and dried under nitrogen. Subsequently, 120 μL of reconstituted solution (acetonitrile:water = 1:1) was added. After vortexing for 30 s and ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C and 40 KHz for 5 min, samples were centrifuged again under the same conditions for 10 min. The supernatants were transferred to vials for analysis. Moreover, 20 μL of supernatant from each sample was pooled to create quality control samples. The LC-MS analysis was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific UHPLC-QExactive HF-X system. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA) was employed. The mobile phase consisted of A (95 % water + 5 % acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid) and B (47.5 % acetonitrile + 47.5 % isopropanol + 5 % water with 0.1 % formic acid). The injection volume was 3 μL, and the column temperature was set at 40 °C. In terms of mass spectrometry, electrospray ionisation was utilised, and data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes.

Statistical analysis

Results are reported as the mean ± standard deviation, based on at least three independent experiments. For each experiment, three technical repeats were performed to ensure repeatability. Significant differences between means were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA), with intergroup differences assessed via Duncan's test. p < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences. The data were plotted using GraphPad Prism software (version10.0) (San Diego, California, USA).

Results and discussion

pH and viable bacterial counts in fermented jujube juice

The number of L. plantarum is a key factor influencing fermentation, as it substantially affects the quality and stability of the final product. Additionally, viable L. plantarum counts serve as an accurate metric for evaluating the adaptability of L. plantarum to their substrate (Chen, Xie, He, Sun, & Bai, 2023).

Because fermentation with L. plantarum involves organic acid production, the pH of the four jujube juices decreased significantly within the first 24 h of fermentation. By approximately 48 h, the pH gradually stabilised at 3.85, below the initial value of 5.00 (Fig. 1). This decrease in pH demonstrated efficient and rapid acid-producing capability of L. plantarum. All four strains exhibited viable cell counts exceeding 1.00 × 108 CFU/mL after fermentation (Table 1). Furthermore, at the end of fermentation, the viable cell count exceeded 108 CFU/mL, a level associated with probiotic functions and health benefits (Castellone et al., 2021). The increase in viable bacterial counts following fermentation indicated that all four strains were capable of growing in jujube juice.

E-tongue assessment of fermented jujube juice

Taste, an essential criterion for assessing the quality of fruit and vegetable juices, significantly affects consumer preference for fermented products. Electronic tongue technology was used in this study to evaluate the sourness, bitterness, sweetness, umami, and richness of jujube juice fermented with the four L. plantarum strains. As shown in Fig. 2, the sourness of fermented jujube juice decreased, with the juices fermented with FWDG and M1–12 showing the most pronounced reduction. This is consistent with the previous section where the pH dropped. As the acidity decreased, the pH value increased. In addition, fermentation with M1–12 and SC-1.1 increased juice sweetness, whereas sweetness decreased in the juices fermented with the other two strains. Furthermore, juices fermented with FWDG showed a decrease in bitterness but an increase in umami and richness, presumably due to the generation of distinctive metabolites that altered their taste profiles.

Determination of °brix and reducing sugar content of fermented jujube juice

Variations in °Brix in fermented jujube juice are attributed to the combined effects of sugar consumption and organic acid accumulation, resulting from the interaction of these processes (Maldonado et al., 2017). In this study, fermentation resulted in a notable decrease in °Brix from 15.00 to approximately 12.90 (Table 2). The sugar content of jujube juice was evaluated by measuring reducing sugars (Fig. 3). Reducing sugar levels in uninoculated jujube juice showed no significant change over 48 h. Conversely, all juices inoculated with L. plantarum showed a decreasing trend. Specifically, the juice fermented with SC-1.3 showed the greatest decrease (by 31.4 %), from 105.16 mg/mL to 72.75 mg/mL. Juices fermented with the other three strains, i.e., FWDG, SC-1.1, and M1–12, exhibited smaller reductions from 105.16 mg/mL to 82.77 mg/mL, 83.07 mg/mL, and 80.51 mg/mL, respectively.

During fermentation, L. plantarum use reducing sugars (glucose and fructose) as a source of carbon and energy to sustain their metabolic processes (Tang et al., 2023). Throughout the fermentation process, these bacteria continuously perform diverse physiological activities, thereby decreasing the reducing sugar content in jujube juice. This has also been reported in studies of carrot juice fermentation (Xu, Hlaing, Glagovskaia, Augustin, & Terefe, 2020). In this study, the varying sugar utilisation and bioconversion efficiencies of the four tested strains during jujube juice fermentation as well as their distinct metabolic characteristics and sugar conversion capabilities within the fermentation matrix are likely the primary factors contributing to differences in reducing sugar content observed among juices.

Total polyphenol and total flavonoid content in fermented jujube juice

Numerous studies have shown that foods rich in total polyphenols and total flavonoids enhance the levels of beneficial compounds in fermented products and promote human gut health (de Souza, Rodrigues, Sabino, Lacerda, & de Brito Alves, 2019). As shown in Fig. 4A, TPC increased significantly in the fermented jujube juices, particularly in the juice fermented with SC-1.3, which increased by 275 % compared with that in the uninoculated juice (from 0.0036 g/L to 0.0135 g/L). In the juices fermented with FWDG, SC-1.1, and M1–12, TPC increased by smaller amounts of 167 %, 158 %, and 186 %, respectively. A similar increase in TPC with extended fermentation time has been observed in L. plantarum–fermented mulberry products (Kwaw et al., 2018). This can be attributed to fermentation-induced disintegration of jujube cell walls, which release phenolic compounds that were previously bound. At the same time, polyphenol oxidase in L. plantarum can depolymerise high-molecular-weight phenolic compounds. The synergistic action of these two processes leads to an increase in TPC (Hur, Lee, Kim, Choi, & Kim, 2014). Studies have shown that different L. plantarum secrete distinct hydrolytic enzymes during fermentation, leading to varied changes in phenolic compounds (Kwaw et al., 2018).

Effect of fermentation with different L. plantarum on the physicochemical properties of jujube juice. (A) Total phenol content; (B) Total flavonoid content. Variance analysis was performed using Duncan's test. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences.

As illustrated in Fig. 4B, total flavonoid and total polyphenol contents in the four jujube juices exhibited distinct trends, initially increasing and then decreasing. Specifically, TFC gradually increased within 24 h of inoculation, followed by a subsequent decrease as fermentation progressed. Notably, during the first 24 h, the juice fermented with the FWDG strain showed the highest increase in flavonoid content (9.59 %). However, between 24 h and 48 h, flavonoid levels in FWDG-fermented juice declined rapidly. Among the four juices, the one fermented with SC-1.1 showed the greatest reduction in flavonoid content after 48 h (from 0.46 mg/mL to 0.39 mg/mL), whereas juices fermented with FWDG, SC-1.3, and M1–12 showed smaller decreases to 0.41 mg/mL, 0.42 mg/mL and 0.41 mg/mL, respectively. This trend may be attributed to L. plantarum producing enzymes that disrupt the structure of jujube cells within the first 24 h, releasing flavonoids into the fermentation broth and potentially promoting flavonoid synthesis through fermentation-derived metabolites. The glycosidases formed after 24 h of fermentation may hydrolyse flavonoid glycosidic bonds, degrading flavonoids and reducing their total content (Chen et al., 2023). The variation in flavonoid content among jujube juices fermented with different strains can be attributed to differences in the molecular composition and structure of L. plantarum affecting metabolic pathways. This trend has also been reported in studies on lemon juice fermentation (B. Liu et al., 2021).

Antioxidant properties of fermented jujube juice

Oxidative stress arises when metabolically generated free radicals exceed the scavenging capacity of the cell and is implicated in ageing, cancer and other chronic disorders (Yuan et al., 2020). L. plantarum can augment food-borne antioxidant activity by secreting bioactive metabolites, as demonstrated for L. plantarum-fermented lemon juice (Hashemi et al., 2017). The antioxidant potential of jujube juice fermented with L. plantarum was therefore quantified by FRAP, ABTS, DPPH and SOD assays.

Compared with the uninoculated juice, FRAP values in all fermented juices increased with extended fermentation time (Fig. 5A). No differences in FRAP values of each L. plantarum strains. The SOD inhibition rate exceeded 91.02 % at 48 h across all experimental groups significantly surpassing the rate in the uninoculated control (Fig. 5B). Fermentation have been reported to exhibit considerable antioxidant activity during, primarily attributed to SOD (Kullisaar et al., 2002). The increasing levels of SOD of fermented juices, indicating that they can effectively mitigate oxidative stress. As shown in Fig. 5C, the juice fermented with FWDG exhibited the highest total antioxidant than other three juices. These differences among fermented juices may stem from the effects of L. plantarum on polyphenolic compounds during fermentation (Kwaw et al., 2018). DPPH is commonly used to evaluate the free radical scavenging activity of natural compounds (Sharma & Bhat, 2009). As shown in Fig. 5D, the DPPH scavenging rate of L. plantarum-fermented jujube juices was significantly increased. Specifically, FWDG showed a scavenging capacity of 66.13 %. This enhancement may be attributed to fermentation increasing the content of polyphenolic compounds with proton-donating properties, which boosts scavenging capacity (Zheng et al., 2023).

Effect of fermentation with different L. plantarum on the antioxidant capacity of jujube juice. (A) FRAP, (B) SOD, (C) ABTS and (D) DPPH. Variance analysis was performed using Duncan's test. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences.

Overall, the antioxidant capacity of the uninoculated control was significantly lower than that of juices fermented with L. plantarum. Among these, the FWDG demonstrated superior capacity in antioxidant indices. It also has a strong antioxidant capacity in FRAP, ABTS and DPPH free. In addition, the indicators of FWDG in the previous taste detection also performed exceptionally well and FWDG has balanced fermentation performance. Therefore, the characteristics of the FWDG-fermented juice were further analysed via non-targeted metabolomics.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis of the FWDG-fermented jujube juice

Non-targeted metabolomics assays are widely used to identify significantly different metabolites between biological samples and elucidate the metabolic processes and mechanisms underlying these differences (Liang et al., 2024). In this study, the differences between fermented and unfermented jujube juices were investigated via non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Because of its remarkable physicochemical properties and antioxidant capacity, the FWDG-fermented juice was further subjected to histological examination.

Fig. 6A shows significant differences in metabolites between the FWDG-fermented juice and the control. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was used to separately investigate the metabolites present in the FWDG-fermented juice and control. The scoring plots and alternative test results are shown in Fig. 6B and C. The cumulative discriminant explanatory power of the total model was R2X (cum) = 0.467 and R2Y (cum) = 1, with a predictive ability of Q2 = 0.969. These results indicate that the OPLS-DA model is stable and reliable.

To better understand the metabolic differences between the FWDG-fermented juice and the control, the differential metabolites detected were screened according to VIP values >1, with significance set at p < 0.05. A total of 438 differential metabolites were identified, 216 and 222 of which exhibited upregulation and downregulation, respectively (Fig. 6D). As shown in Fig. 6E, the 50 metabolites with the highest VIP values were selected and analysed using clustered heatmaps and VIP histograms. Enrichment analysis of these top 50 metabolites along with measurement of their relative content during fermentation revealed that they mainly comprised organic acids, flavonoids, amino acids, peptides and their analogues, lipids and lipid-like molecules as well as other functional metabolites.

Organic acids and their derivatives were the most abundant, playing a key role in the flavour and preservation characteristics of the fermented juices. Compared with the uninoculated jujube juice, the FWDG-fermented juice had a higher N-lactoyl-phenylalanine content (Fig. 7A) but significantly lower contents of L-malic acid and fumaric acid (Fig. 7B–C). N-lactoyl-phenylalanine, a lactoyl amino acid derivative, significantly enhances the mellowness and finesse of fermented products and has been identified as a key flavouring ingredient in studies of aged cheese (Wu et al., 2022). In the present study, metabolomics analysis revealed that fermentation with L. plantarum increased N-lactoyl-phenylalanine content in jujube juice, thereby enhancing the mellow taste of the product. L-malic acid and fumaric acid contents in the juice were significantly reduced by fermentation with L. plantarum, consistent with previous reports of L-malic acid reduction in grapes under similar fermentation conditions. In the case of grapes, such decrease is likely due to L. plantarum converting L-malic acid to lactic acid during fermentation, which reduces perceived acidity in wine (Lee, Hwang, Lee, & Hong, 2009). L-malic acid content has also been shown to decrease during blueberry fermentation, possibly due to its involvement in the malolactic pathway and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Li et al., 2021). Fumaric acid can inhibit malolactic fermentation during wine fermentation, as evidenced by reduced L-malic acid content (Morata et al., 2020). Probiotic-fermented cowpea flour has been shown to exhibit low fumaric acid content and high total polyphenol levels (Kewuyemi & Adebo, 2024), a pattern that was also observed in the present study.

Flavonoids are a class of natural compounds with antioxidant properties beneficial for human health (Erdoğan & Özbakır Işın, 2022). In the present study, the content of some phenolic compounds, including isosakuranetin (Fig. 7D), was shown to increase in fermented jujube juice. Previous studies have shown that isohypericin, which is commonly found in citrus fruits, can be converted to isosakuranetin through microbial transformation. Moreover, ponkanetin in citrus juice can be converted to isosakuranetin after fermentation with Lactobacillus paracasei LPC37 (Cao, Guo, Fang, Ru, & Li, 2022). Isosakuranetin exhibits a wide range of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and anticancer effects on leukaemia cells (Tseng, Lai, Chou, & Huang, 2025).

Amino acids and peptides are important bioactive substances in food and have various functional properties (Zhang, Lu, & Liu, 2022). In this study, the fermented jujube juices showed significantly reduced levels of bitter amino acids, including Phe-Ile, Ala-Ile, Ile-Leu and Val-Ile (Fig. 7E-H). Previous studies have demonstrated that reducing the content of bitter amino acids, such as Ile, can attenuate the bitterness of oats fermented with L. plantarum (Yu et al., 2024). During jujube juice fermentation, the levels of certain bitter amino acids-namely Ile, Leu, Val, and Phe-gradually decreased. This reduction likely decreases the bitterness of fermented jujube juice, consistent with the electronic tongue analysis results.

Previous studies have also indicated that lipids commonly serve as nutrients for microbial growth during fermentation (Mbuyane, Bauer, & Divol, 2021). In this study, the fermented jujube juices exhibited a significant increase in the lipid metabolite dextran, an extracellular polysaccharide biosynthesised by L. plantarum (Fig. 7I) (Petrovici, Pinteala, & Simionescu, 2023). Dextran possesses antioxidant properties, as it is capable of scavenging free radicals and mitigating cell and tissue damage caused by oxidative stress. Moreover, the juices exhibited increased content of 16-hydroxyhexadecanoic acid (Fig. 7J). This fatty acid inhibits adenocarcinoma cell growth and induces apoptosis, serving as a functional substance influencing human health and metabolism (T. Liu et al., 2022). Overall, the increased content of these beneficial metabolites in fermented jujube juice indicates that fermentation with L. plantarum confers the juice antioxidant and anticancer properties.

Furthermore, the fermentation process altered the levels of specific functional metabolites, causing a significant increase in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (Maldonado et al.) content (Fig. 7K) and a reduction in uridine levels (Fig. 7L). Growing evidence indicates that NAD serves as a fundamental mediator in various biological processes, including energy metabolism, mitochondrial function, antioxidant regulation, and oxidative stress regulation (Ying, 2008). NAD mitigates chronic heart failure via mitochondrial redox state mechanisms and exhibits anti-ageing and antioxidant effect (Chaturvedi & Tyagi, 2018)s. Moreover, the reduction in uridine content during jujube juice fermentation implies its potential catabolism into uracil and ribose. Uridine is crucial for enhancing fresh flavour (Valerio et al., 2017), consistent with experimental results showing decreased e-tongue fresh flavour indicators.

The metabolites identified in fermented jujube juice with FWDG strains generally contained higher concentrations of bioactive compounds, primarily associated with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and flavour-related changes, compared with unfermented jujube juice. Notably, NAD and dextran were significantly up-regulated, potentially contributing to the remarkable antioxidant capacity of FWDG. Detailed information on the biological activities of the remaining differential metabolites is limited, targeted metabolomics the need for further investigation, which could inform the development of health-oriented products.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively investigated the effects of fermentation with four L. plantarum strains on the functional properties of Hami jujube juice. Fermentation substantially altered the physicochemical properties of jujube juice, decreasing reducing sugar and TFC while increasing TPC and antioxidant activity. Multi-indicator screening identified the FWDG strain as the optimal functional strain for jujube juice fermentation, showing the highest antioxidant capacity and selected for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Results indicated that FWDG-fermented jujube juice had significantly enriched antioxidant-active substances, including NAD, dextran, and other beneficial compounds, laying the foundation for exploring the functional mechanisms of L. plantarum-fermented jujube juice. This study provides a theoretical basis and identifies superior strains for developing functional jujube juice products with enhanced antioxidant effects. It also provides new insights into the high-value utilisation of traditional jujube products and the development of functional fermented fruit and vegetable juices.
