Increasing digital equity to promote online smoking cessation program engagement among rural adults: a randomized controlled pilot trial
Authors: Sydney Kelpin, Tabetha A Brockman, Paul A Decker, Antonia Young, Kasey Boehmer, Aaron Nguyen, Celia Kamath, Jennifer St Sauver, Pamela S Sinicrope, Pravesh Sharma, Rozalina McCoy, Summer Allen, Ming Huang, Joshua Pritchett, Dmitry Esterov, Michelle Lampman, Carolyn Petersen, Andrea Cheville, Christi A Patten
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCigarette smoking prevalence is higher for rural than urban adults, yet digital access to cessation programming is reduced. We aim to investigate digital access interventions to promote engagement with an online evidence-based cessation treatment (EBCT) program among rural adults.MethodsThis pilot trial used a pragmatic, three-arm, randomized, parallel-group design (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05209451). Inclusion criteria included being aged ≥18, Mayo Clinic Midwest patient, rural residency, and currently smokes cigarettes. All participants received an online, 12-week EBCT program and were randomized to receive one of three digital access interventions: print materials (control, n = 30); print materials + loaner iPad device with data plan coverage (n = 30); or print materials + loaner device + up to six, 15–20-minute motivational interviewing-based coaching calls to support technology needs (n = 30). A composite score of trial engagement (primary outcome) and self-reported smoking abstinence and use of EBCT resources (secondary outcomes) were assessed online at 4 and 12 weeks. Qualitative interviews were used to assess patient experience. Neither participants nor outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment.ResultsResults are reported for all 30 participants in each group. The average age of participants is 51.0 years and 61% are women. We show no significant arm differences for the trial engagement composite score (p = 0.30). We also find coaching support is significantly (p < 0.05) associated with enhanced smoking-related treatment response, including cigarette abstinence and use of EBCT resources, and participants reported positive experiences with the intervention.ConclusionsThe coaching intervention to support technology needs is acceptable and shows preliminary evidence of its efficacy in smoking-related treatment response. Further studies could refine and implement the coaching intervention for trial engagement and long-term cessation.Subject terms: Health services, Cancer prevention

Introduction

Rural communities in the United States (US) experience marked health disparities, including higher risk of chronic disease, worse health status, and increased mortality compared to their urban counterparts (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/people-in-rural-areas-die-at-higher-rates-than-those-in-urban-areas/)1–6. A major contributor to these observed health inequalities is cigarette smoking, which is more prevalent among rural compared to urban adults in the US (26% vs. 21%)7. Furthermore, a recent study found that the odds of quitting smoking were significantly lower in rural compared to urban areas8. Because cigarette smoking is the leading cause of preventable death in the US9, promoting cessation among rural populations is foundational to eliminating existing health disparities.

Structural barriers that impede rural residents’ access to cessation support include local clinician shortages, travel costs required for face-to-face visits, and higher rates of uninsurance7,10. Such barriers to in-person services provide an opportunity to promote free, digital, evidence-based smoking cessation treatments (EBCTs). These include text messaging applications, coaching, and pharmacotherapy provided by state quitlines11. However, many rural dwellers lack the hardware, connectivity, and familiarity with digital technologies that are required to utilize these treatments. National survey data indicate that compared to urban residents, rural residents are less likely to have access to a digital device (i.e., smartphone, tablet) and broadband (BB) internet (https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/08/19/some-digital-divides-persist-between-rural-urban-and-suburban-america/) and report lower levels of digital literacy12,13. Digital inclusivity has been identified as a social determinant of health14–16. National programs highlight the need for translational research into effective, scalable techquity strategies that promote digital access and literacy among underserved populations (https://www.internetforall.gov/sites/default/files/2022-05/digital-equity-act-info-sheet.pdf)17.

Fortunately, reports suggest that some strategies may help to neutralize the digital divide. These include technical training and assistance programs18,19, digital navigators20, tailored technology support21,22, and digital literacy programs23. Evidence remains sparse, yet, to date, effect sizes have been modest and varied across populations worldwide. Research has not explored the use of such strategies among rural populations who smoke.

We, therefore, sought to assess if multiple concurrent strategies would be more impactful on engagement with and response to an online program for smoking cessation among US Midwest rural adults with digital access barriers who smoke cigarettes. We did so using a mixed-methods, randomized, controlled pilot trial called the IDEA (Increasing Digital Equity and Access) study24. The study compared three digital health access interventions of varying intensity - print materials only (control), print materials + a loaner digital device, and print materials + a loaner digital device + coaching support to address digital literacy - for engagement with an online smoking cessation program. We hypothesized that trial engagement and smoking abstinence would be higher for the intervention that included coaching support compared with the other arms. We used qualitative interviews to assess patient experience with the interventions and feasibility of remote trial participation. We show no significant arm differences for a composite score of trial engagement; but coaching support was associated with enhanced smoking-related treatment response, including cigarette abstinence and use of EBCT resources, and participants reported positive experiences with the intervention.

Methods

The trial was designed in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement for pilot and feasibility trials25, and is registered with the Clinical Trials Registry (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05209451; registration date January 25, 2022). The study protocol24 was guided by the Digital Health Equity Framework15 and co-designed with a Midwest rural community advisory committee (CAC) to maximize relevance to rural populations. The first participant was accrued on February 11, 2022, and the last participant on November 18, 2022. Data were collected from January 2022 through March 2023. The trial is completed.

Ethics

We obtained approval of the study protocol from the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB 21-011617), and the authors complied with all relevant ethical regulations in conducting the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all trial participants.

Trial design

This pilot trial utilized a pragmatic, three-arm, randomized, parallel-group design. Before the trial, the study statistician generated the random allocation sequence, and participants completed baseline measures before being informed of their assignment. Participants were stratified based on the state of residence (Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa) and randomized with 1:1:1 allocation to one of three study arms. All participants were provided with an online smoking cessation program and randomized to receive one of three digital access interventions: print materials only (control condition), print materials + a loaner iPad device, or print materials + loaner iPad device + tailored coaching support. All randomized participants were asked to complete online study assessments and remotely conducted breath carbon monoxide (CO) testing at 4- and 12-weeks post-randomization. At the study’s end, we randomly sampled 10 participants from each study arm to participate in an individual qualitative interview regarding their experience with the interventions. Neither participants nor outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment. We mailed participants a $25 cash card as remuneration for completing the baseline survey and $50 for completing each follow-up assessment (2 surveys and 2 breath CO testings); up to $225 total. Individuals randomly selected for and completing the interview received another $75. The pre-specified primary outcome was a composite measure of trial engagement, and secondary outcomes were self-reported smoking abstinence, use of EBCT, and patient experience.

Participant recruitment and screening

From January 2022 through November 2022, research staff recruited adult patients from Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, and the Mayo Clinic Health System (MCHS). The MCHS is a regional network of community clinics, hospitals, and other healthcare facilities with a physical presence in 44 communities in southeast Minnesota, western Wisconsin, and northeastern Iowa (https://www.mayoclinichealthsystem.org/about-us).

We used the Mayo Clinic electronic health record (EHR) to recruit study participants. We identified patients with (1) authorization for the use of their medical record for research; (2) a Mayo Clinic visit at a Rochester/MCHS site during a preceding two-year period; (3) aged 18 or older; (4) current smoking/tobacco use status as reported in the EHR; (5) address in Minnesota, Wisconsin, or Iowa; and (6) Rural resident based on Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes 4-10 (https://www.hrsa.gov/rural-health/about-us/what-is-rural) derived from the ZIP code of current or last known address. About 61,929 current smokers were within this sampling frame. We mailed invitation letters to random samples of 525 patients each (with equal numbers in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Iowa) until we achieved our targeted sample size of 90 participants. Non-respondents were contacted three weeks after the initial mailing by phone and email; up to three total contacts were made.

The invitation letter contained (1) the study-specific email address and toll-free number that participants could contact for more information and screening and (2) a URL link to a screening questionnaire. To enhance credibility, the invitation included a URL link to the study on the Mayo Clinic Clinical Trials webpage and a Mayo Clinic business card with the study contact information. Patients could decline participation and further contact by phone, email, or postal mail.

Study eligibility criteria

The study inclusion criteria were: (1) A Mayo Clinic Midwest patient (Mayo Clinic Rochester or Mayo Clinic Health System [MCHS]) with a Mayo Clinic number; (2) Aged 18 years or older; (3) Can read and speak in the English language. Based on 2019 data, 97% of our Rochester and MCHS patients speak English. The study will provide all participants with a smoking cessation program delivered through the Mayo Clinic patient portal. Currently, Mayo Clinic’s portal services are not offered in languages other than English; (4) Has a Minnesota, Wisconsin, or Iowa address currently or at date of most recent visit to Mayo Clinic Rochester or MCHS; (5) Rural resident based on RUCA codes derived from ZIP code of current or last known address; (6) Has access to a telephone of any type (e.g., landline, smartphone). Access to a telephone is needed to complete the coaching support intervention and to follow-up with participants if there are issues with accessing the patient portal for the smoking cessation intervention; (7) Current smoker defined as one or more cigarettes (even a puff) smoked in the past 30 days (original criteria). This definition allows for enrollment of non-daily and/or light smoking enhancing the generalizability. In addition to cigarettes, cigarillos and cigars are combustible tobacco products and thus relevant as a focus for our study. Thus, the protocol was modified with IRB approval to change this criterion to include other combustible tobacco use. The final criterion was “Current smoker defined as one or more cigarettes, cigarillos, or cigars (even a puff) smoked in the past 30 days.” To enhance the generalizability of our findings, we will enroll participants using other tobacco/nicotine products if cigarette, cigarillo or cigar smoking is the primary product used; (8) For past 3 months not enrolled in a program or use of cessation pharmacotherapy. The protocol was modified with IRB approval to allow for ad hoc use to enhance generalizability. The final criterion was “For past 3 months not enrolled in a program or use of cessation pharmacotherapy other than ad hoc use, for example, used nicotine gum or lozenge once or twice”; (9) Willing to make a quit smoking attempt; (10) Has a patient portal account, or is willing to create a patient portal account; (11) Has an email address to provide to study staff, or is willing to create an email address; and (12) Has digital access barriers defined by one of more of the following: (a) based on participant ZIP code from current or last known address geocoded census block group indicates an area of poor BB coverage (https://www.fcc.gov/); (b) no existing Mayo Clinic patient portal account or (c) has a Mayo Clinic patient portal account but has not used it in the past 5 years; (c) self-reports having no access to broadband (high speed) internet connection for personal use; and/or (e) self-reports low digital literacy defined with the screening question “How comfortable are you using technology to manage your health care remotely? For example, using patient online services (i.e., patient portal) to schedule appointments, check for test results, or send a message to your provider” (included: not at all or a little comfortable).

Exclusion criteria were: (1) Anyone not meeting all inclusion criteria will be excluded; and (2) Has participated in a behavioral smoking cessation program in the past 3 months.

Informed consent process and baseline survey

Interested screen-eligible individuals provided informed consent using a paper form or electronically via DocuSign. Individuals completed a baseline survey using a paper form that was returned using a postage-paid return envelope or electronically by responding to a URL link. Research staff enrolled and randomized individuals who provided informed consent and completed the baseline survey.

Enrollment materials and smoking cessation program

We mailed all randomized participants a welcome letter and written information on how to set up a patient online services account, access to free telephone support (Patient Portal Helpline), and links for portal technical support, including an online instructional video for accessing and using the patient portal. All participants were mailed an iCOquit® Smokelyzer and instructions on its use for breath carbon monoxide (CO) tests to biochemically verify smoking status at follow-up assessments (see Measures). We also mailed participants intervention materials based on their study arm assignment.

Participants in all three study arms received a 12-week, online, smoking cessation program through Mayo Clinic’s patient portal. The program commenced within 1 week after randomization, but if the participant needed to set up a portal account, it began 1 week after their account registration. The program linked participants to free, evidence-based, digital, tailored, interactive, Smokefree.gov web resources (https://smokefree.gov/). Research supporting this approach indicates that 3-5% of individuals who quit without treatment maintain abstinence, but when cessation resources include behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy, the chance of success is up to threefold higher26. Smokefree.gov resources include a website tailored to readiness to quit smoking, a quit plan, a toll-free number (1-800-QUIT-NOW) and weblink to state quitlines that offer free cessation coaching and pharmacotherapy, texting program, smartphone applications, online live chat, and social media platforms11. These resources offer complementary technology options to meet individual needs and preferences. Among a national sample of adults (N = 1308), providing Smokefree.gov web resources resulted in a 12-month smoking abstinence rate of 26%, comparable to the efficacy of other behavioral interventions for smoking cessation27.

The smoking cessation program was entirely asynchronous. Study staff sent participants one weekly message for 12 weeks. The messages provided simplified navigation to specific EBCT resources in Smokefree.gov and the opportunity to answer 1-4 self-reflective questions about their cessation journey relevant to that week’s message (see Supplementary Method 1). Weekly message topics were (1) Why do I Want to Quit Smoking?; (2) Prepare to Quit; (3) About 1-800-QUIT-NOW; (4) Your Quit Plan; (5) Ask for Help; (6) Quit Day; (7) Withdrawal; (8) Cravings and Triggers; (9) Coping with Stress; (10) Managing Mood; (11) Eat Healthy, Stay Active; and (12) Stick With It. Each included a call to action to use different Smokefree.gov resources, following the approach used in the effective CDC Tips from Former Smokers Campaign28, and links to patient portal support. Regular check-ins were not a component of the program. However, the study team called participants who did not open the first patient portal message within one week of receipt to elicit and troubleshoot potential issues with portal access.

Digital access interventions

Control arm

At the time of enrollment, participants were mailed print materials on digital access resources titled “Guide to Finding Computers and Internet Access in Your Area” (Supplementary Method 2). The guide, developed by the study team with CAC feedback, listed public places to access the Internet (e.g., public libraries, feed stores, and coffee shops) and guidelines for connecting, including privacy and security considerations. Participants randomized to this arm received no additional study intervention.

Loaner device arm

Participants assigned to this arm were provided the same print materials provided to the control participants. Participants randomized to this arm additionally were loaned a 10.2-inch iPad with Wi-Fi and pre-paid internet connectivity via a wireless cellular data plan for the 12-week study duration. Data plan charges were directly billed to the study. The iPads were limited to study-specific apps and restricted the use of built-in apps and features such as installing or deleting apps, in-app purchasing, and accessing websites other than Smokefree.gov. Upon enrollment, we mailed the iPad to participants and sent them materials for its return by mail or in person at the program’s end. Participants received the “iPad Loaner Basic User Guide” developed by the study team with CAC feedback, which included instructional text and visual aids for navigating its use.

Coaching support arm

Participants assigned to this arm received the same print materials and loaner iPad. The unique intervention was individualized telephone coaching support to address technology needs. The initial call was scheduled within 1-1.5 weeks after randomization, or after creating a portal account if none existed. The intervention protocol included three weekly telephone sessions in weeks 1-3 and up to three additional telephone check-ins based on the participant’s needs and interests in weeks 4-12. Each call was estimated to last approximately 15-20 min but tailored to the participant’s current digital needs and interest in increasing their engagement with the study technology.

The coaching intervention was manualized (see Supplementary Method 3), including specific topics to be covered in each call and a sample script for the initial and follow-up calls. The research coaches utilized behavior change strategies delivered with motivational interviewing (MI) techniques29. The research coaches established a therapeutic alliance with each participant and assessed their access, utilization, comfort, and ease of use of the study-related technology. The coaches provided tailored advice and problem-solving based on the participant’s digital literacy, including setting up and navigating the iPad and patient portal. In addition, the research coaches elicited the participant’s current motivations for increasing their digital literacy/access and collaboratively set goals regarding their use of the technology in the coming weeks using the SMART goal framework (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-limited)30.

It is important to note that the MI-based coaching focused explicitly on technology use in the study rather than on quitting behaviors and strategies. If participants wanted to discuss quitting smoking or cessation treatment, the research coaches directed the individual to use the online smoking cessation program and links to Smokefree.gov EBCT resources.

Research coaches delivered the intervention (A.Y., A.N.), each with a bachelor’s degree in psychology trained by two clinical psychologists (S.K., T.A.B.) trained to deliver the intervention as specified in the manual. The training included presentations, video demonstrations, other didactic materials, and role-plays. The training was reinforced with monthly check-ins with the trainers to monitor intervention fidelity and provide ongoing skills practice. At each meeting, coaching sessions from the previous month were discussed, highlighting successes and challenges.

Coaches documented participant intervention adherence including the number of coaching calls attempted and completed and the duration of each phone session. Coaches recorded specific intervention components delivered (e.g., SMART goal setting) and technical support provided (e.g., iPad, patient portal, Smokerlyzer, available resources).

Measures

Study staff documented study accrual (Fig. 1). The accrued sample (n = 90) was measured repeatedly at baseline (paper or online survey), 4 weeks post-randomization (online survey plus breath CO testing), and 12 weeks post-randomization (online survey plus breath CO testing). Follow-up surveys were pretested with 11 CAC members, and we used validated measures where possible. Follow-up assessments were conducted by the Mayo Clinic Survey Research Center (SRC) who were separate from study staff delivering the interventions. The SRC staff sent participants a link to an online Qualtrics-administered survey by email or text, depending on the participant’s preference. Participants who did not complete the survey within two weeks were contacted by SRC staff with a reminder to complete the survey. Participants had a 3-week window to complete each survey. Baseline measures

The baseline survey assessed self-reported socio-demographic information on age, race, ethnicity, gender identity, education, income, marital status, occupation/disability, lifetime medical comorbidities (https://hints.cancer.gov/about-hints/learn-more-about-hints.aspx) for which participants were asked “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had any of the following medical conditions?), and health literacy31. Smoking characteristics included cigarettes smoked daily, time to first cigarette32, readiness to quit (Contemplation Ladder)33, and other commercial tobacco and nicotine product use (e.g., electronic cigarettes). Digital literacy was assessed with an item developed for this study “How comfortable are you using technology to manage your health care remotely? For example, using online patient services (i.e., patient portal) to schedule appointments, check for test results, or send a message to your provider?”

Primary outcome-trial engagement

Study staff documented the number of online surveys and breath CO testing completed at follow-up among randomized participants. We evaluated engagement with the online 12-week cessation program via the patient portal with objective data using audit log files obtained from Epic that track user actions, including logging into the application and lists from Patient Portal Help Services. We assessed the number of weekly messages opened by the participant (14 total) and the number of questions within weekly messages answered (25 total).

After the trial commenced, with IRB approval, we modified the study protocol to add the scoring procedure for a composite measure of trial engagement. This was derived by summing the following indicators: survey completed at week 4 (0=no, 1=yes), survey completed at week 12 (0=no, 1=yes), breath CO testing completed at week 4 (0=no, 1=yes), breath CO testing completed at week 12 (0=no, 1=yes), number of weekly patient portal messages opened (0-14), and number of questions responded to within the message (0-25). Because the primary outcome reflected trial engagement, missing data for each indicator was coded as zero engagement. The composite score ranges from 0-43, with higher scores indicating greater trial engagement.

Secondary smoking-related outcomes

The 4- and 12-week online survey assessed our secondary outcomes of self-reported point-prevalence abstinence from cigarette smoking (not even a puff) during the past 7- and 30-day periods, quit attempts, and utilization of Smokefree.gov EBCT resources (e.g., texting program, state tobacco quitline).

At both time points, we also assessed self-reported use of other tobacco/nicotine products (e.g., e-cigarettes) during the past 7- and 30-day periods and biochemically verified smoking abstinence. All participants were asked to complete breath CO tests using the iCOquit® Smokerlyzer®. This small, portable, handheld, Bluetooth-enabled breath CO monitor connects wirelessly to a mobile device or tablet. The iCOquit® Smokerlyzer® works through an app downloaded onto a mobile phone or tablet, displaying a result the participant then sent to the study team through the app or by email. For this trial, we focused on combustible cigarette smoking, based on recommendations from the Society for Research on Nicotine and Tobacco workgroup on measuring abstinence in clinical trials of smoking cessation interventions34. Breath CO measurement can verify abstinence within the previous 24 h and is not confounded by detecting smokeless tobacco use, nicotine replacement therapy, or e-cigarette use because CO is a combustion product35,36. Self-reported abstinence from cigarette smoking was confirmed with CO breath test result 0-3 ppm; participants with missing breath CO data were classified as smoking. Our CO criterion for abstinence provides good sensitivity and specificity for differentiating current smoking from nonsmoking, especially light smoking ( < 10 cigarettes per day)35,37.

Secondary outcome: patient experience

At the study’s end, we randomly selected 10 participants from each study arm for a semi-structured individual, qualitative phone interview to assess patient experience with the interventions and remote trial participation. Study team members developed the interview guide in collaboration with the CAC. It contained open-ended questions with probes to understand the context of participants’ lives and rich and detailed information regarding their experience with the intervention they were assigned, study procedures and materials, and the smoking cessation program itself. Interviews were audio-recorded and lasted 45-60 min. The lead interviewer (CK) summarized notes immediately following each interview, and all interviews were professionally transcribed. Data saturation was reached with 10 participants interviewed from each arm.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Sample size calculation

We sought to obtain effect size estimates from this pilot study to adequately power a subsequent larger-scale effectiveness trial. Our primary outcome was a continuous variable of an objective composite trial engagement score comprised of study assessment completion and engagement with the online smoking cessation program. Based on sample size recommendations for pilot studies using continuous outcome variables38 we determined that with 30 participants randomly assigned to each of the three study arms (total N = 90), this sample size would provide greater than 90% power with two-sided 5% significance to detect a small effect size ( ~ 1 standard deviation unit) between study arms for the primary intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis of trial engagement. Based on sample size guidance for behavioral addictions treatment development39, 30 subjects per study arm would provide relatively stable group proportions for effect size estimates for our dichotomous smoking-related secondary outcomes (self-reported smoking abstinence and use of EBCT). Moreover, for the qualitative interviews to assess patient experience, we estimated n = 10 per study arm based on sample size recommendations for qualitative studies40.

Quantitative data analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS, version 9.4 (https://www.sas.com/en_us/contact.html). We summarized the number of potential participants screened, the number excluded for each inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the number of screen-eligible individuals agreeing to participate and accrued/randomized. To assess the adequacy of the randomization, we summarized baseline characteristics using descriptive statistics (number, percent, mean, standard deviation [SD], range) and compared these across study arms using the chi-square test (exact) and ANOVA (Kruskal-Wallis) as appropriate.

Study arm differences in the composite score of trial engagement (primary outcome) were summarized using descriptive statistics and compared across arms using the chi-square test (exact) and ANOVA (Kruskal-Wallis) as appropriate. We used an intent-to-treat (ITT) approach, including all randomized participants in the analysis (n = 30 per study arm). Because the main outcome reflected trial engagement, missing data for each indicator that made up the composite score was coded as zero engagement (see Measures). We used the chi-square test to compare study arms on the dichotomous smoking-related (secondary) outcomes analyzed using (1) an ITT approach classifying participants eligible but lost to follow-up as smoking (n = 30 per study arm); and (2) a complete case (CC) analysis including only participants in each study arm who completed follow-up (see Fig. 1). We did not include gender, race or ethnicity as a covariate in the analyses because study arms did not differ on these characteristics. Two-sided p values ≤ 0.050 were considered statistically significant.

Qualitative analyses

We used rapid-directed content analysis41–44 to generate response themes among participants completing the qualitative interview (n = 10 per study arm). The qualitative analyst wrote detailed notes during interviews on a Word document structured by interview questions and constructs. These notes were reviewed, reorganized, and grouped by question/construct for each interviewee to produce an analyzable matrix in MS Excel. These coded notes were then validated by comparing them to the interview transcripts. The MS Excel matrix was edited where there were any discrepancies. The qualitative matrix was then examined for patterns across the three study arms. Findings were shared with the CAC for results interpretation.

Results

Participants

Figure 1 illustrates the study flow. Invitation letters were mailed to 2625 patients, of which 232 were screened for eligibility, and 90 were accrued. Participant baseline socio-demographic and health-related characteristics by study arm are shown in Tables 1 and 2. The average age of participants was 51.0 years (SD = 14.1, range 23-76); 61% were women, 94% were White, and 31% reported having a disability, handicap, or chronic disease. The most common self-reported lifetime medical comorbidities were depression or anxiety disorder (54%) and hypertension (42%). All accrued participants smoked cigarettes. Participants smoked a mean ± SD of 16.8 ± 9.6 cigarettes daily, and 83% reported high readiness to quit. Excepting self-reported diabetes, the study arms were comparable in baseline characteristics. Eighty-one percent of participants had an existing patient portal account; study staff helped the remaining individuals to set one up.

Coaching support intervention implementation

Of the 30 participants assigned to receive coaching calls, Fig. 1 shows the number of participants completing 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4-6 calls. Participants completed a mean of 3.5 ± 1.8 (range 0-6) coaching calls, with a mean call duration of 17.0 ± 13.1 (range 4-60) minutes. Providing support and resources for accessing the patient portal was the most frequent topic covered (73.1% of sessions), followed by the iCOquit® Smokerlyzer® (69.2%), iPad (60.6%), and directing participants to available resources (e.g., patient portal helpline, Quitline, study contact information) (59.6%). Goal setting related to technology use was completed in 97.1% of the coaching calls.

Patient experience (secondary outcome)

Box 1 presents qualitative themes on patient experience. Several themes were similar across arms. Helpful aspects noted by all arms were the print materials on digital access resources, written instructions to set up required trial technology, weekly portal messages, and personalized feedback from the Smokerlyzer. For those receiving an iPad, most participants noted using it regularly and described it as user-friendly. However, some participants across all arms endorsed frustrations with the technology and noted the importance of prompt responses to help requests. Despite positive feedback about the iPad, some participants preferred using their personal device (e.g., phone, laptop) which were more familiar to them and had stored passwords. Participants across all arms noted they had involved their family or friends for technology assistance and social support for quitting smoking. Finally, participants across all three conditions expressed interest in receiving nicotine replacement products.

Within each arm, some unique aspects of feedback were provided as well. Within the control arm, participants felt the content and duration of the smoking cessation program could have been condensed. In the loaner device arm, participants felt instructional videos instead of written instructions would have been helpful and expressed interest in receiving more individualized support and feedback throughout the intervention. Finally, participants in the coaching arm appreciated the ongoing one-on-one support and reported interest in increasing the frequency of the coaching calls. They wanted smoking cessation techniques added to the content of the calls.

Discussion

The IDEA pilot RCT addressed important access barriers to digital smoking cessation programming – and digital interventions more broadly - in rural populations. It is the first evaluation of digital access and literacy interventions to promote trial engagement and response to an online, evidence-based smoking cessation program among rural adults. Recruitment of the targeted sample size was feasible. The coaching support was acceptable based on coaching call completion and positive patient experience. The CAC noted that the high call completion rate attests to the need for such services in this population. Further, the coaching support intervention indicated a possible smoking-related treatment response that deserves testing in a larger trial. Based on our qualitative work on patient experience, the ongoing and personal nature of the coaching calls gave participants a feeling of being supported on their quitting journey. In fact, some participants in the study arms without coaching expressed the need for more one-on-one support. However, overall trial engagement and persistence in this rural population could have been more optimal; at week 12, only 42% completed the online survey and 10% completed breath CO testing. Trial engagement across study arms was in the expected direction, with higher composite scores among participants in the coaching intervention, but overall differences across arms were not statistically significant.

To the best of our knowledge, the current study adds novel findings on the potential role of the intervention that included coaching support for technology needs to enhance smoking cessation in rural populations. A recent systematic review included 26 studies evaluating smoking cessation interventions among rural populations in the US, Canada, and Australia45. Interventions had limited short-term effects on smoking abstinence that decreased markedly after six months. Short-term abstinence ( < 6 months) was best supported by financial incentive interventions and long-term abstinence by pharmacotherapy. Our self-reported 7-day point-prevalence abstinence rate for coaching support of 31.3% (complete case) and 16.7% (ITT) at 12 weeks compares favorably with two relevant studies that evaluated telehealth cessation interventions. Byaruhanga et al.46 compared video and phone counseling among 655 Australian rural adults. The self-reported 7-day point-prevalence smoking abstinence rate (ITT) at 12 weeks was 18.9% for video and 12.7% for phone counseling. Among 288 US rural adults randomized to receive a Smokefree.gov guided mobile phone app, Santiago-Torres et al.47 found the 7-day self-reported point-prevalence smoking abstinence rates at 12 weeks were 17% (complete case) and 14.6% (ITT). Our intervention is unique because the coaching addressed technology barriers to accessing cessation support. Trigg et al.45 concluded that digital literacy and technology needs are critical considerations for future research on smoking cessation interventions in rural populations.

Our 12-week study retention rate (42%) is lower than prior telehealth smoking intervention studies at the same time point ranging from 57%-88%46–48. Trigg and colleagues’45 systematic review of rural cessation interventions found that study retention ranged from 11% to 100%. Our findings on trial engagement should be considered in the context of this rural sample recruited specifically for having digital access barriers. Medical comorbidities and disabilities were highly prevalent in this rural population. About half of the sample (54%) reported ever having a depression or anxiety disorder. Unfortunately, we do not have comparable data to determine if this prevalence is characteristic of the Mayo Clinic cohort. These comorbidities could potentially have influenced the study outcomes as well as can inform future research designs by addressing co-morbid conditions. For example, the CAC recommended that mood-related conditions be addressed in future iterations of the interventions. Another factor possibly contributing to the low retention is that the SRC distributed the follow-up surveys which was a separate group from the research team. The SRC emailed or texted participants a link to complete each survey but the email address or phone number did not include the study name. The CAC noted that participants may not have been aware or forgot to look for these emails or may not have recognized the email address or phone number, which could have been a hindrance due to increasing problems with spam calls. Ensuring continuity in the research staff conducting the follow-ups may improve participant retention.

From our qualitative work, common study resources were well received in all three arms (e.g., the written resource guide, the Smokerlyzer, and online links to cessation resources). Reception to the loaner device was mixed because many participants preferred personal, familiar digital devices. A sustainable benefit from the IDEA study was that we set up a process within our healthcare system for purchasing, dispersing, and tracking iPad devices and the data plan remuneration. This infrastructure can be helpful for future decentralized clinical trials, including in rural areas. Future studies could provide the option for a loaner device to rural patients or others who need or request one for eligibility to participate in remotely delivered clinical trials.

A major strength of our study is that the trial design and interventions were co-designed with a rural CAC. We met with its members to share progress and problem-solve barriers to study implementation. We shared the study results with our committee members to assist with interpretation and to create dissemination messaging and materials relevant to rural populations. Other strengths are the accrual of participants from a rurally situated community-based health practice49 and the use of a manualized coaching support intervention delivered by trained coaches with a high call completion rate.

As a pilot study, the small sample size is a limitation. As a secondary outcome, the assessment of smoking outcomes was limited to 12 weeks. The high proportion of missing data limits the interpretation of the study findings. Retention increased from weeks 4 to 12 in the coaching support and loaner device study arms but declined for the control group. Given that non-responsiveness was equated to smoking in the ITT analyses, it is plausible that the differences in cessation rates across study arms can be at least partially attributed to the lower response rate. The sample is restricted to Midwest Mayo Clinic patients and 94% were White, which may limit the generalizability to other rural populations.

Data from this trial provides preliminary information on engaging rural populations in a wide range of clinical research activities and the additional resources and support that may be needed for rural patients with digital access barriers. Refinement and implementation of the coaching support intervention to bolster clinical trial engagement and long-term smoking cessation warrant further study. For example, we learned from our qualitative work that more intensive, tailored education and coaching support may be necessary to address rural culture and contextual factors consistent with the Digital Health Equity Framework15. This pilot study could pave the way toward larger, more generalizable evaluations of broader settings and populations, including non-English speaking rural patients and community members.

In conclusion, the coaching intervention to support technology needs was acceptable, and there was a signal for its efficacy in smoking-related treatment response. Overall, trial engagement was suboptimal in this rural Midwest population with digital access barriers. Refinement and implementation of the coaching support intervention for bolstering remote clinical trial engagement and long-term smoking cessation warrants further study.
