Background

Since the 1960s, intermittent catheterisation (IC) has been the method of choice for bladder emptying in cases of neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) [1, 2].

A questionnaire survey (Geng, personal communication) conducted in German, Austrian and Swiss clinics and facilities treating patients with spinal cord injuries in 2010 revealed considerable heterogeneity in intermittent catheterisation (IC) techniques. There was therefore an urgent need to standardise the IC procedure (self-catheterisation and assisted catheterisation by a caregiver).

As previous recommendations were based solely on experience, members of the Neuro-Urology Working Group and the Nursing Working Group within the German-speaking Medical Society for Paraplegiology (DMGP) established an interdisciplinary, interprofessional working group, the ‘IC Guideline Group’ (ICGG), back in 2012.

The ICGG originally consisted of representatives from the professional groups of neuro-urology, surgery, health and nursing, nursing sciences, urotherapy, and hygiene.

IC is never a “one-size-fits-all” solution, but rather a form of therapy that must always be tailored individually to patients with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (regardless of its cause). In principle, the recommendations for performing IC outlined in this guideline also apply to non-neurogenic voiding disorders (e.g., postpartum, in cases of urogenital descent, following urogynecological surgery, or in cases of urethral stricture or benign prostatic hyperplasia).

Methods

The method used to prepare this guideline was determined by the class to which this guideline was assigned. The Association of the Scientific Medical Societies in Germany (AWMF) Guidance Manual (version 1.0) has set out the respective rules and requirements for different classes of guidelines. Guidelines are differentiated into lowest (S1), intermediate (S2), and highest (S3) classes [3]. The lowest class is defined as consisting of a set of recommendations for action compiled by a non-representative group of experts. In 2004, the S2 class was divided into two subclasses: a systematic evidence-based subclass (S2e) and a structural consensus-based subclass (S2k). The highest S3 class combines both approaches.

The ICGG developed an initial guideline as part of a structured consensus process (several consensus conferences, a nominal group process), which was published by the German Society of Urology (DGU) in 2014 (AWMF 043/048 [4]). Therefore, this guideline was classified as S2k.

The guideline

Introduction

It should be noted that even over the long-term period, aseptic catheterisation constitutes a low-complication method for emptying the urinary bladder that is pressure-free and free of residual urine for patients with neurogenic bladder dysfunctions. Depending on the setting (implementation site) and individualised needs of users, different demands need to be responded to during the implementation of IC.

With the consensus-based Guideline on IC presented here, the interdisciplinary, interprofessional ICGG aims to contribute to the standardisation of IC, eliminate uncertainties, and offer assistance to users (professionals, patients, and their caregivers). IC is never an “off-the-shelf” solution [5], but always a form of therapy that must be developed individually for patients with neurogenic dysfunction of the lower urinary tract. In principle, the recommendations for performing IC presented in this guideline also apply to non-neurogenic bladder emptying disorders (e.g. postpartum, urogenital descent, after urogynecological surgery, urethral stricture, or prostatic hyperplasia).

Disinfection of hands

Hand washing immediately before IC, which is often promoted as sufficient, is often not possible for wheelchair users in various situations. However, hand disinfection can also be carried out after rolling in a wheelchair from the washbasin to the toilet after washing hands, when supporting himself or herself on the wheels to achieve the proper sitting position or if IC is carried out in non-standard facilities due to toilets being unavailable.

8.2 Autonomic dysreflexia.

Any manipulation of the lower urinary tract (catheterisation) or overstretching of the bladder can trigger autonomic dysreflexia (AD) with a sudden critical rise in blood pressure, often associated with clinical symptoms, such as goose bumps, profuse sweating, and/or throbbing headaches.

Recommendation 13If autonomic dysreflexia occurs, the primary aim is to eliminate the cause—empty the bladderIf blood pressure crises persist, immediate treatment should be initiatedIf vegetative symptoms recur, AD should be objectively assessed from a neuro-urological perspective and therapeutic measures initiatedThe patient should be informed and an emergency card issued

Urinary tract infections

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are the most common complications associated with intermittent catheterisation. They are characterised by a combination of clinical symptoms and paraclinical findings (leukocyturia, bacteriuria, positive urine culture). For patients with intermittent catheterisation, the definition of a catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI—infection with an indwelling catheter) does not apply.

Discussion

In recent years, there has been increased discussion about the advantages and disadvantages of intermittent catheterisation and how it differs from indwelling catheterisation (IndC) as a principle of urinary bladder management in NLUTD. This is understandable and certainly justified in light of the demographic changes in the neuro-urological patient population [14] in recent years, especially in the spinal-cord-injury population. The foreword to the current version 3.0 of the IC guideline addresses this discussion in detail.

Overall, the literature highlights the difficulty of comparing different bladder emptying methods. There are various reasons for this, such as the different patient groups and indications for IC or IndC care, which in the available cohort studies lead to comparison groups that sometimes differ greatly from one another. The definition of symptomatic urinary tract infections is particularly challenging in people with neurogenic dysfunction of the urinary tract, as sensitivity in the urinary tract can vary and symptoms of the underlying disease can overlap with symptoms of urinary tract infection.

The diversity of the data is discussed in the literature. The authors of a Cochrane review [15] conclude that no recommendation can be made regarding the type of use and material for IC in relation to UTI frequency and other complications due to the lack of randomised-controlled trials. Nevertheless, Shekelle et al. [16] and Kinnear et al. [17] conclude after systematic reviews that there is a tendency for intermittent catheterisation to be advantageous. However, further studies in other clinical settings would be necessary.

Despite a level of evidence of “only” 3, the current European Association of Urology (EAU) guideline ‘Neuro-Urology’ strongly recommends the use of intermittent catheterisation as standard treatment for patients who are unable to empty their bladder [18].

Therapy recommendations based on evidence-based medicine are supported by three pillars: relevant scientific evidence, the clinical expertise of practitioners, and the wishes and values of patients. A recent survey study from the Netherlands involving over 3300 patients confirms the advantages of IC over suprapubic catheterisation (SPC) and IndC in terms of patient satisfaction and quality of life [19].

Even though high-level evidence from scientific studies is currently largely lacking, intermittent catheterisation represents the "gold standard" for bladder emptying in cases of NLUTD as treatment for voiding problems due to disturbances or injuries to the nervous system or non-neurogenic bladder dysfunction with incomplete bladder emptying [20].

In order to respond to patients’ wishes and values, comprehensive information is required, taking into account the individual situation, possible complications, especially in the long-term, quality of life, and social participation.

Although intermittent catheterisation has been a proven method for decades and has been widely used, until the 2010s, many uncertainties existed amongst those performing the procedure and especially amongst nurses who taught patients how to use it. One reason for this uncertainty was the lack of guidelines and standardised instructions.

In 2013, the first evidence-based guideline from the EAUN (European Association of Urological Nurses) for the best practice in urological nursing was published, explicitly addressing intermittent catheterisation (and intermittent urethral dilation). An update was published in 2024 with numerous current references and a reduction in recommendations from 78 to 57 [21]. In 2014, the first edition of the IC guidelines presented here for Germany was published, with updates every five years. Since then, further guidelines from Canada (2020 [22]) and France (2020 [23]) have been published with explicit recommendations on intermittent catheterisation and numerous tips for its practical application. These guidelines differ only in nuances, largely due to varying national legal requirements.

Another paper on Best Practice and Algorithm on IC in Neurogenic Bladder [24], based on literature published in the last five years with numerous practical tips was published in 2025. Despite these evidence-based recommendations, it remains a challenge to translate the current knowledge about a standardised approach to IC into everyday nursing practice [25].

Therefore, the S2k guideline on IC presented here was introduced in numerous lectures and workshops at various national congresses and discussed with users, particularly from the nursing sector. The guideline is available as a freely downloadable PDF file in several online databases: AWMF, DMGP, and the Neuro-Urology Association (Verein Neuro-Urologie e.V.). In addition, there is a brochure funded by a foundation that is also freely available. The guideline is an integral part of the training of medical students and specialised paraplegic care nurses. In spinal-cord-injury centres in Germany, regular continuing education is provided for all IC users (healthcare professionals) based on this guideline. Finally, the guideline serves as the basis for training the trainers and instructing patients on the use of the IC. Through staff members from various professional societies—including those from nursing and amongst users—the ICGG regularly receives real-world feedback, which is integrated into the regularly required revisions of the guidelines.

A recent scoping review [26] examines in detail the potential barriers faced by patients and healthcare professionals that may prevent the use of intermittent catheterisation. Physical and functional limitations—which, for example, in patients with spinal cord injury naturally depend on the level of paralysis, or in other neurological conditions on the extent of neurological damage—can be decisive for the success or failure of introducing intermittent catheterisation. Training, ongoing support, individually tailored selection of IC products, urinary tract infections and other medical complications, the timing and location of IC procedures, and emotional adjustment processes can all influence the success of IC implementation. The availability of evidence-based guidelines for a structured, standardised approach, together with detailed verbal explanations and practical instructions, also supports the success of IC implementation. This naturally also applies outside the actual scope of application of the guideline presented in Germany.

Even though the development of guidelines, even at the consensus level, is time-consuming and labour-intensive and often fraught with unexpected difficulties, it is extremely valuable—especially in areas where there are few high-quality scientific studies (such as RCTs—randomised-controlled trials) and where it is unlikely that many more will be carried out.

Of course, we must already, and probably even more so in the future, take into account the changes in the demographic background of patients with NLUTD. This includes age and sex, increased comorbidities, and altered causes and neurological characteristics of patients with bladder dysfunction [27].

On the other hand, we will face challenges in the future regarding increasing cost pressures from insurance systems, even in developed countries. Issues of sustainability and environmental compatibility may also gain greater prominence. This may accelerate the development of regulatory-approved, reusable single-use catheters in the future. This guideline will respond to these changes as part of the necessary updates.

Conclusions

This guideline, based on consensus-based evidence, has proven itself in clinical and everyday use for over 10 years. Due to the regular further development of the guideline, the guideline will also be able to meet future challenges, such as changing demographic conditions of our patients with an increase in older patients with complex medical situations and increased use of permanent urinary catheter drainage or increased cost pressure in the healthcare system, even in developed countries.

Consensus-based guidelines are an important component of evidence-based medicine, especially in situations where there are few randomised-controlled trials and none are expected in the future. Such guidelines can also address aspects that are likely to become even more important in the future, such as sustainability and environmental issues.
