A non-invasive end-to-end intelligent assistance system for breast ultrasound
Authors: Jun Zhou, Pilei Si, Yan Zhang, Jianqiong Song, Tao He, Qin Liu, Shaoqi Chen, Mengjie Tu, Han Wang, Qiang Guo, Xiaofeng Qi, Danli Zhang, Qi Zhang, Tingting Zhang, Linlin Pan, Shilin Li, Zhengzhong Tang, Julie Guo, Haitao Lan, Xue Li, Yan Tang, Min Tang, Qingyang Shen, Haiying Luo, Zhongbing Lang, Xiangqian Hu, Hui Du, Rongmei Tian, Xiaoxue Yang, Xiaoqin Gan, Fang Chen, Xuebin Liu, Wenqin Zhang, Qianrong Xie, Jie Zhang, Hang Yang, Xue Yang, Yuanli Kang, Minghao Xiong, Yuwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although artificial intelligence enhances medical image classification to effectively improve lesion diagnosis accuracy and efficiency, it still faces generalization challenges in breast ultrasound and its clinical potential remains underutilized in complex real-world scenarios. Here, we develop and test an end-to-end breast intelligent recognition device (BIRD) for women across diverse institution/population datasets. The accuracy of BIRD in the internal test set is 0.837 (95% confidence interval: 0.827–0.846), which significantly improves radiologists’ accuracy in 2 reader studies (P < 0.05). BIRD is applied in breast cancer screening for 6,817 individuals and shows high consistency (Cohen’s kappa: 0.702 (95% confidence interval: 0.628-0.777)) with clinical assessments in real-world application across 107 hospitals. Pathological and molecular subtype models developed using data from five hospitals also exhibit satisfactory classification performance. These findings confirm BIRD’s ability to improve diagnostic accuracy, assist screening, and characterize breast lesions, facilitating clinical adoption in breast health practice. The Chinese Clinical Trial Register: ChiCTR2300073777.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide1,2, with early detection being the cornerstone of improved survival. In Asian women, who tend to have dense breast tissue, mammography’s sensitivity is markedly reduced, and ultrasound has emerged as the primary screening modality3. However, ultrasound interpretation is highly operator-dependent, and although the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) classification is standardized, it remains subjective4,5, leading to inter-reader variability that may cause unnecessary biopsies or delayed diagnosis.

Beyond morphologic assessment, breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease defined by histologic and molecular phenotypes (e.g., invasive ductal carcinoma, invasive lobular carcinoma, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS); luminal A, luminal B, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) enriched, triple negative)6–8. Accurate pre-operative knowledge of these subtypes is essential for selecting neoadjuvant therapy and surgical strategies9. However, current determination relies on invasive biopsies and pathological analysis, causing patient discomfort and treatment delays that could compromise the therapeutic window. Breast ultrasound, as a non-invasive, real-time imaging modality with universal clinical availability, captures tissue architectural patterns that may reflect molecular characteristics. We therefore hypothesize that the dedicated artificial intelligence (AI) models can directly predict histologic classification and molecular subtypes from ultrasound images, enabling timely, personalized clinical decisions.

With the advent of deep learning, AI has made significant contributions to emerging fields, including molecular feature recognition, molecular structure prediction, drug development, and smart health care10–14. In image recognition, AI efficiently analyzes intricate medical images with remarkable precision and speed, offering distinct advantages15–18. While AI-driven ultrasound analysis has notable success in benign-malignant discrimination19,20, current systems remain fragmented: they either focus narrowly on binary classification with limited attention to BI‑RADS standardization21,22 or molecular heterogeneity23,24, or lack large‑scale prospective evaluation and real‑world generalizability across diverse clinical settings. Consequently, an end‑to‑end AI framework that seamlessly integrates screening‑level decision support with pathology‑grade subtyping remains an unmet need, limiting its integration into comprehensive clinical workflows and hindering translation into routine practice.

In this work, to address this unmet need, we initiate the Breast Evaluation and Ultrasound Technology (BEST) study to develop, externally test, and prospectively deploy an end-to-end AI system that integrates standardized BI-RADS reporting, benign‑malignant triage, histologic classification, and molecular subtyping directly from breast ultrasound (Fig. 1). These models use distinct label sources, with radiologist consensus review for BI‑RADS and pathology‑confirmed labels for triage, histology, and molecular tasks. Results

Characteristics of the datasets

In total, 64,940 ultrasound images with assigned BI-RADS categories were included in the breast intelligent recognition device (BIRD) development and multiple evaluation settings (comprising multi-center external testing, international external testing, three reader studies). These labels were established through a three-level annotation protocol by experienced radiologists, and the distribution across BI-RADS categories 1-5 is detailed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. In addition, the BIRD was subjected to two key clinical application scenarios for comprehensive evaluation. The dataset from Dazhou Central Hospital (DzCH) constituted the development cohort and was partitioned into internal training, optimization, and test subsets. Each subset includes 18,201, 2739, and 5478 images, respectively. In addition, the external test subset comprised 36,326 ultrasound images from a collective of 8 hospitals and 2 international datasets (details on image cleaning and dataset description, see Supplementary Methods 1, 2, Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and Fig. 2). To enhance the system’s generalizability, the ultrasound images gathered from diverse hospitals across the nation and from different ultrasound devices were not normalized. Performance of BIRD on international datasets

For the international datasets BUSI and BUS, the accuracies of BIRD were 0.661 (95% CI: 0.625–0.697) and 0.604 (95% CI: 0.529–0.680). Error analysis revealed that misclassifications predominantly reflected the inherent limitations of static-frame interpretation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Non-standard scanning planes complicated the interpretation of benign cases, while visual ambiguity and overlapping diagnostic criteria complicated intermediate category assignments. False negatives principally involved subtle lesions with ill-defined characteristics. Critically, the lack of dynamic contextual information fundamentally constrained model sensitivity relative to conventional multiplane ultrasound workflows.

Comparison of AI system performance with that of radiologists

To better apply the BIRD in clinical settings, we conducted 3 reader studies to compare the classification performance of BI-RADS, including a retrospective reader study, a prospective reader study, and a prospective, randomized and comparative trial (PRCT).

To establish the baseline diagnostic performance of BIRD relative to radiologists with different levels of expertise, we first conducted a retrospective reader study with a total of 669 ultrasound images (Supplementary Table 2). A total of 14 radiologists were enrolled, categorized into four groups: trainee (n = 3), junior (n = 4), senior (n = 4), and expert (n = 3). In the retrospective reader study, the average accuracies of the trainee, junior, senior, and expert radiologists were 0.682 (95% CI: 0.646–0.717), 0.722 (95% CI: 0.694–0.750), 0.719 (95% CI: 0.669–0.768) and 0.721 (95% CI: 0.536–0.907), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Table 2). BIRD achieved an accuracy of 0.701 (95% CI: 0.666–0.736), and a sensitivity of 0.537 (95% CI: 0.503–0.573), all of which were statistically similar to the performance of 11 radiologists (4 trainees, 3 junior, 3 senior, 1 expert; all P > 0.05). Nationwide real-world application of BIRD

To better comprehend the clinical application effect, we conducted a prospective implementation study of the BIRD across a total of 107 hospitals spanning the eastern, western, southern, and northern regions of China (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 2). Following strict data quality screening, the final analytical cohort comprised 38 hospitals representing diverse tiers: 17 tertiary, 20 secondary, and 1 community hospital (Supplementary Data 3). Population-based breast cancer screening implementation study

To evaluate BIRD’s multi-scenario applicability, we conducted a prospective screening implementation study at 10 screening sites. A total of 6910 women were enrolled, with 6817 comprising the final analysis cohort after deduplication (Supplementary Fig. 6). Among them, 5373 individuals (78.82%) were classified as low risk (BI-RADS 1-2), 1143 (16.77%) as medium risk (BI-RADS 0 and 3), and 301 (4.42%) as high risk (BI-RADS 4-5) (Fig. 4b). The BIRD risk stratification offered crucial guidance for clinical decision-making, which enabled doctors to formulate diagnostic and treatment plans according to different risk levels. A subsequent follow-up of the high-risk group (n = 301), which had a median follow-up of 13 months, found that 3 patients were diagnosed with breast cancer.

Integrated ancillary systems

To facilitate AI-driven breast disease management, we also developed 4 practical systems: the BIRD cloud diagnosis platform, the breast ultrasound teaching system, the mammography intelligent diagnosis system and the scientific research management platform (Supplementary Fig. 7).

First, we developed the BIRD cloud diagnosis platform, which enabled multi-institutional model sharing and evaluation through unified login and standardized, on-demand inference workflows while ensuring patient privacy via real-time processing without data retention. Second, we developed a breast ultrasound teaching system that curated a comprehensive image repository with detailed annotations of lesion morphology, echogenicity, and blood flow signals, allowing novice trainees to systematically study sonographic attributes and accelerate competency development. Third, we developed a mammography intelligent diagnosis system employing deep learning algorithms to automatically detect suspicious lesions and perform benign/malignant classification. Finally, we developed a scientific research management platform that enhanced analytical efficiency through integrated modules for statistical analysis, automated data standardization, text conversion, and table processing.

Performance evaluation of the pathological and molecular subtype models

Visual interpretation of the models

To better understand the decision-making process of the models and verify their attention to clinically relevant features, the predictions of our model were visualized by gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM). Grad-CAM visualization in Supplementary Fig. 9 revealed that the BIRD model’s attention distribution across the seven BI-RADS categories corresponded well with diagnostic sonographic principles: minimal background activation for BI-RADS 1, circumferential low-level activation for BI-RADS 2, moderate focus on regular margins for BI-RADS 3, progressive malignant enhancement across BI-RADS 4a-4c (with incrementally increasing activation of spiculated margins and microcalcifications), and focal concentration on highly suspicious malignant features for BI-RADS 5. The BMM model demonstrated centrally dense activation for malignant samples versus a peripheral weak activation ring for benign samples. The ICM and MSM models achieved discrimination between invasive carcinoma and DCIS and identification of molecular subtypes through distinct heat zone distribution patterns. Despite overall robust interpretability, occasional diffuse mis-activation in low-suspicion samples (BI-RADS 3/4a/5). These findings suggest that Grad-CAM provides interpretability for the model’s decision-making process.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a suite of AI systems based on deep learning algorithms for comprehensive breast disease management using ultrasound images. These systems include the BIRD for BI-RADS classification and lesion localization, the BMM for distinguishing benign and malignant lesions, the ICM for pathological subtype, and the MSM for molecular subtype of breast cancer. Our primary results demonstrated that BIRD achieved reliable diagnostic accuracy, with an internal test set accuracy of 0.837 (95% CI: 0.827–0.846) and accuracies ranging from 0.708 (95% CI: 0.697–0.718) to 0.913 (95% CI: 0.903–0.922) across eight external datasets. Notably, the BIRD significantly improved radiologists’ accuracy in two reader studies (P < 0.05). Critically, the nationwide deployment across 107 hospitals demonstrated stable AI performance despite institutional heterogeneity, achieving a mean Cohen’s kappa of 0.702 (95% CI: 0.628–0.777) with local radiologists. This consistency across diverse healthcare systems suggests BIRD can serve as a reproducible diagnostic reference, a prerequisite for standardizing breast ultrasound reporting. Additionally, the pathological subtype models (BMM and ICM) achieved AUC values of 0.965 (95% CI: 0.964–0.966) and 0.924 (95% CI: 0.922–0.925), respectively, while the MSM showed AUCs ranging from 0.753 (95% CI: 0.667–0.828) to 0.809 (95% CI: 0.750–0.868) for different molecular subtypes. Collectively, these results highlighted the potential of our AI systems to enhance diagnostic accuracy, facilitate early breast cancer screening, and contribute to the comprehensive characterization of breast diseases, thereby promising improved patient care and outcomes.

AI models have demonstrated remarkable potential in the realm of medical image classification, at times surpassing the capabilities of experienced physicians16,19,25–27. However, a considerable portion of these models remains largely confined to research laboratories, primarily due to challenges that impede their practical implementation20,28,29. The key among these challenges is the dependence on extensive, high-quality datasets for training and external testing. Acquiring a sufficient volume of data, characterized by both quantity and quality, is a formidable task in health care, primarily because of the sensitive nature of patient information and the imperative need to safeguard medical data privacy. While we overcame this through multi-center data collection, our study reveals that even with robust datasets, large-scale deployment poses a more fundamental translational gap: operational robustness under resource heterogeneity.

The large-scale deployment of BIRD across 107 clinical institutions addresses this directly. This was achieved through strategic integration that positioned AI systems adjacent to ultrasound devices for real-time inference, seamlessly embedding them into clinical workflows (Supplementary Video 1). Crucially, performance variation across 38 deployment sites should not be interpreted as model deficiency, but as quantification of pre-existing inter-institutional discordance that AI is designed to mitigate. BIRD’s maintenance a specificity > 0.936 across multi-center external test sets, despite diverse equipment and institutional contexts, reinforces its role as a standardization instrument that attenuates subspecialty expertise disparities, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Unlike Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared tools evaluated solely in controlled environments, BIRD’s prospective multi-site implementation addresses a critical regulatory gap where post-market surveillance data for AI devices remains scarce, offering a scalable governance template through continuous monitoring infrastructure. Critically, this deployed infrastructure demonstrated transformative clinical impact: breast cancer screening of 6817 individuals safely triaged 5373 low-risk cases (78.8%) through AI-automated reporting, partially alleviating radiologist workload while confirming 3 cancers in the high-risk cohort (4.4% prevalence), thereby demonstrating pragmatic utility beyond accuracy metrics alone.

Advanced AI technologies provide valuable supplements to health care, assisting in the detection of early signs of disease and facilitating timely interventions and treatments30,31. For instance, in the case of fundus examination, AI systems can be used to detect pathological features associated with liver diseases, whereas chest computed tomography (CT) image analysis aids in the early identification of EGFR mutations and is crucial for early cancer diagnosis32,33. Furthermore, by integrating individual characteristics and genetic information, AI systems have the potential to provide personalized diagnostic and treatment plans, significantly enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of medical care34–36. In our research, we focused on developing a model for pathological and molecular subtyping based on ultrasound images. The aim was to predict the pathological and molecular features of patients during the early ultrasound screening phase, thereby increasing the rate of early detection of breast diseases and reducing the need for unnecessary biopsies.

Compared with previous studies, we have not only achieved the classification of breast masses as benign or malignant based on B-mode ultrasound images (AUC: 0.965), but also the classification performance for benign and malignant cases is superior to that of radiologists jointly reading B-mode ultrasound, elastography, and Doppler ultrasound (AUC: 0.844)37, as well as the performance of the benign-malignant model constructed by Evain et al. based on B-mode ultrasound images (AUC: 0.690)38. To contextualize these results within the regulatory landscape, we further compared BMM with three U.S. FDA-cleared AI tools. The resulting AUC of 0.965 numerically exceeds the values reported for S-Detect (0.725)39 and BU-CAD (0.820)40 and is comparable to that of Koios DS (0.945)41. Moreover, our model could differentiate between DCIS and invasive breast cancer, an area of research that has been minimally explored before. As a non-invasive precursor lesion with a significantly better prognosis compared to invasive breast cancer, early detection of DCIS is pivotal for improving patient outcomes. Our pathological subtype model demonstrates the potential to identify DCIS at the early ultrasound screening stage, which is crucial for enabling timely clinical intervention and optimizing treatment strategies.

In addition, we introduced an updated molecular subtyping classification system, extending it to a more precise 5-category system that encompasses the luminal B-HER2 subtype23,42. This enhancement is expected to provide better guidance for clinical decisions and offer more personalized treatment options for patients. We trained and tested our pathological and molecular subtype models using multi-center data from various hospitals nationwide, not only ensuring the availability of extensive data but also confirming the robustness and generalizability of the model. Consequently, our research holds the promise of significant improvements in the early diagnosis and personalized treatment of breast diseases in the future, ultimately increasing patients’ quality of life and survival rates.

Several limitations warrant consideration while contextualizing our extensive real-world deployment. Performance variations across international datasets indicate that BIRD requires further validation before clinical deployment outside the development and domestic validation setting. These discrepancies predominantly reflect static-frame constraints where nonstandard scanning planes introduce confounding artifacts, morphological overlap complicates intermediate BI-RADS categories, and subtle lesions with low contrast risk underdetection. While failure analysis identifies legitimate technical factors, these findings do not eliminate the need for protocol-agnostic robustness testing and prospective multicenter evaluation in diverse healthcare systems. The false negative rate (FNR) remains within clinically acceptable thresholds for an adjunctive tool, though targeted integration of dynamic imaging context promises further refinement. Our 107-hospital implementation demonstrates that inter-institutional variability can be quantified and partially mitigated through AI standardization, though continued prospective evaluation will be essential for extending generalizability to diverse healthcare systems. Furthermore, agreement (Cohen’s kappa) with local radiologists reflects implementation concordance rather than diagnostic accuracy. The 10-site screening program employed risk-stratified follow-up focused on high-risk participants, which is appropriate for feasibility assessment but limits comprehensive performance characterization, highlighting the distinct need for a formal screening trial with complete follow-up.

Furthermore, while unimodal ultrasound data proved effective, future enhancement through multi-modal integration (e.g., mammography, clinical variables) may capture additional biological dimensions. Inter-observer variability in BI-RADS classification, even among experts, represents an area where AI systems can reduce subjectivity. While we mitigated this through multi-expert consensus, systematic standardization is still needed. The screening program’s verification bias and follow-up duration reflect its feasibility study design; definitive efficacy metrics will be established through future randomized controlled trials. These incremental steps align with the translational imperative of balancing rapid clinical deployment with rigorous, continuous performance optimization.

Our research underscored the importance of multi-center and multi-layer independent evaluation, shifting the research focus from single performance metrics to broader robustness and generalizability. Furthermore, our 107-hospital implementation revealed that inter-institutional variability can be quantified and partially mitigated through AI standardization, extending beyond traditional performance metrics. This experience indicates that future breast AI development should prioritize workflow integration, expertise-adaptive interaction frameworks, and continuous post-market evaluation. Such objectives are most robustly assessed through prospective, real-world deployment rather than retrospective benchmarks alone.

Methods

Human ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of DzCH and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Register (ChiCTR2300073777). All procedures were executed in strict accordance with the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was not required, as the study data were deidentified and were part of a healthcare action.

Study design

We conducted the BEST study, an extensive exploration of breast health using a study design that included 4 AI systems (BIRD, BMM, ICM and MSM) targeting distinct clinical decision points, with all participants were female (sex determined based on clinical records) (Fig. 2). The first AI system, the BIRD, which detects and classifies lesions according to the BI-RADS classification, was developed utilizing ultrasound images from DzCH. The classification performance of the BIRD was rigorously tested on multiple datasets, including datasets from 2 international sources and 8 hospitals across China. Three reader studies were conducted to compare the performance of 19 radiologists with that of the BIRD, offering insights into its clinical applicability. For nationwide real-world applications, the BIRD was deployed in 107 hospitals, highlighting its adaptability and usability. Simultaneously, a large-scale project in 10 hospitals provided free screening for early detection of breast cancer. For robust evaluation, we meticulously separated the data in each cohort to avoid overlap. Moreover, dedicated systems (BMM, ICM and MSM) were developed for the automatic pathological and molecular classification of breast cancer based on ultrasound images from 5 hospitals to increase the diagnostic depth. BMM was a benign and malignant classification model, ICM was a model to distinguish invasive carcinoma from DCIS, and MSM was a molecular subtype model (luminal A, luminal B (HER2 negative), luminal B (HER2 positive), HER2 enriched, and triple negative) of breast cancer. BMM, ICM and MSM were only classification models. All models were evaluated using metrics aligned to their clinical objectives (Supplementary Table 5).

Ultrasound images included in the datasets of this study were acquired using multiple types of clinical ultrasound systems from mainstream manufacturers, covering Canon, Esaote, GE Healthcare, Hitachi, Mindray, Philips, Siemens, SonoScape, and Samsung. The specific models of the ultrasonic equipment, along with their distribution across individual datasets, are detailed in Supplementary Data 1.

Three-level annotation

The radiologists performed a three-level annotation of the images for BI-RADS classification. In the first annotation round, for all ultrasound images containing multiple lesions, each lesion underwent delineation using a rectangular box and was assigned a BI-RADS classification individually (lesions were classified as BI-RADS 1, BI-RADS 2, BI-RADS 3, BI-RADS 4a, BI-RADS 4b, BI-RADS 4c, or BI-RADS 5, and the evaluation criteria were based on the American College of Radiology (ACR) BI-RADS43.). In the second annotation round, a distinct group of radiologists reviewed the annotations from the first round. Any disagreements prompted adjustments, with these differences documented. Instances of inconsistent opinions between the two rounds were reviewed by a fifth radiologist. The final label was determined considering all available clinical information related to the patient. Five radiologists, each possessing 4–16 years of experience, participated in annotating the ultrasound images. Three-level annotations were adopted for the true labels in the BIRD’s internal datasets, multi-center external test datasets, international datasets and three reader studies.

BIRD development and internal evaluation

A total of 147,862 images from 20,445 individuals at DzCH were collected from April 2017 to August 2020 for BIRD development. Identical inclusion criteria were employed for the internal and external datasets, and identical image cleaning procedures were performed. Following cleaning, three-level annotation was conducted to label the images (for details of image cleaning, see Supplementary Method 1). The development cohort comprised 26,418 images from DzCH, which were divided into internal training (18,201), optimization (2739), and test (5478) sets at an approximate ratio of 7:1:2 (Fig. 2). The BIRD utilized the YOLOv5 localization algorithm to identify lesions in breast ultrasound images and the EfficientNet classification algorithm for BI-RADS classification of the identified lesions (for details of the development of the BIRD, see Supplementary Method 3 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Finally, the BIRD provided real-time localization and BI-RADS classification (BI-RADS 1/2/3/4a/4b/4c/5) information for each lesion in each ultrasound image.

External test datasets

In total, 41,382 images from 5321 individuals across 8 hospitals, along with 766 images from 2 international datasets, were retrospectively collected for the external testing of the BIRD. The international datasets, namely the Breast Ultrasound Images (BUSI)44 dataset and the Breast Ultrasound Lesions (BUS) dataset20, were acquired from a global public database. The BUSI comprised data from individuals in Egypt, and the BUS from individuals in Spain. Following the data preprocessing operations (for details of participants selection and images cleaning, see Supplementary Method 1), 35,560 images from the 8 hospitals, 614 images from the BUSI, and 152 images from the BUS were employed for further analysis (Fig. 2).

Reader study

We conducted 3 reader studies to compare the performance of radiologists and the BIRD in BI-RADS classification. When reading ultrasound images, the readers were tasked with delineating the lesions by drawing rectangular outlines and assigning a BI-RADS classification accordingly.

The retrospective reader study included 669 images selected from DzCH and 8 other hospitals, aiming to compare the performance of the BIRD with that of radiologists. Fourteen radiologists with varying experience levels participated: 3 trainees (with an average experience of 2.5 years), 4 junior radiologists (with an average experience of 4.7 years), 4 senior radiologists (with an average experience of 10.3 years), and 3 expert radiologists (with an average experience of 14.3 years). McNemar’s test compared BIRD against each of 14 radiologists, with Holm-Bonferroni step-down correction applied.

In the prospective reader study, 6 readers evaluated images in two rounds. In the first round, the images were evaluated without BIRD assistance, and in the second round, which occurred after a 20-day washout period, the images were evaluated with BIRD assistance. The six readers included 2 trainees, 2 junior radiologists, and 2 senior radiologists, 5 of whom participated in the retrospective reader study. Between April and September 2021, we collected ultrasound data from 1436 participants across three hospitals: Nanchong Central Hospital (NCH, n = 423), XinBei Community Healthcare Center of Chengdu High-Tech Zone (XBCH, n = 82), and the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (STFH, n = 931). Following the standardized image cleaning protocol (Supplementary Method 1), we retained only the highest-quality representative image per individual, yielding a final cohort of 527 images. These images were used to evaluate the performance of radiologists both with and without the assistance of BIRD. To comprehensively assess the AI effect, we predefined two co-primary endpoints: (1) the population-averaged effect of AI assistance in the AI correctness subset (cases where the AI prediction was correct), and (2) the population-averaged effect in the AI error subset (cases where the AI prediction was incorrect). Prior to pooling data across readers, we assessed between-reader heterogeneity using Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics. The absence of substantial heterogeneity supported the use of generalized estimating equations (GEE) to estimate population-averaged effects. We analyzed the two co-primary endpoints using GEE without adjustment for multiple comparisons, as these represented distinct prespecified hypotheses. For individual reader analyses, McNemar’s test was used (12 comparisons, Holm-Bonferroni corrected).

In the PRCT, ultrasound data were gathered from 1362 individuals at DzCH between August 1 and September 1, 2020. After a two-stage exclusion process, 469 individuals with 1000 images were included (Fig. 2). Two-stage exclusion removed 358 individuals for age < 18 years, prior breast surgery, or pregnancy/lactation, and 535 for technically inadequate images (lacking visible breast tissue or poor image quality). We randomly allocated these images to dataset a or b (500 images each) using computer-generated stratified randomization by BI-RADS categorization; both allocation and ground-truth labels were concealed from radiologists throughout the reading sessions (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5). A total of four readers, distinct from those radiologists involved in the prior two reader studies and possessing an average of two years of experience as trainees, participated in the image evaluation. These readers were randomly assigned to two groups, group A and group B, each comprising two individuals. Throughout the reading sessions, neither group A nor group B had access to the actual labels of the images, ensuring the blinding of the assessment process. Group A read images from both datasets a and b without BIRD assistance, while group B read images from dataset b without BIRD assistance. After a 20-day washout period, both groups revisited the images in both datasets, this time with BIRD assistance. The purpose of evaluation different datasets was to eliminate the data’s impact on the radiologists’ decisions and to more rigorously compare readers’ performance in interpreting images with and without BIRD support. The overall AI effect across four radiologists was analyzed using random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird method) due to between-reader heterogeneity (single primary endpoint, no multiplicity adjustment); secondary analyses comprised within-dataset comparisons (McNemar’s test, Holm-Bonferroni corrected, 2 comparisons) and cross-dataset exploratory analyses (Chi-square test, Holm-Bonferroni corrected, 2 comparisons). Dataset balance was assessed using Chi-square tests without correction for multiple comparisons.

Clinical implementation

To further assess the applicability and usability of the BIRD, we conducted nationwide real-world applications and tests. The deployment of the BIRD within the ultrasound department enabled real-time acquisition of ultrasound imagery and the delivery of simultaneous intelligent diagnostic support (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Video 1). To ensure that radiologists could readily access the intelligent diagnostic outcomes, the BIRD system was positioned in close proximity to the ultrasound devices. This strategic placement was critical for integrating the diagnostic assistance into the clinical workflow. From April 2021 to September 2022, BIRD was deployed to 107 hospitals across China in a prospective feasibility evaluation to assess the model’s performance in different health care systems by calculating the consistency between the BIRD and radiologists. Among these, 43 hospitals submitted feedback tables recording paired BI-RADS gradings from both BIRD and radiologists for the same images; radiologists at these sites enrolled eligible participants per predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data statisticians subsequently evaluated the reliability of submitted tables, excluding hospitals with fewer than 10 cases or incomplete data. A total of 38 hospitals (9320 individuals) were included in the final consistency analysis.

Between March 2022 and May 2023, we conducted a large-scale project in 10 hospitals, providing free breast cancer screenings for individuals to facilitate early detection (Supplementary Fig. 6). This was a prospective implementation-feasibility study evaluating AI-enabled portable ultrasound for population screening. A total of 6910 eligible women ( ≥ 18 years, non-pregnant/lactating, without prior total mastectomy) registered via mobile app or on-site appointments, of whom 6817 comprised the final analysis cohort after deduplication. During the breast cancer screening, we deployed the AI system beside the portable ultrasound device. This AI system provided intelligent diagnosis in real time and predict the high/medium/low risks for each lesion (Fig. 4). To facilitate screening, we developed a mobile app for scheduling screenings. After the screening, individuals could view the report on their mobile phones.

Pathological and molecular subtype models

To gain in-depth diagnostic insights, we spearheaded the development of automatic classification systems for identifying pathological and molecular subtypes of breast cancer. A total of 2792 images were collected from 1392 patients from 5 hospitals to develop 2 binary classification models, namely, the BMM and ICM, for pathological subtype recognition (Fig. 2). The dataset included 1043 benign images, 394 DCIS images, and 1355 invasive carcinoma images. A total of 6356 ultrasound images from 1655 patients were collected from 4 hospitals for the development of the MSM. All patient images included in the development and the internal hold-out test of the BMM, ICM and MSM were labeled with the pathological diagnostic information (Fig. 2). At the same time, we also verified the performance of BMM using two international datasets. Specifically, the BUS dataset contains 152 images, including 101 benign and 51 malignant images, while the BUSI dataset comprises 520 images, with 345 benign and 175 malignant images.

The BMM was designed to distinguish between benign and malignant lesions, while the ICM aimed to distinguish between invasive carcinoma and DCIS. The training methods for the BMM and ICM were the same: both involved the use of EfficientNetV2. The images were randomly divided into training, optimization, and test sets at a 7:2:1 ratio (for details on the development of the BMM and ICM, see Supplementary Methods 4).

We developed a model capable of classifying breast cancer into 5 distinct subtypes: luminal A, luminal B (HER2 negative), luminal B (HER2 positive), HER2-enriched, and triple negative45. We divided the data into training, optimization, and test sets, maintaining a ratio of 8:1:1. For the model architecture, we utilized ResNet18, initializing it with weights pretrained on the ImageNet dataset (for details on the development of the MSM, see Supplementary Methods 5).

Heat map generation

To enhance the interpretability of the predictions made by our models, we used the Grad-CAM algorithm46. Grad-CAM generates heatmaps that visually highlight the regions of interest within breast ultrasound images, thereby providing insights into the areas the models focus on during prediction.

Statistical analysis

We evaluated the performance of the classification models using evaluation metrics such as the AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), false positive rate (FPR) and FNR (for details on the calculation formula, see Supplementary Methods 6). One-vs-Rest (OvR) macro-averaging was employed for AUC, sensitivity, specificity, FPR, FNR, PPV, NPV, and F1-score; accuracy was calculated as the global proportion of correctly classified cases. A confusion matrix was also used to provide a more detailed proportion of true and predicted labels. The 95% CIs for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, PPV, NPV, FPR and FNR were calculated through bootstrapping. Reader studies employed study-specific analytical approaches: the retrospective study used McNemar’s test for paired comparisons between BIRD and 14 radiologists; the prospective study used GEE for primary endpoints and McNemar’s test for individual readers; the PRCT used random-effects meta-analysis for the overall effect, McNemar’s test for within-dataset comparisons, and Chi-square tests for cross-dataset comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided. Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied to individual-level comparisons involving multiple radiologists (retrospective: 14; prospective: 12; PRCT: 2 per family), whereas prespecified primary endpoints were not subject to multiplicity adjustment. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. After the application of our AI system, Cohen’s kappa was used to compare the consistency between the AI system and radiologists. The statistical analyses were performed using Python (Version 3.8) and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25).

Computational infrastructure and software

All models were developed on a workstation equipped with four NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs. The software environment comprised Ubuntu 20.04.4 LTS. Training followed parameters specified in Supplementary Methods: BIRD used Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.0002, batch size 64) for 60 epochs; BMM and ICM used SGD (learning rate 0.0001, weight decay 0.0005, batch size 8) for 300 epochs; MSM used Adam (learning rate 0.001, batch size 256) for 100 epochs.

BIRD was deployed on Lenovo Legion R9000P laptops for real-world implementation, each configured with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU (8 GB VRAM), AMD Ryzen 9 7945HX CPU, and 15.3 GiB DDR5 memory, running Ubuntu 20.04.4 LTS. Inference latency enabled real-time clinical workflow integration.
