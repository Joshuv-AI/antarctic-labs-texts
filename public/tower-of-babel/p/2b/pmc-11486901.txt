Progressive failure mechanism of existing-supplementary double-layer piles retaining excavation beneath existing underground space
Authors: Zuoqiang Chi, Meixu Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing degree of urbanization has resulted in traffic and space congestion. When there is no longer any aboveground space for development, the existing space underground is excavated, and the construction of underground buildings is expected to solve the above problems. In the excavation of an underground layer, it is necessary to drive supplementary and existing piles into the soil to form a double-layer pile-supported structure. Aiming at an existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure and considering the pile spacing of the supplementary piles, use of crown beams, and other factors, this study performed a series of indoor model tests and an simulation analysis in combination with finite element analysis software to reveal the bearing characteristics of this structure. The results showed that the installation of supplementary piles could effectively inhibit soil slippage after pile driving, redistribute the soil pressure load of the existing piles, and reduce the number of soil cracks between the piles. With the increase in the supplementary pile spacing, the deformation of the existing–supplementary double-stacked piles gradually intensified, and the bending moment of the pile body and the horizontal displacement at the top of the piles increased. The installation of crown beams in the existing piles could strengthen the connection between the existing and supplementary piles, reduce the number of soil cracks between these piles, and change the force mode of the pile body. Considering the geological conditions and the influence of existing buildings, the deformation laws of the supporting structure and the soil around the foundation piles during the excavation of an underground layer of an L-shaped foundation pit were revealed. The research results are expected to provide a scientific and theoretical basis for the design and construction of downward additional support structures for existing buildings, along with socio-economic significance.Keywords: Existing underground space, Existing–supplementary double-layer piles, Retaining structure, Progressive failure mechanism, Finite element analysisSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Urbanization involves the continuous expansion of urban space, causing sudden population growth, traffic congestion, and living space congestion, which affect the healthy development of cities1–3. To solve the problem of congestion in aboveground space, additional layers in the underground space of existing buildings are being excavated and developed as a new solution4–6. In the process of downward addition of underground space, the existing support piles cannot bear the high lateral earth pressure. To solve this problem, a row of supplementary support piles is placed outside the existing support piles to form an existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure system, as shown in Fig. 1. Under the background of the rapid development in urban underground space addition projects, it is pretty to reveal the bearing characteristics, evolution law, and failure mechanism associated with the entire excavation process of existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structures.

Schematic of the excavation of an underground additional layer with existing–supplementary double-layer-row piles.

Existing–supplementary double-decker piles form a long–short combination of double-decker piles because the supplementary piles are longer than the existing piles. Research on the existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure has mainly covered the following three aspects: the impact of existing pile foundation on new buildings, the impact of pile foundation on existing buildings, and the impact of foundation pit excavation on the existing–supplementary double-layer pile foundation support structure7,8. In studies on the impact of existing pile foundation on the construction of new buildings, the influence of existing pile foundation during the construction of new subways, tunnels, and other buildings has been generally explored9–12. The influence of pile foundation on existing buildings has been typically analyzed by studying the influence of pile foundation on existing bridges, embankments, or tunnels under external loads13–15.For example, Soomro et al.16–19 conducted comprehensive studies through three-dimensional finite element analysis and centrifuge model tests, investigating the effects of twin tunnel construction on laterally loaded pile groups, including the lateral load responses of battered and vertical pile groups during tunneling17, and the impact of twin tunnel excavation on piled raft foundations19. The results indicated that the relative position between tunnel depth and pile length significantly affects pile deformation. For instance, when the tunnel is near the piles, it leads to significant lateral displacement and an increase in bending moment. However, when the tunnel is located deeper relative to the piles, the settlement effects become more pronounced.

The focus of this study was on the impact of excavation on an existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure. When adding underground space below an existing building, the excavation of the additional layer may result in a working condition where the existing and supplementary piles form a double-layer pile-supported structure, which is a new topic that has not been covered in conventional excavation engineering and design methods for foundation pits20,21 Regarding this topic22–25, Several scholars studied the effects of row spacing and length ratio of double-layer piles on the displacement at the top of the piles, bending moment of the piles, and their back soil pressure through large-scale indoor model tests. The displacement and bending moment of the supplementary piles were found to increase with the decrease in the row spacing, and the back soil pressure of the existing piles continued to decrease during excavation, which was positively correlated with the row spacing after reaching the active limit state22,23. In addition, by fitting the test data of the back active earth pressure of the piles, a method to predict the back active earth pressure of the existing piles in terms of the pile row spacing, soil friction angle, and pile–soil friction angle was given23,25.

Most current studies have focused on the mechanical properties of double-layer pile-supported structures in new foundation pits26–29, while the working characteristics of existing double-layer pile-supported systems in underground spaces remain unclear. Yu et al.24 studied the performance of double-row piles in newly constructed underground spaces, focusing on factors such as pile embedment depth, row spacing, and excavation depth, and their effects on pile bending moments. However, their study did not systematically explore the mechanical behavior and failure modes of existing and supplementary piles when expanding underground spaces. Compared with conventional double-stacked piles, the working shape of an existing building when adding layers to the space under it will be quite different because the supplementary piles of an existing double-layer pile-supported system are longer than the existing piles. Hence, it is necessary to explore the evolution law of the excavation process of an existing–supplementary pile-supported system when an underground layer is added below an existing building; the excavation space effect and the collaborative working characteristics of the combined support structure; and the deformation characteristics, force characteristics, excavation space effect, and pile top distribution law of the existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structures under the influence of various key parameters.

Considering the unique challenge of expanding underground space beneath existing buildings, this study focuses on the mechanical behavior and progressive failure mechanisms of an existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported system. By employing both physical model tests and numerical simulations, the research examines how the presence of existing pile foundations in excavation pits influences the double-layer pile-supported structure. A finite element analysis model was developed, incorporating geological conditions and the impact of existing buildings, to reveal the working characteristics of the soil between the piles throughout the excavation process. The results provide a solid scientific basis for designing and constructing support systems for expanding underground spaces beneath existing buildings.

Test scheme

Overview of model testing

Test overview

An L-shaped building was built in 1997, with the project being 76.20 m long, 44.20 m wide, and covering an area of 2600 m2. The building structure was a sheared wall frame structure, with one floor underground and five floors aboveground. The distribution of the underground layers within the scope of the site exploration depth was as follows: the original first floor was the basement, and the excavation depth of the existing foundation pit was 5 m. The foundation pit was excavated from the original basement to build the second-floor basement, and the depth of the additional basement was 6.5 m. A layered excavation method was proposed for the construction. The existing piles were cast-in-place bored piles with a diameter of 0.8 m, a pile length of 15 m, and a pile spacing of 1.5 m. The newly added piles were bored piles with a diameter of 0.8 m, a pile length of 25 m, and a pile spacing of 1.5 m. The row spacing of the existing–supplementary double-layer piles was 3 m. Cast-in-place concrete piles were adopted for the existing pile foundation, arranged in a square shape, with a total of 16 piles. The concrete strength grade of the pile body was C30, the pile diameter was 0.8 m, the pile length was 37.5 m, and the pile spacing was 6.6 m.

Overview of model tests.

The economy and ease of construction of the model material were considered, and a brick model box was selected as the test model box. The model box had dimensions of 3 m × 3 m × 1.6 m (length × width × height), made of 0.36 m-thick sintered bricks. Tiles were pasted at the bottom of the box, the inside of which was smoothed with mortar, and KT plates were installed to reduce the friction between the inner wall of the box and the test soil. Resistance strain gauges and displacement meters were selected. A foil-type resistance strain gauge (model BX120-5AA) was adopted as the resistance strain gauge. The substrate material was phenolic, the dimensions were 60 mm × 8 mm, the sensitivity was 2.0 ± 1%, its resistance value was 120 ± 0.2 Ω, and the accuracy class was A.

Test condition

The evolution process of the supporting structure under various key parameters (such as the supplementary pile spacing and use of crown beams) was studied, and five sets of model tests were conducted on the existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure using the control variable method; Table 3 presents the specific test conditions. In the G2, G3, and G4 groups, the supplementary pile spacing (S2) was controlled, and the supplementary pile spacing was set to 6, 9, and 12 cm, to study the influence of the supplementary pile spacing on the supporting effect. With the experimental groups G3 and G5, the influence of the crown beam on the support performance was experimentally studied by controlling the existing piles with or without the use of crown beams.

Test operation process

The site dimensions for the model test were 3000 mm × 3000 mm, the dimensions of the excavation foundation pit were 1320 mm × 1320 mm, and the depth of the existing foundation pit was 200 mm. The existing–supplementary double-layer pile arrangement was arranged in the central area of the test site. The specific layout scheme was as follows: the existing piles were laid along the perimeter of the foundation pit, 720 mm away from the perimeter of the test site, and 23 existing piles were evenly arranged on each side and distributed equidistantly. The distance between the centers of any two piles was 60 mm. The supplementary piles were laid around the existing piles, with 27 supporting piles laid on each side, and they were distributed equidistantly, with a pile center distance of 60 mm and a spacing of 120 mm between the existing–supplementary double-pile rows.

In the model test, the boundary conditions were assumed to be fixed at the sides of the test site. This means that the soil at the boundaries was restricted from horizontal movement, simulating a confined condition commonly encountered in real excavation projects. However, vertical movement was allowed within the pit to reflect natural deformation patterns. Although these boundary conditions reasonably represent typical excavation scenarios, they may not fully capture extreme conditions, such as in soft soils or areas with significant underground water flow. Therefore, the results from this model test, while applicable to many practical engineering cases, should be carefully considered when applied to sites with highly variable soil conditions.

A layer-by-layer excavation and layer-by-layer control scheme was adopted for the excavation of the test foundation pit, as shown in Fig. 4. Briefly, first, the existing piles were driven into the soil, and the existing space was excavated layer by layer; the depth of the existing space was 20 cm. It was necessary to excavate twice, each layer of excavation was 10 cm thick, and after each layer of excavation, it was left to stand until the soil was stable, and then the supplementary piles were placed. After the soil around the pile was stabilized, the final excavation of the additional layer space was performed; the thickness of the layer space was 30 cm, and a layered excavation method was adopted. In the test excavation process, to reduce the test error, the excavation of the foundation pit was done by the same person, and before each excavation, the strain acquisition system and dial indicator reading were checked for stabilization.

Test results

Analysis of the failure mode of existing–supplementary double-layer-row pile-retaining structure

Influence of supplementary pile spacing

To study the influence of supplementary pile spacing on deformation characteristics, test groups G3 and G4 were compared. Figure 5 shows the deformation of the double-layer pile-supported structure in G3. At H2 = 30 cm, fine arc-shaped cracks appeared in the soil between the piles, but the pile body showed no significant deformation. At H2 = 35 cm, the existing piles began to fail, with cracks forming at the middle and corners, while the supplementary piles remained intact as the embedded section of the existing piles decreased with deeper excavation, leading to their failure due to increased earth pressure (Fig. 5c). At H2 = 40 cm, the existing piles had fully failed. The supplementary pile spacing in G4 was larger than in G3.

Failure process of the retaining structure in the G3 test group: (a) H2 = 25 cm; (b) H2 = 30 cm; (c) H2 = 35 cm; (d) H2 = 40 cm; (e) H2 = 45 cm; (f) H2 = 50 cm.

Figure 6 illustrates the deformation characteristics of the existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure, similar to the failure pattern in test group G4. The analysis shows that in G3, the existing pile collapsed when excavated to H2 = 35 cm, while in G4, some existing piles became unstable and lost bearing capacity at H2 = 30 cm, indicating that supplementary pile spacing significantly affects the support system. As the spacing between supplementary piles increases, the existing piles fail earlier, with more severe damage and reduced structural resistance to deformation. Once the existing piles are no longer part of the support system, the supplementary pile spacing has a more pronounced effect on the support structure—the smaller the spacing, the higher the resistance to soil sliding and reduced soil displacement after pile installation. Therefore, supplementary pile spacing greatly influences the deformation characteristics of the support structure.

Influence of crown beam

Figure 7 illustrates the deformation and failure processes of the existing double-layer pile-supported structure in test group G5 during excavation. At an excavation depth of H2 = 25 and 30 cm, a few cracks appeared on the soil surface between the piles, but the pile body showed no significant deformation. In test group G3, at H2 = 30 cm, more arc-shaped cracks were observed in the soil behind the pile at shallow depths. A comparative analysis indicates that the crown beam in the existing pile helped delay soil cracking and reduced pile deformation, strengthening the early support of the existing–supplementary structure. At H2 = 40 and 45 cm, as shown in Fig. 7c and d, the existing piles were fully exposed with deeper excavation. The sliding soil between the piles flowed into the foundation space, increasing the load on the existing piles until they lost their supporting function and failed. Although the pile body remained largely undeformed, the bottom of the pile was displaced inward by soil movement, while the top moved outward due to the crown beam’s restraint, causing a kicking failure. The two sets of experiments revealed different failure modes for the existing piles, with the crown beam significantly enhancing the support capacity of the existing–supplementary double-layer piles, connecting them as a unified structure and effectively resisting soil slippage.

Failure process of G5 retaining structure of test group: (a) H2 = 25 cm; (b) H2 = 30 cm; (c) H2 = 35 cm; (d) H2 = 40 cm; (e) H2 = 45 cm; (f) H2 = 50 cm.

Under long-term loading and environmental conditions, the structural integrity of the crown beam may be affected by material aging, fatigue, and environmental corrosion, leading to a gradual decline in its effectiveness. Over time, the crown beam may develop cracks, rust, or loosened connections, especially under continuous loading or in harsh environments (such as groundwater or acidic soil), which could reduce its support capacity. Therefore, to maintain the long-term efficiency of the crown beam, regular inspections, protective measures, and reinforcement or repair strategies are recommended. Periodic inspections of key connection points and material surfaces should be conducted to detect any potential cracks or wear; in environments prone to corrosion, waterproof and anti-corrosion coatings should be applied to protect the crown beam from external damage. Additionally, for minor damage, localized reinforcement or repair can extend the crown beam’s lifespan. These maintenance strategies will help ensure that the crown beam maintains its supporting function over the long term, thus ensuring the stability of the overall structure.

Gradual failure mode of existing–supplementary double-layer piles

An analysis of the failure process in test groups G2, G3, G4, and G5 showed that supplementary pile spacing and crown beams significantly influenced the failure mode. The failure modes were divided into two types: (1) In groups G2, G3, and G4, without crown beams, increasing excavation depth led to gradual deformation of the existing piles and more soil cracks. Once excavated to the bottom, the existing piles lost support and overturned, with soil sliding into the pit, leaving the supplementary piles as the main support. Larger supplementary pile spacing increased pile deformation (Fig. 8a). (2) In group G5, crown beams connected the existing piles, limiting deformation and supporting the soil effectively. When excavated to the bottom, the existing piles lost their embedding effect, causing skirting failure as soil was extruded from beneath the piles (Fig. 8b).

Pile top displacement characteristics of existing–supplementary double-layer-pile-retaining structure

Influence of supplementary pile spacing

Figure 9 shows the horizontal displacement of the top of the existing and supplementary piles under each test condition. The top displacement values of the existing and supplementary piles in the different experimental groups increased with the increase in the excavation depth, with the middle pile exhibiting the highest value, followed by the side pile, and lastly the corner pile. With the increase in the excavation depth of the additional layer, the increase in the top displacement of the intermediate pile was greater than that of the side and corner piles, indicating that the middle pile will be the first to exhibit large deformation, consistent with the deformation and stress characteristics of the existing–supplementary double-layer pile structure.

Figure 10a shows that for intermediate piles, the top displacements of the G3 and G4 piles were 1.3 and 1.6 times that of the G2 pile, respectively. For side piles, they were 2.1 and 2.9 times greater, and for corner piles, 1.3 and 2.6 times greater. This indicates that the supplementary pile spacing had a more significant effect on side and corner piles than on intermediate piles. Overall, increased pile spacing led to greater top displacement in the existing–supplementary piles, with a more pronounced effect on existing piles. Additionally, the optimal pile spacing in different soil conditions or under varying loads depends on balancing displacement minimization and structural stability. In soft soils or under higher loads, smaller spacing is preferable, while in stiffer soils, larger spacing may be feasible. However, excessive spacing may result in diminishing returns and increased risks, especially for side and corner piles. This conclusion is drawn from a detailed comparison of pile top displacements under different conditions (G2, G3, and G4). Further research is suggested for optimizing pile spacing in different soil profiles and load conditions.

Mechanical characteristics of existing–supplementary double-layer-row pile-retaining structure

Influence of supplementary pile spacing

Figure 12a shows that in test group G1, the pile’s bending moment does not exhibit an S-shaped distribution, with the maximum moment occurring in the middle of the pile. As excavation depth increases, the bending moment also increases, though at a decreasing rate. In contrast, Fig. 12b–d show that in test groups G2, G3, and G4, the bending moment of the double-layer piles forms an S-shape, with both the bending moment and its rate of increase rising as excavation deepens. The reverse bending point shifts downward, and the maximum bending moment moves closer to the excavation surface. Comparing with G2, the bending moment of the existing piles in G3 and G4 is lower, while the supplementary piles in G3 and G4 experience greater bending moments than in G2. This is due to the larger supplementary pile spacing, which reduces soil resistance and causes soil between the double-layer piles to relax under shear, thereby reducing the load transferred to the existing piles and lowering their internal forces. Additionally, the G3 pile exhibits a higher bending moment than the G4 pile, indicating that increased supplementary pile spacing does not necessarily increase the pile’s internal force.

Numerical simulation analysis

Establishment of numerical calculation model

Material parameters

PLAXIS2D/3D software contains various soil constitutive models. Due to the complex problems of soil nonlinearity, boundary conditions, and pile soil effect observed in excavation engineering, the conventional Mohr–Coulomb model is no longer applicable. Therefore, the small-strain hardening soil model was selected, termed HSS. The HSS is an improved version of the HS model35–38.

The HSS model includes 11 HSS model parameters and two small strain parameters39,40: triaxial test stiffness (Eur), triaxial unloading/reloading stiffness (Eurref) and consolidation meter load strength (Eoed), effective cohesion , Poisson’s ratio , reference stress pref, effective internal friction angle , shear expansion angle , damage ratio Rf, stiffness stress correlation power index m, and resting side-pressure coefficient K0. There are two small strain parameters: shear strain and small-strain reference shear modulus .

Analysis steps.

The boundary conditions for the foundation pit were set as fixed boundaries, restricting horizontal displacement but allowing vertical movement. These boundary conditions reasonably simulate the stress and deformation behavior of the soil in real-world projects, especially in common foundation designs. Additionally, the simulations considered the constraint effects of the surrounding soil, reflecting the boundary effects encountered during excavation. However, soil and environmental conditions at real sites may vary significantly, such as in soft soils or areas with high groundwater levels, which could limit the applicability of the simulation results. Therefore, while these boundary conditions are representative for conventional projects, further validation and adjustments based on actual site conditions are recommended for practical applications.

Analysis of calculation results

Figure 17a–d show the displacements of the supporting structure in the X, Y, and Z directions, as well as the total displacement. As the depth of the underground excavation increased, the deformation of the soil surrounding the foundation pit also increased gradually. The deformation characteristics of the soil around the pit were influenced by the presence of existing buildings, leading to differences compared to the model test results. Specifically, after the piles on the D and E sides near the buildings were installed, the soil displacement was larger, while in the model test, the displacement was greater in the center of the pit. Although the existing buildings impacted the displacement distribution of the soil around the foundation pit, they did not alter the overall spatial effect of the excavation. As the excavation deepened, the displacement of the piles in the center was much larger than that of the corner piles, aligning with the conclusions regarding spatial effects observed in the model test. When excavated to the bottom of the existing pile, the displacement of the pile body in the middle of the longest side A of the foundation pit was significantly greater than that in the B, C, and F sides, indicating that, the longer the existing double-layer pile, the worse its deformation resistance. Therefore, when the existing double-layer pile was added to the longer side, it should be reinforced to improve its deformation rigidity. The soil deformation was maximum along the E edge around the existing load, with a magnitude of 112.6 mm, indicating that the existing building had a significant impact on the foundation pit, and the lateral support could be considered for reinforcement in the annex area during actual construction.

Figure 18 shows the lateral displacement of the pile at the middle of the F side, and the curve reveals several important findings. First, the existing–supplementary double-row piles exhibit a bulge-shaped deformation with minimal displacement at the top and bottom, indicating that the crown beam effectively limits pile top displacement while the supplementary piles’ depth reduces bottom displacement. This finding is consistent with the model test results, where similar deformation patterns were observed, confirming the stabilizing role of the crown beam. As excavation deepens, the lateral displacement of the piles increases, particularly during the third and fourth excavation stages, due to increased soil pressure and reduced embedding of the existing piles, which accelerates their displacement. This trend aligns with the physical model tests, where a significant rise in lateral displacement was recorded in the deeper excavation stages, highlighting the piles’ increasing susceptibility to deformation. Additionally, the peak displacement in the piles shifts downward as excavation progresses. Initially, the supplementary piles show less lateral displacement than the existing piles, but as the excavation deepens and the embedded section of the existing piles decreases, the supplementary piles eventually exhibit greater lateral displacement. This reflects a shift in load-bearing responsibility between the existing and supplementary piles, as the supplementary piles take on more load as excavation progresses. This behavior is consistent with the experimental results, which also showed a similar transition in load distribution and displacement trends, affirming the accuracy of the numerical simulations in capturing the working mechanism of the existing–supplementary double-row pile system.As the supplementary piles take on more load during excavation, the load on the existing piles is reduced, thereby decreasing their risk of fatigue failure. However, due to the increased load on the supplementary piles, they may experience greater fatigue stress over time, especially in soft soil or high groundwater conditions, where the structural integrity of the supplementary piles may be more significantly affected. Therefore, it is recommended to conduct regular monitoring, especially during the critical first five years after construction, to detect any changes in stress or deformation. For areas with soft soil or high groundwater, monitoring frequency may need to be increased. Additionally, higher-strength, fatigue-resistant materials such as corrosion-resistant steel and enhanced concrete should be used in the design, and, if necessary, crown beams or lateral reinforcement beams should be added to strengthen pile support and ensure long-term stability and safety of the piles.

Discussion

Significance and practical application of the study

This study provides an in-depth analysis of the mechanical performance and soil pressure distribution of the existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure in complex urban underground engineering, particularly under deep excavation conditions. The results show that supplementary piles can effectively reduce the load on existing piles, improving the overall stability of the support structure, which is of significant practical value for urban underground space expansion projects. This study not only provides scientific guidance for the design of double-layer pile-supported structures but also demonstrates how optimizing pile spacing and length can effectively control soil pressure, thereby enhancing project safety and construction efficiency.

Based on the research results, we propose the following design recommendations: First, the spacing and length configuration of supplementary piles should be optimized based on the actual geological conditions and load requirements. In soft soil or high-load conditions, smaller pile spacing should be used to reduce displacement and deformation. Second, high-strength, fatigue-resistant materials, such as corrosion-resistant steel and reinforced concrete, should be selected to improve the long-term stability of the piles. Lastly, after construction, particularly during the critical first five years, regular monitoring of pile stress and deformation should be conducted. In soft soil and high groundwater areas, monitoring frequency should be increased, and if necessary, reinforcement measures, such as crown beams or lateral supports, should be implemented to prevent structural failure caused by long-term loading.

General discussion

In this study, the design of the small-scale indoor model tests followed the principles of geometric, kinematic, and mechanical similarity, ensuring that the experimental results accurately reflect the mechanical behavior of large-scale engineering projects. Although the model size is relatively small, the test results revealed deformation characteristics and failure mechanisms of the existing-supplementary double-layer pile-supported structure that are consistent with actual engineering conditions. By reasonably adjusting parameters such as pile spacing and length ratio, the test results provide a reference for large-scale project designs, and thus have a certain degree of applicability. Moreover, by combining physical tests with numerical simulations, we further verified that the primary mechanical responses observed in the model tests are applicable to real-world projects.

The scaling effect may introduce some errors in small-scale tests, particularly with regard to the relative proportions of soil properties and pile stiffness compared to actual projects. To minimize the impact of the scaling effect on the accuracy of the results, we employed finite element numerical simulations to correct the physical tests. Numerical simulations can better represent real-world conditions in large-scale engineering, and by comparing simulation results with test data, we found that they were consistent in terms of deformation trends and stress distribution. Thus, the introduction of numerical simulations not only enhances the accuracy of the test results but also increases their applicability in large-scale construction projects.

In addition to addressing the scaling effect, we considered the variability of soil conditions commonly found in urban environments, such as differences in soil type, moisture content, and compaction. These factors can significantly impact the behavior of pile-supported structures. While the experimental soil represented typical soft soil conditions, the numerical simulations allow for adjusting soil parameters to reflect different site-specific conditions. Therefore, the results are directly applicable to soft soils but can be extended to other soil types by modifying the model inputs. Further calibration is recommended when applying these findings to projects with different geotechnical profiles, ensuring broad applicability to various engineering scenarios.

Conclusions

The research aimed to expand the underground layer beneath a building by conducting model tests on existing–supplementary double-layer pile-supported structures. The study thoroughly examined the deformation, force characteristics, and failure progression of these piles during additional layer excavation. A finite element analysis model was constructed, taking into account geological conditions and nearby buildings. The main findings are summarized as follows:

Supplementary piles effectively prevent soil slippage after pile driving and redistribute the soil pressure on existing piles. This helps reduce soil deformation and cracks between piles. A smaller spacing between supplementary piles enhances resistance to soil slippage. Crown beams further improve the support capacity of the existing–supplementary piles by connecting them and resisting soil slippage.
