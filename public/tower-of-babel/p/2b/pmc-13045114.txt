Global burden and projected trends of ovarian cancer in older women: A GBD 2021 analysis
Authors: Xia Li, Ling Yang, Chenxi Wang, Chenchang He, Shuo Zhao, Zhong Du, Jiashu Ma, Jianqiang Hua, Qingyu Zhou, Haiyu Lin, Pingping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the population ages worldwide, ovarian cancer (OC) poses a serious public health issue for elder women. The objective of this study is to outline the global impact of OC in individuals aged 60 and above from 1990 to 2021, thus guiding specific policies and approaches for prevention, screening, and treatment.MethodsA global secondary data analysis using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data was performed to estimate the burden and project future trends of OC in older women (aged 60 and above). We gathered data regarding incidence, mortality rates, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from the GBD 2021. For trend analysis, we employed joinpoint regression to assess temporal variations, calculating the average annual percentage change (AAPC) to quantify these trends. Furthermore, we used the age-period-cohort (APC) model to examine the influences of age, time, and birth cohort on incidence, mortality, and DALY figures. Additionally, the Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was applied to forecast changes extending to the year 2050.ResultsA worldwide assessment of the impact of OC on public health from 1990 to 2021 highlights notable patterns: the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) showed a net decrease; however, its estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was positive, indicating an overall upward trend in annual rates over the period. In contrast, the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), the age-standardized DALYs, age-standardized death rate (ASDR), and the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) have all shown a decline. The distribution of the disease burden shows considerable regional disparities, with uncertainty analyses indicating a sustained decline in regions with a high socio‑demographic index (SDI) and a corresponding increase in regions with low‑to‑medium SDI. Among older women (aged 60 and above), the disease burden demonstrates a disproportionate concentration in younger elderly subgroups: the 60–64 age group experiences the highest incidence and prevalence burden, while mortality peaks in the 65–69 age group. This pattern is particularly pronounced in regions such as South Asia and Central Europe. Risk factor analysis identifies high body mass index (BMI) at 21% and occupational asbestos exposure at 7% as the two major risk factors contributing to OC mortality. Projections based on the BAPC model indicate that by 2050, the global prevalence of OC will continue to rise, while incidence rates are expected to persistently decline.ConclusionsThe global fight against OC confronts dual challenges: increasing incidence rates and a shifting disease burden toward low- and middle-income regions. Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) ranks as the deadliest cancerous tumor within the global female reproductive system, noted for its elevated mortality rate and substantial risk to women's health [1]. In 2022, the latest global cancer burden data reported 324,398 new cases and 206,839 deaths, positioning it as the eighth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women [2]. Due to the absence of effective early screening methods, 70% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage of the disease, resulting in a generally poor prognosis [3]. Although the absolute number of OC cases is lower than that of breast cancer or cervical cancer, its age-standardized mortality rate remains high, rendering it a particularly severe public health challenge in gynecological oncology.

The intensification of global population aging has led to a shift in epidemiological characteristics towards chronic, non-communicable diseases, with cancer being a prominent example [4]. Existing studies predominantly analyze OC as a whole or focus on women of reproductive age [5, 6]. A critical and increasingly prominent public health issue that has been relatively overlooked is the significant increase in the risk of OC with age, with most cases concentrated among older women. Moreover, notable differences related to age are present in the clinical treatment of OC, frequently leading to inadequate care and restricted availability of sophisticated surgical and medical options, especially for older individuals [7]. Additionally, Elderly patients with OC face numerous challenges, including less prominent early symptoms, multiple comorbidities, diminished nutritional and immune functions, significant psychological and emotional stress, and reduced tolerance to treatment [8–10]. As the global population structure trends irreversibly toward aging, particularly in regions with medium to high socio-demographic indices, the female population aged 60 and above is expanding rapidly [11]. This demographic shift predicts a substantial increase in the number of older women diagnosed with OC in the coming decades, thereby placing immense pressure on healthcare systems and socio-economic structures.

Despite the recognition that aging is a significant risk factor for OC, there is currently a lack of comprehensive and systematic assessments of the global disease burden of OC among elderly women (≥ 60 years). Furthermore, in addition to established reproductive risk factors such as nulliparity and early menarche, metabolic risk factors such as high body mass index (BMI) may have a unique impact on the attributable burden within this demographic [12]. However, their specific contributions remain inadequately defined. More importantly, prospective predictions of future disease burden based on current population trends and the prevalence of risk factors are essential for developing forward-looking cancer prevention and control strategies, as well as for optimizing the allocation of medical resources.

This research intends to leverage the most recent findings from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study to perform perform a comprehensive secondary data analysis of the impact of OC on older women (aged 60 and above) worldwide, as well as at regional and national scales, spanning from 1990 to 2021.We will describe the temporal and spatial trends in incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and assess the attributable burden of related risk factors. Building on this foundation, this study will, for the first time, develop a model to predict the burden of OC in this population up to 2050, with the goal of providing crucial scientific evidence for national and global health policymakers. This will aid in planning future elderly cancer prevention and control systems, effectively addressing the health challenges posed by population aging. Materials and methods

The GBD study, conducted by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), provides a comprehensive array of standardized metrics pertinent to global health. Drawing on information obtained from the GBD 2021 dataset (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021), this research provides essential insights into statistics related to mortality, the prevalence of diseases, and associated risk factors among various regions and demographic groups. This dataset serves as a comprehensive resource, capturing the occurrence, prevalence rates, and mortality figures associated with 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories, differentiated by age and gender [13]. We extracted epidemiological indicators related to OC in individuals aged 60 and above from 1990 to 2021. This includes rates of prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs, alongside data on DALYs associated with various risk factors. The GBD estimation methodologies and their application in our study are outlined below: (1) DisMod-MR 2.1: This Bayesian meta-regression tool was primarily used to ensure consistency between epidemiological parameters (e.g., incidence, prevalence, remission, and excess mortality) for non-fatal health outcomes. It synthesizes all available data, accounts for heterogeneity between sources, and applies spatial and temporal smoothing to address data gaps; (2) Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm): This model was employed to analyze cause-specific mortality data. CODEm tests a vast array of potential model formulations (including spatiotemporal and covariate-based models) and creates an ensemble of the best-performing models to produce the most robust estimates of mortality rates; (3) Spatiotemporal Gaussian Process Regression (ST-GPR): This method was applied as a central smoothing and estimation stage for prevalence rates or mortality fractions. ST-GPR models the underlying geographic and temporal trends while smoothing out random noise in the input data, generating stable and continuous estimates across all locations and years in our study [14, 15].

The attributable burden of risk factors for OC was derived from the GBD 2021 Comparative Risk Assessment (CRA) framework. This framework employs a counterfactual attribution approach. It estimates the population attributable fraction (PAF) by comparing the observed disease burden with the theoretical burden that would have occurred if population exposure to a risk factor had been reduced to an optimal level, known as the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL). The TMREL is defined based on the level of exposure associated with the lowest risk of the health outcome in the available epidemiological evidence [16].

For each risk-outcome pair included in the model, the GBD synthesis integrates evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses of relative risks, representative population exposure data, and cause-specific burden estimates. The framework accounts for mediation (where one risk factor acts through another) by using a mediation matrix to partition burden among correlated risks and avoids double-counting. It does not model statistical interactions beyond those captured in the relative risk estimates from the source meta-analyses. Uncertainty from all inputs is propagated through a simulation process to generate final estimates with 95% uncertainty intervals.

Clear definition of mortality risk factors

Risk factors for mortality encompass any attributes, exposures, or conditions that elevate the likelihood of death for individuals or populations. These factors are not necessarily direct causes of death; rather, they influence mortality by contributing to the onset of disease or injury. Typically, these factors are systematically categorized into major groups: behavioral risk factors, metabolic risk factors, occupational risk factors, environmental risk factors.This study quantifies risk exposure using age-standardised Standardised Effect Values (SEVs), which consider both the severity of the risk and the proportion of the population exposed. Statistical analysis

Descriptive epidemiological methods were utilized to analyze the temporal and demographic characteristics of OC incidence among the elderly population, providing a comprehensive examination of OC incidence stratified by gender and age group. To mitigate the influence of differing population structures on the analysis results, this study employed the World Health Organization (WHO) population structure as the standard. Consequently, the standardized incidence rates of OC in the elderly population were calculated from 1990 to 2021 for further comparative analysis.

The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) value was utilized to represent the overall trend in incidence rates. The analysis employed the logarithm of incidence rates as the dependent variable, with time (year) serving as the independent variable, and regression analysis was conducted accordingly. The EAPC coefficient was calculated using the following formula: EAPC = 100 × [exp(β)—1], where β represents the linear regression coefficient. An upward trend in the coefficient was indicated when the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the EAPC exceeded 0; conversely, a downward trend was indicated otherwise [17].

In our analysis, we have indeed adhered to GBD study protocols and given full consideration to the uncertainty of the estimates. Specifically: 1. Core Principle: We recognize that simple comparisons based on point estimates (e.g., rates or percent changes) may overlook underlying uncertainty. Therefore, for any between-group comparisons or assessments of trends, we have relied primarily on the uncertainty intervals. 2. For the key comparisons (e.g., changes in burden metrics over years, differences between regions or population subgroups), we employed the following approaches to "account for overlapping UIs": (1) Assessment of UI Overlap: As an initial gauge, we examined the overlap of 95% UIs between compared groups. Extensive and substantial overlap typically suggests a statistically non-significant difference; (2) Simulation-Based Inference: For more formal and robust testing, we utilized the 1000 draws provided by the GBD study. By calculating the comparative metric (e.g., rate difference, rate ratio) for each draw, we generated its empirical distribution and the corresponding 95% UI. If the 95% UI of this comparative metric did not contain 0 (for differences) or 1 (for ratios), we considered the observed difference statistically significant. This method directly incorporates the uncertainty from all parameters; (3) Presentation of Results: In both the text and figures/tables (e.g., in footnotes or appendices), we have clearly indicated that all reported point estimates are presented alongside their 95% UIs. When describing comparative findings, we strictly base our statements on the conclusions derived from the above UI-based or simulation methods, e.g., stating "the rate in Group A was significantly higher than in Group B (95% UI of the rate ratio: 1.2–1.8, excluding 1)," rather than relying on point estimates alone [18].

Joinpoint analysis is a method that segments the disease incidence trend to establish regressions by dividing the study period into different intervals. It performs trend fitting and optimization for each interval, thereby evaluating the disease change characteristics specific to each segment. Utilizing the Joinpoint Regression Software (version 5.2.0.0) developed by the National Cancer Institute, indicators such as the annual percent change (APC) and the average annual percent change (AAPC) are calculated to assess the incidence trend. An APC greater than 0 indicates a year-by-year increase in the incidence rate; if there is no joinpoint, then APC equals AAPC, reflecting that the overall trend of the dataset is either monotonically increasing or decreasing [19, 20].

The Age-Period-Cohort (APC) model, which is based on the Poisson distribution, decomposes the target analysis variable into three dimensions: age, period, and cohort. This model enables analysis of the risk of disease incidence or mortality across these dimensions. Among these, the age effect serves as an internal factor, reflecting the influence of individual physiological or social changes; the period effect acts as an external factor, representing the impact of macro policies or social events; and the cohort effect embodies the interaction of both internal and external factors, reflecting the cumulative or delayed effects of individuals experiencing different social events at various life stages [21]. This study divides the period from 1990 to 2021 into seven intervals of five years each: 1990–1994, 1995–1999, 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024 (with the meaning of 2020–2021 data used as a substitute). Age is categorized into nine groups, each spanning five years. Based on the formula for birth cohort, defined as period minus age, a total of 15 birth cohorts is calculated. Using the median age, period, and cohort as the control group, the analysis is conducted with the online analytical software developed by the National Cancer Institute, which is based on the R language.

The prediction of OC incidence in the elderly population is conducted using the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model. The BAPC modeling was implemented using the inla package (version 23.1.12), which leverages Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations for Bayesian inference. In the BAPC model, we specified the following priors: for the intrinsic random walk (RW1) smoothness priors on the age, period, and cohort effects, we used default gamma priors on the log-precision parameters; A non-informative flat prior was used for the model intercept. As we used INLA (a deterministic algorithm, not MCMC), traditional "iterations" do not apply. Instead, convergence is assured by the numerical approximation scheme. We confirmed stable results and model fit through: Examination of the posterior density plots for all hyperparameters and key parameters; Ensuring the log marginal likelihood (a model selection criterion) was stable. The Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) for model comparison; The Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC) as a robust Bayesian measure of predictive accuracy; Posterior predictive checks, where we compared key statistics (e.g., mean, dispersion) of the observed data with those simulated from the posterior predictive distribution. This model effectively integrates sample data with prior information regarding unknown parameters to estimate the posterior distribution, thus facilitating the inference of these unknown parameters [22].

To assess the predictive performance and guard against overfitting, we performed Kfold cross‑validation (with K = 5). The historical data (1990–2021) were randomly partitioned into five folds. The model was trained on four folds and used to predict the held-out fold. This process was repeated five times, and the prediction error across all folds was aggregated to compute a cross‑validated mean absolute error (CVMAE). The final model used for the 2050 projections was trained on the full historical dataset, and its reported uncertainty bands incorporate the variability observed during this validation process.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1.

Results

Trends in the burden of OC

Analysis of global OC burden data obtained from the GBD database indicates a continuing increase in the global burden of OC. The age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) rose from 14.1 (13.0, 15.5) per 100,000 (95% UI) in 1990 to 14.3 (12.9, 15.6) per 100,000 (95% UI) in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.8 (0.8, 0.9) (95% CI). The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) showed a net decrease from 3.8 (3.5, 4.2) per 100,000(95% UI) in 1990 to 3.5 (3.1, 3.8) per 100,000(95% UI) in 2021. However, the EAPC was 0.7% (0.6, 0.7)(95% CI). This positive EAPC—obtained from joinpoint regression that weights all annual data points across the 32-year period—reflects an overall upward slope in the fitted trend line, driven by periods of increase in later years and/or in certain regions, despite the net decline between the two endpoints. The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) decreased from 2.6 (2.4, 2.8) per 100,000 (95% UI) in 1990 to 2.2 (1.9, 2.3) per 100,000 (95% UI) in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.6 (0.6, 0.7) (95% CI). The age-standardized death rate (ASDR) decreased from 69.2 (63.5, 75.9) per 100,000 (95% UI) to 59.5 (54.0, 64.7) per 100,000 (95% UI) in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.5 (0.5, 0.5) (95% CI). Trends in the disease burden of OC across 5 socio-demographic index (SDI) regions

After compiling and visualizing data from five SDI regions (Fig. 3), we observed consistent trends in ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR. Both High SDI and High-Middle SDI regions displayed a significant decline in disease burden. For instance, in High‑SDI regions, the ASIR declined from 6.5 (6.2–6.7) per 100 000 (95% UI) in 1990 to 4.4 (4.1–4.6) per 100 000 (95% UI) in 2021. In contrast, the burden of OC is on the rise in Low SDI, Low-Middle SDI, and Middle SDI regions. Correspondingly, in Low‑SDI regions the ASIR increased from 1.8 (1.2–2.6) per 100 000 (95% UI) to 2.8 (2.1–3.3) per 100 000 (95% UI) over the same period. These findings indicate distinct disease burdens across populations at varying levels of socioeconomic development.

Disease burden patterns among elderly women (aged 60 + , grouped by 5-year intervals across SDI regions)

After grouping elderly women and processing the disease burden data, we observe that the gap between 1990 and 2021 is not significant, with the total disease burden decreasing across higher age cohorts (Fig. 4). Among the ASR, only the ASPR shows a decline with increasing age cohorts. The remaining ASR exhibit either an upward trend or remain constant. In both 1990 and 2021, the highest numbers of cases, prevalence, and DALYs were concentrated in the 60–64 age group, while the highest number of deaths occurred in the 65–69 age group. Furthermore, the number of cases in each category showed a declining trend as the age groups increased.

A subgroup analysis of women aged 60 years and older across 21 GBD regions reveals that prevalence, incidence, and DALYs are concentrated in younger subgroups, whereas mortality is predominantly observed in older subgroups (Fig. 5). Examination of different regions indicates substantial variations in prevalence, incidence, DALYs, and mortality rates across these areas. Notably, in regions such as South Asia and Central Europe, the occurrence of OC is concentrated in younger age groups, while other regions display a more uniform distribution pattern.

The trends for the burden of OC by 21 SDI regions

We compiled and analyzed trends in ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR for OC globally and across 21 GBD regions from 1990 to 2021, generating corresponding fitted curves. These fitted curves reveal regional variations in the association between age-standardized disease burden indicators and the SDI. As illustrated in Fig. 6, ASPR demonstrates a significant similarity with ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR, with most regions closely aligning with the fitted curves. Overall, higher SDI values correlate with a greater disease burden. Beyond a certain threshold of SDI, the disease burden begins to decline. However, regions such as Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Europe, and Australasia exhibit significant deviations above the fitted curve, with calculated correlations reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05). Conversely, regions including Oceania and High-Income Asia Pacific display a notably lower disease burden below the fitted curve, also demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Analysis of risk factors for OC Mortality based on the GBD CRA framework

The GBD 2021 CRA framework estimates that a portion of OC mortality is attributable to specific risk factors with sufficient or convincing evidence. Among these, high BMI accounted for 21% (15–28%) (95% UI) of OC deaths globally in 2021, and occupational exposure to asbestos accounted for 7% (5–9%) (95% UI). These represent the two risk factors with the largest attributable fractions in the GBD 2021 model for OC. It is important to note that the GBD framework currently models only a subset of potential risk factors for OC, based on established evidence meeting its inclusion criteria (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study utilized the GBD 2021 database, in conjunction with historical data, to classify global primary OC cases by Global Burden of Disease regions, Social Development Index, and age. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on prevalence, incidence, DALYs, deaths, age-standardized rates, and their trends among women aged 60 years and older worldwide. The analysis revealed a series of complex and critical shifts. These findings not only illustrate the progress made in combating this disease but also highlight the formidable challenges that lie ahead.

Over the past three decades, the global burden of OC has exhibited a seemingly contradictory yet logically consistent trend: declining incidence and mortality rates, alongside a persistent rise in prevalence. Both ASIR and ASMR have demonstrated downward trajectories. These declines are temporally correlated with, and may be partly explained by, the widespread adoption of primary prevention measures, such as contraceptive use, [23] and significant advances in treatment modalities, including surgical optimization and the application of targeted PARP inhibitors. [24, 25] In contrast, the ASPR has exhibited a slight increase. This phenomenon is consistent with a "side effect" of successful treatment—patients are living longer due to more effective therapies, resulting in a rising number of survivors within the population. Consequently, OC is progressively transitioning from a fatal disease to a chronic condition that necessitates long-term management [25].

From 1990 to 2021, temporal trends across global regions revealed two starkly contrasting epidemiological patterns. Regions such as East Asia and Australasia demonstrated significant improvement, corresponds with rapid economic development, expanded access to healthcare services, and effective public health interventions targeting major diseases. The observed pattern is consistent with the classic theory of epidemiological transition, which describes a shift from a burden dominated by infectious, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases to one dominated by noncommunicable diseases, accompanied by an overall reduction in disease burden. In contrast, multiple regions in Latin America and most of sub-Saharan Africa have experienced either increasing disease burdens or stagnation at high levels. This situation reflects an intensifying dual burden of disease: while infectious diseases and maternal and child health issues remain unresolved, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and other noncommunicable diseases—along with their associated risk factors—are spreading rapidly. Consequently, the overall disease burden continues to rise rather than decline. [26, 27]

Interregional disparities represent a core finding of this analysis. Although Eastern and Central Europe are classified as high-income or upper-middle-income regions, their baseline and endpoint DALY rates are significantly higher than those of Western Europe and Australasia. These differences may stem from historical variations in healthcare systems, elevated mortality rates due to cardiovascular diseases, and differing prevalence levels of behavioral risk factors such as alcohol abuse and smoking. In contrast, the substantial burden in sub-Saharan Africa is primarily linked to infectious diseases, poor sanitation, inadequate maternal and child health service coverage, and the emerging burden of non-communicable diseases [28, 29].

Current research indicates a well-established overall association between BMI and OC, with authoritative bodies such as the World Cancer Research Fund classifying high BMI as a probable risk factor. Epidemiological literature suggests that the strength of this association may vary by histologic subtype. Primary mechanisms proposed in the literature include obesity-related chronic inflammation, elevated estrogen levels, and hyperinsulinemia [30]. The attributable fractions reported in our results (21% for high BMI, 7% for asbestos) represent estimates for OC as a combined entity in the GBD 2021 model and are not stratified by histologic subtype. Conversely, the relationship between occupational exposure and OC is an emerging field with relatively limited but growing evidence. Consistent with findings from various studies, exposure to asbestos, respirable crystalline silica, and certain organic solvents and pesticides has been associated with an increased risk of OC in general [31].

Our findings hold significant implications for public health policy, particularly for low- and middle-income countries facing a rising or persistently high burden of OC. In such settings, tailored strategies should focus on several key areas. Strengthening diagnostic pathways is essential through training primary care providers and community health workers to recognize non-specific symptoms, along with establishing referral networks for timely ultrasound and CA-125 testing. Building surgical and treatment capacity requires investment in task-sharing and targeted training in cytoreductive procedures, alongside developing resource-adapted clinical guidelines and improving sustainable access to essential chemotherapeutics. Furthermore, risk factor reduction can be integrated into existing non-communicable disease platforms to support weight management, while occupational health regulations should be enforced to limit exposure to asbestos, a modifiable risk factor identified in this study. Finally, enhancing surveillance through hospital-based cancer registries and supporting operational research on intervention feasibility will be crucial for monitoring progress and guiding local implementation. These efforts should be incorporated into broader national cancer control plans that address both communicable and non-communicable disease challenges, with ongoing burden-of-disease research to inform priority-setting and policy adaptation [32, 33].

This study has several limitations. First, as an ecological analysis of aggregated data, it identifies population-level associations but cannot establish causality or examine individual-level confounders. Second, our findings rely on the GBD 2021 estimates, which carry inherent uncertainty from input data quality and modeling, particularly in regions with data gaps. Third, a critical constraint is the lack of histological subtype data, preventing analysis of trends and risks specific to different OC subtypes. Fourth, the temporal and projection analyses depend on APC/BAPC models, which assume past trends will continue and may not capture future disruptions. Fifth, the attributable risk estimates are limited to the factors in the GBD framework and do not capture combined effects or all potential risks (e.g., genetic factors). Finally, future projections, while accompanied by uncertainty estimates, cannot account for unforeseen changes in prevention or treatment. Despite these limitations, the GBD provides a standardized basis for global comparison, and our analysis offers valuable insights into broad trends and disparities in the burden of OC among older women.

Conclusion

In summary, the prevention and treatment of OC worldwide stand at a critical crossroads. While medical advancements have contributed to declining mortality rates, the concomitant rise in incidence and the shifting disease burden toward low- and middle-income regions present a dual challenge. Future strategies must be both precise and global in scope: strengthening primary prevention, enhancing global access to diagnosis and treatment, and optimizing chronic disease management for long-term survivors are essential for effectively addressing this increasingly complex global health issue.
