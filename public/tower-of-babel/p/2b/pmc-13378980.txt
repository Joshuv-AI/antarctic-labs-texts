Fat mass and obesity-associated gene overexpression ameliorates endothelial dysfunction after myocardial infarction by mediating N6-methyladenosine modification of ATP-binding cassette subfamily F member 1
Authors: Huichao Pan, Xunlong Xu, Yamei Yu, Zhaohui Qiu, Yafeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Myocardial infarction (MI) is linked to high mortality, which highlights the need for early diagnosis and intervention to prevent heart failure. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation of ribonucleic acid (RNA) influences endothelial dysfunction and MI. Here, the effects and associated mechanisms of fat mass and obesity-associated gene (FTO) on vascular endothelial cell (EC) function, as well as myocardial damage in mice following MI were investigated.Material and Methods:A human umbilical vein EC (HUVEC) model of oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) as well as a mouse MI model were used to detect MI-induced endothelial and myocardial damage. EC function was examined using cell-counting-kit-8, wound healing, migration, and tube formation assays. Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) sequencing and RNA sequencing were utilized to analyze the m6A modifications and RNA expression patterns in control and FTO-overexpressing ECs following OGD. FTO and ATP-binding cassette subfamily F member 1 (ABCF1) levels were evaluated using western blotting. The relationship between FTO and ABCF1 was determined using RNA immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RIPqPCR), MeRIP-qPCR, and RNA stability assays. Echocardiography and Masson’s trichrome and hematoxylineosin staining were used to measure myocardial injury.Results:FTO expression was reduced in infarcted myocardial tissue and OGD-induced HUVECs (P < 0.01). Functionally, FTO overexpression improved the impaired function of OGD-treated HUVECs and restored myocardial function in MI mice (P < 0.01). Mechanistically, FTO reduced m6A methylation of ABCF1 mRNA and raised ABCF1 levels in OGD-treated HUVECs (P < 0.01).Conclusion:Taken together, our study revealed that FTO overexpression restored OGD-induced endothelial dysfunction and myocardial pathological injury after MI. FTO increased ABCF1 expression in OGD-treated HUVECs through an m6A-dependent mechanism. These findings offer new insights into mitigating EC dysfunction and myocardial injury after MI.

INTRODUCTION

Myocardial infarction (MI) can lead to heart failure and sudden death and is thus a major contributor to worldwide mortality.[1,2] Despite advances in prevention and treatment, such as thrombolysis and percutaneous coronary intervention, effective strategies for treating this disease have not yet been established.[3] Previous studies have indicated that a primary trigger of MI is the functional impairment of myocardial microvascular endothelial cells (ECs).[4] EC-mediated angiogenesis is involved in the repair of myocardial tissues and mitigates adverse effects post-MI.[5-7] Thus, targeting the inhibition of EC dysfunction may enhance cardiac outcomes by reducing inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis;[8] however, the mechanisms underlying EC dysfunction post-MI are not well understood, highlighting the need for further studies to identify potential targets for treating MI.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation is observed in ribonucleic acid (RNAs), including mRNAs, tRNAs, rRNAs, and non-coding RNAs.[9] The m6A modification regulates RNA stability, splicing, and translation and affects numerous biological and physiological processes.[10] The m6A modification is associated with various diseases, including MI.[11-14] The regulation of m6A modification involves methyltransferases, demethylases, and readers, with specific proteins or enzymes recognizing and modifying the m6A sites on RNA. Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3)[15,16] and fat mass and obesity-associated gene (FTO)[17] expression are correlated with MI. METTL3 increases EC function and capillary density in ischemic areas, which may represent a potential strategy for treating MI.[18] These results suggest the importance of m6A modification and potential therapeutic approaches for treating MI. Particularly, FTO, an m6A mRNA demethylase, post-transcriptionally modulates mRNA expression in cells.[19,20] A significant decrease in FTO levels was reported in infarcted mouse myocardial tissues with myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury.[21] Notably, FTO enhances cardiac contraction in mice with heart failure.[17] In addition, overexpressing FTO reduces hypoxia/reoxygenation induces myocardial cell apoptosis by attenuating m6A modifications in Myosin heavy chain-associated RNA transcripts (MHRT RNA);[22] however, the involvement of FTO in EC m6A modifications following MI injury is not known.

ATP-binding cassette subfamily F member 1 (ABCF1), a member of the ABCF family, is an ATP-binding cassette transporter.[23] Early studies identified its effect on protein translation through its association with eukaryotic initiation factor 2 and ribosomal proteins. In addition, ABCF1 gene knockout mice are lethal in embryos, indicating that it is essential for cell proliferation and differentiation during embryo development.[24] Another study showed involvement of ABCF1 in the self-renewal of stem cells, where it co-regulates key transcription factors, such as octamer-binding transcription factor 4 and SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2).[25] Moreover, ABCF1 is involved in age-related arterial function[26] and correlates with left ventricle wall thickness.[27] ABCF1 is also involved in tumorigenesis, particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In HCC, ABCF1 expression is significantly increased and promotes the development of malignant tumors.[28] Furthermore, ABCF1 overexpression enhances SOX4 expression, a transcription factor associated with cell survival, angiogenesis, and other processes in HCC.[29]

Here, we demonstrated that FTO overexpression restored oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD)-treated EC dysfunction and myocardial injury after MI by increasing ABCF1 expression in an m6A modification-dependent manner. The results provide a basis for the utilization of FTO in preventing EC dysfunction following MI injury.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Identification of key genes in MI

Data from the GEO database (GSE66360) were analyzed,[30] including the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the criteria of P < 0.05 and |fold change (FC)| > 1.5. Levels of FTO in the dataset were analyzed.

Animals models

Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF)-grade C57BL/6 mice (aged 8 weeks) were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. This study was approved by and confirmed by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Tongren Hospital (approval number A2024-033-01). This work has been performed according to the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Tongren Hospital. The animals were kept under standard laboratory conditions and randomly assigned to 4 groups: The Sham, MI, OE-NC + MI, and FTO overexpression (OE-FTO) + MI groups, with six mice per group and sex-balanced. The MI model was induced by ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery.[31] Sham surgery was performed, except without the coronary ligature. For the OE-NC + MI group, the mice received injections of OE-NC lentivirus (5 × 107 viral genome particles per heart, Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) following MI. For the OE-FTO + MI group, the mice were injected with OE-FTO lentivirus as above, following MI.[32] The lentivirus injection was administered near the area of infarction. Seven days later, the animals were anesthetized (2% isoflurane) and sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and myocardial tissues were harvested.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was conducted using a 30-MHz Vevo 2100 small animal ultrasound system (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada). After anesthetizing the mice with 2% isoflurane, M-mode images were obtained in the short-axis view at the papillary muscle level. Several parameters, including ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS), were assessed.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

Myocardial tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated by an ascending series of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin wax for histological evaluation. The cardiac tissue sections (5 μM) were HE stained using a commercial kit (C0105S, Beyotime, China). The sections were added in absolute ethanol 3 times for 5 min each, and xylene 2 times for 5 min each, stained with hematoxylin solution for 5 min, dehydrated in 95% ethanol for 1 min, and treated with eosin solution for 1 min. The slides were blocked with neutral resin and imaged under a light microscope (BX43, OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

Masson trichrome staining

Myocardial tissues were embedded in paraffin wax as described above. Myocardial paraffin sections (5 μM) were incubated with Masson dye solution (G1006, Servicebio, China) according to the instructions. The sections were added in Masson A solution for 30 min, and then added to both Masson B and Masson C mixture solutions (1:1) for 3 min. Subsequently, the sections were added in Masson D solution for 5 min, Masson E solution for 1 min, and Masson F solution for 30 s. Finally, the sections were added in 1% acetic acid for 2 min and the slides were blocked with neutral resin and imaged under a light microscope (BX43, OLYMPUS, Japan).

Detection of myocardial injury markers

The frozen myocardial tissues were lysed, and the supernatant was harvested by centrifugation at 4°C for 10 min. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were examined by an LDH kit (BC0685, Solarbio, Beijing, China) with a microplate reader (Model 550, Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) at 450 nm, creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) concentrations were examined by a CK-MB kit (ab285231, Abcam) with a microplate reader at 450 nm. Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) concentration was examined by a cTnI kit (D721149, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) with a microplate reader at 450 nm.

Cell culture and OGD model

Human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) were acquired from ScienCell Research Laboratories (DFSC-EC-01, Shanghai, China) and grown in endothelial cell medium medium (PCM-H-040, ScienCell) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, A5670701, Invitrogen) and 50 μg/ml penicillin/streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, MA, USA) at 37°C. For OGD, HUVECs were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 11966025, Gibco, MA, USA) without glucose and 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and at 37°C with 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2 for 12 h. HUVECs were examined by short tandem repeat identification and were mycoplasma-free.

Cell transfections

For gain-of-function studies, the FTO gene was cloned into the genomeditech lentivirus, cytomegalovirus promoter, multiple cloning site (PGMLV-CMV-MCS)-3 × Flag-PGKPuro to yield an FTO overexpression (OE-FTO) plasmid (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China). The negative control vector PGMLV-CMV-MCS-3 × Flag-PGK-Puro-NC (OENC, Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) represented the control. Following transfection of HEK293T cells through Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001, Thermo Fisher, USA) as directed, the supernatant containing lentivirus was collected. Next, HUVECs were transduced with lentivirus expressing OE-NC or OE-FTO for 48 h using Lipofectamine 3000. Cells resistant to puromycin (0.25 μg/mL, A1113803, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were selected for further analysis. Western blotting was used to detect the transfection efficiency.

Cell-counting-kit-8 (CCK-8) assays

HUVEC viability was evaluated using CCK-8 assays (CK04, Dojindo, Japan), as directed. Cells (2 × 103/well) were inoculated in 96-well plates and grown at 37°C for 24 h before measuring absorbances at 450 nm by a microplate reader.

Transwell assays

HUVECs (1 × 105) were inoculated into the top compartment of the Transwell insert (8 μm pore size, 3422, Corning, USA). The lower compartment held 600 μL of DMEM media with 20% FBS. After 24 h, migrating cells were stained by CalceinAM (Y237214, Beyotime, China), and the migrated cells were quantified under a fluorescence microscope (Axio Scope A1, Carl-Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Wound healing assays

HUVECs were inoculated in 6-well plates and allowed to grow until 80% confluent. A scratch was made in the monolayer with a yellow pipette tip. HUVECs were washed and maintained in serum-free DMEM. Cell migration was assessed at 0 and 24 h under inverted microscopy, with ImageJ (version 1.8.0, Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA) utilized for quantification.

Tube formation assays

Matrigel (60 μL, 356237, BD Bioscience, USA) was utilized to coat 96-well plates for 1 h at 37°C, following which HUVECs (4 × 104/well) were inoculated and grown for 24 h at 37°C. The development of vascular networks was assessed under inverted phase-contrast microscopy by calculating the tube number with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plug of ImageJ software.

The m6A modifications analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (15596026, Invitrogen). Overall m6A levels were assessed using the EpiQuik m6A Methylation Quantification Kit (P-9005, Epigentek, USA). First, the purified RNA (200 ng) and the m6A standard were added to the wells following the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, the capture antibody solution and the detection antibody solution were utilized. The m6A levels were measured in each well by assessing absorbances with a microplate reader at 450 nm, and the relative m6A modifications were quantified according to the standard curve.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

The control and OE-FTO HUVEC were exposed to OGD for 12 h. The mRNA was extracted with a NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (E7490, NEB, USA) and NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (E7400, NEB, USA). Fragmented RNAs (~200 nucleotides) were incubated with m6A antibody (ab284130, Abcam, China) using a methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) m6A kit (MeRIP, 17-10499, Millipore, USA). The input mRNA and the m6A-immunoprecipitated mRNA containing m6A peaks were utilized for library development with a NEBNext Ultra kit (E7775, NEB, USA), followed by sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq 6000).

Bioinformatic analysis

Paired-end reads were evaluated for quality, followed by alignment of the clean reads from the libraries. The methylated sites on the RNA (peaks) and differentially methylated sites were classified by model-based analysis of ChIP-Seq and diffReps software, respectively. Differentially methylated RNA sites were identified using P < 0.05 and |FC| ≥ 2. The differential peaks that overlapped with the exons of the mRNAs were selected. The DEGs were identified using the same criteria. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analyses were undertaken using database for annotation, visualization, and integrated discovery to interpret the functional enrichment and annotation of the differentially methylated genes.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol, and PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR037A, Takara, Dalian, China) was utilized for reverse transcription. qPCR was carried out using SYBR Premix ExTaq Kit (639503, Takara) using a qPCR system (QuantStudio 5, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, USA) with the thermocycling conditions: 95°C for 3 min and 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, at 60°C for 30 s and at 72°C for 30 s, and the expression of genes was determined with the 2−ΔΔCT method. qPCR was repeated three times, and GAPDH was used as an internal reference. The sequences of the primers were: FTO: forward 5'-TCCGAGAAGCCAGCAGTGTAT-3', reverse 5'-AGGTCCCGAAATAAGCAGCC- 3'. ABCF1: forward 5'-GGCTTCTTGGATGATGTCTGCAC-3'; reverse 5'-GCCTTCAGCTCCTTCAGCTTTTT-3', GAPDH: forward 5'-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3', reverse 5'- ACCA CCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3'.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1005; Beyotime, China). The samples (30 μg) were resolved through sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore Corporation, MA, USA). The blots were blocked (5% skim milk) and treated overnight with the following primary antibodies: anti-FTO (ab280081, 1/1000, Abcam, China), anti-ABCF1 (107805, 1/1000, NovoPro Bioscience, China), and anti-GAPDH (ab8245, 1/1000, Abcam) at 4°C, and treatment with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody (ab288151, 1/2000, Abcam) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody (SA00001-1, 1/10000, Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (PE0010, Solarbio, China), and ImageJ software was employed to evaluate the gray value of the blots.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-qPCR

A Magna RIP RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation Kit (17-700, Sigma, USA) was utilized. HUVEC lysates were mixed overnight with RIP buffer containing antibodies against FTO (ab280081, Abcam) and IgG (ab172730, Abcam) at 4°C, followed by addition of protein A/G magnetic beads, elution of RNA, reverse transcribed into complementary DNA, and measurement of the enrichment of ABCF1 mRNA by qPCR.

MeRIP-qPCR

A Magna RIP Kit (17-10499, Millipore, USA) was utilized as directed. HUVECs (2 × 107) were lysed, and RNA was fragmented. The RNA fragments were conjugated with magnetic beads linked with anti-m6A (ab286164, Abcam) and anti-IgG (ab172730, Abcam) antibodies for 4 h at 4°C. After Proteinase K digestion for 40 min, the enriched RNA was purified, and the m6A modification in ABCF1 mRNA was analyzed by qPCR.

Actinomycin D treatment

HUVECs were treated with actinomycin D (5 mg/mL, Selleck, USA) for 0–6 h, after which total RNA was extracted, and the levels of ABCF1 were determined by qPCR. The half-life of ABCF1 was determined to evaluate the effect of FTO on the stability of ABCF1 mRNA following m6A modification.

Statistical analysis

Three independent experiments were conducted in all cases. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 22.0 using independent-samples t-tests for comparing two groups or two-way analysis of variance with post hoc Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

FTO expression is decreased in infarcted myocardial tissue and OGD-treated HUVECs

To determine the association between FTO expression and MI, we analyzed publicly available datasets (GSE66360).[30] FTO mRNA levels were markedly decreased in circulating ECs from patients with acute MI (n = 49) compared with those of healthy individuals (n = 50) [Figure 1a]. Next, we assessed m6A modification and FTO levels in the myocardial tissues of MI model animals. The findings indicated that m6A modification was greater in the MI group relative to the sham group [Figure 1b], suggesting an association of elevated m6A methylation with the pathophysiology of MI. Moreover, FTO levels were lower in MI mice relative to the sham group [Figure 1c]. Furthermore, OGD-treated HUVECs exhibited an increase in m6A modification [Figure 1d] and a reduction in FTO levels [Figure 1e] relative to the controls. These results suggest that FTO, an m6A demethylase, is involved in the pathophysiology of MI.

FTO ameliorates OGD-induced HUVEC dysfunction

To determine the functional role of FTO in HUVECs exposed to OGD, a gain-of-function assay was performed. The efficiency of transfection was confirmed through western blot analysis, which indicated that FTO protein level could be effectively increased through overexpression in HUVECs [Figure 2a]. The CCK8 assay revealed that FTO overexpression (OE-FTO) enhanced the viability of HUVECs following OGD treatment [Figure 2b]. Moreover, wound healing and cell migration assays revealed that OE-FTO promoted the migratory ability of HUVECs post-OGD [Figure 2c and d]. Furthermore, OE-FTO increased tube formation in HUVECs following OGD treatment, which suggests a key role in angiogenesis [Figure 2e]. Taken together, these results suggest that FTO overexpression mitigates HUVEC dysfunction caused by OGD.

Examination of the differential m6A methylation enrichment pathway between FTO overexpression and control HUVECs following OGD treatment

To identify the mechanism underlying the effects of FTO in HUVECs post-OGD treatment, MeRIP-seq analysis was conducted. We identified 23,360 distinct m6A peaks in the OE-NC group and 21,028 distinct m6A peaks in the OE-FTO group [Figure 3a]. Overall, 2,048 markedly different m6A-methylated peaks were identified, with 666 increased and 1,382 decreased in the OE-FTO group. Next, we evaluated the m6A-methylated peaks distribution patterns [Figure 3b and c]. Particularly, 37.5% of the m6Amethylated peaks were found in coding regions (CDS), with 2.8% enriched in the 3h untranslated region and 9.4% in the 5he%c for the OE-NC group, whereas 34.8% were in the CDS, with 2.8% in the 3he%% and 10.4% in the 5he4% for the OEFTO group [Figure 3c]. Motif prediction was also performed [Figure 3d]. GO and KEGG analyses revealed differential m6A methylation profiles. GO enrichment analysis showed that the differentially m6A-methylated mRNAs were associated with biological processes, including regulation of cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)-mediated signaling [Figure 3e]. In the molecular function category, these m6A-methylated mRNAs were involved in GTPase regulator activity. At the cellular component level, the lysosomal membrane was highly enriched. KEGG pathway analysis revealed differential m6A methylation patterns in the mRNAs, which included ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter, insulin, and Notch pathways [Figure 3f]. Collectively, these results indicate that FTO-induced alterations in m6A methylation may be functionally linked to OGD-impaired HUVEC dysfunction.

KEGG analysis showed involvement of the up-DEGs with cGMP-PKG pathway, cell adhesion molecules, and related processes [Figure 4d].

A combined analysis of m6A methylation and mRNAs was assessed using the thresholds |FC|> 2 and P < 0.05 for both parameters. We identified 54 mRNAs exhibiting significant changes in m6A modification and mRNA levels. The associations between m6A methylation and mRNA levels were evaluated using a four-quadrant graph and a Venn diagram [Figures 4e], which categorized the significant changes into the following four distinct groups: 29 mRNAs with marked hypermethylation and upregulation, 2 mRNAs with marked hypermethylation and downregulation, 4 mRNAs with significant hypomethylation and upregulation, and 19 mRNAs with significant hypomethylation and downregulation in the OE-FTO group following OGD treatment. As FTO decreased m6A methylation and increased mRNA expression in cells, 4 mRNAs (TBC1D3I, B3GAT2, TMSB15B, and ABCF1) that were significantly hypomethylated and upregulated in the OE-FTO group were selected for further study.

Previous studies demonstrated that ABCF1 is involved in cell proliferation and migration. Our results indicated that ABCF1 mRNA level was downregulated in HUVECs exposed to OGD relative to the controls [Figure 4f]. Thus, ABCF1 was chosen as a potential target because of its potential role in OGD-treated HUVECs.

FTO regulates m6A modification and expression of ABCF1 in HUVECs following OGD treatment

We examined the interaction between FTO and ABCF1 in HUVECs under OGD-induced conditions. First, integrative genomics viewer analysis revealed differential m6A peaks for ABCF1 in OE-FTO and control HUVECs exposed to OGD, and the m6A peak distribution on ABCF1 mRNA was reduced in OE-FTO group than that of the control group [Figure 5a]. This suggests that FTO may affect the m6A methylation of ABCF1 in OGD-treated HUVECs. RIP-PCR confirmed the interaction between FTO and ABCF1 in HUVECs [Figure 5b]. The MeRIP-PCR experiment indicated that FTO overexpression reduced the m6A methylation of ABCF1 in HUVECs [Figure 5c]. These results suggest a regulatory relationship between FTO and the m6A modifications of ABCF1 mRNA. Assessment of RNA stability revealed that overexpression of FTO markedly enhanced the mRNA stability of ABCF1 in OGD-treated HUVECs [Figure 5d]. Western blotting further corroborated these findings by showing that FTO overexpression increases ABCF1 protein level in OGD-treated HUVECs [Figure 5e]. Collectively, these data indicate that FTO increases ABCF1 expression in OGD-induced ECs by reducing ABCF1 m6A modifications and stabilizing ABCF1 mRNA. This interaction represents a novel mechanism associated with MI pathogenesis.

FTO overexpression reduces myocardial pathological injury following MI

To further confirm that FTO may be involved in myocardial pathological injury, mice received injections of the OE-NC or OE-FTO lentiviruses following MI induction. The arrangement of myocardial cells was disordered after MI. Relative to the OE-NC group, OE-FTO improved the disordered arrangement of myocardial cells in myocardial tissues after MI [Figure 6a]. The myocardial fibrosis increased after MI. Relative to the OE-NC group, OE-FTO reduced myocardial fibrosis after MI [Figure 6b]. EF and FS were lower in the MI group, while EF and FS were higher in the OE-FTO group relative to the OE-NC group [Figure 6c and d], suggesting that OE-FTO alleviates MI-induced cardiac dysfunction. Moreover, OE-FTO reduced LDH [Figure 6e], CK-MB [Figure 6f], and cTnI [Figure 6g] levels in myocardial tissues relative to OE-NC animals. Finally, FTO and ABCF1 protein levels were raised in the myocardial tissues of the OE-FTO group relative to the OE-NC group [Figure 6h]. Taken together, these data indicate that FTO overexpression reduces myocardial pathological injury after MI.

DISCUSSION

Recently, more and more studies have found epigenetic m6A modifications are closely linked with MI pathogenesis, suggesting their potential in preventing MI development. Previous reports have exhibited the participation of FTO in hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated cardiomyocytes and ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) mice model. Thus, the abnormal expression of FTO may correlate with MI progression. What is more, EC dysfunction inhibits the repair of damaged myocardial tissues post-MI. However, whether FTO has an effect on EC function post-MI still uncertain. Here, we examined the function of FTO in OGD-induced EC dysfunction and myocardial pathological injury following MI. We found that reduced FTO levels, whereas m6A methylation levels were raised in myocardial tissue from MI model mice and OGD-induced HUVECs. Furthermore, FTO overexpression suppressed HUVEC dysfunction triggered by OGD treatment. Mechanistic experiments revealed that FTO enhances ABCF1 expression by mediating ABCF1 m6A methylation in OGD-treated HUVECs. In vivo experiments revealed that FTO overexpression mitigates myocardial pathological injury post-MI by increasing ABCF1 expression. These results suggest that FTO may inhibit endothelial dysfunction and myocardial injury following MI, implying that it is a target for MI treatment.

The effect of m6A methylation on MI pathogenesis has been extensively investigated. For example, the m6A reader IGF2BP3 inhibits reactive oxygen species level and elevates myocardial regeneration through upregulating matrix metallopeptidase 3 expression in a m6A methylation-dependent manner.[33] Moreover, overexpression of the m6A eraser AlkB homolog 5, RNA demethylase (ALKBH5) enhances yes-associated protein (YAP) expression through demethylating m6A modification on yes-associated protein YAP mRNA, which is helpful to promote the repair of infarcted myocardium.[34] Previous studies have also identified that FTO level is downregulated in myocardial tissue of I/R mice, resulting in an overall increase in m6A modification. Furthermore, FTO overexpression upregulates MHRT expression and reduces m6A modification of MHRT mRNA in hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated cardiomyocytes.[22] In addition, FTO overexpression mitigates calcium overload and improves the cardiac energy metabolism after hypoxia/reoxygenation exposure by increasing the expression of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2a.[35] Our results are consistent with the previous study, and further present that FTO expression reduces in myocardial tissue after MI, implying FTO may be involved in the pathophysiology of MI.

Moreover, EC dysfunction suppresses angiogenesis after MI. Nevertheless, some research on m6A methylation has exhibited its potential to regulate angiogenesis. For instance, overexpression of the m6A methyltransferase METTL3 enhances angiogenesis and improves cardiac function in a mouse model of MI.[18] The m6A eraser ALKBH5 elevates sphingosine kinase-1 level, which enhances angiogenesis after ischemic stress.[36] However, a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between FTO and EC dysfunction following MI is needed. Our study presents that FTO expression is reduced in OGD-treated HUVECs, implying FTO may be involved in EC dysfunction triggered by OGD treatment. To explore the protective effect of FTO on OGD-treated EC, a gain-of-function assay was utilized. Our findings show that FTO overexpression inhibits OGD-triggered HUVEC dysfunction, thus eliciting the potential role of FTO in angiogenesis after MI.

Furthermore, we conducted both MeRIP-seq and RNASeq experiments to evaluate m6A methylation as well as to identify DEGs in OGD-treated HUVECs following FTO overexpression. The results indicated that ABCF1 acts as a target for FTO in OGD-treated EC. Then, we found that FTO overexpression decreased the m6A modification of ABCF1 by MeRIP-PCR. The reduced m6A modification of ABCF1 mRNA may be associated with its RNA stability. Furthermore, RNA stability analysis revealed that FTO overexpression resulted in an increase in ABCF1 mRNA stability in HUVECs following OGD treatment. Taken together, the results suggest that FTO enhances the stability of ABCF1 mRNA by inhibiting its m6A modification in HUVECs, potentially influencing cellular responses under OGD conditions. It has been found that ABCF1 promotes growth and migration in glioblastoma cells through the PI3K/AKT axis.[37] Thus, the stabilization of ABCF1 mRNA under OGD conditions suggests an adaptive mechanism used by ECs to maintain essential functions during OGD exposure. These results indicate the regulatory interplay between FTO and ABCF1 mRNA stability, highlighting the complex molecular mechanisms underlying endothelial cellular adaptation to OGD.

Finally, our data present that FTO overexpression inhibits myocardial pathological injury, as well as reduces myocardial injury marker levels after MI in vivo, therefore improving cardiac function. Our findings also demonstrate that FTO overexpression increases ABCF1 expression in the myocardial tissues after MI. These findings indicate a protective mechanism associated with FTO overexpression in inhibiting myocardial pathological injury after MI.

Although our study has offered new insights into the molecular mechanisms by which FTO inhibits endothelial dysfunction and myocardial injury following MI, some key issues still need to be investigated. First, the clinical samples were not utilized in this study, and hence, detecting the effectiveness of these data in clinical feasibility is difficult. The clinical samples should be employed in future analysis to increase the accuracy of this study. Moreover, the verification of animal experiments should be performed in the future. Our study did not investigate how FTO regulates ABCF1 expression in vivo, or whether FTO affects other possible pathways. In future, the in-depth connection between FTO and ABCF1 in myocardial pathological injury after MI will be explored.

SUMMARY

Our study showed that FTO, an m6A demethylase, ameliorates endothelial dysfunction following OGD and myocardial pathological injury after MI. In addition, FTO increases ABCF1 expression by reducing ABCF1 m6A methylation and enhancing the mRNA stability in OGD-treated ECs. These results offer new possibilities for developing therapeutic strategies to treat MI injury.
