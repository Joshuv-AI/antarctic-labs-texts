Serum LBP and zonulin levels with brain MRS findings and gastrointestinal symptoms in schizophrenia and health
Authors: Aleksander Turek, Natalia Śmierciak, Marta Szwajca, Paulina Karcz, Wirginia Krzyściak, Bogna Batko, Marcin Magacz, Paulina Donicz, Katarzyna Furman, Amira Bryll, Tadeusz Popiela, Maciej Pilecki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSchizophrenia is a chronic, severe psychiatric disorder characterised by abnormal peripheral immune response parameters, central neuroinflammatory processes and blood-brain barrier disregulation. Schizophrenia is thoroughly studied in the gut-brain axis paradigm, showing comorbidity with gastrointestinal disorders. Studies show variations in serum biomarkers of intestinal barrier permeability, zonulin and lipopolysaccharide binding protein (LBP) in schizophrenia. The function of LBP as an acute-phase class I protein and zonulin as a pre-haptoglobin-2 may play a significant role in neuroinflammation. Meta-analyses of brain magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) show irregular metabolite concentrations in patients with schizophrenia.Material and methods28 patients with schizophrenia (SCZ) in remission and 29 healthy participants (CON) were involved in the study. All participants underwent a brain MRS 3T examination in the anterior (ACC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), laboratory blood tests, and fulfilled the Gastrointestinal Symptoms Rating Scale (GSRS). SCZ were evaluated with Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS).ResultsThe groups differed in serum LBP levels (SCZ: 11ug/ml vs CON: 7.9ug/ml; p = 0.02) and did not differ in serum zonulin levels. The partial correlation analysis, controlled for age, ALT, HOMA-IR, and daily neuroleptic dose, revealed a serum LBP association with myo-inositol in PCC (r = −0.49; p < 0.05) in SCZ and in CON (r = −0.46; p < 0.05). In SCZ, serum zonulin correlated with glutamine in ACC (r = −0.43; p < 0.05). LBP correlated with age of onset (r = −0.46; p < 0.05) and lower gastrointestinal symptoms (r = 0.54; p < 0.01) in SCZ, and with upper gastrointestinal symptoms in CON (r = 0.53; p < 0.05). No significant associations between PANSS subscales with LBP or zonulin levels were observed.ConclusionsThis is the first study to assess intestinal permeability markers with brain MRS findings in health and in schizophrenia. Results indicate associations between LBP and myo-inositol in PCC in health and in schizophrenia, suggesting a physiological role of LBP in glial homeostasis. Zonulin association with glutamine levels in ACC may contribute to the complex pathophysiology of schizophrenia. Patients with schizophrenia exhibited increased levels of LBP, which is associated with age of onset and discomfort from lower gastrointestinal symptoms. Healthy controls show associations between LBP and upper gastrointestinal symptoms. A closer examination of the gut-brain axis may provide novel insights into the neuroimmune dysregulation in schizophrenia.Keywords: Gut-brain axis, Schizophrenia, Magnetic resonance spectroscopy, Zonulin, Lipopolysaccharide binding protein, Gastrointestinal symptoms

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a chronic, heterogeneous disorder characterised by delusions, hallucinations, negative symptoms, and disorganisation of speech and behaviour (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Dickinson et al., 2018). An increasing body of scientific evidence indicates the presence of immune response abnormalities in individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. Recently published network meta-analysis indicated elevated interleukins (IL) 1, 1B, 6, 8, 10, IL-1 receptor antagonist, soluble IL-2 receptor, and C-reactive protein (CRP) in people with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls (Halstead et al., 2023). The mechanisms of atypical immune system traits in schizophrenia are not recognised. There is a significant interest in exploring the gut-brain axis paradigm in individuals with psychiatric disorders. Important research has been conducted on schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism spectrum disorders, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and eating disorders (Jang et al., 2020; Kim and Shin, 2018). One of the hypotheses in the field of gut-brain axis applicable for schizophrenia proposes disruption of intestinal barrier integrity, resulting in dysregulated paracellular transport of antigenic molecules and microorganisms from the lumen of the intestines into the bloodstream, resulting in systemic and chronic immune response (Aleman et al., 2023). The hypothesis is supported by numerous studies indicating significant comorbidity of schizophrenia and celiac disease, irritable bowel syndrome, metabolic syndrome, liver diseases, and isolated gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (Severance et al., 2015, 2016; Lu et al., 2022; Grant et al., 2022). Various methodologies for the functionality of the intestinal barrier assessment in vivo are present. The methods typically involve measurement of the marker concentration orally ingested in probes that are subsequently excreted in urine, alongside detecting biomarkers that are mechanistically associated with intestinal barrier function, such as specific proteins or endotoxins (Camilleri and Vella, 2022; Schoultz and Keita, 2020). A key biomarker for studying intestinal barrier permeability is the concentration of zonulin in the serum. Zonulin evolved from complement-associated proteins, developing new functions as a result of mutations in its catalytic domain. The protein is secreted mainly in the liver and acts as an inactive precursor of haptoglobin 2 (pre-haptoglobin 2). Following a proteolytic cleavage, the haptoglobin 2 plays multifunctional roles, e.g. the clearance of haemoglobin, regulation of inflammatory response, angiogenesis, and reduction of oxidative stress (Fasano, 2011; Chmielińska et al., 2021; Levy et al., 2010). The function of regulating intestinal permeability is related to the additional paracrine pathway of zonulin secretion, mainly in the lining of the small intestine. Zonulin is directly involved in the regulation of the reversible opening of tight junctions (TJs) between enterocytes, which impacts the movement of antigens through the tissue via the paracellular route (Wang et al., 2000). The expression and secretion of zonulin have been identified in various organs, including immune cells, kidneys, adipose tissue, the heart, lungs, skin, and the brain. However, the specific functions of zonulin in these tissues are not yet well understood. Significant variations in serum zonulin concentration were observed across a range of gastrointestinal disorders. Elevated levels of zonulin have been linked to non-celiac gluten sensitivity, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), inflammatory bowel diseases, necrotising enterocolitis, cirrhosis, hepatitis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and hepatocellular carcinoma (Barbaro et al., 2020; Caviglia et al., 2019; De Munck et al., 2020; Rezazadegan et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019; Vanuytsel et al., 2013; Voulgaris et al., 2021). Increased serum zonulin was also documented in immune disorders, such as rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, or spondyloarthropathy (Chmielińska et al., 2021; Audo et al., 2022; Ciccia et al., 2017; Tajik et al., 2020). Scientific investigations of serum zonulin considering the gut-brain axis resulted in discoveries of elevated concentrations in schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and autism spectrum disorder (Asbjornsdottir et al., 2020; Dumitrescu et al., 2021; Esnafoglu et al., 2017; Karagözlü et al., 2022; Karim et al., 2022; Kılıç et al., 2020; Özyurt et al., 2018; Stevens et al., 2018; Usta et al., 2021). Other proteins mechanistically related to intestinal barrier functionality include lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP). LBP is produced in hepatocytes and acts as a class I acute-phase protein. LBP is involved in innate immunity and mediates the response to bacterial endotoxins through catalization of lipopolysaccharyde (LPS) transfer to Toll-Like Receptor 4 complexes and CD14 proteins. The reaction enables epithelial and endothelial cells to adequately react to LPS molecules, which results in IL-6 and tumour-necrosis factor-α secretion (Ryu et al., 2017). LBP serum levels peak shortly after bacteremia onset and remain elevated for up to 72 h (Hudgins et al., 2003; Schumann et al., 1990; Tobias and Ulevitch, 1993). Direct involvement in the inflammatory response to LPS led to measurements of LBP concentration and its autoantibodies in a variety of pathological states. Distinctive deviations were observed in neoplasms, metabolic and inflammatory, but also neuropsychiatric disorders, e.g., Parkinson's disease and depression (Chalubinska-Fendler et al., 2019; Gokulakrishnan et al., 2022; Dal Santo et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2023; Hasebe et al., 2022; Hasegawa et al., 2015; Heymann et al., 2022; Just et al., 2021; Moreno-Navarrete et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2014; Pastor et al., 2007; Severance et al., 2013; Scheurink et al., 2023; Tuomi and Logomarsino, 2016; Zhao et al., 2023). Increased concentrations of peripheral cytokines may affect the central nervous system in several ways. Peripheral cytokines may pass through the circumventricular organs by active transport with molecules to the CNS environment. Cytokines may also activate the lining of the cerebral vasculature. Furthermore, inflammatory agents can bind to cytokine receptors related to the vagus nerve, stimulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Moreover, direct neuronal routes may be influenced by cytokine alterations in GI tract. Vagus nerve afferents detect intestinal molecules, resulting in direct signalling to the CNS. Enteric nervous system interacts with antigens and metabolites from the intestinal lumen and transmits signals via intestinofugal neurons to sympathetic ganglia, then through extrinsic primary afferent neurons that follow spinal and vagal routes to the CNS (Cryan et al., 2019). Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) is a widely used imaging technique that enables the detection and quantification of distinct metabolites in the brain. The number of MRS studies investigating patients with schizophrenia delivered data indicating abnormalities in a range of CNS metabolites. The meta-analytical studies show significant variations in gluthatione, GABA, myo-inositol, and glutamate in various brain regions, including posterior and anterior cingulate cortex (Das et al., 2018; Kumar et al., 2020, 2021; Merritt et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2024; Sydnor and Roalf, 2020). There is limited evidence in professional literature showing associations between intestinal permeability traits and neuroimaging findings (Scheurink et al., 2023; Witt et al., 2019; Mayer et al., 2019; Moreno-Navarrete et al., 2017). According to the authors' knowledge, there are no published studies describing relations between serum intestinal permeability markers and brain MRS findings in health and disease. Furthermore, psychiatric literature lacks MRS studies in schizophrenia that simultaneously investigate gastrointestinal symptoms common in this patient group. This study investigates the serum biomarkers zonulin and LBP in people with schizophrenia and their associations with brain magnetic resonance spectroscopy findings, gastrointestinal symptoms, other blood laboratory parameters, and clinical state characteristics.

Material and methods

Groups

29 patients with schizophrenia (SCZ) and 28 healthy controls (CON) were included in the study. The participants were recruited at the Clinical Department of Adult, Child, and Adolescent Psychiatry at the University Hospital in Krakow, Poland. The hospital physicians distributed the study invitations to their patients. The patients in the stable phase of the disease were evaluated by two independent psychiatrists and met the criteria for F20.0-F20.3 of ICD-10 and schizophrenia disorder of DSM-5. The participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 40 years. The healthy control group was recruited through advertisements on the social media of the University Hospital of Krakow. Every participant underwent a specific evaluation according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria for all participants involved, i.a., diagnosis of any gastrointestinal, autoimmune disorder, acute infection, history of microbiota transplantation, antibiotics intake or psychotropic substance abuse in the last three months. The control group was deliberately interviewed for psychiatric diagnoses and psychological treatment history. Participants from the control group with implemented psychiatric pharmacotherapy or with actual psychiatric diagnoses were excluded from the analysis.

27 SCZ participants and 21 CON participants underwent a bioelectrical impedance examination using “InBody120” professional body mass composition analyser. The measurement was performed in the morning, before medication and first meal of the day intake. The data was collected according to the manufacturer's instructions using “Looking’Body120” software.

The patients diagnosed with schizophrenia received prescribed medications at optimal dosages, following the treatment guidelines outlined by the American Psychiatric Association and the latest professional scientific pharmacotherapy literature for managing schizophrenia. Dose conversion of antipsychotics (Antipsychotic Dose Equivalent - ADE) was conducted using DDD (defined daily doses) method with an olanzapine equivalent (Leucht et al., 2015, 2016).

Clinical symptoms assessment

The severity of schizophrenia symptoms was evaluated by a psychiatrist with a standardised procedure using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) and Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) (van der Gaag et al., 2006; Pedersen et al., 2018; Kay et al., 1987; Rzewuska, 2002). The precise clinical interview was conducted with a patient and a close family member, carefully assessing their clinical history and medication intake. In both groups, every participant fulfilled a Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale auto-questionnaire in a Polish, validated version (Kulich et al., 2005, 2008). In the statistical analysis, we included variables symbolising the sum of scores from questions 1 to 8 related to Upper Gastrointestinal Tract symptoms and from questions 9 to 15 related to Lower Gastrointestinal Tract symptoms.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations were performed in the MRI laboratory of the Department of Diagnostic Imaging at the University Hospital in Krakow, Poland. Magnetic resonance imaging and spectroscopy (MRS) were performed using the 3,0 T S Vida Fit scanner using a 20-channel head coil. First, non-contrast brain MRI imaging was performed according to the standard MRI protocol to exclude possible morphological changes in the central nervous system. The MRI brain examination protocol, including 3D sequences: T1-weighted, T2-weighted, dark fluid (slice thickness 1,0 mm, spacing 0,9 mm) and 2D diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (slice thickness 3,0 mm, spacing 0,9 mm and b = 1000 mm/s). Contrast agents were not used during the study. The next step in imaging was to perform spectroscopy using the single voxel technique (SVS – single voxel spectroscopy). The acquisition parameters for MRS were TE (echo time) 35 msec, TR 2000 msec, and 40 averages were acquired. The duration of the MRS sequence was 1,20 min. The MRS signal was obtained from two locations (VOI - volume of interest) situated symmetrically in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) (Fig. 1). The VOI was adapted to the anatomical size of the location from which the spectrum was obtained. The mean VOI volume was 3,4 mm3. MRS data were analysed on LCModel. Concentrations of metabolites are measured as resonance areas of the metabolite (see Fig. 2).

The following metabolites were selected from the spectrum: L-Alanine (Ala 1,48 ppm), Aspartate (Asp 3,8 ppm), Creatine (Cr 3,02 and 3,9 ppm), Phosphocreatine (PCr 3,02 ppm and 3,93 ppm), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA 2,3 ppm), Glucose (Glc 3,43 and 3,8 ppm), Glutamine (Gln 2,45 and 3,7 ppm), Glutamate (Glu 2,1 and 3,7 ppm), Glycerophosphocholine (GPC 3,6 ppm), Phosphocholine (PCh 4,2 ppm), Glutathione (GSH 3,7 ppm), myo-Inositol (Ins 3,6 ppm), L-Lactate (lac 1,33 ppm), N-Acetylaspartate (NAA 2,02 ppm), N-Acetylaspartylglutamate (NAAG 4,1 ppm), scyllo-Inositol (Scyllo 3,35 ppm), Taurine (Tau 3,42 ppm), Lipids (Lip09 0,9 ppm, lip13a and lip13b 1,3 ppm, lip20 2,0 ppm), macromolecule (MM09 0,9 ppm, MM12 1,2 ppm, MM14 1,4 ppm, MM17 1,7 ppm, MM20 2,0 ppm). The concentration of sums of individual metabolites was also calculated: GPC + PCh, NAA + NAAG, Cr + PCr, Glu + Gln, Lip13a + Lip13b, MM14+Lip13a + Lip13b + MM12, MM09+Lip09, MM20+Lip20. The concentration ratios of each metabolite were determined relative to the total concentration of sum creatine and phosphocreatine (e.g., NAA/Cr + PCr). All the values were calculated for each VOI. A qualitative (visual assessment) and quantitative analysis of the reconstructed MRS spectra were performed.

Blood collection for routine laboratory tests

In the morning, following an 8-h fasting period and overnight rest, venous blood was collected from patients and healthy volunteers for comprehensive laboratory examinations. A set of tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity, highly-sensitive C-reactive protein (CRP), complement components C3 and C4, glucose, insulin, and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index, were conducted. Routine analyses were conducted at the central laboratory of the University Hospital in Krakow utilising automated analysers. This laboratory engages in daily internal quality control and systematic external quality control in adherence to established standards for medical diagnostic laboratories. All procedures were completed within one workday. In the morning, from 8:00 - 9:00 a.m., participants had their blood drawn; next, the clinical state assessment was performed, and magnetic resonance imaging was performed from 11:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. The interval between blood collection and clinical assessment was 1 to 3 h, and between blood collection and MRS 3 to 9 h.

Intestinal permeability markers measurement

Zonulin concentration in patient sera was determined by using a commercially available ELISA kit – Human Haptoglobin/Zonulin E1117Hu (BT LAB, Shanghai, China). The procedure was performed according to the manufacturer's datasheet. All assays were performed in triplicate, and the final concentration was expressed as the mean of the replicates. Assay microplates were read using a ThermoFisher Multiskan Sky plate reader (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA), and concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve using ThermoFisher SkanIt software (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA).

Plasma concentrations of LBP were determined using the custom-made xMAP technology Luminex assays (R&D Systems, USA) according to the manufacturer's recommendation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed within the R environment using tidyverse, psych, and GGally packages (R Core Team, 2025; Wickham, 2023; Revelle, 2024; Wright, 2025; Schloerke et al., 2024). Due to multiple cases of nonnormality and some outliers, non-parametric analyses were performed, including Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Spearman's rank-order correlation coefficients.

In the control group, the most frequent education level was a master's degree (64.3%), while in the SCZ group it was secondary education (51.7%). According to professional literature, intergroup testing included serum zonulin and LBP levels, along with potentially related variables concerning their secretion and functionality. Serum zonulin concentrations did not show significant differences between groups, indicating medians in the SCZ 102 μg/mL (Q25-75 = 78.9-153.5) and in the CON 100.2 μg/mL (Q25-75 = 91.6-200). LBP serum level was significantly higher in the SCZ than in healthy controls (Means = 11 ± 5.7 vs 7.9 ± 4.8; p = 0.025). Age did not differ significantly between the two groups (Qi et al., 2017). In terms of inflammatory response agents, no statistical difference between groups was observed, noticing higher serum concentrations of CRP and C3 Complement in the SCZ. Equal C4 Complement protein levels were observed (Sturgeon and Fasano, 2016; Fasano, 2020). Alanine transaminase activity (ALT) showed insignificantly higher activity in patients vs controls (Voulgaris et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023; Pietrukaniec et al., 2019). Finally, HOMA-IR index indicated significantly higher levels in the SCZ (Küme et al., 2017; Moreno-Navarrete et al., 2012; Pepe et al., 2024). Comparing groups with reduced observations (27 for SCZ, 21 for CON), we noticed a significant difference between groups, showing higher BMI (Me = 24.1 kg/m2 (Q25-75 = 20.8-26.7) vs 26.9 kg/m2(Q25-75 = 23.2-29.8)) and WHR (Me = 0.89 (Q25-75 = 0.85-0.93) vs 0.94 (Q25-75 = 0.88- 0.97)) in the SCZ. Graphs included in the Supplement show a tendency for increased LBP levels in women and in participants with higher body fat tissue percentage.

In Spearman's rank correlation analysis (Fig. 4), no significant correlations in both groups were found between serum zonulin concentration and the following variables: age, hsCRP, ALT, C3 and C4 complement, insulin, and HOMA-IR. However, converse correlation tendencies between groups in terms of zonulin and CRP, zonulin and C3, zonulin and C4 was observed. In the SCZ, LBP level correlated positively and significantly (r = 0.41; p < 0.05) with CRP. Additionally, LBP correlated significantly and negatively with ALT variable (r = 0.41; p < 0.05) in the SCZ, with a similar, but not significant trend in the CON. In the histogram analysis of serum zonulin levels, two substantially increased values in the SCZ are present. Due to the heterogeneous nature of schizophrenia, the research team decided to include the observations and favor the non-parametric testing.

In the heatmap graphs of Spearman's rank correlation analysis (Fig. 5, Fig. 6), we considered variables describing clinical state characteristics, GSRS total score and scores split into discomfort from symptoms related to upper (GSRS-UGT) or lower (GSRS-LGT) gastrointestinal tract and metabolite concentration in the anterior (ACC) and posterior (PCC) cingulate cortex using MRS. The research team selected only the metabolites with acceptable reliability in Cram'er-Rao lower bounds analysis. Due to the neuroleptic pharmacotherapy in the SCZ, the possible influence of age, hepatic function, and insulin resistance on LBP and zonulin levels, we additionally performed a partial correlation analysis with random controlled variables for age, ALT, HOMA-IR and ADE. For the CON, we included in the analysis controlled variables for age, ALT and HOMA-IR. The graphs using non-partial correlation analysis are included in Supplement. In both analyses of the SCZ, a significant, negative correlation between LBP and the Age of onset of schizophrenia, with a more substantial effect in non-partial correlation (r = −0.51; p < 0.01) was observed. A significant, positive correlation of LBP and lower gastrointestinal symptoms (GSRS-LGT), with a more substantial effect in partial correlation analysis (r = 0.54; p < 0.01) was noted. The associations between LBP and GSRS in healthy controls show a similar tendency; however, the effect is significant only for symptoms from the upper GI tract (GSRS-UGT). A lack of significant associations between serum zonulin level and GSRS variables was observed. Furthermore, in the SCZ and CON, a significant negative correlation between LBP and myo-inositol concentration in the posterior cingulate cortex (Ins-PCC) was observed, with a stronger effect in partial correlation analysis in the SCZ (r = −0.49; p < 0.05).

Discussion

The study's hypotheses were aimed to evaluate the intestinal barrier integrity markers, zonulin and LBP, and their influence on gut-brain axis characteristics in schizophrenia. This is the first study to simultaneously assess serum intestinal barrier permeability markers and brain MRS findings in health, which show a negative association between serum LBP level and myo-inositol in the posterior cingulate cortex. A similar effect was observed in patients with schizophrenia, with a more substantial result with controlled parameters of age, ALT, HOMA-IR and daily neuroleptic dosage, suggesting a physiologic association between LBP and myo-inositol in the posterior cingulate cortex. Furthermore, we found contrary trends between groups of schizophrenia and healthy controls for associations between serum zonulin level and glutamine metabolite concentration in the cingulate cortex, showing a significant association between serum zonulin and glutamine concentration in the ACC of patients with schizophrenia. Additionally, we identified a significant association between serum LBP levels and reported discomfort from lower GI tract symptoms in people with schizophrenia. Finally, we described a significant difference between patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls in serum LBP concentration, with a significant association with the age of onset of schizophrenia.

In our study, serum zonulin level in patients diagnosed with schizophrenia did not significantly differ from that of healthy controls. The results complete the inconsistent image of serum zonulin levels in schizophrenia across research studies. Usta et al. observed significantly elevated serum zonulin levels compared to healthy controls. Nevertheless, in the cited study, the groups of patients with schizophrenia exhibited significantly higher BMI status than controls, and a significant positive correlation between zonulin and BMI in the schizophrenia group was observed (Usta et al., 2021). Several studies have found a positive association between serum zonulin levels and BMI. Additionally, Aasbrenn et al. noted a significant reduction in serum zonulin levels following conservative weight loss resulting from bariatric surgery (Moreno-Navarrete et al., 2012; Pepe et al., 2024; Aasbrenn et al., 2020; Zak-Gołąb et al., 2013). Widely proven comorbidity of schizophrenia and metabolic syndrome complicates the analysis of BMI's influence on zonulin levels in schizophrenia (Mitchell et al., 2013). Regrettably, the BMI variable could not be utilised in the primary calculations due to incomplete bioimpedance data from the participants we assessed for zonulin and LBP levels. Nonetheless, an analysis of data encompassing 27 SCZ participants and 21 healthy controls is involved in the Supplement. The data indicate higher levels of LBP in women and in participants with higher body fat percentage. Considering the majority of men in the SCZ (69%) and the majority of women in the CON (60.7%), the analysis suggests the possibility of a more pronounced difference between groups in LBP serum level, assuming equal sex distribution. In correlation analysis, BMI, WHR and Body fat percentage show insignificant, weak, positive correlations with LBP and zonulin, suggesting a possible, but not vital influence on LBP or zonulin results. In their study, Gokulakrishnan et al. found that there were notably higher levels of zonulin and LBP in patients with schizophrenia compared to the control group. They also observed a strong and significant positive correlation between zonulin levels and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (Gokulakrishnan et al., 2022). Upon examination of the NLR variable in our study, no substantial correlations were observed between serum zonulin or LBP levels and NLR, regardless of whether they were measured in absolute numbers or percentages. Aydin et al. found no significant difference in zonulin levels between patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls (Aydın et al., 2023). Our analysis revealed no significant associations between serum zonulin or LBP levels with the clinical state characteristics assessed by the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). The results undermine the hypothesis of state-dependent hypothesis of intestinal barrier disintegration in schizophrenia. Nonetheless, a significant correlation was identified between LBP levels and the age of onset of the disease. The negative correlation indicates higher concentrations of LBP in people with a younger age of onset of schizophrenia. The effect weakens in partial analysis controlling daily neuroleptics dosage, ALT, HOMA-IR and age, which suggests possible interplay of pharmacotherapy and its metabolic effects on the observation. This hypothesis is supported by a positive, but nonsignificant correlation trend between LBP and ADE, the number and frequency of hospitalisations and treatment duration, indicating possible influence of long-term disorder or pharmacotherapy exposure effects, degradation of diet, changes in the microbiome related to oral antipsychotics or hospitalisation-related events, e.g. acute psychotic episodes, discontinuation of pharmacotherapy by a patient at home, or rapid changes of pharmacotherapy during hospitalisation (Batko et al., 2023; Dias et al., 2024). Age of onset also correlated positively with N-acetyloaspartate in anterior cingulate cortex, with non-present similar effect between NAA-ACC and LBP. Broad literature suggests the age of onset of first psychosis as an important factor in the lifelong course of schizophrenia. Meta-analytic evidence shows associations between younger age of onset and more hospitalisations, negative symptoms, larger cognitive deficits, and poorer global outcomes (Immonen et al., 2017; Rajji et al., 2009). Considering various immunophenotypes characteristic of early-onset schizophrenia and the psychotic episode itself, the LBP might have an important role in the interplay of immune response agents in early-onset schizophrenia (Chen et al., 2024; Kapelski et al., 2015; Lang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020; Prasad et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024).

The statistical analysis revealed a negative association between serum LBP and myo-inositol concentration in the cingulate cortex. Significant correlation was observed for the posterior cingulate cortex in both groups; however, an insignificant negative correlation for ACC was also observed. Also, in non-controlled analysis, a significant association with ACC was observed in CON. The groups did not differ significantly in myo-inositol concentration in both the anterior and posterior cingulate cortex. Myo-inositol detected through MRS is generally linked to glial abundance and its activity (Haris et al., 2011). As mentioned in the introduction, schizophrenia is characterised by substantial changes in brain metabolite levels. Meta-analytical data reveal a reduction of myo-inositol in the medial prefrontal cortex of people with schizophrenia, the brain region anatomically close to the investigated ACC (Das et al., 2018). The documented reduction in myo-inositol concentration in schizophrenia might contribute to the differences between the SCZ and CON, particularly given the absence of significant correlation between LBP and ACC in the group of patients with schizophrenia. The correlation between serum LBP and myo-inositol levels in the anterior and posterior cortex of healthy controls is the first described link between an intestinal permeability parameter and a brain MRS trait in the literature. In 2023, Scheurink et al. described negative associations between serum LBP and hippocampal volume, observing indirect associations of LBP and lower cognitive functioning (Scheurink et al., 2023). The documented correlations in our study shed light on a possible mechanism between LBP and brain metabolism, enhancing the importance of the gut-brain axis in health and disease. Considering the reflection of glia activity in the myo-inositol MRS levels, the association between serum LBP and brain myo-inositol seems to be intuitive. As mentioned above, LBP serum level is closely related to the peripheral LPS exposure. Many studies have shown substantial changes in brain homeostasis following intraperitoneal lipopolysaccharide exposure, which serves as an animal model for induced neuroinflammation (Lee et al., 2008; Sheppard et al., 2019; da Silva et al., 2024; Skrzypczak-Wiercioch and Sałat, 2022; Zhao et al., 2019). The primary challenge that LPS face in infiltrating the central nervous system and eliciting neuroinflammation is the presence of the blood-brain barrier. Interestingly, many studies have demonstrated the induction of an inflammatory response in the CNS through the peripheral injection of LPS in animal models. However, a comprehensive multi-study analysis reveals significant variability in the experimental outcomes, with the species of the animal being identified as the most significant predictor of these differences. Additionally, several variables were considered as significant factors, e.g. dose of administered LPS (usually septic dose), age, health, and sex of the tested animal (Varatharaj and Galea, 2017). In a real-time experimental study involving radiolabeled LPS administered to murine subjects, it was observed that the penetration of LPS across the BBB was minimal to the extent that it could not elicit a neuroinflammatory response (Banks and Robinson, 2010). In a human study, the increase in choline in the thalamus was observed after LPS injection, suggesting glial changes (De Marco et al., 2022). The subject matter presents a greater degree of complexity, as research suggests that LPS infiltration into the CNS occurs under physiological conditions in rats, likely associated with the transport of apolipoproteins (Vargas-Caraveo et al., 2017). Recently, Kim et al. described the phenomena of microglial response training through repetitive low-dose LPS intraperitoneal injections (Kim et al., 2024). The complexity arises in the schizophrenia population, as the disorder alone and long-term pharmacotherapy may alter the BBB functionality, and potentially expose the CNS to low doses of LPS (Cotel et al., 2015; Moussiopoulou et al., 2025; Pollak et al., 2018). As mentioned in the introduction, the LBP could influence brain metabolism through cytokine interplay, affecting the brain via the transport of inflammatory agents through circumventricular organs, activation of the vasculature lining, stimulation of the vagus nerve or the enteric system, and probably many other indirect routes, as metabolism of neurotransmitters, branched chain amino acids, short chain fatty acids and bile moieties (Kearns, 2025; Cryan et al., 2019). The small differences between groups with LBP and myo-inositol associations may be attributed to the small number of participants; however, the significant difference in LBP serum levels between groups cannot be ignored. The elevated serum LBP concentration could be a result of higher exposure to LPS through the intestinal barrier; however, the lack of significant difference in serum zonulin levels does not favor the elevated intestinal permeability in the SCZ. Assuming the cytokine concentrations as one of the bidirectional ways of communication in the gut-brain axis, and well-documented abnormalities in a variety of inflammatory response agents in schizophrenia, it may be challenging to assess the direct influence of elevated LBP on the immune system and CNS.

The phenomenon of altered BBB in schizophrenia could contribute to the actual results of a significant negative correlation between serum zonulin and glutamine in ACC of patients with schizophrenia. The result was more pronounced in controlled analysis for age, ALT, HOMA-IR and daily neuroleptic dosage, suggesting possible influence of pharmacotherapy. The analysis performed on drug-naïve patients could test the hypothesis and clarify the interactions between zonulin and glutamine levels in the cingulate cortex of people with schizophrenia. The pre-haptoglobin function of zonulin could bring additional possible hypotheses to the result. Despite the concentration of serum zonulin being approximately one thousandth of mature haptoglobins, the possible influence on haptoglobin 2 maturation cannot be overlooked in schizophrenia (Tripathi et al., 2009; Veres-Székely et al., 2023). The researchers concentrated on the topic of schizophrenia and oxidative damage for decades, reaching over 3500 publications (Chen et al., 2023). Associations between schizophrenia and haptoglobin function are present. People at ultra-high risk for psychosis (UHR) present elevated expression of Hp gene (Yee et al., 2022). Furthermore, in the study of Healy et al., investigating proteomics from blood samples, of 99 investigated proteins, only haptoglobin turned out to differentiate healthy controls and those with a clinical high risk for psychosis, with associations of higher haptoglobin with severe symptoms and poorer functioning (Healy et al., 2024). Upregulation of haptoglobin was observed in first-onset, drug-naïve schizophrenia patients [217]. The zonulin or pre-haptoglobin 2 leads to transactivation of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) via proteinase-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) activation to maintain intestinal permeability regulation (Tripathi et al., 2009). In animal studies, Shavit-Stein et al. documented a vital role of PAR2 signalling in synaptic plasticity of hippocampal cells, and Abulkassim et al. found involvement of PAR2 in LPS-induced sickness behaviour (Shavit-Stein et al., 2017; Abulkassim et al., 2016). The EGFR pathway is documented to play a role in many CNS functions, e.g. regulation of neural stem cells and progenitors, differentiation and maturation of astrocytes, oligodendrocytes maturation, axonal outgrowth, and post-injury regeneration. Additionally, abnormalities in the EGFR pathway were reported in Parkinson's Disease, Alzheimer's Disease and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (Romano and Bucci, 2020). Furthermore, several studies linking EGFR to schizophrenia are documented. Futamura et al. found abnormal EGFR expression in the forebrain and serum of patients with schizophrenia (Futamura et al., 2002). Epidermal growth factor gene polymorphism was associated with earlier age of onset of human males with schizophrenia (Hänninen et al., 2007). In their comparative analysis of widely used neuroleptic medications, Pereira et al. elucidated the distinctive capacity of clozapine, a pharmacological agent noted for its remarkable efficacy in alleviating symptoms of schizophrenia, to modulate the extracellular signal-regulated 1/2 (ERK1/2) signalling pathway via phosphorylation of the EGFR. (Pereira et al., 2012). Moreover, gene expression of EGFR involved pathways in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex differentiated schizophrenia and mood disorders patients (Ketharanathan et al., 2024). In light of the previously discussed perturbations of the BBB in the context of schizophrenia, it is important to consider the role of zonulin and its influence on EGFR and PAR2 signalling within the brain. As a regulator of tight junctions, the potential zonulin impact on the BBB integrity should be examined. Multiple studies have suggested that zonulin plays a vital role in regulating TJs in the blood-brain barrier (Bocsik et al., 2016; Karyekar et al., 2003; Menon et al., 2005; Rahman et al., 2018). However, Stuart and colleagues employed various sophisticated techniques to investigate this hypothesis, suggesting no evidence supporting the direct involvement of zonulin in regulating the BBB permeability (Stuart et al., 2022). In CON, the correlation analysis showed no statistically significant relationships between serum zonulin and metabolites in the anterior and posterior cingulate cortex: glutamine, glutamate, glutathione, glycerophosphocholine, myoinositol, N-acetylaspartate, and N-acetylaspartate-glutamate. The lack of substantial correlations between serum zonulin levels and metabolite concentrations in the brains of the control group suggests a negligible or nonexistent influence of zonulin on brain homeostasis or the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) under healthy conditions. Furthermore, if zonulin does indeed exert a significant effect on BBB integrity within the SCZ, one would anticipate observable associations between zonulin and various metabolites.

It is arguable whether the use of pharmacotherapy to alleviate schizophrenia symptoms obscured any potential associations between zonulin or LBP levels and symptom severity. Usta et al. and Gokulakrishnan et al. did not observe any correlation between psychiatric symptoms and serum zonulin levels in the aforementioned studies (Usta et al., 2021; Gokulakrishnan et al., 2022). Moreover, Aydin et al. did not identify substantial variations in serum zonulin levels between periods of symptom exacerbation and treatment response in schizophrenia (Aydın et al., 2023). Cyran et al. discovered a correlation between inflammatory agents and deficit schizophrenia. However, they also observed no link between serum zonulin levels and the clinical characteristics of patients with schizophrenia (Cyran et al., 2023). Maes et al. investigated the integrity of transcellular and paracellular transport pathways by measuring IgM response to, i.a., zonulin. The authors highlighted a significantly greater ratio of IgM to zonulin + occludin/talin + actin + viculin in deficit schizophrenia versus non-deficit schizophrenia, and schizophrenia versus controls, suggesting an upregulated paracellular pathway in schizophrenia (Maes et al., 2019, 2023).

In the present study, the level of serum LBP and not zoulin correlated positively with reported gastrointestinal symptoms measured in the Gastrointestinal Symptoms Rating Scale. In the SCZ, the LBP is associated with more discomfort from lower GI tract symptoms, and in the CON, with higher gastrointestinal symptoms discomfort from upper GI tract (Kulich et al., 2008). The literature concerning LBP serum levels and discomfort from GI symptoms is limited. Kudoh et al. observed a link between serum LBP and abnormal pain threshold in the Japanese population (Kudoh et al., 2020). To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to document a link between serum LBP and discomfort from GI symptoms. The associations of GSRS parameters with other biological and psychometric parameters, involving MRS findings in schizophrenia and health, will be covered in a separate article. The co-occurrence of irritable bowel syndrome and schizophrenia remains an area of inconclusive research. Understanding the distinctions between individuals in the SCZ and those in the CON regarding the associations between LBP and specific gastrointestinal symptoms may be crucial for researchers examining gastrointestinal issues in patients with schizophrenia, particularly those undergoing antipsychotic treatment. A significant part of the data supporting the hypothesis of intense GI tract symptoms in schizophrenia is based on a cross-sectional study performed by Gupta et al. and case reports (Gupta et al., 1997). Koizumi et al. reported underdiagnosed constipation symptoms in patients with schizophrenia (Koizumi et al., 2013). Similarly, Virtanen et al. showed an increased prevalence of constipation and dyspepsia in schizophrenia patients, linking constipation to clozapine intake (Virtanen et al., 2017). As mentioned in the results, the absence of clozapine intake in the SCZ was observed in the actual study. However, Xu et al., in an elegant review, address constipation symptoms prevalent in up to 50% of people prescribed antipsychotic treatment (Xu et al., 2021). The current study concludes that the discomfort from gastrointestinal symptoms related to the lower and upper gastrointestinal tract is not associated with serum zonulin levels in health and in schizophrenia. The findings could be crucial for the unclear literature concerning the role of zonulin as a biomarker for gastrointestinal tract dysfunction symptoms. According to a Letter published in the "Gut" journal, the zonulin serum level is not indicative of irritable bowel syndrome or functional dyspepsia (Talley et al., 2020). Singh et al. found elevated levels of serum zonulin in patients with IBS, but no significant relationship between serum zonulin and the severity of irritable bowel syndrome. The authors highlighted notable positive associations between serum zonulin levels and stool frequency, as well as discontent with bowel habits in individuals with diarrhoea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (Singh et al., 2019). Conversely, Ohlsson et al., in a study involving a relatively large cohort of 393 participants, found no significant correlation between serum zonulin levels and gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms or diseases (Ohlsson et al., 2017). Using the GSRS questionnaire in the actual study that measures subjective discomfort related to GI symptoms, we imply that serum zonulin does not relate significantly to subjective discomfort from GI symptoms in schizophrenia and health.

The sex proportion imbalance between groups in our study may raise uncertainty of the results. The study reporting sex differences in zonulin levels in humans, performed by Maget et al., points to higher levels in female patients with affective disorders. The meta-analytic studies on serum zonulin in liver cirrhosis and celiac disease do not indicate sex as a significant factor in serum zonulin levels. (Maget et al., 2021; umitru et al., 2025; Rabiee et al., 2024). In the study of Asada et al., performed on 2568 participants, the levels of serum LBP between men and women differed significantly; however, the means indicated 11.0 (SD = 2.3) μg/mL in women vs. 11.4 (SD = 2.3) μg/mL in men, indicating a minimal, non-essential difference between sexes. In a study of the Polish population comprising 176 participants, Gawlik-Kotelnicka et al. reported no difference between men and women in baseline serum LBP concentration. (Asada et al., 2019; Gawlik-Kotelnicka et al., 2025). Several studies report differences in metabolite concentrations between men and women using brain MRS. For instance, Tayoshi et al. reported higher Gln levels in the ACC of human males. Reyngoudt et al. studied normal ageing brains, focusing on the PCC, reported gender differences in NAA/H2O signals, and found non-conclusive inositol differences (Tayoshi et al., 2009; Reyngoudt et al., 2012). Assuming minimal sex differences in LBP and zonulin levels based on the aforementioned studies, the main conclusions of the study on novel associations between LBP or zonulin serum concentrations and brain metabolite concentrations should not be compromised by unequal sex distributions across groups.

The rationale for selecting ACC and PCC as regions of interest in our study may seem complex, given that studies concentrated on schizophrenia have reported neuroinflammatory disturbances in other brain regions. In the study by Piltman et al., who used a very similar MRS technique and population (patients under antipsychotic pharmacotherapy), researchers reported no differences between groups in striatal metabolite levels (Plitman et al., 2018). The researchers also cited several studies that indeed report differences in striatal metabolite levels, but usually the effect is visible in the first-episode, drug-naive patients, and the effect diminishes after 4 weeks of antipsychotic treatment. Considering that the patients in our group were already receiving antipsychotic pharmacotherapy, the authors predicted that results based on the region of interest in the striatum might not differentiate between patients and healthy controls. Alternatively, the authors found MRS research studies conducted with the involvement of medicated patients with schizophrenia, which show significant differences in the anterior cingulate cortex (Wijtenburg et al., 2021; Reid et al., 2019). While there is scarce evidence of substantial differences between patients and controls in MRS studies concentrated on the posterior cingulate cortex in schizophrenia, this region was involved in the actual study experimentally, based on cited studies in the manuscript, pointing to an altered default mode network in IBS and schizophrenia patients. Additionally, the regions of interest were selected in accordance with the project's multidisciplinary design, which included numerous blood biomarkers, neuropsychological examinations, and investigations of psychological trauma.

The Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) measures subjective aspects of GI problems, including emotional aspects. Each question in the GSRS relating to GI symptoms starts with the phrase “have you been bothered …”, which implies an acknowledgement of the emotional reaction related to the symptom rated on a given scale. The subjective reports of patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls may be a valuable and original aspect of the study, contributing to a greater understanding of the complex microbiome-gut-brain axis. Considering altered emotional processing in people with schizophrenia, the subjective, emotional aspect of symptom report may influence the results between SCZ and CON. A study by Every-Palmer et al. performed on patients receiving clozapine highlights a significant discrepancy between objectively measured gastrointestinal hypomotility and self-reported constipation, with a sensitivity of only 18; sensitivity reached 50% when using the Rome criteria. Importantly, the problem does not only concern people under antipsychotic treatment, but also healthy people. (Every-Palmer et al., 2020). Grønlund et al., investigating subjective and objective constipation symptoms in a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled study on healthy people under oxycodone therapy, highlighted the great necessity of subjective GI symptoms evaluation in further research due to reported lack of significant correlations between objectively assessed hypomotility and reported symptoms (Grønlund et al., 2018). The subjective assessment of GI symptoms may provide valuable data on schizophrenia and health. Considering that approximately 40% of the global population utilising self-report measures meet the criteria for at least one functional gastrointestinal disorder, the thorough investigation of subjective GI discomfort needs to be conducted (Sperber et al., 2021). Subjective discomfort from GI symptoms aligns with the complex nature of the microbiome-gut-brain axis, where dysfunction of the GI system interfaces with central nervous system processing. Finally, the subjective discomfort associated with the reported GI symptoms directly relates to the patient's suffering. Any possibility of better understanding participants' pain or discomfort may lead to alleviating it in the future.

The study investigates basic pathophysiological differences that may be involved in the yet unknown aetiology of schizophrenia and irritable bowel syndrome. The study also points to differences in brain metabolic responses to gastrointestinal discomfort in patients and healthy adults. The study may help recognise subpopulations of schizophrenia patients with severe GI symptoms, but also better understand the mechanisms of GI symptoms in healthy people. Recognition of GI symptom discomfort mechanisms in further research may improve the quality of life in schizophrenia and irritable bowel syndrome. Understanding and reducing GI symptoms may improve pharmacotherapy choices, enhance the effectiveness of existing pharmaceuticals, improve patients’ compliance, and reduce suffering from GI symptoms.

Limitations

The study was conducted on a limited sample of participants due to the various diagnostic methods employed. Cross-sectional design and small sample size limit the strength of the evidence and the ability to draw causal inferences, emphasising the need for objective interpretation of the results. Analysing the visible histograms of the serum zonulin levels, we could point to two outliers with substantially increased zonulin levels - regarding schizophrenia as a heterogeneous disorder with neuroanatomical, symptomatic, and immunomodulatory subtypes, the greater number of patients could reflect subtypes of schizophrenia with characteristic zonulin levels (Bishop et al., 2022; Castro-de-Araujo et al., 2020; Chand et al., 2020; Ermakov et al., 2022). Many studies have indicated an elevated level of zonulin in liver cirrhosis (Voulgaris et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023; Pietrukaniec et al., 2019). The suggested role of zonulin as a potential biomarker for liver cirrhosis may impact the current study's findings, although ALT levels did not differ significantly between groups in our study, and partial correlation analysis controlled serum ALT variable. Zonulin serum concentrations or gene upregulation have been proposed as a biomarker for type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which could potentially influence the results of the present study, given the significantly higher levels of HOMA-IR index observed in patients with schizophrenia; for that reason, we also involved HOMA-IR in controlled correlation analysis (Carpes et al., 2021; Sapone et al., 2006). It is important to note that the ELISA serum zonulin test does not adhere to the gold standard for measuring intestinal permeability. Alternatively, using orally ingested probes such as the lactulose-mannitol test may offer a more accurate reflection of participants' intestinal permeability. However, it is essential to highlight that this procedure necessitates strict patient cooperation, which could be difficult to achieve in severe psychiatric disorders. Fasano et al. proposed that ELISA tests commonly used in commercial settings may detect one or more undiscovered zonulin family members. Although the author considered the potential for detecting partially complement C3 proteins using the aforementioned test, the absence of associations between serum zonulin levels and serum complement proteins in our study does not substantiate this hypothesis (Fasano, 2020). Unfortunately, the research team did not cover the smoking status of the participants, which could alter the LBP results (Gonzalez-Quintela et al., 2013). The BMI, WHR and body fat percentage variables were covered by the majority of participants, showing a possible, but not key, impact on the results. The groups exhibited differences in sex composition; however, the authors included in the Supplement the Aligned Rank Transform Anova analysis, suggesting no significant interaction of sex on LBP and Zonulin intergroup results. We also included scatterplots showing higher levels of LBP in female patients overall, who were a minority in the SCZ. The existing literature predominantly indicates a lack of significant associations between serum zonulin or LBP levels and sex. The heterogeneity of SCZ in terms of experienced symptoms, treatment history, and medication usage creates additional interconnected relationships that may influence the investigated characteristics of the gut-brain axis. The drug-naive, first-episode psychosis sample may demonstrate novel observations independent of the effects of pharmacotherapy or the disorder exposure. However, research data indicate that factors characteristic of the circumstances of acute psychosis admission, such as severe psychological stress or unclear substance intake history, could significantly influence the results (Linninge et al., 2018; La Torre et al., 2023). The effect of antipsychotic pharmacotherapy could indirectly influence participants' intestinal permeability by altering intestinal microbiota. However, Dias et al., in a systematic review, analysing studies on the influence of risperidone, quetiapine, amisulpride, and olanzapine on intestinal microbiota, reported gross heterogeneity in study designs and inconclusive results (Dias et al., 2024).

Conclusions

Patients with schizophrenia and healthy participants differed significantly in the serum LBP, but not zonulin level. Serum LBP levels significantly correlated with age of onset in patients with schizophrenia. No significant associations between PANSS or GAF characteristics with LBP or zonulin levels were observed. In both groups, a significant relationship between serum LBP levels and concentrations of myo-inositol in the posterior cingulate cortex was observed. In the SCZ, after controlling for the variables of age, ALT, HOMA-IR and normalised daily neuroleptic dosage, the association between serum zonulin and glutamine concentration in the anterior cingulate cortex was observed. In the SCZ, LBP correlated positively with the discomfort from lower GI tract symptoms, and in the CON with upper GI tract symptoms. Serum zonulin was not associated with gastrointestinal symptoms in health and in schizophrenia.

Consent to participate

The article explicitly does not include any sensitive personal information. All data collected during the research process involving human participants has been promptly anonymized and coded. The information obtained from participants has been analysed for the purpose of conducting statistical group analyses.

All participants included in this study provided written consent for their participation in duplicate. Each participant was informed, both verbally and in writing, of their right to withdraw from the study at any point, without the necessity of providing a reason.
