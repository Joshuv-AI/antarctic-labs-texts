Multidimensional mechanism of pulsed electric field synergized L-arginine upon goose meat tenderization: Based on molecular dynamics simulation and protein interaction network
Authors: Rong Ma, Chen Chen, Zihang Shi, Yangyang Hu, Yangying Sun, Qiang Xia, Changyu Zhou, Daodong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough pulsed electric field (PEF) and L-arginine alone can improve meat tenderness, their synergistic molecular mechanism remains unclear. This study first explored the tenderization mechanism of combined PEF and L-arginine (LP) in goose meat. Results showed that LP reduced goose meat shear force by 51.30%, with effects significantly superior to either treatment alone. Hematoxylin-eosin staining revealed that LP enlarged intermuscular spaces. Infrared spectroscopy indicated that LP induced a conformational shift from α-helix to β-sheet in myofibrillar proteins (MPs). Fluorescence spectroscopy showed a significantly increased fluorescence intensity, confirming structural unfolding of MPs. Enzyme activity assays showed that LP elevated calpain activity by 64.33%. SDS-PAGE further confirmed the accelerated degradation of troponin-T. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that PEF primarily drives the restructuring of the enzyme-substrate binding interface, while L-arginine stabilizes the process by maintaining the hydrogen bond network. This study provides a theoretical basis for developing efficient and controllable meat tenderization technologies.Keywords: Pulsed electric field, L-arginine, Tenderness, Protein degradation, Endogenous enzyme, Molecular docking

Introduction

Goose meat is characterized by a hard texture, strong viscosity, and high colloidality, which result in rough and dry meat with poor tenderness and chewiness, severely limiting its market acceptability. Compared with common poultry meats such as chicken and duck, goose meat myofibers exhibit a densely arranged structure aligned in the same direction. The myofiber diameter of 70–90-day-old goose meat ranges from 19.88 to 26.27 μm (Cheng et al., 2025), while that of chicken meat of the same age (90–101 days old) is only 6.6 to 12 μm. This thicker myofiber structure directly increases the mechanical strength and chewing resistance of goose meat. In addition, the proportion of collagen in the total protein of goose meat muscle tissue is as high as 12%–15%, which is significantly higher than that of broiler chicken and duck (Zhou et al., 2018), further increasing the chewing difficulty of goose meat. Therefore, it is necessary to develop efficient and green tenderization technologies suitable for goose meat to improve its tenderness and palatability.

Traditional meat tenderization technologies are mainly classified into three categories: chemical, physical, and biological methods. Biological tenderization methods (such as papain treatment and microbial fermentation) cannot be widely used due to their uncontrollable effects, which are prone to causing excessive tenderization of meat products. Chemical tenderizers include phosphates, amino acids, etc. They form a stable hydration layer by complexing water molecules, thereby improving the water-holding capacity (WHC) of meat products and ultimately achieving a tenderization effect (Gao et al., 2023). Among them, L-arginine (L-Arg), as a natural amino acid, has a basic amino group that can react with acidic substances (such as lactic acid) in post-slaughter meat to neutralize acidity, alleviate muscle rigor mortis and tenderness decline caused by acidification, and prevent early-stage tenderness deterioration (Fan, Gao and Zhou, 2024). In addition, L-Arg can specifically bind to muscle proteins, induce transformations in protein spatial conformation, and thus promote the loosening of myofiber structure. However, the tenderization effect of L-Arg is highly dependent on its degree of penetration and uniform distribution in muscle tissue (Chen et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that the penetration of L-Arg in meat products is not only process-wise complicated but also time-consuming. For example, it is necessary to inject a total of 10 mL of 0.4% (w/v) L-arginine solution into chicken meat in five divided doses, followed by 30 min of tumbling and 12 h of standing, so that the penetration degree of L-Arg can reach 93% - 97% (Zhang et al., 2020b). Therefore, it is urgent to synergistically combine L-Arg with other suitable technologies to improve its penetration efficiency and tenderization effect.

Physical tenderization technologies (such as ultrasound, ultra-high pressure, and pulsed electric field, etc.) can destroy the dense muscle structure through cavitation effects, mechanical disturbance, and electrical stimulation, thereby promoting the penetration and diffusion of exogenous components (such as NaCl) in meat (Khalid et al., 2023). Among them, pulsed electric field (PEF), as a new type of green non-thermal processing technology, has attracted much attention in the field of meat tenderness improvement and flavor enhancement due to its unique electroporation and dielectric effects. By applying instantaneous high-voltage short pulses (at the microsecond to millisecond levels), PEF forms nanoscale pores on subcellular and myofibrillar membranes (Li, Yang, Du and Wu, 2023), which can increase the looseness of muscle tissue and induce the fracture of muscle microstructure without causing macroscopic mechanical damage. Furthermore, the dielectric effect of PEF can regulate the looseness of protein and lipid networks in food matrices, providing convenience for the penetration and diffusion of exogenous components. Previous studies have reported that PEF can directionally increase the diffusion coefficient of salts and water, leading to a maximum salt absorption rate of 77% (Cropotova et al., 2021). Therefore, it is speculated that the combined use of PEF and L-Arg may exert a synergistic effect and significantly improve muscle tenderness.

This study used zhedong white geese (65 days old, 3000 ± 50 g) as the research subjects, attempted to use PEF and L-Arg alone or in combination, and evaluated the effects of different treatment methods on goose meat tenderness. Specifically, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was used to analyze the effect of different treatments on water migration in goose meat; myofibrillar fragmentation index (MFI), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining were used to characterize the degree of muscle tissue damage; sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), protein secondary structure analysis, and endogenous fluorescence spectroscopy were used to evaluate the degradation and conformational changes of myofibrillar proteins (MPs). The activities of calpain, cathepsin, and Ca2+-ATPase in goose meat under different treatment conditions were also detected, and the binding mode between calpain and MPs was analyzed through molecular docking (MD) and molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) experiments to deeply clarify the internal molecular mechanism of goose meat tenderness improvement. This study provides theoretical support for the development of new technologies for meat tenderness improvement.

Materials and methods

Materials

Zhedong white goose meat was purchased from the Ningbo Xiangshan Great White Goose Breeding Base, where the geese were raised in flatbed housing. Food-grade L-arginine was purchased from Hebei Huayang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hebei, China). Analytical-grade chemical reagents were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Z-Arg-Arg-AMC and Z-Phe-Arg-AMC were acquired from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively. The BCA Protein Assay Kit was obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China).

Sample preparation

65-day-old zhedong white geese (3000 ± 50 g) were slaughtered by neck cutting and exsanguination at a local processor in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Welfare Committee of Ningbo University. Within 12 h post-slaughter, the goose meat was transported to the laboratory under 4 °C cold chain conditions. Subsequently, the connective tissue, fat, and fascia on its surface were promptly removed, and the meat was trimmed into 12 cm × 6 cm × 2 cm samples for subsequent experiments. In this experiment, the DTPEF-1501 pulsed electric field sterilization device manufactured by Dalian Dingtong Technology Development Co., Ltd. was used for PEF treatment, and the parameter settings were determined according to preliminary tests. Meat samples were placed into the treatment chamber, treated with PEF at 1.2 kV/cm and 160 Hz for 15 s, and then left to stand; this group was designated as the PEF group. Which was designated as the PEF group. According to the ratio of 10 g of 0.6 wt% L-Arg solution per 100 g goose meat sample, the solution was uniformly sprayed onto the surface of meat samples, and the meat samples were repeatedly flipped until the solution was fully absorbed, and then left to stand; this group was designated as the LA group. For the combined treatment group (LP group), samples were first subjected to PEF treatment, followed by spraying with L-Arg solution and subsequent standing. Meat samples without any treatment were left to stand at 4 °C for 10 h as the control group, which was designated as the C group.

Determination of tenderness-related indices

Shear force measurement

The method was performed according to Fan, Gao, Li, Pan and Zhou (2024) with slight modifications. The goose meat was placed in a 90 °C water bath and heated until its core temperature reached 75 °C. It was immediately removed and cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the goose meat was cut into strips measuring 3 cm × 2 cm × 1 cm. The meat strips were cut perpendicular to the muscle fiber direction at pre-testing, mid-testing, and post-testing velocities of 2, 1, and 1 mm/s, respectively, using a TA.New Plus texture analyzer (ISENSO, USA) equipped with a blade probe. The values of the highest shear force were noted and averaged across six measurements.

MFI determination

The MFI measurement was performed according to the method of Li et al. (2025) with slight modifications. A 2 g meat was placed in 20 mL of pre-cooled buffer (4 °C) containing 0.1 mol/L KCl, 11.2 mmol/L K₂HPO₄, 8.8 mmol/L KH₂PO₄, 1 mmol/L EGTA, and 1 mmol/L MgCl₂, and homogenized three times at 10000 rpm for 10 s each. The homogenate was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C using a 5804R refrigerated centrifuge (Eppendorf, Germany). The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was resuspended in 20 mL of the same buffer and centrifuged again. The resulting precipitate was mixed with 10 mL of buffer, vortexed, and then passed through a 200-mesh sieve. The filtrate was centrifuged once more, and the final precipitate was resuspended in 5 mL of buffer. The concentration of the protein solution was adjusted to 0.5 mg/mL, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 542 nm. The MFI value was calculated as the measured absorbance multiplied by 200.

pH determination

The method was performed according to Fan, Gao, Li, Pan and Zhou (2024) with slight modifications. A 5 g meat was minced, mixed with 10 volumes of distilled water, and homogenized three times (10,000 rpm, 10 s each) using a XHF-D homogenizer (Ningbo Xinzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). The homogenate was filtered through filter paper to obtain the supernatant, and the pH was then measured six times using an FE20 pH meter (Mettler Toledo, China). The mean value was calculated.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

An NMI20-060H-1 LF-NMR analyzer (Niumag, China) was used to acquire proton density images and relaxation time data. The meat samples were cut into pieces of 3 cm × 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm (approximately 15 ± 1 g) and placed into 25 mm nuclear magnetic resonance tubes for measurement. The T2 relaxation time was analyzed using the Q-CPMG sequence, and images were acquired using the MRI imaging system. The measurement parameters were as follows: echo duration = 0.2 ms, number of callbacks = 8000, TR = 3200 ms, and TE = 27.36 ms.

Observation of the microstructure of goose meat

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The meat microstructure was examined according to the method of Cheng et al. (2025) with slight modifications. The muscles were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 72 h, then rinsed with 0.1 mol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by gradient dehydration with 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol sequentially, and finally freeze-dried for 48 h for subsequent use. The freeze-dried samples were sputter-coated with gold, and a SU8600 SEM (Hitachi, Japan) was employed at 3 kV and 150× magnification to examine the microstructure.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

The measurement of H&E staining was modified from the method of Fan, Gao, Li, Pan and Zhou (2024). The samples (5 mm × 5 mm) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 to 48 h, followed by gradient dehydration with ethanol. After anhydrous ethanol replacement, xylene clearing, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and dewaxing, the sections were subjected to gradient hydration in 100%, 95%, 85%, and 70% ethanol for 30 min per concentration, rinsed with distilled water for 5 min, stained with H&E, and observed under a DM2000 light microscope (Leica, Germany).

Structural change analysis of MPs

Extraction of MPs

MPs were extracted according to the method reported by Cheng et al. (2025) with minor modifications. A 5 g meat was mixed with a 4-fold volume of buffer solution (0.1 mol/L Tris, 10 mmol/L EDTA, pH 8.3), homogenized three times at 8000 rpm for 15 s each in an ice bath, and then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The precipitate was washed three times with a 4-fold volume of standard salt solution (0.1 mol/L KCl, 0.02 mol/L K₂HPO₄, 2 mmol/L MgCl₂, 1 mmol/L EGTA, pH 7.0). After washing with 0.1 mol/L KCl and filtering through cheesecloth, the precipitate was finally suspended in 0.1 mol/L NaCl. The obtained myofibrillar proteins (MPs) were stored at 4 °C and used within 48 h.

Secondary structure of MPs

Changes in the secondary structure were analyzed according to the method of Xie et al. (2024) with slight modifications. Freeze-dried MPs (1 mg) was thoroughly ground with 100 mg of KBr, then pressed into a 1-mm-thick pellet. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were conducted using an FT/IR-4000 (Jasco, Japan), with a pure KBr thin sheet as the blank control to subtract background interference. The scanning range was 4000–500 cm−1, with 64 scans and a resolution of 4 cm−1. The obtained spectra were used to analyze the secondary structure of MPs via data preprocessing and peak fitting.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

The method was performed according to Fan et al. (2023) with slight modifications. The MPs solution was adjusted to 0.5 mg/mL. The intrinsic fluorescence spectrum of the MPs solution in the wavelength range of 290–420 nm was determined using a F-4700 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan). The excitation wavelength, scanning speed, and voltage were 280 nm, 1200 nm/min, and 420 V, respectively, and the corresponding buffer served as a blank control for background subtraction.

Determination of intermolecular forces

Intermolecular forces were determined according to the method of Wang et al. (2025). Five solutions—S1 (0.05 M NaCl), S2 (0.6 M NaCl), S3 (0.6 M NaCl +1.5 M urea), S4 (0.6 M NaCl +8 M urea), and S5 (0.6 M NaCl +8 M urea +0.5 M β-mercaptoethanol)—were separately added to 1 mL of protein solution (5 mg/mL). The mixtures were vortexed and left to stand at 4 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined. The contents of ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bonds were calculated based on the differences in protein solubility among the five solutions.

Characterization of the functional properties of MPs

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

H0 was determined according to the method of Li et al. (2024) with slight modifications. One milliliter of MPs solution (0.5 mg/mL) was mixed thoroughly with 40 μL of bromophenol blue solution, allowed to stand for 10 min, and then centrifuged (6000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was diluted 10-fold, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm (designated as A). Solubility

Solubility was determined according to the method of Dong et al. (2020) with slight modifications. The protein solution was adjusted to a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL, allowed to stand at 4 °C for 1 h, and then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min. The protein concentration in the supernatant was measured. Solubility was calculated as the ratio of the protein concentration in the supernatant to that before centrifugation.

Characterization of enzymatic hydrolysis properties and SDS-PAGE

Determination of calpain, Ca2+-ATPase, cathepsin B, and cathepsin L activities

A 2 g meat was homogenized in 25 mL of 50 mmol/L sodium citrate buffer (1 mmol/L EDTA, 0.2% Triton X-100, pH 5.0) at 15000 rpm for 3 × 10 s, and then centrifuged. The activities of calpain, Ca2+-ATPase, cathepsin B, and cathepsin L were determined according to the methods of Zhou et al. (2019) and Zhang et al. (2020c). Suc-LLVY-AMC was used as the substrate for calpain, Z-Arg-Arg-AMC for cathepsin B, and Z-Phe-Arg-AMC for cathepsins B + L. Cathepsin L activity was calculated as the difference between the activity of cathepsins B + L and that of cathepsin B.

SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE analysis was performed according to the method of Li et al. (2025). The MPs solution (5 mg/mL) was mixed with β-mercaptoethanol-containing sample loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v) and heated in a metal bath at 95 °C for 10 min. A 12% separating gel and a 5% concentrated gel were prepared. An aliquot (10 μL) of the sample mixture was loaded into each well. Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for 30 min, after which the voltage was increased to 120 V and maintained for 1.5 h. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained, destained, and imaged.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

Following the approach reported by Shi et al. (2022b), the molecular interaction between goose myofibrillar protein (MP) and calpain was investigated. The amino acid sequences of goose actin (ID: P63256), myosin (ID: A0A8B9CLF2), and calpain (ID: A0A8B9BTY2) were retrieved from the UniProt database. Their three-dimensional structures were predicted via AlphaFold3, with structural preprocessing involving the removal of crystal water, addition of hydrogen atoms, and optimization of hydrogen conformations. Rigid docking of actin and myosin was performed using HDOCK to obtain the MP complex (group C). The docked conformations were comprehensively scored by the iterative scoring function ITScorePP and a confidence score, where negative ITScorePP values indicate binding affinity, with larger absolute values representing stronger binding capacity. The conformation with the top scores for both indices was selected for subsequent analyses. The three-dimensional structure of L-Arg was obtained from the PubChem database, and MD between L-Arg and MP was conducted to generate the MP-L-Arg complex (group LA). The TIP3P water box was constructed using Gromacs 2022, and MP was embedded in the water box. Sodium chloride was added to neutralize the system charge. The AMBER14SB.ff force field was selected, and a Gaussian PEF of 1.2 V/mm was applied along the z-axis (this electric field is a transient Gaussian-shaped pulse in the time dimension, used to simulate the transient electric field effect of PEF). Energy minimization of the system was performed via 2500 steps of the steepest descent method and 2500 steps of the conjugate gradient method. At a maintained system temperature of 298.15 K, a 100 ps NVT ensemble simulation and a 100 ps NPT equilibrium simulation was conducted in sequence. Finally, the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was employed to calculate long-range electrostatic interactions, and a 100 ns MDS was carried out under periodic boundary conditions to obtain PEF-MP (group PEF). The MDS results were analyzed by means of root mean square deviation (RMSD), number of hydrogen bonds, radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA). Further MD between PEF-MP and L-Arg was carried out to generate the PEF-MP-L-Arg complex (group LP). With calpain as the representative endogenous protease, MD was performed between each group's MP complex and calpain to analyze binding mode differences among groups. The PyMOL 2.5.8 software was used for the visual analysis of all results.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed with at least three independent replicates. All results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Duncan's multiple range test, and Pearson correlation analysis were performed using SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with a value of p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Tenderness indicators and pH responses

Shear force and MFI were the core indicators for evaluating meat tenderness (Li et al., 2022). Generally, meat tenderness was significantly negatively correlated with shear force but significantly positively correlated with MFI. Data in Table 1 showed that compared with the C group, the shear force of the LA, PEF, and LP groups decreased by 22.62%, 26.06%, and 51.30%, respectively; meanwhile, the MFI of the three groups increased by 17.40%, 35.84%, and 57.26% compared with the C group, with the LP group showing a significantly higher increase than the other two groups. These results indicated that the combined treatment of PEF and L-arginine exerted a significant synergistic tenderizing effect on goose meat. This might be because PEF treatment could disrupt the cell membrane structure of muscle tissue, forming microchannels, thereby promoting more efficient penetration of L-Arg into the muscle. The interaction between the two could enhance the destruction of myofibrillar tissue, further reduce the mechanical strength of muscle tissue, and ultimately achieve the synergistic improvement of meat tenderness. In addition, this study found significant differences in the pH value of meat samples among different groups. The pH values of the LA and LP groups were 6.33 ± 0.03 and 6.73 ± 0.04, respectively, which were significantly higher than that of the C group, while the PEF group had no significant difference in pH value. As L-Arg is an alkaline amino acid, its entry into muscle tissue could neutralize some acidic substances, so the increased pH value of the LA group was expected. Importantly, the pH value of the LP group was significantly higher than that of the LA group, indicating that PEF pretreatment could effectively improve the diffusion of L-Arg in the muscle matrix, enabling it to distribute more fully and evenly inside the muscle tissue (Cropotova et al., 2021). This further enhanced the neutralization of lactic acid and other acidic substances in the muscle and increased the relative content of alkaline components in the muscle tissue. Additionally, the combined treatment of PEF and L-Arg might trigger additional biochemical reactions, promoting the production of some alkaline metabolites in muscle tissue, thereby further increasing the pH value.

Water-State monitoring

Water retention

Superior WHC mitigates processing-induced moisture loss, thereby enhancing juiciness and textural tenderness. As shown in Fig. 1A, the LA group exhibited a major edge in moisture management. Compared with the C group, the LA group significantly decreased DL and CL and significantly increased WHC. Mechanistically, L-Arg shifted protein charge states away from their isoelectric point, enhancing electrostatic repulsion between proteins. This repulsive force created abundant binding sites for water molecules, thereby improving WHC. In contrast, PEF treatment increased the CL of samples by 7.79%, while the DL was significantly lower than that of the C group. This indicated that goose meat treated with PEF tended to accelerate moisture loss under high-temperature conditions, a phenomenon that might be caused by the increased cell membrane permeability induced by the electroporation effect of PEF, thereby promoting moisture exudation (Beno et al., 2023). However, under unheated conditions, PEF treatment did not exert a significant negative impact on the WHC of meat products. Notably, the DL and CL of the LP group decreased by 37.55% and 17.40%, respectively, while its WHC increased by 10.42%. This demonstrated that L-Arg played a dominant role in counteracting PEF-induced moisture destabilization during co-processing, alleviating moisture loss. This could be because L-Arg penetrates more effectively and adsorbs onto the surface of muscle fibers through the microchannels formed in the cell membrane by PEF treatment, thereby blocking some moisture loss pathways.

State of existence of moisture

LF-NMR enabled rapid and non-destructive analysis of water distribution in meat products, providing technical support for evaluating meat WHC, tenderness, and freshness. Previous studies confirmed that transverse relaxation time (T2) and total peak area (A) are closely correlated with meat shear force (Yang et al., 2021). Table 1 showed three characteristic relaxation peaks (T2b, T21, T22), corresponding to bound water, immobile water, and free water, respectively. Combined with Fig. 1B, different treatments significantly altered the existing state and migration rate of water in meat. The LA group was dominated by immobile water with a significantly lower free water proportion than the other groups, indicating that L-Arg could effectively bind bound water and immobile water to reduce their conversion to free water (Fan, Gao, Li, Pan and Zhou, 2024). This allowed muscle tissue to retain more water, filling gaps in the multilevel structure of MPs and inhibiting their aggregation. Moreover, the LA group had a significantly shorter T2b than the C group, demonstrating that L-Arg could enhance the binding of bound water to muscle protein molecules and improve meat WHC. This pattern was also observed in the T21 results, further confirming the excellent water-retaining property of L-Arg. Furthermore, T22 relaxation time was positively correlated with free water loss risk, and the LA group had the lowest T22, indicating effective inhibition of free water loss. In contrast, the PEF group showed significantly decreased bound water and increased free water proportion, with a markedly longer T2b than the control. This indicated that the electroporation effect of PEF damaged the binding sites between proteins and bound water, leading to a reduced binding capacity of bound water, promoting the conversion of bound water to immobile water and free water, and increasing the risk of water loss. Additionally, the PEF group had the highest T22, confirming that PEF treatment exacerbated myofibrillar structural damage and further increased free water loss risk. The LP group exhibited unique water characteristics. Although its bound water proportion decreased and free water proportion increased, its T2b was close to that of the C group, suggesting that L-Arg could compensate for the damage caused by PEF to the protein-water binding system and maintain the normal binding capacity of bound water in meat. Furthermore, the LP group had a significantly shorter T22 than the control, indicating that it could effectively inhibit free water loss. Meanwhile, its total peak area was significantly higher than those of the C and PEF groups, verifying its strong WHC. Therefore, despite the relatively high free water proportion, the LP group reduced water loss and ensured meat juiciness via excellent WHC. Finally, the imaging results in Fig. 1C further verified the above conclusions. The LA and LP groups had higher and more uniformly distributed water in meat, while the strong blue signals in the C and PEF groups reflected a relatively lower water content.

Microstructure insights

SEM and histological observations revealed that all treatment regimens induced distinct alterations in the muscle microstructure. The myofibrils in the C group exhibited a compact, well-aligned arrangement with clear inter-fibrillar boundaries and intact myofiber bundles (Fig. 2), which were responsible for the firm textural characteristics of the muscle. Following interventions with L-Arg and PEF, the myofibrils underwent obvious distortion and disorganization, accompanied by a reduction in fiber diameter, distinct dissociation from the endomysium, and loss of boundary definition. Specifically, the LA group presented porous surfaces and rough striations on the myofibers. In contrast, the PEF group showed irregular myofiber bundle disruption, widened fascial interstices, and heterogeneous myofiber bundle sizes. Notably, the LP group displayed a hybrid microstructural phenotype integrating the characteristics of both single treatments, with the most pronounced myofibrillar spacing and the highest degree of structural disorganization. Consistent with the SEM results, H&E staining also revealed that the myofiber bundles in the LP group were loosely and disorderly arranged. Quantitative analysis showed that the myofiber spacing in the LP group was 39.1 μm, which was significantly larger than that in the C group (10.8 μm), LA group (19.9 μm), and the PEF group (28.5 μm). These results further confirmed that the combined treatment could significantly increase myofiber spacing and improve the looseness of muscle tissue structure. Furthermore, the sections of the LA and LP groups were lighter in color than those of the C and PEF groups. This may be explained by the infiltration of L-Arg and its solvent into the muscle tissue during spraying, which dilutes the meat pigments responsible for coloration. Collectively, the reduced muscle hardness in the LP group is attributed to the synergistic effects of two complementary mechanisms: PEF-induced disruption of the protein structural barrier and dissociation of the overlapping thick and thin myofilament lattice (Zhang et al., 2022), and L-Arg-mediated acceleration of biodegradation kinetics and proteolytic matrix breakdown, which in turn alleviates post-mortem muscle rigor mortis (Fan, Gao and Zhou, 2024).

Conformational changes of MPs

Secondary structure

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy and intermolecular forces

The tertiary structure of MPs was analyzed by intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy and intermolecular forces (Ekezie et al., 2018). The fluorescence spectra of MPs under different treatments were shown in Fig. 3C. At 334 nm, the fluorescence intensity of the C group was the lowest, while that of LA, PEF, and LP was significantly higher sequentially relative to the C group. This fluorescence variation indicated that PEF and L-Arg intervention induced MPs unfolding, an effect that exposed the tryptophan side chains buried in the protein interior and thus elevated fluorescence intensity (Han et al., 2024). Table 2 showed the differences in intermolecular forces of MPs across all groups. The C group had the strongest ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions. The LA group had significantly higher hydrogen bonding strength than that of both C and PEF, while PEF exhibited stronger hydrophobic interactions than LA but weaker than C. LP markedly weakened ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions and induced the formation of the most robust hydrogen bonding network. Collectively, LA and PEF exhibited distinct intermolecular force reconfiguration patterns compared with the C group. LA favored hydrogen bond formation, while PEF tended to maintain hydrophobic interactions, implying differential regulatory mechanisms of single treatments on MPs. Notably, LP showed the most pronounced intermolecular force alterations and the highest fluorescence signal among all groups, a finding that indicated extensive residue exposure and severe tertiary structural perturbation of MPs. This LP-mediated conformational change drove myofibrillar matrix disaggregation via intermolecular force reprogramming, and the unfolding of MPs domains synergistically remodeled hydrogen bond topology. These effects collectively optimized the hydration microenvironment, ultimately reducing shear force and improving water retention capacity.

Physicochemical Property Changes of MPs

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

The H0 value quantifies the surface water repellency of proteins and directly reflects their hydrophobic properties on the molecular surface. The C group had the highest H0 value, while the H0 values of the PEF, LA, and LP groups decreased successively (Fig. 3D). The changing trend showed that the exposure of surface hydrophobic residues in each treatment group was significantly decreased, implying that the WHC of MPs could be further improved, a finding consistent with the previous results related to WHC. From a mechanistic perspective, L-Arg treatment initially induced the conformational unfolding of MPs, then selectively bound to the hydrophobic regions via its own guanidyl group, which triggered the structural rearrangement of MPs, regulated the exposure level of hydrophobic groups by masking hydrophobic sites, and simultaneously promoted the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, thereby enhancing the hydrophilicity of protein molecules (Gao et al., 2019). PEF treatment could also induce conformational rearrangement of MPs, and mainly regulate hydrophobic groups through a steric hindrance effect (Wang et al., 2022). Notably, the sequestering effect of the LP group on hydrophobic groups was significantly stronger to that of the single treatment groups. As described in Section 3.4.2, the LP group could significantly weaken the intermolecular hydrophobic interactions and the degree of disulfide bond cross-linking of MPs. This effect inhibited the formation of MPs aggregates, prevented the decrease in WHC caused by aggregation, and further confirmed the regulatory advantage of the combined treatment on the surface hydrophobicity of MPs and its promoting effect on WHC.

Solubility

Protein solubility was used to evaluate the aqueous dispersion capacity, which was directly correlated with the hydrophilicity of the molecular surface. Previous studies have shown that the conformational unfolding of MPs would significantly reduce their water solubility if it only led to the exposure of internal hydrophobic groups (Grossmann and McClements, 2023). As shown in Fig. 3D, compared with the C group, the solubility of the PEF group only increased slightly, while that of the LA and LP groups increased significantly. L-Arg could form salt bridges with MPs to mask hydrophobic sites, thereby reducing hydrophobic aggregation and improving solubility (Zhu et al., 2024). Meanwhile, L-Arg could enhance the electrostatic repulsion on the surface of protein molecules by creating an alkaline microenvironment, promote the dissociation and release of myosin and actin from MPs, and further improve its aqueous dispersion (Fan, Gao and Zhou, 2024). The moderate improvement of MPs solubility by PEF was attributed to PEF-induced alteration of the interaction mode between protein peptide chains, which enhanced the binding affinity of MPs to water molecules (Hu et al., 2024). In addition, the low electric potential generated by PEF inhibited the non-specific aggregation of protein molecules, and the combination of these two effects resulted in a slight increase in solubility. The LP group exhibited the most significant enhancement effect in MPs solubility, which could be attributed to the synergistic effect of the alkaline environment and appropriate PEF intensity. Specifically, PEF treatment induced the conformational unfolding of MPs and effectively reduced protein aggregation. Moreover, the electrostatic repulsion effect exerted by L-Arg synergized with the effect of PEF, further optimizing the solubility characteristics of MPs (Wang et al., 2023), and this result was highly consistent with the conclusions of the water-related characteristics analysis discussed earlier.

Endogenous enzyme activity and SDS-PAGE Analysis

Calpains and cathepsins are the key enzymes regulating the degradation of muscle myofibrils. With different substrate specificities, they synergistically participate in the disintegration process of MPs. Among them, calpains mainly target and degrade structural connecting proteins and regulatory proteins in myofibrils, such as titin and troponin-T, and preferentially break the protein binding sites in key connecting regions of myofibrils, thereby destroying the overall structural integrity of myofibrils. Cathepsins, on the other hand, mainly degrade the core functional proteins of myofilaments. In addition to further decomposing titin fragments and troponin-T degradation products, they can also act on core myofilament components such as myosin heavy chains (MHC) and actin, accelerating the fragmentation of myofibrillar structures. The two perform their respective functions and act synergistically to jointly promote the gradual degradation of muscle myofibrils (Grossmann and McClements, 2023). As shown in Fig. 4A, the bar charts reflected the initial activity (approximately 24 h post-slaughter) and residual activity (72 h post-slaughter) of calpain, while the line graph quantified the total activity loss within 24–72 h. All treatment groups accelerated calpain activation, with the LP group exhibiting the highest initial activity. In addition, the total calpain activity loss in all treatment groups was significantly higher than that in the C group, indicating that PEF and L-Arg intervention enhanced the proteolytic kinetic efficiency of calpain. The excellent performance of the LP group in activating calpain and maximizing proteolytic efficacy provided a key impetus for myofibril cleavage in the early postmortem rigor stage, significantly advancing the initiation of the tenderization process (Bhat et al., 2018). Crucially, calpain activation requires intracellular Ca2+ concentration to exceed a threshold, and this concentration is inherently associated with the activity dynamics of Ca2+-ATPase. Although Ca2+-ATPase dose not directly participate in proteolysis, it regulates MPs degradation rate by modulating intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis (Jan et al., 2024). Fig. 4B showed that the Ca2+-ATPase activity loss in the LA and LP groups was significantly higher than that in the C and PEF groups, suggesting premature exhaustion of Ca2+-ATPase function in these two groups during early postmortem aging. This was conducive to the premature release and continuous accumulation of Ca2+ in the cytoplasm, thereby inducing early activation of calpain. In contrast, the PEF group showed no significant Ca2+-ATPase activity loss, but its electroporation effect directly promoted Ca2+ release, also achieving rapid calpain activation. Calpains preferentially act on the binding sites of regulatory proteins, and the specific degradation products generated are precisely captured by SDS-PAGE. As shown in Fig. 5A, the clear bands appeared in the LA, PEF and LP groups in the molecular weight range of 36–39 kDa (corresponding to troponin-T degradation fragments), while almost no band was observed in the C group at this position, demonstrating that all treatment groups significantly promoted troponin-T degradation. In addition, in the molecular weight range corresponding to titin (27 kDa), the band staining intensity of the PEF and LP groups was significantly stronger than that of the C and LA groups, indicating a more intense titin degradation in these two groups. These results were consistent with the calpain activity results and mutually corroborated each other.

As the functional successor to calpains, cathepsins further softened connective tissues, exacerbated myofibril fragmentation, and enhanced tenderization (Madhusankha and Thilakarathna, 2021). Consistent with calpain activation patterns, all treatment groups accelerated cathepsin activation and increased its activity loss, indicating that PEF and L-Arg intervention improved proteolytic efficiency throughout postmortem aging. Specifically, the total activity loss of cathepsin B and cathepsin L in the LP group increased by 73.33% and 68.98% compared with the C group, respectively (Fig. 4C, D), demonstrating that the synergistic effect achieved deep activation of endogenous proteases. Corresponding to these results, more obvious bands of myosin light chain (MLC) were observed in the LP group in Fig. 5A. This effect might result from PEF-induced perforation of lysosomal membranes, which facilitates the rapid release of cathepsins into the cytoplasm. Although L-Arg alone was less effective than PEF in activating cathepsins, the weakly alkaline microenvironment it created promoted the conformational unfolding of enzymes, enhanced their substrate-binding capacity, and thus enabled extremely strong enzymatic hydrolysis. In conclusion, PEF and L-Arg synergistically regulated the activities of calpains and cathepsins, targeted and promoted MPs degradation, weakened the connections between myofibrils, and ultimately significantly improved meat tenderization. This systematic analysis of key endogenous proteases has filled a critical knowledge gap in the mechanism by which PEF and L-Arg regulate post-mortem meat proteolysis, while providing empirical support for their synergistic enzymatic hydrolysis efficacy.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis revealed the regulatory network of LA, PEF, and LP treatments on meat texture (Fig. 5B). The results showed that shear force was significantly positively correlated with H0 (r = 0.93), α-helix content (r = 0.97), and cooking loss (r = 0.77), while significantly negatively correlated with hydrogen bond number (r = −0.94) and pH value (r = −0.95). Combined with the characteristics of the LA treatment, this correlation clarified the regulatory mechanism of L-Arg: L-Arg reduced protein H0 and α-helices, thereby inhibiting abnormal protein aggregation. Meanwhile, the weakly alkaline environment it created enhanced the stability of hydrogen bond networks, stabilized protein hydration layers, reduced water loss, and ultimately improved meat tenderness (Fan, Gao and Zhou, 2024). In addition, shear force was strongly negatively correlated with calpain activity (r = −0.96), which further confirmed the dominant tenderization mechanism of endogenous enzymes. LA activated calpains and promoted the dissociation of MPs, laying a foundation for tenderness improvement.

The correlation matrix of MFI showed that MFI was significantly positively correlated with cathepsin B activity (r = 0.90), hydrogen bond number (r = 0.83), and β-turn content (r = 0.78), while significantly negatively correlated with ionic bond strength (r = −1.0) and α-helix content (r = −0.85). This finding precisely illustrated the regulatory mechanism of the PEF treatment: its electroporation effect damaged lysosomal membranes, promoting the release and activation of cathepsins. Moreover, it induced the reconstruction of protein secondary structure, accelerated the degradation of structural proteins, and increased MFI (Roobab et al., 2024). However, PEF treatment excessively destroyed myofibril structures, reducing protein hydration capacity and impairing water-holding capacity. The core advantage of the LP combined treatment was precisely compensating for the defects of single treatments and achieving functional complementarity and a synergistic effect between PEF and L-Arg. Specifically, the LP treatment not only retained PEF's advantages of improving enzyme-substrate binding efficiency and accelerating protein degradation, but also maintained protein hydration capacity through L-Arg regulation. It ultimately established a dynamic balance between tenderness improvement and water retention, maximizing the improvement of meat texture.

Molecular dynamics simulation and molecular docking

MDS analysis of MP structural changes under different treatment modes

RMSD was the main parameter describing the structural stability of protein under electric field conditions. The protein structural changes became increasingly apparent as the RMSD increased. Fig. 6A showed that the RMSD fluctuated significantly in the range of 20 to 30 ns. When equilibrium was reached at 100 ns, the RMSD was about 1.4, indicating that PEF destroyed the natural state of the protein and significantly reduced the stability of its conformation. Furthermore, the number of hydrogen bonds was employed to predict changes in the secondary structure of proteins. As shown in Fig. 6B, the number of hydrogen bonds within the protein molecule was affected by PEF and exhibited a decreasing trend. When equilibrium was attained at 100 ns, the number of hydrogen bonds was about 11. The decrease in hydrogen bonds indicated that PEF disrupted the protein network, caused shifts in secondary structure toward irregular coiling, and thus rendered the muscle structure loose and disordered. Additionally, Rg could reflect the tightness of the protein structure. Under electric field conditions, Rg fluctuated greatly and rose significantly when reaching equilibrium (Fig. 6C). Therefore, it was confirmed that PEF destabilized the stability of protein structure, weakened the interactions between proteins, and promoted the loosening or unfolding of the protein structure. Fig. 6D showed the trend of SASA under simulated conditions. A higher SASA was detected under an electric field, indicating that protein unfolding occurred. This structural remodeling exposed more specific cleavage sites for endogenous proteases, which could potentially increase enzyme-substrate binding efficiency and thus facilitate targeted degradation. In short, PEF created enzymatic site accessibility by inducing structural unfolding of the protein, which was the core mechanism underlying its efficient degradation. Fig. 6E and Fig. 6F captured the binding of actin and myosin from 0 to 100 ns in the absence and presence of an electric field, respectively. As can be seen, actin and myosin in the no-electric-field group overlapped closely, with a continuous and intact binding interface. In contrast, the binding in the electric field group was clearly loose, which was consistent with the above analysis.

It can be inferred that the endogenous enzyme system may be prematurely activated through a similar conformational change mechanism, which complements the aforementioned mechanism of endogenous enzyme activation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that pulsed electric field (PEF) combined with L-arginine significantly improved the tenderness of goose meat. Specifically, the combined treatment increased goose meat tenderness by 51.30% and myofibril fragmentation index (MFI) by 57.26%, and reduced cooking loss by 17.40%. This optimization effect stemmed from the systematic regulation of the combined treatment on the microstructure, protein structure, endogenous enzyme activity, and molecular interactions of goose meat: the combined treatment weakened the connections between myofilaments and expanded intercellular spaces, thereby enhancing the looseness of myofibers and laying a structural foundation for the improvement of meat tenderness. Meanwhile, it induced the transformation of the structure of myofibrillar proteins (MPs) from α-helix to β-sheet, which promoted protein unfolding—a process that not only significantly increased fluorescence intensity but also created more binding sites for endogenous enzymes. In addition, the combined treatment increased the activities of calpain, cathepsin B, and cathepsin L in goose meat by 64.33%, 73.33%, and 68.98%, respectively. This significant activation of enzyme activities provided crucial mechanistic support for the degradation of major structural proteins such as myofibrillar proteins. Molecular docking results further confirmed at the molecular level that PEF was the core driving force for protein relaxation, and its synergistic combination with L-arginine achieved protein structural rearrangement under the joint guidance of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. In conclusion, this study developed a strategy for precise regulation of meat tenderness. Future research could focus on the shelf life and safety of goose meat treated with LP during cold storage, and extend this processing technology to other poultry meats such as chicken and duck to evaluate its universality.
