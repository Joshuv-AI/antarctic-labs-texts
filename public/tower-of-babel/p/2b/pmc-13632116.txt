Associations of traumatic brain injury with incident cardiovascular disease: Results from the atherosclerosis risk in communities (ARIC) study
Authors: Katherine J Hunzinger, Bernadette A D’Alonzo, Anthony Thompson, Connor A Law, Michael T Nguyen, Sandro Marini, Saef Izzy, Holly Elser, Alvaro Alonso, Michelle Johansen, Andrea LC Schneider
Published: 2026

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is increasingly recognized as a chronic systemic disease with long-term consequences extending far beyond the central nervous system [1]. Prior population-level studies, which have largely relied solely on administrative claims or tertiary-center datasets, have shown associations with TBI and cardiovascular (CV) outcomes [2,3]. Accordingly, there is a pressing need for large, prospective, community-based studies with broader representation, time-varying TBI modeling, long duration of follow-up time, and multi-sourced and adjudicated outcome ascertainment to more precisely define the long-term CV consequences of TBI and to determine whether cumulative or more severe injuries confer disproportionate CV risk. Leveraging detailed, longitudinal data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study, our objective was to investigate associations of TBI sustained in mid-to-late adulthood with incident CV disease, including coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure (HF), and atrial fibrillation (AF).

Methods

Study design and population

The ARIC Study is an ongoing prospective cohort comprised of 15,792 community dwelling adults who were aged 45–64 years at enrollment in 1987–1989 [4]. Participants were recruited from four U.S. communities: Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi, Washington County, Maryland, and the northwest suburbs of Minneapolis, Minnesota. Participants have attended in-person follow-up visits and taken part in telephone follow-up calls. The ARIC Study also conducts active surveillance of hospitalizations and is linked to Centers for Medicare & Medicaid (CMS) data (1991–2020). Participants or their legally authorized representative provided written informed consent. The ARIC study is approved by the institutional review board at each participating institution. For this analysis, we included 12,238 White and Black participants without prevalent CAD, HF, AF, or TBI with complete data on statistical model covariates.

Traumatic brain injury

TBI was defined from: 1) self-reported data from in-person follow-up visits, 2) continuously collected hospitalization surveillance ICD-9/10 code data, and 3) linked CMS emergency department, hospital, and outpatient ICD-9/10 code data (ICD-9: 800.x, 801.x, 803.x, 804.x, 850.x, 851.x, 852.x, 853.x, 984.x, 959.01 and ICD-10: S02.0, S02.1x, S02.8, S02.91, S04.02, S04.03x, S04.04x, S06.x, S07.1). We evaluated time-dependent incident TBI status (yes; no), TBI frequency (0; 1; 2+ TBIs), and severity of the first TBI (mild; moderate/severe; defined according to Department of Defense ICD-9/10 code-based criteria).

Coronary artery disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation

CAD was a physician adjudicated event defined as a definite or probable hospitalization due to myocardial infarction or definite CAD-related death. HF was defined from continuously collected hospitalization surveillance data (ICD-9: 428.x and ICD-10: I50). AF was ascertained using: 1) electrocardiograms performed at in-person visits, 2) continuously collected hospitalization surveillance data (ICD-9: 427.31 and ICD-10: I48.91 in the absence of codes for cardiac surgery during the same admission [ICD-9: 35.x, 36.x and ICD-10: I97.x]), and 3) linkage to the national death index.

Statistical analysis

Follow-up time extended from study enrollment (Visit 1, 1987–1989) to incident CV event, death, loss to follow-up/study withdrawal, or administrative censoring (12/31/2022 for CAD and HF and 12/31/2021 for AF). Adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate associations between TBI and incident CAD, HF, and AF, separately. Models were adjusted for baseline age, sex, race/center, education, income, military veteran status, body mass index, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking status, hypertension, and diabetes.

In sensitivity analyses, we implemented a 1-year lag period to address the possibility that CV disease may be prevalent but undiagnosed at the time of a TBI and then subsequently diagnosed due to an increase in encounters with the medical system after a TBI. We also repeated analyses using Fine and Gray proportional hazards models to account for the competing risk of mortality.

Statistical analyses were performed in Stata SE Version 19.

Results

At study baseline, the 12,238 included participants were a mean age of 54.1 (SD=5.8) years, 58.1% were female, and 27.5% were of self-reported Black race (Fig. 1A).

In analyses of associations of incident TBI with incident CAD, HF, and AF, there were 1442 TBIs (occurring at a median of 13.3 years, IQR=5.7–22.7), 1408 TBIs (occurring at a median of 12.7 years, IQR=5.6–22.5), and 1358 TBIs (occurring at a median of 11.8 years, IQR=5.6–21.9), respectively. There were 1985 incident CHD events (occurring over a median of 26.7 years, IQR=17.0–33.0), 3165 incident HF events (occurring over a median of 26.4 years, IQR=16.9–32.8), and 2769 incident AF events (occurring over a median of 25.8 years, IQR=17.0–32.2). In adjusted models, participants with TBI had 1.23 (95%CI=1.05–1.45) times the rate of incident CAD, 1.38 (95%CI=1.24–1.55) times the rate of incident HF, and 1.21 (95%CI=1.07–1.37) times the rate of incident AF, compared to participants without TBI (Fig. 1B). Increasing rates of incident HF were seen with an increasing number of TBIs (HR=1.32, 95%CI=1.01–1.72 comparing 2+ TBIs to 1 TBI) and with greater severity of the first TBI (HR=1.35, 95%CI=1.02–1.79 comparing moderate/severe TBI to mild TBI. Associations with incident CAD and AF were similar by TBI frequency and severity.

In sensitivity analyses implementing a 1-year lag period and accounting for the competing risk of mortality, results were similar to our primary analysis for associations of incident TBI with incident CAD (HR=1.23, 95%CI=1.04–1.44 and sub-distribution HR (sHR)=1.23, 95%CI=1.04–1.46), HF (HR=1.39, 95%CI=1.24–1.55 and sHR=1.40, 95%CI=1.24–1.58), and AF (HR=1.21, 95%CI=1.07–1.37 and sHR=1.20, 95%CI=1.05–1.38).

Discussion

In this community-based cohort followed for over 30 years, individuals who sustained a TBI in mid- or later-life were at increased risk for incident CAD, HF, and AF, compared to individuals who did not sustain a TBI. Further, the risk of TBI associated with incident HF increased in a dose dependent manner with number of TBIs and with TBI severity. The underlying mechanisms and pathways linking TBI to incident CV disease are likely multifactorial, including pre-injury demographic factors, genetics, and medical comorbidities, injury-related disruption of autonomic regulation, neuroendocrine signaling, and inflammatory pathways, and post-injury behavioral changes such as sleep disturbance and reduced physical activity.

Certain limitations should be considered in the interpretation of our results. First, our TBI definition was sourced from self-reported head injuries which resulted in loss of consciousness or medical care and ICD-9/10 code data, thus there may be exposure misclassification, particularly of milder TBIs. Further, detailed data on injury-related factors, including mechanism of injury and neuroimaging results, were not available. However, we were able to evaluate TBI frequency and severity, but TBI severity was defined only based on ICD-9/10 codes, meaning that these results generalize only to individuals with TBI who present to medical care. Second, while the definition for incident CAD was physician adjudicated, definitions for HF and AF were not. However, prior validation studies in ARIC have reported positive predictive values of 77% for HF defined by ICD-9/10 codes and 89% for AF defined by ICD-9/10 codes, death certificate codes, and electrocardiograms [5,6]. Given the CV outcomes are largely dependent on healthcare contact, there remains the possibility of surveillance bias, whereby individuals with TBI may have more contact with the healthcare system after TBI, resulting in more opportunities to detect CV disease. Lastly, given the observational nature of our study, there remains the possibility of residual confounding.

In conclusion, individuals who sustained a TBI in mid-to-late life were at increased risk for incident CV disease, compared to individuals who did not sustain a TBI. This study identifies individuals with TBI as a high-risk subgroup who may benefit from targeted CV risk assessment, prevention, and risk factor reduction.
