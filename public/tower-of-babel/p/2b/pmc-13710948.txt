Phenotypic and neuropeptidergic control of appetitive behavior in honey bees (Apis mellifera)
Authors: Rafael C da Silva, Mariana P Cassinelli, Rodrigo Velarde, Bérénice Nahmias, Louise Bestea, Martin Giurfa, Maria Gabriela de Brito Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropeptide activity in honey bees varies with age, behavioral role, and season. The short neuropeptide F (sNPF) modulates foraging-related behaviors in summer foragers, including food intake, sucrose sensitivity, and sensory processing, but its role in other castes remains unknown. Winter bees and nurses are two phenotypes, which are considered similar due to their substantial internal energetic reserves. Yet, their appetitive profiles have never been characterized. Here, we combined electrophysiological, behavioral and neuropeptide supplementation approaches to compare appetitive behaviors across bees of different behavioral castes and ages, with or without sNPF supplementation. sNPF treatment enhanced sucrose responsiveness in winter bees and olfactory responses in nurses. Our results reveal distinct appetitive profiles, challenge the assumption that nurses and winter bees behave similarly, and highlight that appetitive motivation is multidimensional. Specifically, they show that phenotype and season can decouple peripheral sensitivity, central responsiveness, learning performance, and food consumption.Subject terms: Animal behaviour, Entomology

Introduction

Neuropeptides are small (3–100 amino acids), structurally simple messengers that regulate diverse behavioral and physiological processes, including pain, mood, appetite, learning, memory, and social behavior1–3. Acting as neurohormones, neuromodulators, or neurotransmitters4, their activity varies with phenotype, age, season, or their interaction5,6. In insects, neuropeptide F (NPF) and short neuropeptide F (sNPF) play critical roles in food- and stress-related responses7–9. In honey bees, research on NPF/sNPF has shown the presence of both NPF and sNPF genes and their respective neuropeptides; but only sNPF appears to have a functional receptor, supporting its active role10,11.

Honey bees are an interesting model for studying how sNPF signaling modulates nutritional and motivational states, because individual foraging behavior is closely tied to the colony’s collective needs rather than to individual needs12. This unique social organization permits researchers to investigate how internal neuromodulatory systems, such as those involving sNPF, contribute to behavioral specialization. In temperate climates, honey bee colonies remain active year-round, despite significant seasonal shifts in behavior and physiology. This continuity enables the observation of distinct behavioral phenotypes such as summer bees (nurses and foragers) and winter bees within a single species13 and the examination of how motivational states, and their potential modulation by sNPF, may change in response to both season and social environment. Foraging motivation may emerge from interactions between external cues (e.g., colony food demands and favorable weather conditions) and specific internal signaling molecules, such as sNPF, among other molecules with comparable roles14. Recent studies show that elevating sNPF levels induces a hunger-like state in partially fed summer honey bees, increasing their food intake, responsiveness to lower sucrose concentrations, visual and olfactory learning abilities, and spontaneous reactions to appetitive floral odors even without reward, while enhancing antennal lobe activity in response to odor stimuli9,15,16. Together, these findings suggest that the activation of sNPF signaling orchestrates a suite of appetitive behaviors associated with internal hunger-like states at least in summer foragers.

In temperate climates, newly emerged summer workers spend their first ~10 days performing in-hive tasks such as feeding larvae, cleaning, and cell maintenance, and are classified as nurse bees17. In late fall and winter, when brood rearing ceases, a subset of workers transitions into a long-lived phenotype specialized for thermoregulation. These winter bees cluster to maintain hive temperature and are physiologically distinct, with greater fat body reserves supporting cold resistance and metabolic demands18–20. Nurse and winter honeybees are regarded as behaviorally and physiologically similar, with winter bees often described as an extended nurse phenotype13,18,21 despite some arguments raised against this idea22. This is due to their common higher energetic reserves, which are absent in forager bees. For instance, nurse and winter bees have higher levels of vitellogenin (Vg), an egg yolk protein, when compared to summer forager bees, and the opposite is true when it comes to their internal circulating levels of juvenile hormone (JH), which is a promotor of division of labor23–25. However, winter bees possess even larger fat bodies than nurses and show distinct fat body gene expression, including genes linked to metabolism, insulin, and Transforming Growth Factor Beta (TGF- β) signaling20. Seasonal demography can also significantly impact bee physiology. Frequent brood production occurs during spring and summer but ceases in winter. The presence of brood and associated caregiving demands substantially affects the physiology of individual bees26–28. Additionally, nurse and winter bees face contrasting environmental factors, such as photoperiod29.

Despite indoor and outdoor differences, winter and nurse bees could rely on sNPF signaling to modulate motivational states to accommodate their specific individual or social demands. Nurse bees, whose primary role is brood care, are expected to be highly responsive to brood pheromones, which guide their in-hive activities30. In contrast, winter bees may prioritize energy conservation while remaining physiologically prepared to initiate foraging when environmental conditions improve. Therefore, sNPF signaling is likely to have different effects in these two phenotypes, reflecting their differing behavioral demands and contributing to the overall coordination of colony functions. While sNPF signaling has been shown to modulate foraging-related behaviors in summer bees, its role in regulating behavior in nurse and winter bees remains unexplored.

Here we studied appetitive-related behaviors across different honeybee phenotypes (foragers, nurses, and winter bees) and compared nurse and winter bees in terms of their appetitive responses following sNPF treatment. We first compared previously published data from starved summer foragers with untreated nurse and winter bees collected in our study. We compared their performance by addressing five appetitive-related traits: (I) sucrose sensitivity at the antennae level, (II) sucrose responsiveness, (III) spontaneous olfactory responsiveness, (IV) appetitive olfactory learning, and (V) food consumption. We predicted that summer bees would outperform both nurse and winter bees for any of the behavior tested given their increased motivation associated with their poor nutritional state coupled with low satiety while searching for food. Secondly, given sNPF’s established role in modulating appetitive behaviors in honey bee foragers, we tested whether enhancing internal levels of sNPF would stimulate summer-like appetitive motivation in nurse and winter bees. We hypothesized that increasing sNPF levels would affect feeding-related traits in winter and nurse bees in a context-dependent manner, enhancing behaviors relevant to their social context. Because taste serves as an initial filter in appetitive decision-making, and unpalatable flavors can reduce feeding motivation depending on satiety31, we conducted tests in which the sugar solution was adulterated with salicin to test whether sNPF supplementation could increase its appetitive value. Salicin was used because it has been shown to reduce the palatability of sucrose solutions for honey bees32,33. Finally, to disentangle whether food consumption—as a component of appetitive motivation—is primarily driven by behavioral phenotype or chronological age, we conducted an independent feeding assay comparing young nurse bees (5 days old), older nurse bees (14 days old), and 14-day-old forage.

Our results show that behavioral castes exhibit distinct appetitive behaviors, with chronological age likely acting as an additional modulating factor. Crucially, sNPF’s effects were not uniform,rather than inducing a generic boost in appetitive motivation, it acted in caste-specific ways, enhancing behaviors aligned with each group’s social roles. Importantly, and contrary to a long-standing assumption, winter and nurse bees proved to be distinct in their appetitive motivation. Together, these experiments uncover a new layer of neuropeptide-mediated plasticity, spanning seasonal and social dimensions of colony life. They demonstrate that appetitive motivation is multidimensional and that phenotype and season can dissociate peripheral sensitivity, central responsiveness, learning performance, and food consumption behavior.

Results

Winter and nurse bees were collected directly from their hives. Winter bees were collected by uncovering a small opening in the hive roof, placing a glass vial over it, and allowing light-attracted bees to enter the vial, where they were subsequently captured. Nurses were collected by exposing the frames and sampling only workers actively inspecting brood cells and exhibiting whitish coloration, respectively. Upon collection bees were immobilized either in individual holders (for sucrose responsiveness, olfactory spontaneous responsiveness, and olfactory conditioning assays) or in individual syringes (for food consumption assay). For electrophysiological recordings of antennal sucrose sensitivity, each bee was immobilized in a small, custom-adapted Eppendorf tube. Since gustatory receptors are more easily accessible on the ventral side of the antenna, the antennae were positioned on a microscope slide with the ventral surface facing upward and gently secured using thin copper wires. A small opening was then made in the contralateral compound eye to insert the indifferent electrode.

Data from summer forager bees were in part obtained from Bestea et al.9 (for sucrose responsiveness, olfactory spontaneous responsiveness, and food consumption assays) and reanalyzed for the present study. Summer forager bees used in the sucrose responsiveness, olfactory conditioning, and food consumption assays were collected upon landing at a feeder containing 40% (w/v) sucrose solution. These bees were not visiting alternative food sources, as we verified that they flew regularly back and forth between the hive from which they had been trained and the feeder. Thus, their crops were expected to be almost empty upon arrival at the feeder as bees leave the hive with only a minimal fuel amount required for the trip, keeping the crop largely empty to maximize nectar transport34–37.

Sucrose concentrations are initially presented in both mM and percentage to provide a clear reference. Thereafter, we consistently use mM for electrophysiology experiments and percentage for behavioral measurements using sucrose, in line with common practice in the literature.

Appetitive behavioral profiles across bee phenotypes

In an initial series of experiments, we performed a first characterization of appetitive profiles across bee phenotypes. We compared the performance of nurses, winter bees, and summer foragers in a set of appetitive assays, assessing antennal sucrose sensitivity, behavioral sucrose responsiveness, spontaneous olfactory responsiveness, olfactory appetitive associative learning, and food consumption.

Antennal peripheral sucrose sensitivity

We studied antennal peripheral sucrose sensitivity using an electrophysiological approach. We examined peripheral taste responses of chaetic gustatory sensilla38, situated on the antennae32 (Fig. 1A). Each sensillum was sequentially stimulated with increasing sucrose concentrations (1, 10, 30, and 100 mM; i.e., 0.03%, 0.34%, 1.02%, and 3.43% (w/v), respectively), allowing us to establish dose–response curves. Spiking activity was quantified during the first second after stimulus onset and compared across concentrations.

Figure 1B shows the mean number of action potentials (spikes/s) recorded in response to sucrose stimulation of the antennae. Summer foragers exhibited significantly higher spike activity than both nurses and winter bees across all sucrose concentrations tested. Compared to nurses, summer foragers showed enhanced antennal responses to 1 mM (emmeans: p < 0.001; for detailed statistical account see detailed statistical tables in Supplementary Information; Table S1), 10 mM (p < 0.001), 30 mM (p < 0.001), and 100 mM sucrose (p < 0.01). Similarly, summer foragers responded more strongly than winter bees at 1 mM (p < 0.001), 10 mM (p < 0.001), 30 mM (p < 0.001), and 100 mM sucrose (p < 0.001). By contrast, nurses and winter bees showed no significant differences in their antennal responses across concentrations (1 mM: p = 0.948; 10 mM: p = 0.998; 30 mM: p = 1.000; 100 mM: p = 0.721).

Figure 1C shows representative recordings illustrating the responses to two stimulation conditions: the control solution (1 mM KCl solution or KCl 0.007% w/v = control) and a 30 mM sucrose solution (30 mM sucrose solution in a 1 mM KCl solution) for the three tested bee phenotypes: winter bees, summer foragers, and nurse bees. A low level of action potential activity can be observed in the control condition (KCl 1 mM), both in the raw traces (Fig. 1C) and in the quantified data (Fig. 1B). The spikes observed under control conditions are typically of much lower amplitude than those recorded during stimulation with KCl + sucrose. Under KCl stimulation alone, spike activity is low or absent, and when present, spikes are generally of small amplitude. In contrast, the addition of sucrose elicits larger-amplitude spikes, while the small-amplitude spikes often diminish or disappear. This pattern suggests the possible involvement of distinct neuronal populations, with small-amplitude spikes likely originating from neurons responsive to low ionic concentrations or osmotic changes, and larger-amplitude spikes reflecting the recruitment of sugar-responsive neurons32,39.

Overall, these results indicate that peripheral antennal sucrose sensitivity is linked to the specific demands associated with each behavioral role and physiological state. Summer foragers show the highest sensitivity, consistent with their role as the colony’s food gatherers. This enhanced sensory responsiveness aligns with their limited internal energetic reserves and low satiety state, which together make them more attuned to detecting external food sources to sustain colony nutrition.

Behavioral sucrose responsiveness

We next tested whether honeybee phenotypes (nurse bees, winter bees, and summer foragers) differ in behavioral sucrose responsiveness at the antennal level. Proboscis extension response (PER) was measured across a series of increasing sucrose concentrations (0.1%, 0.3%, 1%, 3%, 10%, and 30% w/v; i.e., 2.9, 8.8, 29.2, 87.6, 292, 876 mM, respectively) delivered to the bees’ antennae by means of a toothpick40,41. After these six sucrose trials, bees were stimulated with a 50% (w/v) solution (i.e., 1,46 M) as a control; bees not responding to it were excluded. We calculated both a population-level responsiveness (% of bees responding to a given sucrose concentration) and an individual sucrose responsiveness score (number of sucrose concentrations to which an individual responded with PER). A score of 1 indicated a response only to the highest concentration (30%), while a score of 6 indicated responses to all six tested concentrations.

Figure 2A shows the populational responsiveness performance of summer foragers, winter bees and nurses. The GLMM revealed that both summer foragers and winter bees differed significantly from the reference group (nurse bees) (summer foragers: p = 0.001; winter bees: p = 0.007; Table S2). These two groups responded similarly to the range of sucrose concentrations, and both performed above the level of nurse bees (emmeans – nurses vs. summer foragers: p-value = 0.005; nurses vs. winter bees: p-value = 0.021; summer foragers vs. winter bees: p-value = 0.603; Table S2). This pattern was mirrored in the individual sucrose responsiveness scores (Fig. 2B), where summer foragers and winter bees again scored higher than nurse bees (emmeans – nurses vs. summer foragers: p-value = 0.012; nurses vs. winter bees: p-value = 0.042; summer foragers vs. winter bees: p-value = 0.647; Table S2). Together, these results indicate that differences in behavioral sucrose responsiveness among honey bees are more strongly associated with chronological age than with their behavioral phenotype. Moreover, they also show that sensitivity at the level of antennal gustatory receptors does not directly predict sucrose responsiveness at the behavioral level: winter bees exhibited sucrose responses comparable to those of summer foragers, despite their reduced peripheral gustatory sensitivity.

Olfactory spontaneous responsiveness

Harnessed bees were stimulated with linalool and 2-phenylethanol, two floral odors known to evoke spontaneous PER9,42. Both odors were presented to each bee in a randomized sequence9. Bees were excluded from the final analysis if they did not perform PER to antennal stimulation with a 50% sucrose solution after the olfactory tests.

The model identified summer foragers and winter bees as significant predictors of spontaneous olfactory responsiveness (GLMM - summer bees: p-value < 0.001; winter bees: p-value < 0.001; Table S2). Summer foragers and winter bees exhibited similar spontaneous responsiveness, both exceeding that of nurse bees (emmeans—nurses vs. summer bees: p-value < 0.001; nurses vs. winter bees: p-value < 0.001; summer bees vs. winter bees: p-value = 1.000) (Fig. 2C and Table S2).

These results confirm that honey bees can respond innately to different floral odorants regardless of behavioral phenotype. Yet, the likelihood of responsiveness increases with age, and remains conserved across seasons, likely reflecting enhanced olfactory sensitivity, odor experience, or both.

Appetitive olfactory conditioning

Forager, nurse, and winter bees were individually harnessed and subjected to absolute olfactory conditioning, associating a neutral odorant (2-hexanol or 1-nonanal) with 50% sucrose solution (w/v). Each bee received four odor–sucrose pairings, spaced ~10 min apart. In each 39-s trial, the odor was delivered for 6 s; sucrose was applied to the antennae 5 s after odor onset, creating a 1-s overlap. One hour after the last trial, memory specificity was tested by presenting the conditioned odor (CS) and a novel odor (NOd) without sucrose reward, in randomized order43. Bees conditioned with 2-hexanol received 1-nonanal as the NOd, and vice versa.

We recorded unconditioned stimulus (US) responsiveness across trials, conditioned stimulus (CS) responses in each trial to generate population-level learning curves and individual learning scores, and memory specificity (i.e., the percentage of bees responding to the CS but not to the novel odor, NOd) 1 h after the conditioning phase.

Summer forager bees responded more consistently to the US across conditioning trials than both nurse and winter bees, which did not differ from each other (Fig. 3A; emmeans nurses vs. summer foragers: p = 0.001; summer foragers vs. winter bees: p = 0.010; nurses vs. winter bees: p = 0.782; Table S3). These results indicate a higher motivation to respond to the reward during the conditioning phase in summer foragers. To compare learning performances across phenotypes, we used bees that responded always to the US.

All three groups successfully associated the CS with the US during conditioning (Fig. 3B; emmeans; nurses, first trial vs. fourth trial: p < 0.001; summer foragers, first trial vs. fourth trial: p < 0.001; winter bees, first trial vs. fourth trial: p < 0.001; Table S3). However, foragers and winter bees achieved similar, superior performance relative to nurses (GLMM – summer foragers: p < 0.001; winter bees: p < 0.001; Table S3). This suggests that even though some winter bees were less responsive to the US during conditioning, those that were responsive learned as effectively as summer foragers.

Individual learning scores mirrored these trends (Fig. 3C): foragers and winter bees more often responded to the CS after a single trial, frequently reaching the maximum score of 3 (GLMM—summer foragers: p < 0.001; winter bees: p < 0.001; Table S3).

Finally, in the memory test, summer foragers generalized less their response to the NOd than nurses, thus showing a higher level of specific memory (Fig. 3D). Winter bees showed intermediate levels of specific memory (emmeans nurses vs. summer foragers: p = 0.029; nurses vs. winter bees: p = 0.946; summer foragers vs. winter bees: p = 0.161; Table S3).

Altogether, these results suggest that appetitive olfactory learning is strongly influenced by chronological age, with older bees (foragers and winter bees) performing better than younger nurses. However, memory specificity appears to be shaped by both age and seasonal context, potentially reflecting differences in social demands,summer foragers generalized least, winter bees intermediately, and nurses the most.

Food consumption

We measured the quantity of food (µL) ingested individually by each bee enclosed in a syringe during 1 h. A pipette with a cut tip inserted into the syringe hub provided access to either 100 µL of a 20% sucrose solution or to the same volume of 20% sucrose solution spiked with 100 mM salicin (hereafter 20% sucrose + salicin). As mentioned above, salicin was added to make the food less appetizing32,33. We aimed to test whether this adulteration would affect feeding behavior by making the food less attractive and how this effect would differ among the three bee phenotypes. Measuring food consumption is relevant, as each group may differ in its nutritional needs and demand for liquid food depending on its behavioral role.

In both experiments - whether offered a 20% sucrose solution or the same solution adulterated with salicin—summer foragers consumed more food than either winter or nurse bees (Fig. 4 and Table S4). Winter bees consistently showed intermediate consumption levels, while nurse bees consumed the least (emmeans 20% sucrose; nurses vs. summer foragers: p < 0.001; nurses vs. winter bees: p < 0.001; summer foragers vs. winter bees: p < 0.01; emmeans 20% sucrose + salicin: nurses vs. summer foragers: p < 0.001; nurses vs. winter bees: p < 0.001; summer foragers vs. winter bees: p < 0.05; Table S4).

Across all three phenotypes, bees consumed significantly more plain sucrose solution than the salicin-adulterated one (Fig. 4; GLM, nurse bees – sucrose + salicin: p < 0.05; summer foragers − sucrose + salicin: p < 0.001; winter bees − sucrose + salicin: p < 0.001; Table S4). The addition of salicin reduced intake by ~29% in nurse bees, ~41% in summer foragers, and ~29% in winter bees compared with plain sucrose.

These results clearly indicate that food consumption is directly influenced by the physiological state associated with each phenotype. Although summer foragers—bees with the lowest nutritional reserves—showed the greatest reduction in sucrose intake when salicin was added, they were still more likely to feed than the other phenotypes, regardless of food quality. In contrast, better-nourished phenotypes were more selective in their feeding behavior.

SNPF modulation of appetitive responses in winter and nurse bees

In a second series of experiments, we investigated the modulation of appetitive responses by sNPF in nurses and winter bees. We studied the effects of sNPF on sucrose responsiveness, spontaneous olfactory responsiveness, and food consumption in nurse and winter bees, aiming to determine whether these phenotypes—often considered similar—differ in their physiological responses to this neuropeptide. Winter and nurse bees were randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups. The control group remained untreated (“untreated”). Two groups received topical applications of sNPF on the thorax: one with 1 µg/µL (“sNPF1”) and another with 10 µg/µL (“sNPF10”). This method ensures efficient penetration of sNPF into the bee hemolymph16. A fourth group received a topical application of the solvent (DMSO/Acetone) used to dissolve sNPF (“solvent”). All behavioral experiments began 30 min after treatment application9.

Winter bees from different treatment groups responded differently to the sucrose concentrations (Fig. 5A). The model identified both sNPF1 and sNPF10 as statistically significant predictors of responsiveness (GLMM – solvent: p = 0.151; sNPF1: p < 0.001; sNPF10: p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A and Table S5). In contrast, topical treatment of nurse bees with sNPF (Fig. 5C, D) did neither enhance their population-level responsiveness (GLMM population-level performance – solvent: p = 0.452: sNPF1: p = 0.932; sNPF10: p = 0.813; Table S5) nor their individual responsiveness scores (GLM individual scores – Intercept: p = 0.695; solvent: p = 0.617; sNPF1: p = 0.991; sNPF10: p = 0.735; Table S5). These findings suggest that sNPF plays a more prominent role in modulating behavioral sucrose responsiveness in winter bees than in nurse bees.

Olfactory spontaneous responsiveness

In winter bees, spontaneous olfactory responsiveness to linalool and 2-phenylethanol (see above) did not differ significantly between treatment groups (Fig. 6A; GLMM — solvent: p = 0.182; sNPF1: p = 0.548; sNPF10: p = 0.482; Table S6). In contrast, for nurse bees, both sNPF1 and sNPF10 significantly increased spontaneous olfactory responsiveness (Fig. 6B; GLMM — solvent: p = 0.578; sNPF1: p < 0.001; sNPF10: p < 0.001; Table S6). Post-hoc comparisons showed that nurse bees treated with either sNPF1 or sNPF10 were significantly more likely to respond to odorants than controls (emmeans — untreated vs. solvent: p = 0.944; untreated vs. sNPF1: p < 0.001; untreated vs. sNPF10: p < 0.001; solvent vs. sNPF1: p < 0.001; solvent vs. sNPF10: p < 0.05; sNPF1 vs. sNPF10: p = 0.996; Table S6). Overall, these results indicate that sNPF does not modulate olfactory responsiveness in winter bees but enhances it in nurse bees.

Food consumption

In winter bees, increasing levels of sNPF did not enhance food consumption under either condition, pure sucrose solution (Fig. 7A; LMM 20% sucrose: solvent: p = 0.805; sNPF1: p = 0.992; sNPF10: p = 0.486) or sucrose solution containing salicin (LMM 20% sucrose + salicin: p = 0.383; sNPF1: p = 0.078; sNPF10: p = 0.685; Table S7). As previously demonstrated (Fig. 4), the only significant difference in food consumption was observed between the untreated groups offered pure sucrose solution versus sucrose solution spiked with salicin (LMM 20% sucrose vs. 20% sucrose + salicin – sucrose + salicin: p < 0.001; Table S7).

In nurse bees, sNPF10 treatment seemed to induce a lower consumption of pure sucrose solution compared to untreated bees (Fig. 7B; GLMM 20% sucrose – Intercept: p < 0.001; solvent: p = 0.864; sNPF1: p = 0.9941; sNPF10: p = 0.034) (Fig. 6B and Table S7). However, pairwise comparisons revealed that both groups ultimately performed similarly (emmeans – estimate = p = 0.147; Table S7).

Topical sNPF treatment also did not increase food consumption when nurse bees were offered sucrose solution plus salicin (GLMM 20% sucrose + salicin – solvent: p = 0.350; sNPF1: p = 0.432; sNPF10: p = 0.231) (Fig. 7B and Table S7). As observed in previous experiments (see above), the only significant difference in consumption was observed between the untreated groups offered pure sucrose solution vs. sucrose solution spiked with salicin (GLMM 20% sucrose vs. 20% sucrose + salicin – Intercept: p < 0.001; sucrose + salicin: p < 0.01; Table S7). These findings show that sNPF treatment does not promote higher food consumption in either winter or nurse bees, regardless of the resources offered.

Disentangling the effects of behavioral phenotype and age on food consumption

In a third series of experiments, we tested whether food consumption is driven primarily by behavioral role or by age. To address this, we established a single-cohort colony44, i.e., a colony composed entirely of workers of the same age. Newly emerged individuals were color-marked and introduced into the colony. Sampling and testing took place at two time points: 5–6 days later to collect “young nurses” and 14-16 days later to collect both “old nurses” and summer foragers. We thus compared three groups of workers (1) young nurses, collected from brood areas and tested at 5–6 days old; (2) aged nurses, collected from brood areas and tested at 14–16 days old, identified by their continued engagement in brood care; and (3) summer foragers, also 14–16 days old, captured at a feeder to which they had been previously trained. Nurses were identified inside the hive while performing brood care, ensuring they had not yet transitioned to foraging. Obtaining these bees was experimentally challenging, which accounts for the relatively low sample sizes across the different groups studied. The inclusion of the aged nurse group allowed us to separate the effects of chronological age from those of task specialization on appetitive responses.

Upon arrival in the laboratory, all bees were provided with 5 µL of honey. They were then divided into two groups with different feeding statuses: one group (unfed) received no additional food for 4 h, while the other group (fed) had ad libitum access to 50% sucrose solution during the same period. After 4 h, the food consumption assay was conducted. Consumption was quantified by offering each bee a tip containing 100 µL of either 50% sucrose solution or 50% sucrose solution supplemented with 100 mM salicin for 1 h.

In the unfed groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A), foragers and old nurses consumed significantly more 50% sucrose solution than young nurse bees (emmeans – foragers vs. young nurses: p < 0.05; old nurses vs. young nurses: p < 0.01; foragers vs. old nurses: p = 0.999). However, when offered 50% sucrose solution supplemented with salicin, all three groups consumed similar amounts, as the intake of foragers and old nurses decreased to match that of young nurses (LM – old nurses 50% sucrose + salicin: p = 0.917; young nurses 50% sucrose + salicin: p = 0.095; Table S8).

Both unfed foragers and old nurses consumed more of the pure sucrose solution than of the sucrose solution containing salicin (LM foragers – sucrose type (sucrose + salicin): p = 0.038; LM old nurses – sucrose type (sucrose + salicin): p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, young nurse bees consumed similar amounts of both solutions (LM young nurses – sucrose type (sucrose + salicin): p = 0.298).

For the fed groups (Supplementary Fig. 1B), no significant differences were detected among foragers, old nurses, and young nurses when offered 50% sucrose solution (LM – old nurses 50% sucrose: p = 0.204; young nurses 50% sucrose: p = 0.752; Table S8). When offered sucrose with salicin, young nurses tended to consume more than the other two groups (foragers 50% sucrose + salicin vs. old nurses 50% sucrose + salicin: p = 0.990; foragers 50% sucrose + salicin vs. young nurses 50% sucrose + salicin: p < 0.001; old nurses 50% sucrose + salicin vs. young nurses 50% sucrose + salicin: p < 0.001; Table S8).

However, foragers and old nurses still consumed significantly more of the pure sucrose solution than the sucrose solution containing salicin (LM foragers – Intercept: p < 0.001; sucrose type (sucrose + salicin): p < 0.001; LM old nurses – Intercept: p < 0.001; sucrose type (sucrose + salicin): p = 0.001), whereas young nurses showed no significant difference in consumption between the two solutions (LM young nurses – sucrose type (sucrose + salicin): p = 0.173) (Supplementary Fig 1B; Table S8).

Overall, these results suggest that chronological age has a stronger influence on sucrose consumption than behavioral caste, as old nurses performed similarly to foragers. Additionally, regardless of feeding status (unfed vs. partially fed), older bees consistently preferred the more palatable, pure sucrose solution over the less palatable sucrose spiked with salicin.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated how appetitive motivation changes according to behavioral caste, chronological age, and modulation of short neuropeptide F (sNPF) signaling in honey bees. This provided a comprehensive analysis of how these factors influence multiple appetitive responses, enabling us to disentangle their relative contributions to the behavioral profiles of foragers, nurses, and winter bees. Although sNPF manipulation was carried out only in winter and nurse bees, our findings indicate that appetitive behaviors across all groups were strongly influenced by the combined effects of chronological age, nutritional state and season. Contrary to the widely held assumption that winter bees are simply an extended nurse bee phenotype13,18,21, our data reveal that they instead exhibit a phenotype different from those of foragers and nurses. More generally, our findings highlight that appetitive motivation is multidimensional and that phenotype and season can decouple peripheral sensitivity, central responsiveness, learning, and food consumption.

The extensive physiological similarities shared by winter and nurse bees across hormonal, metabolic, and anatomical traits have led to the hypothesis that cessation of brood rearing in autumn allows nurse bees to retain their physiological state, such that winter bees constitute a prolonged nurse phenotype28. Consistently, transcriptomic analyses of abdominal tissues indicate that winter and nurse bees are transcriptionally more similar to each other than to foragers21. In addition, similarities extend to juvenile hormone (JH) and vitellogenin (Vg). JH, a key regulator of behavioral maturation, rises progressively throughout the summer: young nurse bees exhibit low JH titers, which peak upon transition to foraging. At the onset of winter, bees maintain low JH levels comparable to those of nurses, before increasing again toward late winter18. Vg, a multifunctional yolk precursor and antioxidant protein, constitutes 32–40% of hemolymph proteins in nurses, compared with 15–26% in foragers, and remains elevated in early-winter bees18. Similarly, the pharyngeal glands—responsible for producing royal jelly proteins that influence caste differentiation45—are significantly larger in both nurse and winter bees than in foragers18. Together, these observations let to the interpretation that the winter phenotype reflects a prolonged nurse-like physiological state. While partially accepting this idea, Johnson22 noted that although winter bees share several physiological traits with nurse bees— such as low JH, high Vg, and active fat body—they should not be considered identical. Winter bees, contrary to nurses, do not shift tasks in response to colony needs, and the entire colony adopts a uniform winter phenotype, indicating that mechanisms underlying temporal polyethism are largely inactive. He also suggested that neurochemical profiles may differ between these two groups.

Our results question the hypothesis that the winter phenotype reflects a prolonged nurse-like physiological state. The behavioral differences between these categories were important, underscoring that treating them as equivalent can be misleading. Winter bees exhibited significantly higher sucrose and olfactory responsiveness than nurse bees, consumed more food, and showed robust appetitive olfactory learning comparable to that of foragers, although their memory performance was slightly inferior. These learning and memory performances are consistent with those reported by Behrends and Scheiner in their analysis of olfactory learning in winter bees46. Importantly nurse bees exhibited deficient learning and memory performances, further demonstrating that winter and nurse bees are not equivalent.

Artificial elevation of sNPF increased sucrose responsiveness in winter bees and enhanced spontaneous olfactory responsiveness in nurse bees, yet it did not affect food intake in either group. Complementary experiments without sNPF manipulation revealed that older bees—including foragers and aged nurse bees—consumed significantly more sucrose solution than younger nurses and consistently preferred pure sucrose solution over sucrose solution spiked with the aversive compound salicin, regardless of whether they were starved or fed.

Our data suggest that chronological age (with nurses being younger than foragers and winter bees), together with seasonal and physiological factors, contributes importantly to variation in several appetitive-related behaviors—such as sucrose responsiveness (Fig. 2A, B), spontaneous olfactory responsiveness (Fig. 2C), appetitive associative learning (Fig. 3B, C), and food consumption (Fig. 4). However, because age, experience, season, and physiological state are not fully dissociated in the present comparisons, these factors likely act in combination rather than reflecting a single dominant driver. This indicates that physiological maturation is one key factor among others affecting appetitive motivation in honey bees. Yet, some traits may become exclusively active depending on the specific demands that bees experience in their social context, combined with their internal physiological state. This seems to be the case for sucrose sensitivity at the antennal level, as summer foragers show higher gustatory-receptor sensitivity to different sucrose solutions compared to nurses and winter bees (Fig. 1B). This is consistent with the observation that sNPF receptor levels are higher in foragers relative to nurses47, and with evidence from other insects that sNPF can modulate peripheral sensory neurons48. Increased antennal sensitivity to sucrose solutions can be directly associated with food-search behavior, which in this context may be advantageous, as it enables foragers to respond effectively to food resources of varying quality throughout the season. Interestingly, the mismatch between low gustatory-receptor sensitivity and high sucrose responsiveness in winter bees suggests the presence of an internal amplifying mechanism associated with the expression of a “mature phenotype”, one that persists under unfavorable conditions but retains the capacity to shift when seasonal conditions improve. Age-dependent increases in biogenic-amine signaling underlying appetitive motivation (dopamine and/or octopamine; see below) could underlie the differences observed in winter bees.

Together, these findings further illustrate a nuanced, context-dependent role of sNPF, and likely of other related neuromodulatory systems, in regulating sensory processing and feeding behavior, shaped by the intersection of internal physiological states, environmental cues, and social role. The experiments presented here highlight different facets of this regulation and contribute to a deeper understanding of the factors underpinning motivation and division of labor in eusocial insects.

Signaling via sNPF appears highly sensitive to individual internal states. In honey bees, artificially increasing sNPF enhances gustatory responsiveness and food intake in summer foragers9 and improves visual and olfactory memory formation15,16. For instance, bees undergoing a starvation period upregulate snpfR47. In contrast, our study examined naturally well-nourished individuals (winter and nurse bees) yet still observed context-specific modulation of appetitive behaviors, particularly in olfactory sensitivity. Taken together with comparative evidence from ants49, these results suggest that sNPF functions as a flexible neuromodulator that helps align sensory responsiveness and task performance with the colony’s current needs, thereby contributing to division of labor.

While winter and nurse bees are often viewed as behaviorally and physiologically similar13,18,21, our findings indicate that they represent different phenotypes, which is a reflex of their age, specific social demands, and environmental factors. Winter bees emerge in late fall, when brood rearing ceases, and adopt generalist roles such as thermoregulation. They are characterized by low juvenile hormone (JH) and high vitellogenin (Vg) levels—profiles linked to prolonged lifespan and nutritional stability24,25. In contrast, summer nurse bees, although similar in some physiological markers, operate under fluctuating social and nutritional pressures associated with brood care. Thus, despite certain similarities, our results show that winter and nurse bees differ in sNPF signaling and behavioral responsiveness.

The differential effects of sNPF on gustatory and olfactory processing in honeybees likely result from an interplay of internal and external factors shaping the bees’ physiological and behavioral states. Winter bees, which spend most of their lives inside the hive, benefit from steady nourishment and a relatively stable environment. The absence of brood and related stimuli, such as brood pheromones, likely reduces their motivation to forage or respond to appetitive related olfactory cues, which may explain why elevated sNPF primarily enhanced sucrose responsiveness but had little effect on spontaneous olfactory responsiveness in these bees. In contrast, summer or springtime nurse bees, engaged in brood care, face fluctuating internal nutritional states and dynamic colony demands. In this context, sNPF elevation selectively enhanced spontaneous olfactory responsiveness, potentially optimizing their sensitivity to brood-related pheromones. These findings suggest that the sNPF system is finely tuned to meet seasonal and social demands, modulating neural pathways accordingly. The lack of sNPF effect on food intake in both groups aligns with their generally well-nourished status compared to foragers13,20.

Motivation to respond to appetitive cues, as modulated by sNPF, is significantly influenced by a combination of internal nutritional state, task role, and age. For instance, starved forager bees exhibit heightened proboscis extension responses (PER) to floral odors with innate appetitive value, even without rewards, reflecting increased olfactory sensitivity and feeding motivation, while fed foragers show reduced responses9. Interestingly, winter bees in our study responded to floral cues similarly to starved foragers, despite being well-nourished, suggesting that chronological age and physiological maturation may contribute to heightened appetitive motivation, alongside other contextual factors. In Drosophila melanogaster, aging impairs the formation of appetitive memories for non-nutritive sugars, while memory for nutritive sugars is preserved. This shift is supported by increased expression of neuropeptide F and the selective maintenance of dopaminergic circuits signaling nutritional value50. The situation is different in honey bees, where the age-related increase in appetitive motivation varies across castes, reflecting the interplay between aging and social organization. Foragers show consistently high appetitive responsiveness, evident in their enhanced antennal sucrose sensitivity, sucrose responsiveness, food intake, and spontaneous olfactory responsiveness. By contrast, winter bees—although they can exceed the chronological age of foragers—show only a partial increase: antennal sucrose sensitivity is reduced relative to foragers, and food consumption is lower under comparable experimental conditions. These findings suggest that aging can enhance appetitive motivation in social insects, but the resulting profiles differ with life history and social role.

In addition to sNPF, other neuromodulators may independently or synergistically influence appetitive behaviors and motivational states in nurse and winter bees, as has been observed in foragers14. For example, dopamine (DA), a biogenic amine, is closely associated with the “wanting” system during food-seeking behavior in foragers, with elevated DA levels in the brain—especially in foragers flying towards a profitable known food source and in foragers performing the waggle dance to communicate the location of a profitable food source51. Conversely, nurse bees in queenright colonies exhibit significantly lower DA levels52. Interestingly, in queenless colonies, DA levels in nurse bees rise to match those of foragers, suggesting that queen presence and pheromones modulate the DA pathway, potentially regulating nurse bee behavior52. The role of DA signaling in winter bees remains unclear; given its link to appetitive behavior in foragers but not nurses, winter bees might similarly exhibit intermediate DA levels.

Octopamine (OA), another biogenic amine, is elevated in the brain of foragers compared to that of nurse bees, and is primarily involved in reward processing and activating learning-dependent circuits53. This elevation strongly influences recruitment communication54,55. Experimentally supplementing nurse bees with OA accelerates their transition from in-hive tasks to foraging, provided they are of adequate age56, indicating a direct regulatory role of OA signaling in division of labor54,56. During winter, OA neurons innervating flight muscles are essential for thermoregulatory functions57; however, the contribution of OA signaling to appetitive behaviors in winter bees remains unclear.

Leucokinin, another neuropeptide, enhances sucrose sensitivity in Apis cerana. Foragers express higher levels of leucokinin receptors (Lkr) in their antennae and brains compared to nurse bees58. Blocking the Lkr gene raises the sucrose sensitivity threshold, indicating its functional importance58. In our study, nurse bees showed similar sucrose responsiveness across treatments, likely reflecting their lower Lkr expression.

Taken together, appetitive motivation and related behaviors in honey bees likely emerge from a complex, finely tuned interplay among multiple neuromodulators acting within individuals—including, but not limited to sNPF—that regulate sensory processing and behavioral flexibility in response to external social signals. Our findings provide new insights into the neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying gustatory and olfactory processing in honey bees with distinct behavioral roles. We demonstrate that sNPF significantly modulates key appetitive behaviors by interacting with both internal motivational states and external environmental cues.

To date, the most pronounced effects of sNPF have been observed in summer foragers—bees actively engaged in resource collection—highlighting the pivotal role of this neuropeptide in enhancing foraging efficiency and supporting colony sustenance. In contrast, the effects of sNPF in winter and nurse bees appear to be limited to specific behavioral responses, reflecting the unique physiological states and social demands associated with these roles. Notably, our data indicate that chronological age is a likely and important contributor to variation in appetitive behaviors, but not a sufficient standalone explanation across all traits examined. For instance, winter bees responded to sucrose and floral odors in a manner comparable to summer foragers, and older nurse bees consumed sucrose solution at levels similar to foragers. These patterns are consistent with an influence of age-related physiological maturation, while also reflecting the combined effects of seasonal context, experience, and internal state.

Overall, our data show that appetitive motivation comprises multiple components, and that both phenotype and seasonal context can differentially modulate peripheral sensory sensitivity, central neural responsiveness, associative learning, and food consumption behavior in honey bees. Future studies should explore whether social cues within the hive—particularly those encountered by nurses and foragers during the summer—modulate sNPF signaling, which in turn could promote social interactions. Such research would advance our understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms linking individual motivation to the regulation of colony-level needs in eusocial insects.

Methods

Insect collection

The honeybees used in this study were sampled in the apiary of our laboratory. Winter and nurse bees were collected directly from their hives. Winter bees were collected by inserting a glass vial into a circular opening in the ceiling of the wooden hives; bees attracted to the light entered the vial and were trapped inside. As experiments involving winter bees were conducted between January and February, we estimate that these individuals were approximately four to five months old. This estimate is based on established knowledge of the winter bee phenotype13,18, as brood rearing typically ceases in the preceding autumn, between September and October. Nurse bees were obtained by exposing frames with brood and selecting individuals engaged in brood care, specifically those performing cell inspections. We estimate that this group of bees was 5–6 days old, as cell-checking behavior is most frequently observed in nurse bees within this age range59. Bees were collected from four different hives to avoid colony-specific bias. Summer foragers (≈21 days old59) were trained to visit an artificial feeder at our apiary and were collected upon landing. They did not exploit alternative food sources, as confirmed by consistent round trips between their hive and the feeder. Accordingly, their crops were expected to be nearly empty upon arrival, in line with previous studies34–37. These works show that departing bees carry only the fuel required for the trip, thereby preserving crop capacity for nectar transport. Beyond crop load, the physiological state of foragers is critical. As the oldest workers in summer, they have limited fat reserves and undergo marked endocrine changes during the nurse-to-forager transition. Juvenile hormone levels increase with age and peak at foraging onset, whereas vitellogenin declines from high levels in nurses to near absence in foragers60,61. Insulin signaling also increases, with elevated expression of insulin-like peptides and their receptors in the brain, while lipid stores decrease62–64. Together, these changes enhance responsiveness to food-related stimuli, a process linked to sNPF–sNPFR signaling16,47. Old and young nurse bees were prepared under laboratory conditions (more details are provided below).

Summer forager bees data

Part of the data for summer forager bees used in this study were obtained from Bestea et al.9 (access code: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.15134859). To ensure a meaningful comparison among the three behavioral phenotypes, we selected the dataset of starved foragers, representing the group with the highest motivation to engage in appetitive behaviors. The summer forager bees used in the appetitive olfactory associative learning assays were collected after being trained to visit an artificial feeder providing 40% (w/w) sucrose solution. To ensure a high motivational state and low satiety, bees were captured upon landing at the feeder.

Testing the appetitive behavioral profiles across bee phenotypes

Following sampling, summer forager, winter, and nurse bees were taken to the laboratory and either harnessed in individual tubes or placed inside individual syringes depending on the experiments conducted. We transported the bees to the laboratory and placed them on ice for 5 min to facilitate preparation. The behavioral readout of each appetitive experiment is described below.

Electrophysiological characterization of antennal sucrose sensitivity

Electrophysiological recordings were conducted on antennal taste sensilla (sensilla chaetica38), which are readily identifiable based on their external morphology65. These sensilla are located in the ventral zone of the antennal tip39. Recordings were performed using the classical contact-stimulation technique, in which the same electrode serves simultaneously as both stimulating and recording electrode. Accordingly, the stimulation solution (sucrose) contained a low-concentration electrolyte (KCl 1 mM = KCl 0.007% w/v) to ensure electrical conductivity.

Single sensillum recordings were obtained using a glass electrode (external diameter 10–20 µm) pulled from borosilicate capillaries and positioned over an individual taste sensillum. An Ag wire inserted into the contralateral eye served as the reference electrode. Stimulating electrodes, filled with the test solutions, were stored in a humid chamber before use. Stimuli were applied for approximately 2 s with an interstimulus interval of 1 min.

The recording and reference electrodes were connected to a preamplifier (Taste Probe, SYNTECH, Kirchzarten, Germany). Electrical signals were amplified (×10) with a signal connection interface box (SYNTECH) and filtered with a 100–3000 Hz bandpass filter. Contact of the recording electrode with the sensillum triggered data acquisition (sampling rate: 10 kHz), and recordings were stored for offline analysis. Data were processed using Autospikes software, and responses were quantified by counting the number of spikes after stimulus onset during the first second (spike/s). Stimuli consisted of sucrose solutions (1, 10, 30, and 100 mM) prepared in 1 mM KCl, which was also used as a control. Solutions were stored at –4 °C until use. Each sensillum was sequentially stimulated with this sucrose series to obtain a dose-response curve.

Sucrose responsiveness

Bees were harnessed in individual tubes with only the head protruding to allow measurement of their sucrose responsiveness. Prior to the start of the tests, we offered water ad libitum directly to their mouthparts to minimize the chances of them responding to water during sucrose presentations. We started the experiments 30 min after the preparation of the bees. We presented each bee with increasing concentrations of sucrose solution. We followed a standard protocol40,41, presenting concentrations of 0.1%, 0.3%, 1%, 3%, 10%, and 30% (w/v) to the antennae of the harnessed bees using toothpicks41,66,67. We used distilled water to stimulate the antennae between sucrose trials to avoid sensitization. We calculated an individual sucrose responsiveness score for each bee based on the number of sucrose concentrations to which it responded with PER. A score of 1 indicated a response only to the highest sucrose concentration (30%), while a score of 6 indicated a response to all six sucrose concentrations. After delivering the six sucrose concentrations, we stimulated each bee with an additional 50% sucrose solution to assess proboscis extension reflex (PER) integrity. Bees that exhibited PER in response to water stimulation showed inconsistent responses (e.g., PER to 0.01%, 0.03%, 1%, and 3% but not to 10%68) or failed to respond to the 50% sucrose solution were excluded from the study. The sample sizes were as follows: nurse bees (26), summer foragers (25), and winter bees (58).

Appetitive olfactory spontaneous responsiveness

Bees were harnessed as in the previous experiment to quantify their appetitive olfactory spontaneous responsiveness. We quantified PER to linalool and 2-phenylethanol, two floral odors known to evoke spontaneous PER in bees9,42. We purchased the odorants from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint-Quentin Fallavier, France). We conducted the assays 30 min after finishing the preparation of the bees, following the protocol of Bestea et al.9. For olfactory stimulation, we positioned the bees between an olfactory stimulator delivering continuous clean airflow to the antennae and an air extractor69. This setup allowed for controlled odorant pulses while preventing odor accumulation. During odor exposure, each bee initially received 16 s of continuous clean airflow, followed by 6 s of odor stimulation, and then another 23 s of clean airflow. We delivered the odorants by activating valves that redirected air towards a glass vial containing 2 ml of the pure odor. We recorded the occurrence of spontaneous PER during olfactory stimulation as 1 when bees extended their proboscis and as 0 when they did not. Both odors were presented to each bee in a randomized sequence, with a 35-min interval between the two stimulations9. We excluded bees from the final analysis if they did not perform PER to antennal stimulation with the 50% sucrose solution after the olfactory test. None of the tested bees responded to airflow alone. The sample sizes were as follows: nurse bees (264), summer foragers (120) and winter bees (48).

Appetitive olfactory conditioning

Forager, nurse and winter bees were individually harnessed in tube holders and fed with 5 µL of a 30% (w/v) sucrose solution. After resting for 2 h, the bees’ antennae were stimulated with a toothpick dipped in 30% sucrose solution to verify the presence of the appetitive proboscis extension reflex (PER). Bees that did not respond to this sucrose stimulation were excluded from further steps43. Olfactory conditioning began approximately 30 min after antennal stimulation.

Bees underwent absolute olfactory conditioning to associate a single odorant—the conditioned stimulus (CS)—with a 50% (w/v) sucrose solution, serving as the unconditioned stimulus (US). The odorants used as CS were either 2-hexanol or 1-nonanal (Sigma-Aldrich Chimie, Saint Quentin-Fallavier, France), both of which are considered neutral and non-biologically relevant for honeybees, with minimal cross-generalization70. Odor delivery was automated via a microcontroller system (Arduino® Uno, Tilburg, North Brabant, The Netherlands) connected to glass vials containing the odorants. The setup was positioned in front of each bee.

To ensure the CS was neutral at the start of conditioning, bees exhibiting spontaneous responses to the odorant during its first presentation were excluded from the experiment. Conditioning consisted of four CS–US pairings with an average intertrial interval (ITI) of 10 min. Each trial began by placing the bee 15 cm in front of an exhaust system to remove residual odors. Following a 15-s acclimation, the CS was presented for 6 s. The US was delivered 5 s after CS onset, by lightly touching the antennae with a toothpick dipped in sucrose solution, eliciting the proboscis extension response and allowing feeding. This created a 5-s interstimulus interval with a 1-s overlap between CS and US. After stimulation, the bee remained in place for 16 s before removal. Each trial lasted 39 s. In addition to the learning performance, we also registered the consistency to US responsiveness across conditioning trials for all the tested bees as a proxy to access their motivation during this phase.

One hour after the final conditioning trial, a memory retention test was performed. Bees were presented with the CS and a novel odorant (NOd) in the absence of the US to assess memory specificity43. Bees conditioned with 2-hexanol received 1-nonanal as the NOd, and vice versa. Test odorants were presented in a randomized order for each subject. The sample sizes were as follows: nurse bees (93), summer foragers (116) and winter bees (56).

Food consumption

We performed food consumption assays following the protocol of Bestea et al.9. A pipette with a cut tip was fitted into the hub of an enclosing syringe and filled with either 100 µL of a 20% sucrose solution or 100 µL of a 20% sucrose solution supplemented with 100 mM salicin9,33. After 1 h, we measured the volume consumed by each bee from the three different groups using a 200 µL pipette. Salicin was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint-Quentin Fallavier, France). The sample sizes were as follows: Consumption of 20% sucrose solution: nurse bees (59), summer foragers (26) and winter bees (37); Consumption of 20% sucrose solution spiked with 100 mM salicin: nurse bees (53), summer foragers (29) and winter bees (46).

sNPF modulation of appetitive responses in winter and nurse bees

Upon collection, we divided nurse and winter bees into four groups, similar to the protocol of Bestea et al.9, with the exception that no “starved” group was included. This choice reflects the fact that both winter and nurse bees possess substantial internal energetic reserves and therefore cannot be reliably starved13. We transported bees to the laboratory and placed them on ice for 5 min to facilitate handling. Each bee was then harnessed in an individual holder. For Sucrose Responsiveness and Appetitive Olfactory Spontaneous Responsiveness assays, topical treatments were applied after harnessing. For the honey bees used in the Food Consumption assays, topical treatments were administered prior to restraining them in individual syringes. We left one group of bees without treatment (‘untreated’). We topically treated two groups with a 1 µL of sNPF solution on their thorax: one group received sNPF at a concentration of 1 µg/µL (‘sNPF-1’), while the other received it at a concentration of 10 µg/µL (“sNPF-10”). The fourth group (“solvent”) received a topical application of the solvent used to dissolve sNPF, consisting of a mixture of 20% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 80% acetone (as in Bestea et al.9,15). We used different individuals across the three experiments that we performed. The behavioral tests always started approximately 30 min following the treatment phase9,15.

The behavioral readout used to measure appetitive motivation in winter and nurse treated bees follow the same steps as presented in the previous section (1. Disentangling the effects of behavioral phenotype and age on food consumption

We established a single-cohort colony following standard methods44. Approximately 1200, 1-day-old workers, obtained from a source colony, were marked on the thorax with paint within 24 h of emergence and introduced into a hive containing a mated queen and one honey/pollen frame. The colony had outdoor access for natural foraging.

Two age groups were sampled to disentangle the effects of age and task specialization. Sampling was conducted at two time points: (1) 5–6 days after introducing the bees into the experimental hive, marked workers engaged in cell inspection/brood tending were collected and assigned to the young nurse group; and (2) 14–16 days after hive introduction, marked workers engaged in cell inspection/brood tending were collected and assigned to the old nurse group, while marked foragers trained to collect sucrose solution at an artificial feeder were sampled upon landing.

Upon arrival in the laboratory, all bees were provided with 5 µL of honeydew. They were then divided into two groups: one group received no additional food for 4 h, while the other had ad libitum access to 50% sucrose solution during the same period. After 4 h, the food consumption assay was conducted. Thus, bees differed in feeding status: unfed (no food supplementation for 4 h) and fed (ad libitum access to 50% sucrose for 4 h). Consumption was quantified by offering each bee a capillary tip containing 100 µL of either 50% sucrose solution or 50% sucrose solution supplemented with 100 mM salicin for 1 h. The food consumption assay was an adapted version of what was presented above.

Statistics and reproducibility

Antennal sucrose sensitivity (Fig. 1) was assessed by counting the number of spikes produced by antennal sensilla during the first second of stimulation with sucrose solutions of different concentrations. The number of individuals and sensilla from which these recordings were obtained are: nurse bees = 3 bees/10 sensilla; summer foragers = 7 bees/16 sensilla; winter bees = 11 bees/24 sensilla.

For appetitive behavioral responsiveness and learning, the behavioral readout used was the proboscis extension response (PER), recorded as a binary outcome (1 or 0) in response to the US (sucrose responsiveness and learning experiment), the CS (learning and memory experiment), or floral odorants (appetitive olfactory spontaneous responsiveness experiment). The percentage of bees responding to the US, CS, or floral odorants was calculated and presented as a population-level measure. In addition, individual scores were computed based on the number of PERs elicited across multiple stimulations (either US or CS). For each bee, a sucrose responsiveness score was calculated, representing the number of sucrose concentrations to which the bee responded with PER. In conditioning assays, a learning score was calculated as the number of CS presentations to which a bee responded with PER. Food consumption was calculated by subtracting the amount of sucrose left in the tip after 1 h of experiment by the initial amount of sucrose offered 100 μL. For the sucrose responsiveness assays, the sample sizes were as follows: Fig. 2, nurses = 26; summer foragers = 25; winter bees = 58; Fig. 5, winter bees, untreated = 58; solvent = 54; sNPF1 = 62; sNPF10 = 52; nurse bees, untreated = 26; solvent = 24; sNPF1 = 25; sNPF10 = 31. For the olfactory conditioning assays (Fig. 3), the sample sizes were as follows: nurses = 119; summer foragers = 147; winter bees = 78 (before filtering) and nurses = 93; summer foragers = 116; winter bees = 56 (after filtering). For the spontaneous olfactory responsiveness assays, the sample sizes were as follows: Fig. 2, nurses = 264; summer foragers = 120; winter bees = 48; Fig. 6, winter bees, untreated = 48; solvent = 64; sNPF1 = 64; sNPF10 = 64; nurse bees, untreated = 264; solvent = 248; sNPF1 = 158; sNPF10 = 154.

For food consumption assays, the sample sizes were as follows: Fig. 4, 20% sucrose solution, nurses = 59; summer foragers= 26; winter bees = 37; 20% sucrose supplemented with 100 mM salicin, nurses = 53; summer foragers = 29; winter bees = 46; Fig. 7, winter bees presented with a 20% sucrose solution, untreated = 37; solvent = 37; sNPF1 = 43; sNPF10 = 42; winter bees presented with a 20% sucrose solution supplemented with 100 mM salicin, untreated = 46; solvent = 47; sNPF1 = 40; sNPF10 = 30; nurse bees presented with a 20% sucrose solution, untreated = 59; solvent = 29; sNPF1 = 31; sNPF10 = 31; nurse bees presented with a 20% sucrose solution supplemented with 100 mM salicin, untreated = 53; solvent = 28; sNPF1 = 35; sNPF10 = 43.

To assess whether the number of spikes generated by antennal gustatory receptors differed among bee groups as a function of sucrose concentration, we fitted a generalized linear model (GLM) with a negative binomial distribution, as the Poisson model showed overdispersion. The model included spike count as the dependent variable and tested the interaction between bee group and sucrose concentration as fixed factors.

To evaluate population-level performance differences among groups for sucrose responsiveness and appetitive olfactory conditioning, we used either a binomial generalized linear model (GLM) or a binomial generalized mixed model (GLMM) with a logit link function. Sucrose responsiveness scores, treated as count data, were analyzed using Poisson GLM. Model fit was assessed, and overdispersion was checked; dispersion values were below 1.5, indicating no substantial overdispersion. For the appetitive olfactory spontaneous responsiveness experiment, treatment effects on proportions were assessed using a binomial GLM with a logit link.

Food consumption data were analyzed using either linear models (LMs), linear mixed models (LMMs), GLMs or GLMMs with a Gamma distribution when appropriate, based on data distribution. Bee response and total amount consumed were entered as dependent variable, whereas behavioral caste or treatment were entered as fixed factors. Data, hive, and bee ID were entered as random factors whenever appropriate.

Pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means (emmeans) with Tukey correction to adjust for multiple testing where applicable. All analyses were conducted in R Studio (version 2023.06.1) using packages lme471 for fitting GLMs, GLMMs, and emmeans72 for estimated marginal means and pairwise comparisons, multcomp73 for multiple testing corrections, and the base stats package74 for general modeling functions.
