The association between hyperlipidemia and colorectal adenoma risk: a multivariable clinical study based on lipoprotein profile stratification
Authors: Wei Wen, Longfei Liao, Kaixi Ou, Chongling Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) continues to rise, with colorectal adenoma(CRA) being its primary precancerous lesion. Recent studies suggest that hyperlipidemia may promote adenoma development by influencing cell membrane structure, cholesterol metabolism, and inflammatory responses. However, its independent role in the adenoma stage remains unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between hyperlipidemia and colorectal adenoma(CRA) risk and to evaluate the dose–response relationship based on lipoprotein profile stratification.MethodsThis single-center retrospective case-control study included 180 patients with colorectal adenoma(CRA) and 80 colonoscopy-negative controls. An additional 80 patients with pathologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma (CRAC) were included as a secondary exploratory comparison group. Demographic characteristics and lipid parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), were collected. Univariate and multivariable unconditional logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations between lipid abnormalities and colorectal adenoma, with adjustment for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, and family history of colorectal cancer. Variance inflation factors were calculated to assess multicollinearity. Quartile stratification and restricted cubic spline models were used to explore dose–response patterns, and E-value analysis was performed to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding.ResultsHigh TC, high TG, high LDL-C, and low HDL-C were significantly associated with the overall presence of CRA in univariate analysis. After adjustment for potential confounders, these associations remained statistically significant. Quartile stratification showed that higher levels of TC, TG, and LDL-C were associated with progressively higher odds of colorectal adenoma, whereas HDL-C showed an inverse association. The RCS models suggested steeper increases in adenoma odds at higher TC and LDL-C levels, while HDL-C showed an approximately linear inverse association. Although high TG was associated with overall adenoma occurrence, TG levels did not differ significantly across adenoma histological subtypes. Subgroup analyses suggested that smoking, obesity, and family history may modify the associations between dyslipidemia and colorectal adenoma.ConclusionHyperlipidemia was independently associated with CRA in this retrospective case-control study. A comprehensive dyslipidemic pattern, characterized by elevated TC, TG, and LDL-C and reduced HDL-C, was observed among patients with CRA. Lipoprotein profiles may serve as accessible clinical indicators for colorectal adenoma risk stratification. However, because of the retrospective case-control design, causal inference and temporal relationships cannot be established, and the possibility of reverse causality should be considered.

Introduction

Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) is a highly prevalent malignant tumor of the digestive tract worldwide, with colorectal adenocarcinoma (CRA) being its primary pathological type. Clinical epidemiological studies indicate that the incidence and mortality of CRC demonstrate regional and population-specific increasing trends [1–4]. The adenoma–carcinoma sequence has been confirmed as the predominant pathological pathway for CRC development [5, 6]. Traditional risk factors include a high-fat diet, obesity, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and familial genetic predisposition. However, the independent role of metabolic risk factors in CRC pathogenesis remains incompletely defined [7–9].Dyslipidemia, a core phenotype of metabolic syndrome (MetS), encompasses elevated total cholesterol (TC), elevated triglycerides (TG), elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). It exhibits significant associations with the risk of multiple solid tumors [10–15]. Mechanistic studies suggest that abnormal lipid metabolism may promote malignant transformation of adenomas and progression of adenocarcinoma through various pathways, including DNA damage and chromosomal instability induced by lipid peroxidation products, activation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways via oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation, as well as regulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis, and tumor microenvironment remodeling through the cholesterol metabolism-related fibroblast growth factor 19/fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGF19/FGFR4) axis [16–18].Previous epidemiological studies have reported substantial heterogeneity in findings, with some yielding biased results due to insufficient sample size, inadequate adjustment for confounders, inconsistent exposure definitions, or lack of stratified analyses based on lipid profiles. Consequently, whether hyperlipidemia constitutes an independent risk factor for CRA remains controversial.This case-control study aims to systematically evaluate the independent effect of hyperlipidemia on CRA development by integrating lipid profile stratification with multivariable logistic regression models. It further seeks to clarify the differential impacts of various dyslipidemia components on CRA–CRAC progression risk and explore their clinical screening and risk prediction value, thereby providing evidence-based insights for CRC primary prevention and early risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center, retrospective case-control study conducted at our hospital between February 2023 and February 2024. The primary analysis included 180 patients with CRA diagnosed by colonoscopy and pathological examination and 80 colonoscopy-negative controls during the same period. The control group consisted of individuals with no neoplastic lesions or chronic inflammatory bowel disease on colonoscopy, and all control subjects received colonoscopy for asymptomatic routine physical screening or familial high-risk colorectal cancer screening; patients undergoing colonoscopy due to clinical gastrointestinal symptoms such as hematochezia, abdominal pain and altered bowel habits were excluded from the control cohort. In addition, 80 patients with pathologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma(CRAC) were included as a secondary exploratory comparison group to evaluate lipid-related associations across different stages of colorectal neoplasia.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) complete clinical data and lipid profile results; and (3) fasting for at least 12 h before blood biochemical examination. The exclusion criteria were: (1) diseases that could substantially affect lipid metabolism, including diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, hepatic failure, or renal failure; (2) documented use of lipid-lowering drugs, including statins or fibrates, corticosteroids, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within 6 months before lipid testing and colonoscopy; (3) history of other malignancies; and (4) incomplete clinical or laboratory data. Long-term historical use of lipid-lowering medications before the 6-month window was not systematically available in the retrospective records.

Individual matching between cases and controls was not performed. Instead, colonoscopy-negative controls were selected from the same study period, and age and sex were adjusted for in the multivariable regression models to reduce potential confounding. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The People’s Hospital of Weiyuan, Sichuan, China, with the ethics approval number 20,221,105. Because this was a retrospective study using anonymized clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Data collection

Clinical and laboratory data of study subjects were extracted from the electronic medical record system and clinical laboratory database. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol consumption, family history of colorectal cancer, comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, metabolic syndrome), and medication history (especially lipid-lowering drugs, corticosteroids, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs). Laboratory indicators included routine blood biochemical test data collected during pre-enrollment physical examinations or hospitalization periods. All tests were performed using venous blood samples collected after ≥ 12 h of fasting in the morning, analyzed by standardized automated biochemical analyzers in the same central laboratory. Lipid-related indicators comprised total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), with derived indices calculated including non-HDL-C (TC − HDL-C) and TG/HDL-C ratio. To ensure data completeness and consistency, all case and control records were cross-checked by two independent researchers, with discrepancies resolved through third-party review. Missing data handling followed predefined statistical principles: individuals lacking key variables (lipid indicators or outcome variables) were excluded; for non-critical variables with < 10% missing rate, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied to maximize analytical validity. We retrospectively reviewed colonoscopy indications of all enrolled controls via electronic medical records to confirm the exclusion of symptomatic subjects with abdominal pain, hematochezia and other digestive discomfort.

Definition of hyperlipidemia and lipid profile stratification

The diagnosis of hyperlipidemia was based on the US NCEP-ATP III criteria [19]. Specific diagnostic thresholds were as follows: (1)TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L; (2)TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L༛(3)LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L༛(4)HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L for males and < 1.3 mmol/L for females. Subjects meeting any of the above criteria were defined as having hyperlipidemia.

For lipid profile stratification, detailed subgroups were created based on the type and combination of dyslipidemia: (1)isolated high TG; (2)isolated high TG; (3)isolated high LDL-C; (4)isolated low HDL-C; (5)mixed dyslipidemia (presence of two or more abnormalities). To explore the dose-response relationship, each lipid parameter was stratified into quartiles (Q1–Q4), with Q1 as the reference group. Risks of colorectal adenocarcinoma associated with different quartiles were compared sequentially, and trend tests were performed.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and Stata 17.0. Continuous variables were tested for normality and expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the t-test, ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariable unconditional logistic regression analyses were used to assess associations between lipid parameters and colorectal adenoma. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, and family history of colorectal cancer. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF > 5.0 indicating potential multicollinearity. Sensitivity analyses were performed using separate multivariable models for each lipid fraction. Dose–response associations were assessed by quartile stratification and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models. RCS models used four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles, with the median value as reference. Rug plots were added to show lipid value distributions. E-value analysis was performed to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. Histological subgroup analyses were considered exploratory because of limited subgroup sample size. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics

No statistically significant differences were observed between the CRA patient group and the healthy control group in terms of age, sex, or BMI (all P > 0.05). The lipid profile abnormalities were more pronounced in CRA patients: TG, TC, and LDL-C levels were significantly higher than those in the control group (all P < 0.001), while HDL-C levels were significantly lower (P < 0.001). Lipid levels across different CRA pathological types

Comparison of clinical characteristics among CRA patients with tubular adenoma, mixed adenoma, and villous adenoma revealed significant differences in age, sex, and BMI among the three groups (P < 0.05). Regarding lipid levels, TC gradually increased with the increasing villous component in the adenoma histology (Tubular: 4.65 ± 0.51 mmol/L, Mixed: 4.88 ± 0.40 mmol/L, Villous: 5.00 ± 0.45 mmol/L, P = 0.001), suggesting that TC levels may be closely related to adenoma pathological progression. HDL-C also differed among the adenoma types (P = 0.027), with the mixed adenoma group showing the lowest HDL-C level (0.69 ± 0.14 mmol/L). No significant differences were found in TG and LDL-C levels among the three groups (P > 0.05). See Table 2.

Hyperlipidemia and CRA risk

Univariate logistic regression results indicated a significant association between hyperlipidemia and the occurrence of CRA. In the adenoma group, high TC, high TG, high LDL-C, and low HDL-C were all associated with an increased risk of adenoma development. Among these, low HDL-C (OR = 4.00, 95% CI: 2.15–7.44, P < 0.001) and high TG (OR = 3.67, 95% CI: 2.04–6.58, P < 0.001) showed the most significant effects, suggesting that dyslipidemia may play an important role in the adenoma stage. Among other risk factors, smoking was significantly associated with an increased adenoma risk (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.10–3.58, P = 0.022). Although alcohol consumption showed a trend towards increased risk, it did not reach statistical significance. A family history of colorectal cancer was also an independent risk factor for adenoma (OR = 2.67, 95% CI: 1.12–6.37, P = 0.027). Age ≥ 60 years and BMI ≥ 24 kg/m² showed consistent directions of association with adenoma risk in the univariate analysis but the differences were not statistically significant. See Table 3.

Univariate logistic regression analysis of hyperlipidemia and related risk factors with CRA and CRAC risk

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Results from the multivariate logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for confounding factors such as age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, and family history, demonstrated that hyperlipidemia-related indicators were significant independent risk factors at the adenoma stage. Specifically, high TC (aOR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.08–3.75, P = 0.027), high TG (aOR = 2.85, 95% CI: 1.56–5.21, P = 0.001), high LDL-C (aOR = 2.93, 95% CI: 1.60–5.36, P < 0.001), and low HDL-C (aOR = 3.22, 95% CI: 1.64–6.33, P = 0.001) were all independently associated with an increased risk of adenoma development. This suggests that dyslipidemia plays an important role in the early stages of adenoma formation and progression. Besides lipid indicators, a family history of colorectal cancer was also an independent risk factor for adenoma (aOR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.08–6.02, P = 0.033), and smoking was significantly associated with adenoma risk (aOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.01–3.08, P = 0.046). Age, sex, BMI, and alcohol consumption did not show independent effects in the multivariate model. See Table 4.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of hyperlipidemia and related risk factors with colorectal adenoma and adenocarcinoma risk

To evaluate potential multicollinearity among variables included in the multivariable logistic regression model, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was performed. The VIF values were 1.12 for age ≥ 60 years, 1.08 for male sex, 1.21 for BMI ≥ 24 kg/m², 1.18 for smoking, 1.16 for alcohol consumption, 1.05 for positive family history, 2.46 for high TC, 1.39 for high TG, 2.83 for high LDL-C, and 1.44 for low HDL-C. All VIF values were below 5.0, suggesting that severe multicollinearity was not present in the multivariable model.

Considering the biological and statistical correlation among lipid fractions, particularly between TC and LDL-C, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted using separate multivariable logistic regression models for each lipid component. Each lipid variable was entered individually into the model and adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, and family history of colorectal cancer. The associations remained consistent with the primary analysis: high TC was associated with colorectal adenoma (aOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.09–3.54, P = 0.025), high TG was associated with colorectal adenoma (aOR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.55–5.03, P = 0.001), high LDL-C was associated with colorectal adenoma (aOR = 2.88, 95% CI: 1.59–5.23, P < 0.001), and low HDL-C was associated with colorectal adenoma (aOR = 3.18, 95% CI: 1.66–6.09, P = 0.001). These findings suggest that the observed associations were not solely driven by multicollinearity among lipid variables.

Lipid profile stratification and dose-response relationship

To investigate the dose-response pattern between lipid levels and colorectal adenoma risk, this study stratified lipid indicators into quartiles (Q1–Q4), with Q1 as the reference. The results showed that for TC, TG, and LDL-C in adenoma patients, the risk increased significantly in a gradient manner with rising quartiles, and the trend tests were statistically significant (P < 0.001). Specifically, individuals in the highest quartile (Q4) had approximately 2.8 times (TC), 3.2 times (TG), and 3.5 times (LDL-C) higher adenoma risk compared to the Q1 group. HDL-C exhibited an opposite trend: higher HDL-C levels were associated with lower adenoma risk, showing an approximately linear negative correlation. Individuals in the highest HDL-C quartile (Q4) had approximately a 58% lower adenoma risk (OR ≈ 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22–0.80) compared to the Q1 group, with a significant trend (P < 0.01). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models further revealed nonlinear characteristics: the curves for TC and LDL-C were relatively flat at low to medium levels but rose rapidly after exceeding thresholds (TC > 5.2 mmol/L, LDL-C > 3.4 mmol/L). The TG curve showed an overall positive relationship. The HDL-C curve declined steadily without significant nonlinear inflection points, indicating a continuous protective effect. E-value analysis yielded minimum E-values of 2.27 (TC), 2.91 (TG), 3.05 (LDL-C) and 3.38 (HDL-C), verifying that the core associations were robust to moderate unmeasured confounding. Discussion

This single-center retrospective case-control study systematically evaluated the association between dyslipidemia and the risk of CRA. The results indicate that hyperlipidemia at the CRA stage manifests as a comprehensive lipid profile abnormality, including elevated TC, TG, and LDL-C, as well as significantly reduced HDL-C. These indicators were independently associated with an increased risk of adenoma development, suggesting that lipid metabolism disorders play an important role in the formation of adenomas.Further stratified analysis and RCS model results demonstrated a dose-response relationship for TC, TG, and LDL-C, with risk increasing progressively as their levels rise. The risk escalated significantly particularly when TC levels exceeded 5.2 mmol/L and LDL-C exceeded 3.4 mmol/L, while TG showed a linear positive correlation with adenoma risk. In contrast, HDL-C exhibited a continuous and stable protective effect; higher levels were associated with lower risk, and the RCS curve declined approximately linearly without significant inflection points. This suggests that its protective effect is not dependent on a specific threshold but operates across the entire range of levels to counteract risk.Beyond the impact of the lipid profile itself, this study also observed interactions between traditional risk factors such as smoking, obesity, and family history with dyslipidemia, which further amplified the contribution of dyslipidemia to adenoma risk [20]. Specifically, the risk effects of high LDL-C and low HDL-C were particularly pronounced among smokers, indicating that tobacco-related oxidative stress and inflammatory responses may synergize with lipid metabolism disorders to promote adenoma formation. The association between high TG levels and adenoma risk was more prominent in obese individuals, suggesting that insulin resistance and fatty acid metabolism imbalance in the obese state may promote adenoma formation through the triglyceride pathway. The link between high LDL-C and adenoma risk was more significant in individuals with a family history of colorectal cancer, implying that a genetic susceptibility background may interact with cholesterol metabolism pathways in the pathogenesis of adenomas.

The findings of this study indicate that dyslipidemia is significantly associated with colorectal adenoma risk. Particularly, elevated LDL-C and reduced HDL-C levels emerged as independent risk factors, aligning closely with multiple previous epidemiological and clinical studies [21–24]. Large-scale cohort studies have suggested that high LDL-C promotes cholesterol deposition in cell membranes, altering membrane fluidity and receptor signal transduction, thereby enhancing cell proliferative capacity. Conversely, low HDL-C compromises reverse cholesterol transport and antioxidative functions, sustaining inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress, which creates favorable conditions for adenoma development [25–28]. The observed inverse linear correlation between HDL-C and adenoma risk coincides with findings from European prospective cohort studies [29].While LDL-C and HDL-C exhibited relatively consistent associations across studies, TC and TG showed more variable results in prior research. Consistent with our Table 4 findings that elevated TC and TG were independently associated with colorectal adenoma but irrelevant to invasive adenocarcinoma, this staged discrepancy can be reasonably interpreted by the metabolic reprogramming hypothesis of CRA-CRAC sequence supported by existing studies. At precancerous CRA stage, epithelial cells largely rely on circulating serum-derived cholesterol and triglycerides for membrane synthesis and signal activation, so hyperlipidemia serves as an important initiating driver for adenoma genesis. During malignant transformation toward CRAC, oncogenic pathways including MYC and PI3K/Akt/mTOR trigger comprehensive lipid metabolic remodeling; carcinoma cells upregulate de novo cholesterol and fatty acid biosynthesis via activating HMGCR and FASN, gradually getting rid of dependence on peripheral blood lipids, which eliminates the statistical correlation between serum TC/TG and advanced colorectal carcinoma. These discrepancies may stem from population-specific dietary patterns, metabolic profiles, and ethnic variations, implying that TC and TG predominantly influence early adenoma initiation rather than serving as persistent drivers.From molecular mechanistic perspectives, elevated LDL-C facilitates adenomagenesis through multiple pathways: Membrane cholesterol deposition alters lipid raft structure, amplifying EGFR, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling to promote proliferation/survival; Oxidized LDL triggers inflammatory responses via IL-6/TNF-α secretion, fostering chronic inflammatory microenvironments; Additionally, LDL-C elevation increases reactive oxygen species, inducing DNA damage/mutations [30–34]. In contrast, HDL-C exerts protective effects through reverse cholesterol transport to deplete intracellular cholesterol reserves; Apolipoprotein A-I-mediated antioxidative/anti-inflammatory activities neutralize ox-LDL and suppress proinflammatory cytokines; HDL-C also improves endothelial function and microcirculatory perfusion [35–37].The stage-specific roles of TC and TG may relate to metabolic reprogramming: High TC provides substrates for membrane/steroid biosynthesis during early adenoma growth, while elevated TG reflects aberrant lipid metabolism, leading to free fatty acid accumulation that drives metabolic inflammation and cellular dysfunction.Notably, synergistic interactions between dyslipidemia and smoking, obesity, or family history amplified adenoma risk. Among smokers, LDL-C elevation and HDL-C reduction demonstrated particularly strong effects; Obese individuals showed heightened TG-adenoma associations; Familial predisposition exacerbated LDL-C-related risks. These observations suggest exogenous oxidative stress (smoking), insulin resistance/chronic inflammation (obesity), and genetic background collectively potentiate dyslipidemia’s carcinogenic effects. Consistent with these subgroup interaction results, we further explored whether dyslipidemia acts as an independent synergistic factor or a mediating variable in the interactions with smoking and obesity. Existing epidemiological and mechanistic evidence supports dual effects: on one hand, smoking and visceral obesity can induce hepatic lipid dysregulation to elevate circulating TC, TG and LDL-C, whereby dyslipidemia serves as an intermediate mediator partially transmitting the carcinogenic effect of these two risk factors toward colorectal adenoma [38]; on the other hand, abnormal lipid metabolism independently promotes intestinal chronic inflammation and epithelial proliferation, synergistically amplifying adenoma risk together with smoking-related oxidative damage and obesity-driven adipokine disorder. Therefore, dyslipidemia functions as both a partial mediator and an independent synergistic contributor in the combined pathogenic pathway, which explains the excess adenoma risk among participants with combined risk exposures in our study.

This study demonstrates that dyslipidemia is significantly associated with colorectal adenoma risk, with elevated TC, TG, LDL-C and reduced HDL-C all serving as independent risk factors during the adenoma stage, suggesting potential clinical value for lipid monitoring. At the adenomatous stage, lipid abnormalities exhibit comprehensive characteristics - concurrent increases in TC/TG/LDL-C and decreased HDL-C collectively elevate risk. Therefore, systematic evaluation of lipid profiles at this stage may facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals for timely intervention and personalized surveillance, potentially interrupting adenoma-carcinoma progression.As an economical, convenient and widely available laboratory parameter, lipid profiling demonstrates excellent scalability for population applications. When combined with clinical risk factors and molecular biomarkers, it could contribute to multidimensional risk prediction models to enhance screening accuracy and efficiency for population stratification.Abnormal lipid profiles carry dual significance as both risk assessment tools and intervention targets. Previous studies suggest that lipid-lowering agents (e.g., statins, PCSK9 inhibitors) may indirectly reduce cancer risk by lowering LDL-C, while lifestyle modifications (exercise, dietary adjustment, weight management) can improve TG/HDL-C levels. Thus, lipid profiles may serve as crucial intervention points for both pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches in colorectal cancer primary and secondary prevention.From a public health perspective, incorporating lipid testing in high-risk CRC populations could complement colonoscopy screening programs, particularly in resource-limited settings. Combined with conventional clinical risk factors, serum lipid indicators have potential value for preliminary risk stratification of colorectal adenoma. However, we failed to establish a prediction model and calculate AUC in the present study, and subsequent prospective studies with modeling and ROC analysis are required to verify its incremental predictive value before applying lipids to assist screening allocation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective case-control study with a limited sample size; therefore, selection bias and information bias cannot be excluded. To reduce selection bias induced by symptomatic colonoscopy indications, we strictly limited controls to asymptomatic screening individuals and excluded patients receiving colonoscopy owing to hematochezia, abdominal pain and other gastrointestinal complaints; nevertheless, In particular, the use of hospital-based controls who underwent colonoscopy may have introduced Berkson’s bias, as these individuals may not fully represent the general healthy population. Second, the retrospective design precludes causal inference and determination of temporality. Thus, the observed associations between dyslipidemia and colorectal adenoma should not be interpreted as evidence that lipid abnormalities preceded adenoma development. Reverse causality, or protopathic bias, is also possible, as the pre-neoplastic adenoma state itself may influence systemic lipid metabolism. Third, lipid parameters were measured at a single time point, which may not reflect long-term lipid exposure or cumulative lipid burden, the latter of which may be more biologically relevant to colorectal neoplasia. Fourth, although the multivariable models adjusted for several clinically relevant covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle factors, including dietary fiber intake, red meat consumption, and physical activity, may still exist. Fifth, although patients with documented lipid-lowering medication use within the preceding 6 months were excluded, long-term historical use of statins or other lipid-lowering agents was not systematically captured; therefore, potential residual or chemopreventive effects of previous lipid-lowering therapy cannot be fully ruled out. Sixth, the unbalanced sample size between 180 adenoma cases and 80 controls, together with the adoption of covariate adjustment rather than individual matching, may decrease the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Seventh, diabetic patients and users of lipid-lowering medications were excluded to reduce lipid-related confounding. Nevertheless, this excluded high-risk populations with prominent dyslipidemia-adenoma correlation and restricts the external generalizability of our findings. Finally, the histological subgroup analysis was limited by the small number of villous adenoma cases, which may have reduced statistical power and increased the possibility of Type II error. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes, repeated lipid measurements, detailed lifestyle and medication histories, and mechanistic validation are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the temporal relationship between dyslipidemia and colorectal adenoma. Further analyses focusing on risk factor differences across different anatomical segments of the colorectum are also worthy of exploration [39].
