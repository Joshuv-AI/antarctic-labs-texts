The resuscitation team members’ lived experiences regarding futile cardiopulmonary resuscitation in children and newborn infants: A phenomenological study
Authors: Marzieh Hasanpour, Mohammad Salehpoor-Emran, Carole Kenner, Marjan Banazadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFutility is difficult to define in the context of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation , as it refers to the low likelihood of success. Healthcare providers face challenges when performing pediatric/neonatal Resuscitation. This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of resuscitation team members regarding futile Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation in children/newborns. A qualitative descriptive phenomenological approach was used with purposive sampling, resulting in 11 members of the Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation team for children/newborns (7 nurses/4 physicians). Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted online/face-to-face and analyzed using Colaizzi’s descriptive phenomenological method. Findings revealed two main themes: “useful or traumatic futile resuscitation; the paradoxical feelings,” and “the tension of attempting resuscitation despite knowing it is futile.“The first theme included “justifying the usefulness of futile resuscitation” and “examining the adverse outcomes of futile resuscitation,” while the second included “coercion to futile resuscitation for others’ interests,” “symbolic resuscitation for social-legal reasons,” and “coercion to futile resuscitation for the resuscitator’s benefit.” Participants perceived a paradox between futility and usefulness based on their values, beliefs, experiences, and interpretation of consequences. Facing this dichotomy under pressure to perform futile resuscitation created distressing experiences. Given the absence of clinical guidelines for do-not-resuscitate, developing such guidelines is essential to prevent confusion and moral distress among health professionals.Keywords: Lived Experiences, Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation, Futile, Pediatric, Neonates/Newborns, Intensive Care Unit, Phenomenology, Qualitative ResearchSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Pediatric cardiac arrest is uncommon and often leads to death or poor functional outcomes for the survivors1,2. Between 0.25% and 3% of admissions to the NICU and PICU undergo cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). The majority of CPR occurrences take place in patients under 1 year of age. The rate of CPR is tenfold greater in the NICU compared to that at birth3. Healthcare providers face challenges when performing pediatric/neonatal. These scenarios are infrequent, making them not only medically complex but also emotionally taxing4.The choice to cease resuscitative measures in cases of cardiopulmonary arrest in infants and children suffering from chronic conditions is intricate and contentious among both medical professionals and parents5.Ineffective care is intricately associated with moral distress experienced by all members of the healthcare team, resulting in job dissatisfaction, compassion fatigue, and burnout, which in turn diminishes the quality of care provided6. Research indicates that nurses frequently encounter feelings of helplessness and uncertainty regarding their ability to intervene effectively while caring for terminally ill children, a situation exacerbated by systemic obstacles such as poor teamwork, lack of involvement in decision-making, time constraints, and inadequate training7–9.

Futility is challenging to define, particularly in the context of CPR10. For this study, the low likelihood of CPR success was referred to. Deciding to end futile resuscitation efforts in children presents complex ethical and clinical considerations11. Recent research examining futile resuscitation practices has revealed adverse effects on patients, substantial moral distress among healthcare professionals12, considerable financial burdens on families, and significant strain on healthcare systems13.

International guidelines consistently outline the circumstances under which resuscitation efforts may be either withheld or terminated. The European Resuscitation Council (ERC) Guidelines 2025 on Newborn Life Support specifically address the considerations for withholding and ceasing life-sustaining treatments in newborns when resuscitation is regarded as futile14. The 2025 American Heart Association (AHA) and American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) Neonatal Resuscitation Guidelines indicate that if a detectable heart rate is not present after 20 min of high-quality resuscitation, a redirection of care may be contemplated, with decisions made in collaboration between the healthcare team and the family15. Likewise, for infants and children who are beyond the neonatal stage, the 2025 AHA/AAP Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) Guidelines offer more explicit guidance on the cessation of resuscitation, highlighting physiological targets, neurological prognostication, and the importance of shared decision-making between clinicians and families when continued resuscitation is deemed futile16.

When CPR fails to achieve therapeutic objectives or merely prolongs the dying process in critically ill children and newborns, continuation of such interventions creates an unjustifiable burden on the patient while causing profound suffering and unnecessarily prolonging agony17,18.

Background

Healthcare professionals in pediatric intensive care settings frequently encounter clinical scenarios where continued therapeutic interventions no longer provide meaningful benefit to patients. Despite the widespread nature and significant complications associated with futile care among healthcare providers, limited research has been conducted on this critical topic, with even fewer studies specifically examining futile CPR practices. Existing literature on futile care predominantly focuses on adult populations, creating substantial knowledge gaps in pediatric settings. Campwala et al. (2021) conducted a qualitative investigation exploring physicians’ decisions to terminate pediatric resuscitation, identifying five non-medical determinant categories: organizational/resource-related considerations, resuscitation-related factors, parent-related factors, patient-related factors, and physician-related factors11. Hsu et al. (2018) examined eight nurses’ experiences with futile ICU treatments phenomenologically, revealing four themes: futile treatment definition, occurrence, diagnostic criteria, and nurses’ responses19. The authors concluded that ineffective communication among physicians, nurses, and patients represents the primary challenge preventing futile treatment. Within the Iranian context, Yekefallah et al. (2015) explored 25 nurses’ experiences regarding futile care in adult ICUs through phenomenological methodology, identifying four themes: uselessness, resource waste, patient torment, and various futility aspects20. According to Okazi et al. (2014), the absence of clinical guidelines for do-not-resuscitate (DNR) orders constitutes the most significant factor contributing to futile CPR practices in Iran21, where DNR orders are not legally permitted under Islamic jurisprudence22. Additionally, to our knowledge, no studies in Iran and worldwide have focused on the resuscitation team members’ lived experiences of futile CPR in children and newborn infants. Our research aimed to fill this knowledge gap.

The study

Aims

This descriptive phenomenological study was conducted to explore the lived experiences of the resuscitation team members regarding futile CPR in children and newborn infants.

Research question

What are the lived experiences of resuscitation team members regarding futile cardiopulmonary resuscitation in children and newborn infants?

Methods

Design

A descriptive phenomenological qualitative design, grounded in Husserl’s philosophy, was used to explore the lived experiences of pediatric and neonatal CPR team members involved in the futile resuscitation phenomenon23. Descriptive phenomenology aims to explore a phenomenon of interest through the lived experience of individuals24.

Theoretical framework

Grounded in the philosophical tradition of Husserl, descriptive phenomenology aims to comprehend the essence of a phenomenon as it is experienced by individuals, highlighting intentionality, consciousness, and the suspension of researchers’ preconceived notions23. This philosophical foundation corresponds with the aim of the study, which seeks to understand the experiences of resuscitation team members regarding futile CPR in children and newborn infants.

Study setting and recruitment

This study was conducted at the Pediatric Emergency Department (PED), NICUs, and PICUs of three teaching hospitals affiliated with the Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Participants consisted of 11 CPR team members who had lived experience regarding futile CPR in children and newborn infants. They included five physicians (neonatal and pediatric specialists) and 6 nurses (neonatal and pediatric specialists). One participant initially consented to be interviewed; however, due to a demanding work schedule during the COVID-19 pandemic, he ultimately declined to participate. The inclusion criteria were: Iranians who spoke Persian and were willing to participate in the study and share their experiences. Furthermore, the inclusion criteria were as follows:1 being an active member of the resuscitation team2, having a minimum of 1 year of clinical experience in PEDs, NICUs, or PICUs at the time of data collection3,25. having directly participated in at least one futile CPR event involving a child or newborn infant.

Data collection

No relationship was established before the study began. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling with maximum variation to guarantee the involvement of individuals possessing direct and pertinent experience with futile CPR in children and newborn infants. The chief or head nurses played a crucial role in the recruitment process by identifying team members who satisfied the pre-established inclusion criteria set forth by the research team. Their gatekeeping function was strictly limited to recognizing eligible candidates and presenting the study to potential participants; they did not affect the final selection or eliminate any eligible individual based on personal or institutional biases. After being identified by the head nurse, potential participants were provided with a study information sheet and encouraged to contact to the research team on their own. This method reduced the likelihood of gatekeeper bias while maintaining the purposive nature of the sampling strategy.

The team developed the interview questions and reviewed them for clarity and appropriateness. Data were collected from April to July 2021 through in-depth face-to-face and online interviews. Due to repeated COVID-19 peaks in Iran, it was not possible to conduct all interviews in person.

A total of 11 semi-structured interviews were conducted, including two face-to-face interviews and nine synchronous virtual interviews conducted via video- and audio-based communication platforms such as WhatsApp, according to participants’ preferences. Interview appointments were arranged at mutually convenient times for all participants. To ensure methodological rigor and consistency across data collection procedures, all interviews were guided by a standardized interview protocol and conducted using an active listening approach. Allowing participants to select their preferred mode of interview may have enhanced psychological comfort and facilitated more open discussion of the emotionally sensitive phenomenon of futile resuscitation. However, the use of multiple interview modalities is recognized as a methodological limitation and was carefully considered during the analysis and interpretation of the data.

The author (MB), a highly experienced Neonatal and Pediatrics Intensive Care Unit nurse (NICU and PICU), conducted the interviews. She introduced herself to the participants and explained the study’s aims. She has completed courses in qualitative research. The interviews lasted between 34 and 97 min, averaging about 60 min. The face-to-face interviews took place in the physicians’ room adjacent to the ward, with no one else present.

Interviews began with a general question. Participants were asked to describe their experiences of attending a futile CPR. Follow-up questions were tailored based on their responses (see Table 1). Additional probing questions were asked to gain a deeper understanding of the phenomenon, such as “Please explain more? What do you mean? And could you give me an example to clarify?” Interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent.

Interview guide.

Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, indicated by the absence of new insights and repetition across participants’ responses. Ultimately, 11 interviews were conducted and transcribed verbatim immediately following each session. In phenomenological research, saturation occurs when redundancy is identified within participant narratives and the essence of the lived experience has been sufficiently captured to allow for a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon26. To protect participants’ anonymity and confidentiality, pseudonyms and codes such as Participant (P), Nurse (N), and Doctor (D), along with their numbers, were used, for example, PN1 or PD2. The researcher kept a journal to record ideas, feelings, and responses that arose during the interviews and data analysis, which helped generate new questions for subsequent participants.

Ethical consideration

The study was approved by the School of Nursing and Midwifery & Rehabilitation, Tehran University of Medical Sciences Ethical Review Board (No: 98-01-28-41494 IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1398.097). Study aims were explained. The autonomy of participants was guaranteed by acquiring written informed consent, which highlighted the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw at any moment without repercussions, and the freedom to refuse to answer any question. Confidentiality was maintained by using numerical codes (e.g., PN1, PD1), removing identifying details, and securely storing audio recordings and transcripts in password-protected files with restricted access. Access to the data was limited solely to the research team.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method, which provides a systematic approach for extracting, organizing, and describing significant statements and meanings derived from participants’ narratives27,28 including:

1. All authors performed multiple readings of transcripts to ensure a comprehensive understanding and highlighted key phrases.

2. The third author extracted significant statements about futile child/newborn CPR with confirmation from other authors using MAXQDA software10.

3. Statement meanings were formulated without the researcher’s presumptions affecting participant narratives. Given the interpretative nature of phenomenological analysis, complete elimination of researcher bias is neither feasible nor intended. The author (MB), a healthcare professional with clinical expertise in resuscitation, inevitably brought prior knowledge and preconceptions into the analytic process. To enhance methodological rigor, reflexivity was maintained throughout data collection and analysis, supported by a reflexive journal used to systematically document and critically appraise emerging assumptions. In addition, peer debriefing and member checking were conducted to enhance the credibility and confirmability of the findings, ensuring that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ accounts.

4. Formulated meanings were grouped into theme clusters and emergent themes reflecting specific issues.

5. Emerging themes were organized into comprehensive phenomenon descriptions by the third author with team confirmation.

6. Redundant findings were eliminated to develop essential structural aspects.

7. Findings were validated by participants who confirmed consistency with their experiences29. Bracketing was practiced throughout to prevent researcher bias.

Rigor and reflexivity

Rigor and trustworthiness were established using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria30:

Credibility was enhanced through persistent listening to audio recordings, deep transcript analysis, member checking by two physicians and three nurses, maximum variation sampling, and reflexivity to avoid researcher bias. Confirmability was achieved through maximum variation sampling and avoiding professional experience interference. Dependability was established through team meetings discussing findings, reviewing processes, and extracting theme clusters and emergent themes. Transferability was ensured through detailed documentation of decisions for data generation and analysis, serving as an inquiry audit for other researchers31.

Developing clusters of themes and themes from formulated meanings.

Useful or traumatic futile resuscitation; the paradoxical feelings

This theme emerged from two theme clusters: “Justifying the usefulness of futile resuscitation” and “Probing the adverse outcomes of futile resuscitation.” The findings demonstrated that participants had dual feelings about the futile resuscitation in children and newborn infants. On one hand, they not only did not consider this action futile but also considered it useful. On the other hand, they witnessed the adverse effects and consequences of futile resuscitation and inevitably deliberated on those consequences.

Justifying the usefulness of futile resuscitation

On the one hand, participants considered resuscitation useful through “Justifying futile resuscitation for the benefit of the child”, “Justifying futile resuscitation for the benefit of the family,” and “Justifying futile resuscitation for the benefit of task obligation.”

Regarding justification for the benefit of the child, participants gave reasons for the continuation of a futile resuscitation, such as the uncertain prognosis, unpredictability of the resuscitation outcome, and optimism about the possibility of a medical science miracle. Furthermore, they cited their experiences with some successful resuscitation efforts, although the resuscitation did not have the optimal outcomes eventually. They respected these efforts as useful because they kept the child alive for a longer time, even though death had occurred later. One of the participants stated:

“…Sometimes we cannot predict how much the brain problem or hypoxia is. That is, you cannot determine the prognosis 100%…” (PN10).

Regarding justification for the benefit of the family, participants believed families did not accept the child’s death easily, and they justified keeping the child alive in the interest of the family, even for a short time. During this time family was prepared for the child’s death and witnessed the maximum efforts of the healthcare team for saving the baby. Regarding this, one of the participants mentioned:

“Another participant said: “…Sometimes we know we should not resuscitate, like in the emergency room. But we do it and transfer the baby to the NICU. It’s for the parents not to think, we left the child to be expired “(PD2).

Concerning justifying futile resuscitation for the benefit of task obligation, participants continued resuscitation to fulfill their job duty and ensure doing everything to save the child because they believed they were responsible for the patient’s life (Tble 3).

Probing the adverse outcomes of futile resuscitation

On the other hand, participants deliberated on the other side of the futile resuscitation outcomes. “Being traumatic futile resuscitation” and “Wasting resources by futile resuscitation” were two sub-categories of this theme cluster.

Regarding the harmfulness of futile resuscitation, participants were concerned about adverse outcomes, including imposing child pain and suffering, delayed and torturous death, consecutive episodes of resuscitation, long hospitalization, unprogressive clinical conditions, dependence on drugs and types of equipment, recurrent infection, and the child’s poor quality of life. One participant, in this case, mentioned: “…The IV line isn’t working. The skin is lacerated. There is soreness and necrosis. The baby is suffering, we wish the poor baby would die so that s/he would not suffer so much…” (PD7).

Concerning the category of wasting resources, participants believed ongoing futile resuscitation imposed financial and human resources burdens on the family and health system. Concerns were raised regarding the occupation of ICU beds and medical equipment, as well as the depletion of healthcare providers’ energy and motivation (Table 3).

The tension of attempting resuscitation despite being aware of the futility

This theme emerged from three theme clusters consisting of “Coercion to futile resuscitation for the interests of others”, “Symbolic resuscitation for the social-legal interests,” and “Coercion to futile resuscitation for the resuscitator’s interest.” Participants felt under some restrictions and that they were forced to resuscitate the child despite the futility.

Coercion to futilely resuscitate for the interests of others

This theme cluster emerged from two formulated meanings of “Futile resuscitation under the pressure of the family” and “Futile resuscitation under the pressure of the colleagues.” Participants believed that under the pressure of some situations, they were forced to sacrifice the interests of the child for those of the family. They were scared of the anger and violence of the family and worried about being accused of a lack of knowledge and duty negligence. Fear of losing the family’s trust led participants to struggle for saving the child and continue futile resuscitation. In this regard, one of the participants explained: “…Families persist to save the child. We are forced to agree to their request and do everything. This is a moral and legal request, they are parents, we will do our best” (PD7).

Furthermore, under some limitations caused by colleagues, futile resuscitation was continued. Participants continued resuscitation under the pressure of expectations of surgeons and senior physicians for saving the child or newborn infant, and feared being accused of lacking skill. Additionally, chief physicians did not accept the responsibility of recording the “do not resuscitation” order, and this limitation forced participants to continue the futile resuscitation. As one of the Participants stated: “…It is difficult to accept the responsibility to discontinue CPR. Physicians do not order “DNR” (PD7).

The symbolic resuscitation of the social-legal interests

This theme cluster emerged from two formulated meanings of “Futile resuscitation under the pressure of legal restrictions” and “Futile resuscitation under the pressure of a therapeutic-centered atmosphere.” Fear of the family’s legal action, strict legal supervision, and increased mortality rates, as well as a lack of protocols and clinical guidelines, led participants to continue a futile resuscitation. Additionally, legal restrictions forced them to resuscitate to ensure the recording for legal documentation. As one of the Participants mentioned:

“…The family takes legal action against the doctors. They complain all treatments have not been done. There is no law to support the doctor…” (PD9).

Furthermore, based on participant experience, one of the reasons for conducting a futile resuscitation was the existing treatment-oriented atmosphere based on the expectations of the family and society to save a child or infant. As one of the participants explained:

“… sometimes, those who have only this ill baby, or were born after several years of infertility, or have a single baby and may not have children later, do not deal with losing the baby…” (PN3).

Coercion to futile resuscitation for the resuscitator’s interest

This theme cluster emerged from two formulated meanings of “Futile resuscitation under the pressure of personal motives and intentions” and “Futile resuscitation under the pressure of religious beliefs and human emotions.”

Participants continued futile resuscitation under pressure to maintain their benefits. One example was medical students’ need to learn CPR, with ongoing CPR providing opportunities to try and learn procedures in teaching hospitals. Furthermore, nurses tended to continue CPR to keep children alive because child death created a bothersome high workload from managing deceased children (Table 3).

Additionally, according to the participants’ experience, they continued the resuscitation because of their religious values, beliefs, and human emotions. As a result, they endured the tensions arising from this action. One of the participants stated:

“… Some doctors have a religious background, they believe it must be continued, and do resuscitation, for example, for 45 minutes. They repeat epinephrine doses, massaging, and ambo ventilation…” (PD11).

Discussion

The resuscitation team members’ lived experiences regarding futile CPR in children and newborn infants encompassed two emergent themes: “Useful or traumatic futile resuscitation; the paradoxical feelings” and “The tension of attempting resuscitation despite being aware of the futility.”

Participants reported dual experiences and feelings about futile pediatric and neonatal CPR. Most participants favored futile resuscitation and justified procedure benefits, citing reasons including uncertain prognosis and resuscitation outcomes, plus difficulty determining children’s best interests. Participants believed resuscitation was not futile and justified it in the children’s interest. This finding is in agreement with Aghabarary and Nayeri 201732 whereas Bahramnejad et al., 2014, Yekefallah et al., 2016, and Özden, 2013 are in disagreement with our findings22,33,34.

Providing futile care in intensive care units is challenging and causes burdens for families and healthcare providers35. The concept of futile care is complex, and reaching a consensus on a definition is hard. Futile care is defined as an action that fails to achieve the intended goals36. The decision to stop CPR in children and newborn infants is complex and may be different among physicians and across institutions11. Furthermore, individuals may interpret the best interests of the child or newborn infant differently at different times37. Thus, potential disagreements and conflicts may arise when parents and caregivers consider children’s best interests based on their interpretation of optimal options, different valuations, benefit-harm evaluations, and varying perceptions of existing reality38.

Researchers have reported factors affecting the termination of pediatric resuscitative efforts, including futility judgments and provider experience and comfort39, an unknown downtime, and uncertainty with vague and unclear endpoints40. Healthcare providers have various characteristics, beliefs, and values. Consequently, their perception and interpretation of futile care may vary41. Healthcare staff attitudes toward patients in their final days have always been a major challenge influencing CPR decisions. This challenge is perceived differently across countries due to diverse cultural, religious, ethnic, and geographical factors42. Similarly, various studies reported different beliefs and values when caring for critically ill children and recommended protocols and guidelines to prevent individual practice variations8,25,33.

Conversely, participants witnessed adverse effects of futile resuscitation, opposing these consequences and discussing harmful effects. Children’s or newborn infants’ low quality of life, imposed pain and suffering, life dependency on equipment and medicine, and difficult death were among these adverse consequences. Consistent with these findings, Schneiderman (2018) described futile CPR as a show on the body that should be avoided as an undesirable action43. Launer 2017, in a study on NICU nurses’ views on the determinants of quality of life in critically ill infants, found that futile resuscitation efforts resulted in rib fractures, tracheal damage, bruising, and extensive ecchymosis44. Boss et al. (2012) argue that while evidence supports improving quality of life for infants with life-threatening conditions, concerns exist regarding quality due to insufficient pain management, painful and futile ongoing care, and an unclear definition of futile care concept45. Furthermore, some do not believe even failed CPR is ever futile. Even the failed attempt might still serve as a kind of contemporary death ritual and have significant value as a social ritual for the dying and their families46,47.

Difficult death was one adverse outcome of futile resuscitation in this study. Although death is never desirable in childhood, when treatment proves ineffective, calm and pain-free death following patient wishes while respecting patient and family dignity becomes the treatment and care goal48. Dying children may experience increasingly unbearable suffering, challenging them and their families. Therefore, ongoing assessment of dying children’s condition and symptom management effectiveness, plus honest and empathetic communication with children and families about prognosis, planning, and treatment, are essential. Providing families with information enables better preparation for treatment decision processes during illness49. Although the participants in this study considered the difficult death as one of the adverse consequences of continued futile CPR, studies have emphasized providing a calm and pain-free death for the child48,49. Therefore, interventions preventing such death are needed. This gap could result from lacking written clinical guides for palliative care and standard end-of-life care in pediatric and neonatal fields. Hence, developing written instructions and clinical guidelines for timely interventions to prevent child suffering and provide dignified death seems necessary.

The tension of attempting resuscitation despite awareness of futility emerged as another theme from pediatric and neonatal CPR team members’ lived experiences. Participants faced a dilemma between justifying usefulness and deliberating harmful effects of futile resuscitation, while under existing pressures and restrictions requiring every effort for futile resuscitation, creating tension and conflict. These pressures included family and co-worker coercion, child-saving expectations, legal restrictions, treatment-centered atmosphere, personal benefit protection, and adherence to individual beliefs. In line with our study, CPR provided hope for a delay of death as a way for families to encourage and accompany the dying in their last struggle, and for society to maintain a sense of continuing the fight against death10. Bremer and Sandman (2011) found futile CPR efforts can be performed for various reasons, including benefiting others (family, relatives, and resuscitation team members). According to them, family and relatives require care. Resuscitation team members help families through bereavement processes by performing futile resuscitation and conveying that everything possible was done. They also stated that resuscitation team members may feel performing futile CPR is easier than providing support and grief consultation. Healthcare providers should undergo training courses to acquire necessary skills50.

Parents may consider various factors other than the best interests of the child when making treatment decisions for their children, and make decisions that, in some cases, put the child at serious risk of harm. In these situations, physicians may be reluctant to challenge parents, as there is a perception that their commitment requires dealing with the family as a unit and that each member of the unit will be influenced by the decisions made51. Parents’ perceptions of child care and their request for or not to continue treatment are influenced by culture, values, religious beliefs, individuals’ perceptions of the healthcare system, and the norms of the community in which they live52. Families may tend to do everything for a dying family member, even if these treatments are unnecessary53. Unrealistic expectations of technological evolution in society make parents overly optimistic about the possibility of treatment54. In society, childhood mortality creates a strong emotional burden55. Usually, the death of a child is considered a medical failure and a loss of hope56. In the present study, nurses and physicians continued resuscitation to combat family denial and prevent family guilt, violence, and mistrust, substituting ongoing resuscitation for bereavement care. Therefore, providing facilities to support families coping with the event is necessary. Providing families with bereavement care by educated health providers may prevent resuscitation team members from being forced to continue futile resuscitation.

Additionally, participants experienced the tension of the effort to continue futile resuscitation under the pressure of legal restrictions. In line with this finding, Voultsos et al., 2021 found fear of legal action and the lack of a regulatory framework were important reasons behind providing futile medical care57. Physicians simply prefer to continue all treatments to avoid legal problems58. The law determines the standard of practice, and all care providers must act within the law’s scope59. In Iran, as a Muslim country, national laws are set according to Islamic principles, and gaps exist in national law and medical ethics codes concerning initiation, continuation, or discontinuation of life-saving treatments. Consequently, most physicians encounter legal gaps and make decisions based on their religious or moral values60. Furthermore, DNR orders are not legally accepted in Iranian laws, and practitioners are reluctant to prescribe such orders because there are no formal guidelines, laws, or official statements about end-of-life care and legal clauses on stopping ineffective treatments causing practitioners to fear for family legal actions and prosecution61,62. DNR decisions can help promote a dignified death for patients who are thought to be dying63. Evidence suggests healthcare professionals are uncertain about DNR orders, their ethical aspects, and implementation. This is affected by their attitudes and demands, plus different factors such as culture, religion, race, and geographical features. Thus, compared to European countries, DNR order implementation in Asian and Muslim countries can be somewhat justified64.

Findings also revealed tension from futile resuscitation efforts under therapeutic-centered atmosphere pressure. Jagnan’s study (2020) discovered Canadian pediatric ICU nurses’ experiences of moral distress during life-sustaining interventions in complex medical cases. According to her, participants described pediatric ICU characteristics, including culture and realities associated with individual personality traits and characteristics. They noted ICU culture often operates under rescue rule status: “We are going to save all children, but we cannot save them all. We must try everything until there is no hope for anything else.”65.

In our study, participants also felt tension from futile resuscitation efforts under therapeutic-centered atmosphere arising from parents’ expectations of saving children after years of infertility or saving single children. This places parents in critical situations when children are born through infertility or experience extreme prematurity and sickness66. In the field of pediatrics, Iranmanesh et al. 2015 identified that, from nurses’ perspective, the most significant perceived barrier was the reluctance of families to accept a poor prognosis for their child7. At this time, parents want to do everything possible to save the child, meaning providing all treatments and intensive care. During this time, parents hope their child will survive, even with poor survival chances. In this case, parents leave control of child survival decisions to the treatment team67. Health professionals should help parents perceive that doing everything for their dying child means providing care and treatments that reduce pain and suffering and improve the attachment of the parent-child in the child’s final moments, life, making the dying process more dignified68. In a research conducted by Banazadeh et al. in 2015, it was found that nurses perceived they provide a peaceful, dignified bedside scene for family members once the child has died69.

Moreover, in this study, participants experienced tension from futile resuscitation efforts under pressure of personal motives, religious beliefs, and human emotions. Healthcare providers are morally obligated to adhere to professional ethics, be sensitive to ethical aspects, do no harm to children, and act in their best interests37. Ethical principles can guide healthcare providers to make appropriate clinical judgments and decisions based on their values while acting within the law70. To this end, the nursing and medical ethics codes can guide healthcare workers to avoid immoral behaviors and practices71,72.

Findings revealed tension from futile resuscitation efforts under religious beliefs and emotional pressure. Gagman’s study similarly showed psychological and emotional impacts throughout the patient’s death trajectory from decision to death. Nurses occasionally experience extreme distress and dishonesty regarding care decisions and their consequences for patients or families65. Voultsos (2021) reported that Greek healthcare professionals’ emotions, beliefs, and attitudes are important reasons behind providing futile medical care57. According to Campwala (2020), physicians may be influenced by their own emotions during emotionally charged resuscitation and experience distress over child loss or pressure when declaring death. This factor may lead them to resuscitate the child11. Aghabarary and Nayeri (2017) revealed that adherence to religious beliefs was among the main personal motives of the Iranian nurses and medical specialists for providing futile medical treatments58.

Additionally, physicians evaluate and support the best interests of the child when ethical and vital decisions are needed. However, they cannot claim that because their values are better than those of the child’s parents, they can make paternalistic decisions. In cases of inconsistency, clinical ethics committees provide healthcare workers with moral comfort and legal support for difficult decisions while facilitating parent communication73.

Strengths and limitations of the work

Participant diversity was a study strength. However, potential researcher bias represents a common qualitative research limitation, mitigated through member checking and co-researcher collaboration. Limited generalizability across diverse societies due to cultural and social differences constitutes another limitation. While novel insights may be influenced by cultural contexts regarding healthcare management and policies, certain elements of resuscitation team members’ lived experiences with futile pediatric and neonatal CPR have universal appeal, transcending national and cultural boundaries. A further limitation of this study relates to the heterogeneity of interview modalities, as most interviews were conducted remotely via WhatsApp due to COVID-19 restrictions rather than face-to-face. This may have influenced researcher–participant rapport and limited access to non-verbal communication cues, with potential implications for the interpretation of the findings.

Recommendations for further research

Future research should investigate long-term psychological effects of futile resuscitation on healthcare professionals to develop targeted interventions. Qualitative studies examining family perspectives on futile resuscitation would enhance understanding of this complex issue. Intervention studies evaluating various bereavement care strategies and their impact on families and healthcare providers would strengthen evidence-based practices in this sensitive field.

Implications for policy and practice

Research findings have significant policy and practice implications. Healthcare organizations should prioritize developing explicit DNR guidelines for pediatric and neonatal settings to reduce moral distress and decision-making conflicts. Educational programs including ethical decision-making, end-of-life care, and family communication training for pediatric and neonatal specialists require updates. Healthcare systems need interdisciplinary ethics committees for complex futile resuscitation cases. Standardized bereavement support protocols should assist grieving families while reducing provider pressure for futile interventions. Regular debriefing sessions and psychological support services are essential for CPR team members managing emotional strain from futile resuscitation attempts.

Conclusion

Findings revealed that pediatric and neonatal CPR team members experiencing futile resuscitation encounter duality and conflict about resuscitation utility versus futility. They perceived this paradox through their values, beliefs, experiences, and interpretation of futile resuscitation consequences. Facing this dichotomy under existing pressures created unpleasant experiences during strenuous futile resuscitation efforts. Given these experiences and lack of clinical DNR guidelines, developing such guidelines is increasingly necessary to prevent physician and nurse confusion and moral distress. Guidelines would reduce healthcare personnel’s behavioral diversity and guide actions toward children’s best interests. Including educational programs on legal and ethical challenges of pediatric and neonatal futile resuscitation, palliative care, and end-of-life in fellowship and master’s curricula for Neonatal and Pediatric Intensive Care Nursing is recommended. Providing families with bereavement care may prevent resuscitation team members from being pressured by families to continue futile resuscitation.
