Reconstruction of the Anterior Cruciate Ligament and Anterolateral Ligament Using Internal Brace
Authors: Sergio Augusto Campolina de Azeredo, Maria Mascarenhas, Rodrigo de Paula Mascarenhas Vaz, Camila Cohen Kaleka, Pedro Debieux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The integration of extra-articular procedures in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is crucial, especially in the context of the high incidence of ACL injuries, which are a leading cause of knee instability and potential progression to post-traumatic osteoarthritis. Injuries to the anterolateral ligament (ALL) and knee capsule are present in nearly 90% of ACL ruptures and are associated with significant rotational instability and grade 3 pivot-shift displacement. This study outlines a technique using the internal brace, a biocompatible device made of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene fibers, not only to augment the ACL reconstruction but also to reinforce the ALL repair. Historically used across various ligament reconstructions, the internal brace aims to enhance postsurgical stability of the knee without increasing morbidity. This approach combines primary ACL reconstruction through arthroscopy with percutaneous reinforcement of the ALL. The method is designed to improve knee function and stability after reconstruction, thereby reducing the risk of ACL rerupture, protecting meniscal repairs, and increasing the rate of return to preinjury athletic performance.

In recent decades, numerous studies have highlighted the advantages of incorporating reinforcement procedures in reconstruction of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) for specific groups, aiming to enhance knee stability.1, 2, 3, 4 This approach gains relevance given the high frequency of ACL ruptures, one of the most common injuries in orthopaedic and sports medicine, often resulting in knee instability and functional impairments that can progress to post-traumatic osteoarthritis.5 Persistent instabilities after ACL reconstruction remain a concern, with recurrent instability rates ranging from 11% to 30%.6,7 This phenomenon correlates strongly with less satisfactory clinical outcomes, emphasizing the need for effective stabilization strategies.

Recent clinical and radiologic studies have shown that injuries to the anterolateral ligament (ALL) and the knee capsule are associated with nearly 90% of ACL ruptures, representing a significant risk factor for rotational instability, specifically in grade 3 pivot-shift displacement in acutely injured knees.8, 9, 10 Various extra-articular reconstruction techniques have been proposed to address this instability, including lateral tenodesis and anatomic reconstruction of the ALL.11,12

The outcomes of combined reconstructions of the ACL and ALL have shown success in restoring long-term stability, reducing ACL failure rates, protecting meniscal repairs, and increasing the rate of return to preinjury athletic performance.13, 14, 15 The internal brace consists of an aggregate of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene fibers, which has been used for many years as reinforcement or even direct repair in ligament reconstructions of the ankle, elbow, shoulder, and knee. It is biologically compatible and does not add morbidity to the surgical procedure.

In this technique, the internal brace is used to augment reconstruction of the ACL and to reinforce the ALL. By integrating primary ACL reconstruction via arthroscopy with percutaneous reinforcement of the ALL, this approach uses the internal brace to augment both ligaments, thereby improving overall knee function and stability.

Surgical Technique

Patient Preparation and Bony Landmarks

All patients provide informed consent before undergoing ACL reconstruction augmented with the internal brace, as well as ALL repair with internal brace augmentation. The patient receives standard preoperative antibiotics and is placed in the supine position under spinal anesthesia combined with an ultrasound-guided femoral nerve block. The leg is positioned with the knee free to move from full extension to 90° of flexion. An examination under anesthesia confirms ACL and anterolateral instability including grade 3 pivot shift. A tourniquet is applied around the proximal thigh, and the leg is prepared and draped in a sterile fashion. Bony landmarks are identified, including the lateral epicondyle, Gerdy tubercle, and fibular head (Fig 1). Graft Harvesting and Preparation

An anteromedial longitudinal incision is made over the pes anserinus insertion. The semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are identified and harvested using a closed tendon stripper to create the autograft (Fig 2A). The tendons are meticulously cleaned of muscle tissue and measured to ensure a minimum total graft diameter of 8 mm and minimum length of 9 cm.Fig 2(A) Prepared autologous graft, consisting of harvested semitendinosus and gracilis tendons. (B) Prepared autologous graft with FiberTape augmentation.

(A) Prepared autologous graft, consisting of harvested semitendinosus and gracilis tendons. (B) Prepared autologous graft with FiberTape augmentation.

During graft preparation, the 2 tendons are quadrupled and secured using a FiberTape loop (Arthrex, Naples, FL) proximally, which is passed through a TightRope adjustable cortical fixation button (Arthrex) (Fig 2B). The central portion of the graft is prepared to allocate approximately 2.5 to 3 cm within the femoral tunnel, 2.5 cm intra-articularly, and the remainder within the tibial tunnel. This configuration may be adjusted based on intraoperative measurements.

Femoral and Tibial Tunnel Preparation for ACL Reconstruction

An accessory anteromedial portal is established under direct visualization (Fig 3). The native ACL footprint is debrided, and an anatomic femoral tunnel is created, positioned between the lateral intercondylar ridge anteriorly and the posterior articular cartilage border of the lateral femoral condyle, using a 105° arthroscopic offset guide (Fig 3). A 2.4-mm guidewire is drilled through the lateral femoral cortex, followed by retrograde reaming using a FlipCutter (Arthrex) to the desired diameter (8-9 mm) and a depth of approximately 30 mm. A shuttle suture is left in place for later graft passage.Fig 3Guidewire (starter pin; black arrow) positioned laterally showing the angulation of the lateral femoral tunnel (patient supine, left knee flexed at 90°). The anteromedial anterior cruciate ligament portal to establish the tibial tunnel entry is marked by the white arrow.

Guidewire (starter pin; black arrow) positioned laterally showing the angulation of the lateral femoral tunnel (patient supine, left knee flexed at 90°). The anteromedial anterior cruciate ligament portal to establish the tibial tunnel entry is marked by the white arrow.

A tibial aiming guide is set at 55° and positioned just posterior and medial to the Gerdy tubercle, targeting the center of the native ACL tibial footprint. An 8- to 9-mm cannulated drill is used over the guidewire to create a 30-mm-deep tunnel. A passing suture is inserted through the tibial tunnel and retrieved via the femoral tunnel.

Femoral Tunnel Preparation for ALL Reinforcement

A small lateral percutaneous incision is made approximately 1 cm distal and posterior to the lateral epicondyle. Dissection is carried down through the subcutaneous tissue, and the iliotibial band is incised longitudinally. Via an outside-in technique, a 4.5-mm tunnel is drilled in the femur starting from the posterolateral cortex, angled anteromedially, 1 cm anterior to the posterior femoral cortex and just below the lateral epicondyle (Fig 3). A guidewire is inserted to maintain tunnel orientation.

Tibial Tunnel Preparation for ALL Reinforcement

The tibial tunnel for the ALL is created via a small skin incision approximately 2.5 cm distal to the fibular head and 1 cm posterior to the Gerdy tubercle. A 5-mm cannulated drill is used to create a 25- to 30-mm-long tunnel directed from lateral to medial. A passing suture is inserted for FiberTape passage.

Internal Brace Reinforcement of ALL

The FiberTape is fixed first in the ALL. Guided by the placement of a starter guide (Fig 4), a proximal SwiveLock anchor (Arthrex) is inserted into the femoral tunnel, securing one end of the FiberTape (Fig 5). The FiberTape is then passed subcutaneously toward the tibial tunnel using a suture-passing wire (Fig 6 A and B). The distal end is pulled through the tibial tunnel and fixed at 45° of knee flexion using another SwiveLock anchor (Fig 6C). ACL Graft Passage and Fixation

The ACL graft is pulled through the tibial tunnel into the joint and femoral tunnel using the shuttle sutures (Fig 7). Under direct arthroscopic visualization, femoral fixation is achieved using the TightRope button, which is deployed over the lateral femoral cortex. The graft is pre-tensioned at full extension with posterior drawer applied.Fig 7Back-table assembly of the semitendinosus-gracilis autograft combined with FiberTape internal brace, positioned at the anteromedial aspect of the right knee, prior to tibial tunnel passage (patient supine, knee flexed to 90°).

Back-table assembly of the semitendinosus-gracilis autograft combined with FiberTape internal brace, positioned at the anteromedial aspect of the right knee, prior to tibial tunnel passage (patient supine, knee flexed to 90°).

Tibial fixation is achieved with a bio-composite interference screw (Arthrex) with the knee in full extension (Fig 8). The FiberTape used for ACL reinforcement is tensioned intraoperatively using the “finger tension” technique. This approach optimizes the FiberTape’s role as a backup stabilizer while avoiding stress shielding.Fig 8Tibial fixation of the anterolateral ligament internal brace using a 4.75-mm bio-composite interference screw (black arrow) (patient supine, left knee flexed to 30°, anterolateral view).

Postoperative Management

Postoperative rehabilitation begins immediately to manage pain, control swelling, and promote mobility. In the first 2 weeks, patients focus on achieving full knee extension and gradually increasing flexion to about 110°, starting with 50% weight-bearing and progressing to full weight-bearing. Stationary biking is introduced in the third week, and flexion reaches 130° by the fifth week.

From weeks 6 to 12, emphasis shifts to complete knee flexion and dynamic exercises such as cycling and treadmill walking. Strength training expands to include closed- and open-chain exercises, preparing patients for normal activities.

From week 13 onward, rehabilitation targets strength building, running drills, and sports-specific movements. Intensive training continues until about week 39, focusing on muscular power and sport-specific skills, with field rehabilitation generally starting around week 25.

Discussion

The efficacy of ACL reconstruction is paramount given the high frequency of ACL injuries, which often lead to significant knee instability and functional impairments.5 Recent research has underscored the critical role of rapid intervention in preventing the progression of osteoarthritis, as reported by Granan et al.,16 who found that each month’s delay after rupture increases the risk of cartilage damage by nearly 1%. Whereas traditional intra-articular ACL reconstruction alone often fails to re-establish normal kinematics in cases of combined ACL and anterolateral injury, Inderhaug et al.17 showed that additional anterolateral reconstruction could substantially improve outcomes.

ACL surgery primarily aims to stabilize the knee, particularly by controlling rotatory instability, which, if left unaddressed, is associated with poor results.18,19 In a retrospective study, Helito et al.1 showed that patients who underwent ACL reconstruction combined with ALL reconstruction or iliotibial band tenodesis had a lower failure rate than patients who underwent isolated ACL reconstruction.

In this context, our technique leverages the internal brace to augment both the ACL and ALL reconstructions. This approach not only aims to reduce morbidity but also enhances postsurgical stability.
