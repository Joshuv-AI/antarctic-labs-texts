Prehabilitation in patients with low physical fitness undergoing colorectal cancer surgery: a scoping review
Authors: Neil Botting, Ana-Carolina Gonçalves, Todd Leckie, Sally Wheelwright, Stefanie Harding, Christina Koulouglioti, Luke Hodgson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe primary treatment modality for colorectal cancer is surgery. Prehabilitation is advocated to mitigate risk and reduce complications, with benefits likely to be greatest in the least physically fit patients. There is an intersection between physical inactivity, socioeconomic deprivation and multimorbidity that increases surgical risk; however, these patients are under-represented in trials. This review aimed to scope the available evidence for prehabilitation in the least fit patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. MEDLINE, EMBASE and CINAHL were searched for studies investigating exercise prehabilitation in adults undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. Studies were screened for low physical fitness using pre-determined criteria. Data charting included reporting of intervention characteristics (Template for Intervention Description and Replication [TIDieR] framework), adherence, outcomes, socioeconomic status and multimorbidity.ResultsTwelve studies investigating 408 patients were identified. Despite a relatively uniform high-risk patient profile, prehabilitation interventions were heterogeneous, encompassing six distinct approaches with limited tailoring to low baseline fitness. Trials reporting improved clinical outcomes demonstrated high adherence, but this was inconsistently defined and reported. Behaviour change strategies were not incorporated. No study reported socioeconomic status, and multimorbidity reporting was variable.ConclusionsPrehabilitation research in patients with colorectal cancer and low physical fitness has focused on physiological risk. Wider socioeconomic and behavioural determinants of participation require consideration. A conceptual framework is proposed to guide future practice. This emphasises comprehensive risk screening, flexible and patient-centred interventions, and strategies to optimise adherence in those high-risk patients who stand to benefit the most.Keywords: colorectal cancer, health inequalities, high-risk surgery, multimorbidity, prehabilitation, socioeconomic deprivation

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the fourth most common cancer in the UK, accounting for 11% of all new cancer diagnoses and nearly 44 000 new cases each year, with an annual cost of at least £1 billion.1,2 Surgery is a mainstay of treatment, with approximately 60% of patients undergoing major resection. Survival rates have improved from 22% to 56% at 10 yr.3 However, postoperative complications are associated with morbidity and mortality, impacting patients on an individual level and accounting for a third of hospital costs.4 Given the high prevalence of CRC and economic burden, interventions to minimise the risk of complications are of interest to clinicians and patients.

There is overwhelming evidence that physical activity has numerous health benefits, such as primary and secondary prevention of chronic diseases, including cancer, and reducing the risk of premature death.5,6 In the surgical context, patients who are fitter have better outcomes.7 There is a physiological rationale for this, as fitter patients have greater resilience to surgical stress and reserve to facilitate healing, leading to lower complication rates.8 Prehabilitation utilises exercise, as well as nutritional and psychological optimisation, to improve physical fitness before surgery, as recommended by national institutions and guidelines.9, 10, 11 However, despite the signal of benefit, optimum timing, modes of delivery and patient characteristics are unclear.12 This uncertainty reflects the heterogeneity of interventions, often small cohorts and diverse outcomes.13,14 A Cochrane review included just three RCTs investigating multimodal prehabilitation for CRC.15

Physical activity (bodily movement that results in energy expenditure) and physical fitness (measurable attributes including strength, endurance, flexibility and body composition) are related but distinct concepts.16 Increased activity often leads to increased fitness, particularly if exercise (structured, repetitive activity performed with the goal of improving fitness) is incorporated.16 The close relationship between physical inactivity and low fitness can lead to overlap in reporting, but both increase perioperative risk.17

It is hypothesised that prehabilitation interventions are most likely to benefit patients who are least active, as they have the worst functional capacity, least physiological reserve and highest risk of complications.18, 19, 20 Underpinned by large-scale observational datasets utilising wearable devices, there is a growing acknowledgement of a significant early, non-linear dose-response to exercise, with the greatest gains transitioning from inactive to light activity, further suggesting that the least active stand the most to gain from prehabilitation.21, 22, 23 This theory is challenging to test, as inactive patients are unlikely to be included in studies, which tend to enrol the most motivated and active.12,24, 25, 26

CRC poses specific challenges for patients and prehabilitation services. Surgery is major and prolonged, with a median inpatient recovery of 6 days.27,28 Care is provided in 148 UK hospitals, leading to a diversity of settings and a mixture of enhanced recovery pathways. Postoperative care can be distributed across wards, level one units, high dependency and intensive care according to local set-up.28 Patients may have gastrointestinal symptoms leading to undernutrition, weight loss and malabsorption, as well as anaemia and iron deficiency, which can compound physiological fragility.29 There is also a short window for optimisation, as neoadjuvant therapies are often not indicated and the timeframe for surgery ideally is under 4 weeks from diagnosis.30

Patients from lower socioeconomic groups have an increased risk of morbidity and mortality following surgery.31,32 These poorer outcomes persist even when other risks factors are controlled for; however, the association is complex.33 It may in part be explained by the socioeconomic gradient in physical activity, but there is also significant overlap with multimorbidity (the presence of two or more chronic health conditions) and other lifestyle factors, such as smoking.34,35 Patients waiting for surgery from the most deprived areas live with significantly more risk factors.36 In Canada, while patients from the most deprived areas have lower recruitment and retention to programmes, prehabilitation was effective in those who participated, regardless of socioeconomic status.37 Adding to uncertainty for clinicians, patients from lower socioeconomic groups are under-represented in RCTs.38 Whether the association is an independent risk factor or secondary to the coalescence of other risks in this group, considering health inequalities in design and delivery is emphasised in the latest UK Macmillan prehabilitation guidelines.11

Despite the promise of exercise prehabilitation, its role is unclear in the least active. Programmes often employ high-intensity interval training (HIIT), which has been shown to be safe and effective39; however, an early study found just 16% of participants were fully adherent to a HIIT-based regimen.40 Given that just 10 min of exercise has been shown to have wide-ranging health benefits, it is unclear whether HIIT-based prehabilitation in this patient cohort is practical or necessary.41 Despite prehabilitation requiring significant patient engagement and complex behavioural change, focus is often on the intervention rather than the behaviour change required to maximise benefit.42 This is a further area recognised in the Macmillan guidance and may aid the development of patient-centred approaches and meaningful outcomes.11

In contrast to prior reviews, which have pooled studies with a wide variety of fitness strata,43, 44, 45 this review focuses on patients with demonstrably low baseline fitness, as well as investigating how socioeconomic status and multimorbidity are considered. The aim was to scope and assess the evidence base pertaining to prehabilitation in the least fit patients undergoing CRC surgery in order to inform future practice.

Methods

The diversity of study designs, patient populations and prehabilitation interventions make direct comparison and assimilation challenging for systematic review and meta-analysis. To identify the available evidence and inform future research concepts, a scoping review was identified as the most suitable method.46 Standard methodology was used as per the Joanna Briggs Institute, encompassing five stages: identifying the research questions, searching the literature, screening studies for inclusion, extracting data from each study (charting) and synthesising results.47,48 Reporting of findings follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews Checklist.49 The review was registered prospectively (https://osf.io/c8mup/overview).

Identifying the research question

In order to address the aim of the review, the following research questions were devised:

First, what are the characteristics (including tailoring and reported outcomes) of prehabilitation exercise interventions used in studies investigating patients with CRC with low baseline fitness or physical activity levels?

Second, what are the socioeconomic and multimorbidity profiles of patients recruited into these studies?

Third, what are the recruitment, retention and adherence rates to the interventions?

Finally, describe and evaluate the use of behaviour change theories used in prehabilitation studies investigating patients with CRC with low baseline fitness or physical activity levels.

Searching the literature

A search strategy was devised with an experienced clinical knowledge specialist to identify studies investigating a preoperative exercise intervention for patients undergoing CRC surgery (Supplementary Material). Once the search strategy was finalised, three databases were searched in August 2025: MEDLINE and EMBASE via Ovid and CINAHL via EBSCO. To ensure robustness, citation tracking using reference lists of studies and previous reviews was undertaken. Reference management was performed using Rayyan (www.rayan.ai – Rayyan Systems Inc., MA, USA). Automated deduplication was performed and manually checked.

Screening studies for inclusion

Screening by title and abstract (NB, LH) was followed by full-text assessment using the following eligibility criteria: all participants aged >18 yr with CRC; full text article accessible in English; study investigating a preoperative exercise intervention with prospective or retrospective methodology, including a quantifiable measure of exercise capacity at baseline; and patient cohort pragmatically assessed as unfit at baseline if one of the following criteria was met: functional assessment: 6-min walk test (6MWT) < 400 m, or 6MWT < 70% predicted; high-risk cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET): VO2 at anaerobic threshold < 11 ml kg−1 min−1, or VO2 at peak < 15 ml kg−1 min−1; or patients reporting low levels of moderate or vigorous exercise per week through a recognised and quantifiable tool.

When studies investigated patients with CRC as one of multiple cancer types, they were excluded to minimise heterogeneity.

Two authors (NB, LH) assessed the complete texts, abstracts, and titles of every selected article. Disagreements were resolved by consensus following discussion with a third reviewer (TL).

There is no universal definition of unfitness. A pragmatic approach was utilised to screen studies, reflecting clinical practice. Baseline fitness is recorded by CPET, functional tests or self-reported scores. CPET variables are established for assessing cardiopulmonary functional capacity, and high risk is often quoted as an ‘anaerobic threshold’ of <11 ml kg−1 min−1.50,51 VO2 peak of >20 ml kg−1 min−1 is considered low risk,52 with values <15 ml kg−1 min−1 equating to exercise capacity <4 metabolic equivalents (METs) and worse outcomes after abdominal surgery.53,54 One MET is the amount of oxygen consumed while sitting at rest, approximately 3.5 ml kg−1 min−1.55 These values were considered appropriate for this review.

The 6MWT can assess baseline fitness and is a common outcome measure in the prehabilitation literature; an increase of 20 m is considered clinically meaningful.56,57 Baseline 6MWT results are associated with recovery from surgery, and studies suggest its use as a screening tool to undertake CPET testing, with a cut-off of 427 m proposed and less than 409 m correlating with a VO2 peak <15 ml kg−1 min−1.58,59 The inability to walk 400 m is associated with mortality and disability.60 The mean 6MWT in healthy individuals has been shown to be 578 m, and there is a significant association with age and gender.61 Due to this variability, some studies use a percentage predicted model for comparing 6MWT, and a cut-off of 70% predicted is associated with postoperative pulmonary complications.62 Based on this, a baseline 6MWT < 400 m or < 70% predicted was used for this review.

Self-reporting tools are a further method for assessing physical activity. Examples include the Veterans specific activities questionnaire (VSAQ), which can predict exercise capacity, the Community Healthy Activities Model Program for Seniors and the General Practice Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPPAQ).63, 64, 65 If used, a response indicating high risk or inactive was considered for inclusion.

Charting and summarising data

Data extraction tables were constructed to address the above research questions (Supplementary Materials). Charting was performed by two authors (NB, LH), and tables were constructed in Microsoft Word 2025 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Key data extracted included study design, country, sample size, baseline fitness of participants, recruitment and adherence to the intervention. To assess the prehabilitation intervention, a recognised framework was used (Template for Intervention Description and Replication [TIDieR]).66

Results

Selection of sources and characteristics of articles

Following deduplication, 7730 articles were screened and 254 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 12 articles being included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Of the 139 excluded studies that included a prehabilitation intervention, 33 (24%) were excluded based on the fitness of the included patients.

Study populations

Tables including full data charting can be found in the supplementary materials. The total number of patients deemed to be unfit included in the intervention groups of the studies was 408 (excluding the subgroup of Gillis and colleagues78). Baseline fitness was determined using 6-min walk test (6MWT, n=8),68, 69, 70, 71,73,74,77,78 CPET (n=3),67,75,76 or both (n=1).72 Seven studies reported baseline physical activity.67,68,71,73,76, 77, 78 Two studies used a clinical decision tool to perform CPET if self-reported physical activity was low.67,76 In both studies, a high-risk mean anaerobic threshold was seen in participants (9.6 ml kg−1 min−1 and 9.3 ml kg−1 min−1).67,76

Eight studies planned to identify a cohort of patients who were unfit, older, frail or high-risk.67, 68, 69,73, 74, 75, 76,78 The other four studies did not specifically target a cohort of patients who were unfit but recruited patients meeting the criteria set for this review.70, 71, 72,77 One study was included based on low patient-reported exercise, with a mean of 16.7 min of moderate exercise per week and no vigorous exercise (significantly lower than the recommended 150 min week−1 of moderate exercise and 75 min of vigorous exercise).77 The mean baseline 6MWT for these patients was 435 m, but in the context of being the fifth youngest cohort, with a mean age of 72, this may have represented a low predicted 6MWT (not reported). One other study cohort had a mean 6MWT of greater than 400 m (421 m) but reported this as 65% predicted and was therefore included.71

Question 1a: characteristics of prehabilitation interventions

A variety of prehabilitation interventions were investigated. Six papers reported information encompassing all aspects of the TIDieR tool in sufficient detail.67,68,73,75,76,78 Three papers covered all aspects of the TIDieR tool but in insufficient detail to be reproducible.69,71,77 Three papers did not report any details in one or more domains.70,72,74

Two studies reported on unimodal (exercise) prehabilitation.67,69 The other 10 included exercise with a combination of nutrition, medical optimisation or psychological interventions.68,70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78 There were five predominantly home-based prehabilitation programmes.69, 70, 71, 72,76 The remaining seven were predominantly either healthcare- or fitness facility-based.67,68,73, 74, 75,77,78 All the studies with exercise interventions taking place in healthcare or fitness facilities also had an unsupervised component at home. One paper investigated a home-based intervention with telemonitoring.76

There was heterogeneity seen in the exercise intervention, with six different approaches investigated and differences within similar approaches. Of the three studies using HIIT combined with resistance training, there were three different methods of identifying high intensity.67,73,75 The commonest group of exercise interventions was moderate aerobic exercise combined with resistance training.68,71,72,77,78 The other interventions were vigorous aerobic,69 HIIT alone,76 moderate aerobic and breathing exercises,70 and resistance training alone.74

One study used wearable accelerometers to monitor physical activity during the intervention.69 The prehabilitation group had higher median daily step counts (6374 vs 4677) and higher percentage time stepping over 100 steps min−1 (14% vs 2%) but similar sedentary time (73% vs 72%).69 A further study used step count targets for the aerobic component of the intervention but did not describe how steps were monitored.72

Question 1b: reported outcomes

Of seven studies reporting 6MWT at baseline and preoperatively, six demonstrated a clinically significant improvement with prehabilitation.68,70, 71, 72, 73,77 The study that did not report significant improvement demonstrated low adherence and intensity of exercise sessions.69 The two studies that reported CPET variables showed similar improvements in oxygen consumption at anaerobic threshold preoperatively of 10.1% and 11%.67,75

Two studies demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in postoperative complications in the prehabilitation groups.67,73 These studies had comparable exercise interventions, in the form of supervised HIIT sessions combined with unsupervised home-based exercise. Both papers reported adherence of over 90% to the supervised sessions. One study reported a statistically significant reduction in the length of hospital stay.74 The sub-analysis of Carli and colleagues68 and Gillis and colleagues78 2021 which compared patients who had been able to obtain a preoperative 6MWT of 400 m vs those who could not, showed significant differences in clinical outcomes. The group that successfully obtained a distance of 400 m had a shorter length of stay (3 days vs 6 days, P=0.020), fewer complications at 30 days (21% vs 61%, P=0.009), and significantly more patients had improved their 6MWT by a clinically significant distance (89% vs 39%, P<0.001).78

Question 1c: tailoring interventions for patients with low physical fitness

There were some examples of tailoring the exercise intervention to account for baseline low exercise tolerance. Programmes using HIIT determined high intensity using either a percentage of maximum heart rate, work during baseline CPET, or a rating of perceived exertion score.67,73,75 Three studies based resistance training on baseline one repetition maximum.67,73,75 These approaches allow individualisation of aerobic effort or strength training; however, the target intensity followed a generic framework. One study also made adjustments based on recovery and participant experience and preference.76

Three studies permitted participants to choose the mode of aerobic exercise, with options of cycling, treadmill, rowing, swimming and stair climbing included.71,73,76 One study used a pre-determined rule to stratify patients into different exercise groups based on their baseline 6MWT. If this was >350 m, patients would be prescribed to walk 7500 steps; 200–350 m, 5000 steps; and <200 m, 3500 steps.72 A further study prescribed the exercise intervention based on baseline 6MWT and Borg perceived exertion scale.71

Question 2a: multimorbidity

The presence of multimorbidity was not reported in six studies.70, 71, 72,74,77,78 The six that did reported the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), with a high prevalence of multimorbidity.67,69,73,75,76,78 In the two studies reporting averages, this was 3 (moderate comorbidity)68 or 4.7 (moderate-severe comorbidity).73 The other four studies had high proportions of patients with a CCI greater than 4, including one study in which all patients with a CCI of 6 or greater.67,69,75,76

Question 2b: socioeconomic reporting

There was no reporting of socioeconomic status or other indices of deprivation. Ethnicity was not reported. One study did not automatically exclude patients based on a language barrier, with 28% of participants requiring an interpreter.77 Not speaking English or French was an exclusion for the Canadian studies.68,71,78 One study reported educational attainment.75

Question 3: recruitment, retention and adherence to exercise intervention

Recruitment after screening of patients ranged from 12.5%,75 to 90.9%.72 Some studies had low recruitment rates due to specific eligibility criteria; for example, Carli and colleagues,68 2020 recruited 120/418 patients screened (28.7%), but 290 of those excluded were due to a Fried Frailty score of 1. Various reasons were reported for patients declining to participate in the studies, including no inclination, inability to use an exercise bike, considering themselves to be fit, and being too busy. The two studies with the highest recruitment of 90.9% and 84% investigated exercise interventions that were prescribed based on baseline 6MWT, with stratification according to fitness.71,72 When recruitment rates were low, detailed exploration of patient-related reasons was not reported.

Three papers did not report adherence data.70,72,74 Acceptable adherence to prehabilitation interventions is not well established, with one review suggesting 75% equates to ‘good’ adherence.79 In these papers, adherence was reported in a variety of ways, such as adherence to supervised sessions or overall adherence, including self-reported sessions. Four studies reported greater than 75% adherence.67,71,75,77 One paper that compared prehabilitation plus rehabilitation with rehabilitation alone noted that adherence to rehabilitation was consistently higher in the group that had also done prehabilitation.71 Two studies reported high adherence but at a lower exercise intensity than prescribed.69,76 Some studies reported greater than 75% adherence to one aspect of the programme, but lower overall adherence.68,73

Question 4: behaviour change theory

Behaviour change theory or strategies had little mention, with one study including a psychologist to “provide suggestions on how to enhance and reinforce patients’ motivation”.71 Otherwise, there was no direct reporting of strategies to encourage behaviour change.

Discussion

This review focuses exclusively on prehabilitation interventions in patients with CRC with low physical fitness. Twelve studies were identified. In this relatively uniform, high-risk population, there was significant variation in the exercise interventions, with six different approaches investigated (HIIT with resistance, moderate aerobic with resistance, vigorous aerobic, HIIT alone, moderate aerobic and breathing exercises, and resistance alone). There were examples of tailoring the intervention to low exercise tolerance, such as allowing patient choice of mode of exercise; however, only two studies stratified the exercise prescription according to an objective baseline test.71,72 Adherence to the intervention appears to be important, as the two studies demonstrating statistically significant improvement in clinical outcomes had very high adherence to the exercise intervention.67,73 However, there was little mention of strategies to maintain adherence, for example, using behavioural change science, particularly with the aim of sustaining positive change in the long term. Despite the association between poor surgical outcomes, physical inactivity, and low socioeconomic status, this review found an absence of consideration of the latter. Adherence may be an important driver of efficacy; however, adherence rates have been reported in only 36% of prehabilitation studies.80 Of the 10 studies reporting adherence, definitions varied from attendance at supervised sessions to self-reported completion of unsupervised sessions, making comparison difficult. A sub-analysis of an RCT investigating multimodal prehabilitation in an unfit mixed cancer cohort showed that patients with high adherence had lower complication rates, a finding not replicated in the cohort as a whole.81 The same group subsequently found similar results in a large multi-centre RCT of patients undergoing surgery who are living with frailty.82 In a pre-specified cohort with high adherence, outcomes including disability, complications, and health-related quality of life were all improved.82 This study embedded discussion of barriers to participation in its design and identified behavioural, medical, and structural reasons to inform future practice.82 Identifying strategies to maximise adherence may benefit research in this patient group.

One such strategy is developing patient-centred programmes.83 Tailoring the intervention to account for differing patient attitudes, including flexibility of location (home vs hospital), intervention (high, moderate, or lower intensity), and support (face-to-face or telephone), may help facilitate adherence, but at a logistical cost to clinicians. This review found that interventions titrated exercise intensity to exertion and, in some cases, patients chose the exercise mode. However, it could be argued that this is limited personalisation when considering the wider context of an individual’s social, economic, physical, and cultural background. A mixed-methods review investigating access and adherence to cancer prehabilitation found adjustments may be needed to accommodate individual preferences, with interpersonal connections being a core focus.84 Knowledge regarding barriers to participation should be central to both planning trials and clinical services. A further rapid review characterises key themes of time, location, and individuals’ health as areas to address.85

Intervention heterogeneity affects prehabilitation evidence synthesis.80 It is, however, striking in the context of the uniformity of the patient group in this review. The commonest exercise approach was moderate aerobic with resistance, but delivery was variable. When combined with differences in duration, frequency, location, and combinations with different modes (nutrition, psychological, and medical optimisation), no two programmes were reliably comparable. One study utilised accelerometers, showing increased step counts and time spent performing higher-intensity exercise in the intervention group.69 Large-scale observational studies show that increasing daily steps from 2000 to 7000 lowers all-cause mortality by 47% (hazard ratio, 0.53; 95% confidence interval, 0.46–0.60), has at least moderate correlation with questionnaire-based activity assessment, and that wearing activity trackers results in 1800 extra steps per day.86, 87, 88 In this high-risk patient group, strategies to promote any participation in regular exercise, such as provision of wearable trackers, may yield the most meaningful results at a population level, given the significant early gains that are achievable.21,22

This review sought to identify to what extent behaviour change theories are utilised in prehabilitation studies for patients with low physical fitness, which is recommended by contemporary guidance.11,42 The CHALLENGE trial demonstrated the potential success of integrating behavioural support with exercise interventions in a population of CRC survivors following adjuvant chemotherapy.89 Frameworks such as goal setting, graded tasks and self-monitoring can be integrated to enhance autonomy, and utilising co-design methods such as the person-based approach may help the development of programmes achievable for all patients.79 It is noteworthy that disease-free survival in the CHALLENGE trial was significantly higher in the group randomised to structured exercise, underlining the health benefits of exercise and the merit of developing long-term interventions for patients.89 This pivotal trial suggests that longer-term support, rather than concentrating on a brief window prior to surgery, should be the goal of exercise interventions in this cohort. No studies in this review reported the inclusion of behavioural scientists or change theories in intervention design or delivery.

This review found a lack of socioeconomic reporting while targeting high-risk patients. The link between socioeconomic status and surgical outcomes is complex. Observational studies show worse short- and long-term outcomes in patients experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage, and mortality after emergency laparotomy has been shown to be worse.31,32,90,91 In a prospective registry analysis, increasing distressed communities index was associated with complications and resource utilisation, even after risk adjustment.91 The underlying drivers are likely multifactorial and include the prevalence of multimorbidity and high-risk behaviours such as smoking and poor diet, reduced access to healthcare, and delayed presentation of symptoms.92 The extent to which socioeconomic status can be considered an independent risk factor is unknown; however, the convergence of risks in this group warrants acknowledgement and can be screened for. There was variable reporting of other risk factors, including multimorbidity (six studies) and low physical activity (seven studies). To comprehensively screen for high-risk patients, consideration could be given to all these important factors, rather than relying solely on a marker of fitness.

To successfully include patients from lower socioeconomic groups, challenges of engagement will need to be overcome, given the evidence of lower participation in multiple areas of healthcare.31,37,38 Specific barriers relevant to prehabilitation include travel, finances, and language. However, when patients from lower socioeconomic groups are engaged, prehabilitation is effective.12,37 This potential disconnect between surgical risk and the design and implementation of prehabilitation services is highlighted in guidelines, which emphasise consideration of health inequalities.11

This review focused on the exercise intervention, where it is likely the largest improvements can be made, but a comprehensive approach would address all risk factors, including nutrition. The meta-analysis by McIsaac and co-authors included over 15 000 patients across a variety of surgical specialties. Exercise was the most common prehabilitation component and most likely to improve critical outcomes, including complications.14 Multimodal interventions that included exercise had the highest probability of reducing length of stay and improving health-related quality of life.14 Additionally, it concluded that nutritional prehabilitation is likely beneficial.14 A recent meta-analysis of 13 prehabilitation trials in CRC surgery by Zhang and colleagues concluded that a multimodal approach improved the 6MWT with moderate certainty.93 While adding to the findings of McIsaac et al to guide future enquiry, it is distinct from the current scoping review, given its inclusion of studies regardless of baseline fitness. Following a similar approach to previous CRC surgery reviews,43, 44, 45 it also did not map the literature pertaining specifically to high-risk patients. Molenaar and colleagues performed the largest RCT of prehabilitation in CRC to date, recruiting 251 patients to investigate a multimodal approach.94 The included participants had superior fitness to the populations in this review and demonstrated reduced severe complications.94 Evidence for unimodal nutrition prehabilitation also suffers from heterogeneity and uncertainty; however, a meta-analysis found benefit alone or combined with exercise.95,96 Frameworks to risk-assess the nutritional status of patients preoperatively, offer universal advice to patients not currently malnourished, and targeted nutritional support to those who are, have been recommended for surgical oncology patients and broadly adapted into the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism guideline for surgical patients.97,98 Widespread adoption of this strategy may further aid screening of high-risk patients as part of a multimodal strategy.

Studies in this review reported combinations of clinical, functional, or CPET outcomes. Identifying the most appropriate patient-centred outcomes for prehabilitation remains of interest, including for less fit individuals. A recent review found that 184 different measures of 50 different outcomes have been used in prehabilitation RCTs.99 This heterogeneity further hinders the synthesis of the literature. A core standards set, including tailoring and personalisation, and outcome set has been developed for colorectal prehabilitation, the use of which may aid standardisation and future evidence synthesis.100

When considering patients with poor baseline fitness, findings suggest that screening to identify low physical activity, fitness, socioeconomic status, nutritional risk, multimorbidity, and frailty would appropriately identify those at greatest risk. Physical activity and fitness could be pragmatically assessed using a combination of questionnaires (GPPAQ, VSAQ), patient-owned devices, and field tests (6MWT and/or CPET). Following this, stratifying exercise to baseline fitness, offering patient choice regarding location, mode, intensity, and timings of exercise, providing financial aid, addressing language barriers, and optimising adherence by providing additional support, with the utilisation of behaviour change theories, should all be considered when designing services. Limitations in this scoping review are recognised. While focusing on patients with low baseline fitness improves the heterogeneity of the patient cohort, the relatively low patient numbers and narrow inclusion criteria limit the generalisability of findings to patients with CRC as a whole. Findings should therefore be interpreted with a focus on patients with low fitness and higher-risk patients and used as a framework for future enquiry. There are multiple existing reviews with wide inclusions that draw conclusions with low certainty for broader patient groups.43, 44, 45 Secondly, a universal definition of low fitness does not exist. Too few studies utilised validated variables from CPET. The selection criteria used were pragmatic and clinically orientated, using thresholds associated with increased perioperative risk. However, there is a risk of excluding comparable studies with this protocol. Thirdly, owing to resource constraints, only articles available in English were considered.

Conclusion

Prehabilitation is an appealing means of mitigating risk in patients with CRC undergoing surgery. To maximise benefits in patients with lower baseline fitness, clinicians should proactively screen for high-risk features, such as low physical activity, fitness, socioeconomic status, nutritional risk, multimorbidity, and frailty, and prioritise adherence by developing programmes adaptable to individual patients’ needs and preferences, accounting for their wider social situation.
