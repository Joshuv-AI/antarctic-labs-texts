Anomalous Origin of the Right Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery
Authors: Nobuko Tanitame, Keizo Tanitame
Published: 2024

A 2-year-old girl with a cardiac murmur was referred to the pediatric cardiology department. Upon physical examination, a grade 1/6 early systolic murmur was detected at the cardiac apex. Electrocardiography indicated normal sinus rhythm at a rate of 110 beats/min. A transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal right and left ventricular size and function; however, color Doppler revealed abundant collaterals in the interventricular septum and abnormal blood flow into the main pulmonary artery (MPA). Although the left coronary artery (LCA) ostium was normal, the echocardiography showed the absence of the right coronary artery (RCA) ostium in the right sinus of Valsalva. Left coronary angiography revealed a normal LCA in the early phase (Fig. 1A) while identifying small collaterals between LCA and RCA and retrograde blood flow from the RCA into the MPA from the intermediate (Fig. 1B) to late phase (Fig. 1C). The diagnosis was an anomalous origin of the RCA from the PA (ARCAPA). Subsequently, she underwent successful reimplantation of RCA into the aorta, leading to her good recovery.

ARCAPA is a rare coronary anomaly with a prevalence of approximately 0.002% in the general population. It is much rarer than the anomalous origin of the LCA from the PA (ALCAPA). In newborn babies with ARCAPA, elevated pulmonary pressure leads to antegrade flow of deoxygenated blood through the RCA. The subsequent reduction in pulmonary pressure results in retrograde blood flow into the pulmonary artery, leading to a phenomenon called “coronary steal”.1 Newborn babies with insufficiently developed collateralization between the LCA and RCA may present with symptoms related to ischemia in the RCA territory. However, if there is abundant collateral circulation and pulmonary pressure remains low, the LCA can provide oxygenated blood to the entire heart, enabling the patient to develop normally and reach adulthood without symptoms.

While most patients with ARCAPA remain asymptomatic, diagnosis often occurs in childhood due to a cardiac murmur. Symptomatic cases may manifest with dyspnea, fatigue, chest pain, and congestive heart failure.2 ARCAPA carries a risk of myocardial ischemia or sudden death. Therefore, even in asymptomatic patients without risk factors, it is recommended to reimplant the anomalous RCA into the aorta to prevent potential cardiac dysfunction from the systemic-to-pulmonary shunt and to establish a dual coronary artery system.34

Radiographic diagnosis of ARCAPA involves echocardiography, CT scan, MRI, or catheter angiography.5 CT coronary angiography using multi-detector row CT is highly accurate for detecting congenital coronary anomalies. MR coronary angiography is a non-invasive option that avoids the X-ray exposure and contrast medium but has the disadvantages of poor spatial resolution and prolonged scan time. Catheter angiography is more invasive than other methods but provides a detailed assessment of collateralization and coronary steal.
