Ureteroenteric strictures following retrosigmoid vs. conventional ileal conduit – The MOSAIC study (The DaBlaCa‐16 study)
Authors: Simone Buchardt Brandt, Stefanie Korsgaard Körner, Rikke Vilsbøll Milling, Ninna Kjær Nielsen, Ulla Nordström Joensen, Lasse Bro, Thor Knak Jensen, Astrid Helene Livbjerg, Knud Fabrin, Marie‐Louise Vrang, Gitte Wrist Lam, Michael Vangedal, Pernille Skjold Kingo, Jørgen Bjerggaard Jensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the rate of left‐sided ureteroenteric strictures (UES) following robot‐assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) with a retrosigmoid ileal conduit compared to a conventional ileal conduit in a randomised controlled trial (RCT).Patients and MethodsThis multicentre, RCT included 303 patients undergoing RARC between May 2020 and August 2022. Patients were randomised to receive either a retrosigmoid or a conventional ileal conduit. Analyses were performed an intention‐to‐treat basis.ResultsOf the 149 patients randomised for the retrosigmoid ileal conduit, a total of 137 (92%) patients received the allocated conduit. In the control group, 150 of 154 (97.4%) patients underwent RARC and one patient was lost to follow‐up. At a median follow‐up of 754 days, the rate of left‐sided UES was 4.1% (six of 148) in the intervention group and 6.7% (10 of 149) in the control group (P = 0.31). No significant differences were observed in postoperative creatinine clearance or metabolic complications, including vitamin B12 deficiency and metabolic acidosis.ConclusionThe rate of left‐sided UES was not significantly reduced with the retrosigmoid conduit compared to the conventional approach. However, the procedure was feasible without increased risk of metabolic complications and therefore may be a good alternative when extensive resection of the left ureter is needed.Keywords: benign ureteroenteric strictures, bladder cancer, muscle‐invasive bladder cancer, radical cystectomy, retrosigmoid ileal conduit, robot‐assisted surgery

Introduction

Radical cystectomy (RC) with pelvic lymph node dissection and a urinary diversion remains the standard treatment for patients with localised muscle‐invasive bladder cancer [1]. RC is a complex surgical procedure with a high risk of postoperative complications [2, 3, 4].

Benign ureteroenteric strictures (UES) are among the common long‐term complications following RC and a bowel‐based urinary diversion. Reported rates of UES vary considerably in the literature, ranging from approximately 2.5% to 17%, depending on study design, follow‐up time, and diagnostic criteria [5, 6]. Notably, our own previous studies have found a rate of UES of ~15% [7, 8]. Most UES occur on the left side, and the majority develop within the first 2 years after surgery [7, 8, 9, 10]. It is hypothesised that ischaemia of the distal ureter plays an important role in developing UES, especially for left‐sided UES [11]. Studies investigating surgical techniques to prevent UES have focused on resecting more of the left ureter when constructing the urinary diversion [11, 12].

The retrosigmoid ileal conduit was initially described in open RC [13, 14]. The technique has been suggested to lower the risk of left‐sided UES owing to a more extensive resection of the left ureter at the cost of a longer ileal segment where the conduit is transposed under the sigmoid to the left‐hand side. This is opposed to the conventional method where the left ureter is tunnelled under the sigmoid to the right‐hand side (Fig. 1) [13, 14, 15].

Illustration of (a) the conventional ileal conduit ad modum Bricker and (b) the retrosigmoid ileal conduit (MOSAIC).

Long‐term complications after RC with use of a bowel segment for urinary diversion include metabolic acidosis, B12 deficiency, and chronic diarrhoea [15]. The theoretically higher risk of metabolic complications with an elongated ileal segment that is needed for a retrosigmoid ileal conduit have not been investigated previously [16].

The MOdified urinary conduit to lower Strictures After radIcal Cystectomy (MOSAIC) study is the first randomised controlled trial (RCT) comparing the retrosigmoid vs conventional ileal conduit and the first to investigate the retrosigmoid ileal conduit in robot‐assisted RC (RARC). Previously, we have shown that the robot‐assisted intracorporeal retrosigmoid conduit (MOSAIC) is feasible and safe, with no difference in operative time nor 90‐day complications compared to the conventional ileal conduit [17].

The primary aim of the study was to compare the frequency of left‐sided UES between the two study arms within a 2‐year follow‐up period. The secondary aim was to evaluate long‐term complications of the retrosigmoid ileal conduit and overall rate of UES.

Patients and Methods

From May 2020 to August 2022, this open‐label RCT was conducted nationwide, at all five hospitals performing RCs in Denmark.

Study Design

The study design and surgical technique have previously been described in detail, and the full study protocol is available in the Data S1 [17]. Eligible patients were diagnosed with bladder cancer and scheduled for RARC with an intracorporeal ileal conduit.

Randomisation was performed 1:1 between the conventional and the retrosigmoid ileal conduit (MOSAIC) using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap; Vanderbuilt University, Nashville, TN, USA) hosted at Aarhus University [18]. The randomisation was stratified according to the operating hospital and previous hydronephrosis with hydroureter. All procedures were carried out by experienced surgeons who routinely perform RC at the respective hospitals; however, their experience with the modified technique was sparse with zero to five performed per surgeon. Patients were followed in accordance with Danish guidelines, with a CT urography and laboratory tests at 4, 12, 24 and 36 months after surgery. Additional visits were planned at 8 and 18 months for high‐risk patients (pathological T stage ≥3, or N+, or small‐cell carcinoma) [19]. Additionally, renography was performed both prior to surgery and at 4 months postoperatively.

The end of follow‐up was defined by death or completion of the 2‐year follow‐up.

Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria included previous abdominal or pelvic radiotherapy, previous major abdominal surgery involving resection of bowel or construction of an enteric stoma, urostomy planned on the left side of the abdomen, single kidney; anatomical or functional (<20% on renography), severely decreased estimated GFR (<30 mL/min/1.73 m2), or complete ureteric duplication (either uni‐ or bilaterally), known at time of inclusion.

Control Group

The intracorporeal ileal conduit was constructed using an ~15‐cm ileal segment. The left ureter was tunnelled through the retrosigmoid passage and anastomosed separately end‐to side with a running suture after spatulation of the distal ureteric end.

Intervention Group

The intracorporeal retrosigmoid ileal conduit (MOSAIC) was constructed using an ~20–25‐cm ileal segment. The oral end of the ileal segment was tunnelled retrosigmoidally to the left ureter in the retroperitoneal space, where the left ureter was anastomosed using an end‐to‐side technique, thereby increasing the extent of resected left ureter [17].

The resection of the right ureter was approximately at the crossing of the iliac vessels in both groups. The ureter was spatulated, stented, and anastomosed using an individual end‐to‐side technique. Both ureters, regardless of randomisation, had ureteric stents removed between 5 and 10 days postoperatively, depending on the operating site.

Data

Data were collected continually by unblinded local project staff. Comorbidities were classified using age‐adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (ACCI) [20]. The following additional specific comorbidities with potential influence on the risk of developing UES were registered: hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, and diabetes mellitus including subtype. Hypertension and hypercholesterolaemia were defined as medically treated at the time of inclusion. Creatinine clearance (CrCl) was determined by applying the Cockcroft–Gault equation to the pre‐surgery and end of follow‐up creatinine measurements, with adjusted body weight when body mass index (BMI) was ≥25 kg/m2 [21].

The protocol was approved by the regional Ethics Committee (1‐10‐72‐72‐20) and was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04391790). The study acronym was ‘DaBlaCa‐16 – MOSAIC’ (Danish Bladder Cancer Group study number 16).

Power Calculation

We hypothesised that within 2 years, 15% of patients in the control group and 5% in the intervention group would develop a UES in the left ureter. Based on an alpha of 5% and a power of 80%, 140 patients were required in both groups to collect sufficient data. With a dropout of 10 patients in both groups after randomisation, we aimed to enrol 300 patients in total.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [22]. All statistics were performed as intention‐to‐treat analysis, excluding patients who did not undergo cystectomy as they were not at risk.

Time until UES was calculated as the time from date of RC until the date of UES diagnosis according to the pre‐defined definitions. Time until UES was summarised as median (interquartile range [IQR], lower quartile [Q1]–upper quartile [Q3]). The two groups were compared using Fisher's exact test or chi‐squared test for categorical variables, parametric and non‐parametric data, respectively. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to compare the two groups with continuous variables.

The cumulative incidence function for left‐sided UES was plotted adjusted for competing risk of left‐sided nephroureterectomy and all‐cause death. A comparison of the cumulative incidence function between the two groups were calculated with Gray's test.

Results

A total of 303 patients were enrolled from May 2020 to August 2022. Bladder cancer care continued in Denmark as usual during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram shows information regarding enrolment, allocation of intervention, and follow‐up (Fig. 2). In total, 149 patients were randomised to the intervention group, whereof 135 patients received the allocated RARC with intracorporeal retrosigmoid ileal conduit (MOSAIC); two patients underwent open RC due to no available RARC‐certified surgeon on the day of surgery. One patient refused RC and chose a second transurethral resection of bladder tumour instead. The conventional ileal conduit was constructed in 7.4% (11 of 148) of the patients in the intervention group. This was due to the following: forgetfulness of the surgeon involved or misconception of the protocol (five patients), large or fatty sigmoid colon (three patients), inability to identify the cecum (two patients, of which one was converted to open RC), and adhesions (one patient).

The median (IQR) follow‐up was 755 (710–785) days. A total of 64 of 297 patients (21.5%) experienced a recurrence of bladder cancer, and 52 of 297 (17.5%) died during the 2‐year follow‐up period. Among those who died during follow‐up, the median (IQR) follow‐up was 270 (124–505) days, whereas it was 762 (740–793) days for patients who were still alive at the end of follow‐up. One patient was lost to follow‐up after the first visit at 4 months postoperatively, as she declined further follow‐up visits. Comorbidity was equally distributed between the two groups estimated by the ACCI. Additionally, specific comorbidities associated with a high risk of UES were equally distributed between the two groups (Table 1).

Discussion

The MOSAIC study is the first to investigate the retrosigmoid ileal conduit in a randomised setting and the first to describe the intracorporal technique. Two previous non‐randomised studies with open RC were identified. A retrospective study with a median follow‐up of 19 months diagnosed no UES among 42 patients with a retrosigmoid ileal conduit [12]. Another study compared the retrosigmoid ileal conduit with a Wallace technique [11]. They found significantly fewer patients with UES in the retrosigmoid ileal conduit group (0%) compared to the control group (16%); however, with a significantly longer follow‐up in the control group [11]. The UES rates (right‐ and left‐sided) in the current MOSAIC trial was 6% in the intervention group and 10% in the control group.

The low overall rate of UES in both study arms might be explained by the exclusion criteria of previous radiotherapy and abdominal surgery, which are known to increase the risk of UES [23]. Additionally, there could be an effect of the surgeons’ increased awareness of the risk of UES in the study setting, with more meticulous surgical approach. However, it is possible that trial participation increases awareness of UES, thereby potentially leading to a higher reported rate than what is presented in register‐based observational studies. Improved prognosis has previously been suggested for patients with cancer enrolled in clinical trials [24, 25]. Regardless of the underlying reasons, the overall low rate of UES limits the statistical power of the study. Future randomised trials investigating the retrosigmoid ileal conduit should therefore aim for a larger sample size to ensure sufficient power.

The definitions of UES vary considerably in literature, which complicates comparisons across studies. In the present paper, we have used pre‐defined criteria; however, in a post hoc analysis, the rate of UES did not change when we applied newly suggested UES criteria [7].

Time until UES differs between the two groups, with a delayed development in the control group for both left‐ and right‐sided UES. This may be related to the overall low rate of UES, particularly for right‐sided UES, where the difference is greatest. Alternatively, it could reflect different underlying aetiologies for UES, resulting in different time intervals from RC until UES development, as previously suggested in a retrospective study [8]. Here, it is proposed that irritation, such as ureteroenteric leakage, previous abdominal surgery, or hydronephrosis, may lead to early UES development. However, these factors were equally distributed between the group in the present study. By comparison, late UES development was suggested to be associated with ischaemia. In the previously mentioned study, a portion of left‐sided UES were found associated with metabolic risk factors such as hypercholesterolaemia and high BMI, which may predispose to ischaemia when combined with a longer remaining ureter. However, in the present trial, baseline characteristics—including rates of hypercholesterolaemia and BMI—were similar between groups, indicating that these factors alone cannot explain the observed differences in the time intervals. Instead, the extended ureteric resection in the intervention group may have reduced the risk of ischaemic UES, supporting the hypothesis that a longer ureteric resection reduces the risk of ischaemia‐related UES [8]. This theory is supported by Fig. 3, which shows that time to left‐sided UES in the control group tended to occur later, often beyond the first postoperative year.

Different modifications of diversions have been investigated, with the increasing awareness of the role of distal ischaemia in the development of UES. They have focused on resecting a longer segment of the distal left ureter, to lower the risk of compromised vascular supply [9, 11, 12, 14]. Another way of assessing vascularity is the use of fluorescence. Indocyanine Green during RC has been investigated in two different studies, which demonstrated that its use increases the resected length of the ureters and lowers the rate of UES, although these studies were not randomised [10, 26]. However, a study using a similar approach have found contradicting results [27]. Additionally, retrospective studies have documented that increasing the length of resected ureter, may reduce the risk of UES [28]. These findings highlight the importance of sufficient ureteric resection to lower the rate of UES.

The present study illustrates a feasible way to achieve this additional resected length in the majority of patients. In a previous publication, we demonstrated that a significantly larger portion of the left‐sided distal ureter was resected in the intervention group compared to the control group (5.5 vs 3.9 cm, P < 0.001), while the right ureter resection was not significantly different (5.0 vs 5.0 cm, P < 0.60) [17]. These lengths of resections coupled with the rate of left‐sided UES, may support the link between distal ischaemia and the development of UES. Additionally, the present study adds useful knowledge for treatment of patients where further resection of the left ureter is necessary. In these cases, the retrosigmoid ileal conduit is a feasible and safe choice.

Metabolic acidosis and vitamin B12 deficiency are well‐known complications associated with terminal ileum resection [16]. The present study found no difference in long‐term metabolic complications when comparing the two groups. The intended length of the resected ileal segment was ~15 cm for the control group and ~25 cm for the intervention group. However, the length of the resected ileal segment was not measured systematically in the present study, as a precise and reproducible measurement of a bowel segment poses challenges due to its elasticity. Another limitation of the present study is the open‐labelled design; however, the possible effect of this seems limited with primary and secondary outcomes based on blood tests and imaging.

In this first RCT comparing the retrosigmoid ileal conduit to the conventional conduit in RARC, we found the rate of left‐sided UES was not significantly lower in patients undergoing intracorporeal retrosigmoid ileal conduit. Despite an overall low rate of UES, this study indicates that the retrosigmoid ileal conduit is feasible without increased risk of metabolic complications and may be a good option in patients where extensive resection of the left ureter is needed.
