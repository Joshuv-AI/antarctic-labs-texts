Secondary hemodialysis catheter malposition associated with azygos vein dilation in the setting of acute hemodynamic changes
Authors: Masato Obayashi, Toru Iwahashi, Shun Suzuki, Toshiki Fujiyoshi, Ikki Kojima, Yusuke Shimahara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of secondary hemodialysis catheter migration into the azygos vein in a 53-year-old critically ill woman. Despite correct initial catheter placement under fluoroscopy, the catheter tip migrated into the azygos arch 4 hours later. Retrospective imaging revealed that the azygos arch had dilated from 3.4 mm to 9.2 mm, likely associated with high positive-pressure ventilation and fluid overload. This case suggests that azygos vein dilation during various procedures may contribute to catheter malposition. These findings indicate that, in critically ill patients, initial imaging alone may be insufficient for accurate catheter placement, suggesting the importance of continuous postprocedural monitoring.Keywords: Azygos vein, Catheter malposition, Hemodialysis catheter, Secondary migration, Acquired dilation

Malposition of central venous catheters (CVCs) into the azygos vein is rare (approximately 1.2% of all CVC placements), but is clinically significant owing to risks of perforation and thrombosis.1,2 Left internal jugular vein (IJV) insertion is a leading risk factor owing to the sharp angulation at the brachiocephalic-superior vena cava junction.3 We report a case of repeated malposition of a large-bore hemodialysis catheter (HDC) into the azygos vein. This case is notable for secondary migration of the catheter despite initial successful fluoroscopy-guided placement, likely resulting from azygos arch dilation that occurred during critical care management. We discuss the interplay of device biomechanics, dynamic anatomical changes, and procedural challenges contributing to HDC malposition. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

A 53-year-old woman underwent emergency thoracic endovascular aortic repair for an acute type B aortic dissection complicated by hemorrhagic shock. Her postoperative course was complicated by cerebral infarction and profound respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation with high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). On postoperative day (POD) 2, progressive renal failure necessitated renal replacement therapy. Although the right IJV is generally preferred for hemodialysis, the CVC placed in the right IJV during surgery was being used for the continuous administration of high-dose vasoactive agents. We thus decided to preserve this line to avoid the risk of hemodynamic instability associated with catheter exchange. Additionally, considering the patient's coagulopathy, we aimed to minimize vessel punctures. Consequently, we selected the left IJV, and a 20-cm HDC was inserted under ultrasound guidance.

Despite straightforward placement, blood flow remained inadequate. Chest radiography showed that the catheter tip was in the superior vena cava but oriented laterally against the vessel wall (Fig 1, A). An attempt to achieve a more coaxial position by replacing it with a 25-cm HDC over a guidewire resulted in malposition with the tip directed posteriorly (Fig 1, B). The catheter was therefore removed, and access was established via the right femoral vein.

Initial malposition of the hemodialysis catheter (HDC). (A) Anteroposterior chest radiograph immediately after insertion of a 20-cm catheter shows the tip abutting the lateral wall of the superior vena cava (arrow). (B) Approximately 1 hour later, after exchanging the original catheter with a 25-cm catheter, the tip is shown to be malpositioned with a posterior orientation, suggesting entry into the azygos vein (arrowhead).

On POD 17, to avoid infection risk, the HDC was replaced via the left IJV (the right IJV was unavailable owing to thrombotic occlusion). Before insertion, review of the initial preoperative computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a normal-caliber azygos arch (3.4 mm) (Fig 2, A). However, a follow-up CT scan on POD 14 revealed a significant interval change: the azygos arch orifice had dilated to 9.2 mm (Fig 2, C). Three-dimensional reconstruction demonstrated the anatomical orientation of the single-rooted azygos arch (Fig 2, B and D). During this 14-day period, the patient was managed with high-pressure mechanical ventilation (PEEP up to 14 cm H2O), and her cumulative positive fluid balance reached +8500 mL. Furthermore, point-of-care ultrasound and CT scans consistently showed an inferior vena cava diameter of >20 mm, suggesting elevated central venous pressure and right atrial pressure.

(D) A 3D reconstruction image derived from the CT dataset in (C), visualizing the anatomical orientation of the azygos arch.

Subsequently, a second left IJV access attempt was performed with a 25-cm HDC under fluoroscopic guidance. During the procedure, the guidewire repeatedly entered the dilated azygos arch, but was successfully manipulated into the cavoatrial junction. Accidental deep right atrial entry of the guidewire acted as a landmark to verify tip position relative to the heart shadow before adjustment. Immediate postinsertion radiography confirmed an acceptable catheter tip position (Fig 3, A). However, radiography 4 hours later showed that the catheter tip had spontaneously migrated posteriorly into the azygos arch (Fig 3, B). Despite the malposition, both withdrawal and return flows were stable without dysfunction. Dialysis was performed stably at 120 mL/min (set to manage hemodynamic instability) with normal venous pressures (50-100 mm Hg). Considering this stability, we decided to use the catheter under careful observation. Renal replacement therapy was continued uneventfully until the patient's renal function recovered, after which the catheter was removed without complication on POD 31.

Discussion

This case suggests that the azygos vein diameter changes dynamically owing to circulatory dynamics, potentially causing shifts in HDC position. Although azygos vein diameter generally reflects right atrial pressure,4 this case suggests that acute iatrogenic factors, such as, high PEEP ventilation and fluid overload, can induce substantial vein dilation. A nearly threefold dilation of the azygos arch occurred after a period of high intrathoracic pressure and substantial fluid overload. Because high PEEP and fluid loading are known to dilate the superior vena cava and its tributaries,5,6 these factors likely contributed to the dilation in this patient.7 However, the dilation to 9.2 mm represents a particularly pronounced change that acutely transformed a routine procedure into a high-risk one. Although the right IJV is generally preferred, we retained the existing right-sided CVC to avoid interrupting vasoactive agent administration during the acute phase. Additionally, we selected the left IJV to minimize bleeding risks associated with new punctures, considering the patient's coagulopathy.

Furthermore, the patient had established baseline risk factors for azygos catheter malposition, namely, a left-sided approach and female sex.3,8,9 Specifically, a smaller body habitus and shorter vessel length predispose the catheter tip to settle near the azygos orifice.3,8,9 Radiographically, azygos malposition shows a fishhook appearance on anteroposterior images, characterized by abrupt posterior turns near the right tracheobronchial angle.10 This combination of an acquired, dynamic anatomical change and preexisting patient factors likely interacted with the specific biomechanics of the HDC. High rigidity likely prevented smooth navigation, directing the tip into the enlarged orifice.11, 12, 13, 14 This contrasts with flexible catheters15; rigid HDCs are prone to migration with pressure or position changes.16,17 However, the present case highlights that catheter length is also a crucial factor. Although 20- to 25-cm catheters are routinely used in our patient population (predominantly Asian) owing to their small body habitus, our case highlights that relying on standard lengths is insufficient considering anatomical risks. We selected a 25-cm multilumen catheter (the longest that was available) for antibiotic administration, but a longer catheter (eg, >30 cm) is required to ensure a safety margin, particularly with a left-sided approach. In our patient, factors such as repositioning and altered respiratory patterns likely contributed to this secondary migration. This case highlights that even the gold standard of fluoroscopic guidance does not guarantee permanent correct catheter positioning, underscoring the need for postprocedural surveillance.

Correlations between changes in azygos arch diameter and circulatory dynamics, such as PEEP or central venous pressure, have not been fully elucidated to date. At present, monitoring changes in catheter tip shape is the only available method for detecting changes in circulatory dynamics. Because the azygos vein arch is a dynamic structure, time-course imaging and functional assessment appear to be necessary to prevent catheter malposition in patients in critical condition. Additionally, when inserting an HDC into anatomically or functionally high-risk patients, device selection and positioning the tip 1 to 2 cm below the cavoatrial junction should be considered. Our present case suggests that even fluoroscopy-guided placement cannot preclude secondary migration. Although repositioning or replacement is typically indicated, continuing catheter use with close monitoring was deemed appropriate considering its adequate function and the patient's limited vascular access.

Additionally, we could not evaluate the effects of the malpositioned catheter on the vein wall during use. Postremoval noncontrast CT scan showed that the diameter decreased to 7.2 mm but remained dilated vs baseline, likely owing to persistent positive pressure ventilation.

Conclusions

Critical care hemodynamics may induce secondary azygos migration. Thus, relying on initial imaging is insufficient, necessitating continuous monitoring and vigilance for complications.
