Association of patient, physician and visit characteristics with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in Japanese primary care: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Atsushi Miyawaki, Joseph B Ladines-Lim, Daichi Sato, Kei Kitajima, Jeffrey A Linder, Michael A Fischer, Kao-Ping Chua, Yusuke Tsugawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTObjectiveTo assess the prevalence and associated factors of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in Japanese primary care.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed all antibiotic prescriptions written between 1 October 2022 and 30 September 2023, using an electronic health record database of primary care clinics across Japan. Using a previously developed classification algorithm, we determined whether diagnosis codes occurring on or during the 3 days before the antibiotic prescribing date ‘always’, ‘sometimes’ or ‘never’ justified antibiotic use. We classified antibiotic prescriptions into one of four mutually exclusive categories: ‘appropriate’ (associated with ≥1 ‘always’ code), ‘potentially appropriate’ (associated with ≥1 ‘sometimes’ code but no ‘always’ codes), ‘inappropriate’ (associated only with ‘never’ codes) and ‘not associated with a recent diagnosis’. A linear probability model examined patient, physician and visit characteristics associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing among solo practice clinics.ResultsAnalyses included 2 058 021 outpatient antibiotic prescriptions to 1 267 708 patients at 2809 clinics. Among these prescriptions, 176 181 (8.6%) were appropriate, 1 238 549 (60.2%) were potentially appropriate, 348 949 (17.0%) were inappropriate and 294 342 (14.3%) were not associated with a recent diagnosis. Among solo practice clinics, inappropriate prescribing was more likely to patients aged <18 versus ≥65 years (+2.6%; 95% CI +0.3% to +4.9%) or with Charlson Comorbidity Index score ≥2 vs 0 (+2.0%; 95% CI +0.6% to +3.4%), for physicians aged ≥65 versus <45 years (+7.3%; 95% CI +3.6% to +11.0%), for physicians in the highest tertile of antibiotic prescribing volume (+4.9%; 95% CI +3.0% to +6.8%), during telehealth visits (+3.9% vs office visits; 95% CI +0.02% to +7.7%) and during regular hours care versus after hours care (+2.1%; 95% CI +0.7% to +3.5%). These findings were qualitatively unchanged when including both solo and group practice clinics.ConclusionsTargeting younger patients, patients with comorbidities, older physicians, physicians with high antibiotic prescribing, telehealth visits and regular hours care may further increase stewardship effectiveness.Keywords: Epidemiological Monitoring, Public Health, Communicable Disease Control

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major threat to global health.1 Reducing inappropriate outpatient antibiotic prescribing is a key step towards preventing AMR.2 To achieve this goal, it is important to understand the prevalence of inappropriate prescribing and associated factors across all conditions, as opposed to specific conditions or groups of conditions, such as acute respiratory tract infections. To date, most studies assessing inappropriate antibiotic prescribing across all conditions have been from North American countries, particularly the USA.3,8 However, the prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing and associated factors likely varies by country.

Understanding the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing in Japan is important, as antibiotic use is high and poses an important health policy issue in Japan.9 Although Japan uses slightly fewer antibiotics per capita than the USA and many European countries,10 it has high broad-spectrum antibiotic consumption; such antibiotics account for approximately 80% of all antibiotics used in Japan.11 Since the launch of the National Action Plan on AMR in 2016,12 13 Japan has promoted outpatient stewardship efforts in several ways, including a stewardship manual for health professionals, AMR awareness campaigns and financial incentives for appropriate antibiotic use in children.14 The aim of these efforts was to achieve a 33% reduction in antibiotic use per capita by 2020 relative to 2013.12 However, as of 2019, antibiotic use per capita was only 4% lower than in 2013.15

In Japan, primary care accounts for more than 75% of all antibiotic prescribing, but there are no recent national data on the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing across all conditions in Japanese primary care.15 Furthermore, national data on the patient, physician and visit characteristics associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing are scarce.16 Therefore, in this study, we evaluated antibiotic prescribing appropriateness across all conditions in primary care visits in Japan using recent large-scale electronic health record (EHR) data with claims data across Japan. Additionally, we determined patient, physician and visit characteristics associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing. Findings could facilitate the optimal design and targeting of outpatient antibiotic stewardship initiatives in Japan.

Materials and methods

Data

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of data from the Japan Medical Data Survey (JAMDAS), collected and compiled by the corporation M3, Inc (Tokyo, Japan).17 The JAMDAS integrates EHR data with claims data from clinics across Japan, encompassing all types of outpatient visits, including office visits, home visits, nursing home visits and telehealth (telephone or video) visits. The number of visits to clinics continuously enrolled in JAMDAS from October 2022 to September 2023 was approximately 40 million. According to the national survey,18 there are approximately 90 million primary care visits per month in Japan, meaning that JAMDAS accounts for approximately 4% of all primary care visits in Japan. Compared with nationally representative estimates of primary care visits from the Patient Survey (a government statistical survey),19 patients registered in JAMDAS have similar sex composition but are slightly younger (online supplemental eFigure 1). Furthermore, when compared with a nationally representative sample from the 2022 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions,20 the prevalence of several comorbidities included in the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) generally corresponded with national estimates in the older population but was lower among the younger population (online supplemental eFigure 2).

The JAMDAS has clinic, patient and encounter information. Clinic information includes the owner physicians’ sex and age and the clinic region.21 For solo practice clinics, the database enabled us to identify individual physicians providing healthcare services. For group practices, it did not specify which physician provided which healthcare services within a given clinic.

Patient information includes demographics and diagnoses with start and end dates. Start dates reflect dates on which diagnoses were first recorded. End dates are dates on which physicians determine that treatment for the condition has been completed. This variable is often missing because recording the end date is not mandatory for reimbursement. Therefore, old diagnoses may remain for months or years. In Japan, physicians are required to record at least one diagnosis code in the patient record when prescribing antibiotics for newly diagnosed infectious diseases. Despite this requirement, physicians often fail to assign a relevant diagnosis code or reuse previously assigned diagnosis codes without updating the start date of these codes.22

Encounter information includes prescriptions, where the visit took place (eg, office, home, nursing home or via telehealth ie telephone or video), and whether the visit was during regular hours or after hours. These practice settings were determined using the Japanese Medical Practice Codes, a collection of standardised codes representing medical procedures, supplies, products and services used in Japan.23

This manuscript follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines for observational studies.24

Sample

We included all oral antibiotic prescriptions written during visits at 2809 primary care clinics that were continuously included in JAMDAS and accounted for at least one oral antibiotic prescription from 1 October 2022 to 30 September 2023. During this 1-year period, these clinics had a total of 41 528 832 visits for any reason by 9 571 737 patients. The unit of analysis was the prescription: individual patients could be associated with multiple antibiotic prescriptions. We identified antibiotic prescriptions based on the list of antibiotic agents provided by the Antimicrobial Resistance Clinical Reference Center (Tokyo, Japan), which includes all oral agents in the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical second level ‘J01’ category and 13 agents in other categories (online supplemental eTable 1).25

Outpatient antibiotic appropriateness classification algorithm

Following prior studies, we assumed that diagnosis codes in close temporal proximity to the antibiotic prescription date were potential indications for the antibiotic.26 Specifically, we considered diagnosis codes to be potential indications if (1) the diagnosis start date occurred during a look-back period spanning the 3 days before and through the antibiotic prescribing date and (2) the diagnosis end date occurred on or after the prescribing date or did not exist. We made an exception for 97 ICD-10 diagnosis codes (1.5% out of the 6291 ICD-10 diagnosis codes used in Japan) for infections or conditions that might require chronic antibiotics (eg, acne, chronic sinusitis, tuberculosis), following a prior study (online supplemental file 2).27 We considered these chronic antibiotic codes to represent potential indications if the start date occurred within a look-back period beginning 180 rather than 3 days before the date the prescription was written.

Using a modified version of a previously developed classification algorithm,4 we determined whether each non-chronic antibiotic code that could be a potential antibiotic indication ‘always’, ‘sometimes’ or ‘never’ justified antibiotic use (online supplemental file 2). In contrast, we only determined whether each chronic antibiotic code ‘always’ or ‘sometimes’ justified antibiotic use. In this algorithm, several members of our study team—a general paediatrician (K-PC) and general internists in the USA (JAL and MAF) familiar with medication prescribing in administrative data—determined classifications using a consensus-based approach. Due to some differences between the ICD-10 diagnosis codes used in Japan and those used in the USA, the study team modified the original algorithm using a consensus-based approach (online supplemental eMethods 1). This modification was performed by a general internist in Japan familiar with measuring health services use in administrative data from Japan and the USA (AM), an internist/paediatrician and infectious disease specialist (JBL-L) and a paediatrician (K-PC.).

We assigned each antibiotic prescription to one of four mutually exclusive categories: ‘appropriate’ if associated with ≥1 ‘always’ diagnosis code that could be a potential antibiotic indication, ‘potentially appropriate’ if associated with ≥1 ‘sometimes’ code but no ‘always’ codes, ‘inappropriate’ if associated with only ‘never’ codes and ‘not associated with a recent diagnosis’ if no diagnosis codes occurred during the look-back period. Antibiotics would be ‘not associated with a recent diagnosis’ if there were diagnoses with a start date from before the look-back period (ie, the physician reused an old code) or if the diagnosis start date occurred during the look-back period but the end date occurred before the date of the prescription (see online supplemental eFigure 3).

Patient, physician and visit characteristics

We examined the association between inappropriate antibiotic prescribing and patient, physician and visit characteristics. Given that more than half of Japanese clinics are solo practices,28 and it was more accurate to attribute patients to a single physician for solo practices, our main regression analysis was restricted to solo practice clinics. In a sensitivity analysis, to test the generalisability of our findings, we reanalysed the data including both solo practice and group practice clinics.

Patient characteristics included sex, age group (<18, 18–64 or ≥65 years) and CCI score (categorised as 0, 1 or ≥2).29 30 We defined comorbidities included in the CCI according to the ICD-10 diagnosis codes assigned to patients at the time of their first visit (for any reason) to one of the analytic primary care clinics during the 1-year study period.

Physician characteristics included sex, age (<45, 45–64 or ≥65 years), two measures of practice volume (monthly patient volume31 32 and antibiotic prescription volume33) and regions where physicians were located (Hokkaidō/Tohoku, Tokyo Metropolitan Area, Chūbu, Kansai, Chūgoku/Shikoku, and Kyūshū/Okinawa).22 We categorised monthly patient volumes as low (≤669 visits per month), middle (670–1215 visits per month) or high (≥1216 visits per month) based on the tertile in which each physician fell. We defined antibiotic prescription volume as the number of prescriptions per 1000 visits (including visits for both infectious and non-infectious conditions) and categorised this variable as low (≤12.6 prescriptions), middle (12.7–40.1 prescriptions) or high (≥40.2 prescriptions) based on the tertile in which the physician fell. We included these two measures of practice volume to account for variation in patient volume and antibiotic prescriptions between clinics.

Visit characteristics included place (office, home, nursing home or telehealth including both telephone and virtual visits) and time (regular vs after hours). Of note, every primary care clinic has different definitions for ‘after hours’ in Japan.

Statistical analysis

First, for the antibiotic prescriptions written in clinics overall (including both solo practice and group practice clinics), we described the characteristics of patients who received ≥1 antibiotic prescription during the study period, both overall and among three age groups: children (aged <18 years), adults (18–64 years) and older adults (≥65 years). Second, we calculated the prevalence of antibiotic prescriptions in each appropriateness category. Third, for each category, we identified the three most frequent associated diagnoses using the modified version of the ICD-10 diagnosis code grouping algorithm used by the Japanese government, that is, the Statistical Classification of Diseases, Disorders, and Causes of Death (online supplemental eMethods 2), which categorises ICD-10 diagnosis codes into 358 mutually exclusive, clinically meaningful categories.34 For each category, we also determined the three most frequently prescribed antibiotics. We repeated these analyses separately for children, adults and older adults.

Finally, after restricting to solo practice clinics, we examined the association between the prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions and patient, physician and visit characteristics, using a multivariable linear probability model. We used robust SEs clustered at the physician level to account for correlation between antibiotic prescriptions written by the same physician. For regional differences, we tested whether the prevalence of inappropriate prescribing was equal in all regions (ie, whether at least one region was not the same as the others) using the F-test. We calculated the adjusted prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions for each patient, physician and visit characteristic using predictive margins.35 For each prescription, we calculated predicted probabilities of inappropriate prescriptions for each level of the covariates, averaging over the distribution of covariates in the sample. In regression analyses, but not in the descriptive analyses described above, we sequentially excluded 86 clinics (2.3% of the total 2809 clinics) lacking the physician owner’s age and 1000 group practice clinics (36.7% of the remaining 2723 clinics).

For the multivariable regression model, two-tailed p values ˂0.05 indicated statistical significance. For non-multivariable analyses, we did not show p values because sample sizes were so large that traditional statistical testing was not meaningful. We used Stata, V.17 (StataCorp LLC) for all statistical analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

First, to assess the sensitivity of our results to the length of the look-back period, we calculated prevalence of antibiotic prescriptions in each appropriateness category by using look-back periods of 30 and 60 days instead of 3 days. Second, we repeated multivariable regression analyses when using look-back periods of 30 and 60 days. Third, to test the generalisability of our findings beyond solo practice clinics, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which we included both solo and group practice clinics. Among group practice clinics, we attributed prescriptions to the clinic owner physician’s characteristics. In addition to the adjustment variables included in the main analysis of solo practice clinics, we also adjusted for an indicator for group practice (vs solo practice) to account for differences by practice type. Furthermore, to distinguish between overall clinic-level volume and physician-level patient load, we included both the total monthly patient volume of the clinic (ie, practice size) and the average monthly patient volume per physician (ie, physician-level patient load). In the main analysis, these two variables took the same value and, therefore, we only included the physician’s total monthly patient volume.

Secondary analyses

First, we examined the prevalence of broad or narrow-spectrum antibiotic prescriptions in each appropriateness category. We defined broad-spectrum antibiotic prescriptions as prescriptions that included the ‘watch’ or ‘reserve’ antibiotics of the Access, Watch and Reserve classification of antibiotics (eg, second-generation or third-generation cephalosporin, macrolides and quinolones; see online supplemental eTable 125 36) and narrow-spectrum antibiotic prescriptions as any other prescriptions. Second, we calculated the prevalence of chronic antibiotic use. We defined chronic antibiotic use using two definitions: (1) a broad definition, which included prescriptions associated with diagnosis codes that might require chronic antibiotics regardless of the nature of any other associated non-chronic diagnosis codes during the look-back period and (2) a narrow definition, which included prescriptions associated with such diagnosis codes only when not associated with any non-chronic codes.

Patient and public involvement

We did not involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our study due to time limitations. It was not possible to inform study participants of the results because we used deidentified data. Results will be disseminated through this publication, legacy and social media.

Results

Sample characteristics

During the study period, there were 2 058 021 antibiotic prescriptions for 1 267 708 unique patients (mean [SD] age 37.7 [23.6] years, 706 915 [55.8%] women) at 2809 clinics. Patients who received antibiotics included 283 009 children aged <18 years (22.3%), 785 458 adults aged 18–64 years (62.0%) and 199 241 older adults aged ≥65 years (15.7%; table 1). Among the 1 267 708 patients, 910 099 (71.8%) received one antibiotic prescription during the study period, 205 622 (16.2%) received two, 69 540 (5.5%) received three and 82 447 (6.5%) received four or more. The three most frequent antibiotics were clarithromycin (359 006 [17.4% of total antibiotic prescriptions]), cefcapene (212 867 [10.3%]) and amoxicillin (210 542 [10.2%]).

Patient, physician and visit characteristics associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions

The main regression analysis included 1 091 553 prescriptions from 1723 physicians at solo practice clinics (figure 1 and online supplemental eTable 1). Antibiotic prescriptions were more likely to be inappropriate if written for patients aged <18 years versus ≥65 years (+2.6%; 95% CI +0.3% to +4.9%), for patients with CCI score ≥2 versus 0 (adjusted difference: +2.0%; 95% CI +0.6% to +3.4%), by physicians aged ≥65 years versus <45 years (adjusted difference: +7.3%; 95% CI +3.6% to +11.0%), by physicians with high versus low antibiotic prescription volumes (adjusted difference: +4.9%; 95% CI, +3.0% to +6.8%), in telehealth versus office visits (adjusted difference: +3.9%; 95% CI, +0.02% to +7.7%), and in regular hours care versus after hours care (adjusted difference: +2.1%; 95% CI +0.7% to +3.5%). We also found that antibiotic prescriptions were less likely to be inappropriate if written by female physicians versus male physicians (adjusted difference: −2.9%; 95% CI −5.5% to −0.3%), by physicians with high versus low patient volumes (adjusted difference: −3.7%; 95% CI −6.9% to −0.6%) and in nursing home versus office visits (adjusted difference: −3.3%; 95% CI −6.0% to −0.6%). The adjusted prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions did not vary by patient sex or region.

Association of patient, physician and visit characteristics with prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions in solo practice clinics. To accurately attribute prescriptions to physician characteristics, this analysis focused on prescriptions written by physicians at solo practice clinics. We analysed 1 091 553 prescriptions from 1723 solo practice clinics, sequentially excluding 966 468 prescriptions from 86 clinics lacking the physician owner’s age and 1000 group practice clinics. Using this sample of antibiotic prescriptions, we examined the associations of patient, physician and visit characteristics with the prevalence of being categorised as ‘inappropriate’ using a multivariable linear regression model that adjusted for month indicators. Sensitivity analyses

When using extended look-back periods of 30 and 60 days, the prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions was 17.4% and 17.7%, respectively, compared with 17.0% in the main analysis (online supplemental eTables 3 and 4). The prevalence of antibiotic prescriptions not associated with a recent diagnosis was 6.8% and 5.3%, respectively, compared with 14.3% in the main analysis. Findings regarding factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing were qualitatively unchanged, except that the differences between children and older adults, female and male physicians, nursing home versus office visits and telehealth versus office visits lost significance (online supplemental eTables 5 and 6).

When we reanalysed the data including both solo and group practice clinics, our findings were qualitatively unaffected, except that difference between female and male physicians was no longer statistically significant. We found no evidence of difference between group practice versus solo practice clinics. We also found that neither the total patient volume of the clinic nor the average patient volume per physician was significantly associated with inappropriate prescribing (online supplemental eTable 7).

Secondary analyses

The prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions was higher among prescriptions for broad-spectrum antibiotics (17.4%) versus narrow-spectrum antibiotics (15.1%) (online supplemental eTable 8).

The prevalence of chronic antibiotic prescriptions was 18.6% under the narrow definition and 27.1% under the broad definition in the total prescriptions (online supplemental eTable 9). The prevalence was higher among older age groups.

Discussion

In this analysis of a large-scale EHR database of primary care clinics across Japan, one in six antibiotic prescriptions were inappropriate, indicating the importance of antibiotic stewardship in primary care. Inappropriate prescriptions were more common when written for patients with comorbidities and younger patients, by older physicians, by physicians with high antibiotic prescription volumes, during telehealth visits and during regular hours care. Findings suggest that targeted antibiotic stewardship initiatives focused on these populations and clinics/physicians may be warranted.

The prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions in our study (17.0%) was lower than in other studies using the same classification algorithm in other countries. For example, 23.2% of antibiotic prescriptions to a privately insured US cohort were inappropriate,4 compared with 39.2% of prescriptions to adults in Alberta, Canada,6 51.5% of outpatients in acute care hospitals in China7 and 45.4% of privately insured patients in South Africa.8 However, many of these studies used databases other than EHRs, such as insurance claims, making direct comparisons challenging. Differences in disease prevalence may also contribute to the discrepancy. For example, the prevalence of Helicobacter pylori infections, a condition for which antibiotics are often prescribed, is particularly high in Japan due to its older population.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess primary care antibiotic appropriateness across all conditions nationally in Japan, particularly after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior studies in Japan have examined antibiotic use.37,40 Existing studies have also examined appropriateness under narrow-focused conditions, such as prophylaxis for dental procedures,41 respiratory tract infections42,44 or COVID-19.21 While informative, these studies did not examine a comprehensive set of appropriateness across all outpatient conditions commonly seen in primary care, thereby limiting overall understanding of appropriate antibiotic use. One exception is a study that used Japan’s national claims database for outpatient visits from April 2012 to March 2015, reporting antibiotic prescription appropriateness for outpatient visits with infectious disease diagnosis codes.22 However, this study did not analyse antibiotic prescriptions not associated with any recent infectious disease codes, which accounted for approximately 30% of prescriptions in the study. In addition, this study was based on data collected prior to the launch of the national AMR initiative and the COVID-19 pandemic45 46 and, thus, does not capture potential shifts in antibiotic prescribing patterns that may have occurred in response to these events. More importantly, no study to date has examined physician-level factors associated with inappropriate prescribing, limiting insights into the development of targeted interventions aimed at modifying physicians’ prescribing behaviour. This study addressed these important gaps, providing policy-relevant evidence to inform and strengthen antimicrobial stewardship initiatives in Japan.

Our finding that the higher prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in patients with comorbidities is consistent with past studies of both children47 and adults.48 One explanation is that physicians may be more likely to err on the side of prescribing antibiotics to patients with comorbidities who have vague infectious symptoms, feeling that failure to treat a bacterial aetiology may lead to more harm among such patients. Consistent with a study in Canada,5 but not with another study in the USA,4 the prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing was higher in children compared with older adults in our findings. This is notable, as antibiotic overuse for children has been a priority issue in Japan with a reimbursement policy since 2018 that provides financial incentives to physicians who do not prescribe antibiotics for the common cold and diarrhoea in children.45 However, the difference in the prevalence of inappropriate prescribing between children and older adults did not persist in sensitivity analyses using longer look back periods, suggesting that the difference may not be robust.

Our finding that older physicians were more likely to inappropriately prescribe antibiotics is consistent with several studies in the USA and Canada3132 49,51 and with our prior study showing that older physicians were more likely to prescribe antibiotics for COVID-19 or upper respiratory infections in Japan.21 52 One possible explanation is generational differences in clinical training on appropriate antibiotic prescribing. Physicians with higher antibiotic prescription volumes were also more likely to inappropriately prescribe antibiotics, consistent with a Canadian study finding that overall volume of antibiotic prescribing was associated with unnecessary antibiotic prescribing.33 There was no significant difference in the rate of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in our study. This finding is inconsistent with a study conducted in the Netherlands, which reported a higher rate of inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions in solo practices.53 However, that study only focused on antibiotic prescribing for upper respiratory tract infections.

Our finding that inappropriate antibiotic prescribing was more common in telehealth visits is consistent with a recent meta-analysis showing a higher prevalence of antibiotic prescribing during telehealth visits for otitis media and pharyngitis.54 A recent viewpoint found it ‘effortless’ to acquire an antibiotic prescription in a telehealth visit.55 Inappropriate antibiotic prescribing during telehealth visits could be a key area of improvement for stewardship efforts in Japan, as past literature suggests in-person stewardship initiatives can be adapted to telehealth settings.56 57 Indeed, telehealth-based stewardship interventions in other settings have demonstrated improvements in outcomes, such as decreased antimicrobial expenditures58 and increased guideline adherence.59 60

In our study, inappropriate antibiotic prescribing was less common during after hours care than during regular hours. After hours care may have a higher proportion of patients with urgent conditions requiring antibiotic prescriptions than regular hours care. Studies in some European countries note that inappropriate antibiotic prescribing is more common in after hours primary care,61 62 although these studies only focused on specific conditions, whereas our study focused on all conditions.

Approximately one in seven antibiotic prescriptions in this study was not associated with any recent diagnosis codes. These prescriptions may represent instances in which physicians failed to assign a relevant diagnosis code or reused previously assigned diagnosis codes without updating the start date of these codes.22 As an example of the latter scenario, if a physician diagnoses acute otitis media on 1 May 2023 without prescribing antibiotics but later prescribes antibiotics at the second visit on 8 May 2023 without updating the start date of the acute otitis media diagnosis, the antibiotic prescription would be labelled as ‘not associated with a recent diagnosis’ when using the 3-day look back period. In sensitivity analyses using a longer look-back period, the prevalence of antibiotic prescriptions not associated with a recent diagnosis decreased, potentially supporting the possibility that physicians were not appropriately updating the start dates of previously assigned diagnosis codes. Findings suggest that efforts to improve coding quality should be included in Japanese antibiotic stewardship initiatives.

We found that approximately 80% of all antibiotic prescriptions were for broad-spectrum agents, one-fifth of which were prescribed for conditions where antibiotic use was considered inappropriate. As shown in table 3, a large proportion of these inappropriate broad-spectrum prescriptions involved clarithromycin and cefcapene. Even among appropriate prescriptions, the widespread use of fluoroquinolones, including the frequent use of tosufloxacin in paediatric patients, raises concerns about inappropriate antibiotic selection based on age and clinical indication. These findings highlight the need for interventions that focus not only on whether antibiotics are prescribed but also on the appropriateness of the specific agents selected, providing important implications for future antimicrobial stewardship efforts and clinical education.

Our study had limitations. First, we evaluated indications of antibiotics using diagnosis codes recorded in administrative data, rendering misclassification possible. Second, our study did not assess appropriateness of choice of antibiotic agent or duration of therapy. Third, the determination of the appropriateness of antibiotic use in this study was based on a prior study conducted by some members of our team, who are US clinician-scientists. We adopted this algorithm because appropriateness of antibiotic use (whether to use antibiotics) is primarily determined by whether the condition involves a bacterial infection, which is consistent across countries. Using this algorithm also allows international comparisons with prior studies employing the same methodology.4 26 27 46 63 64 Nevertheless, future studies may be warranted to validate the algorithm in the Japanese clinical context. Fourth, we acknowledge that other researchers may have chosen different classifications of appropriateness for the ICD-10 codes. Fifth, due to the inherent limitations of claims data, it is possible that CCI scores were underestimated, as certain conditions could be undercoded. Sixth, the study population was a convenience sample of individuals recorded in a specific EHR system, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Finally, while our data are the most recent in Japan to our knowledge since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, we did not examine whether antibiotic appropriateness changed during the pandemic.

Conclusions

This study of antibiotic prescriptions using a large-scale EHR database of primary care clinics across Japan shows that one-sixth of antibiotic prescriptions in Japanese primary care are inappropriate, highlighting the importance of antibiotic stewardship in primary care. Our findings suggest that targeting children, patients with comorbidities, older physicians, physicians with high antibiotic prescribing volumes, telehealth settings and regular hours care could further increase stewardship effectiveness.
