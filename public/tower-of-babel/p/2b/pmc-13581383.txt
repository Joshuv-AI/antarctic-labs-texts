EUS versus magnetic resonance imaging for the diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesion in patients with chronic pancreatitis
Authors: Jin-Hui Yi, Jin-Jie Xu, Jia-Yi Ma, Xiao-Xian Zeng, Teng Wang, Jia-Qi Wang, Song-Lin Zhang, Pei-Dong Han, Xiao-Yu Zhou, Xi He, Guang-Ming Chen, Ning-Li Chai, Zhao-Shen Li, Dan Wang, Liang-Hao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Distinguishing pancreatic cancer from inflammatory conditions in patients with chronic pancreatitis (CP) is challenging. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and EUS are key diagnostic modalities, but their optimal use (alone or in combination) for accurate diagnosis remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the diagnostic efficacy of EUS and contrast-enhanced MRI for focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients.Methods:This single-center retrospective study enrolled CP patients with indeterminate focal pancreatic lesions detected by contrast-enhanced computed tomography. Patients underwent contrast-enhanced MRI, EUS, or both, and were categorized into 3 groups: MRI group, EUS group, and Combination group. Primary diagnoses were based on the reports of imaging or EUS-guided tissue acquisition, while final diagnoses were confirmed by surgical pathology or follow-up. The primary outcome was diagnostic performance.Results:A total of 103 patients were enrolled: 48 in the MRI group, 10 in the EUS group, and 45 in the Combination group. There were no statistically significant differences in diagnostic performance between these 2 independent groups. However, in the paired subgroup analysis, EUS demonstrated significantly higher accuracy than MRI (84.4% vs. 68.9%, P = 0.039). Further comparison revealed no significant difference in sensitivity (50.0% vs. 64.3%, P = 0.500), specificity (100.0% vs. 83.9%, P = 0.063), and accuracy (84.4% vs. 77.8%, P = 0.453) between EUS and the combination diagnosis.Conclusion:EUS was a superior individual diagnostic modality to MRI for evaluating focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients, and MRI did not provide significant additional diagnostic benefit over EUS alone.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is characterized by progressive fibroinflammatory changes and acinar atrophy, ultimately leading to abdominal pain as well as exocrine and endocrine pancreatic insufficiency. The prevalence of CP ranges from 13.5 to 560 cases per 100,000 individuals, with a mortality rate of 0.09 per 100,000.[1] Pancreatic cancer (PC) has become a major global public health concern due to its high mortality. In China, it accounts for 5.1% of cancer incidence and has a 5-year survival rate of 3%.[2] Numerous studies have identified CP patients as high-risk individuals for PC, with 0.7%–6.6% of CP patients eventually developing PC.[3–6] Approximately 27%–50% of CP patients present with focal pancreatic lesions, which may indicate PC or inflammatory conditions such as mass-forming CP, focal autoimmune pancreatitis, or groove pancreatitis, and occasionally other pancreatic tumors.[7]

Accurate differentiation between PC and inflammatory conditions in CP patients is critical. Diagnostic delays can allow undiagnosed PC to progress to an unresectable stage, significantly increasing mortality. However, distinguishing PC from inflammatory lesions in CP patients with focal masses is challenging due to pancreatic fibrosis, vascular invasion, or adjacent organ involvement.[7] The American Gastroenterological Association recommended magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and EUS for PC screening in high-risk individuals.[3] Many studies have reported the value of EUS in evaluating focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients. A retrospective study showed that the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of EUS-fine needle aspiration were 53.5%, 100%, and 91.4%, respectively, while a prospective study reported values of 73.9%, 100%, and 91.5%.[8,9] Despite being invasive, EUS enables real-time, dynamic, and detailed visualization of pancreatic lesions and facilitates EUS-guided tissue acquisition (EUS-TA) for pathological examination. Contrast-enhanced MRI offers advantages of non-invasiveness and convenience.

However, the optimal application of these 2 modalities (alone or in combination) for the accurate diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions in CP remains unclear. Therefore, this single-center retrospective study was conducted to compare the diagnostic efficacy of contrast-enhanced MRI and EUS for focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients.

METHODS

Patients

This retrospective study included patients referred to Changhai Hospital between December 2012 and September 2022. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changhai Hospital (CHEC2025-151), and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Inclusion criteria: CP patients with indeterminate focal pancreatic lesions detected by contrast-enhanced computed tomography. CP was diagnosed according to the Asia-Pacific consensus criteria.[10] Exclusion criteria: (1) No prior contrast-enhanced MRI or EUS; (2) Lack of surgical pathology results or follow-up data precluding definitive diagnosis of the focal lesion; (3) Final diagnosis of pancreatic pseudocyst.

Baseline data collection and primary diagnosis

Demographic data, clinical characteristics, and the details of contrast-enhanced MRI and EUS findings were recorded.

Patients were categorized into 3 groups. MRI group: Patients who underwent MRI only, with the primary diagnosis based on MRI reports. EUS group: Patients who underwent EUS only, with the primary diagnosis based on EUS reports; for those who underwent EUS-TA, cytological and histological results were used as the primary diagnosis. Combination group: Patients who underwent both MRI and EUS. The combination diagnosis was defined as positive for malignancy if either MRI or EUS indicated malignancy, and negative only when both modalities indicated benign findings.

Contrast-enhanced MRI procedures

Contrast-enhanced MRI was performed with a 3.0-T system (Signa HDxt MR750 3.0-T, GE Healthcare Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA; Signa Excite 3.0-T, GE Healthcare Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA; Skyra 3.0-T, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). All patients were placed in a supine position, with a phased-array receiver coil covering the upper abdomen. The breath-hold single-shot fast-spin echo-coronal T2-weighted sequence (TR/TE, 6316/87 ms; FOV, 360 × 420 mm2; matrix, 224 × 270; slice thickness, 5 mm; slice gap, 1 mm) and unenhanced T1-weighted fat-suppressed sequences (TR/TE, 2.58/1.18 ms; FOV, 440 × 440 mm2; matrix, 224 × 270; slice thickness, 5 mm; no slice gap) were used. Dynamic contrast-enhanced images, including those taken in the arterial phase (15 seconds), pancreatic parenchymal phase (20 seconds), and portal venous phase (40 seconds), were obtained using gadoteridol (Prohance, Bracco Imaging). The contrast agent was administered intravenously at a rate of 2 mL/s and a dose of 0.2 mL/kg; thereafter, 20 mL of normal saline was administered to flush the tube.[11]

EUS procedures

Patients were placed in the left lateral decubitus position and received anesthesia-assisted sedation with intravenous propofol (2.0–2.5 mg/kg for induction, followed by 8–10 mg/kg/h for maintenance) immediately before the procedure.[12] Heart rate, blood pressure, and peripheral oxygen saturation were continuously monitored. EUS was performed by experienced endoscopists using equipment from Olympus (GF-UCT2000, GF-UCT180, GF-UCT260, GF-UE260-AL5, GF-UC240P-AL5, Tokyo, Japan), Pentax (EG-3870nUTK, EG38-J10UT, Tokyo, Japan), and Fujifilm (EG-580UT, EG-530UR, EG-580UR, Tokyo, Japan). EUS-TA was performed if necessary. The choice of needle size and type, number of passes, and suction technique were all at the discretion of the endoscopists. Similarly, the use of elastography was determined on a case-by-case basis by the endoscopists.

Final diagnosis

The final diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions was confirmed by surgical pathology or follow-up outcomes. For surgical patients, the final diagnosis was based on the pathological results of the resected specimen. For non-surgical patients, the final diagnosis was determined by follow-up, incorporating clinical manifestations and additional pathological assessments (such as EUS-TA, cytology brushing). All patients were followed for at least 2 years or until death. Lesions were diagnosed as benign if they spontaneously shrank or if patients showed no exacerbated clinical manifestations (such as anemia, weight loss, icterus, gastric outlet/duodenal obstruction, death from pancreatic neoplasm-related complications). Lesions were diagnosed as malignant if patients died of pancreatic neoplasm-related causes, lesions enlarged, or additional pathological assessments confirmed malignancy. Malignant lesions included PC, sarcomas, hematolymphoid malignancies, and pancreatic cystic neoplasms with malignant potential, which was confirmed according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines and European evidence-based guidelines on pancreatic cystic neoplasms.[13–15]

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the diagnostic yield of the MRI group, EUS group, and the Combination group. The secondary outcome was the associated factors for diagnostic accuracy of contrast-enhanced MRI and EUS.

Statistical analysis

Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and predictive values were calculated. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and skewed distribution as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were described as counts (percentages). Three-group comparison for continuous variables was performed using one-way analysis of variance when data were normally distributed with homogeneous variances. When normality was met but variances were unequal, Welch’s one-way analysis of variance was used. For continuous variables with non-normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

For diagnostic performance comparisons among the 3 independent cohorts (MRI group, EUS group, and Combination group), the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. For paired comparisons within the Combination group, McNemar’s test was employed to compare the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy between the 2 modalities. Furthermore, the diagnostic yield of the Combination diagnosis was then compared with that of the better-performing single modality using McNemar’s test to determine whether the combination strategy provided additional statistical benefit. In cases where zero frequencies occurred, such as no discordant pairs in one direction, an exact binomial test was used as an alternative to McNemar’s test.

A logistic regression model was used to identify the risk factors for diagnostic accuracy of contrast-enhanced MRI and EUS through univariable analysis and multivariable analysis. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were compared using the 2-tailed Student t test, while those with a skewed distribution were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test in different groups. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for both the univariable and multivariable analysis. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 4.5.2, R Foundation，Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

A total of 201 CP patients with indeterminate focal pancreatic lesions detected by contrast-enhanced computed tomography were assessed for eligibility. After exclusion (71 patients without MRI/EUS, 24 without final diagnosis, and 3 with pancreatic pseudocyst), 103 patients were enrolled. Among them, 48 underwent only MRI, 10 underwent only EUS, and 45 underwent both. Thus, the MRI, EUS, and Combination groups consisted of 48, 10, and 45 patients, respectively. Final diagnoses were confirmed by surgical pathology (n = 41) and follow-up (n = 62) (Figure 1).

EUS group (N = 10)

EUS-TA was performed in 7 patients (70.0%). Reasons for unsuccessful EUS-TA included no obvious mass observed (33.3%, 1/3), safe puncture prohibited by vessels (33.3%, 1/3), and acoustic shadows created by pancreatic stones (33.3%, 1/3). Primary diagnosis: 8 benign and 2 malignant. Final diagnosis: 6 benign and 4 malignant (0 false positives, 2 false negatives) (Figure 2). The 2 false negatives were all PC (n = 2). Diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV were 50.0%, 100.0%, 80.0%, 100.0%, and 75.0%, respectively (Table 2).

Combination group (N = 45)

EUS-TA was performed in 27 patients (60.0%). Reasons for unsuccessful EUS-TA included no obvious mass observed (22.2%, 4/18), acoustic shadows created by pancreatic stones (27.8%, 5/18), safe puncture prohibited by vessels (16.7%, 3/18), benign lesion not requiring puncture (27.8%, 5/18), and the use of anticoagulant therapy (5.6%, 1/18). Primary diagnosis: 31 benign and 14 malignant. Final diagnosis: 31 benign and 14 malignant (5 false positives, 5 false negatives) (Figure 2). The 5 false negatives included PC (n = 3), neuroendocrine tumors with malignant potential (n = 1), and intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms with high-grade dysplasia (n = 1). Diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV were 64.3%, 83.9%, 77.8%, 64.3%, and 83.9%, respectively (Table 2).

No statistically significant differences were observed in sensitivity (P = 0.874), specificity (P = 0.529), accuracy (P = 0.920), PPV (P = 0.540), and NPV (P = 0.844) among the MRI, EUS, and Combination groups.

Diagnostic yield of the Combination groups

In the Combination groups, the diagnostic yield of MRI and EUS was first compared. For MRI, the diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were 35.7%, 83.9%, and 68.9%, respectively, while for EUS, they were 50.0%, 100.0%, and 84.4%, respectively. McNemar’s test showed that EUS had significantly higher accuracy than MRI (P = 0.039), whereas no significant differences were observed in sensitivity (P = 0.687) or specificity (P = 0.063) between the 2 modalities.

Given that EUS was the superior individual modality, it was further compared with the combination diagnosis. McNemar’s test revealed no statistically significant difference in sensitivity (50.0% vs. 64.3%, P = 0.500), specificity (100.0% vs. 83.9%, P = 0.063), and accuracy (84.4% vs. 77.8%, P = 0.453) between EUS and the combination diagnosis.

Predictive factors for diagnostic accuracy of contrast-enhanced MRI

There were 93 patients who underwent contrast-enhanced MRI, among whom 67 patients were diagnosed correctly. Univariable analysis identified lesion location (P = 0.017) and T2-weighted MRI mass density (P = 0.002) as factors associated with diagnostic accuracy among 15 potential risk factors. Multivariable logistic regression showed lesion location (OR = 0.160, 95% CI: 0.033–0.771, P = 0.022) and T2-weighted mass density (OR = 0.105, 95% CI: 0.021–0.523, P = 0.006) were associated with diagnostic accuracy (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

As reported previously, 27%–50% of CP patients develop focal pancreatic lesions during the disease course, which may represent PC or inflammatory abnormalities.[7] Given the poor prognosis of PC, distinguishing it from chronic inflammatory lesions in CP is crucial. Currently, contrast-enhanced MRI and EUS are preferred imaging modalities for suspected PC.[3] A meta‑analysis showed that the sensitivity and specificity of EUS-fine needle aspiration for solid lesions in CP were 65.0% and 96.8%.[16] In our study, the sensitivity of EUS was 50.0%, lower than previous data, possibly because 38.2% (21/55) of patients did not undergo EUS-TA.

Our study found no statistically significant differences in sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, or NPV among the MRI, EUS, and Combination groups in the independent cohort comparisons. However, in the paired subgroup analysis, EUS demonstrated significantly higher accuracy than MRI (84.4% vs. 68.9%, P = 0.039), while no significant differences were observed in sensitivity or specificity between these 2 modalities. Further comparison between EUS and the combination diagnosis revealed no significant differences in diagnostic performance. The lack of statistically significant differences among the 3 independent groups may be attributed to the small sample size. In our study, only 10 patients were enrolled in the EUS group, which limited the precision of efficacy estimates and the power of between-group comparisons. Due to the fact that the paired design could eliminate the influence of interindividual differences on comparisons and enable more sensitive detection of true differences between diagnostic modalities, we focused on the patients in the Combination group. This result suggested that, among patients undergoing both MRI and EUS, EUS has significantly higher diagnostic accuracy compared with MRI. Further comparison between EUS and the combination diagnosis revealed similar diagnostic yields, indicating that the addition of MRI to EUS did not enhance diagnostic accuracy. Given that the combination strategy would increase healthcare resource consumption and patient financial burden, the results of this study did not support the addition of MRI for combined interpretation on the basis of EUS.

Among incorrect MRI diagnoses, 57.7% (15/26) were missed diagnoses and 42.3% (11/26) were misdiagnoses. Lesion location and T2-weighted mass density were associated with MRI diagnostic accuracy. This can be explained by the fact that PC and chronic inflammatory lesions share similar histopathological features (hypovascularization and extensive fibrosis), leading to overlapping MRI findings on T2-weighted images and making differentiation difficult.[17]

Previous studies have discussed secondary signs for diagnosis. The duct-penetrating sign (a nonobstructed main pancreatic duct traversing the mass) is a typical MRI finding for chronic inflammatory lesions. Ichikawa et al.[18] divided the duct-penetrating sign into a broad sense (nonobstructed main pancreatic duct) and a narrow sense (only normal main pancreatic duct). Sensitivities, specificities, and accuracy were 85%, 96%, and 94% (broad sense) and 36%, 100%, and 87% (narrow sense) for distinguishing PC from chronic inflammatory lesions. In our study, the duct-penetrating sign (narrow sense) was more common in correctly diagnosed cases but not statistically significant (P = 0.189), possibly due to reduced sensitivity and accuracy with the narrow definition.[18] Other secondary signs (collateral duct dilatation and double duct sign) showed limited diagnostic performance in our cohort.[19,20]

EUS-TA is widely recognized as a standard method to obtain tissue diagnosis and evaluate solid pancreatic lesions.[21–23] However, previous studies have reported a relatively high incidence of false-negative biopsy results for focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients.[24–27] Fibrosis and stones in CP may compromise the quality of EUS-TA samples, highlighting the need for close follow-up in patients with high clinical suspicion for PC despite negative biopsy results.

Lesion size was associated with EUS diagnostic accuracy, but its impact remains controversial. Agarwal et al.[28] reported that EUS detected PC larger than 21 mm more effectively, while Uehara et al.[29] found comparable accuracy for lesions 11–20 mm and > 21 mm. Haba et al.[30] identified lesion size as the most influential factor affecting diagnostic accuracy in a large cohort.

There were some limitations in the study. Firstly, given that not all patients underwent EUS-TA, factors such as the needle size and type, number of passes, suction technique, and other risk factors associated with EUS-TA were not included in the univariable and multivariable analysis. Moreover, EUS-elastography was used at the endoscopist’s discretion. The lack of standardized protocols and the small number of cases precluded meaningful analysis of these advanced imaging techniques. Secondly, due to the noninvasive nature and procedural convenience of contrast-enhanced MRI, 77.8% of patients underwent MRI before EUS, potentially introducing diagnostic bias as endoscopists may have considered MRI findings when interpreting EUS results. These biases were inherent to retrospective studies using real-world clinical data. Thirdly, as a single-center retrospective study, information bias was inevitable. Blinding was not possible, and standardized scoring systems were not routinely applied. Interobserver reliability could not be assessed because each examination was interpreted by a single radiologist/endoscopist as part of routine clinical care. Fourthly, EUS-TA was performed in only 61.8% of EUS cases, with the remainder relying on morphology alone due to CP-related technical failures, which may have systematically underestimated EUS performance. However, this was an inherent limitation of EUS application in patients with CP. More recently, elastography and contrast-enhanced EUS have emerged to improve diagnostic performance in such settings, and further studies should be conducted to address this challenge. Fifthly, the events-per-variable ratio was low, which may have led to overfitting and unstable estimates, as reflected by wide CI and variable OR. Therefore, the results of the multivariable analyses should be interpreted with caution.

CONCLUSION

EUS was a superior individual diagnostic modality to MRI for evaluating focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients, and MRI did not provide significant additional diagnostic benefit over EUS alone.
