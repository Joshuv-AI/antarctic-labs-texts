pH-shifting extraction reveals conformation-dependent flavor binding to sea cucumber collagen proteins
Authors: Xinran Li, Enhui Ma, Qiang Geng, Jian Jiao, Songyi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sea cucumber collagen fibers (SCCF) are important nutritional and functional resources in aquatic foods, yet their interactions with flavor compounds remain insufficiently understood. This study innovatively applied pH-shifting extraction to isolate sea cucumber collagen fiber proteins extracted at alkaline (ALP), neutral (NP), and acidic pH (ACP), revealing conformation-dependent mechanisms in flavor binding. Key amino acids (Glu, Gly, Phe) were identified as crucial for structural stability and binding potential. Multi-spectroscopic analyses demonstrated that flavor interaction induced significant conformational changes: a reduction in α-helix and β-sheet content, with an increase in β-turns and random coils. FL and FTIR spectra confirmed that hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions were the dominant forces driving complex formation. The molecular weight distribution and amino acid composition of the proteins varied substantially with extraction pH, directly influencing their distinct flavor-binding capacities. These findings provide a novel strategy for optimizing flavor retention in seafood by tailoring protein extraction conditions.

Introduction

Sea cucumber (Apostichopus japonicus), a highly nutritious marine organism, has been increasingly recognized for its potential as a functional food ingredient. Known for its high protein, low fat content, and abundance of bioactive compounds, sea cucumber has long been valued in East Asia as both a food and medicinal product. Its collagen, comprising approximately 70% of the total protein in the body wall, plays a critical role in the texture and structural integrity of sea cucumber products (Huang et al., 2023). Flavor quality is an important factor affecting the utilization of marine products, especially as environmental and ecological factors may influence the biochemical composition and quality attributes of marine-derived proteins (Bhuiyan et al., 2026). Despite its widespread consumption, the structural characteristics of sea cucumber collagen and its interactions with flavor compounds are not fully understood, limiting the optimization of its flavor profile in food processing (Chen et al., 2023).

In food systems, Flavor refers to the complex sensory impressions perceived by human sensory organs (Hu et al., 2025), proteins are key contributors to flavor formation, with their interactions significantly influencing the sensory experience of the final products. Proteins can interact with flavor compounds through multiple mechanisms, including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic forces, which are strongly influenced by protein structure and environmental conditions (Waiho et al., 2021). Research shows that the binding of fish myofibrillar proteins to aldehyde compounds is primarily mediated by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces. Alterations in the hydrophobic cavities of myofibrillar proteins further contribute to differences in aldehyde-binding capacity (H. Zhao et al., 2024). In contrast, milk proteins can form covalent bonds with aldehydes and furans via Schiff base formation and disulfide linkages (Wang, Han, et al., 2023). Recent reviews have emphasized that protein–flavor interactions, particularly in aquatic food systems, are strongly influenced by protein conformation and molecular interactions, which govern flavor binding, retention, and release behaviors (Verma et al., 2026). Furthermore, dynamic structural transitions of food proteins, driven by environmental factors such as pH and ionic conditions, play a crucial role in modulating the accessibility and affinity of flavor-binding sites, thereby influencing overall flavor perception and stability (Bhuiyan, 2026).

Previous studies have applied various protein extraction techniques, such as acid solubilization followed by alkali precipitation, and the isoelectric point precipitation method has proven effective in isolating proteins with diverse functional properties from aquatic sources (Du et al., 2019; Senadheera et al., 2023). The isoelectric point (pI) of collagen is a critical physicochemical parameter that governs its solubility, aggregation state, and conformation. Extraction at pH values near the pI typically yields precipitates with different structural and functional properties compared to those obtained under alkaline or acidic conditions (Ahmad & Benjakul, 2011). The pH-shifting method, which exploits the solubility changes of proteins at different pH values, has been widely used to extract functional proteins from aquatic by-products, such as fish and krill proteins (Yao et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023). However, its application to sea cucumber collagen has not been extensively studied (Geng et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2022). Compared with plant proteins, freshwater- and marine-derived proteins provide superior nutritional profiles and distinctive flavor characteristics (Han et al., 2019). This study aims to fill this gap by applying the pH-shifting method to sea cucumber collagen and investigating how different pH conditions affect the protein's binding interactions with typical flavor compounds.

In this context, this study systematically explores the interaction mechanisms between sea cucumber collagen proteins and various flavor compounds. By examining proteins extracted under alkaline, neutral, and acidic conditions (ALP, NP, and ACP), this study aimed to provide new insights into how the molecular characteristics of sea cucumber collagen influence its flavor-binding potential. Multi-spectroscopic techniques, including fluorescence spectroscopy, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, are employed to investigate the conformational changes and molecular forces underlying the protein-flavor interactions. These methods allow us to gain a deeper understanding of the structural modifications induced by flavor binding and the key molecular interactions that govern these processes.

Thus, the primary objectives of this study are: (1) to investigate the molecular weight distribution and amino acid composition of sea cucumber collagen under different pH conditions, (2) to evaluate the binding capacity of sea cucumber proteins to selected flavor compounds, and (3) to explore the conformational changes and molecular forces involved in the binding process. This work aims to contribute to the broader field of food chemistry and flavor science, offering a theoretical foundation for improving the quality and flavor retention of protein-rich seafood products.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Salted sea cucumbers (Apostichopus japonicus) were procured from Changhai fish market in Dalian, China, and transported to the laboratory via a cold chain. Prior to experimentation, the sea cucumbers were subjected to desalting by rinsing with running water for 24 to 48 h. After desalting, the cleaned sea cucumbers were carefully incised with a scalpel to remove the sand spouts and then stored at −80 °C. All analytical grade reagents utilized in this study were provided by Aladdin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of sea cucumber collagen fractions (SCCF) and SCCF-flavor

Following minor modifications to the preliminary method (Li et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2017), Sea cucumber collagen crude extract was first dispersed in deionized water at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and homogenized. The suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the precipitate was collected and re-dispersed in deionized water at 4 °C. To extract alkaline-soluble proteins, the pH of the suspension was adjusted to 11.0 ± 0.5 using 0.1 M NaOH and stirred overnight under 4 °C (All the following operations were conducted at 4 °C). The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected as the alkaline-soluble protein fraction (ALP). The obtained alkaline supernatant was subsequently adjusted to pH 5.5 ± 0.5 using 0.5 M acetic acid. After standing and stirring to allow protein precipitation, the mixture was centrifuged under the same conditions. The precipitate was collected as the acidic protein fraction (ACP). The remaining supernatant was further adjusted to neutral pH and collected as the neutral protein fraction (NP). All collected fractions (ALP, ACP, and NP) were freeze-dried and stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

For flavor-binding experiments, equal masses of protein and selected flavor compounds were placed into 20 mL headspace vials, sealed at room temperature, and incubated for 240 min prior to analysis. To ensure reliable interpretation of protein–flavor interactions, several experimental considerations were taken into account. All incubations were conducted under identical and tightly controlled conditions, allowing for relative comparisons among treatments. Although no separate control experiments were designed specifically for flavor compound volatility, protein degradation, or buffer-only systems, the study focused on comparative differences under consistent conditions, thereby minimizing the impact of systematic bias across treatments.

Volatile compound analysis by SPME–GC–MS

Volatile compounds were analyzed using solid-phase microextraction (SPME) coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), according to Li et al. (2025) with minor modifications. A DVB/CAR/PDMS (50/30 μm) fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was conditioned prior to use. Samples were transferred into 20 mL PTFE-capped vials and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. Volatile compounds were extracted from the headspace for 45 min and thermally desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C for 5 min.

GC–MS analysis was conducted on an Agilent 7890B/5977B system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a VF-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The temperature program was as follows: initial temperature 35 °C, ramped to 50 °C at 3 °C/min, to 150 °C at 5 °C/min, and to 240 °C at 10 °C/min, where it was held for 5 min. Retention indices (RI) were calculated using n-alkanes (C7–C30, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as external standards. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing RI values and mass spectra with the NIST 11 library.

Determination of molecular weight distribution of SCCF

The assay design was derived from the experiment conducted by (Shen et al., 2025). The SEC system (Waters ARC, Milford, MA, USA) was equipped with a Superdex 200 column, specifically designed for protein separation based on size. The mobile phase consisted of a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (pH 7.4), ensuring that proteins remain in their native form and avoiding denaturation. The flow rate was set at 0.5 mL/min, and protein standards of known molecular weights were used to construct a calibration curve by plotting the logarithm of the molecular weights against the corresponding retention times.

Amino acid composition analysis

Total amino acid content was determined using a slightly modified version of (L. Qi et al., 2025). Approximately 0.05 g of freeze-dried powder was hydrolyzed with 6 M hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 h under nitrogen protection. After hydrolysis, the pH of the hydrolysate was adjusted to 2.20, and the solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. A 50 μL aliquot of the filtrate was injected into a liquid chromatograph (LC-2010C; Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a CAPCELL PAK C18 MGII column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). Gradient elution was performed using acetonitrile and water. External calibration was carried out using a series of amino acid standards (not L-leucine) to create a calibration curve. Internal calibration was performed using L-leucine as the internal standard, which was added prior to sample hydrolysis to correct for procedural losses. The UV detector was set at 338 nm, and amino acids were identified based on their retention times and quantitated using the calibration curve.

Ultraviolet spectroscopy

Following the method of Li et al. (2022), the ultraviolet absorption spectra of SCCF-Flavor samples were measured using a UV–Vis Lambda 35 spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA). UV spectra of SCCF-Flavor samples at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL were measured in quartz cuvettes with a path length of 1.0 cm. The spectral scan range was 200–700 nm at a scan speed of 480 nm/min.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

The circular dichroism spectrum of the SCCF-Flavor sample was measured using a J-810CD spectrophotometer (Jasco Corporation, Japan) at 25 ± 1 °C. The scan was performed at a rate of 20 nm/min within the wavelength range of 190 to 250 nm using a quartz cuvette with a width of 1.0 cm. The contents of four types of secondary structures (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil) were analyzed using the Cdpro software package (S. Zhang et al., 2019). An average of three scans is reported.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectra of SCCF-Flavor samples were acquired using a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) at 25 ± 1 °C. Each sample was mixed with KBr in a 1:100 ratio and pressed into pellets. Measurement conditions were as follows: scan range 400–4000 cm−1, resolution 4 cm−1, 32 scans, with air used for background scanning. Spectra were analyzed using OMNIC 9.7 software (Thermo Nicolet, Madison, WI, USA) and normalized using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to assess SCCF conformational changes.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The effects of flavor compounds on SCCF fluorescence spectra were evaluated according to the detailed methodology described by Wang, Wang, et al. (2022). The intrinsic fluorescence intensity of SCCF-Flavor was measured at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-2700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), and emission spectra were acquired within the wavelength range of 290–450 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using three independent biological replicates, and each measurement was performed in triplicate (technical replicates). Data were statistically analyzed by IBM SPSS Statistics 18 (Chicago, IL, USA). Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In addition, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were considered in the statistical interpretation. Effect sizes were evaluated based on the magnitude of differences between groups to support the interpretation of statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test at a significance level of P < 0.05. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (San Diego, California, USA).

Results and discussion

Molecular weight distribution and amino acid composition of SCCF

The molecular weight distributions of ALP, NP, and ACP extracted from SCCF using the pH-shifting process are presented in Fig. 1 and Table 1. Significant differences in molecular weight profiles were observed among the three protein fractions (p < 0.05). In the ALP fraction, high-molecular-weight components (>10,000 Da) accounted for the largest proportion (42.39 ± 1.96%), followed by 5000–10,000 Da (23.22 ± 1.18%), 2000–5000 Da (5.56 ± 0.38%), and < 2000 Da (1.91 ± 1.52%). In contrast, NP was predominantly composed of low-molecular-weight peptides (<2000 Da), accounting for 91.36 ± 1.52%, with only minor contributions from higher molecular weight fractions. ACP also showed a relatively high proportion of low-molecular-weight components (<2000 Da, 65.01 ± 3.08%), but with a more distributed profile across intermediate ranges compared to NP. Such differences may be attributed to pH-induced variations in protein solubility, aggregation, and structural stability during the extraction process. Overall, the distinct molecular weight distributions among ALP, NP, and ACP suggest that pH-shifting extraction modulates protein size characteristics, which may subsequently influence the accessibility of binding sites and their interaction behavior with flavor compounds. Analysis of the binding capacity of SCCF to flavor compounds

Extending previous work from our group, we investigated the binding of sea cucumber proteins with different isoelectric points to flavor compounds was investigated using SPME-GC–MS. ALP exhibited a significantly higher binding capacity toward eucalyptol—a cyclic ether with relatively low polarity and limited water solubility that exhibits hydrophobic characteristics in aqueous systems—compared to NP (P < 0.05), with the mean value being approximately 7.6-fold greater (Fig. 2A). This can be attributed to the differences in their molecular charge and hydrophobicity profiles. At alkaline pH, ALP proteins possess a negative charge due to the deprotonation of amino acids like glutamic acid and aspartic acid. This results in enhanced electrostatic attraction between ALP proteins and the positively charged regions of eucalyptol, facilitating stronger binding interactions. In contrast, NP, with its relatively neutral charge at pH 7, demonstrates weaker electrostatic interactions and therefore shows reduced binding affinity for eucalyptol. Conversely, NP showed a significantly higher binding capacity toward estragole than ACP (P < 0.05), with an approximate 5.5-fold difference (Fig. 2B). The higher binding capacity of NP for estragole may be due to its relatively neutral pH, which maintains the proteins in a more stable, folded state. Estragole is a hydrophobic compound, and the hydrophobic interactions between neutral proteins and this compound might be stronger due to their lower surface charge and less structural disruption compared to ALP and ACP, which are more susceptible to conformational changes at extreme pH conditions. Additionally, linalool is a naturally occurring monoterpene alcohol with a branched structure, exhibited superior binding capacity across different isoelectric point proteins, with ALP demonstrating the highest binding capacity compared to ACP and NP. The ability of ALP to bind to linalool may be attributed to the exposure of hydrophobic residues like glycine and proline under alkaline conditions, which increases the protein's affinity for linalool, a hydrophobic compound. In summary, the binding capacity of SCCF proteins varied significantly with their isoelectric point. ALP showed superior binding for eucalyptol and linalool, attributed to its negative charge and exposed hydrophobic residues under alkaline conditions. In contrast, NP exhibited higher affinity for estragole, likely due to its stable conformation at neutral pH favoring hydrophobic interactions. These results directly demonstrate that pH-shifting extraction alters protein surface properties, thereby selectively modulating their flavor-binding profiles. These structural differences may influence their interaction behavior with proteins, particularly in terms of hydrophobic interactions and binding affinity.

The binding experiments revealed distinct affinities of ALP, NP, and ACP for individual flavor compounds. However, in real food matrices, flavors are complex mixtures. Our competitive adsorption experiment (Fig. 3) demonstrated that in a multi-compound system, linalool consistently dominated the binding sites across all protein types, despite the varying affinities observed in single-compound systems. This suggests that beyond intrinsic affinity (influenced by hydrophobicity and charge as discussed), molecular properties such as volatility, steric hindrance, and kinetic factors may govern binding outcomes in complex mixtures (Wang, Zhu, et al., 2022; Zou et al., 2023).Fig. 3The proportion of each flavor compound when SCCF competes with a complex flavor compound for binding.Fig. 3

Flavor compound volatility over the incubation period was expected to occur uniformly across all samples, and thus its influence on comparative analysis is considered limited. Similarly, potential protein structural changes induced by pH treatment were inherent to the experimental design and are discussed as part of the mechanism underlying observed functional differences. In addition, buffer conditions were kept constant across all treatments, ensuring that any potential buffer effects on flavor binding would be consistent and would not interfere with the relative comparison of different protein samples.

The use of controlled experimental conditions and replicated measurements ensured the reliability of the observed trends, while the combination of statistical significance and effect magnitude provides a robust basis for data interpretation.

Secondary structure of SCFF binding with flavor

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of SCCF-flavor complexes (Fig. 4) revealed significant changes in the secondary structure of the proteins, indicating a substantial conformational rearrangement upon binding with flavor compounds. Differences between proteins with varying isoelectric points and their interactions with flavor compounds are illustrated in Fig. 4A–C. The protein-flavor complex displayed a negative peak near 200 nm, a typical feature of the α-helix in protein, which corresponds to π-π* electronic transitions of the peptide backbone (W. Xu et al., 2024). Compared with pure protein samples, the peak intensity at 200 nm showed marked variation in the complexes, indicating conformational changes in the secondary structure. A decrease in ordered secondary structures was observed, accompanied by a significant increase in random coil conformation, suggesting a partial unfolding of the protein and a loss of native collagen-like triple-helical structure. It is important to note that while the CD spectra of native collagen are dominated by a characteristic polyproline II (PPII) helix signal, the pH-shifting extraction process can induce partial denaturation, yielding a mixture of structures more akin to gelatin. In ALP, the addition of eucalyptol, estragole, linalool, and mixed flavor compounds led to a further reduction in ordered secondary structures (quantified as a decrease in the spectral contributions resembling α-helix and β-sheet features by 9.0%, 20.9%, 8.6%, and 11.7%, respectively), with a concomitant increase in the proportion of random coil conformations (Fig. 4D–F). β-sheet content decreased by approximately 14.0%, while random coil structures increased to varying extents. Similar trends in the reduction of ordered secondary structures and an increase in conformational disorder were observed in NP and ACP. These findings indicate that the binding of flavor compounds promotes a further loosening and disordering of the already partially denatured collagen structure. This behavior is consistent with previous observations on the structural flexibility of gelatin and denatured collagen upon interaction with small molecules (Qin et al., 2016). Further analysis revealed that specific amino acid residues, particularly Glu, Pro, and Gly, play a critical role in these structural changes. The polar and charged residues like Glu are involved in hydrogen bonding with the flavor compounds, while the hydrophobic residues (e.g., Pro and Gly) interact with hydrophobic flavor compounds like eucalyptol and linalool through van der Waals forces. This conformational change exposes hydrophobic patches, thereby enhancing the protein's interaction with flavor compounds. The increase in β-turns and random coils indicates protein flexibility and increased exposure of hydrophobic residues, which likely contribute to the protein's enhanced binding capacity for various flavor compounds. This suggests that the binding of flavor compounds induces significant structural disorder, which may improve the protein's functional properties and enhance its interaction with volatile flavor compounds.

Analysis of the functional group of SCCF-flavor

FTIR spectroscopy revealed notable spectral shifts in NP upon binding with flavor compounds (Fig. 5A). The O—H and N—H stretching band at 3350 cm−1 shifted to higher frequencies (up to 3399 cm−1), which typically indicates a weakening or disruption of hydrogen bonding interactions rather than their formation. This suggests that the interaction with flavor compounds may alter the local microenvironment and reduce intermolecular hydrogen bonding within the protein matrix(Brand et al., 2015). The extent of the shift varied with compound functional groups. In addition, a characteristic band at approximately 1037 cm−1, associated with C–O–C stretching vibrations, was observed. Changes in the intensity of this band suggest the involvement of polar groups in the interaction process. This observation is consistent with previous findings on collagen-aroma systems, where hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding were identified as the primary forces driving the binding of volatile compounds to the protein matrix (N. The most abundant amino acids in alkaline proteins were Gly and Glu. Under alkaline conditions, these amino acids facilitated the exposure of hydrophobic residues, including Gly, Pro, and Ala. Pro plays multiple positive biological roles in collagen stability, while Glu is a typical umami amino acid that contributes significantly to seafood flavor. Glu also accounted for a substantial proportion in both neutral and acidic proteins.

Asp and Glu are recognized as major umami amino acids in sea cucumber proteins. Glutamic acid, in particular, plays a critical role in flavor formation in seafood systems (S. Cao et al., 2025). In this study, the content of Asp and Glu in ALP was higher than that in NP and ACP (Fig. 6A), suggesting potential differences in interaction behavior. The negatively charged carboxyl groups of Asp and Glu residues may participate in hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with flavor compounds (Wang, Luan, et al., 2023). The content of Asp and Glu in ALP is 2 to 3 times higher than in NP and ACP. In an alkaline environment, hydrophobic amino acids such as glycine, proline, and alanine residues are exposed. Pro contributes to the structural stability of collagen due to its role in maintaining the triple-helix conformation. The Gly-to-Pro ratio was approximately 1.7, which is comparable to values typically reported for marine collagen systems when accounting for species variation and processing effects (Saito et al., 2002; M. Zhang et al., 2023). Variations in the Gly-to-Pro ratio have been widely documented and may be attributed to differences in biological origin, extraction conditions, and structural modifications incurred during processing.

The amino acid composition of SCCF proteins may influence their interaction with flavor compounds. For example, polar amino acids such as Glu may contribute to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, while non-polar residues may facilitate hydrophobic interactions. Additionally, pH-induced changes in amino acid distribution may alter the overall charge and conformational flexibility of the proteins, thereby affecting their binding behavior with different flavor compounds.

UV–vis and fluorescence spectra analysis of SCCF-flavor

The UV–Vis spectra of SCCF–flavor complexes (Fig. 6D-F) exhibited a characteristic absorption peak around 280 nm, which is mainly associated with aromatic amino acid residues such as Trp and Tyr. Variations in absorbance intensity and slight shifts in peak position were observed after interaction with flavor compounds, suggesting changes in the microenvironment surrounding these residues (Yang et al., 2025). Such spectral changes are commonly associated with protein–ligand interactions and alterations in molecular conformation (Diao et al., 2022).

In the ALP–flavor system, the magnitude of absorbance variation was greater than that observed in NP and ACP, which may be related to differences in amino acid composition, particularly the relative abundance of aromatic residues. In mixed flavor systems, the degree of spectral variation fell between those of single-compound systems, suggesting potential competitive interactions among multiple ligands. Overall, UV spectroscopy provides supportive evidence for protein–flavor interactions through monitoring changes in the local environment of aromatic residues.

The intrinsic fluorescence of proteins is typically modulated by aromatic amino acid residues such as Phe, Tyr, and Trp. Since the exposure of aromatic amino acid residues in a liquid environment leads to changes in fluorescence emission intensity, these variations indicate conformational changes in SCCF. The fluorescence intensity of the protein solution decreased upon binding with flavor compounds (Fig. 6 G-I), indicating an interaction that quenches the intrinsic fluorescence. This phenomenon indicates that flavor substances can interact with SCCF and quench its intrinsic fluorescence (Z. Zhao et al., 2025). The maximum emission wavelength of SCCF was initially observed at approximately 330 nm, indicating that Trp residues were located in a relatively nonpolar environment. Following interaction with flavor compounds, a red shift of the emission maximum (from ∼330 nm to ∼340 nm) was observed, accompanied by decreased fluorescence intensity. These changes suggest that Trp residues became more exposed to a polar environment, possibly due to conformational rearrangements of the protein structure (C. Cao et al., 2024). It should be noted that fluorescence quenching may arise from multiple mechanisms, including static or dynamic quenching, and therefore cannot be attributed to a single type of interaction. Nevertheless, the combined decrease in fluorescence intensity and red shift in emission wavelength indicates alterations in the local environment of aromatic residues upon binding. Taken together, the UV–Vis and fluorescence results suggest that protein–flavor interactions induce conformational changes and modify the microenvironment of aromatic amino acids. These changes may involve multiple interaction forces, including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions (Liu et al., 2025).

While this study provides systematic insights into the pH-shifting extraction and functional properties of sea cucumber-derived proteins, several considerations define the scope of the findings. First, the raw material was obtained from a single source, which ensured consistency in evaluating pH-induced effects. However, natural variability related to biological and environmental factors may influence protein characteristics and should be explored in future studies. Second, the experimental procedures involved repeated sample preparation and multiple collections under controlled conditions to ensure data reliability. Further validation across independent production batches would strengthen the broader applicability of the results. Third, a simplified model system was employed to isolate the effects of pH on protein structure and functionality. While real food matrices are more complex, this approach enables clearer identification of underlying mechanisms and provides a foundation for future studies in complex systems. Fourth, sensory and in vivo evaluations were beyond the scope of this study, as the primary focus was on physicochemical and structural characterization. These results serve as a necessary basis for subsequent application-oriented and perceptual investigations.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the molecular mechanisms of sea cucumber collagen-protein interactions with flavor compounds using pH-shifting protein extraction. Our findings revealed that the molecular weight distribution of proteins extracted at different isoelectric points significantly influences their binding affinity for various flavor compounds. Specifically, ALP exhibited the strongest binding to hydrophobic flavor compounds, while NP showed greater affinity for hydrophilic flavor compounds. Spectroscopic analyses (CD, FTIR, UV–Vis, and fluorescence) consistently indicated that protein–flavor interactions are accompanied by conformational rearrangements and changes in the local microenvironment of functional groups and aromatic residues. FTIR results suggested alterations in hydrogen bonding interactions, while fluorescence and UV–Vis analyses revealed changes in the environment of aromatic amino acids, indicating partial structural reorganization upon ligand binding. Combined with amino acid analysis, the results suggest that both polar residues (e.g., Glu) and non-polar residues (e.g., Pro and Gly) may contribute to protein–flavor interactions through multiple forces, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic effects. These findings support a mechanism in which pH-induced structural modulation, altered amino acid exposure and charge distribution, changes in intermolecular interactions, differential flavor binding behavior.

In conclusion, this study provides a mechanistic understanding of how pH-shifting extraction influences protein structure and composition, thereby modulating protein–flavor interactions. This insight may offer a useful basis for regulating flavor retention in marine protein-based food systems through controlled processing conditions.
