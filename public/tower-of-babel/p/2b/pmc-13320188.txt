Molecular characterization of emerging bacterial communities associated with honey bees
Authors: Shazia Amin, Shakir Ullah, Nasreen Nasreen, Ioannis A Giantsis, Maria V Alvanou, Sadaf Niaz, Turki M Dawoud, Esmael M Alyami, Youssouf Ali Younous, Adil Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHoney bees are vital pollinators essential for ecosystems and global agriculture, but their populations are declining due to pressures caused by pests. This study reports the presence of Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus terrae, and Pseudomonas spp. in four different honey bee species in Pakistan for the first time, uncovering a previously neglected aspect of honey bee microbiology. From January 2023 to June 2024; 1190 honey bees were collected from four districts in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and they were identified. Molecular screening based on PCR targeting the microbial 16 S rDNA resulted in the detection of potential bacterial pathogens across different districts. In Buner, K. pneumoniae and E. coli were detected in Apis mellifera and Apis cerana individuals while Apis florea was positive only for K. pneumoniae. In Swat, Pseudomonas spp. was identified in A. mellifera, Apis dorsata and A. florea, while in A. cerana no pathogen was detected. In Lower Dir, A. mellifera and A. florea were positive for Pseudomonas spp., and A. cerana for K. pneumoniae, with A. dorsata being pathogen-free. In Malakand, K. pneumoniae and E. coli was found in A. mellifera and A. cerana, while A. florea harbored only Pseudomonas spp., and A. dorsata had no pathogens. Finally, P. terrae was detected exclusively in A. mellifera from Buner and Swat. Morphological analysis revealed significant interspecies variation, with A. dorsata exhibiting the largest values in tongue length (4.27 ± 0.21 mm), forewing size (12.8 ± 0.19 mm), and the highest cubital index (2.71 ± 0.04 mm). A. mellifera was classified in second place, followed by A cerana and A. florea, which characterized by the lowest measurement values. In the present study significant bacterial pathogens were identified representing emerging threat to honey bees in Pakistan while data regarding their phenotypic characterization and phylogenetic position were provided for the first time in the region.Keywords: Honey bees, Bacterial pathogens, Apis sp., Pakistan, Monitoring, Escherichia, Klebsiella, Proteus, PseudomonasSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Microbiology, Zoology

Introduction

Honey bees Apis mellifera represent significant pollinators and are of major importance for agricultural ecosystems as well as for wild flora and fauna1. They support biodiversity conservation and food production. Inextricably linked to their environment, honey bees are responsible for the pollination of over 75% of the major crops globally and are expected to contribute between 235 to 577 billion US dollars per year in global crop production2, 3. However, honeybee’ significant ecological and economic role is presently in jeopardy due to decline in their populations caused by an array of stressors such as habitat erosion, biocides, climate change and invasive pathogen species4, 5, 6. Amongst these, bacterial infections have been reported to be one of the prominent issues concerning honey bee health7, 8. Some bacterial populations have vital roles towards honeybees digestion, immunity, and pathogen defense9. However, variations in the types and quantities of microbial inhabitants as a result of environmental alterations or invasion by pathogenic bacteria may be catastrophic for bees causing dysbiosis7, 10. Some bacterial threats identified to pose high risk to honey bees globally include Paenibacillus larvae and Melissococcus plutonius responsible for American foulbrood and European foulbrood diseases respectively11, 12. Nevertheless, quantitative-infectious risk, especially regarding potential pathogenic bacteria, is relatively unknown, mainly in developing countries including Pakistan where apiculture pathogenicity studies are scarce 13, 14, 15. It is therefore crucial to investigate the presence of potential pathogens inhabiting honey bees population mainly for monitoring potential future risks of apiculture.

Apiculture in Pakistan still remains underdeveloped; however it is characterized by high potential towards the increase of income of rural sectors and improval of agriculture production16. Βacterial threats are invaluable to fill a significant knowledge gap in the microbial ecology of honey bees in Pakistan. Since the country has different agro-ecological zones and various types of plants and trees, the honey bee colonies can experience a wide range of environmental stress factors enhanced by bacterial infections. Misuse of pesticide and inefficiency in management of hives in Pakistan are expected to worsen the situation.

Pathogenic bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis, and Serratia marcescens, have been detected to contribute to mortality outcomes in adult honey bees9. Furthermore, in 2015, bee diseases caused by Enterobacteria were first reported in Eastern Europe, specifically in the Krasnodar Territory of the Russian Federation; with E. coli included among the bacterial microorganisms that were detected17. E. coli belongs to the family Enterobacteriaceae while it has been found in honey bees and may represent a sign of environmental pollution or possible stress factors that weaken immunity18, 19. In general, studies have shown that the bee intestine harbors bacterial populations from at least 10 genera, including in the family of Enterobacteriaceae, such as Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Providencia, Proteus, Citrobacter, Hafnia, Escherichia, Pantoea, Morganella, and Serratia20. A member of Klebsiella genus; Klebsiella aerogenes was detected as the etiological factor for bee colony collapses in Ukraine21. In the same study also K. pneumoniae pure culture was obtained while the same species was also isolated from adult honey bees in Ethiopia22. Pseudomonas species were detected in the bacterial communities associated with A. mellifera foragers23, while it was also found as a major core taxon in solitary bees, suggesting a significant role in their gut microbiome24. Additionally, Pseudomonas species have been isolated from the stomachs of honey bees, with Pseudomonas spp. being predominant in April25. The metabolic versatility of Pseudomonas allows them to produce a wide array of bioactive enzymes and secondary metabolites. This adaptability suggests they may play a role in bee well-being26, 27. Currently, there is limited scientific literature specifically addressing the presence or role of Proteus sp. in honey bees. However, studies have observed that Proteus mirabilis, was increased in abundance in the gut microbiota of honey bees infected with Nosema ceranae, a common intestinal parasite28. P. mirabilis, which showed an increased relative abundance in infected honey bees, is believed to have beneficial effects against Paenibacillus larvae, which is responsible for American foulbrood disease29.

A vast body of research on honey bee pathogens has primarily focused on viruses and mites (e.g., Varroa destructor), as these factors have posed significant and long-standing challenges to the beekeeping sector worldwide. While these urgent issues have understandably been a major research focus, bacterial infections in honey bees have received comparatively less attention30, 31, 32. Given that apiculture serves as a vital economic resource for rural communities in Pakistan and contributes significantly to agricultural productivity, there is a growing need to expand our understanding of all potential threats to honey bee health. This study aims to contribute to that effort by monitoring the presence of bacteria that may act as potential pathogens in the future across four honey bee species (Apis mellifera, Apis dorsata, Apis cerana, and Apis florea) collected from four different districts in Pakistan.

Materials and methods

Study area

The current research focuses on four districts situated in Northern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa: These districts are Buner which is located at geographical coordinates (34.3943° N, 72.6151° E), Lower Dir (34. 9161° N, 71. 8097° E), Swat (34.8065° N, 72.3548° E), Malakand (34.5030° N, 71.9046° E). The topography of these areas concerns a wide variety of habitats including mountains, rich green woods, deciduous wood lands, frozen grounds and human habitats. Due to the cloud cover, the region experiences high precipitation rates and low evaporation leading to moderate temperatures ranging between − 3 and 35 °C. Interestingly, variations of humidity range at a yearly level of 60%. Coordinates were identified using a Global Positioning System GPS tool and they were documented on a Microsoft Excel table. These coordinates were further used in the R Statistical Software version 4. 3. 1. to generate distribution map of the analyzed regions as the one shown in Fig. 1.

Honey bee collection and morphological identification

A total of 1190 honey bee was collected from the four districts during January 2023 to June 2024. 465; 292; 258 and 185 specimens were collected from Buner; Swat; lower Dir and Malakand districts respectively. To eliminate the possibility of damaging the DNA, all individuals were washed with distilled water before being placed in Eppendorf tubes containing 70% ethanol and 5% glycerin; which were labeled respectively and left at room temperature for further analysis. Details concerning the collected specimens – locality, host, number of honey bee, and the date of collection were also documented. All the specimens were morphologically identified using a stereo-zoom microscope following the morphological keys described by33, 34.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

DNA was extracted from 600 samples in total, i.e. 200 from A. mellifera, 180 Apis cerana, 155 A. florea and 65 A. dorsata. To extract DNA, the insect bodies were washed with distilled water and dried on sterile filter paper. Using a surgical blade to dissect each specimen individually, the honeybees were then crushed up into little pieces with a sterilized mortar and pestle. Following that, sterile 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes were filled with the pellet. The phenol-chloroform method was used to extract genomic DNA, adhering to the procedure described by Sambrook, Fritsch, and Maniatis (1989). A NanoDrop spectrophotometer was used to evaluate the extracted DNA’s quality and quantity.

Using a previously designed primer pair provided in Table 1 (Youngseob et al. 2005)35, a segment of 460 bp of the16S rDNA was amplified for the molecular identification of pathogens. PCR reactions were carried out in total volumes of 25.5 µL containing 12 µL of the Master mix, 0.4 pmol of the forward and reverse primers, 8 µL of nuclease-free PCR water and 3.5 µL of the 50ng genomic DNA template. Negative controls were added by substituting DNA template with PCR water. The positive control for 16 S DNA was used previously isolated from tick and Rickettsia spp (unpublished data). The amplified DNA fragments were subsequently electrophorized in 1.6% agarose gel, stained with 2% ethidium bromide to reveal the presence of different bands in the samples. The gels formed were further observed in UV light using GelDoc software of BioDoc-It™ Imaging Systems, UVP, LLC.

Sequencing, phylogenetic and statistical analysis

Thermo Fisher Scientific’s ExoSap-IT was used to purify all PCR products of the anticipated size. The purified PCR products were shipped to Mission Biotech in Taipei, Taiwan, for Sanger sequencing, with both forward and reverse primers, in an ABI Prism 310 Genetic Analyzer capillary sequencer (Applied Biosystems). The sequences were first trimmed using FinchTV (version 1.4.0) to remove primer-contaminated areas and any misread nucleotides at the beginning and end of the sequence. Using the NCBI platform’s Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) method, further sequences were selected and obtained for further analysis. The ClustalW multiple sequence alignment technique in MEGA 11 was used to align all sequences36. The aligned sequences were exported as nexus file to be used for subsequent phylogenetic analysis. A model selection test was conducted for all sequences using IQ-tree Model Finder. The best-fit substitution model was chosen based on Akaike information criterion (AIC), corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc), and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values, with the “best-fit” replacement model being identified as the one with the lowest BIC, AIC, or AICc. The Maximum Likelihood technique in IQ-Tree was used to create phylogenetic trees, together with the 1000 rapid bootstrap approximation37, and 1000 approximate likelihood ratio test. Eventually, all data related to honey bee were inserted into MS Excel spreadsheet. The data have been analyzed in Excel to calculate the total prevalence and morphometric analyses.

Results

Overall, 1190 honeybee individuals where collected and identified morphologically, belonging to 4 species, as follows: 361; 285; 275 and 269 specimens of A. mellifera, A. dorsata A. florea and A. cerana respectively. Regarding each different district, the highest individuals number was collected in District Buner (465 individuals) followed by Swat (282 individuals), lower Dir (258 individuals), and Malakand (185 individuals). All the collected samples were screened for pathogen detection. In District Buner, K. pneumoniae and E. coli were recorded in A. mellifera and A. cerana populations, respectively. A. florea was only positive for K. pneumoniae while no pathogen was recorded in A. dorsata collected from the same district. Pseudomonas spp. were recorded in all the three species A. mellifera, A. dorsata and A. florea collected from the district Swat, with A. cerana collected from this district being negative for all pathogens. A. mellifera and A. florea of lower Dir found positive for Pseudomonas spp.; A. cerana for K. pneumoniae; while A. dorsata was negative for all pathogens. In district Malakand K. pneumoniae and E. coli were recorded in A. cerana and A. mellifera, with A. florea being only positive for Pseudomonas spp. while no pathogens were recorded in A. dorsata. Finally, P. terrae was detected in A. mellifera collected from district Buner and district Swat. All the aforementioned findings are summarized in Table 2; Fig. 2.

Morphometric analysis and morphological feature

All A. mellifera individuals were medium-sized bees (12–14 mm long). The color varied from golden-yellow to dark brown or black, often with orange-banded abdomens, with dense hair on the thorax, especially in workers and distinct wing venation patterns. The colony size was large accounting up to 60,000 individuals. A. melifera is characterized by a high level of domestication while it is adapted to a wide climates range38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43. A. dorsata (Giant Honey Bee) were always larger in size reaching 17–20 mm in length. Their color were mostly black with golden-yellow bands on the abdomen and their hair sparse compared to A. mellifera. Wings are strong and longer than other honey bee species. A. dorsata were characterized by a more aggressive behavior, turning its domestication into a difficult task44, 45, 46, 47. A. florea (Dwarf Honey Bee) individuals were smaller than A. melifera, and A. dorsata, reaching 7–10 mm in length. Their color was reddish-brown with light yellow bands on the abdomen and their wings were shorter relatively to their body size48, 49, 50. A. cerana (Asian Honey Bee) sizes were smaller than A. mellifera, about 10–12 mm with dark color and narrow, distinct yellow or brown bands on the abdomen, as well as hair and sparse on the body and dense on the thorax. The wings were slightly smaller than A. mellifera, but with similar venation patterns40, 51, 52, 53. We also found here that A. dorsata had the longest tongue (4.27 ± 0.21 mm), forewings (12.8 ± 0.19 mm), highest cubital index (2.71 ± 0.04 mm), most wing hooks (27), and widest tergites (3rd: 2.72 mm, 4th: 2.79 mm). A. mellifera ranked second in all these traits, with a tongue length of (3.77 ± 0.17 mm), forewings measuring (3.15 ± 0.05 mm), cubital index of 2.90 ± 0.06 mm, whereas A. florea had the smallest measurements: tongue (2.21 ± 0.13 mm), forewings (2.0 ± 0.06 mm), cubital index (2.97 ± 0.29 mm), A. cerana. Discussion

In this study, a comprehensive screening for potential bacterial pathogens was conducted across four honeybee species. A. mellifera plays a pivotal role in global agriculture as a dominant pollinator of numerous vegetable food crops and a key contributor to plant biodiversity1. Pollination services provided by honeybees enhance both the quantity and quality of fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds. Additionally, honeybees contribute significantly to the production of economically valuable products such as honey, beeswax, and royal jelly, which are integral to human diets and constitute major internationally traded commodities68, 69. This dual role underscores their ecological and economic significance70.

However, honeybee populations have been experiencing global declines due to multiple stressors, including pests, pathogens, pesticide exposure, and habitat degradation71, 72. While extensive research has focused on viral diseases, parasitic mites such as Varroa destructor, and the effects of pesticide treatments, bacterial pathogens affecting honeybees remain relatively understudied73, 74. This study represents one of the few investigations aimed at identifying potential bacterial pathogens in honeybee colonies within Pakistan, a country where apiculture has only recently begun to develop.

E. coli, is among the bacterial species that was detected in the present study in the honey bee samples. It has been observed that honey bees can be susceptible towards E. coli infection. Previous research has shown that injecting high doses of viable E. coli into honey bee larvae and adults caused no mortality, indicating a robust immune response. However, drone and worker pupae exhibited susceptibility to E. coli infection, leading to increased mortality. This suggests that while adult bees can effectively manage E. coli exposure, pupae are more vulnerable75. The immunocompetence of honey bees towards the E. coli infection is influenced by a plethora of factors i.e. developmental stage; colony position and season. Furthermore, the pathways that are related to immune response in honey bees are found to be altered after an E. coli infection. More specifically, winter honeybees kill viable E. coli faster and more reliably in comparison to summer honey bees; which is also depicted from the upregulation of only three gene pathways (hymenoptaecin, defensin1 and immune responsive protein 30 IRP30) in comparison to the upregulation of seven pathways ((defensin1, abaecin, hymenoptaecin, phenoloxidase (PO), carboxylesterases (CEs), peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRPs), and (IRP30)) in summer and winter honey bees respectively.

Furthermore, in the present study also K. pneumoniae was also identified in bee samples. Certain Klebsiella species, notably K. pneumoniae and Klebsiella aerogenes, have been identified as pathogens in honey bees. These bacteria can cause a condition referred to as “bee klebsiellosis,” characterized by high mortality rates in both larvae and adult bees76. In honey bees, the colonization with K. pneumoniae predisposes the bees to secondary opportunistic infections, especially where the colony is under other forms of stress such as Varroa mite infestation or exposure to chemical pesticides77, 78. Infected bees exhibit symptoms such as apathy, anorexia, dyspepsia, diarrhea leading to body dehydration, loss of flight ability, and intoxication of larvae. These manifestations can culminate in high mortality rates within colonies76. Furthermore, K. pneumoniae has been isolated from the digestive tracts of honey bees, indicating its role in gut infections22.

Proteus spp. bacteria can be detected in bee intestines79. From a previous study members of the Proteus genus were detected in bees fed sugar supplement, constituting a relatively large part of the bacteria found in this group80. In general members of the Proteus genus, has been found to play commensal, pathogenic and beneficial roles as well81. A consensus in bee microbiota studies suggests that low levels of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and high levels of Proteobacteria, which include Proteus species, are indicative of gut dysbiosis. Such imbalances can impair nutrient absorption, weaken immune responses, and increase susceptibility to pathogens. The presence of Proteus species is typically minimal or absent in healthy bees, further underscoring that their proliferation is associated with dysbiotic conditions. The proliferation of Proteus mirabilis in the gut microbiota of honey bees is associated with dysbiosis, particularly under stress conditions such as Nosema ceranae infection28. Furthermore, flies and cockroaches are widely recognized as vectors of various microorganisms, including Proteus mirabilis and Proteus vulgaris strains. These insects can harbor the bacteria on their bodies, facilitating their spread and contributing to food contamination, spoilage, and potential human infections81. Thus, summarizing the above, the detection of P. terrae in bee samples is a very interesting finding opening a new research perspective.

While Pseudomonas species are commonly found in association with bees and bee products, their role as pathogens in natural settings remains unclear27. Experimental evidence suggests that certain strains, like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, can be pathogenic under specific conditions. However, in natural environments, Pseudomonas may play beneficial roles within the bee microbiome. Further research is necessary to fully understand the interactions between Pseudomonas species and bee health. From previous study, after bee immersion them into P. aeruginosa bacterial suspension a mortality rate of 66.8% within 10–50 h post-infection, highlited that under certain conditions, P. aeruginosa can be pathogenic to honeybees82. Pseudomonas sp. is also categorized among the The major bacterial genera associated with stingless bees83. Furthermore84, have demonstrated 12 bacterial genera in the core pollen microbiome, among which the most abundant was Pseudomonas, while it is closely associated with the plant species and the pollination type.

Thus, while E. coli, Pseudomonas sp., Proteus sp., and K pneumoniae have been identified in association with honey bees, their exact roles—whether as pathogens, opportunistic invaders, or transient environmental contaminants remain incompletely understood. Experimental evidence suggests that certain strains, such as P. aeruginosa, can exhibit pathogenic potential under specific conditions, leading to bee mortality. However, these bacteria are also commonly found in the bee microbiome and colony environment, where their presence does not always indicate disease. Given the growing concerns over pollinator health, further research is necessary to determine whether these bacteria play a direct role in honey bee diseases or if they are secondary invaders exploiting weakened immune systems. Understanding their interactions with the bee microbiome, immunity, and environmental stressors will be crucial in assessing their true impact on honey bee populations.

Conclusion

The present study provides the first report on the association of E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. terrae, and Pseudomonas spp. with honey bees in Pakistan, highlighting potential microbial risks to these essential pollinators. The findings reveal previously uncharacterized bacterial presence within the honey bee colony microbiome and their potential role in colony health decline and hive agitation. By integrating microbiological and morphometric analyses, this research offers valuable insights into microbial infections and physiological variations in honey bees. The results underscore the need for targeted investigations into the pathogenicity, transmission dynamics, and ecological interactions of these bacteria. Environmental stressors, such as intensive agricultural practices and exposure to contaminated resources, may contribute to their emergence, raising concerns about overall ecosystem integrity. Consequently, the establishment of surveillance programs is crucial to monitoring and mitigating microbial threats. While the revival of certain industries may benefit the economy, it could also introduce pollutants that negatively impact estuarine and terrestrial ecosystems. Preventive measures, including habitat restoration and microbiota management, could play a pivotal role in mitigating these risks. This research lays the foundation for protecting honey bee colonies and preserving their critical pollination services, which are essential for both agricultural sustainability and ecosystem stability.
