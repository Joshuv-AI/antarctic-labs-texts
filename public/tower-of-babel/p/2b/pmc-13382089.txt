Socioeconomic and smoking-related disparities in lung Cancer prevalence among U.S.Adults: a pooled analysis of the behavioral risk factor surveillance system, 2022–2024
Authors: Gemechu B. Gerbi, Alicia L. Best, Brenton Powers, Stephanie Miles-Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine independent and cumulative effects of sociodemographic, socioeconomic, geographic, and smoking-related factors associated with lung cancer among U.S. adults aged ≥18 years using pooled Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) 2022–2024 data.MethodsWe conducted a pooled, survey-weighted cross-sectional analysis of BRFSS 2022–2024 to examine factors associated with self-reported lung cancer among U.S. adults aged ≥18 years. Sequential logistic regression models assessed independent and cumulative effects of demographic, socioeconomic, geographic, and smoking-related variables.ResultsIn fully adjusted models, lung cancer was associated with female sex (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.19), Black race (OR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.34, 4.17), low income (<$25,000: OR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.19, 5.26), and residence in the Northeast (OR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.39, 4.12) and Midwest (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.19, 3.18). Education remained independently associated after smoking adjustment. Heavy and very heavy cumulative smoking exposure showed strongest associations (31–40 pack-years: OR = 3.01; >40 pack-years: OR = 4.40). Adjustment for pack-years attenuated effects of age of smoking initiation, indicating cumulative exposure as the dominant behavioral driver.ConclusionsLung cancer among U.S. adults is strongly patterned by cumulative smoking exposure and persistent socioeconomic and geographic disparities. Prevention should prioritize tobacco exposure reduction alongside structural interventions addressing social disadvantage.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States and globally, accounting for a substantial proportion of all cancer deaths despite advances in prevention, early detection, and treatment strategies (Siegel et al., 2024; Sung et al., 2021). Tobacco smoking is the most important modifiable risk factor, responsible for most cases worldwide (US Surgeon General, 2014; Humans, I.W.G.o.t.E.o.C.R.t., 2004). However, smoking alone does not fully explain persistent disparities in lung cancer incidence and mortality across population subgroups.

Socioeconomic disadvantages, racial/ethnic inequities, and geographic variation remain key determinants of lung cancer risk and outcomes, often independent of smoking behavior (US Surgeon General, 2014). These structural factors shape exposure to tobacco and constrain access to prevention, screening, and timely treatment, compounding risk among vulnerable populations. Mechanisms include greater exposure to environmental carcinogens, reduced access to cessation resources and care, and chronic psychosocial stress with potential biological effects (Singh and Jemal, 2017a; Hastert et al., 2016; Adler and Rehkopf, 2008). Racial disparities persist, with Black Americans experiencing disproportionate mortality despite similar or lower smoking prevalence in some groups, reflecting structural inequities in healthcare access and quality (DeSantis et al., 2019; Haiman et al., 2006a).

Although prior studies have examined smoking and socioeconomic disparities, fewer have jointly assessed these factors using nationally representative data with cumulative exposure measures such as pack-years (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). It also remains unclear whether socioeconomic factors independently predict lung cancer after accounting for detailed smoking exposure. Therefore, this study estimates lung cancer prevalence among U.S. adults and examines independent and combined associations of socioeconomic factors and smoking exposure using sequential multivariable models to inform comprehensive prevention strategies addressing both behavioral and structural risks.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used pooled 2022–2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data, a nationally representative survey of non-institutionalized U.S. adults aged ≥18 years (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). BRFSS is the largest ongoing Unted States (U.S.) health surveillance system, collecting standardized information on health behaviors, chronic conditions, and preventive practices from more than 450,000 respondents annually across all states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). Three consecutive survey years were combined to improve statistical power and stabilize estimates for rare outcomes such as lung cancer.

BRFSS uses a dual-frame landline and cellular random-digit-dialing design, with one randomly selected adult per household for landlines and direct selection for cellular respondents. The survey employs a multistage probability sampling design with stratification and clustering. Survey weights adjust for unequal selection probabilities, nonresponse, and post-stratification to generate nationally representative estimates (American Association for Public Opinion Research, 2023). Response rates are calculated using AAPOR RR4, with a median state response rate of 47.0% in 2024 (range: 25.1%–60.1%) (American Association for Public Opinion Research, 2023).

The final analytic sample included 48,302 U.S. adults aged ≥18 years from the 2022–2024 BRFSS after applying eligibility criteria and excluding records with missing outcome or key covariate data. A complete-case approach was used, retaining respondents with valid information on lung cancer status, sociodemographic factors, and smoking variables (age at initiation and pack-years), while excluding “don't know,” “refused,” and missing responses. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies. The STROBE checklist was used to ensure complete and transparent reporting of the study design, data sources, analytical methods, and results.

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a surveillance system administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The dataset contains no personally identifiable information and is publicly available for research purposes. Therefore, this study involved secondary analysis of anonymized data and did not involve direct contact with human participants. The study was conducted in accordance with applicable guidelines for the protection of human subjects, including privacy and confidentiality safeguards.

Measures

The primary outcome was self-reported lung cancer status, classified as yes or no based on participants' responses to the BRFSS cancer history questionnaire.

The primary exposures were cumulative smoking exposure and age at smoking initiation. Cumulative smoking exposure was assessed using pack-years, a comprehensive measure that integrates smoking intensity and duration and provides a more accurate estimate of lifetime tobacco dose than smoking status alone. Pack-year exposure was categorized as never smokers (0 pack-years), light smokers (0.1–10 pack-years), mild-to-moderate smokers (11–20 pack-years), moderate smokers (21–30 pack-years), heavy smokers (31–40 pack-years), and very heavy smokers (>40 pack-years), enabling evaluation of dose-response gradients across increasing exposure levels (U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, 2021).

Age at smoking initiation was included as an indicator of early-life exposure timing and potential biological vulnerability to carcinogenesis. It was categorized as early initiation (<15 years), middle adolescence (15–18 years), late adolescence (19–20 years), adult initiation (≥21 years), and never smokers, reflecting critical developmental periods during which earlier exposure may confer heightened long-term cancer risk (Hoffman et al., 2015; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2023a).

Smoking intensity measured as cigarettes per day (CPD) was initially considered; however, it was excluded from the final models due to collinearity with pack-years and resulting instability in regression estimates when simultaneously modeled. Pack-years and age at initiation were retained because they capture complementary and more stable dimensions of tobacco exposure, representing cumulative dose and timing of initiation, respectively, and improving interpretability of long-term smoking-related risk (US Surgeon General, 2014; Hoffman et al., 2015).

Covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with lung cancer risk and included key sociodemographic and geographic characteristics. These variables comprised sex; age group (18–44, 45–64, and ≥ 65 years); race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, and Other); educational attainment (less than high school, high school/GED, some college, and college graduate); household income (<$25,000 to ≥$150,000); and U.S. Census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West). These variables were included in multivariable models to control for potential confounding and adjust the associations between smoking-related measures and self-reported lung cancer for relevant sociodemographic and geographic factors.

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex BRFSS sampling design by incorporating stratification, clustering, and survey weights to produce nationally representative estimates of the U.S. adult population. Three survey cycles (2022–2024) were pooled before analysis to enhance statistical power and improve the precision and stability of estimates for the relatively rare outcome of self-reported lung cancer.

Descriptive statistics were computed to characterize sociodemographic, geographic, and smoking-related factors according to lung cancer status. Group differences were assessed using survey-weighted chi-square tests.

Multivariable associations between covariates and self-reported lung cancer were examined using sequential survey-weighted logistic regression models. A staged modeling approach was used to evaluate the incremental contribution of key domains of risk factors. Model 1 included sex and race/ethnicity; Model 2 added age group; Model 3 further adjusted for educational attainment; Model 4 incorporated household income; Model 5 added U.S. Census region; Model 6 included age at smoking initiation; and Model 7 incorporated cumulative smoking exposure measured in pack-years. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Cary NC USA, 2026)).

Results

Among 48,302 U.S. adults included in the pooled 2022–2024 BRFSS analytic sample, 1185 respondents (2.45%) reported a history of lung cancer. Lung cancer prevalence varied significantly across sociodemographic, geographic, and smoking-related characteristics, with a consistent concentration among older adults and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations (Table 1).

Prevalence was slightly higher among females (2.63%) than males (2.23%), and marked racial/ethnic disparities were evident, with the highest prevalence among Black adults (4.90%), followed by Other race/ethnicity (2.94%), White adults (2.35%), and Hispanic adults (2.04%) (Table 1). A strong age gradient was observed, increasing from 0.92% among adults aged 18–44 years to 2.69% among those aged ≥65 years (Table 1).

Educational attainment showed a pronounced inverse association, ranging from 1.37% among college graduates to 6.61% among those with some high school education. A strong socioeconomic gradient was also evident by household income, decreasing from 5.01% among individuals earning <$25,000 to 0.98% among those earning ≥$150,000 (Table 1). Geographic variation was significant, with higher prevalence in the Midwest (2.75%) and Northeast (2.73%), and lowest in the West (1.45%) (Table 1).

Smoking-related characteristics demonstrated the strongest gradients, with prevalence increasing from 1.60% among light smokers (0.1–10 pack-years) to 9.58% among very heavy smokers (>40 pack-years). Early smoking initiation (<15 years) was also associated with markedly higher prevalence (6.67%) (Table 1). Overall, Table 1 highlights a clear clustering of lung cancer among individuals with high cumulative tobacco exposure and socioeconomic disadvantage.

In multivariable analyses (Table 2), lung cancer remained independently associated with female sex (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.19), Black race (OR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.34, 4.17), low household income (<$25,000: OR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.19, 5.26), and residence in the Northeast (OR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.39, 4.12) and Midwest (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.19, 3.18). Socioeconomic disadvantage, particularly low income, showed a strong and consistent dose–response relationship, while regional disparities remained stable after adjustment (Table 2). Discussion

This nationally representative pooled analysis of U.S. adults (BRFSS 2022–2024) identified pronounced and multifactorial disparities in self-reported lung cancer prevalence across sociodemographic, geographic, socioeconomic, and smoking-related factors. The overall lung cancer prevalence of 2.45% observed in this study is slightly higher than estimates reported in prior BRFSS-based studies of self-reported cancer history, which have generally ranged from approximately 1.0% to 2.0% among U.S. adult populations (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2023b; Dwyer-Lindgren et al., 2018). This variation likely reflects differences in analytic approach across studies. The present study pooled three years of BRFSS data (2022–2024), which enhances statistical stability and increases the likelihood of capturing respondents with a history of lung cancer compared with single cross-sectional estimates. Differences in self-reported case ascertainment and survey weighting approaches may also contribute to variation in prevalence estimates across studies (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2023b; Dwyer-Lindgren et al., 2018).

A central and most robust finding of this study is the strong dose–response relationship between cumulative smoking exposure and lung cancer prevalence. As shown in Table 1 and Table 2, very heavy smoking (>40 pack-years) was associated with more than fourfold higher odds of lung cancer compared with light exposure (0.1–10 pack-years), even after full adjustment for socioeconomic and demographic factors. This finding is consistent with a large body of epidemiologic evidence establishing tobacco smoking as the leading cause of lung cancer, with risk increasing in a clear dose–response manner with duration, intensity, and cumulative exposure (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2014; IARC, 2004; Jha and Peto, 2014). Importantly, attenuation of early smoking initiation effects after inclusion of pack-years in the fully adjusted model suggests that cumulative exposure is the principal pathway through which early initiation increases lung cancer risk.

Socioeconomic disadvantage emerged as another dominant and independent determinant of lung cancer burden. Individuals with low income and lower educational attainment experienced substantially higher prevalence and odds of lung cancer across all models. These findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating persistent socioeconomic gradients in lung cancer incidence and mortality in the United States and other high-income countries (Singh and Jemal, 2017b; Mackenbach et al., 2008). Structural disadvantage may increase risk through multiple pathways, including higher smoking prevalence and intensity, greater exposure to occupational carcinogens, reduced access to preventive care, and lower uptake of lung cancer screening and early diagnostic services (Lortet-Tieulent et al., 2014). The persistence of income effects in fully adjusted models suggests that socioeconomic position operates beyond smoking behavior alone and reflects broader structural determinants of health.

Racial and ethnic disparities were also evident, with Black adults experiencing the highest prevalence of lung cancer in both descriptive and multivariable analyses. Although these disparities were partially attenuated after adjustment for smoking and socioeconomic factors, elevated odds persisted in fully adjusted models. This pattern is consistent with prior studies documenting higher lung cancer incidence and mortality among Black adults, particularly Black men, even after accounting for smoking behavior (Haiman et al., 2006b; National Cancer Institute, 2023). Potential explanations include differences in environmental and occupational exposures, healthcare access, and structural racism affecting prevention and early detection opportunities (Aldrich et al., 2019).

The observed sex differences, with females exhibiting higher odds of lung cancer in fully adjusted models, are notable and align with evidence suggesting a narrowing of the historical male predominance in lung cancer burden (Jemal et al., 2018). While differences in cumulative smoking exposure and cohort smoking patterns may still contribute, increasing attention has been directed toward non-tobacco determinants of lung cancer risk. Environmental exposures such as residential radon and ambient air pollution, including fine particulate matter (PM2.5), have been increasingly recognized as important contributors to lung carcinogenesis independent of active smoking. Recent studies have demonstrated that long-term exposure to PM2.5 is associated with an increased risk of lung cancer through chronic inflammatory, oxidative stress, and molecular pathways that may promote tumor development even among nonsmokers (Chen et al., 2024). Similarly, previous research has identified residential radon as a major environmental carcinogen and the leading cause of lung cancer among never-smokers, highlighting its continued public health significance (Martin-Gisbert et al., 2023). Differences in residential environments, housing quality, occupational exposures, and environmental exposure profiles may therefore contribute to sex-related differences in lung cancer burden. Beyond environmental exposures, biological susceptibility and hormonal influences have also been proposed as potential modifiers of lung cancer risk. Furthermore, prior studies have reported that women account for a substantial proportion of lung cancer cases among never-smokers, suggesting that factors beyond active tobacco use may play an important role in disease etiology (Couraud et al., 2012). Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed sex differences may reflect a broader shift in lung cancer etiology in which non-tobacco environmental exposures increasingly contribute to disease risk, warranting further investigation into sex-specific exposure pathways and prevention strategies.

Geographic variation in lung cancer prevalence further highlights the importance of place-based determinants of health. Higher odds in the Northeast and Midwest regions, compared with the West, persisted after adjustment for socioeconomic and smoking-related variables. These findings align with prior reports showing regional clustering of lung cancer incidence that reflects differences in historical smoking patterns, industrial exposures, occupational risks, socioeconomic conditions, and healthcare access (Henley et al., 2014). Regional variation may also reflect differences in tobacco control policies, cessation program implementation, and environmental exposures such as radon and air pollution.

Taken together, these findings underscore that lung cancer in the United States is driven by a complex interplay of behavioral risk factors and structural determinants. While smoking remains the dominant causal factor, as evidenced by the strong and graded association with pack-years, socioeconomic disadvantage and geographic context independently shape disease distribution. The attenuation of several demographic associations after inclusion of cumulative smoking exposure highlights the mediating role of tobacco use in explaining population disparities, while the persistence of income, race, and region effects indicates additional non-smoking pathways.

From a public health perspective, these results reinforce the need for a dual-pronged prevention strategy. First, continued strengthening of tobacco control efforts—including cessation programs, taxation, and targeted interventions for high-risk populations—remains essential (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2014; Jha and Peto, 2014). Second, addressing upstream social determinants of health, including poverty reduction, educational opportunity, environmental justice, and equitable access to lung cancer screening, is critical for reducing persistent disparities (Adler and Rehkopf, 2008; Haiman et al., 2006a). Expansion of low-dose CT screening in eligible high-risk populations, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged and racial/ethnic minority groups, may further reduce mortality gaps if implemented equitably (US Preventive Services Task Force, 2021).

This study's strengths include the use of pooled nationally representative BRFSS data (2022–2024), providing a large sample and strong statistical power to assess lung cancer disparities across sociodemographic, geographic, socioeconomic, and smoking-related factors. Survey-weighted analyses enhance national generalizability, and detailed smoking measures (including initiation age and pack-years) allowed robust evaluation of dose–response relationships.

Limitations include reliance on self-reported lung cancer and smoking history, which may introduce recall bias or misclassification, although cancer self-reports are generally reliable in population surveys (National Cancer Institute, 2017). The BRFSS excludes institutionalized and non–telephone-access populations, limiting generalizability to some high-risk groups.

Overall, our findings show that lung cancer disparities in the United States are driven by both cumulative tobacco exposure and socioeconomic disadvantage. Although smoking remains the strongest risk factor, persistent associations with low income, lower education, and geographic variation suggest that tobacco control alone is insufficient. Comprehensive prevention strategies should integrate smoking cessation, lung cancer screening, and interventions addressing social determinants of health. Targeted efforts in high-risk and disadvantaged populations are essential to reduce disparities and improve health equity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, lung cancer in the United States is strongly associated with cumulative smoking exposure and shaped by persistent socioeconomic, racial/ethnic, and geographic disparities. Although smoking remains the dominant determinant, these inequities underscore the need to address structural and contextual drivers of risk. Integrated prevention strategies combining tobacco control with broader social and environmental interventions are essential to reducing lung cancer burden.
