Lead poisoning as a differential diagnosis in pediatric patients with chronic abdominal pain: a case–control study in Tehran-Iran
Authors: Reihaneh Mohsenipour, Majid Aflatoonian, Hossein Alimadadi, Parisa Rahmani, Nazanin Esmaeili, Mohammad Yazdi, Farzaneh Abbasi, Fatemeh Solgi, Farshad Sharifi, Nahid Vafaii, Ali Mohebbi, Maryam Khazdouz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic abdominal pain is a potential symptom of lead poisoning, which is often challenging to diagnose. This case–control study aimed to evaluate blood lead levels in pediatric patients with chronic abdominal pain.MethodsThe case–control study was conducted on 190 pediatrics who presented to the Children's Medical Center Hospital clinics, Tehran between April 2021- 2023. The children were divided into two groups: the case group, consisting of 81 patients with chronic abdominal pain, and the matched control group; 109 children without any gastrointestinal symptoms. The statistical analysis of the data was performed using STATA 16. A multiple logistic regression model was used to assess the association of different independent variables with chronic abdominal pain.ResultsThere was no significant difference between mean (± standard deviation [SD]) of age (8.80(2.7) years vs. control group: 9.23(3.9) years), sex, and BMI (16.55(4.6) vs. 17.32(4.7)) of the patients with chronic abdominal pain (case group) and the control group, whereas the mean weight was remarkably low in patients with chronic abdominal pain: 27.25(± 12.1) kg vs. 31.70(± 14.7) kg (P value = 0.028). Fifty-nine percent of children with chronic abdominal pain had serum lead levels ≥ 10 µg/dL. The mean (SD) of blood lead levels was statistically high in the case group: 11.09 (± 5.35) µg/dL vs. control group: 8.26 (± 5.01) µg/dL) (P value ≤ 0.05). The appetite level was significantly low in the case group: 3.8 (± 2.5) vs. control group 5.4 (± 1.3).ConclusionsLead poisoning could be a possible cause of children's chronic abdominal pain. Regarding the high rate of lead poisoning in children exerting appropriate measures to reduce their exposure to lead is necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03337-1.Keywords: Lead, Chronic abdominal pain, Pediatrics, Poisoning

Background

Lead is a toxic metal with no critical physiological role in the human body. Due to its characteristics, it is broadly applied in industry, which has led to widespread environmental pollution [1, 2]. Gastric and pulmonary absorptions are the chief routes of contamination with lead; however, it can enter the body through dermal and prenatal exposures [3, 4]. About 90% of lead is stored in mineralized and soft tissues, with a maximum half-life of ≤ 30 years, hence, regarding its circulation between different tissues, individuals may show high levels of lead from a few months to several years after exposure [5, 6].

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that approximately 1 million individuals die from lead poisoning each year [7]. According to the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), around 800 million children worldwide, which is equivalent to one-third of all children, have blood lead levels that are equal to or exceed 5 µg/dl [8].

Lead poisoning is one of the oldest and most persistent health risks in Iran. Iran's evidence demonstrates clearly that lead toxicity and exposure provide a serious risk to human health [9]. Since, a country-scale study in 2020, the mean of lead blood levels was remarkably high between patients with probable signs and symptoms of lead poisoning in Iran [10].

Although the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have established a blood lead cut-off of 3.5 µg/dL as the point at which medical interventions should be considered for children aged 1–5 years, no specific reference value for a safe blood lead level has been determined to prevent adverse effects [11]. Lead intoxication is a multisystem disease with non-specific symptoms, making it difficult to diagnose and screen [12]. Patients suffering from lead intoxication may experience neurological symptoms (e.g., ataxia, seizures, hyperirritability), hematological manifestations, renal impairments, and gastrointestinal disorders like anorexia, vomiting, constipation, and chronic abdominal pain [1, 4, 13].

Abdominal pain is one of the major complications that leads to the admission of patients into health departments [14]. It has an impact on the child's health, and there are significant costs associated with missing school days and utilizing medical services [15]. Although a diverse range of conditions could result in abdominal pain, the etiologies of 20–40% of cases remain unknown [16].

Since the lead exposure is extremely prevalent in Iran, and chronic abdominal pain is one of the most common symptoms of lead toxicity [9]. In Iran, recent research has evaluated the lead levels of infants who experience abdominal pain. A few studies have demonstrated a potent relationship between high lead levels and abdominal pain or functional gastrointestinal disorders [13, 17, 18]. Conversely, Famouri, et al. discovered that the mean blood lead levels were less than 5 µg/dL, and there was no statistically significant difference between the study groups [19]. Rate of children with functional gastrointestinal disorders and the blood levels of lead in the patients was different among the studies. So, the study was performed with the aim of evaluating lead poisoning as a potential etiology of chronic abdominal pain in pediatric patients.

Methods

This case–control study was carried out according to STROBE guidelines. The study was ethically confirmed by the ethical council of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.REC.1395.2927). The study was performed on pediatric participants presented to the Children's Medical Center Hospital, Tehran, Iran between April 2021- 2023. The hospital is located in center of Tehran and is the most referral pediatric hospital of the country. The sample size was calculated according to the expert consensus and Hosseini et al. findings [13]. The patients (under 18 years) with the chief complaint of abdominal pain who were referred to the gastrointestinal disease clinic were randomly enrolled in the case group. Chronic abdominal pain was described as persistent or recurrent episodes of pain with at least one pain attack per month for three consecutive months that interfere with daily activities [20]. The patient’s medical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, and results of endoscopy and radiography were used to distinguish between organic and non-organic chronic abdominal pain. Organic abdominal pain is secondary to known physiological or anatomical dysfunctions [20]. Patients with organic causes of abdominal pain such as acute abdominal pain, fever, diarrhea, constipation, inflammatory bowel disease, or any other gastrointestinal diseases were excluded; hence, the study was performed on those with no known causes of abdominal pain. A control group was randomly selected from pediatric patients presenting to the same hospital with no complaints of gastrointestinal diseases or other chronic diseases. These groups were matched for both age and sex. Trained personnel used precise electronic scales (0.1 kg) and fixed centimeters (0.1 cm) to measure the children's weight and height. The body mass index (BMI) was found by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Consent forms were obtained from all participants or their respective parents or legal guardians. The appetite of children was evaluated using a designed questionnaire. Parents were asked, in a clinical setting, to rate their child's appetite on a scale of 0–7; “0” represented “ate very little”, 4 was sufficient, and “7” represented “ate very much”. To measure serum levels of lead, blood samples were collected in heparin test tubes and stored at -20 oC until analysis. The samples were referred to the Tarbiat Modares University laboratory. The Shimadzu atomic absorption graphite furnace Model AA-670 G was used to measure the concentration of lead using the atomic absorption method. Blanks, calibrators, and samples were prepared as follows: Blank consists of 0.25 ml of triple distilled water and 1 ml of diluent. Calibrators are made by mixing 0.25 ml of each calibrator with 1 ml diluent. 50 µl of samples were diluted with 200 µl triton × 100 (0.01%), and then 10 µl of diluted sample was mixed with 10 µl of diluent reagent. 10 µl of blank, calibrators, and samples were injected into the instrument. Serum levels of lead greater than 10 µg/dL were considered to be lead poisoning [21].

On the quantitative variables, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed to identify their distribution. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median, interquartile range, or minimum-to-maximum ranges were used to describe quantitative variables. Two groups of patients' continuous variables were compared using the t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test. Chi-square test was conducted to evaluate categorical variables. A multiple logistic regression model was used to assess the association of different independent variables with chronic abdominal pain. An ANOVA/ ANCOVA model was performed to compare primary outcomes between groups. P values below 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. Intervals of confidence (CI) at 95% and odds ratios (OR) were presented to illustrate the strength of the association. The statistical analysis of the data was done using STATA 16.

Results

In the case–control study, 190 children were divided into two groups: children with chronic abdominal pain and controls. The selection flow chart for study participants is shown in Fig. 1. Of the total participants, 78 (41.0%) were male, and 112 (58.9%) were female. The mean (± SD) age of participants was 9 (± 3.4) years (range 2.5–16 years). The mean (± SD) weight, height, and BMI were respectively 29.79 (± 13.83) kg, 1.31 (± 0.19) cm, and 16.98 (± 4.7). Table 1 illustrates the demographic data for the group of patients with chronic abdominal pain and the control group. According to the findings, there was no statistically significant difference between the patients with chronic abdominal pain and the control group in age, sex, height, and BMI, whereas the mean weight was remarkably low in patients with chronic abdominal pain: 27.25 (± 12.1) kg vs. the control group: 31.70 (± 14.7) kg (P value = 0.028). The difference was significant after adjustment for age (P value ≤ 0.00) (Table 1). We have divided age to subgroups (< 9 and 9 ≤ year) and analyzed mean weight and blood lead levels in similar age groups. We have categorized age into two subgroups: those younger than 9 years old and ≥ 9 years old and have examined the mean weight and blood lead levels within each of these age groups. The average weight was considerably lower in participants with abdominal pain in both subgroups (P value < 0.05). The average blood lead levels in the case group were greater than those in the control group in both subgroups. The data is provided as a supplemental file. The appetite level was significantly low in the children with chronic abdominal pain, 3.8 (± 2.5) vs. control group 5.4 (± 1.3) (P value ≤ 0.00).

Discussion

This observational study was conducted to assess lead poisoning as a risk factor contributing to chronic abdominal pain in pediatrics. The results showed the prevalence of lead poisoning in the study children was extremely high. Also, the lead concentration in the serum of the children with chronic abdominal pain was significantly higher than the control group.

Despite preventive protocols exerted globally to control lead exposure, lead poisoning remains a serious issue, especially in children, regarding its detrimental impacts on their development [9]. A previous study found that the average Chinese child's blood lead levels were significantly higher than the normal values [22]. Although numerous studies have been conducted on lead contamination in Iran's environment, a few have evaluated the current status of lead exposure and toxicity among Iranian children [9]. In our study, overall, almost of study participants in both groups (77%) had serum lead ≥ 5 µg/dL. In line with our study, Faranoush et al. evaluated children in the city of Semnan and found that blood lead levels in 78.8% of the children were above 10 µg/dl [23]. In a study done in Tehran, 32% of randomly enrolled children had higher than 10 g/dl of blood lead [24]. Hatami et al. (2012) evaluated blood lead levels in children ages 6 to 7 in the city of Bushehr and discovered that 34.6% of children have blood lead values greater than 5 µg/dl [25]. The variation in percentages observed in studies could be attributed to differences in geographic location, study time and papulation or methodology.

lead poisoning is of great concern in children who live in Tehran and may lead to deleterious complications [26]. Air pollution is a major cause of lead poisoning, according to earlier research conducted in Iran [27]. Children who live in industrial areas and in areas with high air pollution records are at a significant risk of lead poisoning since the respiratory system is the main route through which lead is absorbed. In recent years, Tehran and other Iran’s capital cities have been dealing with serious air quality problems, particularly with regard to particulate matter pollution, and industrialization and a lack of effective air pollution control plans have made matters worse, having a significant negative impact on public health [13, 28]. Therefore, it is possible that the elevated rate of lead toxicity in the study participants is connected to air pollution or other harmful impacts of industrialization.

In the study, children with abdominal pain exhibited significantly elevated serum levels of lead. On the other hand, we found a positive relationship between chronic abdominal pain and elevated blood lead levels. Some studies in line with our results have shown high lead blood concentrations may cause gastrointestinal symptoms. In a cross-sectional study carried out by Hosseini et al. blood lead concentration in children with chronic abdominal pain was examined. In the study, nearly 20% of participants had blood lead levels higher than 5 µg/dL [13]. Also, in the Zamani et al. study, abdominal pain was the most frequent symptom accompanying constipation among lead-poisoned children [17]. The participants and research methodology in both studies appear to be similar. However, the discrepancy in average serum lead levels between our study and theirs might likely be attributed to variations in the methodologies used to measure blood lead levels, particularly considering that the study was done in Tehran. In line with our research findings, the study conducted by Ataee et al. [18], revealed that 41.8% of children who experienced constipation or abdominal pain had blood lead levels exceeding 5 µg/dL. Also in other cross-sectional study conducted in Turkey revealed that 51.9% of children with functional gastrointestinal disorders had blood lead levels ≥ 5 µg/dL and the blood lead levels in the children was notably higher than in the control group [29].

The first occurrence of abdominal colic symptoms among metal workers was observed in 500 BC. This symptom had already been documented in medical literature long before its precise cause was understood [30]. Gastrointestinal manifestations (e.g., abdominal pain, anorexia, or constipation) can be presented among the high blood lead level complications [13, 31]. Lead might act in neurotoxicity mechanism or even directly impact muscles and affect the contractility of the intestines [32]. Also, lead competes with other polyvalent cations (e.g. calcium and zinc) cell membrane transportation so, interferes with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and the intracellular signaling system which can affect the neural plexus of the intestines [33, 34]. Considering the elevated levels of lead in children with chronic abdominal pain, and, the significant association between lead concentration and chronic abdominal pain, it may be advisable to suggest treatments such as minimizing exposure to lead and following a diet high in calcium and iron, for the patients. Actually, our research suggests that the level of lead toxicity in children is considerably higher than that observed in previous studies conducted in the region in recent years. This emphasizes the need for a more comprehensive evaluation and investigation of the acute or chronic consequences of lead toxicity than has been carried out in the past.

In our study, the average weight of patients suffering from chronic abdominal pain although after adjustment for age was seen to be lower than control group. This observation implies an association between elevated blood lead levels and being underweight. Some studies have investigated into the link between lead concentration and growth failure in children and reported that lead reduced anthropometric indexes in children [35]. Kaji and Nishi have shown a negative relationship between somatic growth and blood lead level in children [36]. Chinese children with ages 3–5 were at an increased risk for stunting when they had a high blood lead concentration [37]. In another study, elevated blood lead levels were identified as a predictor of being underweight [2]. A cross-sectional study in 2014 found a significant association between stunting and elevated blood lead levels, that may be attributable to the direct influence of lead on human growth [38]. However, in contrast with our result, in a longitudinal study in Boston, lead exposure in childhood led to obesity [39]. A study conducted in the Iranian province of Zanjan found no significant difference in growth parameters, such as weight and height, between children with elevated blood lead levels and the control group [40]. Thus, the exact association between blood lead levels and child growth is controversial. Poor growth might be caused by the deleterious impacts of lead on bone growth and growth hormone secretion [41]. Given the limited understanding of its precise mechanism, possible factors such as gastrointestinal symptoms or poor appetite may be responsible for being underweight.

Our results revealed that patients with abdominal pain had a lower appetite than the control group. Anorexia is a non-specific symptom, usually evaluated concurrently with a child’s growth and developmental retardation [42]. A study has reported anorexia is one of the major complaints of children poisoned with lead [12], and lead toxicity could cause in-appetite and slow weight gain [43]. Hammond et al. have proposed that lead suppresses appetite in rats through both systemic and gastrointestinal impacts [44]. Concerning the low mean weight seen in the lead-poisoned group in our study, it is possible that exposure to lead leads to a decreased appetite, ultimately resulting in long-term failure to gain weight.

As far as we know, this is the first study in Iranian pediatrics evaluating lead poisoning and appetite. The evaluation of blood lead levels can help in identifying the underlying factors contributing to poor appetite and growth failure in children. However, there were some limitations to our study. Some studies have indicated a relationship between blood lead concentration and low socioeconomic status (e.g., living in old houses) [13, 45]. Hosseini et al. have shown that lead exposure is lower in children with housekeeping mothers than employed mothers, as the latter spend more time out of the house and expose themselves to more lead-contaminated dust, subsequently bringing them into the child’s environment [13]. In the study, there was no assessment of the lead exposure risk factors (parents’ smoking habits, parents’ job or education levels, toy material, metal pipes at home, soil playing, pica, household dust, etc.). The comprehensive evaluation could help to identify confounding factors. Furthermore, other lead poisoning complications such as anemia or signs of hyperactivity were not measured. In addition, in the study, only the weight and height of the patients were considered anthropometric indices. Therefore, evaluating additional anthropometric parameters, such as fat mass or fat-free mass, may help detect the effects of lead exposure on children growth. Based on the findings of this study, it is recommended that additional research be conducted to determine the precise cause of the rise in the serum lead level in children and the detrimental consequences of lead toxicity.

Conclusions

This study showed that lead poisoning is still a serious problem in Iran, despite the precautions against utilizing lead in different industries. Lead poisoning could result in various symptoms in children, including chronic abdominal pain, poor appetite, and growth failure. These results should be taken seriously as a health concern, especially for children. Neglecting the risks of lead exposure in pediatrics might have irreversible effects.
