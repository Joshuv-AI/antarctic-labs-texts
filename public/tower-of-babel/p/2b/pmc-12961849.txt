CRISPR/Cas9-mediated B2m knockout paves the way for allogeneic basal cell transplantation
Authors: Huanjie Chen, Weizhan Luo, Ning Ma, Shiyue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutologous transplantation of basal cells (BCs) has shown promise in treating respiratory diseases, but disease-specific subpopulations among BCs probably diminish the treatment efficacy. An alternative approach involves generating universal and healthy BCs, which offers a potentially more efficient and accessible solution for avoiding using abnormal BCs. However, such hypoimmunogenic BCs have not yet been transplanted into the airways of immunocompetent animals.MethodsBefore producing hypoimmunogenic BCs, the predominant transplantation antigen in BCs was explored through RT-qPCR and flow cytometry to identify the key target of CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated editing. The proliferation and expression of specific markers of BCs were evaluated after gene editing by CCK‐8 and RT‐qPCR, respectively. These gene‐edited BCs and wild‐type (WT) BCs, which were both derived from the same BALB/c mouse, were subsequently allogeneically transplanted into C57BL/6 mice with polidocanol‐induced airway injury to evaluate the differentiation and immune response in the recipient mice via immunehistological staining.ResultsIn the present study, it was demonstrated that major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) is the predominant transplantation antigen in BCs. The hypoimmunogenic BCs were generated through editing beta‐2 microglobulin (B2m) participating in the encoding of MHC‐I. The knockout of B2m in BCs did not affect their proliferation or the expression of specific markers in vitro. Both WT BCs and B2m‐edited BCs (B2m‾ BCs) successfully differentiated into ciliated and secretory cells in the tracheas following allogeneic transplantation and did not elicit an immune response during the 26‐day observation period in the tracheas. However, WT BCs, compared to B2m‾ BCs, induced severe lung injury by provoking an immune response in the lower airways and alveolar regions, as indicated by increased infiltration of CD45+ immune cells, epithelial cell shedding in the bronchi, and obvious alveolar hyperemia with collapse.ConclusionsThe hypoimmunogenic BCs generated through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated B2m gene editing retained their ability to differentiate and maintained viability in the allogeneic respiratory system, supporting the potential application in the cell regeneration therapy of airway diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03595-5.Keywords: Basal cells, B2m, CRISPR/Cas9, Allogeneic cell transplantation, Hypoimmunogenicity

Introduction

Basal cells (BCs) are stem cells located on the basement membrane of the airway epithelium [1]. They can proliferate and differentiate into various cell types within the pseudostratified epithelium, such as ciliated cells, goblet cells, club cells, thereby playing a critical role in the repair of the airway epithelium after injury [2–4]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that autologous BC transplantation into the tracheas or lungs can alleviate recurrent benign tracheal stenosis in beagle dogs and improve lung function in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [5, 6]. However, producing BCs from patient samples is time-consuming, which results in prolonged waiting periods for autologous BC transplantation. Furthermore, BCs derived from patients with airway diseases such as COPD contain abnormal subpopulations that could potentially diminish the efficacy of the treatment [7]. Thus, the therapeutic potential of autologous BC transplantation may be compromised.

Sorting healthy BCs could offer a potential solution, but no reliable biomarkers currently exist to effectively separate the normal cells from the abnormal ones during in vitro BC production. For example, squamous cells represent one such abnormal subpopulation. Although CD44 and Caveolin-1 are more highly expressed in squamous cells than in normal BCs, these markers are not sufficient for accurate separation, as both cell types express them [8]. Given this circumstance, BCs isolated from healthy donors and engineered to be hypoimmunogenic could serve as an alternative cell source to avoid the complications associated with abnormal subpopulations. Additionally, cryopreservation of these healthy allogeneic BCs in advance could reduce waiting times prior to transplantation. Achieving hypoimmunogenicity, however, requires overcoming histocompatibility barriers, as allogeneic BCs express antigens that may trigger immune rejection [9]. The major histocompatibility complex (MHC), particularly MHC class I (MHC‐I), is the primary transplantation antigen responsible for such immune responses [10]. MHC‐I, which plays a key role in presenting antigens to T cells, is widely expressed on the surface of all nucleated cells, including those in the airway epithelium [11]. Knockout (KO) of MHC‐I can prevent T‐cell recognition and subsequent immune activation [12].

In mice, MHC-I is a heterodimer composed of a highly polymorphic heavy chain (H‐2 K), and a conservative light chain, beta‐2 microglobulin (B2M) [13]. Due to its conserved structure, B2m KO has been widely applied in the production of hypoimmunogenic cells [14, 15]. For example, Wiegmann et al. demonstrated that B2m KO human cord blood‐derived endothelial cells do not induce allogeneic T‐cell responses and are resistant to antibody‐mediated complement‐dependent cytotoxicity [16]. However, few studies have specifically investigated hypoimmunogenic BCs in the context of airway transplantation, particularly in allogeneic in vivo models.

In this study, we initially confirmed that MHC-I is the main transplantation antigen expressed in BCs. We then used CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock out B2m, generating single clone‐derived B2m‐KO BCs (i.e. B2m‾ BCs). To mimic allogeneic transplantation in humans, we performed inter‐strain transplantation between BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice referring to the published papers [17–19]. Hypoimmunogenic BCs derived from BALB/c mice were transplanted into the airway of C57BL/6 mice following polidocanol (PDOC)‐induced injury. After transplantation, B2m‾ BCs successfully differentiated into ciliated cells and secretory cells in the tracheas and lungs without provoking immune rejection. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of generating and applying hypoimmunogenic BCs as a potential therapeutic strategy for the regeneration of airway epithelium in the treatment of respiratory diseases.

Methods

Isolation and culture of BCs

Epithelial cells were isolated from a four-week‐old male BALB/c mouse using the Papain Dissociation System as described in references (#LKO03150, Worthington, USA) [20]. The trachea was excised and incubated in the digestion solution at 37 °C for one hour. Subsequently, the cells in the supernatant were seeded into a flask and cultured in our patent BC medium (ZL 2022 1 0011084.3, China) supplemented with Y‐27,632 (#HY‐10182, MCE, USA), CHIR‐99,021 (#HY‐10182, MCE, USA), D‐MH1 (#HY‐12273, MCE, USA), and A‐8301 (#HY‐10432, MCE, USA). Mesenchymal cells were removed through magnetic separation employing epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) microbeads (#130‐105‐958, Miltenyi, Germany). Following two passages in culture, BCs were further purified from the isolated EpCAM‐positive cells. A bright‐field image was subsequently acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope DMi8 (Leica, Germany).

Subcellular location of Cas9 in BCs

BCs were seeded in a 48-well plate at a density of 6,000 cells per well. Following a 24‐hour incubation period, the Cas9 protein conjugated with enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) (YK‐Cas9‐50, Ubigene, China) was transfected into the BCs using Lipofectamine™ CRISPRMAX™ Transfection Reagent (#CMAX00001, Invitrogen, USA). To circumvent EGFP signal loss and autofluorescence associated with cell fixation, hoechst 33,342 (Ho342) (C1022, Beyotime, China) was employed to stain the nuclei of live BCs. On days 1, 2, and 3 post‐transfection, three of the wells were randomly selected each day for Ho342 staining, and the cells were subsequently imaged using DMi8 microscopy.

B2m gene editing

For the B2m KO in BCs using CRISPR/Cas9, the single guide RNA (sgRNA) was obtained from Ubigene company (China), and Cas9 protein (#1081060) was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies company (USA). The sequence of the sgRNA is AGTATACTCACGCCACCCAC. BCs were seeded at a density of 2 * 10 ^ 4 cells per well in 12-well plate. After 24 h of culture, 36 pmol of sgRNA and 36 pmol of Cas9 protein were transfected into the BCs using Lipofectamine™ CRISPRMAX™ Transfection Reagent (#CMAX00001, Invitrogen, USA). DNA from the wild type (WT) and KO BCs was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (#DP304, Tiangen, China). B2m‐specific primers (Forward: CACAGCGCACAAGCACTGC; Reverse: CCACACAGATGGAGCGTCC) were used to amplify the sequence targeted by the sgRNA‐Cas9 complex. The amplification product was subsequently submitted to Tsingke company (China) for Sanger sequencing.

Single clone screening

To screen single clones, B2m-KO BCs were seeded into a 96‐well plate at a density of one cell per well. A Celigo image cytometer (Nexcelom, USA) was used to capture the images of cells at 12 h and on day 4 after seeding. Proliferating clones identified on day 4 were pre‐selected, and the final selection was based on the images captured at 12 h after seeding to exclude the mixed clones. Single‐clone‐derived BCs were passaged. DNA sequencing, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR), and flow cytometry were used to confirm whether the selected clones were optimal, defining them as B2m‾ BCs. Bright‐field images were captured using DMi8 microscopy.

Lentivirus infection

EGFP overexpression in BCs was achieved by transfecting with the lentiviruses encoding EGFP (#YV-OE‐LV001‐GFP‐1000, Ubigene, China). The lentiviruses (multiplicity of infection: 50) were transfected into WT BCs or B2m‾ BCs in a medium containing 2 µg/mL polybrene (#YV‐OE‐LV001‐GFP‐1000, Ubigene, China) for 48 h. The culture medium was then refreshed to remove the lentiviruses and polybrene. Puromycin (#P8230, Solarbio, China) at a concentration of 1 µg/mL was applied to eliminate EGFP‐negative BCs. DMi8 microscopy and flow cytometry (BD, USA) were used to verify EGFP expression in BCs.

To obtain WT and B2m‾ BCs that proliferate from a single cell and express similar levels of EGFP, the EGFP-expressing BCs containing multiple clones were screened as described above. Both the EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs were examined using an inverted fluorescent microscope and flow cytometry to determine the percentage and fluorescence intensity of EGFP‐positive cells. Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay (C0037, Beyotime, China). EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs with similar percentages and fluorescence intensities were selected for transplantation into C57BL/6 mice.

Immunofluorescence of BCs in vitro

BCs were seeded in 96-well plates. After a three‐day culture, the BCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X‐100 (X100‐500, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for an additional 15 min. Cells were then incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (4240GR100, Biofroxx, Germany) for one hour at room temperature to block non‐specific antigens. The BCs were then labeled with primary antibodies, including rabbit anti‐keratin 5 (KRT5) antibody (1:200, #ab52635, Abcam, UK), mouse anti‐tumor protein 63 (p63) antibody (1:50, #ab735, Abcam, UK), mouse anti‐CD47 antibody (1:50, #sc‐12730, Santa Cruz, USA) and mouse anti‐MUC5AC antibody (1:100, #ab3649, Abcam, UK). The secondary antibodies were anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor® 594 antibody (1:500, #ab150116, Abcam, UK) and anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor® 488 antibody (1:500, #ab150077, Abcam, UK). The cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. DMi8 microscopy was used to capture the images of BCs.

BCs differentiation in vitro

BCs were seeded in transwell plates at a density of 2 * 10 ^ 5 cells per well for the culture of air-liquid interface (ALI). After the two-day submerged culture, the medium was replaced with PneumaCult™-ALI Medium (05001, STEMCELL, Canada) for a 21-day differentiation.

Flow cytometry

BCs pre-stimulated with 50 ng/mL interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) (#HY‐P7071, MCE, USA) for 48 h were enzymatically dissociated into single‐cell suspension for the detection of B2M, H‐2Kd (the heavy chain of MHC‐I in BALB/c mice) and 1‐Ad (MHC‐II in BALB/c mice). For B2M staining, BCs were incubated with PE‐conjugated anti‐mouse B2M antibody (#154503, BioLegend, USA) diluted in PBS containing 2% FBS for 10 min at 4 ℃. To assess the expression of MHC‐I and MHC‐II, BCs were incubated with Brilliant Violet 421‐conjugated anti‐mouse H‐2Kd antibody (#116623, BioLegend, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 488‐conjugated anti‐mouse I‐Ad antibody (#115008, BioLegend, USA), respectively. Prior to flow cytometry analysis, BCs were stained with 7‐AAD (#420403, Biolegend, USA) to distinguish the live and dead cells. BCs stained with 7‐AAD but not the antibodies served as the negative control. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and percentage were calculated. PE‐conjugated anti‐mouse CD47 antibody (#127507, BioLegend, USA) was used to evaluate the expression of CD47 in BCs.

RT-qPCR

BCs were lysed using Trizol (#ET111-01, TransGen, China), followed by total RNA extraction employing an RNA extraction kit (#ER501‐01, TransGen, China). cDNA was synthesized using the Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (#11141ES60, Yeasen, China). The mRNA levels of B2m, Krt5, Trp63, Sox2, Sox9, Mki67, Ngfr and Itga6 were detected using a RT‐qPCR SYBR® Green kit (#11198ES08, Yeasen, China). Gapdh was used as the reference gene for normalization. The primers are listed in Table 1.

Animal experiment

Six- to eight‐week‐old male healthy C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech company (China). Mice were randomly divided into four groups using SPSS software (USA) (6 mice per group, 24 mice in total): the control (CTRL) group, the PDOC group, the WT BCs group and the B2m‾ BCs group. The number of animals was determined according to the Four R principles (i.e. Replacement, Reduction, Refinement and Responsibility) of the Animal Ethics Committee and the animal livability observed in our preliminary experiments.

To remove most of the epithelium in tracheas, 20 µL of 2% PDOC (#P9641-50G, Merck, USA) dissolved in PBS were administered to PDOC, WT BCs and the B2m‾ BCs groups via oropharyngeal aspiration on day 0. The CTRL group received an equivalent volume of PBS in the same manner. On day 2, the WT BCs group and the B2m‾ BCs group were transplanted with EGFP‐expressing WT BCs and B2m‾ BCs (5*10^5 per mouse) in the airway via oropharyngeal aspiration, respectively. The PDOC group was administered with an equal volume of PBS in the same way. Avertin (JX750100, GenXion, China) was used on day 0 and day 2 to anesthetize the mice. To ensure optimal deposition of PDOC or BCs on the basal membrane in the tracheas, mice were placed on an operative platform at a 45° angle during the liquid administration and were positioned horizontally as soon as possible after the operation until anesthetic resuscitation. Mice were housed 6 per cage at 20–25 °C and 50%–60% humidity. All mice were maintained on a 12:12 h light: dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Chopped corn cob was used as bedding.

On day 28, mice from each group were euthanized by cervical dislocation for the dissection of tracheas and lungs. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde as soon as possible. To protect the tracheal epithelium, the lungs were submerged into the 4% paraformaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp, China) directly.

Histological staining

For paraffin sections, lungs were dehydrated using gradient ethanol and then embedded in paraffin blocks. The blocks were cut into sections of 3–5 μm in thickness, placed on polylysine-coated glass slides, and stored at room temperature for further use. For the subsequent staining experiments, sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated in graded alcohols, and then rinsed in distilled water. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was carried out following the standard protocol [21].

For immunological staining, the dewaxed and rehydrated sections underwent heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) (G1219, Servicebio, China) or Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) (G1218, Servicebio, China) using a microwave oven. For immunofluorescent staining, tracheal sections were incubated with primary antibodies after a one‐hour incubation with 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were mouse anti‐EGFP (1:50, #ab184601, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti‐KRT5 (1:200, #ab52635, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti‐αTubulin (1:100, #ab179484, Abcam, UK) and rabbit anti‐CC10 (1:200, #ab213203, Abcam, UK). The secondary antibodies were anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor® 488 antibody (1:500, #ab150117, Abcam, UK) and anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor® 594 antibody (1:500, #ab150088, Abcam, UK). DAPI was used to label the cell nuclei. For immunochemistry staining, lung sections were incubated with primary antibodies following 5% bovine serum albumin and 0.3% hydrogen peroxide: mouse anti‐EGFP (1:50, #ab184601, Abcam, UK) and rabbit anti‐CD45 (1:100, #ab10558, Abcam, UK). The secondary antibodies used were anti‐mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (1:1000, #ab6789, Abcam, UK) and anti‐rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (1:1000, #ab6721, Abcam, UK). Hematoxylin was used to label cell nuclei. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight and with secondary antibodies at room temperature for one hour.

Stained sections were captured using DMi8 or DMI3000 B (Leica, Germany) microscopies. We selected one tissue section per mouse for imaging, with five visual fields captured per section. The acquired images were used for subsequent statistical analysis. Sections incubated only with the secondary antibodies served as negative controls. The percentage of positive areas in EGFP-stained and CD45‐stained sections was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (USA). Homogeneity of variance test and normality test were performed on the data in our study. Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t‐test. For statistical comparisons among three groups, ANOVA was followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as percentages. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, **** indicates the p value of less than 0.0001, while ns indicates no significance.

Result

Isolation of BCs and identification of genes to be edited

Tracheal BCs were isolated from a BALB/c mouse via enzymatic digestion, followed by magnetic separation using anti-EpCAM conjugated magnetic beads. The isolated and purified BCs exhibited characteristic morphological features, being small and cuboidal with relatively large, round nuclei (Fig. 1A). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that all BCs expressed specific markers KRT5 and p63 (Fig. 1B). Prior to generating hypoimmunogenic BCs, it was necessary to identify target genes contributing to main immunogenicity to circumvent excessive genetic editing. IFN‐γ stimulation is known to upregulate the expression of both MHC‐I and MHC‐II molecules in most cells including epithelial cells [22–25]. To ascertain the primary antigen responsible for immune rejection, BCs were pre‐treated with IFN‐γ for 48 h. Flow cytometry analysis was then performed to evaluate the expression levels of H‐2Kd (i.e. MHC‐I) and 1‐Ad (i.e. MHC‐II). As illustrated in Fig. 1C, both the MFI and percentage of H‐2Kd‐positive cells were significantly increased in the IFN‐γ‐stimulated group compared to the unstimulated group. Nevertheless, no alteration was observed in 1‐Ad expression following IFN‐γ stimulation. These results indicated that MHC‐I is likely the primary antigen driving immune rejection and thus represents a rational target for gene editing. However, it has been demonstrated that MHC‐I‐deficient cells will be recognized and attacked by natural killer (NK) cells in individuals possessing a normal immune system [26]. Previous studies have reported that CD47 expression enables MHC‐I‐deficient cells to evade NK cell recognition [27, 28]. Therefore, CD47 was detected via immunofluorescence staining in BCs. The expression of CD47 was confirmed in the experiment, suggesting that these cells may be capable of avoiding NK cell‐mediated clearance (Fig. 1D). Together, these findings reveal that the KO of MHC‐I is a feasible and simple strategy to generate hypoimmunogenic BCs.

Generating B2m-KO BCs

Prior to generating hypoimmunogenic BCs, we first investigated the spatial distribution of Cas9 protein following transfection to determine the optimal timing for terminating gene editing, given the scarcity of existing literature. Cas9 protein conjugated with EGFP was transfected into BCs, which were also counterstained with Ho342, a live-cell nuclear dye. On the first day post‐transfection, EGFP‐labeled Cas9 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm surrounding the nuclei (indicated by the arrowheads in Fig. S1A). A minor fraction of Cas9 exhibited a punctate distribution pattern, adhering to the extracellular surface of the 48‐well plate (denoted by the arrows in Fig. S1A). By the following day, a small proportion of EGFP‐labeled Cas9 had translocated into the nuclei (arrowhead in Fig. S1B), with nuclear accumulation increasing over time (Fig. S1C). These results reveal that the timing of nuclear localization of Cas9 protein should be considered to optimize the gene editing procedure in BCs.

To eliminate BC immunogenicity, the B2m gene, which encodes a conserved component of MHC-I, was selected for editing using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. An sgRNA was designed to target exon 2 of the B2m gene (Fig. 2A). After transfection of the combination of Cas9 protein and sgRNA into BCs, genomic DNA was extracted and analyzed by Sanger sequencing. B2m‐KO BCs exhibited overlapping peaks (red arrowhead) in the sequencing chromatogram, indicating editing at the DNA level (Fig. 2B). To assess B2m expression at the transcription level, RT‐qPCR was conducted (Fig. 2C). Before IFN‐γ stimulation, there was no significant difference between WT and B2m‐KO BCs in the level of B2m mRNA. Following IFN‐γ stimulation, B2m mRNA was significantly increased in both WT and B2m‐KO BCs. Under the same stimulation of IFN‐γ, however, the mRNA level of B2m was significantly lower in B2m‐KO BCs than in WT BCs. Further validation at the protein level was performed using flow cytometry. After IFN‐γ stimulation, nearly all the WT BCs exhibited high levels of B2M and H‐2Kd, while B2m‐KO BCs did not express these two proteins (Fig. 2D and G). IFN‐γ significantly increased the MFI of B2M and H‐2Kd in WT BCs, but not in B2m‐KO BCs (Fig. 2E and H). The percentages of B2Mhigh and H‐2Kdhigh BCs in the WT BCs group were significantly augmented by IFN‐γ stimulation compared to those in the KO BCs group (Fig. 2F and I). These results indicate the successful editing of the B2m gene in the majority, if not all, of the BCs.

However, even a few residual immunogenic cells can induce immune rejection and diminish the therapeutic effect. To ensure complete B2m editing in all BCs, single cell-derived clones were isolated from the KO cell pool. Following the expansion from a single BC (Fig. 3A), Sanger sequencing was conducted. One clone exhibited a cytidine deletion within the sgRNA‐targeted region, leading to a frameshift mutation that disrupted the amino acid sequence and introduced a premature stop codon (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometry further validated that this clone did not express B2M or H‐2Kd following IFN‐γ stimulation (Fig. 3C and F). As expected, IFN‐γ significantly increased the MFI of B2M (Fig. 3D) and H‐2Kd (Fig. 3G), as well as the proportion of B2Mhigh (Fig. 3E) and H‐2Kdhigh (Fig. 3H) cells in the WT BCs group, but not in the selected B2m‾ BC clone.

Given that the absence of 1-Ad and the expression of CD47 are the two important characteristics in terms of BCs escaping host immune recognition and attack, we evaluated their expression through flow cytometry (Fig. S2). The results indicated that the B2m editing did not increase the 1-Ad or decrease the CD47 expression.

These results collectively confirm the successful and complete KO of the B2m gene in BCs, producing a homogeneous population suitable for further transplantation studies.

B2m‾BCs exhibiting normal differentiation ability, morphology and gene expression profiles

In order to confirm if the gene editing influences the differentiation ability of BCs, we seeded WT and B2m‾ BCs in the ALI system, and performed immunofluorescence assay to observe the ciliated cells and secretory cells differentiated by the BCs (Fig. S3A, S3B). The data showed that there were no significance between WT and B2m‾ BCs in the differentiation of ciliated cells and secretory cells (Fig. S3C, S3D). B2m‾ BCs also continued to express KRT5 and p63—canonical markers of BCs (Fig. 4A).

Subsequently, to prepare for transplantation experiments, WT and B2m‾ BCs were transduced with the lentiviruses encoding EGFP (Fig. 4B). Clones with high EGFP expression were selected (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed comparable fluorescence intensity of EGFP between the EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs (Fig. 4D). Additionally, the CCK-8 assay showed no significant differences in viability between EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs (Fig. 4E). To further evaluate the impact of B2m KO on transcriptional profiles, RT‐qPCR was conducted to measure the mRNA levels of Krt5, Trp63, Sox2, Sox9, Mki67, Ngfr and Itga6. The results showed that the transcriptional levels of these genes in B2m‾ BCs were not significantly altered compared to those in WT BCs (Fig. 4F), indicating that KO of the B2m gene did not influence key molecular features of BCs.

Ultimately, two clones, one from each group, with equivalent EGFP expression and cell viability were selected for use in subsequent transplantation experiments.

The differentiation and rejection of allogeneic BCs in C57BL/6 mice after PDOC injury

To create space for BC colonization, PDOC was used to remove the epithelial layer of tracheas and bronchi (Fig. S4). Following intratracheal administration of PDOC via the glottis in C57BL/6 mice, single clone-derived EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs were transplanted onto the denuded basal membrane on day 2 post‐injury (Fig. S5). Tracheal tissues were collected on day 28 post‐injury for histopathological analysis. H&E staining of these sections showed complete epithelium repair in both the EGFP+ WT BC‐ and the EGFP+ B2m‾ BC‐transplanted groups (Fig. 5A). Rare immune cell infiltration was observed in the basal membrane and surrounding mesenchymal tissue in both groups, indicating a limited immune response (Fig. S6). Furthermore, the epithelium appeared intact and showed no signs of epithelial flattening or denudation, features commonly associated with epithelial rejection [29, 30]. Both EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs adhered effectively to the basal membrane (Fig. 5B) and differentiated into ciliated cells (Fig. 5C) and secretory cells (Fig. 5D). Notably, no significant difference in the percentage of EGFP+ cells was observed between the EGFP+ WT BC‐ and EGFP+ B2m‾ BC‐transplanted groups (Fig. 5E). Together, these results reveal that hypoimmunogenic allogeneic BCs can integrate into the tracheal epithelium and differentiate appropriately in vivo, without inciting evident immune rejection during the 28‐day observation window.

B2m‾ BCs exhibited hypoimmunogenic response in PDOC-damaged lungs

To further investigate the effects of transplanted BCs beyond the trachea, immunohistochemistry and H&E staining were conducted on both intrapulmonary airway and alveolar tissues. Our findings revealed that both EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs reached the lower airways and alveoli following transplantation (Fig. 6A). By day 28 post-PDOC injury, most lung tissues in the EGFP+ B2m‾ BC‐transplanted group exhibited normal morphology, akin to the PDOC‐only group (Fig. 6B). In contrast, lungs from mice that received EGFP+ WT BCs showed signs of severe injury, including bronchial epithelial cell shedding and alveolar hyperemia with collapse, which was not observed in the EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs group (Fig. S7). Additionally, compared to PDOC infusion and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs transplantation, transplantation of EGFP+ WT BCs significantly increased CD45+ immune cell infiltration in the lungs (Fig. 6C). These results indicate that although both EGFP+ WT BCs and EGFP+ B2m‾ BCs did not induce an immune response in the tracheas, allogeneic transplantation of BCs will provoke immune rejection in the lower airway and alveoli, which could be addressed by using hypoimmunogenic BCs.

Discussion

Airway BCs, located in the basement membrane of the airway epithelium, play essential roles in airway development and homeostasis, as they have the capacity to proliferate and differentiate into various epithelial cell types, including ciliated, goblet and club cells [31]. Some respiratory diseases, such as COPD, can disrupt the niches of BCs, highlighting the potential of BC transplantation as a therapeutic strategy for these conditions [32]. Autologous BC transplantation offers the advantage of avoiding immune rejection; however, it has notable limitations. Firstly, a significant proportion of abnormal BC subsets have been identified in COPD patients [7]. Given the absence of an effective method to purify normal BCs, this raises concerns regarding the therapeutic efficacy of autologous BC transplantation. Furthermore, the prolonged time required to cultivate autologous BCs may exceed the waiting period for patients in critical need. Therefore, hypoimmunogenic allogeneic BCs represent a viable alternative to address these challenges.

In the present study, we aimed to generate allogeneic hypoimmunogenic BCs, and evaluate their differentiation and survivability after transplantation into the respiratory system. Firstly, we confirmed that MHC-I is the main transplantation antigen expressed in BCs, and revealed the characteristics of subcellular localization of Cas9 protein in BCs. Then, we used the CRISPR/Cas9 system to knock out the B2m gene, which encodes the conserved light chain of the MHC‐I, to produce hypoimmunogenic cells. Although pooled B2m‐KO cells exhibited decreased expression of B2M and H‐2Kd, some BCs still expressed B2M and H‐2Kd at normal levels. To prevent the potential immune response triggered by these un‐edited BCs, we selected and screened single clone‐derived B2m‾ BCs, ensuring complete B2m KO in each BC. These homogeneous B2m‾ BCs, derived from a BALB/c mouse, were then transplanted into the respiratory system of C57BL/6 mice. Though the denudation of basement membrane was evident on day 0.5 post PDOC injury (Fig. S4A), cell transplantation at this time point led to a high incidence of asphyxia in mice in our preliminary experiments. In contrast, cell transplantation on day 2 avoided unnecessary mouse deaths, thereby reducing the number of animals used (in line with laboratory animal ethics). In return, no significant difference in recipient mortality was observed in the present study. It was reported that the basement membrane was covered by residual BCs within 24 h of PDOC injury, but a lot of colonization space remained available for exogenous BCs in the tracheas on day 2 in our study (Fig. S4B) [33]. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the use of PDOC with different molecular formula or chemical structure. Consequently, cell transplantation was performed on day 2 after PDOC-induced injury in this study. And our results confirmed that the B2m‾ BCs successfully differentiated into ciliated and secretory cells and were able to survive in both the tracheas and lungs.

In the field of hypoimmunogenic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have been extensively studied. The application of hypoimmunogenic iPSCs based on B2m KO has also been reported in anti-tumor therapy and cardiovascular tissue regeneration [34–36]. Due to their capacity for indefinite proliferation in vitro, iPSCs serve as one of the ideal sources for hypoimmunogenic BCs [37]. However, hypoimmunogenic BCs derived directly from isolated adult BCs offer distinct therapeutic advantages. Unlike iPSCs, adult BCs do not require in vitro differentiation prior to transplantation. Following gene editing, adult BCs can be transplanted immediately, thereby streamlining the therapeutic application process. Thus, hypoimmunogenic BCs show promising and unique potential in clinical applications.

CRISPR/Cas9 is a novel, highly specific gene-editing technology that primarily influences the normal translational expression of proteins encoded by target genes, resulting in premature termination of the edited genes during subsequent protein translation [38]. To address the challenge of transfection efficiency in adult stem cells, we combined the sgRNA and the Cas9 protein extracellularly, and then transferred the combination into the BCs via the liposome transfection method, a technique reported in several studies [39, 40]. Our gene-editing method reportedly bypasses the difficulties associated with the transcription and/or translation of both sgRNA and Cas9 protein and their combination efficiency in cells, thereby reducing off‐target effects [41]. This approach successfully edited the B2m gene, which subsequently silenced the expression of B2M protein in BCs. Moreover, B2m gene editing did not interfere with the transcriptional expression of the other important genes in the BCs. KRT5‐ and p63‐expressing B2m‾ BCs demonstrated their potential in regenerating the tracheal epithelium after transplantation.

In the aspect of immune rejection, our study revealed the distinct responses between the tracheas and lungs after BC transplantation. In the tracheal epithelium, both WT BCs and B2m‾ BCs were capable of differentiating into the ciliated and secretory cells, surviving up to 28 days post-PDOC injury without evidence of immune cell infiltration. This finding aligns with the work of Farrow et al., who reported that human BCs could survive in nasal airways for at least 8 weeks after PDOC‐induced injury, suggesting that allogeneic BC transplantation in upper airways may not be easy to provoke rejection [42]. Conversely, the transplantation of WT BCs into the lungs resulted in pronounced immune rejection, evidenced by bronchial epithelium exfoliation and alveolar collapse, mirroring the pathological features observed in immune rejection after lung transplantation [29, 30]. Immunohistochemical staining for CD45 in lung sections further suggests that WT BCs may incite an immune response in the lungs, a phenomenon not observed with B2m‾ BCs. Additionally, H&E‐stained histological sections indicated that transplanted BCs are likely exposed to distinct microenvironments within the trachea, intrapulmonary airway, and alveolar regions post‐PDOC injury (Fig. S4B), indicating that BC–immune cell interactions may differ across different respiratory compartments.

Despite the promising findings, our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the PDOC-induced airway injury model, while providing a reproducible method to remove the airway epithelium and create space for transplanted cells, does not fully recapitulate the complex pathophysiological processes underlying human airway diseases such as COPD, cystic fibrosis, or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Moreover, the advantage on transplantation efficiency of hypoimmunogenic BCs over wild-type BCs was not observed in the present study because of the lack of immune cells infiltration in the tracheas after PDOC injury (Fig. S6). As a result, the therapeutic potential of hypoimmunogenic BCs in clinically relevant disease contexts remains to be validated. Second, our evaluation of immune rejection was primarily based on histopathological assessment and pan-leukocyte (CD45) immunostaining. Although these approaches clearly demonstrated differences between WT and B2m‾ BCs transplantation, they do not allow for detailed characterization of the infiltrating immune cell subtypes, their activation states, or the specific molecular pathways involved. Technical challenges, including strong erythrocyte autofluorescence in the alveolar tissues and the inability to perform bronchoalveolar lavage without compromising epithelial integrity, limited our ability to conduct in-depth immune profiling. Third, the observation period in this study was restricted to 28 days post-transplantation. While this timeframe was sufficient to evaluate initial engraftment, differentiation, and acute immune responses, it does not address the long-term survival, functional integration, or potential delayed rejection of transplanted BCs. Finally, although we demonstrated the feasibility of generating hypoimmunogenic BCs through B2m KO, additional modifications, such as engineering of immune checkpoint molecules to achieve disease-specific immune evasion or design of kill-switch systems to provide extremely tight control over cell survival and/or proliferation, may be required in more stringent or clinically relevant settings [43].

Conclusions

In this study, we established a strategy to generate hypoimmunogenic BCs by disrupting the B2m gene with the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The resulting B2m‾ BCs preserved key BC characteristics, retained their capacity to differentiate into ciliated and secretory lineages, and demonstrated reduced immunogenicity following transplantation. Notably, these cells engrafted and contributed to epithelial repair in both proximal and distal regions of the respiratory tract without provoking overt immune rejection during the observation period. Together, these findings highlight hypoimmunogenic BCs as a promising candidate for universal airway regenerative therapy, while also underscoring the need for long-term studies and disease-relevant models to fully evaluate their clinical potential.
