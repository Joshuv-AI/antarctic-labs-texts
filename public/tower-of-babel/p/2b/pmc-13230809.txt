DNMT3A drives mitochondrial dysfunction in intracerebral hemorrhage via PGC-1α promoter hypermethylation
Authors: Jun Zhao, Qiongyan Tang, Lu Lu, Minmin Xu, Chunlin Zhang, Ruifang Ren, Ping Zhang, Jing Cao, Weimin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial dysfunction drives secondary injury in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), yet its upstream regulatory mechanisms remain elusive. This study examines whether ICH-induced DNA methyltransferase 3 A (DNMT3A) activation triggers PGC-1α promoter hypermethylation, causing mitochondrial impairment and neuronal damage through epigenetic silencing of this key metabolic regulator. Both in vitro and in vivo approaches were employed. Primary mouse cortical neurons were treated with hemin (20 µM) to mimic ICH injury. Epigenetic editing using CRISPR-dCas9-DNMT3A, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and chromatin immunoprecipitation were applied to assess locus-specific methylation and promoter activity. In vivo, a collagenase VII-induced striatal ICH model was established in C57BL/6 mice. Interventions included the DNMT3A inhibitor 5-Aza-CdR (1 mg/kg, i.p.) and AAV-shDNMT3A gene therapy. Mitochondrial function (ATP/ROS levels, TEM imaging), gene/protein expression (qPCR/Western blot), promoter methylation (MeDIP-qPCR), and neurobehavioral outcomes (mNSS, rotarod, H&E staining) were evaluated. ICH significantly upregulated DNMT3A expression and activity, leading to hypermethylation of the PGC-1α promoter and transcriptional repression of PGC-1α. This suppression impaired mitochondrial biogenesis (↓TFAM, ↓NRF1) and antioxidant capacity (↓SOD2), resulting in ATP depletion, ROS overproduction, and increased neuronal apoptosis. Both pharmacological inhibition (5-Aza-CdR) and genetic knockdown (AAV-shDNMT3A) of DNMT3A reversed PGC-1α promoter hypermethylation, restored mitochondrial homeostasis, attenuated neuronal apoptosis, and improved functional recovery post-ICH (P < 0.05 vs. ICH group). Our findings demonstrate that DNMT3A promotes PGC-1α promoter hypermethylation, exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction and brain injury after ICH. Targeted inhibition of DNMT3A mitigates these effects, supporting the DNMT3A–PGC-1α axis as a promising therapeutic target for ICH treatment. Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), accounting for 10–15% of all strokes, is the most devastating cerebrovascular disease with a 30-day mortality rate exceeding 40% and a profound lack of effective therapies1. The primary neurological injury results directly from hematoma formation, but the ensuing secondary brain injury—driven by complex cascades including inflammation, oxidative stress, and cytotoxic edema—is responsible for much of the long-term disability2. Within this pathogenic cascade, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a critical hub, where iron and heme overload from lysed erythrocytes trigger bioenergetic failure, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and ultimately neuronal death3–5.

The peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) serves as a master regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis, coordinating programs for mitochondrial biogenesis (via nuclear respiratory factor 1[NRF1] and mitochondrial transcription factor A [TFAM], antioxidant defense (via superoxide dismutase 2 [SOD2] and glutathione peroxidase [GPx]), and cellular energy metabolism6–9. Its pivotal role in neuron survival is well-documented in chronic neurodegenerative diseases, where PGC-1α deficiency exacerbates pathology, and in ischemic stroke models, where its activation is neuroprotective10–13. Paradoxically, despite the established centrality of mitochondrial failure in ICH pathology, the regulation and function of PGC-1α in this acute hemorrhagic context remain almost entirely unexplored.

Epigenetic mechanisms, particularly DNA methylation, have recently gained recognition as key modulators of gene expression in neurological disorders14. DNA methyltransferase 3 A (DNMT3A), responsible for de novo DNA methylation, can silence neuroprotective genes by catalyzing promoter hypermethylation15. Aberrant DNMT3A activity has been implicated in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease and traumatic brain injury16–18. Most notably, recent evidence reveals DNMT3A-mediated hypermethylation of the PGC-1α promoter in models of cardiac ischemia and Alzheimer’s disease, directly linking this epigenetic mechanism to mitochondrial impairment19–21. However, whether this DNMT3A-PGC-1α axis operates in ICH—a condition dominated by acute hematotoxic components distinct from chronic neurodegeneration or ischemia—remains a significant and unaddressed question.

Our preliminary data provided the first evidence of concurrent DNMT3A upregulation and PGC-1α suppression in the murine ICH striatum, suggesting a potential causal relationship. We therefore hypothesize that ICH-induced DNMT3A overexpression drives pathological hypermethylation of the PGC-1α promoter, leading to its transcriptional repression and subsequent impairment of mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense, ultimately exacerbating neuronal apoptosis. To test this hypothesis, we employed an integrated approach combining locus-specific epigenetic editing via CRISPR-dCas9-DNMT3A, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and dual-luciferase assays for mechanistic validation, alongside therapeutic interventions using the clinically approved DNMT inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-CdR) and AAV-shDNMT3A gene therapy. This study aims to establish a novel epigenetic-metabolic pathway in ICH pathogenesis and identify a promising therapeutic strategy for this devastating condition.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethical statement

Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks, 22–25 g, Vital River Laboratory) were housed under controlled conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 °C) with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No EC-2022-705). All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978) and the ARRIVE guidelines 2.022. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Experimental groups and interventions

For in vivo studies, C57BL/6 mice (n = 15 per group) were randomly assigned to the following groups: the Sham group received intrastriatal saline injection followed by intraperitoneal (i.p.) PBS; the ICH group was subjected to collagenase VII-induced striatal hemorrhage followed by i.p. PBS; the ICH + CdR group underwent ICH induction and received i.p. injection of the DNMT3A inhibitor 5-Aza-CdR (1 mg/kg) starting at 1 h post-ICH and continuing daily for 7 days; and the ICH + shDNMT3A group received intrastriatal co-injection of AAV9 particles carrying short hairpin RNA targeting DNMT3A (AAV9-shDNMT3A, 1 × 10¹² vg/mL) during ICH induction.

For in vitro experiments, primary cortical neurons from E16.5 C57BL/6 mice were cultured for 7 days and divided into groups: the Control group was remain untreated; the Hemin group was exposed to 20 µM hemin for 24 h to mimic ICH conditions; the Hemin + siDNMT3A group was transfected with 50 nM siRNA targeting DNMT3A prior to hemin exposure, with a parallel Hemin + siCtrl group receiving non-targeting scrambled siRNA to control for off-target effects; and the Hemin + CdR group was pretreated with 1 µM 5-Aza-CdR for 1 h before hemin treatment. Furthermore, in specific CRISPR-dCas9-DNMT3A epigenetic manipulation experiments, neurons transfected with non-targeting scrambled sgRNA served as essential negative controls to account for potential non-specific effects of the CRISPR system components.

ICH model establishment and histopathological assessment

The ICH model23 was induced in anesthetized mice (maintained under 1.5–2% isoflurane in 70% N₂O/30% O₂) via stereotactic injection of collagenase VII (0.075 U in 0.5 µL saline) into the left striatum at predetermined coordinates (AP + 0.2 mm, ML + 2.0 mm, DV − 3.0 mm from bregma) using a precision infusion pump set at 0.1 µL/min, while Sham-operated controls received an equal volume of saline following identical surgical procedures with core body temperature maintained at 37 °C throughout the operation. For histopathological evaluation, coronal brain Sect. (20 μm thickness) encompassing the striatal region were prepared at both 24 h and 7 d post-ICH timepoints, followed by standard Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining procedures to visualize hemorrhage and tissue morphology; subsequently, the lesion area was systematically quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) with three representative sections analyzed per animal to ensure consistent morphological assessment.

At the end of experiments, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) followed by transcardial perfusion with PBS for tissue collection. For animals that exhibited severe distress or reached predefined humane endpoints (including sustained weight loss > 20%, inability to access food or water, prolonged immobility, or seizures), euthanasia was performed immediately following the same protocol. Animal welfare and clinical signs were monitored at least twice daily by trained personnel.

Animal mortality and exclusions were monitored throughout the study. In the collagenase VII-induced ICH model, overall mortality was approximately 3–5% across all ICH-operated groups, with no significant differences between treatment groups. The reported sample size (n = 15 per group) represents the final number of animals analyzed after excluding those that died before the endpoint or met predefined exclusion criteria. To achieve this, 16–17 animals were initially enrolled per ICH-operated group to account for anticipated attrition. Exclusion criteria were established a priori and included: death before endpoint, failure to induce hematoma (confirmed by H&E staining), technical surgical failure, or severe distress requiring early euthanasia (e.g., weight loss > 20%, seizures, prolonged immobility). Excluded animals accounted for < 5% of the total and were balanced across groups. No animals were replaced after exclusion.

Mitochondrial ultrastructure and functional analysis

Mitochondrial integrity and function were evaluated through ultrastructural examination and biochemical assays. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, peri-hematomal striatal tissues were collected at 24 h post-ICH and processed through sequential fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide, followed by epoxy resin embedding; ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were subsequently stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and imaged using a Hitachi HT7800 microscope, with mitochondrial cristae density and cross-sectional area quantified from at least 50 mitochondria per experimental group.

Concurrently, ATP levels in both peri-hematomal tissues (collected at 24 h post-ICH) and cultured neurons (after 24 h hemin treatment) were measured using a commercial ATP Assay Kit according to manufacturer specifications, with luminescent signals detected by a microplate reader to reflect bioenergetic status.

To assess oxidative stress, mitochondrial ROS production was detected via MitoSOX Red staining (5 µM) at 24 h post-ICH in tissue sections and after 24 h hemin treatment in cultured neurons, employing flow cytometry for cellular analysis and fluorescence intensity quantification using ImageJ software for tissue sections, thereby providing complementary functional and morphological assessment of mitochondrial homeostasis. For flow cytometric analysis of mitochondrial ROS, peri-hematomal striatal tissues were collected at 24 h post-ICH and processed immediately. Tissue dissociation was performed using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-092-628) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, tissues were minced into small pieces (< 1 mm³) using sterile scalpels and transferred to C-tubes containing enzyme mix (200 µL of enzyme P and 100 µL of buffer Y in 4.7 mL of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Samples were dissociated using the gentle magnetic-activated cell sorting Octo Dissociator with heaters (Miltenyi Biotec) using the programmed protocol ‘37C_NTDK_1’. After dissociation, the cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove debris, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of HBSS (Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-free) containing 0.5% BSA.

To isolate neurons, we performed magnetic-activated cell sorting using Neuron Isolation Beads. The single-cell suspension was incubated with 10 µL of biotin-conjugated anti-neuron-specific antibodies (Neuron Isolation Kit, Miltenyi Biotec, 130-115-390) for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by 20 µL of anti-biotin magnetic microbeads for an additional 15 min at 4 °C. Cells were then loaded onto LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec) placed in a magnetic field. The columns were washed three times with 3 mL of HBSS/BSA buffer, and the magnetically labeled neurons were eluted by removing the column from the magnetic field and flushing with 5 mL of buffer. An aliquot of the isolated cells was stained with NeuN antibody (1:100, Millipore MAB377) to verify neuronal purity, which consistently exceeded 92% across all samples.

For mitochondrial ROS detection, isolated neurons were resuspended in pre-warmed HBSS containing 5 µM MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen, M36008) and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cells were washed twice with warm HBSS and immediately analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). MitoSOX Red fluorescence was excited at 510 nm and detected at 580 nm. For each sample, 20,000 events were acquired, and debris was excluded based on forward/side scatter gating. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8, BD Biosciences). Mean fluorescence intensity was calculated for each sample and normalized to the Sham group mean. All procedures were performed under dim light to minimize photobleaching.

Western blot and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ICH-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, comprehensive protein and gene expression analyses were performed. Protein and RNA were extracted from peri-hematomal striatal tissues collected at 24 h post-ICH or from cultured neurons after 24 h hemin treatment. Protein extracts from peri-hematomal tissues or cultured neurons were prepared using RIPA buffer and analyzed by Western blotting with specific antibodies against DNMT3A (1:1000, ab2850, Abcam), PGC-1α (1:1000, ab54481, Abcam), TFAM (1:1000, #8076, Cell Signaling), SOD2 (1:1000, #13194, Cell Signaling), and Cleaved Caspase-3 (1:1000, #9664, Cell Signaling) to evaluate key regulatory proteins in the DNMT3A-PGC-1α pathway. Parallel qPCR analysis was conducted using SYBR Green methodology to measure corresponding mRNA expression levels. Primer sequences for all qPCR analyses are provided in Table 1.

Analysis of DNMT3A-PGC-1α promoter interaction and transcriptional regulation

Complementary epigenetic investigations included methylated DNA immunoprecipitation (MeDIP-qPCR), wherein genomic DNA was immunoprecipitated with an anti-5-methylcytosine antibody (28692, Cell Signaling) followed by qPCR quantification of PGC-1α promoter methylation. MeDIP-qPCR and ChIP-qPCR analyses were performed on samples collected at 24 h post-ICH (tissue) or after 24 h hemin treatment (neurons). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed to examine DNMT3A binding to the PGC-1α promoter, using anti-DNMT3A antibody (ab2850, Abcam) for immunoprecipitation and specific primers for amplification. To further validate the functional consequence of this interaction on transcriptional regulation, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted. For dual-luciferase reporter assays, HEK293T cells were harvested 48 h after transfection. The human PGC-1α promoter fragment spanning from − 1500 to + 200 bp was cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector (Promega), and HEK293T cells were co-transfected with this promoter-reporter construct along with either a DNMT3A overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-DNMT3A) or corresponding empty vector control. After 48 h of transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega E2920) to quantitatively assess the impact of DNMT3A overexpression on PGC-1α promoter activity.

Assessment of neuronal apoptosis via TUNEL staining

Apoptotic cells in brain sections were detected using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR red (Roche 11684795910), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sections were counterstained with DAPI. TUNEL staining was performed on brain sections collected at 24 h and 7 d post-ICH and on cultured neurons after 24 h hemin treatment. TUNEL-positive cells were counted in five random fields per section under a fluorescence microscope.

Neurobehavioral and functional outcome evaluation

Functional recovery following ICH and subsequent interventions was systematically assessed through a series of neurobehavioral tests conducted by an investigator blinded to the experimental groupings. Neurological deficits were quantitatively evaluated using the modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) at 1, 3, and 7 days post-ICH, wherein a comprehensive assessment of motor, sensory, and reflex functions was performed on a standardized 0–18-point scale with elevated scores indicating more severe neurological impairment. Complementary evaluation of motor coordination and balance was carried out exclusively at day 7 post-ICH using the rotarod test, wherein mice were placed on an accelerating rotating rod (4–40 rpm over a 5-minute period) and the latency to fall from the apparatus was precisely recorded as the primary outcome measure for locomotor function.

Statistical analysis

For in vivo experiments, sample sizes are specified in the figure legends. For molecular and biochemical analyses (Western blot, qPCR, MeDIP‑qPCR, ChIP‑qPCR), ‘n’ refers to the number of biologically independent animals (or independent cultures for in vitro experiments) used per group; typically 6 animals per group were randomly selected for these assays, as detailed in the respective figure legends. For TEM quantification, mitochondria from 3 animals per group were analyzed. For behavioral and histological assessments, all 15 animals per group were included.

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (parametric data) or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test (non-parametric data). Longitudinal data (mNSS) were analyzed by two-way repeated measures ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 (with *p < 0.05 vs. sham and #p < 0.05 vs. ICH group) was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.

Cell culture and source

HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC® CRL-3216™) were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂. No specific ethical approval was required for the use of this commercially available, established cell line.

Results

ICH induces mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic regulator dysregulation

The collagenase VII-induced ICH model successfully generated striatal hematomas, as confirmed by H&E staining at 24 h post-ICH. Compared to the Sham group, the ICH group exhibited significant hematoma formation with extensive erythrocyte extravasation and tissue structural disruption in the striatal parenchyma (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis of the lesion cross-sectional area at 24 h post-ICH revealed a value of 3.8 ± 0.08 mm2 in the ICH group, which was significantly larger than that in the Sham group (n = 15 per group, P < 0.01, Fig. 1B). This was accompanied by significant mitochondrial ultrastructural damage in peri-hematomal neurons at 24 h post-ICH, as revealed by TEM. Compared to the Sham group, the ICH group exhibited disrupted cristae (cristae density decreased by 42.7%, P < 0.01) and marked mitochondrial swelling (cross-sectional area increased by 2.3-fold, P < 0.001, Fig. 1C-E). These structural defects were correlated with severe functional impairment. Compared to the Sham group, the ICH group showed a 58.4% decrease in ATP levels (P < 0.001) and a 3.1-fold increase in mitochondrial ROS measured at 24 h post-ICH (P < 0.001, Fig. 1F-G).

Concurrently, DNMT3A expression was markedly upregulated 24 h post-ICH. Compared to the Sham group, the ICH group showed significant increases in DNMT3A mRNA (2.8-fold, P < 0.001) and protein levels (2.5-fold, P < 0.01), along with a 67.3% increase in its enzymatic activity (Fig. 2A–C, P < 0.01) at 24 h. In contrast, PGC-1α expression assessed at 24 h post-ICH was significantly suppressed. Compared to the Sham group, the ICH group exhibited reductions in PGC-1α mRNA (62.1% decrease, P < 0.001) and protein levels (54.8% decrease, P < 0.001, Fig. 2D–E). This suppression extended to its key downstream targets, with the ICH group showing significantly lower protein levels of TFAM (48.2% decrease, P < 0.01) and SOD2 (51.7% decrease, P < 0.01) compared to the Sham group (Fig. 2F–G).

DNMT3A directly suppresses PGC-1α via promoter hypermethylation

Epigenetic analysis of peri-hematomal brain tissue at 24 h post-ICH revealed significant hypermethylation of the PGC-1α promoter. Compared to the Control group, the ICH group showed a 3.9-fold increase in methylation enrichment at the PGC-1α promoter (P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). This epigenetic alteration was recapitulated in vitro in primary cortical neurons treated with 20 µM hemin for 24 h to mimic ICH conditions. Compared to the Control group, hemin treatment induced a 3.2-fold increase in PGC-1α promoter methylation (P < 0.01, Fig. 3B). Crucially, this hemin-induced hypermethylation was effectively abolished by either DNMT3A knockdown or 5-Aza-CdR pretreatment. Compared to the Hemin group, the Hemin + siDNMT3A group showed a 71.4% reduction in promoter methylation (P < 0.001), while the Hemin + CdR group showed a 68.9% reduction (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B).

To establish a direct causal relationship, we employed CRISPR-dCas9-DNMT3A for locus-specific methylation targeting the PGC-1α promoter. Compared to the Scrambled sgRNA group, neurons with targeted PGC-1α promoter methylation exhibited a 63.7% reduction in PGC-1α expression (P < 0.001, Fig. 3C–D) and exacerbated ATP depletion (52.3% decrease, P < 0.01, Fig. 3E). ChIP analysis was performed on neurons after 24 h hemin treatment confirmed the direct binding of DNMT3A to the PGC-1α promoter. Compared to the Control group, the Hemin group showed a 4.1-fold increase in DNMT3A enrichment at the PGC-1α promoter (P < 0.001, Fig. 3F). Consistent with these findings, dual-luciferase reporter assays conducted 48 h after transfection demonstrated that compared to the empty vector control, DNMT3A overexpression suppressed PGC-1α promoter activity by 59.6% (P < 0.001, Fig. 3G).

Mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis are PGC-1α-dependent

Suppression of PGC-1α directly impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense mechanisms. Compared to the Scrambled sgRNA, neurons (after 24 h hemin treatment) with targeted PGC-1α promoter methylation exhibited significant impairment of mitochondrial biogenesis, as evidenced by reduced TFAM protein levels (51.2%, P < 0.01, Fig. 4A) and decreased NRF1 mRNA expression (48.7%, P < 0.01, Fig. 4B). Concomitantly, antioxidant defense capacity was substantially compromised, with marked reductions in SOD2 protein expression (56.3%, P < 0.01, Fig. 4C) and GPx activity (↓49.8%, P < 0.01, Fig. 4D).

These PGC-1α deficiency-induced mitochondrial and antioxidant dysfunctions were directly linked to exacerbated neuronal apoptosis. Compared to the Scrambled sgRNA group, neurons with targeted PGC-1α promoter methylation showed a 4.8-fold increase in TUNEL⁺ cells (P < 0.001, Fig. 4E-F) and a 3.3-fold elevation in cleaved caspase-3 levels (P < 0.001, Fig. 4G). The consistent apoptotic activation following targeted epigenetic silencing of PGC-1α confirms its critical role in driving neuronal death under conditions mimicking hemorrhagic injury.

Targeting DNMT3A rescues mitochondrial function and reduces neuronal apoptosis

To evaluate the transduction efficiency of AAV9‑shDNMT3A, a separate cohort of mice received intrastriatal injection of AAV9‑shDNMT3A or AAV9‑shScramble control expressing eGFP. Immunofluorescence analysis performed 7 days post‑injection revealed widespread eGFP expression throughout the striatum, with minimal spread into adjacent cortical regions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Quantitative analysis of eGFP mean fluorescence intensity showed no significant difference between the AAV9‑shDNMT3A and AAV9‑shScramble groups (1,056.2 ± 52.7 vs. 1,024.6 ± 58.3, P > 0.05), confirming comparable transduction efficiency between the two viral preparations. In addition, striatal tissues were collected at 1, 3, and 7 days post‑injection, and DNMT3A protein levels were quantified by Western blot to detect the knockdown effect (Supplementary Fig. 2). No significant difference in DNMT3A expression was observed between groups at day 1 (0.99 ± 0.03 vs. 0.88 ± 0.04, P > 0.05). At day 3, DNMT3A expression was significantly reduced in the AAV9‑shDNMT3A group compared to the AAV9‑shScramble control (0.99 ± 0.03 vs. 0.69 ± 0.05, *P < 0.05), corresponding to a knockdown efficiency of 29.8%. By day 7, DNMT3A expression was further decreased in the shDNMT3A group (1.00 ± 0.03 vs. 0.32 ± 0.06, **P < 0.01), achieving a knockdown efficiency of 68.3%. These data confirm that substantial DNMT3A knockdown (> 65%) is achieved by day 7 post‑injection, which aligns with the endpoint used in the main experimental study.

Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition with 5-Aza-CdR or genetic knockdown via AAV-shDNMT3A effectively reversed the epigenetic and molecular alterations induced by ICH. All molecular and functional assays were performed on tissues collected at 24 h post-ICH. Epigenetic analysis demonstrated that compared to the ICH group, both the ICH + CdR group and ICH + shDNMT3A group showed significant attenuation of PGC-1α promoter hypermethylation, with reductions of 65.1% (P < 0.001) and 69.8% (P < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 5A). Concomitantly, compared to the ICH group, Western blot analysis revealed substantial restoration of PGC-1α protein expression, with increases of 2.1-fold in the ICH + CdR group (P < 0.001) and 2.3-fold in the ICH + shDNMT3A group (P < 0.001, Fig. 5B). This restoration extended to key downstream effectors of PGC-1α, with both intervention groups showing significant upregulation of TFAM and SOD2 protein levels relative to the ICH group (P < 0.01, Fig. 5C).

Functional assessment confirmed that these molecular recoveries translated into marked improvements in mitochondrial integrity. Compared to the ICH group, ATP levels recovered to 85.7% of sham values in the ICH + CdR group (P < 0.01) and 89.2% in the ICH + shDNMT3A group (P < 0.001, Fig. 5D). Concurrently, mitochondrial ROS was significantly suppressed in both treatment groups relative to the ICH group, with reductions of 68.4% (ICH + CdR, P < 0.01, Fig. 5E) and 72.1% (ICH + shDNMT3A, P < 0.01, Fig. 5E). Evaluation of cellular survival demonstrated a profound decrease in neuronal apoptosis following DNMT3A inhibition. Compared to the ICH group, TUNEL⁺ cell counts were reduced by 62.7% in the ICH + CdR group (P < 0.01) and 67.9% in the ICH + shDNMT3A group (P < 0.01, Fig. 5F).

DNMT3A targeting improves neurobehavioral outcomes and alleviates histopathological damage

Critically, the cellular and molecular benefits conferred by DNMT3A inhibition culminated in significant functional recovery. Longitudinal assessment of neurological function using the mNSS was performed at 1, 3, and 7 days post-ICH (Table 2). At day 1, all ICH-operated groups exhibited severe neurological deficits with no significant differences among them, confirming comparable initial injury severity. By day 3, however, both the ICH + CdR and ICH + shDNMT3A groups showed significant improvement compared to the ICH group (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively). This recovery was further enhanced by day 7, where both treatment groups demonstrated markedly lower mNSS scores relative to the ICH group (P < 0.001; Fig. 6A; Table 2). Compared to the ICH group (12.3 ± 0.28), the ICH + CdR group achieved a significantly lower score of 6.2 ± 0.21 (P < 0.001), while the ICH + shDNMT3A group showed further improvement to 5.7 ± 0.18 (P < 0.001, Fig. 6A). Motor coordination, evaluated by the rotarod test, demonstrated parallel recovery. Compared to the ICH group (77.5 ± 1.3 s), the latency to fall from the accelerating rod significantly increased to 142.1 ± 1.8 s in the ICH + CdR group (P < 0.01) and 158.9 ± 2.2 s in the ICH + shDNMT3A group (P < 0.01, Fig. 6B).

Histopathological examination via H&E staining at day 7 post-ICH provided further corroboration of therapeutic efficacy. Compared to the ICH group, both intervention groups exhibited substantial reduction in lesion area, with decreases of 52.7% in the ICH + CdR group (P < 0.01) and 57.3% in the ICH + shDNMT3A group (P < 0.01, Fig. 6C-D). Neuronal damage was similarly attenuated, as quantified by neuronal density counts showing increases of 48.6% in the ICH + CdR group (P < 0.01) and 51.2% in the ICH + shDNMT3A group (P < 0.01) compared to the ICH group (Fig. 6C-D). These histopathological improvements closely paralleled the observed functional recovery, indicating comprehensive neuroprotection through DNMT3A targeting.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that DNMT3A-mediated epigenetic silencing of PGC-1α constitutes a fundamental pathogenic mechanism in ICH-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Our findings delineate a pathological axis wherein hematoma-derived components trigger DNMT3A upregulation, which in turn catalyzes hypermethylation of the PGC-1α promoter, leading to its transcriptional repression and subsequent disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis. The robust hypermethylation of the PGC-1α promoter, coupled with confirmed DNMT3A binding through ChIP analysis, establishes a direct mechanistic link beyond previously reported correlations in other disease models. Notably, the application of CRISPR-dCas9-DNMT3A for locus-specific methylation represents a significant methodological advancement, enabling us to demonstrate that targeted PGC-1α promoter methylation alone suffices to recapitulate ICH-like mitochondrial damage in vitro, thus establishing PGC-1α suppression as a causative factor rather than merely a correlative observation.

The therapeutic implications of this epigenetic pathway are substantial, as demonstrated by the significant neuroprotection achieved through both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of DNMT3A. The efficacy of 5-Aza-CdR is particularly promising from a translational perspective, given its FDA-approved status, established pharmacokinetics including blood-brain barrier penetration, and compatibility with acute intervention timelines. While AAV-shDNMT3A exhibited superior specificity in mitochondrial rescue, the broader demethylating activity of 5-Aza-CdR may offer additional benefits through simultaneous reactivation of multiple neuroprotective pathways, though this non-specificity also necessitates careful consideration of potential off-target effects. Our work represents a paradigm shift from previous mitochondrial-targeted strategies that primarily focused on downstream ROS scavenging, instead addressing the upstream epigenetic drivers of mitochondrial failure and offering the advantage of multi-pathway modulation through concurrent enhancement of both mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant capacity.

The contextualization of our findings within the broader field of mitochondrial medicine reveals important distinctions from other cerebrovascular conditions. Unlike ischemic stroke where PGC-1α agonists show efficacy, the unique hematotoxic milieu of ICH appears to necessitate epigenetic reactivation rather than transcriptional stimulation, highlighting the importance of disease-specific therapeutic design. Furthermore, the temporal dynamics of DNMT3A upregulation preceding maximal ROS generation provides an early intervention window that aligns favorably with clinical emergency timelines.

Several limitations warrant consideration in interpreting our results. While our model primarily examines neuronal mechanisms, potential DNMT3A regulation of PGC-1α in glial cells and its influence on neuroinflammation remain to be fully elucidated. The 7-day endpoint, while sufficient to demonstrate significant functional recovery, precludes assessment of long-term outcomes and potential chronic adaptations. Future validation in human ICH specimens and exploration of combined therapeutic approaches, such as 5-Aza-CdR with iron chelators, represent promising directions for further investigation. Additionally, the potential of PGC-1α promoter methylation as a prognostic biomarker merits exploration in clinical settings. We also acknowledge the absence of an AAV-shScramble control group in our in vivo study. While the concordance between pharmacological (5‑Aza‑CdR) and genetic (shDNMT3A) interventions supports the specificity of DNMT3A targeting, future studies incorporating a scrambled shRNA viral control will be necessary to definitively exclude non‑specific effects of viral delivery or the injection procedure.

In conclusion, our identification and validation of the DNMT3A-PGC-1α epigenetic-metabolic axis provides not only a significant advance in understanding ICH pathogenesis but also establishes a rationale for targeting epigenetic mechanisms in stroke therapeutics. The convergence of mechanistic insights and therapeutic validation through multiple intervention strategies underscores the translational potential of this pathway for addressing the critical unmet need in ICH management.
