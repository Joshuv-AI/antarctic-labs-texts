Spatial mapping of microbial communities by an integrated automation platform of sequential FISH
Authors: Zhaohui Cao, Wenlong Zuo, Lanxiang Wang, Lei Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySpatial mapping of microbial communities at single-cell resolution is opening up dimensions to understand microbiome organization and function. However, current fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) methods for microbiomes are limited by multiplexity and scalability. Here, we present the sequential error-robust FISH spatial mapping platform (SEER-Map) for fully automated imaging of complex microbial communities. We show that an integrated platform of fluidics control and fluorescence microscopy can perform 40 rounds of sequential FISH. We apply SEER-Map to profile complex microbial communities colonized on plant roots and identify distinct spatial patterns and species co-occurrence at the micron-scale. Our work establishes SEER-Map as a high-throughput and scalable platform for high-resolution spatial profiling of microbiomes.

Introduction

The spatial organization of microbial communities plays a critical role in microbe-microbe and host-microbe interactions.1,2,3,4,5,6 However, conventional microbiome analysis methods typically lose this vital spatial information during sample processing. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) targeting ribosomal RNA (rRNA) offers a powerful solution, enabling the direct visualization and quantification of specific microorganisms within their native spatial context at single-cell resolution.7,8,9,10

Despite its potential, traditional FISH approaches encounter significant limitations in taxonomic resolution and multiplexity, constrained by optical imaging technology.11,12 Several advanced methods have been developed to enhance FISH multiplexity for microbiome applications. CLASI-FISH utilizes combinatorial labeling and spectral imaging to achieve species discrimination beyond traditional fluorophore limitations.11,13 HiPR-FISH further increases multiplexing capacity through a two-step hybridization protocol coupled with advanced spectral unmixing.14,15,16 Previously, we developed sequential error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (SEER-FISH) that achieves high multiplexity through sequential rounds of probe hybridization with error-robust encoding schemes.17 While this approach demonstrated the potential for comprehensive spatial measurements of complex microbial communities, its reliance on manual operations limited its scalability.

To overcome these challenges, we develop the sequential error-robust FISH spatial mapping platform (SEER-Map) with the following innovations: (1) a custom-integrated platform of fluidics control and fluorescence imaging that achieves automation over dozens of hybridization cycles18,19,20,21; (2) an optimized sample pretreatment protocol that improves hybridization efficiency and cell detection, while reducing reagent costs by a 16-fold decrease in probe concentration22,23; and (3) a systematic probe design24 and error-robust encoding strategy19,25 that ensures accurate taxonomic identification. We spatially profile a 30-member synthetic bacterial community on Arabidopsis thaliana roots, revealing microbial colonization patterns on wild-type and mutant plants that would be undetectable by conventional approaches. Overall, our work demonstrates that SEER-Map is a scalable and user-friendly platform for high-resolution spatial analysis of complex microbiomes.

Results

An automated fluidics and imaging platform enables robust and scalable sequential hybridization

To overcome the scalability limitations of manual sequential hybridization, we developed an integrated system that combines a custom fluidics controller with a programmable fluorescence microscope (Figure 1A). The fluidics system features dozens of independently addressable reagent ports connected through fluidic manifolds to a peristaltic pump, allowing precise selection and delivery of hybridization buffers, wash solutions, and dissociation reagents with valve switching. The system outlet connects directly to a temperature-controlled flow chamber (Bioptechs FCS2) mounted on the microscope stage, enabling real-time buffer exchange during imaging, while maintaining stable focus through the Perfect Focus System. The SEER-Map workflow consists of three integrated operational phases (Figures 1B and 1C). Sample preparation and mounting occur offline using adhesive coverslips, followed by automated pretreatment to enhance cell permeabilization and probe accessibility. The core sequential hybridization process then proceeds within the specialized flow chamber, where each cycle comprises probe hybridization, washing to remove unbound probes, multi-channel fluorescence imaging, and probe dissociation20 (Figure 1B). Critical to the process is the maintenance of constant temperature and the prevention of air bubble formation through tight connections in pipelines as well as active degassing mechanisms. In each round, specific fluorescent probes bind to targets, generating raw fluorescence images during the imaging phase. The series of raw images collected over these sequential rounds are then computationally processed based on a predefined barcode codebook to generate a final, high-multiplexity spatial map where each taxon is identified with a unique color.17 This automated workflow eliminates manual intervention between cycles while maintaining precise spatial registration across imaging rounds.

To validate system performance and reliability, we conducted a 40-round automated experiment over approximately 15 h, with each hybridization-dissociation and image cycle completed in 22 min (Figures 1D–1F). Quantitative analysis of the resulting fluorescence signals revealed both high signal stability and uniform performance across the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), CY3, and CY5 channels. Mean hybridization intensities remained significantly elevated above residual dissociation signals (p < 0.001) across all channels, with nearly identical performance profiles between fluorophores (Figure 1D). Notably, the median residual fluorescence after each dissociation step was maintained below 5% for all channels, confirming thorough probe removal and minimal signal carryover between rounds (Figure 1E). Representative images from rounds 1, 2, 3, and 40 show strong hybridization signals and minimal residual signal after dissociation (Figure 1F). These results demonstrate the capability of SEER-Map for scalable and automated spatial profiling of microbial communities.

Optimization of sample pretreatment significantly improves hybridization efficiency

To maximize bacterial detection efficiency in complex microbial communities while minimizing reagent consumption, we systematically optimized the sample pretreatment protocol by evaluating the combined effects of lysozyme treatment and probe concentration. Three different protocols were compared using the universal bacterial probe EUB338: condition C1 (no lysozyme, 200 nM probe), condition C2 (5-min lysozyme treatment, 200 nM probe), and condition C3 (5-min lysozyme treatment, 12 nM probe). These conditions were tested on complex microbial communities comprising multiple bacterial species in both pure culture and Arabidopsis thaliana root-colonized samples.

In pure bacterial cultures, lysozyme pretreatment dramatically improved bacterial cell detection rates (Figures 2A–2C). Quantitative analysis of bright-field-identified cells revealed that lysozyme treatment nearly doubled the proportion of fluorescently labeled bacteria from 41% in the untreated condition (C1, n = 6,356 cells) to approximately 80% in both lysozyme-treated conditions (C2: 81%, n = 19,540 cells; C3: 75%, n = 17,260 cells). To validate the optimal probe concentration, we performed a systematic titration using a synthetic community ranging from 1.5 nM to 200 nM. Quantitative analysis revealed that the detection efficiency reaches a plateau around 12.5 nM (Figure S1). Therefore, 12 nM was selected as the optimal working concentration closely matching this threshold to balance performance and cost. The optimization benefits were even more pronounced in the challenging environment of root-associated microbial communities. To ensure direct comparability, we applied all three treatment conditions sequentially to identical root locations (Figures 2D–2F). The number of detected bacterial cells increased progressively from 5,090 in the untreated condition (C1) to 9,338 following lysozyme treatment with high probe concentration (C2) and reached 10,769 cells under the optimized low-concentration protocol (C3). This represents a remarkable 111% enhancement in bacterial detection compared to the baseline condition.

Interestingly, condition C3 achieved superior cell counts compared to C2 despite utilizing substantially lower probe concentrations. This counterintuitive result can be attributed to improved image segmentation in densely populated bacterial clusters (Figure 2D). Under the high-concentration condition (C2), probe saturation caused excessive fluorescence that diffused into neighboring regions, resulting in merged fluorescence signals that hindered the automated segmentation to resolve individual bacteria. In contrast, the optimized low-concentration protocol (C3) produced signals that remained below the saturation threshold while maintaining sufficient brightness for reliable detection, thereby enabling more effective segmentation and accurate single-cell resolution. Root-colonized bacterial cells were clearly detected by the universal probe EUB338. This specificity was further validated in pure cultures and by the absence of signal for the negative control probe rEUB338 on colonized roots, and for EUB338 on germ-free roots (Figures S2A and S2B). Thus, we demonstrate that bacterial cells on the rhizoplane can be correctly identified by SEER-Map without non-specific binding.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that our optimized pretreatment protocol achieves superior bacterial detection efficiency while reducing probe consumption by 16-fold, making sequential FISH applications more cost-effective and accessible for large-scale microbial community profiling.

Spatial profiling of synthetic microbial communities reveals specific micro-scale colonization

To demonstrate the capabilities of our optimized SEER-Map platform, we performed comprehensive spatial profiling of a 30-member synthetic microbial community (SynCom30) colonizing Arabidopsis thaliana roots from two genotypes: the wild-type ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) and the f6′h1 mutant (Figure 3A; Table S1). The F6′H1 (feruloyl-CoA 6′-hydroxylase 1) gene is essential for the biosynthesis of scopoletin and other catechol-type coumarins, which are key specialized metabolites secreted by Arabidopsis roots into the rhizosphere.26 These coumarins play a dual role: they facilitate plant iron uptake under nutrient-limiting conditions and act as selective antimicrobial agents that actively sculpt the root microbiome composition.26,27,28 By comparing the wild-type Col-0 with the coumarin-deficient f6′h1 mutant, we demonstrated the capability of SEER-Map to resolve shifts in the spatial distribution of the microbiome under chemical cues of the plant host. We employed a systematic probe design strategy to ensure high specificity and hybridization efficiency for each target taxon (Figure 3B). The workflow began with the collection of full-length 16S rRNA sequences for all 30 bacterial strains, followed by phylogenetic clustering and consensus sequence generation for each taxonomic group. Using the DECIPHER package with stringent parameters,24 we designed specific oligonucleotide probes and conducted comprehensive specificity screening against the entire community database. The resulting probe specificity was validated through computational analysis, as demonstrated in the hybridization efficiency (HybEff) matrix (Figure 3C). Specifically, HybEff is a theoretical metric derived from the Gibbs free energy (delta G) of the probe-target duplex, predicting the thermodynamic stability of hybridization events. This matrix displays predicted hybridization efficiencies ranging from 0.00 (no hybridization, white) to 1.00 (perfect hybridization, dark green). The strong diagonal pattern confirms exceptional probe specificity, with target taxa showing high HybEff values (>0.85) while exhibiting substantially lower cross-reactivity (<0.3) with non-target species, even among phylogenetically related organisms.

To ensure accurate taxonomic identification across multiple hybridization rounds, we generated an error-robust R8HD4 (Round 8, Hamming distance 4) codebook (Figure 3D). Here, the Hamming distance between two barcodes equals the number of hybridization rounds with different colors. Our codebook incorporates a minimum Hamming distance of four. This design provides sufficient separation between barcodes to ensure that even if some hybridization round fails or produces a non-specific signal, the cell can still be correctly identified.

Application of this comprehensive framework yielded high-resolution spatial maps of microbial colonization patterns within approximately 4 mm from the root tip (Figure 3E). Individual bacterial cells and their multicellular arrangements were visualized with sub-micrometer precision across both plant genotypes. The SEER-Map analysis pipeline successfully processed multi-round imaging data, identifying 28 of the 30 inoculated strains across both Col-0 and f6′h1 roots, demonstrating robust detection efficiency (Figure 3F).

We conducted analysis on the spatial ecology of root-colonized microbial communities. Using the linear dipole algorithm,31 we detected non-random clustering patterns extending up to approximately 20 μm, indicating structured spatial organization rather than random distribution (Figures 3G and S3). Further analysis uncovered complex spatial associations within 10 μm between different taxa, revealing both positive and negative co-occurrence relationships that indicate non-random spatial organization within the root-associated microbiome (Figure 3H). Notably, we observed genotype-specific colonization patterns for individual taxa. Agrobacterium sp. showed preferential colonization in the root differentiation zone of both genotypes (Figure 3I), while Lysobacter sp. exhibited distinct spatial preferences, with enhanced abundance near the root tip specifically in f6′h1 plants (Figure 3J). These findings highlight the sensitivity of our method to detect subtle but interesting biological differences in host-microbe interactions. Thus, the SEER-Map platform demonstrates the capacity for high-resolution spatial mapping of microbial communities and quantitative analysis of community structure.

Discussion

In this study, we present SEER-Map, an integrated platform of sequential error-robust FISH for spatial mapping of complex microbial communities. By addressing critical limitations in scalability and hybridization efficiency, SEER-Map is a powerful platform for high-resolution, multiplexed imaging of microbes in their native context. Key innovations include a fully automated fluidics and imaging system, a highly optimized sample pretreatment protocol, and a systematic probe design workflow that ensures high specificity for accurate species identification.

Manual operation is a major bottleneck in sequential FISH, limiting both throughput and reproducibility. Our custom-integrated platform overcomes this challenge, enabling the robust execution of 40 consecutive hybridization rounds. The reliability of such long-duration experiments is ensured by critical engineering optimizations. Specifically, system airtightness is secured through rigorous assembly of tubing and the imaging chamber. Furthermore, we incorporated a water-based bubble elimination mechanism that proactively prevents air entry during reagent exchanges. While highly effective, this mechanism could be further enhanced by integrating a low-pressure vacuum system to actively remove dissolved gases. Beyond operational reliability, our platform significantly lowers technical barriers by leveraging standard fluorescence microscopy instead of the expensive spectral imaging hardware required by other high-multiplex methods. Furthermore, the holistic automation of the entire workflow—including enzymatic pretreatment—eliminates manual variability, ensuring high reproducibility for large-scale spatial profiling.

In addition, our optimization of sample pretreatment not only dramatically improves bacterial detection efficiency but also simultaneously enables a 16-fold reduction in probe concentration, substantially reducing reagent costs while maintaining high labeling efficiency. Although hybridization kinetics may vary slightly due to sequence-specific factors (e.g., GC content), the lysozyme treatment primarily targets the cell wall—a universal physical barrier. Therefore, the optimized permeabilization conditions established with the universal probe are broadly applicable to the entire probe library, as evidenced by the robust detection of phylogenetically distinct strains in our SynCom experiments. Our analysis also revealed sample-dependent complexities in signal-to-background ratio (SBR) optimization. In pure microbial communities, lysozyme treatment increased both cellular fluorescence and background signals proportionally, resulting in reduced SBRs compared to untreated controls (C1: 4.43 vs. C2: 2.53, C3: 3.15). Interestingly, this trend was reversed in the more challenging environment of root tissues. Here, the optimized low-concentration protocol (C3) not only maximized cell detection but also achieved the highest SBR (11.69), outperforming both the untreated (C1: 10.88) and high-concentration probe (C2: 4.73) protocols. This difference likely reflects the complex optical environment within plant tissues. These findings underscore that while our framework provides a robust starting point, optimal results for different sample types may require empirical adjustments to balance cell permeabilization, signal intensity, and background fluorescence.

To demonstrate the versatility of SEER-Map, we applied the optimized protocol to profile microbial colonization on Arabidopsis thaliana leaves and Oryza sativa seedling roots (Figures S2C and S2D). These results suggest that the platform is broadly applicable to diverse plant-microbe interfaces.

Furthermore, the high-resolution spatial data obtained by SEER-Map allows for quantitative analysis of microbial community structure. Our analysis revealed complex spatial associations between different taxa. It is important to note that while our null model explicitly rules out random distribution, these positive or negative spatial associations do not solely imply direct ecological interactions (such as cooperation or competition). They may also reflect shared or distinct niche preferences driven by the host environment. For instance, positive co-occurrence may arise when multiple taxa independently colonize the same favorable micro-environment on the root surface, while negative associations may indicate colonization of spatially distinct niches.

The detailed methodology in this work enables straightforward implementation for researchers in their own laboratories and opens avenues for exploring the community structure and function of host-associated and environmental microbiomes.32,33,34 The most compelling future direction lies in expanding SEER-FISH beyond taxonomic profiling to include functional characterization. Integration of probes targeting functional genes (mRNA) alongside the current rRNA-targeted approach would enable simultaneous investigation of community structure and metabolic activity at single-cell resolution.23,35,36 Such capabilities would provide unprecedented insights into the interplay between spatial organization and phenotypic heterogeneity within microbiomes.

Limitations of the study

Although SEER-Map provides an easily accessible and scalable method for in situ microbial profiling, it has several limitations. First, as a probe-based method, SEER-Map is inherently limited to identifying known microbial taxa. Second, though automation greatly enhances the scalability of imaging experiments, the overall sample throughput is still lower than sequencing-based techniques. Third, the application of SEER-Map to different types of samples, such as mammalian tissues, may require optimization of sample clearing and permeabilization.37

Experimental model and study participant details

Bacterial strains

The synthetic microbial community (SynCom30) consists of 30 bacterial strains used to colonize Arabidopsis thaliana roots. The specific strains and their genomic information were sourced from the collection reported previously.29 All strains were cultured in ½ Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) medium at 28°C with shaking at 200 rpm under a normal aerobic atmosphere. A full list of bacterial strains is provided in the Table S1.

Plant growth conditions

Germ-free seedlings of Arabidopsis thaliana (wild-type Col-0 and f6′h1 mutant) and Oryza sativa (ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare, rice) were prepared according to a previously described method38 with minor modifications as detailed below. Seeds of Arabidopsis and rice were surface-sterilized by sequential treatment with 70% ethanol for 1 min and 5% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, followed by five times washes with sterile water. After sterilization, Arabidopsis seeds were stratified at 4°C for 2 days in the dark. Both Arabidopsis seeds and rice seeds were sown on 1× Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium with 3% sucrose and 0.6% agar. All the plates were oriented vertically and kept in a growth chamber (22°C for Arabidopsis, 26°C for rice, 16h light/8h dark, 50% humidity) to generate sterile seedlings.

Method details

Bacterial cell fixation

Bacterial cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 ×g for 5 min. After the supernatant was discarded, the bacterial pellet was resuspended in an equal volume of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and incubated at 4°C for 1–3 h. Following incubation, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 5000 ×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 1× PBS to reach a concentration of 108 to 109 cells/mL. One volume of ice-cold 100% ethanol was added and mixed with the cell suspension. Cells preserved in 50% ethanol were stored at −20°C prior to FISH imaging.

Bacterial preparation and inoculation

Prior to inoculation, individual bacterial strains to be inoculated were pre-cultured in half-strength Tryptic Soy Broth (½ TSB). Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation and subsequently washed with 1× PBS. The optical density at OD600 of each strain was determined using a BioTek plate reader. A synthetic bacterial community was prepared by mixing all strains at equal proportions.

For colonization experiments, 9-day-old Arabidopsis and 3-day-old rice seedlings were used. Wild-type Arabidopsis (Col-0) seedlings were cultured in liquid MS medium, whereas f6′h1 mutant seedlings were transferred to 6-well plates containing liquid modified Hoagland (-Fe) medium. Rice seedlings were maintained in their respective growth setup. The prepared synthetic bacterial community was inoculated into the respective liquid culture media to a final OD600 of 0.01. Seedlings were co-cultured with the bacteria for 6 days (Arabidopsis) or 1 day (rice) under sterile conditions.

Plants sample fixation

For plants colonization experiments, 9-day-old Arabidopsis and 3-day-old rice seedlings were inoculated with a synthetic microbial community for 6 and 1 day, respectively. Then the colonized roots are harvested using sterile scissors to dissect them from stems. The roots are transferred with sterile tweezers to a Petri dish containing 1× PBS and gently rinsed. The rinsed root samples from the same group are transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube containing 1 mL fresh 4% paraformaldehyde, ensuring complete immersion with no more than 10 roots per tube. The samples are incubated with fresh 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 h at room temperature or up to 24 h at 4°C. After incubation, the paraformaldehyde is removed and the samples are washed three times with 1× PBS in a Petri dish. The washed root samples are then transferred into 1.5 mL tubes filled with 1 mL 50% ethanol, ensuring full immersion. These samples can be stored at −20°C for several months before FISH imaging.

Adhesive coverslips preparation

To prepare adhesive coverslips for SEER-FISH, coverslips (40-mm, #1.5, Bioptechs) are immersed in a potassium dichromate/concentrated sulfuric acid solution for 2 h, followed by thorough rinsing with deionized water at least three times. The cleaned coverslips are soaked in 95% ethanol for 12 h and then air-dried in a laminar flow hood or a clean, dust-free environment. A 1:50 working solution of 50× Anti-Slice Escaping Agentia APES (Sangon Biotech, E676003) is prepared by diluting with acetone and used immediately. The ethanol-treated coverslips are dipped into the freshly prepared APES solution for 20–30 s, briefly paused, and then rinsed three times with deionized water to remove unbound APES. The coverslips are air-dried in a dust-free environment and stored until required.

Probe design and codebook generation

Full 16S rDNA and/or 23S rDNA sequences are preferred to enable construction of specific probes. When available, 23S rDNA sequences can be used to design probes targeting the large subunit rRNA.

The probe design workflow consists of the following steps: (i) collection of full 16S/23S rDNA sequences of all bacteria in the samples of interest; (ii) construction of a comprehensive gene database in GenBank format containing species taxonomic information for all bacteria with aligned sequences; (iii) importation of this database into the DECIPHER package in R; (iv) definition of target groups for probe design based on taxonomic information using the IdentifyByBank function in DECIPHER, allowing for targeting at various taxonomic levels from strain-specific to phylum; (v) creation of k-mers representing sequences in each group using the TileSeqs function; and (vi) probe design using the DesignProbes function with parameters modified from default settings.

The parameters used for probe design include: start = 120, end = 1450, minLength = 18, maxLength = 22, maxPermutations = 0, minCoverage = 1, hybTemp = 46, p = 1.5e-08, Na = 1, FA = 30, minEfficiency = 0.7, numProbeSets = 0, batchSize = 300, target = “SSU”. Probes generated for each target group are ranked based on their score values, with top-ranking probes selected to create a comprehensive community probe set.

Codebook generation

Multiplexity is determined by the number of targets to be distinguished in the sample. Codebooks with desired properties were generated using the CodeGenerator function in SEER-FISH package: [Codebook] = CodeGenerator(Number_of_colors, Length, HD), which generates a codebook with the specified number of colors, barcode length, and Hamming distance. The codebook is encoded numerically to represent different channels or fluorophores. Barcodes are then assigned to targets, either randomly or based on performance evaluation using the F1ScoreCal function, which calculates the F1 score for each strain by simulating barcode performance based on the specificity matrix.

Error-robust decoding strategy

The error-correction capability (t) is mathematically determined by the minimum Hamming distance (d) according to the formula t = floor((d-1)/2). For our R8HD4 codebook (d = 4), this theoretically guarantees the correction of all 1-bit errors. To further maximize decoding accuracy, we implemented a nearest neighbor decoding algorithm. This algorithm calculates the Hamming distance between the observed barcode and all reference barcodes in the codebook. Beyond correcting 1-bit errors, this strategy allows for the correction of certain 2-bit errors, provided that the observed barcode has a unique nearest neighbor with a Hamming distance of 2 (i.e., it is distinct from all other reference barcodes). Cells with observed barcodes that were ambiguous (equidistant to multiple reference barcodes) or exceeded the error tolerance were categorized as ‘Unidentified’.

Flow system assembly and operation

The automated fluidic control system (FluidicLab, Shanghai) consists of 32 independent injection ports and a PTFE manifold for superior chemical resistance. To minimize reagent cross-contamination and dead volume, all fluidic connections utilized PEEK tubing with a 1.6 mm outer diameter and a 0.5 mm inner diameter. Furthermore, the system was designed with a minimal port-to-port volume of 17.3μL to reduce reagent waste. Reagent delivery is precisely regulated by integrated valves with an actuation time of less than 200 ms, supporting a versatile flow control range of 50–500 μL/min. Sample reservoirs and wash solutions were loaded into designated channels, and tubing was connected to the FCS2 chamber inlet and outlet.

Liquid delivery was controlled via a custom script in the MultiChannelPump GUI software, using TTL synchronization with the imaging system. Pump speed and aspiration durations were calibrated prior to experiments. During SEER-FISH operations, the script automatically selected reservoirs and controlled buffer flow in each hybridization and wash cycle.

Microscopy setup and recording

The appropriate microscope is selected based on the imaging samples, with the Bioptech FCS2 chamber being suitable for inverted microscopes. Both wide-field fluorescence microscopes and confocal fluorescence microscopes can be used for imaging, with confocal microscopes preferred for samples with complex structures like root tissue due to their better signal-to-noise ratio.

To assemble the flow chamber for imaging, the FCS2 chamber attached to the FCS2 Stage Adapter is positioned onto the microscope’s 96-well plate stage holder. The adapter is secured to the holder using mounting screws, followed by the application of laboratory tape to stabilize the adapter onto the stage holder to prevent chamber displacement during stage movement.

Imaging parameters are set according to the chemical properties of the fluorescent molecules used. In preliminary experiments, three probes modified with distinct fluorescent molecules (universal probe EUB338) are sequentially imaged. Laser intensity and gain are optimized to ensure strong and consistent fluorescence intensity across the channels while minimizing background fluorescence in non-modified channels.

Multiple imaging positions are selected during the first round of SEER-FISH imaging by systematically scanning the sample to preview the field of view. The multi-point imaging module in Nikon’s ND2 software is used to record each position, with precise documentation being critical. After establishing target positions in the initial imaging round, subsequent rounds must re-image the identical positions to ensure spatial registration accuracy. Multiple fields of view (4–36, each 125 μm by 125 μm) are collected at each defined position using Nikon nd2 automatic acquisition mode with sequential excitation of laser lines 488 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm. Constant focus during imaging is achieved using Nikon PFS autofocus.

Sequential fluorescence in situ hybridization

The SEER-FISH protocol consists of iterative cycles of probe hybridization, imaging, and dissociation to achieve multiplexed spatial mapping of microbial communities. Fixed samples are first assembled into a FCS2 flow chamber (Bioptechs) with temperature control for automated buffer delivery and synchronized imaging. The complete workflow comprises sample preparation, pretreatment, and sequential hybridization cycles performed under controlled fluidic conditions.

Flow chamber assembly

Fixed samples were adhered onto adhesive coverslips (40 mm diameter, #1.5 thickness) and air-dried completely. A silicone gasket with a central rectangular cavity was positioned on the coverslip, followed by the placement of a micro-aqueduct slide to create a sandwich structure, allowing buffers to flow through the cavity housing the samples. The assembled chamber was mounted on the microscope stage, and the heating element was connected to the FCS2 temperature controller. Fluidics were controlled via a peristaltic pump set to a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, with all buffers prewarmed to the appropriate temperatures prior to delivery.

On-Chip sample pretreatment

To enhance cell permeabilization, 1 mL of fresh lysozyme solution (10 mg/mL prepared in lysozyme buffer) was flowed across the sample and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. Subsequently, the sample was washed with 1.5 mL of 1× PBS to remove the lysozyme.

Sequential hybridization cycles

The sequential hybridization process was executed through iterative rounds of probe hybridization, imaging, and dissociation. In each cycle, specific probes (12 nM), selected according to a predefined codebook, were suspended in a hybridization buffer (0.9 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, 30% formamide, 0.01% SDS). The sample was incubated with 1 mL of this solution at 46°C for 5–15 min. To eliminate residual and non-specifically bound probes, the sample was subsequently washed with a washing buffer (112 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH, 0.01% SDS, 5 μM EDTA) for 3 min at 46°C.

Following hybridization, imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective (NA 1.45). Data were acquired across 4–36 fields of view (125 μm × 125 μm each) via sequential excitation at 488 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm wavelengths, with phase-contrast images captured for structural reference and alignment. An automated focus system was employed to maintain constant focal depth, and imaging parameters were optimized during the first round to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio without saturation.

To complete the cycle, hybridized probes were stripped by incubating the sample with 1 mL of dissociation buffer (80% formamide, 0.01% SDS, 20 mM Tris-HCl) for 3 min at 46°C, followed by a 1.5 mL wash with 1× PBS to prepare for the subsequent round.

To validate probe specificity, control experiments were performed including hybridization with the non-sense probe rEUB338 on inoculated samples and hybridization of germ-free Arabidopsis roots with the universal probe EUB338.

Upon completion of all experimental cycles, the system was flushed with 5 mL of distilled water, and the flow lines and FCS2 chamber were disassembled for storage.

Image analysis

Automated image processing and target identification from the raw images were performed using the custom SEER-FISH analysis package, which has been previously described and validated.17 This package also enables downstream spatial correlation analysis. The specific version of the open-source code and detailed documentation used in this study are archived at Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7656027).

For image alignment and registration, the nd2reader submodule in the package is used to load.nd2 files captured by Nikon microscopy. Other image formats (e.g., .tif) can be accommodated by adapting the code as necessary. Appropriate channels are selected for image alignment, typically phase-contrast images, though for samples with complex backgrounds, superimposed fluorescence images of different channels could improve alignment accuracy. Cross-correlation is computed for image registration using the image from the first round as the reference. The position of images from each round is adjusted to maximize the cross-correlation value, ensuring proper alignment across all rounds. This procedure can be accomplished using the Alignment function from the SEER-FISH package, which can align a single image stack to the first image in the stack or align multiple image stacks to the same position as a reference stack.

For image segmentation and mask generation, an input image is selected, ideally a phase contrast image where bacteria cells appear darker than the background. For samples with complex backgrounds, the reversed fluorescent image obtained with the universal bacterial probe EUB338 can be used instead. The ImageSegment function provided by the SEER-FISH package employs an auto-threshold and watershed algorithm to generate a segmented mask from the input image. The resulting binary image delineates each cell for subsequent analysis, with parameters adjustable based on specific needs and results.

For fluorescence intensity quantification and cell identification, the mean fluorescence intensity of each cell is quantified within its defined mask for each fluorescence channel according to the segmented mask image. A statistical t test is performed to compare the fluorescence intensity of each cell in each channel against its local background fluorescence. If the fluorescence intensity in all channels is not significantly (p > 0.05) brighter than the background, a color code of 0 is assigned, indicating the absence of signal. Each bacterial cell is assigned a color code per imaging round, determined by the channel displaying the maximum fluorescence intensity and significantly brighter than the background. This process can be accomplished using the ColorIdentify function provided by the SEER-FISH package: [ResultCode] = ColorIdentify (Mask, Channel1, Channel2, Channel3, Index).

The obtained results are matched to the corresponding bacterial species using the provided codebook and necessary bit corrections. The StrainIndentifySim function is used to decode the results: [StrainLikehood, Decode] = StrainIndentifySim(Codebook, ResultCode, CorrBit). Here, StrainLikelihood represents the similarity between the detected code of each cell and the barcode of each species, while Decode contains the identified species ID and the correction bits for each cell. A bacterial cell is defined as “lost” if it is not detected in any fluorescence channel (i.e., assigned a code of 0) for more than three consecutive imaging rounds.

Clustering analysis

The clustering behavior of bacterial cells is examined using the linear dipole algorithm.31 The pair cross-correlation function between two distinct bacterial populations is formulated as:(Equation 1)g(r)=Pr2DiDj

where Pr is the probability of finding a dipole of length r with ends falling into the two populations. Di and Di indicated the density of population i and j.

To analyze bacterial cells belonging to the same population, the correlation function is expressed as:(Equation 2)g(r)=PrD2

where D is the density of the specified target population. When bacterial cells exhibit random distribution at distance r, g(r) equals 1; conversely, when clustering occurs at distance r, g(r) exceeds 1.

For each image, a root mask is manually delineated to define the region of interest based on phase-contrast imaging. Linear dipoles are evaluated exclusively when both endpoints are positioned within the masked regions, thereby excluding bacterial cells not located on the root surface from the analysis. Cell density is determined by calculating the ratio of the area occupied by bacterial cells to the total root area.

Spatial association analysis

The spatial association between two microbial taxa was quantified by comparing their observed co-occurrence frequencies against a stochastic null model.14 First, the spatial coordinates of every single cell were identified based on the segmentation results. Then, for each field of view, we calculated the observed number of neighbor pairs between any two taxa within a 10 μm radius. To determine if these associations were statistically significant (non-random), we generated a Null Model by performing 100 simulations. In each simulation, the spatial coordinates of all cells were kept fixed, but their species identities were randomly shuffled (maintaining the total abundance of each species). The Fold Change was then calculated as the logarithm of the ratio: (Observed Frequency/Mean Simulated Random Frequency). A positive fold change indicates that two taxa co-occur more frequently than expected by chance (spatial attraction or shared niche), while a negative fold change indicates spatial segregation. Cases exhibiting insufficient co-occurrence events were excluded from the analysis. Statistical significance was evaluated using independent t-tests, with significance thresholds adjusted via Bonferroni correction based on the total number of unique taxon-taxon associations tested.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Image processing and statistical analyses are performed using MATLAB (Version R2022a) and custom scripts. The R package DECIPHER (Version 2.24.0, running in R version 4.3.1) is used for probe design and specificity analysis. Statistical details of experiments including the exact value of n (representing the number of independent biological replicates, e.g., roots, or the total number of analyzed bacterial cells), definition of center, and dispersion and precision measures (e.g., mean ± SEM) are reported in the Figures and Figure Legends. p value < 0.05 is considered as statistically significant. No statistical methods are used to determine whether the data meet assumptions of the statistical approach. The distribution of fluorescence intensity data is assumed to be normal for statistical comparisons but is not formally tested. Regarding specific statistical tests, a statistical t test (p < 0.05) is performed in MATLAB to compare the fluorescence intensity of each cell against its local background to determine the presence of signal for cell identification. Furthermore, the statistical significance of differential spatial associations is evaluated using independent, two-tailed Student’s t test, with p-values adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple hypothesis testing. No statistical methods are used to predetermine sample size. No data are excluded from the analyses. No blinding is performed during data acquisition or analysis.
