Factors Influencing Quality Improvement Success in Primary Care: A Comparative Case Study of High‐ and Low‐Performing Sites in Patient Hypertension Outcomes
Authors: Jacqueline M Knapke, Manpreet Kaur, Sonal J Patil, Shari D Bolen, Kurt C Stange, Randy Wexler, Aleece Caron, Jackson T Wright, Jordan Fiegl, Hannah E Hill, Doug Einstadter, Thomas E Love, Christopher Taylor, Stephanie Kanuch, Jaden Koren, Deborah Cohen, Saundra L Regan, Adam Perzynski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApproximately 50% of adults with hypertension have their blood pressure under control. Although the success of quality improvement (QI) initiatives aiming to improve blood pressure control varies across clinics, little is known about factors related to this variability. We conducted a mixed‐methods comparative case study to describe the factors that affected clinics' QI success within a statewide cardiovascular health collaborative.MethodsForty‐eight primary care clinics (caring for 357 158 patients) implemented QI for heart health. Using blood pressure control improvement rates from pre‐ to postimplementation, practice type, and location, we selected 8 sites for our case comparison. We then conducted site observations and interviews with team members at the high‐performing sites (hypertension improved 11.7%–15.2% among 1364 patients) and low‐performing sites (hypertension worsened −1.8% to −11.2% among 3614 patients).ResultsKey facilitators shared by all sites included QI coaching, data monitoring, regular team meetings, use of program resources, and commitment to QI. Key barriers shared by both high‐ and low‐performing sites included staff challenges, internal barriers, and competing priorities. Notably, although thematic similarities were found across all sites, differences in performance emerged in how the sites responded: either proactively or reactively.ConclusionsThis study examined differences in implementation between high‐ and low‐performing practices that participated in a statewide QI initiative. Results highlight the importance of proactive approaches to addressing barriers to achieve improvements in blood pressure control. Findings can inform future QI work in primary care, underscoring the need for tailored strategies to promote effective implementation and better outcomes.Keywords: blood pressure, hypertension, mixed methods, primary care, quality improvementSubject Categories: Hypertension, Quality and Outcomes

As of 2023, the World Health Organization estimated that approximately 33% of adults between the ages of 30 and 79 years are affected by hypertension, defined as having systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic BP ≥90 mm Hg or taking medication for hypertension. 1 Hypertension is a key driver of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality globally, and disproportionately affects minority and disadvantaged populations. 2 Hypertension prevalence in Ohio has been relatively unchanged for many years, remaining between 30% and 35% from 2011 to 2018, according to resident‐reported survey data. 3 The persistently high prevalence and elevated rates of uncontrolled hypertension in Ohio have led to the development of several quality improvement (QI) initiatives.

Payers, health care system leaders, and policymakers are faced with an ongoing dilemma about the value of QI initiatives and other care‐delivery optimization efforts. Many QI studies have shown improvements in BP control after implementing evidence‐based hypertension practices, such as team‐based care, provider audit and feedback, accurate BP measurement, timely medication intensification, shared decision‐making, and patient education and participation. 4 , 5 Reviews have found that QI strategies reduce systolic BP from −2.2 to −6.6 mm Hg, but few studies have identified the specific practice‐level factors that may contribute to successful outcomes. 6 , 7 Parchman et al found that despite implementing the same QI strategies aimed at improving cardiovascular health, practices had varying degrees of success and had different levels of QI capacity. 8 A recent study in Ohio used the Plan‐Do‐Study‐Act (PDSA) cycle for QI improvement and implemented accurate BP measurement, effective treatment, and timely follow‐up guidelines in addition to QI coaching in 2 practice sites. 9 Although both sites' combined data showed an increase in repeat BP measurements, scheduling return appointments, and percentage of patients with controlled hypertension, the practices individually saw differing levels of success for each measure.

Although the benefits of QI have been studied extensively, the mechanisms through which practices succeed are not fully understood. 6 Therefore, we conducted a comparative case study of 4 high‐performing sites and 4 low‐performing sites participating in 1 statewide QI collaborative known as the HHOI (Heart Healthy Ohio Initiative). The HHOI supported 48 primary care sites across Ohio through the development of clinical QI teams and implementation of evidence‐based QI strategies for hypertension control. Sites involved in the study had varying levels of success in improving BP control in their practice. Improvement results of this QI project are reported elsewhere. 10 We used mixed methods to understand and describe the factors that contribute to the performance of primary care clinics undertaking QI projects aimed at improving patient cardiovascular outcomes, paving the way for more successful QI application in primary care clinics across Ohio.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This was a prospective, observational, mixed‐methods comparative case study. The HHOI is a statewide collaborative that aims to create a sustainable QI support infrastructure for Ohio primary care practices to lower BP and improve cardiovascular health. 11 Forty‐eight diverse primary care practices participated in HHOI, receiving monthly coaching from a QI expert with the goal of conducting PDSA cycles to improve patient outcomes between 2022 and 2023. Practices could choose to improve drivers of patients' cardiovascular outcomes by focusing on either BP control or smoking cessation.

Our approach to mixed methods had 5 components: (1) using quantitative electronic health record (EHR) data to select high‐ and low‐performing sites, (2) collecting additional data from multiple sources at selected practices, (3) collating and condensing collected data into case report forms for selected sites, (4) thematic analysis of interview data and site visit notes, and (5) integrative interpretive review of converging and diverging findings from the multimodal data sources. All components of this study were reviewed and approved by the MetroHealth, Ohio State University and University of Cincinnati institutional review boards and were approved as not human subjects research, and no informed consent was required.

Participants and Study Setting

The selection of sites for this case comparison study was conducted at the conclusion of the final QI measurement period in 2023, with 4 high‐performing and 4 low‐performing clinic sites selected. To select sites, we used improvement data comparing baseline BP control (defined as <140/90 mm Hg) to BP control in the final 3 months of HHOI participation, identifying the top third and bottom third of sites who showed the highest percentage of improvement and lowest percentage of improvement. We used 140/90 mm Hg as our cut point, because this is the national Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set quality measure that practices focused on the system level. Within the top and bottom third of the BP control distribution, we purposefully selected practices to ensure representation from a diverse array of sites based on location (urban/rural) and type of practice (private, academic, free clinic, or federally qualified health center). Practice Surveys

Pre‐, midpoint‐, and postsurveys were administered via Research Electronic Data Capture, capturing data such as the type of practice, zip code, number and type of clinicians, patient‐reported race and ethnicity demographics for the clinic's patient population, payer mix, presence of a residency program, presence of a QI lead, if they were located in a medically underserved area, the average number of patient visits per week, and any disruptive events that occurred at the site at baseline.

EHR Data Collection and Analysis

After finalizing data use agreements, sites used secure file transfer protocol to transfer monthly EHR data for all patients aged ≥18 years seen at the site during the previous month to a central data processing center. The data included the following variables: demographics (age, sex, race and ethnicity), insurance coverage (Medicaid, Medicare, commercial, or self‐pay), visit date and type of visit, scheduled appointments, vital signs measured at each visit (all BPs) with time measured, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes for all diagnoses on the problem list, current medication information (medication name, start and stop dates, strength, dose, route), and information on tobacco use and referral for cessation. A unique patient identifier was included to allow longitudinal tracking of patients. After receipt, the data were transformed into a standard format, and any physiologically implausible values were removed. Data for all sites were then combined for aggregate reporting of study outcomes and process measures on a web‐based dashboard. Information from the EHR data further includes the population size for analysis of improvement outcomes, the baseline hypertension control percentage, and the hypertension control percentage by the end of project implementation, as well as run charts displaying baseline to completion for hypertension control at 140/90 mm Hg, baseline to completion repeat BP rates, and baseline to completion follow‐up visit rates.

Coaching Logs

For each high‐ and low‐performing site, we reviewed coaching log forms where details from each meeting between the site and the practice QI coaches that were provided by the HHOI were recorded. The log forms included: topics of discussion, whether practice data were reviewed, any HHOI toolkit resources that were shared at the meeting, what the focus and status of ongoing PDSA cycles were, action items identified at the meeting, and an assessment of ongoing progress.

Clinic Observations and Key Informant Interviews

Clinic observation visits and interviews examined how well participants felt the QI plan was working, whether they felt they had sufficient time and resources to meet project expectations, and the extent to which their local teams were able to respond to emerging challenges. Thirty‐four patient interaction observations were recorded across the 8 case clinics, and 15 interviews were conducted with key informants at 7 of the 8 case clinics. Interviewees included physicians, medical assistants, nurses, nurse practitioners, a pharmacist, nurse coordinator, and organization president. Seven interviews were conducted during site observation visits, and 8 interviews were conducted virtually after the site visit occurred.

Observation sessions were planned and scheduled with permission from clinic sites. There were 1 to 2 observers from the research team who spent at least half a day at each site observing office flow in the hallways, waiting areas, staff workspaces, triage areas, examination rooms, and patient visits. A written protocol for data collection was followed, and observers had flexibility to take notes on everything they perceived as relevant to project processes and outcome perspectives. Observers completed 2 data collection instruments while on site: a general clinic observation notes form and a patient observation tool for each patient interaction that was observed (see Appendix B). Observers also added postobservation debriefing memos to the general clinic observation notes form.

Key informant interviews used a semistructured interview guide developed by the research team (see Appendix B), and key informants were identified during the observation visits in consultation with HHOI practice coaches and project leads at each site. All interviews, including those conducted on site, lasted between 10 and 90 minutes and were audio recorded, transcribed, and deidentified by an outside transcription service, and uploaded to Quirkos (www.quirkos.com), a web‐based collaborative data analysis program. 12

Statistical Analysis

Thematic analysis was used to examine the observational data and the key informant interviews, which were analyzed with a focus on eliciting themes grounded in the perspectives of participants and finding evidence for the trustworthiness and credibility of qualitative findings through recurrence and comparison/triangulation with quantitative findings. 13 , 14 , 15 Neither the analysis team nor the clinic participants were blinded to their case comparative classification; all had seen some version of HHOI collaborative data that showed clinic‐level performance and had access to a QI portal with clinic versus collaborative run charts (such as those seen in the case report forms in Appendix A). Two members of the research team coded 2 initial interview transcripts using line‐by‐line coding to develop a comprehensive codebook of themes, categories, and patterns that focused on barriers and facilitators to project success and the extent to which intervention development decisions reflected the participation of all stakeholders. This codebook was thoroughly discussed within the research team to ensure a similar interpretation of the data and then used by 4 members to code all of the key informant interview transcripts in Quirkos. The study findings, quantitative (clinical outcomes, survey measures) and qualitative (interview, ethnographic, thematic), were integrated using an iterative review of comparing thematic contents with findings from other data collection modalities summarized on care report forms to understand the synthesis, overlap, and departure of key themes and findings. Following the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research and using systematic procedures to ensure credibility and rigor in mixed methods research outlined by Tong and colleagues, 16 the research team had prolonged engagement with the data, had regular meetings to discuss the coding of transcripts, and engaged in peer debriefing, where 1 member of the research team who did not participate in the coding acted as a quality and reliability auditor to ensure rigorous thematic analysis. Additionally, we used member checking where results were shared with participants at an HHOI‐wide close‐out webinar. Potential biases were managed by peer debriefing and also by member checking. 17 , 18

A critical step in our mixed methods analysis was to summarize process and outcome data from surveys, the EHR, and coaching logs on the case reporting forms. The case reporting form summarizes site information including practice type, racial demographics of patients, insurance/payer breakdown of patients, a burnout measure reported by site staff from survey data, baseline BP control measures, and a BP improvement measure. The case reporting form also includes line graphs on repeat BP measures, follow‐up BP visits, and hypertension control for each site. These run charts are identical to those that were available to practice sites throughout the HHOI intervention period. Short summaries of qualitative thematic results from interviews and site visits were also transposed onto the case reporting forms (Appendix A). Thus, the case report forms jointly served as an intermediate hub for data analysis. Our study team developed this approach to address the challenge of depicting important practice details that are too complex to synthesize line‐by‐line and segregated by data collection modality. The side‐by‐side manner of reviewing data from multiple sources and types of data collection is best described as immersion crystallization, wherein a wide range of insights are arranged into an array and viewed together. 19

RESULTS

We identified 4 high‐performing sites (H1, H2, H3, H4) and 4 low‐performing sites (L5, L6, L7, L8) following the case comparison selection process described above. The high‐performing sites demonstrated improvement percentages between 11.7% and 15.2% in hypertension control from the start to the end of intervention, and the low‐performing sites demonstrated improvement percentages that worsened between −1.8% and −11.2% in hypertension control from start to finish. The high‐performing group included 3 urban practices and 1 rural practice, including 1 clinic associated with a hospital system, 1 free clinic, 1 federally qualified health center, and 1 clinic associated with an academic system. The low‐performer group included 3 urban practices and 1 rural practice, including 3 clinics associated with academic systems and 1 clinic associated with a hospital system. Using case comparison thematic analysis of qualitative data, we identified several themes to describe facilitators and barriers to conducting hypertension QI work in the study clinics. An initial impression of the data is that all 8 sites seem to have faced similar challenges and described similar facilitators. However, a close reading of themes reveals nuanced differences in the language key informants used to describe their approaches to undertaking QI work. Most barriers and facilitators identified were present at both high‐ and low‐performing case sites; themes that were shared by both types of performers often had important contextual differences that described a more general difference in approach to QI work; low‐performing case clinics demonstrated the reactive QI approach, whereas high‐performing case clinics exhibited the proactive QI approach. This key difference in approach will be described as each theme is presented below. Figure 2 provides a visual summary of themes by performance type.

Facilitators

We identified 5 themes to describe facilitators at high‐ and low‐performing case clinics. The first theme, QI coaching, describes the important role played by the practice coaches provided by HHOI in facilitating QI processes and understanding with participants. High performers were seemingly more proactive in that they were engaged in data‐driven improvements using coaches' insights, whereas low performers tended to be more reactive, struggling with PDSA complexity and often relying on their coaches for clarification of misperceptions about PDSA cycles. The second theme was data monitoring, which participants described as regularly accessing and reviewing QI data to better understand what changes were needed, what patients or interventions to target, and how to motivate team members to stay on track. In particular, high performers engaged in a detailed understanding of their data to develop targeted efforts for follow‐up appointments and medication changes. Relevant to the importance of proactive data monitoring, the third theme was regular team meetings, where participants described the usefulness of getting the team together frequently not just to review data, but also to discuss what was working and to keep the QI project moving forward.

Again, high‐performing sites used data monitoring and regular meetings in a proactive manner, meeting frequently with all key team members and making data‐informed decisions about how to improve PDSA cycles. Low‐performing sites tended to be more reactive in their data monitoring and regular meetings, describing periodic data checks and a focus on correcting the general direction of their data, but not using their regular meetings and data monitoring to develop any targeted efforts. The fourth facilitator theme was the use of QI and hypertension management resources provided by the HHOI. Participants cited several specific HHOI resources that they found beneficial to their QI work, including lessons from other participating sites, instructional videos, and provider and patient education materials. High‐performing sites demonstrated more proactivity in using HHOI resources, valuing clear and simple visual aids for staff, and using resources to improve patient‐centered interactions. Low‐performing sites were more reactive, using resources to address knowledge gaps in QI terms and tools to improve staff understanding of QI concepts.

The fifth theme shared by both low‐ and high‐performers was a commitment to QI. Fundamental differences in approach were present in this theme, with high performers devoting adequate time to gain team buy‐in and reporting enthusiastic adoption of new processes designed to achieve QI goals and low performers needing more time to make the case for QI and the HHOI project, reporting limited adoption of QI methods as part of the case site culture. High‐performing case sites described effective staff education as a critical facilitator of their success, and low‐performing case sites described uneven staff education as a critical barrier to their success. This thematic difference is further evidence of a proactive approach supporting better QI outcomes when compared with a reactive approach. Barriers

Both high‐ and low‐performing sites also identified several barriers to their ability to implement QI initiatives, such as staffing challenges, internal barriers, competing priorities, and a lack of QI project engagement. Participant descriptions of these barriers did not reflect either a proactive or reactive approach, but rather indicate common hurdles in a primary care setting. Staffing challenges at comparative case sites were nearly universal:

H1: “I tried rolling [follow‐up appointments] to nurses, but we couldn't get that up and running for all kinds of turnover issues. We definitely had our fair share of problems organizationally, with turnover.”

L6: “Staffing is the biggest thing, that was the biggest barrier for us and any projects that we tried to do in this clinic. We just get hit by like a staff member leaving or now our practice managers leaving end of this month. So, so it's kind of, very tough to really have a sustainable change, you know, when you don't have the support.”

Internal barriers that were site‐specific came up in interviews with participants at both high‐ and low‐performing sites, such as implementing proper documentation processes or hanging signs or informational flyers in offices and patient rooms. One participant described how they see patients in a clinical space that is not their own, so they are limited in their ability to make changes:

H3: “… our clinic is not in our own space. So to make changes in a space that's not yours, it takes a little bit more salt, and still keeping the patient first is important.”

Another participant at a high‐performing site described the challenge of properly documenting BP rechecks so they would show up in their data reports, and a participant at a low‐performing site (L8) said they were not allowed to hang signs on the walls of their clinic or use external patient education resources due to hospital system policies:

L8: “Now [participant's hospital system] doesn't like us hanging signs, but it seems like if you… I don't know. I always thought when this first got started, maybe a handout that's given to the patient when they walk in about what's an appropriate BP, ‘Please follow these instructions.’”

Competing priorities were also a challenge across multiple sites, both high and low performing. For 1 high‐performing site and 1 low‐performing site, other QI initiatives were occurring around the same time as HHOI. Both described the challenge of managing multiple projects:

H3: “Our biggest barrier to a lot of QI projects is getting the appropriate staff to work on them … this other QI project that we're doing with high BP is extremely complicated and demands a lot of time.”

A participant at L6 described competing demands more generally, saying:

“… when we were part of the quality project, we did see a good outcome where our BP numbers were improved. But unfortunately, we're not tracking it anymore. Just, there are a lot of things going on in this clinic right now.”

One additional barrier was identified by participants at 2 low‐performing sites, and that was a lack of QI project awareness and engagement. Participants described this barrier as not knowing about QI efforts in general, not knowing the HHOI project in particular was occurring, not knowing what the project entailed, and not knowing who was on the project team:

L5: “don't, you know, as I said, I don't get a good sense of exactly what's going on. I don't see anything really communicated. I don't see anything posted there in terms of how we're doing. Or in fact, if I weren't part of this project, I wouldn't even know that there was any sort of quality improvement going on. It's all sort of hidden. And I, so I think on the clinic level, sort of more kind of advertising or at least sort of communicating what's going on would be helpful. And, for myself, I mean, I haven't really gone out of my way to participate in things other than just, you know, sort of being there in clinic. So, you know, being more active would probably be a good thing.”

L7: “I actually do remember doing some stuff with it, but I honestly don't know what all it entailed …. It would've been nice to been informed … of something you haven't heard of before.”

Prior similar work to improve chronic care management in our region found that increasing timely follow‐up and accurate BP measurements were primary drivers of improved outcomes, with medication intensification happening at the timely follow‐up visits. 9 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Although timely follow‐up was a primary driver of BP outcomes in prior eras of care delivery, in this comparative case study, EHR‐derived measures of timely follow‐up did not differentiate between practices that were low and high performing (see Table 4). Qualitative insights from key informant interviews and site observations suggest that staffing and clinic access constraints were too severe across most practices to allow for improvements in timely follow‐up for patients with hypertension, even in those practices that improved outcomes. Thus, although improving timely follow‐up is undoubtedly beneficial for patients over time and across practices, contextual differences in EHR‐based measurement (eg, pharmacy visits and home visits were not captured by the EHR‐derived measure) and practice circumstances likely distorted the between‐practice comparisons on the process measure. Case study practices with alternative practice payment and employment structures (eg, volunteer clinics) in some cases encouraged adaptability and a high level of participation in improvement activities.

The mixed methods comparison in Table 4 further indicates that higher‐performing practices had similar but slightly higher burnout scores and disruptive events. Higher‐performing practices had lower patient volumes and more timely follow‐up with patients at baseline and follow‐up; however, 3 of the 4 high‐performing practices experienced a reduction in care process quality measures (lower rates of timely follow‐up) by the end of their participation in the HHOI. Perhaps most notable is that only 1 of the high‐performing practices had a higher‐than‐average score on the Change Process Capacity Questionnaire. Evidence from ethnographic notes taken during the site visits provides perhaps the most provocative explanatory insights. We found a permeating level of proactivity at high‐performing practices. For example, front desk, medical assistant, pharmacy, and social work staff at practice H1 informed the research team of the motto that their new chief executive officer had offered, “We Can, We Will,” and then gave examples ranging from improvising extra appointment times to mini‐PDSA cycles to simply dropping off home BP cuffs and changed medication packs at patients' homes. DISCUSSION

We examined differences in implementation between high‐ and low‐performing primary care practices that participated in a statewide QI initiative focused primarily on improving BP control. Prior evidence from evaluations of other multisite collaboratives has highlighted the importance of practice leadership characteristics, involvement of multidisciplinary teams, availability of continuous feedback, and planning for sustainability in achieving clinical care improvements. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 The themes identified in this case comparison study highlight the importance of a supportive QI infrastructure that facilitates regular meetings, standardized training, access to resources, and consistent review of quality data, ideally with a QI coach. Additionally, results point to the necessity of a proactive and engaged clinical team that is motivated to participate in QI work.

Several contextual changes in the health care‐delivery system since 2020 have required primary care clinics to make changes and adapt. These include an aging workforce and labor shortages, 29 an aging patient population with more comorbid health conditions and more medications, 30 a social context of inflationary pressures on costs, 31 and an overall failure of care systems to respond to social drivers of health. 32 Amid these challenges, it might be surprising that practice teams could make any improvements in cardiovascular health outcomes. However, our findings highlight the adaptability and resourcefulness of these care teams. In summary, we found that barriers to care, such as labor shortages and the management of increasingly complex patients, were a challenge for all participating sites, irrespective of their level of hypertension care improvement. Where these teams differed was in their response to such challenges.

The findings from our mixed‐methods comparative case study substantially extend existing implementation science paradigms for the secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease in several important ways. First, we bring together evidence from multiple sources including EHR data, care team surveys, practice observation, and qualitative interviews. Second, we describe a broad range of barriers and facilitators across high‐ and low‐performing practices that all received the same care improvement intervention. A major strength of this study is the thorough exploration of how and why some practices improve whereas others do not, particularly in the post‐COVID context, where primary care practices have been struggling with even the most fundamental resources necessary for basic clinical work.

Nonetheless, our work has clear limitations. Although we had broad participation in the collaborative from many types of practices with varied payment structures and patient populations, our work was conducted only in Ohio, and practices were not selected randomly, so results might not be fully transferable to other settings. Clinics in the study chose to participate in the HHOI program, which could introduce a form of collider bias to the study design. We also analyzed BP changes only among clinic visits, so our analysis may not capture other aspects of care (eg, patients not seen in the clinic at all). Although our nested, sequential mixed‐methods design permits deep, robust, and rigorous inference in comparing low‐ and high‐performing practices, owing to the time and resource intensive requirements of qualitative data collection and analysis, we collected qualitative data at only 17% of the practices. It is possible that further insights would be uncovered upon inspection of other clinic sites. Primary care systems have nuanced sources of complexity that prevent even some straightforward data analytic inferences. For example, some of the participating practices joined coaching sessions together with peers from other clinics within the same health system. These joint sessions made it difficult for practice coaches to attribute sentiment, progress, idea sharing, and accomplishments during multisite sessions, and thus these data were not fully available for comparison in our analysis.

A systematic review by Knudsen et al found that although many QI projects in clinical medicine settings have reported positive outcomes, only a small fraction of these adhered closely to recommended core QI components (eg, use of an iterative cyclic method, use of continuous data collection, small‐scale testing, an explicit theoretical rationale). 33 Our work extends this prior work by emphasizing the importance of clinic‐level proactivity and reactivity which, respectively, furthered or impeded implementation of BP improvement strategies.

Although thematic barriers and facilitators were similar across all clinics in this case comparison, the high performers were able to leverage their facilitators differently than the low performers and achieve better outcomes for their patients. We found that a proactive stance toward improvement was a defining feature of practices that demonstrated improvement, even amid difficult local clinic circumstances. To better support practices with lower improvements, clinic and QI leaders may want to consider cultivating a more proactive environment, as exemplified by the rapid, sustained training and accountability loops (see Figure 3). Scalability within rural and urban settings presents a persistent challenge for cardiovascular health implementation efforts. Innes has offered accountability frameworks as potential pathways to addressing care‐delivery crises and failures and described 3 core program accountabilities: (1) timely patient assessment and disposition planning; (2) budget, space, and nursing care for patients; and (3) contingency plans. 34 Evidence from our comparative case study suggests that low‐performing care teams were reactive to care‐delivery challenges. Including contingency plans within cardiovascular health improvement efforts could serve as a form of proactive adaptation for managing organizational challenges and efficiently achieving better outcomes.

This study examined differences in implementation between high‐ and low‐performing practices of BP control changes that participated in a statewide QI initiative. Results highlight the importance of proactive approaches to addressing barriers to achieve improvements in BP control. Findings can inform future QI work in primary care, underscoring the need for tailored strategies that consider the unique challenges and resources of different clinical settings to promote more effective implementation and better outcomes for all.
