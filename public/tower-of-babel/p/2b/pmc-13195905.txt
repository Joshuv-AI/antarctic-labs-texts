A novel, wearable, in-ear EEG technology to assess sleep and daytime sleepiness
Authors: Jonathan Berent, Prabhjyot Saini, Andrew X. Stewart, Devansh Bheda, Konstantin Borodin, Akshay Paul, Brian Tracey, Caroline Dong, Dmitri Volfson, Derek L. Buhl, David B. Rye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In-ear electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional in-laboratory sleep studies, offering greater comfort and practicality. Here we present a novel in-ear EEG system, comparing in-ear recordings against scalp EEG channels acquired concurrently as part of polysomnography (PSG). The study enrolled 16 healthy control participants in a single-visit overnight-plus-daytime design, and 8 participants with central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH) in a randomized crossover daytime design (medication vs. medication-holiday). For overnight sleep recordings, ear-EEG and scalp EEG sleep staging showed substantial agreement (Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.77$$\end{document}). For daytime MWT trials, agreement was moderate (Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.50$$\end{document}), reflecting the predominance of wake epochs in this paradigm. For the primary Maintenance of Wakefulness Test (MWT) endpoint of sleep onset latency (SOL), at the per-subject level (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 24$$\end{document})—averaging across trials as in standard clinical practice—agreement was good (ICC = 0.71, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.75$$\end{document}, MAD = 5.1 min). Among the 37 of 126 trials where both devices detected sleep (approximately 30% of trials), agreement was strong (ICC = 0.82, MAD = 1.9 min), with excellent agreement in healthy controls (ICC = 0.95, MAD = 1.2 min). Overall trial-level agreement across all 126 trials was moderate (ICC = 0.55), reflecting 22 discordant trials in which scalp EEG detected sleep but in-ear EEG did not—predominantly brief, subtle N1 transitions, concentrated in a subset of CDH participants. For overnight sleep architecture (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 16$$\end{document} healthy controls), total sleep time (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.94$$\end{document}, ICC = 0.85), sleep efficiency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.94$$\end{document}), and wake after sleep onset (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.93$$\end{document}) showed strong agreement, with small systematic biases consistent with reduced N1 detection sensitivity. These findings support the feasibility of in-ear EEG for sleep staging and daytime sleepiness assessment in laboratory settings, and motivate larger confirmatory studies—including home-based longitudinal monitoring—to establish clinical utility, particularly in populations with altered sleep architecture.

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process essential for the survival and wellbeing of mammalians including humans (Tobler 2011) that is tightly controlled and regulated (Achermann and Borbély 2017). Sleep plays multiple critical roles in multi-organ physiologies and brain-mediated behaviors (Leong and Chee 2023) including learning (Stickgold et al. 2001), development (Mander et al. 2017; Paruthi et al. 2016), memory consolidation (Stickgold et al. 2001; Diekelmann and Born 2010), cardiovascular health, emotional valence (Goldstein and Walker 2014), metabolic homeostasis (Xie et al. 2013), integrity of the immune response (Besedovsky et al. 2019), diurnal and circadian rhythms (Saper et al. 2005), intracellular trafficking (Cirelli et al. 2004), and generally our broader physical and psychological health and homeostasis (Buysse 2014).

Understanding sleep requires the ability to measure it accurately, efficiently, and longitudinally. The clinical reference standard is polysomnography (PSG), conducted in a specialized facility. PSG relies upon a throng of strategically placed surface electrodes to detect electroencephalographic (EEG) activity, eye movements (EOG), muscle activity (EMG), heart rhythms (ECG), and respiration during sleep (Vaughn and Giallanza 2008). PSG provides high-fidelity, multi-modal physiological data and is indispensable for the diagnosis of many sleep disorders, but it is resource-intensive (Malhotra et al. 2013; Flores et al. 2016), limited to in-laboratory settings, and may itself perturb habitual sleep—most notably via the well-documented first-night effect, in which the unfamiliar laboratory environment disrupts a subject’s habitual sleep architecture sufficiently that additional study nights are often required to obtain clinically interpretable data (Byun et al. 2019; Newell et al. 2012); indeed, both the U.S. FDA and the European EMA recommend two consecutive nights of PSG before and after each measurement timepoint in therapeutic drug-development trials specifically to overcome this artifact (Léger et al. 2025). Single-night assessment can also be subject to diagnostic misclassification where symptoms vary across nights (Punjabi et al. 2020).

Out-of-laboratory EEG technologies have expanded the accessibility of brain-based sleep measurement, enabling longitudinal and at-home paradigms that complement laboratory PSG rather than replace it. Ear-centered EEG, first introduced by Looney et al. (2011) and subsequently characterized as a recording method in healthy participants (Kidmose et al. 2013; Looney et al. 2012; Mikkelsen et al. 2015; Kappel et al. 2019; Goverdovsky et al. 2016), has been evaluated for sleep staging in two prior validation studies. Mikkelsen et al. (2019) reported an overall sleep-staging accuracy of 83.5% (F1 = 0.84) for automatic whole-night classification using dry-contact single-ear electrodes in 20 participants (80 nights), approaching the 86.4% accuracy achieved by expert human scorers on concurrent scalp EEG. Sterr et al. (2018) reported 58.5% epoch-by-epoch concordance for manual scoring of behind-the-ear cEEGrid electrodes (Bleichner 2017) in a 15-participant proof-of-concept study. For daytime vigilance, Alqurashi et al. (2018) is the only prior ear-EEG validation of sleep-onset latency, reporting latency differences of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<3$$\end{document} min in 84–94% of naps against the Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT) in healthy adults—a protocol used for diagnostic discrimination among the central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH) rather than for treatment-response assessment. To our knowledge, no prior work has evaluated ear-EEG against the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test (MWT)—the protocol favored for objective assessment of medication efficacy in hypersomnolence—nor in a clinical hypersomnolence cohort. Here we present a novel in-ear EEG system designed to (i) achieve high-quality, reliable biosignal acquisition for sleep monitoring; (ii) maximize comfort and practicality for overnight and daytime testing; and (iii) assess performance in both healthy controls and participants with central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH). The hardware platform of the NextSense in-ear EEG system has been demonstrated previously in epilepsy and seizure-prediction work (Joyner et al. 2024); here we evaluate the same system for sleep state classification and for MWT-based assessment of daytime sleepiness.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study evaluated the feasibility of an in-ear EEG system (NextSense Inc., Mountain View, CA) for assessing sleep architecture and daytime sleepiness relative to conventional in-laboratory polysomnography (PSG) and American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) scoring standards. The study was conducted under two IRB-approved, pre-specified feasibility protocols at Emory University covering the Healthy Control arm (16 participants; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05114616) and the Central Disorders of Hypersomnolence (CDH) arm (8 patients; NCT05066009) (Table 1). The feasibility-study designation and the cohort sizes were pre-specified in the protocols. Ethics, oversight, and consent

All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted under Emory University Institutional Review Board approval (Emory IRB version 00-00−2019) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (healthy controls: NCT05114616; CDH cohort: NCT05066009). Study procedures were monitored by the principal investigator and trained sleep laboratory personnel. Adverse events were not anticipated; any events were documented and reported per institutional requirements.

Participants

Healthy controls

Sixteen healthy adult volunteers (ages 18–60 years) residing within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}20 miles of the Emory Sleep Center were recruited via flyers and local advertisements. Inclusion criteria included: normal BMI (18.5–28.0 kg/m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}), regular sleep-wake habits (7–8.5 hours nightly sleep with consistent bed/wake times within 1–2 hours, and typical bedtime between 9:30–11:30 p.m.), and no routine use of sleep aids or wake-promoting medications.

Exclusion criteria included: history of diagnosed or suspected sleep disorder (e.g., sleep-related breathing disorders, circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, central disorders of hypersomnolence, parasomnias, sleep-related movement disorders), regular caffeine consumption >200 mg/day, major psychiatric disorder or suicidal ideation, substance dependence within the past 6 months (including nicotine, marijuana, alcohol, or other substances likely to affect sleep), attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, recent shift work (past 6 months), or inability to safely tolerate earbuds due to recent injury, skin breakdown, or infection.

Central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH) cohort

Participants with CDH (narcolepsy type 1 [NT1], narcolepsy type 2 [NT2], or idiopathic hypersomnia [IH]) were recruited from the Emory Sleep Center clinical database of patients who had provided consent to be contacted for research. Inclusion criteria included: age 18–60 years; prior clinical diagnosis of NT1, NT2, or IH; and use of daily wake-promoting medication(s) for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}3 months with documented symptomatic benefit. Exclusion criteria included: suspected or diagnosed comorbid sleep-related breathing disorder, parasomnia, or other sleep-related movement disorder; unstable psychiatric disorder or active substance abuse; recent malignancy (active or remission \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<2$$\end{document} years) or active infection at screening; inability to safely tolerate earbuds; uncorrected near visual acuity worse than 20/50; or other investigator-determined conditions likely to affect safety or outcomes.

Recruitment and screening

Healthy controls were screened by research staff for eligibility using medical history, medication review, and protocol inclusion/exclusion criteria. Participants meeting eligibility criteria completed an enrollment visit and were familiarized with the sleep laboratory environment. Prior to the visit, anatomical scanning was performed at least 3 weeks prior to the first in-clinic visit. Habitual sleep times and circadian rhythm were assessed at study enrollment via in-person interview and the Owl-Lark questionnaire; healthy controls and CDH participants were of neither extreme chronotype, with similar total scores (HC: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$55.81 \pm 2.81$$\end{document}, CDH: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$51.75 \pm 3.10$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.602$$\end{document}).

Devices and instrumentation

Gold-standard polysomnography

Conventional PSG was performed using a AASM-compliant laboratory system (Philips ALICE 6 PSG) with EEG, EOG, chin EMG, leg EMG, ECG, and standard respiratory channels as clinically indicated. Sleep staging was performed on 30-second epochs per AASM scoring rules.

NextSense in-ear EEG system

The NextSense in-ear EEG system consisted of custom-fit, EEG-enabled earbuds (EEGBuds) with dry-contact electrodes positioned within the external auditory canals. Custom earmolds were produced from high-resolution ear geometry scans using a ring/laser scanning approach (reported accuracy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}0.1 mm) and fabricated using a 3D-printing workflow to ensure comfort and stable electrode-skin contact. The in-ear EEG system captured EEG and motion signals; in this validation study, in-ear EEG data were recorded concurrently with PSG during overnight sleep and daytime MWT sessions.

Study procedures

Healthy controls underwent sleep-restricted overnight PSG followed by a 4-session MWT the following day. Participants with CDH completed two MWT days in a randomized crossover design (medication vs. medication-holiday) (Fig. 1).

Overnight sleep recording (Healthy controls)

Sixteen healthy subjects underwent standard overnight PSG with a full 10–20 montage setup, where the primary exception was wake-extension. Lights-out was at 02:00 and lights-on was at 06:00, restricting sleep to a 4-hour window. The NextSense earbuds were placed in the subjects’ ears shortly before or during the PSG setup, which occurred between 19:00 and 22:30. The 10–20 PSG montage was placed on the participant by a registered sleep technologist (RPSGT-certified). Prior to lights-out at 02:00, subjects were in the bed or in the recliner used for the MWT and were actively monitored using scalp-EEG and in-room audio and visual equipment to ensure they remained awake during the wake-extension portion. Subjects were vocally alerted over microphone if they appeared drowsy or had fallen asleep. Actual time-in-bed across the 16 participants was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$239.4 \pm 1.8$$\end{document} min (median 240.0 min; range 232.9–240.5 min), confirming the intended fixed 4-hour TIB across all subjects.

Daytime maintenance of wakefulness test (Healthy controls and CDH)

A standard 4-session MWT, commencing at 08:00, followed the overnight sleep period. The MWT consisted of four scheduled nap trials spaced approximately two hours apart (08:00, 10:00, 12:00, and 14:00). Each trial lasted up to 40 minutes. Participants were seated in a comfortable recliner chair in a dark room with a night-light positioned behind the chair below eye level, and were instructed to remain awake. Standard EEG, EOG, and EMG leads captured data, and the environment was kept dark, quiet, and free of distractions or substances that might alter sleep. Audiovisual monitoring occurred throughout, and a light breakfast and lunch were recommended at specific intervals (Krahn et al. 2021). Standard PSG and in-ear EEG recordings were acquired concurrently during each MWT.

Sleep onset latency (SOL) was defined from “lights off” to sleep onset based on AASM criteria applied to 30-second epochs. Consistent with standard laboratory practice, sleep onset during MWT was scored as either: (i) the first epoch of any sleep stage other than N1 (N2, N3, or REM), or (ii) the first of three consecutive epochs of stage N1. SOL was computed from a 40-minute window beginning at the documented lights-off time, excluding pre-trial calibration epochs. Trials in which neither device detected sleep were assigned a SOL equal to the full trimmed trial duration (40 min), per the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommended protocols for the MWT (Krahn et al. 2021), which state that “if no sleep occurs on a trial, then 40 minutes is used as the sleep latency and in the calculation of the mean sleep latency.” A sensitivity analysis treating non-detection trials as right-censored rather than exact is reported in Detection performance and conditional agreement analyses section. Both in-ear EEG and scalp EEG SOL were derived from the consensus scorer (epoch-by-epoch majority vote of three technologists, with a pre-specified tiebreaker; see Consensus scoring section) to ensure methodological consistency.

Trials were terminated in real time by the monitoring technologist after scalp EEG showed three consecutive epochs of N1 sleep or one epoch of any other sleep stage, per standard MWT monitoring procedures. Because both devices recorded concurrently, the in-ear EEG annotation files covered the same duration as the scalp files. The primary SOL analysis includes all 126 MWT trials. In trials where only the scalp EEG detected sleep (and in-ear EEG did not), the in-ear EEG SOL equals the full trial duration; these trials are analyzed separately to characterize the detection discrepancy (Maintenance of wakefulness test: sleep onset latency section).

Patient (CDH) Crossover procedures and randomization

CDH participants completed two separate MWT test days (four-trial MWT each day) under a single-blind, 2-period randomized crossover design: (i) medication day (usual prescribed wake-promoting medication[s]) and (ii) medication-holiday day (withholding usual wake-promoting medication[s]). The order of conditions was randomized 1:1 (Group A vs. Group B). Randomization was conducted using the ’Sealed Envelope’ web service, with blocked random permuted blocks to balance assignment. Investigators were blinded to intervention where possible; medication administration on study days was recorded by a study coordinator and kept blinded from scorers and analysts as applicable. Among the 8 CDH participants, prescribed wake-promoting regimens at enrollment were as follows. Four participants were on monotherapy to promote wakefulness: modafinil (1 IH female, 1 NT2 female), solriamfetol (1 NT2 female), and mixed amphetamine salts (1 NT1 male). Three participants were prescribed an oxybate salt at bedtime (two of these repeating the same dose 3–3.5 hours later) together with one additional daytime wake-promoting agent: bupropion (IH female), pitolisant (NT1 female), or mixed amphetamine salts (NT2 male). The eighth participant (NT2 female) was chronically taking maximal recommended daily doses of modafinil and mixed amphetamine salts, with as-needed dextroamphetamine. Other neuroactive medications, when prescribed, were continued at habitual dosages throughout both study days: venlafaxine (cataplexy suppression in the NT1 male) and venlafaxine plus escitalopram (for anxiety in 1 NT2 male and 1 NT2 female). For the medication-holiday arm, daytime wake-promoting agents were discontinued at least 5 days prior to study; participants prescribed oxybate took only one-half of their habitual bedtime dose and forwent the second nightly dose. One participant prescribed three individual wake-promoting agents was only able to safely participate free of medication for 3 days prior to study, and was provided hotel accommodation for that interval.

Data acquisition, synchronization, and processing

Ear-EEG and PSG streams were time-aligned for analysis. Where applicable, synchronization between systems used a laboratory-compatible marker/sync channel. Depending on the output of an electrically-isolated RNG, the NextSense in-ear EEG device randomly writes a dedicated ’Optosync’ channel to high/100 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}V, and simultaneously writes the next 5 values as high via a EEG-scale output channel, before returning to 0 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}V for a RNG-determined random wait. This output can be simultaneously streamed and recorded by a clinical EEG or PSG system. With the signals from this Optosync signal recorded via both modalities and all data combined in one biosignal EDF file, any deviation in the timing of these two Optosync channels can be readily identified and corrected. Raw data were exported as EDF-format files for preprocessing and analysis, mostly using in-house Python tools (https://github.com/nextsense-io).

In-ear EEG preprocessing was performed using an in-house Python pipeline built on top of the brainflow library. Raw in-ear signals were digitally bandpass-filtered between 0.3 Hz and 40 Hz using a 4th-order Butterworth filter applied in a zero-phase (bidirectional) configuration, with 30 s front and 15 s back reflection padding to suppress filter transients at segment boundaries. Three bandstop (notch) filters were subsequently applied to remove narrow-band interference: a 60 Hz bandstop to remove AC power-line artifact, and two additional 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth bandstops spanning 19 Hz to 21 Hz and 39 Hz to 41 Hz, targeting harmonic artifacts associated with the acquisition electronics. Where required for direct per-epoch comparison against the PSG, in-ear signals were resampled from the native device rate (200 Hz) to a common target rate (100 Hz for automated pipelines; 200 Hz for manual scoring) using an 8th-order Butterworth low-pass filter at the target Nyquist frequency followed by mean-aggregation-based decimation. The NextSense device itself provides hardware anti-aliasing upstream of the analog-to-digital converter as part of its acquisition chain. PSG channels used standard channel-specific filtering for manual scoring (EEG 0.3 Hz to 35 Hz, EMG 10 Hz to 95 Hz, EOG high-pass 0.3 Hz) consistent with the AASM scoring manual.

Spectral power features were computed across canonical frequency bands (delta, 0.5 Hz to 4 Hz; theta, 4 Hz to 8 Hz; alpha, 8 Hz to 13 Hz; beta, 13 Hz to 30 Hz) using Welch’s method with a Hann window. For overnight sleep and MWT analyses, the Welch window length was 30 s (matching the AASM scoring epoch) with 0% overlap; for short-protocol spectral analyses, a 2.5 s Hann window with 90% overlap was used. Power spectral densities were reported in decibels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10 \log _{10}$$\end{document}) over the 1 Hz to 35 Hz range with 0.5 Hz frequency resolution and a 0.5 Hz Gaussian frequency-domain smoothing kernel for display. For daytime-sleepiness analyses, the theta/(alpha+beta) ratio was quantified using a moving 30 s window. Micro-sleeps were defined as EEG slowing arising from wakefulness lasting 3–15 seconds and were scored/derived in blinded fashion.

Manual sleep scoring

Three independent registered polysomnographic technologists (RPSGT certified) manually scored PSG and in-ear EEG recordings according to AASM criteria. Scorers were blinded to participant identity and clinical condition, and (for the CDH crossover) to medication assignment. Prior to scoring in-ear EEG, technologists completed an in-ear EEG training and certification procedure using practice data and were required to show competence prior to scoring study data. The certification was repeated at the study midpoint as part of quality control.

For both MWT and overnight sleep sessions, recordings were staged in 30-second epochs as:

0 - Wake (W), 1 - N1, 2 - N2, 3 - N3, 5 - REM

A - Arousal (brief awakening)

- - Empty/unknown

Each of our three expert sleep techs scored annotations alone.

Consensus scoring

For both PSG and in-ear EEG, a consensus hypnogram was generated using epoch-by-epoch majority vote across the three technologists. If all three scorers assigned different stages for a given epoch (three-way tie), the stage from the scorer whose labels most frequently appeared in the majority consensus was used as the tiebreaker. The most consensus-aligned scorer was determined independently for each system and condition: LY for PSG MWT, TBR for in-ear EEG MWT, and AM for both PSG and in-ear EEG overnight sleep. Epochs marked as movement time or unscorable by any scorer were excluded from epoch-level agreement analyses.

Outcome measures

The primary efficacy endpoint for daytime testing was MWT SOL. Secondary endpoints included epoch-level agreement metrics (confusion matrices, F1 scores, Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}), inter-rater reliability (Fleiss’ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}), and derived sleep architecture variables including total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency (SE), wake after sleep onset (WASO), stage durations and latencies, and REM-related metrics. This manuscript reports the secondary endpoints of sleep staging agreement and overnight sleep architecture, as well as the primary MWT endpoint of SOL.

Design of the NextSense in-ear EEG system

The NextSense In-Ear EEG System device consists of a 3D-printed enclosure housing the electronics including an analog-to-digital converter (ADC), micro-SD card reader, lithium-ion battery, opto-sync mechanism for synchronizing with clinical systems, and additional microprocessors and components for communication and related functions (Fig. 2).

Two custom-molded earbuds are connected to the enclosure via a micro-HDMI cable. Each earbud is 3D printed with biocompatible resin (Carbon SIL30) from a high-resolution 3D scan of the subject’s ear, acquired with the United Sciences eFit Scanner (United Sciences, Atlanta, Georgia, USA) and modeled in the Secret Ear Designer software (Cyfex AG, Zürich, Switzerland). Earbuds are coated with a proprietary conductive polymer on regions corresponding to the canal and cymba of each subject. Silver rivets are threaded through the earbud to bridge the conductivity between the earbud and the electronics module (Waters et al. 2024). Proper insertion requires little-to-no preparation, other than avoiding an excess of wax. For EEG validation testing, the recording box was connected to the PSG headbox via an “Optosync” channel outputting a 20-sample, 250 Hz signal for post-processing alignment and validation.

Beyond the modality-agreement analysis, the CDH crossover design permits a within-subject comparison of off-medication vs. on-medication MWT SOL on the same in-ear EEG hardware (Fig. 9). Subject S206 was inadvertently on wake-promoting medication on both study days and is excluded from the paired off-vs-on comparison. Per-subject mean SOL

In standard clinical practice, the MWT result for a patient is the average SOL across four trials. At the per-subject level (n = 24 subjects), averaging across all available trials, agreement was good (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.75$$\end{document}, ICC(2,1) = 0.71, MAD = 5.1 min), indicating that the in-ear EEG system captures between-subject differences in daytime sleepiness despite trial-level discordances.

Discordant trials: scalp-only sleep detection

In 22 of 66 trials where at least one device detected sleep (7 healthy, 15 CDH), the consensus scorer identified sleep onset on scalp EEG but scored the entire in-ear EEG recording as wake. These discordant trials were concentrated in a subset of participants: the 7 healthy discordant trials were contributed by 6 of the 16 healthy controls, while the 15 CDH discordant trials were contributed by 5 of the 8 CDH participants (subjects 201, 204, 210, 211, and 212; contributing 1, 5, 3, 1, and 5 trials, respectively, of the 15). Among the individual NXT scorers (AM, LY, TBR), one scorer (AM) detected sleep in some of these trials while the other two did not, suggesting that the ear-EEG signal contained ambiguous features at sleep onset that did not meet scoring criteria for most raters. Detection performance and conditional agreement analyses

The moderate all-trials SOL agreement reported above reflects two distinct sources of variability: (i) whether each device detects sleep at all on a given trial, and (ii) given that both detect sleep, how well their latency estimates agree. To disentangle these, we report two complementary analyses alongside the primary all-trials SOL agreement: a separation of detection performance from latency estimation, and a sensitivity analysis of SOL agreement conditional on both devices detecting sleep.

Detection Performance (in-ear EEG vs. scalp-EEG truth).

Treating the binary outcome “any sleep detected during the trial” as observed, with scalp-EEG consensus as truth, the in-ear EEG system yielded the following per-trial detection statistics (Table 6). Overall sensitivity was 0.627 (37 of 59 trials in which scalp detected sleep were also detected by in-ear EEG), overall specificity was 0.896 (60 of 67 trials in which scalp detected no sleep were also scored as no-sleep on in-ear EEG). Per-cohort performance differed substantially: healthy sensitivity 0.731 vs. CDH sensitivity 0.545, with specificity approximately equal across cohorts (0.895 vs. 0.897). Positive predictive value was 0.841 overall; negative predictive value was 0.732 overall.

Sensitivity Analysis I: SOL agreement conditional on detection.

We report SOL agreement restricted to the 37 trials in which both devices detected sleep, to remove the influence of the max-trial-duration imputation used for non-detection trials in the primary analysis. In this conditional analysis (Table 7), overall agreement was strong (ICC(2,1) = 0.82, MAD = 1.9 min, bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1.1$$\end{document} min, LoA \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-7.9, +10.1]$$\end{document}); agreement was excellent in healthy controls (ICC = 0.95, MAD = 1.2 min) and moderate in CDH (ICC = 0.64, MAD = 2.7 min). The substantial attenuation of the healthy-CDH performance gap under the conditional analysis (compared to the all-trials analysis, ICC 0.70 vs. 0.43) indicates that the all-trials gap is driven primarily by detection failures in CDH rather than by poorer SOL estimation per se when detection occurs.

Sensitivity Analysis II: bivariate Tobit with right-censoring at 40 minutes.

As a second, statistically principled sensitivity analysis, we fit a bivariate Tobit model to the paired (PSG-SOL, NXT-SOL) observations, treating non-detection trials as right-censored at 40 min rather than as exact observations. The bivariate model was fit by maximum likelihood over the four observation cases (both observed, one observed and one censored, or both censored), yielding latent-variable estimates of the between-device correlation, bias, and limits of agreement (Table 7, bottom row). The censoring-adjusted latent correlation was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = 0.71$$\end{document}, higher than the naive Pearson \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.58$$\end{document} on the AASM-imputed data, indicating that the max-duration imputation attenuates the true between-device rank agreement rather than inflating it. As an independent cross-check, a univariate right-censored Tobit regression of NXT SOL on PSG SOL yielded a latent slope of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1 = 1.24$$\end{document} (implying that latent NXT SOL is approximately 24% longer than latent PSG SOL on average, consistent with the ear’s lower detection sensitivity for brief sleep onsets in CDH); this second formulation agrees with the bivariate model on every point estimate of the latent bias. The Tobit-implied bias (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+13.8$$\end{document} min) and limits of agreement (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-38.7, +66.3]$$\end{document} min) are correspondingly wider than the primary (AASM-convention) estimates, because the Tobit model extrapolates the latent SOL distribution beyond the 40-minute MWT cap—a theoretical construct, since in the MWT protocol wake trials terminate at 40 min by design and “SOL \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 40$$\end{document} min” is not clinically meaningful. We therefore retain the AASM convention (Krahn et al. 2021) as the primary analysis for clinical interpretability, and present the Tobit result as evidence that the primary analysis, if biased at all, is biased toward under-estimating device agreement.

In the overnight sleep condition, individual in-ear-EEG scorers achieve \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.65$$\end{document}–0.79 against the scalp consensus (NXT-CL vs. PSG-CL: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.77$$\end{document}). In the MWT condition, which is dominated by wake and in which sleep events are sparse and brief, individual agreement is lower (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.30$$\end{document}–0.55; NXT-CL vs. PSG-CL \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.50$$\end{document}). One scorer (TBR) shows consistently lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} than the other two across both conditions.

To quantify the robustness of our reported 3-scorer consensus to this most-deviant scorer, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to the subset of epochs on which the two higher-agreement NXT scorers (AM and LY) assigned the same stage label—effectively removing TBR’s influence on the consensus. AM and LY assigned the same NXT label on 89.9% of MWT epochs and 79.3% of overnight sleep epochs. On this AM–LY-concordant subset, agreement with the scalp-EEG consensus was higher than the 3-scorer NXT consensus against the scalp-EEG reference: MWT \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.636$$\end{document} (vs. 0.502 for the full 3-scorer NXT-CL; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta = +0.134$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 10{,}143$$\end{document} epochs) and overnight sleep \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.883$$\end{document} (vs. 0.768 for NXT-CL; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta = +0.115$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 12{,}342$$\end{document} epochs). In other words, the reported 3-scorer consensus is a conservative estimate of ear–scalp agreement: when the most-deviant scorer’s influence is removed, agreement increases substantially, and the overnight sleep value approaches the within-scalp scorer-to-consensus agreement (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.92$$\end{document}; Table 2). We therefore elected to retain the 3-scorer consensus as the primary reported metric throughout this paper, both to avoid post-hoc scorer selection and because it represents the harder test.

Characterization of Scalp-Only Discordant Trials: Missed vs. Subtle-Subthreshold.

For each of the 22 scalp-only discordant trials, we examined the NXT consensus hypnogram to determine whether in-ear EEG scored any non-wake epochs during the trial (indicating that the ear signal contained sleep-compatible features that did not meet AASM sleep-onset criteria) or remained full-wake throughout (indicating that the sleep event was undetectable in the ear signal under manual scoring). Among the 22 scalp-only trials, 15 (68%) were “missed entirely” (zero non-wake epochs on in-ear EEG) and 7 (32%) were “subtle sub-threshold” (at least one N1 epoch on in-ear EEG that did not meet the three-consecutive-N1 or any-N2/N3/REM threshold). The pattern differed between cohorts: in the healthy cohort, 3 of 7 scalp-only trials were missed entirely and 4 were subtle sub-threshold; in the CDH cohort, 12 of 15 were missed entirely and 3 were subtle sub-threshold. Clinically, this distinction matters for MWT-termination decisions: a technician relying solely on in-ear EEG would have no signal to act on in the “missed entirely” cases, whereas the “subtle sub-threshold” cases could in principle be captured with a more-sensitive scoring rubric or automated classifier (Figs. 10 and 11).

Summary metrics

SOL showed no systematic difference between devices (bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+0.1$$\end{document} min, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.89$$\end{document}, ICC = 0.74, MAD = 1.6 min). TST was highly correlated (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.94$$\end{document}, ICC = 0.85), with a small but significant bias of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-4.8$$\end{document} min (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.001$$\end{document}), indicating that in-ear EEG scored slightly less total sleep. SE followed a similar pattern (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.94$$\end{document}, ICC = 0.85, bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-2.0\%$$\end{document}). WASO was also well-correlated (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.93$$\end{document}, ICC = 0.82), with in-ear EEG detecting approximately 5 minutes more wake (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.001$$\end{document}). The consistent direction of these biases—slightly less sleep, slightly more wake on in-ear EEG—is compatible with the reduced N1 detection sensitivity observed in epoch-level analyses (In-ear EEG and scalp EEG sleep stage agreement section), whereby borderline sleep epochs are more often scored as wake on in-ear EEG.

Stage durations

Individual sleep stage durations showed moderate agreement. N3 (slow-wave sleep) was the best-correlated stage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.64$$\end{document}, ICC = 0.59), with in-ear EEG underestimating N3 by 11.5 minutes on average, though this did not reach significance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.07$$\end{document}). N2 duration showed wide variability between devices (ICC = 0.40), likely reflecting epoch-by-epoch disagreements on N1/N2 boundaries. N1 duration showed a significant positive bias on in-ear EEG (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+2.6$$\end{document} min, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.03$$\end{document}), consistent with ambiguous transitional epochs being scored as N1 rather than wake on in-ear EEG. REM duration showed moderate agreement (ICC = 0.44), with in-ear EEG underestimating by 5.1 minutes.

These stage-level discrepancies are consistent with the epoch-level confusion patterns reported in In-ear EEG and scalp EEG sleep stage agreement section, where N1 was the most frequently misclassified stage. Importantly, the stage-level disagreements partially cancel at the summary level, yielding strong agreement for clinically relevant metrics (TST, SE, WASO).

Discussion

Advancements in wearable EEG for sleep monitoring

The present study was designed to establish, in a controlled laboratory setting, whether in-ear EEG can produce sleep-staging and sleep-latency measurements concordant with concurrent scalp EEG, across both healthy controls and a clinical cohort with central disorders of hypersomnolence. The study was conducted in-laboratory under technologist supervision and with concurrent gold-standard recording; home-use characteristics (unsupervised recording, user self-application, multi-night variability) were not directly evaluated here and are not claims of the present data. The design choices of the NextSense system—inter-electrode distance, high-resolution digital acquisition, custom-molded electrode fit, and a small, overnight-tolerable form factor—are relevant engineering antecedents to clinical utility, but their individual contributions to signal quality or staging agreement were not experimentally dissected in this study.

The 4-channel in-ear EEG system yielded substantial sleep-staging agreement with the scalp consensus during overnight sleep (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.77$$\end{document}) and moderate agreement during daytime MWT trials (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.50$$\end{document}); the overnight-sleep value is more representative of the staging task in which all five AASM stages are present in meaningful proportions, whereas the MWT \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} is depressed by the predominance of wake epochs. For comparison, within-modality scalp-EEG inter-rater agreement (individual scorer vs. scalp consensus) was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.92$$\end{document} for overnight sleep and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.78$$\end{document} for MWT. Using the widely cited (Landis and Koch 1977) benchmarks, the overnight ear-vs-scalp agreement falls within the “substantial” range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.61$$\end{document}–0.80), while the within-scalp scorer-to-consensus agreement for overnight sleep falls within the “almost perfect” range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa \ge 0.81$$\end{document}). The MWT ear-vs-scalp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} falls in the “moderate” range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.41$$\end{document}–0.60), consistent with the more challenging trial-level detection problem that the MWT paradigm poses for any modality (MWT sleep onset latency agreement section). Whether the smaller form factor translates into reliable unsupervised, home-based recording at comparable quality remains to be confirmed in dedicated home-based studies (see Constraints, limitations, and future work section).

Comparison with prior in-ear EEG validation studies

These results compare favorably to prior ear-EEG sleep-staging validation work. Mikkelsen et al. (2019) reported \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.73$$\end{document} for automatic whole-night sleep-stage classification using dry-contact in-ear EEG electrodes in a single-ear configuration (20 healthy participants, 80 nights). The present manual-scoring overnight-sleep agreement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.77$$\end{document} is comparable, with our cross-head (interaural) bipolar configuration providing greater dynamic range and delta-band power than single-ear derivations (Crosshead EEG configuration maximizes dynamic range and sleep feature detection section), and a manual consensus-scoring methodology rather than automatic classification (Mikkelsen). Sterr et al. (2018) demonstrated a proof-of-concept behind-the-ear montage using cEEGrid electrodes (Bleichner 2017), reporting a group-level manual-scoring agreement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.42 \pm 0.21$$\end{document} across sleep stages and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.55 \pm 0.24$$\end{document} for sleep–wake discrimination (15 participants, one night each). The present study’s substantially higher agreement reflects both the in-canal electrode placement—which captures signals closer to cortical sources than cEEGrid’s behind-the-ear location (Bleichner 2017)—and the multi-scorer consensus methodology. To our knowledge, no prior ear-EEG validation has included a clinical hypersomnolence cohort or the MWT paradigm. The present study is also, to our knowledge, the largest ear-EEG sleep-staging validation to date in terms of total scored sessions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 142$$\end{document}; 126 MWT trials + 16 overnight sleep sessions) across both healthy controls and a clinical population.

N1 sleep stage agreement

Agreement for stage N1 was notably lower than for other stages (53%), a pattern consistent with the widely acknowledged difficulty of N1 scoring even among expert human scorers using full-scalp EEG; recent meta-analytic estimates place inter-rater Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} for N1 at the lowest of any sleep stage (approximately 0.24, vs. 0.57–0.70 for W/N2/N3/REM in conventional PSG; Léger et al. (2025)). N1 is a transitional state with subtle EEG features (attenuation of alpha rhythm, emergence of low-amplitude mixed-frequency activity) that lacks the distinctive hallmarks of other stages such as K-complexes (N2), high-amplitude slow waves (N3), or rapid eye movements (REM). The reduced signal amplitude available from ear-canal electrodes relative to scalp montages may further attenuate the already subtle N1 markers. Automated scoring algorithms trained specifically on ear-EEG data may help improve N1 detection in future work.

MWT sleep onset latency agreement

To our knowledge, this is the first validation of ear-EEG against scalp EEG for MWT sleep onset latency. The only prior ear-EEG sleep-latency validation used the Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT) in healthy adults only: Alqurashi et al. (2018) reported \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.71$$\end{document}–0.79 with latency differences of <3 minutes in 84–94% of naps, but did not include clinical populations or report ICC. The present study extends this work to the MWT paradigm and includes both healthy controls and CDH participants.

The moderate overall trial-level agreement (Table 5) warrants careful interpretation. Nearly half of all trials (60 of 126) resulted in neither device detecting sleep, an expected outcome of the MWT protocol, in which participants actively resist sleep onset. Among the subset of trials where both devices detected sleep (37 of 126, approximately 30% of trials), SOL agreement was good to excellent (ICC = 0.82 overall, ICC = 0.95 in healthy controls). This subset analysis should be understood as a conditional agreement estimate: it shows that when both systems identify sleep onset in the same trial, they do so at consistent times, but it does not itself speak to the device’s ability to detect sleep in the remaining 70% of trials (see also the detection-performance and sensitivity analyses in Detection performance and conditional agreement analyses section). At the per-subject level—the metric that mirrors standard clinical MWT practice of averaging across four trials per patient—agreement was good (ICC = 0.71), suggesting that the device captures clinically meaningful between-subject differences in daytime sleepiness despite trial-level discordances.

The CDH crossover design adds a complementary within-subject view: paired off- vs. on-medication MWT SOL on the same in-ear EEG hardware (Fig. 9) show that the mean change in subject-level Ear-EEG SOL was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+8.83$$\end{document} minutes on the on-medication day relative to the off-medication day (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 7$$\end{document} evaluable; subject S206 was inadvertently medicated on both study days and is excluded), with three CDH participants (S207, S210, S211) crossing from below to above the healthy-control mean. The two NT1 participants showed the smallest improvements in SOL on medication (S204: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1.84$$\end{document} min; S212: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+4.68$$\end{document} min), consistent with the recognized partial pharmacological response in NT1. This indicates that the system is sensitive to medication-related changes in the MWT clinical endpoint at the patient level, even where trial-level discordances exist. We emphasize, however, that this is an observation about the clinical SOL endpoint that the MWT measures, not an evaluation of medication-effect sensitivity on the EEG signal itself; a formal evaluation of medication-induced changes in EEG signal characteristics would require a separate, adequately powered study (see Clinical positioning and implications section).

Beyond these point estimates, the wide overall Bland–Altman limits of agreement (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-24.3$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+33.6$$\end{document} min) and the positive systematic bias of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+4.7$$\end{document} min in in-ear SOL (all trials) reflect the material impact of the detection discrepancies on trial-level estimates. Agreement was notably weaker in the CDH cohort than in healthy controls (ICC = 0.43 vs. 0.70, respectively), driven primarily by 15 CDH trials in which scalp EEG detected sleep but in-ear EEG did not; 5 of 8 CDH subjects accounted for all 15 such trials. This performance gap has direct clinical implications: the populations that are most likely to benefit from longitudinal ear-EEG monitoring—patients with altered sleep architecture—are also those in which the device’s current detection sensitivity is most limited in absolute terms. Confirmatory studies in larger CDH samples will be important for establishing the extent to which this gap is driven by patient-specific factors (electrode fit, signal quality) versus the predominance of subtle N1-only sleep onsets that challenge ear-canal recording in principle.

It is also useful to consider these results against the gold standard’s own reproducibility. Tankere et al. (2024) showed that two expert scorers using full-scalp EEG disagree on the MWT outcome in up to 32.6% of patients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.54$$\end{document} for a 33-minute threshold, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa = 0.27$$\end{document} for 19 minutes). The ear-versus-scalp agreement reported here falls within that range of inherent MWT scorer variability, which provides context for the observed discordances without excusing them.

The discordant trials—predominantly scalp-detected sleep missed by in-ear EEG—represent a genuine detection limitation. The scalp-detected sleep in these trials was predominantly brief and subtle, often consisting of scattered N1 epochs that did not reliably meet AASM scoring criteria on the ear-EEG signal. Among the three in-ear EEG scorers, one detected sleep in some of these trials while the other two did not, confirming that the signal contained ambiguous features near the threshold of detectability. The in-ear EEG’s reduced sensitivity to alpha rhythm attenuation—the primary marker of the wake-to-N1 transition—likely contributes to the lower detection rate for these brief events (see N1 sleep stage agreement section).

Overnight sleep architecture agreement

The small, consistent biases observed in summary sleep architecture metrics—approximately 5 minutes less TST and 5 minutes more WASO on in-ear EEG—follow directly from the N1 detection limitation discussed above. Borderline sleep/wake transitions are more often scored as wake on in-ear EEG, reducing TST and inflating WASO, while leaving SOL essentially unaffected (bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+0.1$$\end{document} min). Crucially, these biases are predictable in direction and small in magnitude, making them amenable to correction in longitudinal applications.

Individual stage durations showed more variability, particularly at the N1/N2 boundary—a discrimination challenge even among expert scorers using full-scalp EEG. However, these stage-level disagreements partially cancel at the summary level, yielding clinically useful TST and SE estimates. For applications where night-to-night trends matter more than absolute values—medication titration, treatment monitoring, longitudinal research—the strong correlations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r> 0.93$$\end{document}) may be more relevant than the absolute biases.

Clinical positioning and implications

The present findings reflect a controlled, in-laboratory evaluation of in-ear EEG against concurrent gold-standard PSG, conducted under technologist supervision in 24 participants (16 healthy controls and 8 with central disorders of hypersomnolence). Within those constraints, the observed sleep-staging and per-subject MWT SOL agreement motivate continued development toward longitudinal and out-of-laboratory applications; they do not, on their own, establish home-use performance.

For patients with CDH—including narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia—longitudinal monitoring of sleep architecture and daytime alertness currently requires repeated in-laboratory PSG, which is costly, disruptive, and limited to single-night snapshots. The current ambulatory alternative is actigraphy, for which the American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical-practice guidelines provide only a conditional recommendation for monitoring total sleep time prior to MSLT (the diagnostic counterpart of the MWT used in the present work) in patients with suspected CDH (Smith et al. 2018); recent comparative work has further documented that actigraphy systematically overestimates 24-hour total sleep time in idiopathic hypersomnia by 35–119 minutes depending on the scoring algorithm—directly affecting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge 660$$\end{document}-minute TST criterion that is itself a diagnostic feature of IH (Biscarini et al. 2024). The accompanying editorial explicitly identified the need for “multisensor wearables, particularly those including electroencephalography,” as tools to measure sleep latency in the home setting (Cook et al. 2024). The present in-laboratory validation of in-ear EEG against concurrent PSG addresses the EEG-bearing component of that need; ambulatory-setting performance remains to be established in dedicated home-based studies.

If the agreement reported here replicates in larger cohorts and in home environments, an in-ear EEG system could in principle support multi-night assessment and objective treatment monitoring; the present results should be viewed as a necessary precursor to, not a demonstration of, such use. The randomized crossover design in the CDH cohort (medication vs. medication-holiday) establishes that the in-ear EEG system can be deployed within a pharmacological intervention protocol, which is a prerequisite for its eventual use in clinical trials or treatment-optimization studies. Beyond this design feasibility, the within-subject paired-SOL data presented in CDH cohort section (Fig. 9) show that subject-level ear-EEG SOL changes overall in the direction expected for wake-promoting therapy across off- vs. on-medication days (mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta = +8.8$$\end{document} min on-drug, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 7$$\end{document} evaluable; one additional subject was inadvertently medicated on both visits and excluded). Six of seven evaluable subjects showed improvement on medication—including the two NT1 subjects with the smallest improvements (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1.84$$\end{document} min and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+4.68$$\end{document} min, consistent with the recognized partial pharmacological response in NT1)—while one subject showed a small worsening. This observation, on n = 7 evaluable CDH participants, demonstrates that ear-EEG can detect within-subject pharmacological response on the standard MWT clinical endpoint that clinicians rely on for treatment-response assessment. We note that the present data do not directly evaluate medication effects on the EEG itself, and we caution against extrapolating from staging agreement or from this preliminary SOL observation to claims about medication-effect sensitivity on the EEG signal, which would require separate, adequately powered analyses.

Constraints, limitations, and future work

Sample size and scope. The total enrolled cohort (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 24$$\end{document}: 16 healthy controls, 8 CDH participants) means that several key findings—in particular the 15 scalp-only CDH discordant trials—are driven by 5 of 8 CDH subjects. The overnight sleep-architecture analysis is restricted to healthy controls; CDH overnight sleep was not recorded. The study was conducted in-laboratory under technologist supervision, and home-use characteristics were not evaluated. Generalization to broader clinical populations and to unsupervised home-based deployment will require larger confirmatory studies.Censoring in MWT SOL estimation. When a device did not detect sleep during a trial, we assigned SOL equal to the trimmed trial duration (40 min), following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommended protocols for the MWT (Krahn et al. 2021), which specify that “if no sleep occurs on a trial, then 40 minutes is used as the sleep latency.” We adopt this convention as the primary analysis for clinical interpretability. Strictly, such observations are also consistent with right-censoring of the underlying latency. To address the concern that the AASM convention could bias agreement metrics, we report two complementary sensitivity analyses: (i) SOL agreement restricted to the 37 trials in which both devices detected sleep (Table 7, Sensitivity I), where overall agreement was strong (ICC = 0.82, MAD = 1.9 min) and the healthy-CDH gap attenuated substantially; and (ii) a bivariate Tobit model with right-censoring at 40 min (Table 7, Sensitivity II), which yielded a censoring-adjusted latent correlation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = 0.71$$\end{document} (higher than the naive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.58$$\end{document}), indicating that the AASM-convention analysis, if biased at all, under-estimates between-device agreement. We also report a separate analysis of detection performance against scalp-EEG truth (Table 6), showing overall in-ear-EEG sensitivity = 0.63 and specificity = 0.90, with healthy sensitivity 0.73 vs. CDH sensitivity 0.55. Together, these analyses localize the primary performance gap to detection of brief, subtle sleep onsets (especially in CDH) rather than to imprecise latency estimation once sleep is detected.In-ear EEG is not exactly another scalp channel. In-ear EEG signals are not natively identical to scalp EEG and are not directly interpretable without some minimal training. Standardized scorer training and automated scoring approaches may mitigate these limitations.Comparator scope. Censoring in MWT SOL estimation. When a device did not detect sleep during a trial, we assigned SOL equal to the trimmed trial duration (40 min), following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommended protocols for the MWT (Krahn et al. 2021), which specify that “if no sleep occurs on a trial, then 40 minutes is used as the sleep latency.” We adopt this convention as the primary analysis for clinical interpretability. Scorer blinding and modality awareness. Scorers were blinded to participant identity, study arm, and (for the CDH crossover) medication assignment, but the annotation software preserved channel names, making it evident to each scorer which file was in-ear EEG vs. scalp EEG. This modality awareness introduces a potential source of scoring bias: scorers may, consciously or not, apply different thresholds of evidence for the same sleep stage on the two modalities. The direction of such bias is not a priori predictable—a scorer might lean toward conservatism on the less familiar modality (under-scoring sleep on in-ear EEG) or toward leniency (preserving stage assignments across modalities). The observed pattern of systematic under-detection on in-ear EEG in the CDH discordant trials is consistent with multiple underlying mechanisms, of which modality-aware conservatism is only one. A second, structural source of un-blinding is intrinsic to the MWT paradigm itself: the live monitoring technologist terminates each trial in real time when three consecutive epochs of N1 sleep, or one epoch of any other sleep stage, are observed on the scalp record, which means the scalp-EEG record establishes the trial’s effective endpoint regardless of subsequent retrospective scoring. Within these constraints, the strongest available mitigation is the use of three independent scorers with randomized assignment of files, as implemented here; scorer-stratified agreement is reported in Table 8 to allow readers to assess robustness to individual-scorer differences (one scorer, TBR, shows consistently lower per-scorer agreement than the other two, and a sensitivity analysis excluding that scorer is reported alongside). Multimodal sleep detection. The largest source of disagreement in the MWT analysis was 22 trials in which scalp EEG detected sleep but in-ear EEG did not, predominantly in the CDH cohort (15 of 22). The in-ear EEG device used in this study also contains an inertial measurement unit (IMU) that was not utilized for sleep staging. Future work could incorporate IMU-derived motion and head-position features into a multimodal sleep-detection pipeline, potentially improving sensitivity to sleep onset in cases where the EEG signal alone is ambiguous.
