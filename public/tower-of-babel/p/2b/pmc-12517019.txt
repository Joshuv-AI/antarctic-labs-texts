Liver fibrosis with persistently normal alanine transaminase levels exhibits a distinct treatment response in MASLD
Authors: Ling Luo, Congxiang Shao, Long Teng, Shuyu Zhuo, Zhi Dong, Wei Wang, Junzhao Ye, Bihui Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) presents considerable variability in disease progression and treatment outcomes. We aimed to determine whether specific patterns of liver inflammatory flares are correlated with distinct treatment responses.MethodsWe conducted an analysis of a well-characterised prospective cohort involving treatment-naïve MASLD patients from January 2015 to November 2023 at The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Participants underwent a standardised 48-week lifestyle modification programme, with follow-up extending through December 2024. Liver fat content (LFC) was assessed using MRI-based proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF), whereas liver stiffness measurements (LSMs) were performed using two-dimensional shear wave elastography at baseline and after 48 weeks.ResultsParticipants were stratified by alanine transaminase (ALT) and liver fibrosis status: normal ALT/no fibrosis (n=149), elevated ALT/no fibrosis (n=264), normal ALT/fibrosis (n=91) and elevated ALT/fibrosis (n=178). While MRI-PDFF (≥30% LFC decline) and ALT responses (≥17 U/L decrease) did not differ between groups, the elevated ALT/fibrosis group exhibited a significantly higher probability of LSM response (≥1 fibrosis stage improvement) than in the normal ALT/ fibrosis group (53.4% vs 31.9%, p=0.001; OR=2.53, 95% CI: 1.31 to 4.85, p=0.006). Receiver operating characteristic analysis revealed that the cut-offs for weight loss (8.55% vs 4.94%, p=0.023) and LFC reduction (39.85% vs 20.57%, p=0.062) associated with LSM response were higher in patients with normal ALT/fibrosis than in those with elevated ALT/fibrosis.ConclusionMASLD patients with liver fibrosis and persistently normal ALT levels exhibited a less favourable treatment response to fibrosis than those with elevated ALT levels, necessitating more substantial reductions in steatosis and weight to achieve the desired outcomes.Keywords: FIBROSIS, HEPATITIS, LIVER METABOLISM, OBESITY

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease, characterised by fluctuating liver inflammation and progressive fibrosis resulting from metabolic dysregulation, affecting over 30% of the adult population worldwide.1,3 MASLD is associated not only with liver-related outcomes, such as liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, but also with an increased risk of multiple extrahepatic complications, including type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, chronic kidney disease and extrahepatic malignancies, thereby imposing a rapidly escalating health and economic burden.4 5 While the approval of resmetirom by the Food and Drug Administration is a significant advancement for treating MASLD, comprehensive lifestyle modifications remain the cornerstone of effective treatment regimens.6,9 Nevertheless, combining these treatments has not achieved more than 30% histological remission.6

Elevated alanine transaminase (ALT) levels are commonly associated with progressive steatotic liver conditions, including varying degrees of inflammation, hepatocellular injury, liver fibrosis and cirrhosis.7 8 10 While elevated ALT levels necessitate intensified monitoring, patients with persistently normal ALT levels represent a critical subgroup where disease progression may be overlooked, as normal ALT levels typically indicate a static disease condition or a relatively benign clinical course. However, some patients newly diagnosed with MASLD present with persistently normal ALT levels despite exhibiting differing severities of fibrosis.11 12 Current guidelines fail to provide specific recommendations for this phenotype, which may be underestimated by non-invasive fibrosis scores, as ALT levels could be a significant variable, potentially delaying interventions until advanced stages of the disease are reached. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether this subgroup demonstrates a consistent treatment response to lifestyle management interventions. Identifying differential treatment responses among these patients could inform risk-stratified management protocols, thereby optimising resource allocation and preventing the progression of fibrosis.

MRI-based proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) is a reliable and accurate method for repeatedly measuring liver fat content (LFC), surpassing liver histology in tracking longitudinal changes in liver fat among patients with MASLD.13 Patients with MASLD exhibiting an MRI-PDFF response (defined as a relative decline in liver fat of ≥30%) are significantly more likely to show a histological response than those without an MRI-PDFF response.14 Moreover, improvements in fibrosis (defined as a reduction of at least one stage) are strongly correlated with the resolution of metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).15 Two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) is a widely employed non-invasive technique for evaluating liver fibrosis. A meta-analysis of 13 045 patients with MASLD revealed a pooled area under the curve (AUC) of 0.95 for 2D-SWE in detecting advanced fibrosis.16 Therefore, the combination of MRI-PDFF and 2D-SWE offers a highly accurate alternative to paired liver biopsy for monitoring the treatment of MASLD.

In this study, we used a well-characterised prospective cohort to identify and compare treatment response patterns among Chinese patients with MASLD, stratified by serum ALT and liver fibrosis status over 48 weeks.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, enrolling consecutive patients with MASLD from January 2015 to November 2023, with follow-up extending until December 2024. The study protocol was registered in 2014 with a definition of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; however, the collected variables encompassed all necessary criteria for diagnosing MASLD in accordance with the 2024 joint European Association for the Study of the Liver–European Association for the Study of Diabetes–European Association for the Study of Obesity MASLD Clinical Practice Guidelines.5 As demonstrated in our previous study, this MASLD cohort underwent standardised lifestyle interventions and received dynamic MRI-PDFF and 2D-SWE monitoring.17 This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting observational cohort studies (online supplemental file 1).

Participants were categorised into four groups based on their serum ALT levels and liver fibrosis status at baseline: (1) the normal ALT and no fibrosis group, characterised by persistently normal ALT levels and fibrosis stage 0; (2) the elevated ALT and no fibrosis group, defined by elevated ALT levels and fibrosis stage 0; (3) the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group, identified by persistently normal ALT levels and fibrosis stage ≥1; and (4) the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group, characterised by elevated ALT levels and fibrosis stage ≥1. Persistently normal ALT levels were defined as three measurements within the normal range (33 IU/L for men and 25 IU/L for women) taken over a 6-month period, with a 2-month interval between each measurement.7 10 18 Patients who did not meet these criteria were classified as having elevated ALT. The exclusion criteria are described in online supplemental digital content 1.

Clinical and biochemical evaluations

Anthropometric measurements and biochemical testing are described in online supplemental digital content 2. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) score was calculated as fasting glucose×fasting insulin (FINS)/22.5. Elevated ALT levels were defined as >33 IU/L in men and >25 IU/L in women.7 The cut-off value for hypersensitive C reactive protein (hs-CRP) was established at 2.0 mg/L.9

Radiographic examination

The severity of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis was quantitatively assessed using MRI-PDFF and 2D-SWE, respectively. Detailed procedures and methods are described in online supplemental digital content 3 and 4.17 19 20 Hepatic steatosis was defined as an average LFC of ≥5.0%, with severity classified into three levels: mild (5–10%), moderate (11–18%) and severe (16–23%) steatosis.7 The cut-off values for liver stiffness measurement (LSM) corresponding to fibrosis stages were as follows: F0: ≤6.3 kPa, F1: 6.4–7.5 kPa, F2: 7.6–8.8 kPa, F3: 8.9–9.8 kPa and F4: ≥9.9 kPa.21 In a subset of 151 patients undergoing liver biopsy, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses demonstrated that the 2D-SWE thresholds achieved AUC values of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.85 to 0.96) for ≥F1 fibrosis, 0.90 (0.84 to 0.96) for ≥F2, 0.89 (0.80 to 0.98) for ≥F3 and 0.91 (0.78 to 0.99) for F4 cirrhosis, respectively (online supplemental figure S1 and table S1). The diagnostic sensitivity ranged from 0.82 to 0.93, while specificity ranged from 0.88 to 0.98 (online supplemental table S2).

Follow-up and study endpoints

All participants received guidance from a nutrition specialist (SZ) regarding lifestyle modifications following the current MASLD guidelines, which encompassed dietary restrictions and regular physical activity.7,9 Each participant was given a portable brochure containing personalised dietary and exercise recommendations tailored to their sex, age, body mass index (BMI) and medical history. Caloric restriction was defined as a reduction in daily energy intake of at least 500 kcal/day.22 Regular exercise was defined as engaging in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for a minimum of 30 min at least once a week. This included recreational and occupational activities that promote perspiration, such as cycling, volleyball, football and lifting or carrying heavy loads.17 23 For individuals with baseline obesity (BMI≥25.0 kg/m2), an intensified intervention was recommended, which included either an experimental diet or orlistat therapy, along with an increase in physical activity to 150–240 min per week. For patients requiring treatment for dyslipidaemia, hypertension, hyperglycaemia or hyperuricaemia, the multidisciplinary team for fatty liver would prescribe statins for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) control, fenofibrate for triglyceride (TG) control, renin–angiotensin blockers or calcium channel blockers for blood pressure management, metformin or pioglitazone for blood glucose control and benzbromarone for uric acid (UA) control.24,27

During the 48-week follow-up period, participants were instructed to adhere to their personalised dietary and physical activity routines. Follow-up interviews were conducted by a professional nutritionist every 12 weeks until the 48-week visit. In these interviews, participants reported the frequency of their physical activities and the quantity of their usual food intake over the preceding month, which included meat, seafood, eggs, vegetables, fruits and nuts, to assess compliance with the lifestyle modification regimen (online supplemental appendix 1). Additionally, serum ALT levels were measured at 12-week intervals (0, 12, 24, 36 and 48 weeks) to evaluate longitudinal fluctuations. All participants underwent imaging examination at baseline and after 48 weeks. In terms of hepatic steatosis, MRI-PDFF response was defined as a ≥30% decrease in LFC from baseline.14 For liver injury, ALT response was considered an absolute decrease of ≥17 IU/L from baseline, in accordance with recent clinical practice guidelines for MASLD.7 8 This analysis included patients with elevated ALT levels, encompassing both the elevated ALT and no fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group. Regarding liver fibrosis, LSM response was determined as a reduction of ≥1 stage from baseline, which included participants with a baseline fibrosis stage of ≥1, comprising both the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R V.4.3.1, with two-sided p values of <0.05 considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical methods are described in online supplemental digital content 5.28

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

The study included 682 MASLD patients, consisting of 491 (72.0%) men, with a mean age of 42.3±13.2 years. Among the participants, 149 (21.9%) were classified in the normal ALT and no fibrosis group, 264 (38.7%) in the elevated ALT and no fibrosis group, 91 (13.3%) in the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group, and 178 (26.1%) in the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group. The cohort consisted of 491 (72.0%) men, with a mean age of 42.3±13.2 years. Table 1 summarises the baseline characteristics across the various MASLD categories. Patients with elevated ALT levels, including those in both the elevated ALT and no fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group, exhibited significantly higher levels of total cholesterol, TG, LDL-C, FINS, UA, ALT, aspartate aminotransferase, γ-glutamyl transferase, hs-CRP and LFC compared with those with normal ALT levels, which included individuals from the normal ALT and no fibrosis group as well as the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group (all p<0.05). Furthermore, the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group demonstrated a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus compared with the two groups without liver fibrosis (normal ALT/no fibrosis vs elevated ALT/no fibrosis vs normal ALT/fibrosis vs elevated ALT/fibrosis: 8.1% vs 12.9% vs. 31.9% vs 30.7%, p<0.001).

After 48 weeks of lifestyle intervention, significant reductions were observed across the overall cohort in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, systolic and diastolic blood pressures, blood lipids (except high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), liver enzymes, as well as LFC and LSM levels (all p<0.05, online supplemental table S3). Figure 1 illustrates that body weight and LFC levels underwent significant decreases within each MASLD category (all p<0.05). ALT values decreased compared with baseline levels in both the elevated ALT and no fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group, while LSM values decreased in the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group (all p<0.05). Additionally, patients with elevated ALT levels exhibited significantly higher ALT levels at all time points (0, 12, 24, 36 and 48 weeks) compared with those with normal ALT levels (all p<0.001, online supplemental figure S2).

Dynamic changes in body weight and liver characteristics, including alanine aminotransferase levels, liver fat content and liver stiffness, in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease stratified by serum alanine transaminase (ALT) and liver fibrosis status. Variables with normal distribution were compared by independent t-tests, and variables with non-normal distribution were compared by Mann-Whitney U tests.

In this study, 682 patients were categorised into three distinct clusters: a control cluster (n=439, 64.4%), a cardiometabolic cluster (n=125, 18.3%) and a liver-specific cluster (n=118, 17.3%) (online supplemental table S4). Among the 91 patients with liver fibrosis and normal ALT levels, 62 (68.1%) were classified into the control cluster, while 29 (31.9%) were assigned to the cardiometabolic cluster. Similarly, of the 178 patients with liver fibrosis and elevated ALT, 74 (41.6%) were mapped to the control cluster, 55 (30.9%) to the cardiometabolic cluster and 49 (27.5%) to the liver-specific cluster (online supplemental figure S3).

Treatment response differences in liver steatosis, injury and fibrosis

In the overall cohort and in subgroups stratified by steatosis grade, fibrosis stage and sex, no significant differences were observed in the proportions of MRI-PDFF response and ALT response across the MASLD categories (all p>0.05, figure 2 and online supplemental figure S4). However, within the mild steatosis subgroup, the proportion of MRI-PDFF response in the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group was significantly higher than that in the elevated ALT and no fibrosis group (40.7% vs 17.2%, p<0.05, figure 2B). The proportion of LSM response was significantly higher in the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group compared with the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group (53.4% vs 31.9%, p=0.001, figure 2D). Further subgroup analyses indicated that this difference in the LSM response ratio was present in male patients (59.4% vs 31.8%, p<0.001), as well as in individuals with moderate-to-severe steatosis (54.7% vs 24.3%, p=0.001), fibrosis stage 1 (48.9% vs 22.7%, p=0.004) and fibrosis stage 2 (60.8% vs 30.8%, p=0.013) (figures2D 3A). Additionally, MASLD patients with hs-CRP>2.0 mg/L demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of LSM response compared with those with hs-CRP≤2.0 mg/L (57.8% vs 40.9%, p=0.010), particularly within the normal ALT and fibrosis group (52.4% vs 25.7%, p=0.021) (online supplemental figure S5). After conducting a propensity score matching for age, sex, BMI and TG levels, significant differences were observed in the proportion of LSM responses between the elevated ALT and fibrosis group and the normal ALT and fibrosis group (55.0% vs 31.1%, p=0.003, online supplemental figure S6 and table S5).

Furthermore, we conducted both univariable and multivariable analyses to evaluate the associations between each baseline MASLD category and the odds of treatment response at 48 weeks. No significant correlations were identified between the MASLD categories and MRI-PDFF response, ALT response, or a ≥30% relative decrease in ALT levels, both in the overall patient population and in subgroups (all p>0.05, online supplemental table S6–S8). The group with elevated ALT and liver fibrosis exhibited a significantly greater likelihood of LSM response compared with the group with normal ALT and liver fibrosis (OR=2.45, 95% CI: 1.44 to 4.16, p=0.001). Multivariable analyses were stratified to control for key confounders, including age, sex, baseline BMI, hs-CRP and LFC levels, lipid-lowering therapy, antidiabetic therapy and relative changes in body weight and LFC. Both models demonstrated that elevated ALT and liver fibrosis status independently predicted LSM response (model 1: OR=2.04, 95% CI: 1.14 to 3.64, p=0.016; model 2: OR=2.52, 95% CI: 1.31 to 4.85, p=0.006) (figure 3A and online supplemental table S9). Stratification by steatosis grade (mild vs moderate-to-severe steatosis), fibrosis stage (F1–2 vs F3–4), and sex (male vs female) revealed that the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group exhibited a higher likelihood of LSM response compared with the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group among individuals with mild steatosis (OR=4.61, 95% CI: 1.43 to 14.87, p=0.011) and moderate-to-severe steatosis (OR=2.81, 95% CI: 1.06 to 7.41, p=0.037), as well as among those with fibrosis stages 1–2 (OR=3.75, 95% CI: 1.69 to 8.31, p=0.001) and male patients (OR=3.02, 95% CI: 1.41 to 6.46, p=0.004) (figure 3B–D and online supplemental table S8). Additionally, we assessed the dose–response relationship of ALT levels with the likelihood of LSM response in male (upper limit of normal: 33 IU/L) and female (upper limit of normal: 25 IU/L) patients using RCS analysis. A linear correlation was observed between the multiple of the upper normal ALT level and the likelihood of LSM response in male patients (p overall <0.001, p non-linear=0.107, figure 3E), whereas no significant correlation was found in female patients (p overall=0.338, p non-linear=0.189, figure 3F).

Differences in predictive values of body weight and LFC changes for monitoring fibrosis improvement

Weight loss and LFC reductions were prioritised based on the clinical guidance for MASLD, which identifies these factors as key modifiable predictors of histological improvement.5 6 In both the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group, the proportion of LSM response among participants with a relative change in body weight of ≥5.0% was higher than that of those without such a change (figure 4A). Meanwhile, the proportion of LSM response was significantly greater in individuals exhibiting a relative change in LFC of ≥30% (figure 4B). Furthermore, ROC analysis revealed that the AUC for the relative change in body weight was significantly higher in patients with normal ALT and liver fibrosis compared with those with elevated ALT and liver fibrosis (0.76 (95% CI: 0.66 to 0.86) vs 0.61 (95% CI: 0.53 to 0.69), p=0.023), with corresponding cut-off values of 8.55% and 4.94%, respectively (figure 4C). Additionally, patients with normal ALT and liver fibrosis showed a trend towards higher AUCs for the relative change in LFC compared with those with elevated ALT and liver fibrosis (0.74 (95% CI: 0.64 to 0.85) vs 0.62 (95% CI: 0.54 to 0.70), p=0.062), with corresponding cut-off values of 39.85% and 20.57%, respectively (figure 4D). Exploratory analyses were conducted to evaluate the predictive capacity of relative changes in glycosylated haemoglobin and HOMA-IR for treatment response in hepatic fibrosis across the entire cohort and within subgroups stratified by diabetes mellitus status. The AUC values indicated limited predictive accuracy (all AUCs<0.60, p>0.05) in both the normal ALT and liver fibrosis group and the elevated ALT and liver fibrosis group (online supplemental figure S7).

Discussion

This prospective longitudinal study revealed a relatively high rate of liver fibrosis and persistently normal ALT levels in patients with MASLD. This demonstrates significant variability in treatment responses after 48 weeks of lifestyle modification within this MASLD category. The treatment efficacy for liver steatosis and injury did not differ among the four MASLD categories. However, a lower proportion of fibrosis improvement was observed in patients with liver fibrosis and persistently normal ALT levels than in those with elevated ALT levels. Furthermore, the relative changes in body weight and LFC for predicting fibrosis improvement exhibited higher cut-off values in patients with persistently normal ALT levels than in those with elevated ALT levels.

Increased serum ALT levels have been recognised as a biomarker for liver cell injury and inflammation.7 8 The novel observation from this study reveals that patients with MASLD, liver fibrosis and elevated ALT levels are more likely to experience fibrosis regression. This observation suggests that patients with fibrosis and persistently normal or elevated ALT levels exhibit distinct patterns of fibrosis progression and regression. Elevated ALT levels likely indicate active hepatocyte repair, which facilitates fibrosis reversal through extracellular matrix remodelling via collagen degradation.29 30 In contrast, persistently normal ALT levels in fibrotic patients suggest a state of low-grade inflammation, characterised by quiescent stellate cells that resist apoptosis and exhibit altered macrophage polarisation. This phenotype is associated with reduced proteolytic activity, potentially impairing the regression of fibrosis.31 32 The regulation of inflammatory responses represents a critical determinant of fibrosis regression. Our findings demonstrate that elevated hs-CRP levels are significantly associated with enhanced therapeutic responsiveness in hepatic fibrosis regression. Future investigations should implement longitudinal tracking of key inflammatory mediators, including interleukin 6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha, to delineate the mechanistic interplay between inflammatory pathway activation and phenotypic therapeutic plasticity in MASLD.

The profibrotic regression effect of liver inflammation can be influenced by individual differences, including the stage of fibrosis and sex. Elevated ALT levels may positively affect fibrosis regression in stages 1–2 in patients with MASLD, whereas their benefits appear limited for fibrosis stages 3–4. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon remains unclear; however, a plausible explanation is that liver fibrosis becomes more stable and resistant to reversal as the degree of fibrosis increases. Advanced fibrosis typically indicates significant alterations in liver structure, including the formation of hepatocellular nodules and the development of portal hypertension, which make it less amenable to regression.29 33 34 Furthermore, elevated ALT levels may correlate with changes in the liver microenvironment, which can affect the activation status of hepatic stellate cells. This leads to enhanced activation of Kupffer cells and increased production of matrix metalloproteinases in the liver, thereby facilitating the degradation of fibrous tissue.31 35 Consequently, another potential explanation may involve changes in the types of fibrotic cells: activated hepatic stellate cells are the predominant fibrotic cells during the early stages of fibrosis, whereas later stages may exhibit a shift in cell types, including an increase in myofibroblasts.35 This study also found that elevated ALT levels facilitated fibrosis regression exclusively in men, with no such effect observed in women. Dysregulation of hepatic interleukin 6/STAT3 signalling pathways and androgen receptor-mediated stellate cell senescence mechanisms may drive the observed sex disparities in fibrotic progression.36 37 Experimental evidence suggests that testosterone enhances the biosynthesis of proresolving lipid mediators by Kupffer cells in men, whereas oestrogen-dominant signalling in women may promote the persistence of fibrogenesis.

Weight loss that targets specific goals has been strongly associated with treatment outcomes in patients with MASLD. A prospective cohort study involving 293 Cuban patients with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, which included a 52-week lifestyle modification programme and histological endpoints, demonstrated that a weight loss of ≥5% reduced steatosis, while a reduction of ≥10% improved fibrosis.38 While the MASLD subtype identified by Raverdy et al highlighted patients at risk for cardiovascular outcomes,28 this study further revealed that integrating non-invasive fibrosis assessment with ALT status is essential for personalising the thresholds for changes in body weight or LFC. The optimal cut-off value for the relative change in body weight predicting liver fibrosis regression was found to be 8.55% in patients with fibrosis and persistently normal ALT levels, which is higher than the 4.94% observed in those with elevated ALT levels. Prior studies have shown that a relative reduction of ≥30% in MRI-PDFF-measured LFC was associated with MASH resolution and fibrosis regression.14 39 Our analysis revealed that the optimal cut-off value for the relative change in LFC predicting liver fibrosis regression was 39.85% in patients with fibrosis and persistently normal ALT levels, whereas it was 20.57% in those with elevated ALT levels. Consequently, these findings suggest that a timely diagnosis and intervention for metabolic abnormalities are more effective in patients with MASLD who exhibit liver inflammation. Conversely, when liver inflammation is relatively inactive, fibrosis is more likely to represent the remnants of previously formed collagen deposits, necessitating more rigorous and targeted treatment strategies for reversal.32

This cohort study has several limitations. First, although histological assessment via liver biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, its invasiveness limits its feasibility in large longitudinal cohorts. Future studies that combine non-invasive imaging with targeted biopsies may further validate our findings. Second, the study cohort was recruited from a single tertiary referral centre with a moderate sample size, which may constrain the external validity of the findings. For instance, while elevated ALT levels appeared to promote the reversal of liver fibrosis in stages F1–2, this effect diminished in stages F3–4. However, this result should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size in the advanced fibrosis subgroup (F3–4, n=21). Moreover, the predominance of men (72%) in our cohort may limit the generalisability of our findings to women, particularly considering the established sex differences in fibrosis progression.40 Future multicentre cohorts with adequate statistical power are needed to validate these observations and comprehensively assess sex differences, geographical variations, socioeconomic disparities and ethnic diversity in therapeutic responsiveness. Third, lifestyle factors were assessed through self-report questionnaires instead of using specific technologies or biomarkers. This approach introduces a potential recall bias, and the absence of daily in-person guidance regarding diet and exercise for participants affected the evaluation of adherence to lifestyle interventions. Fourthly, as we retrospectively identified the patients’ ALT levels prior to their referral to our centre, we could not determine the reasons why patients with MASLD, particularly those with elevated ALT levels, were not treated for metabolic disorders. Although longitudinal data confirmed sustained intergroup differences throughout the 48-week follow-up (online supplemental figure S2), ALT dynamics were not included in the group reclassification. Future studies should incorporate serial ALT assessments to evaluate the impact of intraindividual variability on outcomes. Finally, our findings from the 48-week study represent significant interim milestones in fibrosis regression. Further studies involving larger cohorts are warranted to assess the sustained durability of these 48-week findings and to investigate the impact of serum ALT on fibrosis regression, which can help identify potential therapeutic targets for patients with MASLD.

In summary, our study revealed that patients with MASLD, liver fibrosis and persistently normal ALT levels exhibited a poorer treatment response to liver fibrosis than those with elevated ALT levels. Furthermore, patients with MASLD and persistently normal or elevated ALT levels exhibit varying targets for steatosis reduction and weight loss in predicting fibrosis improvements. Specifically, patients with MASLD and persistently normal ALT levels require a higher weight loss goal to achieve the desired outcomes. This finding underscores the necessity for close monitoring and the adoption of more intensive strategies for patients with MASLD and persistently normal ALT levels to improve fibrosis.
