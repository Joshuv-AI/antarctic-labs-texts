A fast and genotype-independent in planta Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method for soybean
Authors: Heng Zhong, Changbao Li, Wenjin Yu, Hua-ping Zhou, Tara Lieber, Xiujuan Su, Wenling Wang, Eric Bumann, Rafaela Miranda Lunny Castro, Yaping Jiang, Wening Gu, Qingli Liu, Brenden Barco, Chengjin Zhang, Liang Shi, Qiudeng Que
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient genotype-independent transformation and genome editing are highly desirable for plant biotechnology research and product development efforts. We have developed a novel approach to enable fast, high-throughput, and genotype-flexible Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using the important crop soybean as a test system. This new method is called GiFT (genotype-independent fast transformation) and involves only a few simple steps. The method uses germinated seeds as explants, and DNA delivery is achieved through Agrobacterium infection of wounded explants as in conventional in vitro-based methods. Following infection, the wounded explants are incubated in liquid medium with a sublethal level of selection and then transplanted directly into soil. The transplanted seedlings are then selected with herbicide spray for 3 weeks. The time required from initiation to fully established healthy T0 transgenic events is about 35 days. The GiFT method requires minimal in vitro manipulation or use of tissue culture media. Because the regeneration occurs in planta, the GiFT method is highly flexible with respect to genotype, which we demonstrate via successful transformation of elite germplasms from diverse genetic backgrounds. We also show that the soybean GiFT method can be applied to both conventional binary vectors and CRISPR-Cas12a vectors for genome editing applications. Analyses of T1 progeny demonstrate that the events have a high inheritance rate and can be used for genome engineering applications. By minimizing the need for tissue culture, the novel approach described here significantly improves operational efficiency while greatly reducing personnel and supply costs. It is the first industry-scale transformation method to utilize in planta selection in a major field crop.Key words: soybean, genotype-flexible transformation, genome editing, in planta regeneration, herbicide selection

Introduction

Soybean is a highly valuable and widely cultivated crop, known for its diverse applications in food, feed, biofuel, and many other industrial and consumer applications (Chen et al., 2012). Soybean production has significantly increased over the years despite many constraints, such as biotic and abiotic stresses (Hartman et al., 2011). Traditional plant breeding has played a crucial role in enhancing soybean productivity. However, the limited genetic variability and narrow gene pools of soybean germplasms have posed challenges for further improvements using conventional breeding techniques (Vieira and Chen, 2021). To meet the increasing demand for soybean and promote sustainable production, new breeding technologies have become essential. Two key advances in soybean breeding are genetic modification (GM) with transgenes and, more recently, genome editing (GE). Conventional GM technology involves introducing exogenous genes into soybean plants to confer traits such as herbicide resistance, pest tolerance, and improved nutrition (Homrich et al., 2012). Since the introduction of the first transgenic herbicide-resistant soybean product in the 1990s, soybean has become one of the major commercially grown GM crops around the world (Pagano and Miransari, 2016). In recent years, GE has been applied to soybean to enable precise and predictable modifications of native genes to obtain desired traits (Xu et al., 2020; Cai et al., 2023; Sim et al., 2023).

Both GM and GE studies rely heavily on efficient transformation systems (Gao, 2021). The earliest successes in soybean transformation used Agrobacterium tumefaciens (Hinchee et al., 1988; Chee et al., 1989; Parrott et al., 1989) and biolistics (Christou et al., 1988; McCabe et al., 1988; Sato et al., 1993) to deliver the DNA. However, these early methods were highly genotype dependent and had low transformation efficiency. There were many reports of improvements in soybean transformation methods, but the overall efficiency remained low for most approaches (Finer and McCullen, 1991; Chee and Slighton, 1995; Chowrira et al., 1995; Droste et al., 2000; Yan et al., 2000; Ko et al., 2003; Wang and Xu, 2008). The cotyledonary node method, which employs Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, has gained popularity because of its simplicity and relatively higher transformation efficiency compared with the biolistic method (Olhoft et al., 2003; Paz et al., 2004; Liu and Wei, 2005; Homrich et al., 2012; Yamada et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2013; Mariashibu et al., 2013; Tiwai et al., 2023). Significant improvement in transformation frequency has also been achieved using alternative explants such as the “half-seed” (Paz et al., 2006; Li et al., 2017; Pareddy et al., 2020), cotyledon–leaf node (Olhoft et al., 2007), cotyledon–epicotyl (Khan, 2004; Liang et al., 2023), and embryo axis (McCabe et al., 1988; Aragão et al., 2000; Martinell et al., 2002; Ye et al., 2011; Cho et al., 2022). Despite reports of alternative bacterial systems for gene delivery and the ability of Ochrobactrum-mediated delivery to achieve high soybean transformation frequency and a high percentage of single-copy events (Hooykaas et al., 1977; van Veen et al., 1988; Broothaerts et al., 2005; Rathore and Mullins, 2018; Cho et al., 2022), Agrobacterium-mediated delivery remains the preferred method for soybean transformation (Xu et al., 2022). The preference for Agrobacterium is justified by several key advantages, including simple and cheap operation, highly efficient gene delivery, and high percentage of low-copy events.

Despite these advances, challenges in soybean transformation remain, including low transformation efficiency, genotype dependency, the requirement for tissue culture, time-consuming procedures, and lower throughput (Mangena et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2022). To address these challenges, the in planta transformation approach has been investigated as an alternative method; this approach involves direct transformation of seedlings, intact plants, or plant tissues without the need for regeneration from tissue culture, thereby reducing time and labor cost (Bent, 2000; Kesiraju and Sreevathsa, 2017; Kaur and Devi, 2019; Mangena, 2019; Saifi et al., 2020; Cao et al., 2023). However, usually, many plants or seedlings need to be infected by Agrobacterium and many T1 progeny plants screened to recover a few transformation events (Chen et al., 2020; Kesiraju et al., 2020). Thus, in planta transformation still has challenges to its ability to achieve high transformation efficiency, good event quality, and heritability in most crops, including soybean (Mangena, 2022; Xu et al., 2022).

Regardless of its low transformation efficiency and high incidence of chimeras in primary transformants (also called T0 events) of crop plants, the in planta transformation method is simple, fast, and genotype independent (Bent, 2000; Karthik et al., 2018; Kesiraju et al., 2021; Vasudevan et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). On the other hand, the in vitro culture-based transformation method has high gene delivery and transformation efficiency, but it is labor intensive and time consuming and requires a dedicated tissue culture facility. If the advantages of both in vitro and in planta transformation approaches could be combined, it would be possible to obtain highly efficient and genotype-flexible transformation systems. Here, we report studies that attempt such a combination to address the challenges encountered in soybean transformation and describe the development of a high-throughput transformation method called GiFT (genotype-independent fast transformation), applicable to a broad range of soybean germplasm.

The novel GiFT method involves only a few simple steps: seed explant preparation, Agrobacterium infection, a short liquid culture phase followed by transplantation to soil, and in planta herbicide selection. The short liquid culture is performed in the presence of a sublethal level of selection to suppress proliferation of non-transformed tissues and promote proliferation of transformed regenerable cells. After transplantation, the seedlings are sprayed with herbicide to allow preferential proliferation and regeneration of transformed axillary meristematic cells to form transgenic shoots. Because the untransformed main shoot is inhibited or damaged, apical dominance is also released. In planta selection with systemic herbicide also eliminates the production of chimeric shoots. The results demonstrate that the GiFT method is highly efficient and significantly accelerates the soybean transformation process. Transgenic T0 plants are obtained in as little as 4–5 weeks from initiation. We also showed that the GiFT method can be used to generate both transgenic and genome-edited events in many tested soybean elite varieties. The efficiency of transgene inheritance was confirmed in T1 progeny. The successful development of the highly efficient GiFT method in soybean represents a breakthrough in in planta crop transformation technology after more than 30 years of research. The principle underlying the GiFT method should be applicable to other crops.

Results

In planta regeneration of transgenic shoots from the axillary meristem

In most reported in planta transformation methods, transformants were obtained from flowers derived from infected apical or floral meristems without post-infection selection (Kaur and Devi, 2019; Kesiraju et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022). Lack of selection leads to low transformation efficiency, high T0 plant chimera rates, and higher percentages of low-quality events containing a high transgene copy number and vector backbone (Chen et al., 2020). It is well known that only transformed meristematic (germline) cells can give rise to heritable transformants. It is also well established that the axillary regions in the cotyledonary and leaf nodes are the target tissues of current in vitro-based soybean transformation protocols (Martinell et al., 2002; Khan, 2004; Paz et al., 2004, 2006; Ye et al., 2011; Liang et al., 2023). We also postulated that it was critical to inhibit growth of the untransformed main shoot in order to promote proliferation of transformed axillary meristematic cells and thus achieve good in planta transformation efficiency. To test this hypothesis, we removed the main shoot apical region of soil-germinated soybean seedlings, wounded the cotyledonary node axillary regions, and then infected the wounded regions with Agrobacterium cells containing the binary vector 23093 (Figure 1) in a cotton ball with infection medium (Figure 2A). When examined under a fluorescence microscope, some regenerated shoot primordia were fluorescent (Figure 2B), suggesting that they were derived from transformed axillary meristematic cells. To test whether transgenic plants could be obtained from these transformed buds, fresh cotton balls containing glyphosate herbicide were placed on the nodal region. After about 2–3 weeks, glyphosate-resistant shoots grew from the axillary region of the cotyledonary node (Figure 2C). This initial experiment demonstrated that transgenic plants could be obtained from in planta-infected axillary meristematic regions. It should be noted that the in planta-transformed T0 plant produced with our new approach is very different from those described previously, in that it is a composite plant containing a wholly transformed upper part (newly regenerated shoot) and an untransformed lower part (cotyledon, hypocotyl, and roots). Further experiments recovered heritable transgenic events from three known highly transformable soybean varieties, Jack, 06KG, and FX017, within about 2 months of infection with Agrobacterium harboring binary construct 18891 containing the ALS marker gene using a different herbicide, bensulfuron methyl (BSM), at up to 5% transformation frequency (results not shown). These results demonstrated the feasibility of producing heritable transgenic events in a relatively short time in multiple genotypes using an in planta transformation method.

Enhancement of in planta transformation efficiency by incorporation of in vitro culture conditions

The initial experiments demonstrated the feasibility of in planta transformation in soybean (Figure 2C). However, the efficiency was low in comparison with in vitro transformation. To overcome the low transformation frequency, treatments known to contribute to efficient in vitro Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Liang et al., 2023) and rapid in planta transformation were combined in a new transformation process (Figure 3). The critical conditions from high-efficiency in vitro-based soybean transformation include: (1) use of seed explants with high regeneration potential, targeted wounding, Agrobacterium infection, and co-culture; (2) culture with a hormone (cytokinin) to promote proliferation of transformed cells and regeneration of shoots under sublethal selection; and (3) selection to inhibit the growth of untransformed tissues to reduce chimeras and escapes. The use of a sublethal level of selection is critical so that the hypocotyl and root can still develop normally after transplantation into soil. The treatments from conventional in planta transformation include: (1) direct establishment of healthy and fast-growing plants in soil, (2) fast generation of shoots, which is highly genotype flexible, and (3) most importantly, establishment of a chimeric composite plant with untransformed organs (roots and cotyledons) supporting the growth of transformed tissues (shoots). This new in planta transformation method—which incorporates in vitro-based treatments in the early stage of the transformation process—enables high-throughput production of transgenic events in a short period of time (Figures 2D–2M and 3).

Flowchart and timeline of the soybean GiFT transformation method.

The transformation process starts with imbibing mature dry seeds in sterile water overnight. Cotyledon explants with epicotyl are prepared by removing the seed coat, one of the cotyledons, and the primary leaf primordia. The explants are further wounded and inoculated with Agrobacterium suspension. After 3–5 days of co-cultivation, the explants are incubated with liquid medium containing the herbicide selection agent for 1 week, then transplanted into soil for continuous herbicide selection for an additional 3 weeks. Leaves of regenerated shoots are sampled for TaqMan assays to determine the copy number of transgenes and gene-editing target sequences. The complete GiFT transformation process takes about 35 days.

As in the in vitro-based fast soybean transformation method (Liang et al., 2023), the new in planta method uses an explant containing one cotyledon and epicotyl region of an imbibed seed as the target tissue for transformation. Healthy sterilized mature seeds were fully imbibed in sterile distilled water overnight (Figure 2D). To achieve high transformation frequency, it was critical to completely remove both primary leaf primordia after discarding the seed coat and one of the cotyledons when applying the sonication-assisted wounding treatment (Figure 2E). After evaluating various frequencies and durations, the preferred conditions for sonication wounding were determined to be 100–200 explants in about 50 mL of Agrobacterium suspension in a SteriCon-8 culture vessel and treatment at a frequency of 45 kHz for 30 s (Figure 2F). Alternatively, explants were wounded with a scalpel blade as described previously (Liang et al., 2023). Explants wounded by sonication were incubated with Agrobacterium suspension for about 30 min, arranged inside a Petri dish with adaxial sides up (Figure 2G), and incubated in an airtight plastic container at 23°C in the dark for 4–6 days (Figure 2H). To promote active cell division and suppress non-transgenic cell development, an adequate amount of SoySiPR1 liquid medium with appropriate selection agent was added to the plates to ensure that all explants had full contact with the liquid medium after co-cultivation (Figure 2I). Various concentrations of selection agents were evaluated. Our results revealed that the optimal concentrations of glyphosate and BSM for the EPSPS and ALS genes are 100 and 2 μM, respectively (data not shown). The hypocotyls and cotyledons of the explants rapidly developed at 25°C under light in 2 days (Figure 2J). To help with easy transplantation into soil, 25 developed explants were placed in a SteriCon-8 culture vessel, and about 25 mL of SoySiPR1 medium with appropriate herbicide was added to promote transgenic shoot meristem formation with 5 additional days of pre-selection (Figure 2K). It is important to make sure that the wounded region of the explants is in direct contact with the selection medium. Explants were ready to be transplanted into soil for continuous selection by daily spraying with an appropriate concentration of herbicide under light (Figure 2L). It is highly recommended that the seedlings be kept under high humidity to promote healthy development of the transgenic shoots in the early stage. This can be achieved by covering the tray with a clear plastic dome after daily spraying or by placing it in a high-humidity room. Optimal concentrations of herbicides are 2 μM BSM or 3 μM imazapyr for the ALS selectable marker gene and 100 μM glyphosate for the EPSPS selectable marker gene. Three weeks after transplantation into soil in the greenhouse, the transgenic shoots were fully developed and ready for sampling for a TaqMan transgene copy-number assay (Figure 2M). Transgenic shoots developed without apparent abnormal phenotypes and could easily be distinguished from chimeras and escapes. Chimeras had yellow and green leaves in the same shoot or showed chimeric sectors in a transgenic leaf (Figure 2N), whereas non-transgenic escapes usually had small and narrow leaves. The transgenic plants (shoots) were identified by TaqMan assay and transplanted to large pots for growth to maturity and seed setting. The process timeline from explant preparation to transgenic event recovery is summarized in Figure 3. This new in planta method for transgenic event production is very fast: the total duration required is only about 35–45 days from explant preparation to identification of transgenic plants with the TaqMan assay and transplantation to large pots.

In planta selection of elite varieties with different herbicides

The soybean variety 06KG was used to compare the transformation frequency of the new in planta method described above with that of the fast in vitro method (Liang et al., 2023) using both ALS and EPSPS selectable markers. The transformation results with four different constructs, 23093, 18891, 21365, and 22223 (Figure 1), are summarized in Figure 4 and Supplemental Table 1. Experiments were performed in three replicates with a total of 406–740 explants per vector. Only one transgenic event from each target explant was allowed for calculation of transformation frequency. The results indicated that the in planta method had comparable or better transformation frequency than the in vitro method for constructs with different transfer DNA (T-DNA) sizes using both ALS and EPSPS markers (Figure 4A; Supplemental Table 1). The in planta method also demonstrated comparable or lower escape frequency than the in vitro transformation method (Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 1).

A group of additional varieties representing different maturity groups was evaluated for transformation competency using the EPSPS selectable marker construct 23093 or the ALS selectable marker construct 18891 with the in planta method, and the results are summarized in Supplemental Figure 1. The results demonstrated that the in planta method can be applied to various varieties for both EPSPS and ALS selection with good transformation frequency. Among the nine varieties tested for both selectable markers, eight showed better or comparable transformation frequency with the ALS selectable marker than with the EPSPS selectable marker. No or few escapes were present with either selectable marker using the in planta method, indicating the effectiveness of the new in planta transformation process. Because the new in planta transformation approach enables fast and genotype-flexible transgenic event generation, it is also called the GiFT method.

In planta transformation of a broad range of elite soybean germplasms

Many elite soybean varieties are recalcitrant to in vitro transformation and exhibit poor culture response, including low regeneration and shoot elongation and even tissue death, leading to low transformation frequency. Some varieties also show precocious flowering in culture and in soil, probably due to stress response. Elite varieties with such poor in vitro responses were selected for testing with the GiFT method using the EPSPS or ALS selectable marker, and the results are shown in Supplemental Figure 2. Each experiment used about 100 explants. In in vitro transformation with the EPSPS selectable marker, the elite variety BA017 transformed with construct 23093 had about 10% transformation frequency and a low escape rate but showed a precocious flowering phenotype in culture and in soil after transplantation (Supplemental Figure 2A). Three elite varieties, DD010, DD228, and XK611, had less than 3% transformation frequency, and DD228 and XK611 also showed a high escape rate with vector 21365. When these four varieties were tested with the GiFT method using the same constructs, transformation frequency was significantly improved to 11%–19%, and escape frequency was dramatically reduced (Supplemental Figure 2A). Transgenic plants developed normally, and no precocious flowering was observed in any of the four varieties.

Eight elite varieties that showed various levels of recalcitrance in our previous in vitro experiments (results not shown) were selected to compare transformation using ALS marker vector 22223. Among these eight varieties, three (DD938, DD152, and DH616) had good transformation frequency but a large number of escapes, two (DD547 and DH628) had low transformation frequency, two (WR520 and WR538) had a prolonged duration of shoot elongation, and three (DD938, DH616, and BS695) had stressed shoots with precocious flowering. However, good transformation frequency was obtained in these recalcitrant varieties using the GiFT method, and the escape frequency was low (Supplemental Figure 2B).

To validate the genotype flexibility of the GiFT method, numerous varieties from different maturity groups were tested with constructs 23093, 18891, 21365, and 22223. The test results are summarized in Table 1. Our results demonstrated that the GiFT method was adaptable to all varieties tested, with 2%–30% transformation frequency depending on the selectable marker and T-DNA size (Table 1). It should be noted that some commercial varieties may already be resistant to BSM or glyphosate owing to the presence of native STS (sulfonylurea tolerance soybean), SR (sulfonylurea ready), or Roundup Ready traits. For these varieties, the right marker should be chosen, or alternative herbicide-resistance markers such as BAR, PAT, or HPPD should be considered.

Inheritance of transgenes by progenies

Single-copy, backbone-free T0 transgenic events identified by TaqMan assay were transplanted to large pots for further growth and seed production in the greenhouse. To determine transgene inheritance by T1 progeny, expression of the AmCyan gene was analyzed in immature embryos (T1) from seven elite varieties transformed with construct 18891 or 23093. Immature embryos were isolated from each event and examined under UV light for AmCyan expression (Figure 2O and 2P). The results are summarized in Supplemental Table 2. The root tips of germinated T1 seeds from 10 elite varieties transformed with construct 21365 or 22223 were subjected to TaqMan assay to evaluate the inheritance of EPSPS or ALS, respectively. The results are shown in Supplemental Table 3. The results indicated that the transgenes were heritable at a high rate (>90% on average) in events recovered from the GiFT method. There was a rate of less than 10% for periclinal chimeric transgenic events for both ALS and EPSPS selectable markers, which is comparable to the chimera rate for the in vitro transformation method. A χ2 analysis indicated that inheritance of both AmCyan expression and selectable marker genes followed Mendelian inheritance as a single dominant locus (3:1 segregation ratio) in more than 90% of the heritable T0 events.

The GiFT method has been extensively used in our GM trait research and development efforts. An example is its application to the characterization of a pair of atypical nucleotide-binding leucine-rich-encoding genes that confer Asian soybean rust (ASR) resistance in soybean (Hao et al., 2024), in which homozygous T2 or T3 plants from three T0 events harboring the Rpp6907-7 R gene demonstrated nearly complete immunity to all 14 tested ASR populations. Inheritance of the R gene transgenes was confirmed by TaqMan assay (Supplemental Table 4), along with ASR resistance efficacy in both T2 and T3 generations.

Application of the GiFT method in GE

A comparison experiment for LbCas12a-mediated GE was performed with the soybean line 06KG and the ALS selection system, using both in vitro transformation (Liang et al., 2023) and GiFT methods. Supplemental Table 5 shows the transformation frequency and gene editing rate obtained via TaqMan assay from three GE constructs, all of which targeted a single-copy unique genomic target sequence. The results demonstrated that the GiFT method had high editing efficiency despite showing a slight reduction compared with the in vitro method. The lower transformation frequency observed in two of the three GiFT experiments was probably due to variation when the method was still in optimization.

The inheritance of mutations from primary transformants (T0, aka primary edited mutants E0) to E1 progeny (first-generation progeny of E0 edited lines) was first assessed by TaqMan assay on the target site, and mutations in E1 progeny lines of biallelic mutant events were confirmed by deep sequencing analysis (see Supplemental Table 6 for quantitative real-time PCR [qPCR] and NGS results for a portion of these events and their progeny). A total of 38, 5, and 4 biallelic E0 mutants transformed with constructs 26357, 27962, and 27963, respectively, were retained for seed setting. The E0 plants were self-pollinated to produce E1 seeds. Twenty E1 seeds from each event were germinated in the greenhouse and sampled for TaqMan assays and sequence analysis. All E1 progeny from 37, 4, and 4 biallelic E0 mutants of 26357, 27962, and 27963 had a biallelic mutation, indicating that the edits were stably inherited by the progeny plants. However, it is likely that the edits in two events were non-heritable chimeras. One event from 26357 (SYKE212907A007A) did not have a detectable target site mutation and transgene insertion in any of its progeny. Another T0 event, SYET224607A008A from vector 27962, was also likely a chimera because none of the 20 progeny lines contained a transgene, but interestingly, one of the progeny plants contained a gene edit (Supplemental Table 6). We further selected some plants for deep sequencing to characterize the target sequence mutations (Supplemental Table 6). Mutations were present in many LbCas12a-negative segregants, e.g., plant 2 of SYET224610A001A, both plants of SYET224610A006A and SYET224610A013A, etc. (Table S6). These gene edits in LbCas12a transgene-null segregants should be fixed already. In some LbCas12a-positive plants, the mutations were also fixed, as they could no longer be recognized and cleaved by LbCas12a (plant 1 of SYET224610A001A and plant 2 of SYET224610A015A). However, several haplotypes could be identified in LbCas12a-positive progeny plants of SYET224607A009A and SYET224607A016A, suggesting that even though the mutations at the T0 stage were identified as “biallelic mutations” by the TaqMan assay, the edited target sequences could still be recognized and further edited by the LbCas12a expressed in the progeny plants.

Discussion

The conventional in vitro-based soybean transformation method is a highly genotype-dependent and labor-intensive process despite its high efficiency in selected varieties (Xu et al., 2020; Liang et al., 2023). In planta transformation, on the other hand, bypasses the complexities associated with tissue culture and holds enormous potential for streamlining the soybean transformation process (Jan et al., 2016; Niazian et al., 2017; Saifi et al., 2020). In extensive efforts over the past three decades, researchers have struggled to develop efficient in planta transformation methods specifically for soybean (Hu and Wang, 1999; Liu et al., 2009; Mangena, 2019). Most of these efforts did not yield positive outcomes. By contrast, successful in planta transformation has been reported in diverse plant species (Supartana et al., 2005; Kojima et al., 2006; Razzaq et al., 2011; Mayavan et al., 2015; Kesiraju and Sreevathsa, 2017). To establish an efficient and robust soybean transformation process that is tissue-culture free and highly genotype flexible, two factors must be considered: (1) a target tissue that is highly receptive for DNA delivery and has efficient regeneration competency across different genotypes and (2) a selection method for effective transformant recovery and reliable transgene inheritance.

Competency of the target explants for effective transformation and efficient regeneration is the prerequisite for establishing an efficient and genotype-flexible transformation process. The seedling apical meristem is a common target for gene delivery in in planta transformation (Kesiraju et al., 2021; Ganguly et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2018). This technique necessitates puncturing or wounding of the meristematic regions, followed by Agrobacterium infection. However, this approach typically results in transformation of only a few cells within the meristematic area, leading to a high degree of chimeric transformation. The shoot apical meristem is composed of densely packed cells, with only a few undifferentiated stem cells distributed across different zones (Agata, 2021). Consequently, transformation of these stem cells poses a technical challenge, leading to inefficiency in heritable transformation. In addition, this method often results in independent transformation events occurring in different cells within a single meristem. As a result, meticulous screening must be undertaken in subsequent generations to identify stable and heritable transformation lines. The inherent inefficiency and the additional efforts required for screening render this approach less suitable for the large-scale transformation required for commercial applications. Half-seeds were also successfully demonstrated to be excellent explants when coupled with selection for in planta transformation in certain plants such as peanuts and watermelon (Karthik et al., 2018; Vasudevan et al., 2021). This method involves wounding the cotyledonary area of germinated seeds by removing one cotyledon. The wounded explants are then infected with an Agrobacterium strain containing a binary vector with a plant selection marker gene. Subsequently, the infected explants are placed on a selection medium gel to suppress non-transgenic cells and promote the proliferation of transformed cells engaged in organogenesis for the development of transformed buds. This approach significantly reduces the occurrence of chimeric and non-transformed plants. However, the seedling generation process is performed on a gel medium, resulting in procedures that closely resemble in vitro transformation. Consequently, the throughput of this method is somewhat limited because of its similarities to in vitro transformation operational processes. Recently, in planta transformation has also been reported in sweet potato and a few other species through transformation of stem cuttings or root tissues and subsequent generation of transgenic plants from transformed tissues (Cao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). However, this technique works only for plants that exhibit efficient root or bud regeneration from cuttings or roots. For most plant species that lack root suckering ability, regeneration of shoots from the root system is extremely difficult and impractical. To date, with the exception of a few plant species such as Arabidopsis (Bent, 2000; Tague and Mantis, 2006), reported in planta transformation methods for crop species have generally yielded low rates of successful transformation and transgene heritability. In our initial experiments, we aimed to test whether in planta transformation could be achieved with cotyledon axillary region cells using seedlings germinated in soil. We used a procedure that involved wounding of the axillary area and infection with Agrobacterium, followed by a 3-week selection process using cotton balls soaked in herbicide agents to suppress non-transgenic cell divisions. Our observations of vigorous adventitious shoot regeneration from the wounded cotyledon axillary region in soil-grown soybean seedlings further support the potential of this tissue as an ideal target for in planta transformation in soybean. Although we successfully generated T0 transgenic events, the overall transformation frequency remained low, approximately 5%, primarily because of inefficient Agrobacterium infection and variation in genotype responses under in planta conditions. On the other hand, our conventional in vitro transformation method using explants containing epicotyl regions prepared from germinated seeds as target tissues demonstrated consistent and efficient Agrobacterium-mediated transformation across various soybean varieties (Liang et al., 2023). The epicotyl region encompasses several tissues, including the cotyledon axillary meristem, its adjacent area, the primary leaf axillary meristem, and the shoot meristem. In efforts to overcome low transformation frequency in in planta transformation, numerous experiments were performed to adapt target explants from the in vitro transformation process into in planta transformation through optimization of the Agrobacterium infection conditions and the regeneration capacity of target tissues. Our observations indicated that the regeneration response from different tissues in the epicotyl region varied greatly among different varieties under tissue culture conditions. Our work on in vitro transformation also indicated that transformation frequency was reduced by more than 50% without mechanical wounding or sonication-assisted wounding of the explants after primary leaf removal (results not shown). The application of previously optimized explant-preparation and gene-delivery conditions for the in vitro method (Liang et al., 2023) to in planta transformation is critical to the high transformation efficiency of our GiFT method.

An effective selection system is crucial for promoting the regeneration of transgenic shoots, ensuring the survival of the mother plant and allowing for the growth of non-transgenic roots in fast soybean transformation. The nature of organogenesis gives rise to both transformed and non-transformed tissues during the regeneration process, and ineffective selection can hinder the formation of transgenic shoot meristems and result in the regeneration of non-transgenic or chimeric shoots. We evaluated various strategies for robust selection and developed an effective system consisting of two steps: a short period of in vitro pre-selection in liquid medium, followed by soil-based in planta selection. The in vitro pre-selection in adequate liquid medium containing an herbicide selection agent inhibits the growth of non-transformed cells, facilitates vigorous organogenesis from transgenic cells, and eliminates the formation of chimeric shoots. The soil-based in planta selection with daily spraying of liquid selection medium further suppresses the regeneration of non-transgenic shoot meristems while promoting the growth of transgenic shoots. The presence of a well-developed “untransformed” root system from the original seedlings and the nutrients supplied by the attached cotyledon enable the rapid growth and development of the transformed shoots. By combining in vitro pre-selection with soil-based in planta selection, we successfully developed a well-balanced, comprehensive selection strategy that fully utilizes the advantages of the explant.

The GiFT method is highly genotype flexible and has been demonstrated to work in diverse elite soybean varieties (Table 1). To date, we have successfully transformed over 50 elite soybean varieties representing diverse genetic backgrounds with this GiFT transformation process. We have also used an automated herbicide spray system for high-throughput production of events (results not shown). Transformation events were generated in all tested elite varieties with comparable or better transformation frequency and low escape rate compared with the in vitro transformation process (Figure 4). In particular, the method works for recalcitrant varieties, resulting in good event quality. Molecular analysis revealed that non-heritable periclinal chimeric transgenic events amounted to less than 10% (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). Analysis of T1 segregation data revealed that more than 90% of single-copy T0 events were consistent with the Mendelian segregation pattern of single-gene integration. We also demonstrated the application of the GiFT protocol to GE for highly efficient targeted mutagenesis (Supplemental Table 5). In addition, edited mutations in the primary transformants (T0) were transmitted to the progeny (T1) at a high rate (Supplemental Table 6). It should be noted that the mutant target sequences are fixed in progeny lines without the LbCas12a transgene. On the other hand, if the LbCas12a transgene is present in T1 progeny plants, the target sequence can be further edited if it can still be recognized by LbCas12a (Supplemental Table S6). The ability to directly transform diverse elite varieties without laborious tissue culture offers a more streamlined and efficient approach for both GM and GE research efforts.

The GiFT method is much faster, has higher efficiency, and has a much simpler operation compared with the conventional in vitro transformation method. Typically, the conventional in vitro method requires 50–90 days from initiation of transformation to obtain transgenic plants and approximately 5–6 months to obtain transgenic T1 seeds; by contrast, the GiFT method reduced the time to 35 days for recovering T0 plants (Figure 3) and 120 days for obtaining T1 seeds. Whereas the conventional in vitro method is tedious, time consuming, and limited in throughput, involving multiple tissue culture steps and vast amounts of lab consumables and human labor, the GiFT process replaces all subculture steps with one step of adding adequate liquid medium. The soil-based in planta selection enables large-scale synchronized event generation through the spraying of selective agents. The implementation of an automated spraying system, which requires minimal attendance during the 3-week selection period, further enhances the throughput and efficiency.

In summary, development of the efficient and fast GiFT soybean process marks a significant advance in in planta transformation. By addressing the challenges of low efficiency and high chimera rate that have hindered the practical use of in planta transformation in crops for over three decades, we have achieved a breakthrough. This transformation process offers several notable advantages, including high genotype flexibility, high throughput, high capacity, and cost efficiency. The GiFT method represents the first industry-scale in planta transformation method for a major field crop. This novel transformation approach opens up new possibilities for direct transformation of elite crop varieties, making it particularly well suited to GE applications. Furthermore, the concept of the GiFT method can also be applied to other crops, extending its potential impact beyond soybean.

Methods

Agrobacterium strain and constructs

The A. tumefaciens strain used in this study was Chry5d3 recA− (Chen et al., 2019). Four binary vectors, 23093, 18891, 21365, and 22223, were used for development of the transformation method, and three constructs containing different LbCas12a variants, 26357, 27962, and 27963, were used for GE evaluation. All three gene editing vectors expressed the same gRNA (5′–AGATA GGACG CATGC AGGT TGGT3′) targeting a single-copy chromosomal target sequence. The T-DNA size and components for each construct are illustrated in Figure 1, and sequences of selectable marker gene cassettes are shown in Supplemental Sequence File 1.

Agrobacterium cells were streaked from glycerol stock stored at −80°C onto YPC (5 g/l yeast extract, 10 g/l peptone, 5 g/l NaCl, 1 g/l calcium chloride dehydrate, 15 g/l Bacto agar [pH 6.8]) plates containing appropriate antibiotics and grown in a 23°C incubator. Before explant inoculation, Agrobacterium cells were collected from the plate and uniformly suspended in liquid infection medium (SoyInf) in a sterile disposable 50-ml centrifuge tube and diluted to an A660 of approximately 0.3–0.4. Final concentrations of 80 mg/l acetosyringone and 150 μg/ml dithiothreitol were added to induce virulence gene expression. The Agrobacterium suspension was incubated at 23°C for at least 30 min before use in explant infection. SoyInf contains 1.1 g/l MS basal salt mixture, 20 g/l sucrose, 10 g/l glucose, 4 g/l MES, 1 ml/l Gamborg’s B5 vitamins (1000×), and 2 mg/l zeatin riboside.

Target tissue preparation

Soybean seeds of different Syngenta elite varieties were sterilized, and the target tissue was prepared as described previously in a detailed protocol (Liang et al., 2023). In brief, healthy seeds were selected for transformation and fully imbibed in sterile water at 22°C–24°C overnight for about 16 h. The seed coat was removed without trimming the hypocotyl. Cotyledon explants with epicotyls were prepared by removing and discarding one of the cotyledons. The primary leaves (leaf primordia) were removed from the explants with a scalpel. The region containing the apical meristem and the cot-node were further wounded with the sharp end of the scalpel blade or by sonication-assisted wounding as described below.

Infection and co-cultivation of soybean seed explants

Infection of explants was performed in the presence of an Agrobacterium suspension. Explants wounded with a scalpel blade were immediately immersed in the prepared Agrobacterium suspension. When wounding was performed by sonication, about 100–200 explants were immersed in 50 ml of Agrobacterium suspension and treated at 45 kHz for 30 s in a SteriCon-8 culture vessel (C 2118, Phytotech Labs) using a W-113 ultrasonic multi-cleaner (HONDA Electronics). Explants were incubated in Agrobacterium suspension for at least 30 min at room temperature. Following infection, the explants were removed from the Agrobacterium suspension. About 100 explants were placed adaxial side up directly on each 25 × 150 mm Petri dish with no medium for incubation (co-cultivation). The Petri dishes with explants were placed in an airtight plastic container and incubated at 23°C in the dark for 4–6 days.

Recovery and in vitro pre-selection

The in vitro transformation process was performed as described in a previous publication (Liang et al., 2023). For the GiFT method, approximately 25 ml of SoySiPR1 liquid medium (3.1 g/l Gamborg’s B5 basal medium, 1× ferrous sulfate chelate solution [Sigma-Aldrich F0518], 1× Gamborg’s B5 vitamins, 1 g/l MES, 100 mg/l glutamine, 100 mg/l asparagine, 300 mg/l Timentin, and 2 mg/l BAP), with or without a corresponding selection agent, was first added to just cover the explants in the co-cultivation plates after co-cultivation. The cultures were then incubated at 25°C under a light intensity of 60–80 μE/m2/s with a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for 2 days. After recovery, approximately 25 ml of SoySiPR1 with appropriate selection agent was added to the culture plates. For easy transplanting into soil, about 25 explants from the culture plates were transferred to each SteriCon-8 culture vessel before approximately 25 ml of SoySiPR1 was added with appropriate selection agents. The culture plates or vessels were then incubated at 25°C under 60–80 μE/m2/s light intensity with a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for 5 days before being transplanted into soil for further selection. An appropriate concentration of selection agent (100 μM glyphosate for the EPSPS marker gene and 2 μM BSM for the ALS marker gene) was added to the SoySiPR1 medium before use.

In planta selection and recovery of transgenic plants

Following pre-selection, the infected germinated seedlings were transplanted into soil for additional selection and growth of transgenic shoots. The soil used was Sun Gro Fafard-3 (BFG Supply, Burton, OH, USA), which possesses excellent aeration capabilities. Before transplantation, the soil in the flats was saturated with water, and excess water was allowed to drain. The seedlings were transplanted into the soil, covering the roots and part of the hypocotyl. The cotyledon was oriented to face upward so as to ensure that the wounded areas were exposed to the selection agent for spray application. After transplantation, the flats containing seedlings were covered with a plastic dome and placed under 16-h light (150–250 μE/m2/s light intensity) at about 26°C with about 90% humidity in a growth chamber (Figure 2L). The regeneration areas of the seedlings were sprayed daily with SoySiP2 herbicide selection solution for the first week after transplantation and SoySiP3 for the second and third weeks. The SoySiP2 selection solution contained 1 mg/l MES, 2 mg/l BAP, and the appropriate selection agent: 100 μM glyphosate for vectors with the EPSPS marker gene and 2 μM BSM or 3 μM imazapyr (Ecomazapyr 2SL, Alligare) for vectors with the ALS marker gene. SoySiP3 was the same as SoySiP2 except that BAP was reduced from 2 to 1 mg/l. Approximately 50 ml of SoySiP2 or SoySip3 selection solution was used for each flat containing 76 to 100 seedlings. The seedlings were covered with the dome after each application of the selection solution. Weekend applications can be skipped.

After the 3 weeks of herbicide selection spray, the regenerated shoots were visually inspected and sorted. Explants without regenerated shoots were discarded. A single healthy shoot per explant was retained, and extra shoots were removed if present. To prevent potential elongation of new non-transgenic shoots after completion of herbicide selection, additional soil can be added to cover the cotyledon and cotyledon node. After sorting of the regenerated shoots, plants were watered with liquid fertilizer (Flora fertilizer solution) and covered again for an additional 1–2 days. The plants were then moved to a growth chamber with 250 μmol/m2/s or higher light intensity to promote plant growth.

Transgenic plant identification and confirmation

Leaf punches were sampled from putative transgenic plants 7 days after sorting and subjected to a qPCR TaqMan assay for gene copy number analysis (Ingham et al., 2001) for the presence of the gene of interest, such as a selectable marker gene, binary vector backbone, visible marker gene, trait genes, and LbCas12a, and for editing of the genomic target sequence(s). TaqMan assay targets and primer and probe sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 7. The transformation and escape frequencies were calculated from the number of positive or negative events of the selectable marker gene as determined by the qPCR TaqMan assay divided by the total valid number of explants. Target site status (wild type or mono- or biallelic mutation) was determined by a qPCR assay with a TaqMan probe spanning the LbCas12a cleavage site (Supplemental Table 7), and the detailed mutant target sequence was determined by Illumina deep sequencing as described previously (Dong et al., 2021).

Plant growth to maturity and inheritance of the transgene

After TaqMan analysis, backbone-free single-copy transgenic or genome-edited plants were selected for transplantation to large pots for seed production. For events transformed with construct 18891 or 23093 containing the AmCyan gene, immature embryos of T0 plants were isolated from 10 pods per event and examined under UV light for expression of CFP after removal of the seed coat. For events transformed with constructs 21365 and 22223, T1 seeds harvested from independent transgenic plants were germinated in paper towels, and root tips from T1 germinated seeds were sampled and subjected to TaqMan analysis for presence of the EPSPS or ALS gene.

Statistical analysis

Data from transformation experiments (means and standard deviations) were analyzed using Student’s t-test. For differences in transformation frequency and escape rate between two methods, P < 0.05 was considered to be significant. Inheritance and segregation of transgenes were analyzed by χ2 test for the difference from the expected ratio of 3:1 at P < 0.05 with 1 degree of freedom.
