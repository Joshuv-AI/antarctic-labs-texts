Research trends and hotspots of CAR-T cell therapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia: A bibliometric analysis
Authors: Xiaolong Tian, Yingping Zhang, Chen Zhang, Dongdong Liu, Yanmin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to analyze the global research patterns and emerging trends in CAR-T cell therapy for ALL through a bibliometric analysis. Publications were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection database. The bibliometric analysis utilized VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and the R package “bibliometrix” to visualize collaborations, keyword co-occurrences, and emerging research trends. A total of 844 articles from 253 journals by 6,459 authors across 44 countries were analyzed, showing an annual growth rate of 40.08%. The USA (372 articles, 38,065 citations) and China (275 articles, 5,636 citations) dominated research output. The University of Pennsylvania (353 articles), Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (170), and Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (131) were the most productive institutions, while Blood (41 articles) published the most articles. Stephan A. Grupp (38 articles, H-index = 30), Carl H. June (26 articles, H-index = 24), and Shannon L. Maude (25 articles, H-index = 20) were the most influential authors. Keyword analysis revealed five research clusters, including basic mechanisms, CAR design, population treatment integration, clinical outcomes, and toxicity management. Burst keyword analysis showed the evolution from basic science (2010–2013) to clinical translation (2014–2016), toxicity management (2017–2019), and recently to long-term outcomes and risk assessment (2020–2024), with “term follow-up” and “risk” as the only keywords with active bursts in 2024. This bibliometric analysis reveals that research on CAR-T therapy for ALL has progressed from foundational concepts to clinical implementation and now focuses on optimizing long-term outcomes and patient selection. Future research should prioritize biomarker development, next-generation CAR designs, and combination strategies to overcome persistence, toxicity, and resistance limitations in ALL treatment.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is an aggressive hematological malignancy characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature lymphoid cells in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and extramedullary sites.1 It represents approximately 25% of childhood cancers and accounts for 75%–80% of pediatric leukemias, with a second incidence peak in adults over 50 y.2 Conventional treatment approaches involve multi-agent chemotherapy regimens, potentially supplemented with radiation therapy and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) for high-risk patients.3 While pediatric ALL has seen remarkable improvements in outcomes with 5-y overall survival rates exceeding 90%, adult ALL continues to have a more challenging prognosis with survival rates of 30%–40%.4 Patients with relapsed or refractory disease face particularly poor outcomes, with 5-y survival rates below 30%.5 This therapeutic challenge has driven the exploration of novel targeted approaches, including immunotherapies, to overcome chemoresistance and improve long-term outcomes while reducing treatment-related toxicities.6

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy represents a groundbreaking advancement in cancer immunotherapy for hematological malignancies.7 This approach involves genetically engineering a patient’s T cells to express synthetic receptors capable of recognizing specific tumor-associated antigens, most commonly CD19 for B-cell malignancies.8 Following ex vivo expansion, these modified T cells are reinfused into the patient to specifically target and eliminate cancer cells expressing the target antigen.9 The remarkable efficacy of CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy in B-cell ALL was first demonstrated in clinical trials, reporting unprecedented complete remission rates of 70%–90% in heavily pretreated patients.10 These promising results led to the FDA approval of tisagenlecleucel in 2017 for pediatric and young adult patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell ALL.11 Since then, research has expanded to focus on enhancing efficacy, mitigating toxicities, and exploring next-generation CAR designs, alternative targets, and combinatorial strategies.12,13 Despite growing interest in CAR-T cell therapy for ALL, no comprehensive study has systematically summarized the research trends and focal points in this area.

Bibliometrics provides a quantitative framework for analyzing the structure, evolution, and impact of scientific research.14 Previous bibliometric studies have separately examined research landscapes in acute leukemia15 and broader aspects of cancer immunotherapy.16 Despite the clinical significance and rapidly expanding literature on CAR-T therapy specifically for ALL, a comprehensive bibliometric analysis focusing on this particular therapeutic application is lacking. This study aims to analyze the global research patterns and emerging trends in CAR-T cell therapy for ALL through a bibliometric analysis.

Materials and methods

Data collection and search strategy

A systematic search was performed in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), a multidisciplinary database specializing in high-quality, peer-reviewed publications such as journal articles, conference proceedings, and books. WoSCC also provides a comprehensive citation network and key bibliometric indicators essential for analysis.17 The literature search was conducted on December 31, 2024, covering articles published from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2024. The search formula was as follows: TS = ((CAR-T OR “CAR T cell” OR “CAR-T cell” OR “CAR T-cell” OR CAR-T-cell OR “chimeric antigen receptor T cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor-T cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor T-cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor-modified T-cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor-transduced T-cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor-redirected T cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor redirecting T-cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor engineered T cell” OR “chimeric antigen receptor-engineered T-cell”) AND (“acute lymphoblastic leukemia” OR “acute lymphocytic leukemia”) AND TS = (treat* OR therap*)).18,19 The inclusion criteria were: 1) English language; 2) published between January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2024; 3) eligible articles on CAR-T therapy for ALL identified via the search strategy. Additionally, item types such as reviews, editorials, letters, and meeting abstracts were excluded. The inclusion of reviews, editorials, and letters would artificially inflate citation metrics and conflate novel research fronts with retrospective commentary, thereby obscuring the true intellectual trajectory; conference abstracts were removed due to incomplete metadata and unreliable citation indexing, while their inclusion could compromise the accuracy of co-citation networks. The information extracted from the selected studies included country/region, institutions, journals, publication and citation counts, authors, and keywords.

Statistical analysis and visualization

Bibliometric analysis was performed using three complementary software tools, VOSviewer (version 1.6.20),20 CiteSpace (version 6.3.R1),21 and the R package “bibliometrix” (version 4.3.3).22 VOSviewer was primarily employed for constructing and visualizing bibliometric networks, including co-authorship, co-occurrence, and citation networks.20 CiteSpace was utilized for detecting and visualizing emerging trends and critical transitions in the research field, particularly for keyword burst detection and timeline visualization.21 The R package “bibliometrix” was used for comprehensive bibliometric analysis, including productivity metrics, collaboration patterns, and impact assessment.22

Scientific impact was evaluated using several bibliometric indicators: the H-index, which measures productivity and citation impact (where h publications have at least h citations);23,24 the G-index, which gives more weight to highly cited articles (the largest number g where g publications have at least g2 citations);25 and the M-index, which normalizes the H-index by dividing it by the number of years since the author’s first publication.26 Journal impact was assessed using Journal Citation Reports (JCR) quartile rankings (Q1: top 25%, Q2: 25%–50%, Q3: 50%–75%, Q4: bottom 25%) and Impact Factor (IF), which represents the average number of citations received by articles published in the journal during the preceding 2 y.27

Results

Overview of publications

The literature search and screening process identified 844 articles from 1,768 initially retrieved studies after removing reviews (n = 655), editorial materials (n = 36), letters (n = 44), meeting abstracts (n = 160), non-English publications (n = 25), and other document types (e.g., correction; proceeding paper; retracted publication) (n = 104) (Figure 1). The temporal distribution of publications on CAR-T cell therapy for ALL showed a distinctive growth pattern from 2010 to 2024. Publications remained minimal from 2010 to 2012 with only one article per year, then began a slow increase from 2013 (3 articles) to 2016 (20 articles), followed by a steep acceleration from 2017 (31 articles) to a peak in 2022 (157 articles), with a moderate decline in 2023 (129 articles) and 2024 (112 articles) (Figure 2). Analysis of keywords

The keyword co-occurrence network visualization revealed five distinct clusters representing different research dimensions: (1) basic mechanisms and cell engineering (red cluster), featuring terms such as “activation,” “adoptive transfer,” “antigen,” “CD8(+),” “cell therapy,” “cytotoxicity,” “differentiation,” “gene,” and “memory”; (2) CAR design and target selection (green cluster), containing keywords such as “4-1bb,” “4-1bb costimulation,” “acute lymphoblastic-leukemia,” “adoptive immunotherapy,” “antitumor efficacy,” “B-cell,” “chimeric antigen receptor,” “CD28 costimulation,” and “domains”; (3) patient demographics and treatment combinations (blue cluster), encompassing “adolescents,” “adult patients,” “blinatumomab,” “blood,” “chemotherapy,” “childhood,” “children,” “clofarabine,” “combination,” and “cyclophosphamide” (4); clinical outcomes and follow-up (yellow cluster), with terms such as “acute lymphocytic-leukemia,” “adult B-cell,” “antibody,” “bone-marrow-transplantation,” “clinical-trials,” “diagnosis,” “donor,” “follow-up,” “minimal residual disease,” and “quality-of-life”; and (5) toxicity management and product specification (purple cluster), featuring “axicabtagene ciloleucel,” “biomarkers,” “CAR-T,” “CD19,” “central-nervous-system,” “cytokine release syndrome,” “disease,” “leukemia,” “lymphoblastic-leukemia,” “management,” “neurotoxicity,” and “outcomes” (Figure 7(A)). Analysis of burst keywords

This temporal pattern aligned with the burst detection analysis, which identified keywords with sudden increases in occurrence frequency. The most significant citation burst belonged to “adoptive immunotherapy” (strength = 7.56, 2010–2019), followed by “B cell” (strength = 8.16, 2017–2019), “antitumor activity” (strength = 6.26, 2010–2018), and “in vivo” (strength = 6.3, 2011–2018). Recent burst keywords (2021–2024) included “term follow up” (strength = 2.79) and “risk” (strength = 3.1) (Figure 7(B)).

Discussion

This study analyzed 844 articles on CAR-T cell therapy for ALL published between 2010 and 2024. The USA and China emerged as the leading countries in CAR-T therapy for ALL, with institutions such as the University of Pennsylvania, and Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center being major contributors. The dominance of the USA and China can be attributed to substantial investments in CAR-T research and drug development, as well as the presence of renowned experts and research centers in these countries.28,29 The most prolific journals publishing research in this field were Blood, Blood Advances, Leukemia, and Clinical Cancer Research. These high-impact hematology and oncology journals have been at the forefront of disseminating cutting-edge research on CAR-T therapies for ALL. Blood, with the highest number of publications and citations, has played a particularly prominent role in advancing this field.30 Prominent authors such as Stephan A. Grupp, Carl H. June, and Shannon L. Maude have made significant contributions to the field, as evidenced by their high publication and citation counts. Their research has focused on the development, efficacy, and safety of CAR-T therapies for pediatric and adult ALL patients.31,32

Research hotspots and frontier trends

Keyword co-occurrence analysis provides valuable insights into the intellectual structure and evolving research focuses within a field. Our visualization of keyword co-occurrence in CAR-T cell therapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia research revealed five distinct clusters, each representing different research dimensions and frontier trends.

Cluster 1 (red): basic mechanisms and cellular engineering

Cluster 1 encompasses fundamental research on the cellular and molecular aspects of CAR-T cell therapy, featuring keywords such as “activation,” “adoptive transfer,” “antigen,” “CD8(+),” “cell therapy,” “cytotoxicity,” “differentiation,” “gene,” and “memory.” This cluster represents the mechanistic foundation of CAR-T therapy, focusing on T cell biology, genetic engineering, and preclinical development. Early work by Milone et al. established the fundamental concept of genetic redirection of T cells against CD19-expressing malignancies,33 while subsequent studies by Long et al. explored how different costimulatory domains affect T cell persistence and functionality.34 The keyword “memory” reflects the growing recognition that formation of memory T cell subsets is crucial for long-term CAR-T efficacy, with studies by Fraietta et al. demonstrating that persistence of CAR-T cells with memory phenotypes correlates with durable clinical responses.35 The inclusion of “naive” highlights research into how starting T cell phenotypes influence final product efficacy, with Sommermeyer et al. showing that defined composition products derived from naive and memory T cells exhibit superior potency.36 Keywords such as “inhibition” and “resistant” indicate ongoing challenges in overcoming immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments and antigen escape mechanisms, with cutting-edge approaches including development of logic-gated CARs and combination with checkpoint inhibitors as described by Chong et al.37 The presence of “clinical-trial” and “phase-1” underscores the translational nature of this research cluster, with continuous feedback between laboratory discoveries and clinical observations driving iterative improvements in CAR design and manufacturing.

Cluster 2 (green): CAR design and target selection

Cluster 2 focuses on CAR design and target selection, containing keywords such as “4-1bb,” “4-1bb costimulation,” “acute lymphoblastic-leukemia,” “adoptive immunotherapy,” “antitumor efficacy,” “B-cell,” “chimeric antigen receptor,” “CD28 costimulation,” and “domains.” This cluster represents the engineering aspects and molecular targeting of CAR-T technology. The prominence of “4-1bb costimulation” and “CD28 costimulation” reflects the dichotomy in signaling domain approaches, with pivotal work by Kawalekar et al. demonstrating that these domains differentially affect T cell metabolism and memory formation.38 The inclusion of both “acute lymphoblastic-leukemia” and “acute myeloid-leukemia” indicates expanding applications beyond the initial B-cell ALL focus, with Kenderian et al. exploring CD33-targeted approaches for AML.39 The keywords “chimeric antigen receptor” and “chimeric receptors” emphasize the core technology, while “affinity” and “design” highlight engineering aspects critical for optimizing clinical performance, as demonstrated by Liu et al. in their work on how CAR affinity affects efficacy and toxicity profiles.40 The appearance of “chronic lymphocytic-leukemia” alongside “multiple-myeloma” and “non-hodgkin-lymphoma” reflects the broadening application of CAR-T therapy across hematologic malignancies, with Porter et al. reporting durable responses in CLL patients.41 The presence of “PD-1 blockade” indicates combination strategies with checkpoint inhibition, while “in-vivo persistence” and “sustained remissions” highlight the critical relationship between CAR-T cell persistence and durable clinical responses.

Cluster 3 (blue): CAR-T population treatment integration

Cluster 3 addresses patient demographics and treatment combinations, including keywords such as “adolescents,” “adult patients,” “blinatumomab,” “blood,” “chemotherapy,” “childhood,” “children,” “clofarabine,” “combination,” and “cyclophosphamide.” This cluster highlights the application of CAR-T therapy across different patient populations and its integration with existing treatment modalities. The keywords “children,” “childhood,” and “adolescents” reflect the initial focus on pediatric populations, with Gardner et al. reporting high response rates in pediatric and young adult patients.42 The term “adult patients” indicates expansion to older populations, where Turtle et al. demonstrated that defined composition CAR products could achieve efficacy in adult ALL patients despite age-related T cell deficits.43 The inclusion of “blinatumomab” and “chemotherapy” points to integration of CAR-T therapy within broader treatment algorithms, with Brown et al. investigating sequential use of these therapies.44 The presence of “gvhd” (graft-versus-host disease) suggests research into allogeneic CAR-T approaches or post-CAR-T transplantation complications, while “combination” reflects emerging strategies to enhance CAR-T efficacy through combinatorial approaches. Keywords such as “cyclophosphamide” and “clofarabine” highlight the importance of lymphodepletion regimens in optimizing CAR-T cell expansion and persistence, as demonstrated by Turtle et al. in their dose-finding and lymphodepletion optimization studies.43

Cluster 4 (yellow): clinical outcomes and follow-up

Cluster 4 encompasses clinical outcomes and follow-up, with keywords including “acute lymphocytic-leukemia,” “adult B-cell,” “antibody,” “bone-marrow-transplantation,” “clinical-trials,” “diagnosis,” “donor,” “follow-up,” “minimal residual disease,” and “quality-of-life.” This cluster reflects the evaluation of CAR-T therapy efficacy, safety, and long-term outcomes in clinical practice. The presence of “minimal residual disease” highlights a critical endpoint, with Hay et al. demonstrating that MRD status after CAR-T therapy strongly predicts long-term outcomes (Kevin A.).45 Keywords such as “hyper-cvad” and “imatinib” represent conventional therapy components that CAR-T cells have been integrated with or compared against, as discussed by Jain et al.46 The terms “follow-up” and “phase-ii” emphasize the increasing attention to durability of responses and advancement to later-phase clinical trials, with Park et al. reporting long-term outcomes from early CAR-T trials.47 The appearance of “bone-marrow-transplantation” reflects ongoing questions about optimal sequencing with cellular therapy, with Pulsipher et al. investigating whether CAR-T therapy can serve as a bridge to transplant or potentially replace it.48 Keywords such as “escape” and “mortality” indicate recognition of treatment failures and their consequences, while “quality-of-life” represents the growing emphasis on patient-reported outcomes, as examined by Ruark et al.49 The presence of “inotuzumab ozogamicin” indicates research into alternative or sequential CD22-targeted therapies for patients relapsing after CD19-directed CAR-T therapy.

Cluster 5 (purple): toxicity management and product specification

Cluster 5 focuses on toxicity management and product specification, containing keywords such as “axicabtagene ciloleucel,” “biomarkers,” “CAR-T,” “CD19,” “central-nervous-system,” “cytokine release syndrome,” “disease,” “leukemia,” “lymphoblastic-leukemia,” “management,” “neurotoxicity,” and “outcomes.” This cluster represents the critical interface between therapy efficacy and safety, with particular focus on managing unique toxicities. The prominence of “cytokine release syndrome” and “neurotoxicity” reflects the defining toxicities of CAR-T therapy, with landmark work by Neelapu et al. establishing standardized grading and management approaches.50 The inclusion of “axicabtagene ciloleucel” alongside other product-specific terms indicates the expansion of commercial products, as demonstrated by Locke et al. for non-Hodgkin lymphoma.51 The term “biomarkers” highlights efforts to identify predictors of both efficacy and toxicity, with Hay et al. characterizing cytokine profiles associated with severe CRS.52 Keywords such as “central-nervous-system” reflect specific toxicity manifestations, with Gust et al. elucidating mechanisms of CAR-T-associated neurotoxicity involving blood–brain barrier disruption.53 The appearance of “management” alongside toxicity terms demonstrates the development of mitigation strategies, including early tocilizumab intervention as reported by Le et al.54 The term “prognostic-factors” indicates the growing emphasis on patient selection and risk stratification, with studies by Nastoupil et al. identifying baseline characteristics associated with outcomes.55 The presence of “1st-in-human” alongside established products reflects the continuous pipeline of novel approaches entering clinical testing, including next-generation constructs designed to enhance control over CAR-T activity.

Study trends transition

The burst keywords analysis revealed the temporal evolution of research priorities in CAR-T cell therapy for ALL. During 2010–2019, research progressed through distinct phases. Keywords including “adoptive immunotherapy” and “antitumor activity,” reflected basic mechanisms and proof-of-concept studies. Seminal work by Brentjens et al. established the preclinical efficacy of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells,56 while Kochenderfer et al. demonstrated first-in-human evidence.57 Keywords including “antibody,” “transplantation,” and “activation” marked a transition toward clinical translation, coinciding with Maude et al.‘s landmark publication on sustained remissions.32 The strong burst of “B cell” paralleled tisagenlecleucel’s FDA approval in 2017.

The most recent phase (2020–2024) demonstrated an evolution toward practical clinical considerations and long-term outcomes. Burst keywords included “pediatric patients” (strength 3.15, 2018–2020), “inotuzumab ozogamicin” (strength 2.74, 2018–2020), and “CD8(+)” (strength 3.66, 2019–2021). The emergence of “survival” (strength 2.55, 2019–2020) signaled increased attention to long-term efficacy as follow-up data matured. Notably, the most recent burst keywords “term follow up” (strength 2.79, 2021–2024) and “risk” (strength 3.1, 2022–2024) indicated a current research frontier focused on long-term efficacy, safety, and risk stratification. This trend aligns with recent studies by Pasquini et al. examining real-world outcomes58 and Shah et al. investigating predictive biomarkers for patient selection.59 Beyond our bibliometric scope, CAR-T manufacturing heterogeneity remains a key challenge. Recent frameworks reveal that harvest timing dictates product composition60: mid-harvest yields memory-enriched, metabolically active cells, whereas late-harvest yields terminally differentiated but functional cells. This identifies novel quality attributes governing performance and may yield biomarkers to guide next-gen design.

Limitations

However, the study has some limitations. Firstly, relying exclusively on the WoSCC may have omitted relevant literature indexed in other major databases (e.g., Scopus, PubMed, Embase) or regional repositories. Furthermore, the restriction to English-language publications may have narrowed the scope of the analysis. Therefore, when interpreting the results, their potential influence should be taken into consideration in the absence of cross-validation or robustness checks. For instance, high-quality clinical trials and technological advancements in non-English-speaking regions may be underrepresented, which could lead to an underestimation of their impact. Future studies should consider integrating multiple databases and analyzing broader document types to provide a more comprehensive understanding of research trends in this field. Secondly, it is important to note that bibliometric analysis, which depends on citation-based indicators (such as citation counts, H-index, G-index, and M-index), is not designed to directly evaluate research quality. For instance, recently published high‑quality clinical trials may not yet have accumulated a substantial number of citations, which could result in an underestimation of their scholarly impact within such an analysis and may overstate the current hotspots and future frontiers concluded by analysis of keywords.

In conclusion, this bibliometric analysis identified five primary research hotspots in CAR-T cell therapy for ALL including basic mechanisms and cell engineering, CAR design and target selection, patient demographics and treatment combinations, clinical outcomes and follow-up, and toxicity management and product specification. Future research directions may focus on enhancing long-term outcomes through improved risk stratification models and developing predictive biomarkers for patient selection.
