Antimicrobial Treatment of Mild Mycobacterium avium Complex Pulmonary Disease Predicted to Increase Survival and Quality-Adjusted Life Years
Authors: Omri A. Arbiv, Gemma Postill, Yunjoo Im, Byung Woo Jhun, Petros Pechlivanoglou, Sarah K. Brode, Ahmed M. Bayoumi, Theodore K. Marras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) can lead to chronic lung disease, which can necessitate intensive antimicrobial therapy. In patients with mild MAC-PD, uncertainty exists regarding whether guideline-based antimicrobial treatment should be used as compared with watchful waiting.Research QuestionWhat are the long-term consequences of initiation of antimicrobial treatment compared with watchful waiting in patients with mild MAC-PD?Study Design and MethodsWe simulated a cohort of 70-year-old Canadian individuals with nodular bronchiectatic MAC-PD with a macrolide-susceptible specimen and normal lung function. We used a lifetime model to compare guideline-based treatment with observation for patients with MAC-PD, accounting for common adverse events, culture conversion rates, and disease recurrences from prior literature. Our outcomes were quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and life years. The primary analysis was a probabilistic Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the mean and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for each outcome under treatment and observation, as well as the treatment to observation difference. Additional outcomes included deterministic analysis and 1-way and 2-way deterministic sensitivity analyses.ResultsIn the primary analysis, the cohort of individuals receiving antimicrobial treatment showed a mean of 15.9 QALYs (95% UI, 12.4-19.3 QALYs), whereas those under observation showed 13.4 QALYs (95% UI, 9.0-16.4 QALYs), with a treatment to observation difference of 2.5 QALYs (95% UI, 0.8-4.7 QALYs). Similarly, those receiving treatment showed a mean 18.3 life years (95% UI, 19.2-25.5 life years), and those under observation showed 15.8 life years (95% UI, 17.3-22.2 life years). The treatment to observation difference was 2.5 years (95% UI, 1.0-4.2 years). Increase in QALYs and life years was consistent across almost all sensitivity analyses.InterpretationIn a simulated cohort, guideline-based treatment increased QALYs and life years in patients with mild MAC-PD. Our findings can support patients and physicians in deciding on treatment approach.

Take-Home PointsResearch Question: Does guideline-based antimicrobial treatment for patients with mild Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) lead to an improvement in long-term quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) compared with watchful waiting?Results: Using a microsimulation of a cohort with mild MAC-PD, antimicrobial treatment led to an improvement in QALYs by a mean of 2.5 QALYs compared with observation.Interpretation: Implementation of early antimicrobial treatment, rather than observation, in patients with mild MAC-PD may lead to improved quality of life.

Take-Home Points

Research Question: Does guideline-based antimicrobial treatment for patients with mild Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) lead to an improvement in long-term quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) compared with watchful waiting?

Results: Using a microsimulation of a cohort with mild MAC-PD, antimicrobial treatment led to an improvement in QALYs by a mean of 2.5 QALYs compared with observation.

Interpretation: Implementation of early antimicrobial treatment, rather than observation, in patients with mild MAC-PD may lead to improved quality of life.

Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) is a chronic lung disease that is increasing in incidence.1,2 Manifestations of MAC-PD can range from mild cough or minor exertional limitation to devastating pulmonary necrosis.3 Treatment of MAC-PD requires an intensive antimicrobial regimen. Guidelines from the American Thoracic Society (ATS), European Respiratory Society (ERS), European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), and Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) recommend treatment with 3 antimicrobials (rifampin, ethambutol, and azithromycin) for 12 months after sputum culture conversion has been achieved.4

The decision to initiate treatment is complex. Although antimicrobial treatment can reduce symptoms and prevent further disease progression,5 treatment can be complicated by toxicities, most commonly visual disturbances and gastrointestinal intolerance.6,7 Despite antimicrobial treatment, some patients never achieve cure.6, 7, 8 Moreover, not all patients with MAC-PD experience disease progression, and some patients achieve spontaneous culture conversion without treatment, potentially obviating the need for treatment.9,10 Given these considerations, it is difficult to know if typical patients with mild MAC-PD, over the course of their lives, would have greater benefit from treatment or from clinical observation.

Our goal was to evaluate the effect of the ATS, ERS, ESCMID and IDSA recommended first-line treatment on quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) of patients with mild MAC-PD. Using a perspective that included patient preferences, we created a microsimulation model that included data from recent trials and observational cohorts to estimate the effect of treatment on patient quality of life and overall survival.

Study Design and Methods

Model Strategies

We evaluated 2 treatment strategies for patients with stable mild MAC-PD. In the first strategy, patients received treatment based on ATS, ERS, ESCMID, and IDSA guidelines with thrice-weekly rifampin, ethambutol, and azithromycin as first-line medications. Treatment was intensified with amikacin liposomal inhaled suspension if culture conversion was not achieved at 6 months. In the second strategy, patients received no treatment unless disease progression was demonstrated. In our model, treatment was protective of disease progression.

Model Overview

We conducted a clinical decision analysis using a microsimulation cohort-state decision model, in which we simulated the clinical course of 10,000 individuals’ data from the medical literature.11 In our base case, we modeled a cohort of 70-year-old Canadian male and female patients with mild nodular-bronchiectatic MAC-PD, defined as having minimal symptoms and normal lung function. All simulated individuals harbored a macrolide-susceptible organism. The age selected reflects the most median age of MAC-PD diagnosis in a recent Canadian cohort.12

Simulated individuals could transition each cycle between health states, including starting and stopping treatment. We selected a cycle length of 1 month to approximate the minimal follow-up period during which patients typically would receive treatment. We used a 40-year time horizon to model events over the course of patients’ remaining life (ie, we simulated individuals until most of the cohort was expected to die).

Our primary outcome was survival adjusted for quality of life, which we measured with QALYs. A QALY integrates survival with time-dependent quality of life, measured as a utility (scaled with anchors of 0 indicating death and 1 indicating best possible health).13 Life expectancy was the secondary outcome. We did not discount future health outcomes in our primary analysis, because our model is a clinical decision analysis. Individual discount rates for health outcomes vary widely, with many individuals expressing a discount rate of 0.14,15 A sensitivity analysis explored discount rates for health outcomes between 0% and 3%.

Model Structure

We modeled the clinical course of individuals by defining relevant health states and possible transitions between states. The state diagram is shown in Figure 1, with more detailed depiction in e-Figure 1. In the treatment strategy, therapy was administered for 13 to 24 months, depending on when culture conversion was achieved. If culture conversion was achieved, treatment was continued for an additional 12 months and individuals were assumed to be cured. If culture conversion was not achieved, treatment was stopped at 24 months and patients returned to observation. Individuals not receiving treatment transitioned to a treatment state if disease progression occurred and to the cure state if spontaneous culture conversion occurred. Individuals who experienced disease recurrence transitioned from the cure state to the observation state. Validity of the model was assessed by comparing model outputs with previously published literature.Figure 1Simplified model diagram depicting the flow of simulated patients through the model.

Simplified model diagram depicting the flow of simulated patients through the model.

Drug intolerance was modeled as a transient decrease in utility for the month before alteration in treatment to a second-line medication. Disutility associated with treatment discontinuation was modeled as gastrointestinal intolerance with rifampin and azithromycin and visual toxicity with ethambutol.6 Toxicity for ethambutol or rifampin was treated with a change to second-line medication, whereas azithromycin intolerance was modeled as a change to clarithromycin or reduction in dose, which was shown to be tolerable in prior studies.6 We assumed that gastrointestinal intolerance would occur in the first 6 months of treatment initiation, whereas ethambutol toxicity could occur at any time during treatment. In cases where re-treatment was necessary (ie, treatment with subsequent disease progression), individuals who had experienced rifampin or azithromycin toxicity were treated with first-line treatment, whereas those with ethambutol toxicity were treated with an ethambutol-free regimen. Multiple intolerances were possible, but only 1 intolerance could occur per cycle. The model was written in R version 4.4.3 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and is provided in a public repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17989201).

Summary of Inputs Used in the Model

MAC-PD = Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease.

Plausible range captured all reasonable values for each model input, based on values obtained from the literature and expert judgment (S. K. B. and T. K. M.).

Each value was modeled as a distribution for the primary analysis to evaluate uncertainty in our outcome estimates. Utility and probabilities were converted to beta distributions using the method of moments, and hazard ratios were converted to log-transformed normal distribution using their published means and confidence intervals.

In addition, we assigned a distribution to each parameter for use in a probabilistic analysis. We assumed that probabilities and utility parameters followed beta distributions31 and that the natural log of hazard ratios (HRs) followed normal distributions. We estimated distribution parameters using the method of moments, based on published means and measures of dispersion.31

We estimated an annual probability of disease progression requiring treatment and spontaneous culture conversion of 4.3% and 15.7%, respectively. We assumed a 77% probability of culture conversion in the first 6 months of treatment6,18,20 and a 29% probability of cure thereafter.21 The probability of medication intolerance was estimated based on detailed findings from a retrospective cohort study,6 and cure probabilities without first-line medications similarly were obtained from retrospective cohort studies.22,23 In the absence of data to support differing efficacy, we assumed that individuals who could not tolerate azithromycin were able to tolerate either clarithromycin or lower-dose azithromycin while maintaining similar efficacy.

Overall survival rates were obtained from the age-specific 2022 Canadian life expectancy tables,32 assuming an elevated HR in all patients with MAC-PD.24 Those who achieved culture conversion within 6 months of treatment initiation showed reduced mortality with a HR of 0.51 compared with those who did not attain culture conversion in 6 months, based on an estimate that adjusted for smoking, medical comorbidities, acid-fast bacilli smear, disease severity, and watchful waiting period.25

QALYs were calculated using EQ-5D utility values, a patient-reported outcome measure. The utility associated with treatment and observation states was assumed to be equivalent, provided no toxicities were encountered, because we did not identify evidence of change in quality of life with treatment in the literature. In a sensitivity analysis, we added a disutility to treatment ranging from 0 to –0.1 to evaluate the mild negative effect that patients may experience with chronic treatment. The disutility of visual side effects and gastrointestinal side effects were assumed to be transient—lasting 1 cycle—and associated with treatment alteration. Disutility from disease progression was modeled as a utility decrement of 0.032 that persisted throughout the model.30 Detailed rationale behind each input parameter is presented in e-Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Our primary analysis used Monte Carlo probabilistic methods, in which we sampled randomly each input parameter from its distribution 10,000 times11 to estimate the joint effect of all model parameter uncertainties. That is, input parameters with wide variances were modeled with a wide range of plausible values, whereas those that are more precise (ie, narrow variance) were modeled with a narrower range of values to reflect their precision. For each outcome, we reported mean values and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) formed from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile value of the outcome distribution, as well as the treatment to observation differences and associated 95% UI. We also conducted a yearly comparison of QALYs in each treatment strategy to evaluate how these approaches changed quality of life over time. We performed a deterministic analysis in which we measured QALYs and life-years using the mean base case value for each input parameter. We also performed 1-way sensitivity analyses in which we varied each parameter across its plausible range to evaluate its effect on the model output. Finally, we performed 5 prespecified 2-way sensitivity analyses in which 2 variables in the base-case scenario were covaried to evaluate the combined downstream consequences of the uncertainty surrounding the estimates of variable pairs were expected to have relevant joint uncertainty. The 2-way sensitivity analyses included probability of spontaneous culture conversion and progression of disease, QALYs in the observation and treatment state, QALYs in the treatment and cure state, utility of vision loss and probability of ethambutol intolerance, and mortality HR with early culture conversion and the overall mortality HR.

Results

In this simulation, 30% of those under observation progressed to treatment (Table 2). Simulated individuals in the treatment strategy received a mean of 23 months of treatment and were in the cured state for a mean of 9.6 years over their lifetime, whereas simulated individuals in the observation strategy received a mean of 6 months of treatment and spent a mean of 6.8 years in the cured state. Thirteen percent of participants initiated on treatment required drug alteration, most commonly for rifampin (8%). Intolerance to > 1 medication occurred in 0.4% of simulations in the treatment strategy and 0.1% simulations in the observation strategy. These results have reasonable face validity and are comparable with prior observations6,8,10 and systematic reviews.33 In the deterministic base-case analysis, QALYs for those receiving treatment was 16.1, whereas it was 13.5 for those under observation. Life expectancy was 18.7 years for those receiving treatment and 15.8 years for those under observation. Under all plausible 1-way sensitivity analyses, treatment led to more QALYs and a greater life expectancy than observation (e-Figs 2, 3). Similarly, QALYs were increased in all 5 of the 2-way sensitivity analyses conducted. Life expectancy was greater with treatment compared with observation, except for when the HR for mortality with early culture conversion was set to 1, in which case life expectancy was about the same (e-Fig 4).

Discussion

In a microsimulation of therapeutic approaches for participants with mild MAC-PD, intensive antimicrobial treatment led to increased quality of life and life expectancy compared with observation. These findings were robust to almost all sensitivity analyses. Overall, we found that treatment led to a mean lifetime increase of 2.5 QALYs and 2.5 life-years compared with observation.

Our results can help to address persistent uncertainty about which patients with nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) should be treated. The ATS, ERS, ESCMID, and IDSA guidelines provide a conditional recommendation based on low quality of evidence to initiate treatment in individuals who meet the criteria for NTM-PD.4 Yet, a recent survey of 619 physicians in Japan and Europe treating individuals with NTM-PD showed that only one-third of a sample of their patients living with NTM-PD were initiated on treatment.34 The most common reasons physicians provided for not starting treatment included judging patient symptoms not to be serious enough, good patient health, and the risk of harm greater than the potential benefit. This highlights the importance of obtaining evidence on how to best care for patients with NTM-PD experiencing mild symptoms.

Some of our assumptions embedded in the model are worth considering further. We assumed that quality of life is the same for patients receiving treatment compared with those under observation, unless a patient experienced a toxicity requiring treatment alteration. Based on clinical experience, some patients experience mild toxicities that worsen their quality of life, but do not lead to treatment alteration. Conversely, some evidence suggests that starting treatment seems to improve patient symptoms,35,36 although it is unclear to what extent this applies to patients with mild MAC-PD. Given the uncertainty, we assumed similar utilities whether patients were receiving treatment, although in a sensitivity analysis where we reduced utilities for individuals receiving treatment by as much as 0.1, antimicrobial treatment still was preferred strategy. In 2-way sensitivity analysis, simultaneously varying the utility of the treatment and observation strategies did not affect our results. Patients in our simulation did not develop cavitation or experience pulmonary exacerbations, which likely biased our findings toward favoring the observation strategy. In addition, we assumed that in patients with mild (noncavitary, macrolide susceptible, treatment-naïve) disease, treatment is protective against progression, based on clinical impressions and data that treatment improves respiratory symptoms and leads to improvement in imaging.36,37

For simplicity, we accounted for only drug discontinuation among first-line agents and ignored the possibility of intolerance of second-line agents. These assumptions likely biased our findings toward the treatment strategy, although in sensitivity analyses that increased the rates of adverse events 10-fold, treatment continued to be the preferred strategy. Disutility from treatment intolerance was modeled as gastrointestinal intolerance for rifampin and azithromycin and visual disturbance for ethambutol. Although this does not account for less common side effects that could lead to disutility (such as hearing loss or liver failure), this likely is balanced by more common reasons for drug discontinuation that carry with them no disutility, such as asymptomatic liver enzyme elevation or cytopenias.

In the absence of evidence to the contrary, we assumed that quality of life is identical whether cure occurs by spontaneous culture conversion or by antimicrobial treatment. We also assumed that change to clarithromycin or azithromycin at lower doses similarly is effective to azithromycin at standard doses. This assumption is based on prior studies that found that macrolides often could be tolerated with these alterations6 and that macrolide serum level was not associated with treatment success.38 In our sensitivity analyses, we reduced the efficacy of macrolide alteration, and it did not alter our results meaningfully. Another simplification we incorporated was assuming that individuals would not demonstrate drug resistance while receiving treatment. We assumed that resistance is unlikely given the low bacillary burden of patients with noncavitary nodular-bronchiectatic disease with minimal symptoms and the evidence that development of resistance is rare when patients are treated with an ethambutol-containing regimen.39 We are uncertain of which direction these assumptions would bias our finding.

Our approach has several strengths. A simulation approach allowed us to understand the long-term patient consequence of treatment strategy on patient quality of life. The need to consider the patient experience of the disease40 and an understanding that the disease affects patients through multiple domains35 are growing. Obtaining this understanding would be prohibitive with a prospective study design. We also were able to model uncertainty in modeling estimates by using a probabilistic analysis. In addition, we made our model reproducible and publicly available to allow for further scrutiny of our methods and for others to build on our work.

Our approach has limitations. To attain a lifetime model, we extrapolated what was known on utility and treatment probabilities from relatively short-term studies. These data represent the best available current knowledge. We also did not account for costs in our model, because the decision to initiate treatment generally is centered on discussion between the patient and their health care provider based on patient preferences, rather than the cost accrued by the health care system. Additionally, our model incorporated survival differences in patients with early culture conversion (within 6 months of treatment initiation) from a single-center cohort.25 However, in a sensitivity analysis in which we removed the effect of early culture conversion on survival, the utility of treatment remained greater than observation. Finally, our base cohort was 70 years of age and may not be applicable to patients of different ages, although in a sensitivity analysis extending our findings to those 60 to 80 years of age, we found that the treatment strategy remained superior to observation in both QALYs and life expectancy.

Interpretation

Using a patient-centered simulation model, we showed that treatment was associated with improved patient quality of life and life expectancy in mild MAC-PD compared with observation. This model was built with modern data modeling techniques using the best available current data. These results support the recommendations provided by the ATS, ERS, ESCMID, and IDSA guidelines and can inform patients and physicians about treatment decisions for those living with MAC-PD.
