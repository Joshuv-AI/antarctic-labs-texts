Invasive Management for Acute‐on‐Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension: Feasibility and Clinical Outcomes in a Single‐Center Cohort
Authors: Jun Yamashita, Ryosuke Ito, Sayo Ikeda, Koki Hasegawa, Yoshikazu Takeuchi, Tomohiro Kasahara, Naotaka Murata, Shun Suzuki, Hideaki Kanazawa, Yusuke Shimahara, Kazuhiro Satomi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute‐on‐chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (acute‐on‐CTEPH) is associated with rapid clinical deterioration, yet the role of invasive intervention in the acute phase remains poorly defined. We retrospectively analyzed consecutive patients with acute‐on‐CTEPH presenting with severe hypoxemia and/or cardiogenic shock at a single center between 2023 and 2026. Acute‐on‐CTEPH was defined as acute clinical deterioration requiring ventilatory or circulatory support, including ECMO, with imaging evidence of chronic thromboembolic disease and superimposed acute thrombi. Invasive strategies included pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) and/or balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), selected based on multidisciplinary assessment. The primary outcome was change in pulmonary hemodynamics; secondary outcomes included acute‐phase mortality and clinical recovery after stabilization. A total of 15 patients (median age 69 years; 53% male), all in World Health Organization functional class IV, were included. Mechanical ventilation and ECMO were required in 53% and 47% of patients, respectively. Initial treatment consisted of PEA (40%) or BPA (60%), with combined approaches in selected cases. Acute‐phase mortality was 20%. Among survivors, mean pulmonary artery pressure decreased from 42 to 17 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance from 11.6 to 2.4 WU, and cardiac index increased from 1.7 to 2.7 L/min/m2. Among patients requiring ECMO, 5 were successfully weaned and 4 survived to discharge. At follow‐up, 11 of the 12 survivors were in WHO functional class I or II, and 8 (67%) had discontinued home oxygen therapy. Despite substantial acute‐phase mortality, invasive strategies were associated with marked hemodynamic improvement and may represent a potential therapeutic option in this life‐threatening clinical setting.Keywords: acute‐on‐chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, balloon pulmonary angioplasty, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, pulmonary endarterectomy

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a potentially curable form of pulmonary hypertension characterized by organized thromboembolic obstruction of the pulmonary arteries and secondary microvascular remodeling [1, 2]. Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) remains the treatment of choice for operable disease, while balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) has emerged as an effective alternative for patients with inoperable or residual disease, leading to substantial improvements in pulmonary hemodynamics and clinical outcomes [3, 4, 5].

In contrast to the relatively stable clinical course typically observed in CTEPH, a subset of patients experiences acute clinical deterioration, often associated with superimposed acute thrombus formation on pre‐existing organized lesions [6]. This condition, hereafter termed acute‐on‐chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (acute‐on‐CTEPH), is characterized by critical hemodynamic compromise, severe hypoxemia, and right ventricular failure, frequently requiring advanced cardiopulmonary support such as mechanical ventilation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Despite its clinical severity, acute‐on‐CTEPH remains poorly characterized, and optimal management strategies have not been established.

In particular, the role of invasive interventions such as PEA and BPA in the acute phase is unclear. While these procedures are well established in stable CTEPH, their application in critically ill patients raises concerns regarding procedural risk, timing, and patient selection. Consequently, treatment decisions are often individualized, and data on feasibility and outcomes in this high‐risk population are limited [7, 8].

Because of this profound hemodynamic compromise, patients with acute‐on‐CTEPH often require intensive supportive care, including mechanical ventilation, vasoactive agents, and ECMO. Previous case reports and small case series have highlighted the potentially catastrophic clinical course of patients presenting with severe acute‐on‐CTEPH requiring intensive supportive care, although high‐quality evidence remains limited [7, 9, 10]. Given the limited evidence regarding the optimal management of these critically ill patients, our institution adopted a multidisciplinary strategy that considered early invasive treatment. However, whether such an approach can lead to meaningful physiological and clinical recovery in this critically ill population remains insufficiently studied.

Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the feasibility and hemodynamic and clinical outcomes of invasive treatment strategies, including PEA and BPA, in patients with acute‐on‐CTEPH presenting with severe clinical deterioration.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a single‐center retrospective observational study of consecutive patients diagnosed with CTEPH at our institution between January 2023 and January 2026. Among 107 consecutive patients treated during the study period, 20 patients experienced acute clinical deterioration requiring admission to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) with ventilatory or circulatory support. These patients were screened for possible acute‐on‐CTEPH. Five patients were excluded because their clinical deterioration was considered attributable primarily to the progression of right heart failure without evidence of superimposed acute thrombi. Consequently, 15 patients fulfilling the operational definition of acute‐on‐CTEPH were included in the present analysis. As a specialized center for CTEPH management, invasive treatment strategies were considered through multidisciplinary evaluation on a case‐by‐case basis.

At our institution, invasive intervention was generally favored for patients presenting with acute‐on‐CTEPH and clinical deterioration. All cases were evaluated by a multidisciplinary CTEPH team, and an individualized invasive strategy was selected based on hemodynamic status, lesion characteristics, and surgical eligibility.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokyo Medical University (Approval Number: T2022‐0161). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Definition of Acute‐on‐CTEPH

For the purpose of this study, we applied an operational definition of acute‐on‐CTEPH. Acute‐on‐CTEPH was defined by the presence of all of the following criteria:

(1) acute clinical deterioration requiring admission to the CICU, with the need for ventilatory or circulatory support, including ECMO,

(2) imaging evidence of underlying chronic thromboembolic disease, and

(3) imaging evidence of superimposed acute thrombi.

Acute clinical deterioration was defined by objective findings, including hypoxemia requiring ventilatory support, such as noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) or endotracheal intubation, cardiogenic shock requiring vasopressor or inotropic support, or the need for ECMO at any time during the acute phase. ECMO was initiated for refractory hypoxemia or cardiogenic shock despite optimized supportive therapy, including ventilatory and vasoactive support, based on multidisciplinary clinical assessment. The timing of ECMO initiation relative to invasive intervention was recorded and categorized as before transfer, before intervention after admission, or during/after the invasive procedure. Evidence of underlying chronic thromboembolic disease was determined using characteristic imaging findings on computed tomography (CT) or pulmonary angiography, including eccentric filling defects, abrupt vessel narrowing, webs, ring‐like stenosis, or pouch defects. Supportive clinical information, such as a history of progressive dyspnea and pre‐event evidence of right heart strain on electrocardiography or chest radiography, was also considered. Evidence of superimposed acute thrombi was identified by imaging findings, including intraluminal filling defects on contrast‐enhanced CT and corresponding angiographic findings.

Procedural findings were incorporated into the diagnostic assessment. In patients who underwent PEA, surgically removed specimens were evaluated for organized thrombotic material. In patients who underwent BPA, angiographic findings obtained during the procedure were used as additional diagnostic evidence.

In some patients, initial management was undertaken under the working diagnosis of acute pulmonary embolism at referring institutions. However, lack of clinical improvement following thrombolytic therapy or catheter‐based thrombectomy, and/or subsequent identification of chronic thromboembolic lesions on imaging or during intervention, led to the final diagnosis of acute‐on‐CTEPH. Clinical Assessment and Data Collection

Baseline clinical characteristics were collected at the time of admission. The requirement for advanced cardiopulmonary support and adjunctive medical therapies during the acute phase was also recorded.

Hemodynamic parameters were obtained using right heart catheterization and included mean PAP, PVR, and CI.

Given the critical condition of the study population, patients underwent emergency PEA or rescue BPA promptly after initial hemodynamic assessment. Therefore, analyses were based on measurements obtained immediately prior to intervention and those obtained after clinical stabilization.

Post‐stabilization was defined as the time point at which clinical and hemodynamic conditions were considered stable after invasive treatment, with no further acute‐phase interventions required, as determined by the treating team on a per‐patient basis. For analysis, measurements obtained at follow‐up or at the time of the final planned intervention were used.

Treatment Strategy

All patients received anticoagulation and intensive supportive care as clinically indicated. Urgent invasive intervention was considered when severe hypoxemia, hypotension, or low‐output syndrome, worsening organ dysfunction, or an escalating requirement for ventilatory or vasoactive support persisted without sufficient clinical improvement. Emergency PEA was selected for surgically accessible disease when immediate surgery was considered tolerable. Rescue BPA was selected when emergency PEA was considered excessively high risk because of acute hemodynamic instability, advanced age, active infection, major comorbidities, previous thoracic surgery, or poor general condition.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was change in pulmonary hemodynamics between pre‐intervention and post‐stabilization assessments, including mean PAP, PVR, and CI, as markers of physiological recovery following invasive treatment. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of a conservative treatment control group, the study was not designed to evaluate the survival benefit of invasive intervention. Secondary outcomes included acute‐phase mortality and clinical recovery, assessed by changes in World Health Organization (WHO) functional class and the requirement for home oxygen therapy.

Major procedure‐related or postprocedural complications were defined as adverse events occurring during or after BPA or PEA that resulted in death, caused clinically significant pulmonary injury, or required an unplanned rescue intervention, including reoperation or initiation of ECMO support. BPA‐related pulmonary hemorrhage was recorded per session and was defined as bleeding attributable to balloon dilation or pulmonary artery wire injury that required unplanned rescue treatment or caused clinically significant pulmonary injury.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are presented as the median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons of hemodynamic parameters between pre‐intervention and post‐stabilization measurements were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Given the exploratory nature of the study and the small sample size, statistical testing was interpreted descriptively, and emphasis was placed on the magnitude and consistency of observed changes rather than formal hypothesis testing.

Individual patient trajectories among surviving patients were visualized using spaghetti plots to assess the directionality and consistency of treatment response.

Results

After exclusion of 5 patients whose clinical deterioration was attributed primarily to worsening right heart failure without evidence of superimposed acute thrombi, 15 patients fulfilling the operational definition of acute‐on‐CTEPH were included in the analysis, representing 14% of all patients with CTEPH treated at our institution during the study period. The median age was 69 (60, 78) years, and 8 patients (53%) were male. All patients presented with severe clinical deterioration and were classified as WHO functional class IV at admission. A history of venous thromboembolism was present in 5 patients (33%), malignancy in 3 (20%), and thrombophilia in 1 (7%).

Advanced cardiopulmonary support was frequently required: NPPV in 8 patients (53%), mechanical ventilation in 8 (53%), and ECMO in 7 (47%), continuous renal replacement therapy in 2 (13%), and inhaled nitric oxide in 7 (47%), indicating profound respiratory and circulatory failure. Among the seven patients requiring ECMO, two were transferred while receiving ECMO support, two underwent ECMO initiation before invasive intervention after admission, and three required ECMO during or after the invasive procedure because of cardiogenic shock or refractory hypoxemia (Supporting Information S3: Table 3). Vasoactive medications were also frequently administered during the acute phase, including noradrenaline in 8 patients (53%), dobutamine in 13 (87%), and milrinone in 1 (7%). No oral pulmonary hypertension‐targeted therapy or intravenous epoprostenol was administered before invasive intervention. After stabilization, riociguat was initiated in three patients and selexipag in four patients.

Laboratory findings showed elevated markers consistent with systemic inflammation and right heart strain, including a median brain natriuretic peptide level of 715 (272, 1222) pg/mL and D‐dimer of 6.55 (2.62, 15.4) μg/mL. Renal dysfunction and mild hepatic congestion were also observed in some patients.

Blood gas analysis demonstrated impaired oxygenation, with a median P/F ratio of 147 (118, 196), reflecting moderate to severe hypoxemia despite oxygen support. Mixed venous oxygen saturation was low (SvO2 60.0 [45.1, 66.0] %), consistent with low cardiac output (CO) physiology.

Echocardiographic findings indicated right ventricular overload, with a median tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient of 56.0 (52.4, 80.3) mmHg and inferior vena cava diameter of 20.2 (19.8, 23.5) mm, while left ventricular systolic function was preserved.

Baseline hemodynamics confirmed severe pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular failure, with a mean PAP of 42.0 (37.8, 53.8) mmHg, PVR of 11.6 (6.3, 16.2) WU, and a CI of 1.7 (1.3, 2.4) L/min/m2.

Among the 9 PEA procedures, major postprocedural complications occurred following 3 procedures (33.3%). These included pulmonary hemorrhage causing atelectasis and prolonged ICU stay in 1 patient, postoperative ECMO support in 1, recurrent pulmonary embolism resulting in death in 1, and re‐thoracotomy for hemostasis in 1. These complications were not mutually exclusive; the patient requiring postoperative ECMO was successfully weaned but subsequently developed recurrent pulmonary embolism and died.

Among the 7 patients who required ECMO support, 5 were successfully weaned. Of these, 4 survived to discharge, whereas 1 died during the same hospitalization. The remaining 2 patients could not be weaned from ECMO and died. At follow‐up, among the 12 surviving patients, 8 improved to WHO functional class I and discontinued home oxygen therapy, 3 were in class II, and one remained in class IV.

Discussion

CTEPH is a rare disease, and acute‐on‐CTEPH represents an even rarer and clinically distinct subgroup [6]. Although CTEPH was historically associated with poor prognosis, treatment outcomes have improved substantially with advances in PEA, BPA, and medical therapy [3, 4, 11]. Nevertheless, awareness of the disease remains limited, and a considerable delay from symptom onset to diagnosis is still commonly observed [12]. In this context, acute‐on‐CTEPH—defined as acute hemodynamic deterioration on a background of chronic thromboembolic disease—constitutes a highly unstable clinical phenotype characterized by abrupt circulatory collapse. Despite its clinical importance, evidence regarding optimal management remains limited [8]. To date, the literature consists predominantly of case reports and small case series, and the optimal management strategy for acute‐on‐CTEPH remains poorly defined. The only available case series including patients with similarly severe presentations was reported by Fukuda and colleagues, which evaluated rescue BPA in patients with low cardiac output syndrome due to CTEPH; however, only 2 of the 9 patients in that study were classified as acute‐on‐CTEPH [10]. Importantly, given the high procedural risk in this population, rescue BPA has been recommended to be performed in specialized centers [13].

In the present study, all patients exhibited advanced disease severity, with all patients classified as WHO functional class IV, and required ventilatory and/or circulatory support, with nearly half requiring ECMO. These findings underscore the profound hemodynamic compromise and severely limited physiological reserve associated with this condition. Accordingly, the optimal treatment strategy for this population remains poorly defined. In conventional clinical practice, particularly in critically ill patients, invasive interventions are often deferred because of perceived procedural risk, and management is typically limited to anticoagulation and supportive care. Before the adoption of the current early invasive strategy, our institution had encountered a small number of patients with suspected acute‐on‐CTEPH who were managed with anticoagulation and intensive supportive care alone but experienced fatal in‐hospital outcomes. These historical cases were outside the present study period, were not systematically identified using the current operational definition, and cannot be regarded as a comparator cohort. Nevertheless, this clinical experience prompted multidisciplinary discussion between the medical and surgical teams and led to the development of our current strategy incorporating early PEA or BPA when clinically appropriate.

Although this historical experience cannot establish the effectiveness of invasive treatment, it provides important clinical context for the adoption of our multidisciplinary strategy. In acute‐on‐CTEPH, organized thromboembolic lesions create fixed mechanical obstruction and reduce the ability of the pulmonary vascular bed to accommodate superimposed acute thrombi. Therefore, supportive therapy alone may be insufficient to reverse the critical increase in right ventricular afterload. In the present study, although some patients initially had relatively preserved systemic perfusion, clinical deterioration persisted despite intensive supportive therapy, including ventilatory support, vasoactive medications, anticoagulation, and ECMO when indicated, prompting urgent invasive intervention. Consistent with this concept, a recent case report described a patient with acute‐on‐chronic thromboembolic disease who could not be weaned from VA‐ECMO despite emergency surgical embolectomy and 4 days of anticoagulation, ultimately requiring rescue PEA [14]. These observations support the concept that supportive therapy should not necessarily be viewed as a strategy to postpone intervention until complete stabilization, but rather as a means of maintaining adequate organ perfusion while definitive reperfusion therapy is being arranged and performed.

Within this clinical context, the present study demonstrates that invasive treatment strategies—including rescue BPA, emergency PEA, and combined approaches—can be performed in selected critically ill patients when guided by a multidisciplinary framework. Importantly, these interventions were associated with significant overall improvements in pulmonary hemodynamics among survivors. The observed reductions in mean PAP and PVR, together with increases in CI, suggest restoration of pulmonary vascular flow and improvement of right ventricular function.

In parallel, recent advances in catheter‐based therapies for acute pulmonary embolism, including large‐bore aspiration systems and mechanical thrombectomy devices, have demonstrated promising efficacy in rapidly reducing thrombotic burden and improving hemodynamics [15, 16, 17]. Although these technologies could potentially be applied to the acute phase of acute‐on‐CTEPH, their efficacy is likely limited to the removal of acute superimposed thrombi. In acute‐on‐CTEPH, the underlying organized lesions—which are often more rigid and adherent to the vessel wall—constitute the fundamental cause of reduced vascular reserve and remain inaccessible to standard aspiration or thrombectomy devices. Therefore, an integrated approach combining these emerging technologies for the removal of acute thrombi with established interventions such as BPA or PEA targeting chronic organized lesions may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for acute‐on‐CTEPH. However, the feasibility, optimal timing, and safety of such combined approaches remain to be determined in future studies.

From a pathophysiological perspective, acute‐on‐CTEPH is characterized by the coexistence of acute thrombotic material and pre‐existing organized lesions. This dual pathology may represent a uniquely modifiable substrate, in which removal or dilation of obstructive components can result in rapid hemodynamic improvement. The overall changes observed in the paired analyses, despite some interindividual variability, are consistent with physiological improvement over the course of invasive treatment.

These findings have potential clinical implications. First, they suggest that acute‐on‐CTEPH should not be uniformly regarded as a contraindication to invasive treatment. Second, they support a flexible, patient‐tailored strategy integrating surgical and catheter‐based approaches according to anatomical and clinical characteristics. In our cohort, the choice between emergency PEA and rescue BPA was not based solely on operability but also incorporated acute physiological status, comorbidities, overall clinical condition, and surgical risk. Finally, they highlight the importance of multidisciplinary decision‐making in balancing procedural risk against the risk of continued clinical deterioration in patients who fail to stabilize despite anticoagulation and intensive supportive care [7]. Given the severity of illness and persistent clinical deterioration in this cohort, survival to discharge in 12 of 15 patients was clinically encouraging, although no comparison with conservative management can be made.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study included a small number of patients from a single center, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the retrospective design and absence of a control group preclude any causal inference regarding treatment effectiveness. Third, treatment strategies were not standardized and were determined by clinical judgment, introducing potential selection bias. In addition, post‐stabilization hemodynamic analyses were restricted to survivors with paired measurements, introducing potential survivor and availability bias. Because post‐stabilization measurements were obtained at variable time points after individualized staged interventions, the observed hemodynamic changes should be interpreted as physiological changes over the entire treatment course rather than as the effect of any single intervention. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating in nature.

Conclusions

In patients with acute‐on‐CTEPH presenting with severe hemodynamic compromise, invasive treatment strategies including BPA and PEA were feasible when performed within a multidisciplinary framework and were associated with overall improvements in pulmonary hemodynamics and clinical status among survivors. Although definitive conclusions regarding survival benefit cannot be drawn from this exploratory study, the observed physiological improvements suggest that invasive strategies may be beneficial in carefully selected patients with this life‐threatening condition. Future multicenter studies including an appropriate conservatively managed comparator group are warranted to determine whether these physiological improvements translate into improved survival and to define optimal patient selection and treatment strategies.
