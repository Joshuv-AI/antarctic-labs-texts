Safety, Efficacy, and Feasibility of Nebulized Long-Acting Bronchodilators vs Short-Acting Bronchodilators in Hospitalized Patients With Acute Exacerbations of COPD
Authors: Rajiv Dhand, Samuel Treat, Jennifer Ferris, Paul D. Terry, Tracy Walker, Scott Elder, Daniel Church, Danielle Dennis, Barbara Faircloth, Gulsah Onar, R. Eric Heidel, Isaac Biney, Martin Valdes, Milind Bhagat, Nicholas Fuerst, Shannon Cusick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-acting bronchodilators are preferred over short-acting bronchodilators in patients with stable COPD, but prospective studies are needed to determine the safety and efficacy of nebulized long-acting bronchodilators in hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD.Research QuestionIn hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD, is a combination of nebulized long-acting formoterol/revefenacin as safe and effective as the short-acting nebulized albuterol/ipratropium combination?Study Design and MethodsWe conducted a prospective, randomized, parallel-group study comparing a nebulized formoterol/revefenacin combination with an albuterol/ipratropium combination among 60 hospitalized patients (30 in each group) with exacerbations of COPD who tested negative for COVID-19. Treatments were administered every 12 to 24 hours (formoterol/revefenacin) or every 6 hours (albuterol/ipratropium) by jet nebulizers for up to 7 days. Safety and efficacy assessments were recorded on days 1, 3, and 7, or at hospital discharge. Assessment of dyspnea by the Modified Borg dyspnea scale was the primary outcome. Data were analyzed with parametric or nonparametric statistical tests, and generalized estimating equations with an identity link function were used to analyze differences in the two groups. Statistical significance was assumed at an unadjusted two-sided alpha value of 0.05.ResultsParticipants’ mean age was 63.2 (SD, 9.30) years, and 39 (65.0%) reported active tobacco use. Patients’ age, sex, race, smoking history, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index scores, supplemental oxygen requirements, and length of hospital stay were similar in the two groups (P > .05 for all). Patients receiving formoterol/revefenacin had similar Modified Borg dyspnea scores (P = .95) and required fewer total drug doses (P < .001), but there was a nonsignificant trend for more frequent rescue treatments (P = .08) compared with those receiving albuterol/ipratropium. There were no serious adverse events or treatment failures in either group.InterpretationOur findings indicate that in nonventilated hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD, the institution of nebulized formoterol/revefenacin in combination within 24 to 36 hours of hospital admission provided convenient dosing and required fewer drug doses to achieve comparable efficacy and safety with the standard short-acting nebulized albuterol/ipratropium combination.

COPD affects > 390 million people and is the third leading cause of death worldwide.1,2 Between 30% and 50% of people with COPD experience at least one exacerbation per year,3 characterized by worsening symptoms such as dyspnea, cough with purulent expectoration, and chest tightness. Annually, > 1 million severely ill patients with exacerbations of COPD are admitted to US hospitals, making it among the leading and most costly causes of adult hospitalizations.4,5

Nebulized short-acting bronchodilators (albuterol and ipratropium), with systemic corticosteroids, antibiotics, supplemental oxygen, and as-needed ventilatory assistance form the standard of care for hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD.6 Frequent nebulizer treatments (4-6 hours daily) with short-acting bronchodilators lead to peaks and troughs in airway responses and are cumbersome for patients, nursing staff, and respiratory therapists (RTs). The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD)6 Committee in its 2024 report recommended initial therapy with short-acting inhaled bronchodilators for patients with exacerbations of COPD and continued use of long-acting bronchodilators in those already receiving them. The GOLD report recommends inhaled long-acting beta-agonists (LABAs) in combination with long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs) in patients with stable COPD because combination therapy provides greater bronchodilation than each component.6 However, an inhaled LABA/LAMA combination has not, to our knowledge, been prospectively studied in hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD.6

Formoterol, a nebulized LABA, has an onset of action within 5 minutes, a 12-hour duration of action, and a favorable safety profile.7 Revefenacin is a nebulized LAMA with a 24-hour duration of action.8,9 Formoterol twice daily combined with revefenacin once daily has been shown to provide sustained bronchodilation in patients with stable COPD.10 A few investigators reported pilot studies or retrospective analyses that showed the benefits of using long-acting bronchodilators in patients with exacerbations of COPD.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 However, a large cohort study of 421 US hospitals included in the Premier Healthcare Informatics (Charlotte, North Carolina) database (N = 77,378 patients) found no improvement in clinical or economic outcomes with long-acting bronchodilators in patients with exacerbations of COPD.19 All these studies were performed before nebulized LAMAs (revefenacin and glycopyrrolate) became commercially available. For patients with acute dyspnea hospitalized for exacerbations of COPD, variable drug delivery with handheld inhalers related to coordination with actuation, peak inspiratory flow, volume of air inhaled, and breath-hold time could negatively impact clinical outcomes.20 Therefore, nebulization is preferred for administering inhaled medications in the hospital setting because it requires little patient effort and coordination21, 22, 23 and is well suited for patients with acute dyspnea.24,25

Accordingly, we prospectively compared the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of nebulized formoterol/revefenacin combination instituted within 24 to 36 hours of hospital admission with nebulized albuterol/ipratropium combination in a randomized trial among hospitalized patients with ECOPD.

Study Design and Methods

We conducted a prospective, randomized, parallel-group study to compare nebulized formoterol (20 μg every 12 hours) and revefenacin (175 μg every 24 hours) with nebulized albuterol (2.5 mg) and ipratropium (0.5 mg) 6 hours daily among 60 patients (30 in each group) hospitalized at the University of Tennessee Medical Center in Knoxville, Tennessee, with a primary diagnosis of ECOPD or acute respiratory failure with a secondary diagnosis of exacerbations of COPD (International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision [ICD-10] codes J 44.1 and J 96-J96.2 with J44.1). The study adhered to the tenets of Good Clinical Practice, was approved by the University of Tennessee Graduate School of Medicine in Knoxville, TN (IRB # 4586) institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained before performing any study-related procedures.26 The investigators designed the study, wrote the protocol, conducted the study, analyzed the results, and wrote the manuscript. Mylan and Theravance Biopharma sponsored the study and provided financial support and study medications.

Participants

Eligible patients were ≥ 40 years of age, with none excluded for sex or race, able to understand and comply with study procedures, and tested negative for COVID-19 by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction test. Intubated patients, those receiving mechanical ventilation by mask/artificial airway, and rapidly decompensating patients imminently needing ventilator support were excluded. Patients with unstable cardiovascular disease (recent myocardial infarction within 12 weeks, unstable angina, atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response, ventricular tachycardia, uncontrolled hypertension), decompensated congestive heart failure, asthma, other pulmonary diseases, lobar pneumonia, lung cancer requiring treatment, acute psychiatric illnesses, history of glaucoma, or urinary retention; those hypersensitive to the study drugs; and women who were pregnant or nursing were excluded. Study physicians carefully reviewed patients’ electronic medical records, laboratory tests, ECGs, and radiologic findings to determine that they were consistent with the diagnosis of exacerbations of COPD and that they met all inclusion and exclusion criteria before enrollment.

Randomization

The study pharmacist (D. D. and B. F.) randomized patients 1:1 to either nebulized albuterol/ipratropium or nebulized formoterol/revefenacin. Each patient had a single randomization number indicating the allocated treatment. The treatment assignments were generated using a true random number generator (https://ctrandomization.cancer.gov/home/) with stratification for age groups (< 50, 50-69, ≥ 70 years). The clinical investigators were aware of the assigned treatment group.

Interventions

Trained RTs administered nebulizer treatments with jet nebulizers (West Med; Vix One) and wall oxygen. Patients received revefenacin at the same time each morning with formoterol, and the latter was given each evening after a 12-hour interval. Short-acting bronchodilators were given at the times scheduled by the local bronchodilator protocol. Formoterol and revefenacin were administered sequentially with the same nebulizer, whereas albuterol and ipratropium were mixed and administered together.

All study participants continued taking other medications as prescribed (eg, parenteral, enteral, or inhaled corticosteroids; beta-blockers; diuretics; oral or parenteral antibiotics), according to the recommendation of their treating physicians. Other long-acting inhaled bronchodilators, except formoterol and revefenacin, were not permitted. Nebulized albuterol was provided for breakthrough symptoms in both groups.

Patients continued to receive supplemental oxygen and their assigned treatments for up to 7 days, hospital discharge, failure of treatment, or death prior to 7 days.

Assessments

Screening and baseline assessments were completed within 24 to 36 hours of hospital admission. As shown in Figure 1, medical history, laboratory data, chest radiographs (posteroanterior and lateral), and concurrent medications were reviewed. We measured breathlessness on the modified Borg dyspnea scale, a 10-point numerical scale ranging from 1 (very slight) to 10 (maximal). An unmarked dyspnea scale was administered twice daily by the RT before dosing the study drug. The therapist also recorded Spo2, lowest oxygen flow (L/min) achieved each day, and any reported adverse events (AEs). Other assessments included physical examinations, serum chemistries, CBC count and differential, and ECGs performed before enrollment and on days 1, 3, and 7 or at the end of the trial visit.

Data Analysis

The efficacy, secondary, and safety analyses were performed on the randomized participants who received at least 1 dose of study medication. The primary outcome measure was the difference in modified Borg dyspnea scores between the two groups. The a priori secondary end points were as follows: (1) total visits for study drug administration per day received by each patient, (2) total doses of bronchodilators per hospital stay, (3) number of rescue doses needed per day of hospital stay, (4) number of patients needing to switch/change their treatment regimen, (5) failure of treatment (need for mechanical ventilation or death), and (6) need for supplemental oxygen (lowest oxygen flow in L/min by nasal cannula) on days 1, 3, and 7 of hospital stay. The end points for safety included the number of related AEs, number of related serious AEs (SAEs), side effects reported by patients or observed by the study team, and stability of study participants’ vital signs and laboratory evaluations. We performed the efficacy analysis on the level of dyspnea assessed by the modified Borg dyspnea scale and the safety analysis on the number of related AEs and SAEs reported. Due to the violation of independence of observations and the presence of correlated data associated with some of the primary and secondary outcomes, generalized estimating equations (with an identity link function) were used to test the hypotheses associated with the Borg score, lowest oxygen saturation, total number of study drug doses administered, and total number of rescue doses administered. Marginal means with 95% CIs were reported and interpreted for the generalized estimating equations analyses. Post hoc power calculations were performed for primary and secondary end points that did not achieve statistical significance. Nonparametric tests were used for data that were not normally distributed, as assessed by skewness and kurtosis statistics. Baseline characteristics between the two groups were compared using between-patient statistics, including Mann-Whitney U tests (for nonnormal continuous variables) and χ2 analyses (for categorical variables). The safety analyses were descriptive. Data were reported as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range). With one primary outcome being assessed in the trial, statistical significance was assumed at an unadjusted two-sided alpha value of 0.05. In terms of multiplicity, no adjustments were made to the alpha value for the secondary outcomes. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 29 (IBM Corp).

Overall, both regimens were well tolerated. Only one participant receiving formoterol/revefenacin switched treatment and one patient receiving albuterol/ipratropium required high-flow oxygen by nasal cannula from day 4 onward. There were no instances of failure of treatment (need for mechanical ventilation/death) in either group. One patient receiving albuterol/ipratropium had a transient fall in Spo2 levels after treatment. The episode resolved after an increase in supplemental oxygen flow. No SAEs were reported or observed in either group.

Discussion

In this prospective, randomized study among nonventilated hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD, we found the safety and efficacy of nebulized formoterol every 12 hours and revefenacin every 24 hours in combination instituted within 24 to 36 hours of hospital admission were comparable with the combination of nebulized albuterol and ipratropium administered every 6 hours.

The major goal of treatment in hospitalized patients with severe exacerbations of COPD is lung deflation by intensive bronchodilator therapy that reduces airway resistance, accelerates time constants for lung emptying, restores neuromechanical coupling, and relieves dyspnea.27,28 In outpatients with COPD, bronchodilators reduce lung hyperinflation and pulmonary gas trapping, resulting in increases in inspiratory capacity and vital capacity.29,30 Also, in patients with stable COPD, long-acting bronchodilators, both LAMAs and LABAs, provide greater reductions in hyperinflation than short-acting bronchodilators,31,32 and the LABA/LAMA combination produces additive bronchodilation with a good safety profile.33 The efficacy and safety of the formoterol/revefenacin combination was previously reported in a randomized, double-blind, 42-day trial in outpatients with stable COPD.10

In patients with exacerbations of COPD, the 2024 GOLD report6 recommends the use of inhaled short-acting bronchodilators as initial therapy and suggests continued use of inhaled LABA/LAMA in patients already receiving them or initiation of LABA/LAMA therapy before hospital discharge; however, they acknowledge that no high-quality evidence from randomized controlled trials supports these recommendations. Therefore, our study showing the efficacy of nebulized formoterol/revefenacin combination supports the early initiation of nebulized LABA/LAMA therapy in hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD.

Most comparative bronchodilator studies in patients with COPD rely on objective changes in pulmonary function.34,35 However, changes in spirometric values may be unreliable in patients with exacerbations of COPD because of patients’ inability to perform maximum expiratory maneuvers due to acute dyspnea, bronchospasm, air trapping, and muscle weakness.36,37 Therefore, we chose patient-reported intensity of dyspnea as our primary outcome measure because it correlates well with response to treatment and predicts clinical outcomes in exacerbations of COPD.38

The modified Borg dyspnea scale measures the degree of breathlessness on a 10-point numerical scale from 1 (very slight) to 10 (maximal). The scale is a valid and reliable assessment tool for dyspnea in patients with both stable COPD and exacerbations of COPD.39, 40, 41 In outpatients with exacerbations of COPD managed with pharmacologic treatment, an improvement of 0.9 in the modified Borg dyspnea scale was clinically meaningful.42 Other investigators have suggested that a change of 1.0 point in the Borg dyspnea scale represents a minimal clinically important difference.43 We found that patients in both treatment groups achieved meaningful improvements in their modified Borg dyspnea scale scores by day 3 and the scores were comparable in the two groups (Table 2).

The short-acting bronchodilator regimen must be administered every 4 to 6 hours, is cumbersome for patients, and requires waking them at night to receive treatments. The frequent treatments, coupled with the large number of hospitalized patients needing nebulizer treatments, often overwhelm the ability of RTs, and up to 25% of scheduled treatment doses may be missed.44 Use of long-acting bronchodilators is more convenient for patients and RTs, the treatments did not need to be given at night, and the total visits by RTs for drug administration were reduced by 30% compared with the albuterol/ipratropium regimen (197 vs 285 total doses) (Table 3). Over the duration of the hospital stay, the total doses of study drugs were significantly lower in those receiving formoterol/revefenacin compared with those receiving albuterol/ipratropium (P < .001) (Table 2).

There were no reported SAEs; only one patient had a short-lasting fall in Spo2 after albuterol/ipratropium administration that resolved with an increase in supplemental oxygen flow rate. There were no failures of treatment or deaths. Hospital length of stay (Table 1) and requirement for supplemental oxygen (e-Table 2) did not differ in the two groups of patients, and comparison of laboratory values (e-Table 3), ECGs (e-Table 4), and chest radiographic findings (data not shown) were also similar.

The patients had severe illness as manifested by a mean Charlson Comorbidity Index score of 4.7 and ≥ 3 comorbidities in most patients (≥ 80%) (Table 1). Patients in the two groups had similar comorbidities (e-Table 1). The patients receiving formoterol/revefenacin required fewer RT visits for study nebulizer treatments (P < .05 for days 1 and 3) (Table 3). We administered formoterol and revefenacin sequentially; however, the solutions are compatible45 and can be administered together without loss of efficacy,10 thereby reducing the number of doses and treatment duration for future studies. Although patients receiving formoterol/revefenacin required a higher number of rescue doses, this difference was not statistically significant (P = .08) (Table 2). Overall, most patients in either group did not need any (18 patients in formoterol/revefenacin vs 27 patients in albuterol/ipratropium group) or one (four patients in formoterol/revefenacin vs one patient in albuterol/ipratropium group) rescue dose.

The present study has several limitations. Waiting for a negative test for COVID-19 delayed enrollment. Although we enrolled patients within 24 to 36 hours of hospital admission, we can only speculate if earlier institution of long-acting bronchodilators would have influenced our results.

Only 20 of 60 patients (9 receiving formoterol/revefenacin and 11 receiving albuterol/ipratropium) had prior pulmonary function studies, and all of them showed airflow obstruction with FEV1/FVC ratios of 47.62 (SD 11.02) and 49.40 (SD 13.12) and FEV1 values of 1.19 (SD 0.62) L, and 1.15 (SD 0.67) L, respectively (ρ > 0.05 for both FEV1/FVC ratios and FEV1). We are confident that the patients had COPD because (1) all patients who had pulmonary function tests in the past had evidence of airflow obstruction and (2) no other cause for their exacerbation was identified during their hospital stay. Furthermore, a careful review of patient data by study physicians before enrollment provides confidence in the diagnosis of exacerbations of COPD in the patients.

The absence of double anonymization could have theoretically influenced study personnel’s assessment of outcomes, especially patient-reported outcomes. However, double anonymization would have significantly complicated the design and execution of the study.

Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to determine whether long-acting bronchodilators more effectively relieve dyspnea than short-acting bronchodilators in hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD and to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the long-acting regimen. We recognize the higher acquisition costs for long-acting bronchodilators; however, total costs of therapy include other costs (eg, personnel time to administer the doses, device costs) and further studies are planned to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the long-acting regimen relative to the short-acting regimen. In conclusion, in this prospective, randomized, parallel-group study among nonventilated hospitalized patients with exacerbations of COPD, the institution of nebulized formoterol/revefenacin combination within 24 to 36 hours after hospital admission provided convenient dosing, with fewer drug doses required to achieve efficacy and safety comparable with the standard albuterol/ipratropium combination.
