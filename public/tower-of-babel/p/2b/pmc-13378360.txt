Age and sex-dependent tumor microbiome programs shape pancreatic cancer prognosis
Authors: Jun Yang, Yajing Guo, Shang Cai, Longyun Ye, Pengcheng Zhou, Yong Li, Shuaiwu Luo, Zhenduo Shao, Zhihao Huang, Wenjun Liao, Hui Chen, Yali Li, Linquan Wu, Wei Chen, Enliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPancreatic cancer prognosis is shaped by host factors and the tumor microenvironment. Here, we identify a survival advantage in young female (≤55 years) pancreatic cancer patients—absent in older patients and males. Integrated analysis of SEER and TCGA datasets reveals that host immune and inflammatory pathways associated with bacterial response are enriched in young males, old males, and old females, but notably absent in young females. Direct microbial profiling of 30 pancreatic tumors shows that young female tumors harbor a distinct microbiome with lower Staphylococcus abundance. Functional experiments in a murine model further demonstrate that Staphylococcus promotes tumor growth in an estrogen-dependent manner, providing experimental evidence consistent with the clinical association. Older female tumors exhibit distinct microbial metabolic pathways. Our findings suggest that age and sex jointly shape the pancreatic tumor microbiome, highlighting demographic-specific approaches in cancer microbiology.Keywords: pancreatic cancer, tumor microbiome, sex differences, prognostic biomarker, cancer microenvironment

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, with a five-year survival rate of approximately 10%, despite advances in surgical techniques and systemic therapies.1 Its aggressive biology, frequent late diagnosis, and limited response to conventional treatments contribute to this poor prognosis.2 In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted the crucial role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in pancreatic cancer progression and therapeutic resistance.3 Notably, the intratumoral (i.t.) microbiota—a previously overlooked component of the TME—has emerged as a critical factor influencing carcinogenesis, immune modulation, and treatment outcomes.4,5

The human microbiome, particularly the gut microbiome, has been extensively studied in the context of health and disease.6 However, evidence now suggests that microorganisms also reside within tumor tissues themselves, including those of the pancreas.7,8 These i.t. bacteria are not merely passive bystanders; they can actively influence tumor biology through multiple mechanisms, such as modulating local immune responses, metabolizing chemotherapeutic agents, and producing oncogenic metabolites.9,10 For instance, specific bacterial species have been shown to promote gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) by expressing cytidine deaminase, which inactivates the drug.5 Other studies suggest that microbiota can alter the tumor immune landscape, thereby affecting responses to immunotherapy.11,12,13 However, the causal role of the microbiome in cancer initiation and progression remains uncertain and actively debated.14,15,16 Several recent studies have cautioned that many reported tumor-associated microbes may represent laboratory contaminants, transient bacteria from other body sites, or secondary colonizers of established tumors, rather than true tissue-resident drivers of carcinogenesis.16,17,18

Beyond the simple presence of bacteria, the composition and functional attributes of the tumor microbiome may vary based on host factors.19 Among these, biological sex represents an important yet underexplored variable. Epidemiological and clinical studies have consistently reported sex differences in cancer incidence, progression, and treatment response across multiple tumor types, including pancreatic cancer.20,21 Generally, males exhibit a higher incidence of pancreatic cancer, but the influence of sex on survival remains controversial, with some studies suggesting that females may experience better outcomes, particularly in younger age groups.22,23 These observations raise the possibility that sex-specific differences in the tumor immune microenvironment or microbiome could underlie disparities in clinical behavior.24,25

Indeed, recent work has begun to uncover interactions between sex hormones, immune function, and the microbiome.26,27 For example, estrogen signaling has been shown to shape the gut microbiome composition, which in turn can affect systemic immunity and potentially anti-tumor responses.28,29 However, whether such mechanisms operate within the pancreatic TME and whether they contribute to sex differences in pancreatic cancer outcomes are unknown. In particular, the potential role of an i.t. microbiome that is stratified by sex and age has not been systematically investigated.30,31

A critical barrier to understanding sex-specific influences in pancreatic cancer microbiology lies in the substantial confounding inherent in clinical datasets, where factors such as age, comorbidities, and tumor characteristics often differ between males and females and independently affect outcomes.32,33 To isolate the biological effect of sex, we employed propensity score matching (PSM) using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry, a method that mitigates these confounders and enables more accurate comparisons.34,35

Beyond clinical correlations, molecular data from sources like The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) allow examination of sex-based transcriptomic differences, potentially revealing divergent immune or metabolic pathway activities—including those related to bacterial response, such as Staphylococcus aureus infection, which may signal underlying microbiomic disparities.36,37,38,39

To directly test whether microbial communities differ between sexes and contribute to tumor behavior, we conducted targeted microbiome analyses. We combined 16 S rRNA sequencing with microbial culture and spatial profiling to assess the composition, viability, and distribution of bacteria within tumors.40,41,42 Furthermore, we inferred functional profiles to identify sex-enriched metabolic pathways that may influence disease progression or treatment response.17,43 This integrated approach aims to elucidate whether and how sex-specific microbiomic differences affect pancreatic cancer biology.

Results

Cohort characteristics and PSM

To delineate the clinicopathological landscape of pancreatic cancer, we first conducted a comprehensive analysis of 19,645 patients from the SEER database. As detailed in Table S1 and visually summarized in Figures 1A–1F, the cohort was predominantly male (50.7%), older than 55 years (85.2%), and white (80.1%). Most tumors were located in the head of the pancreas (70.3%), classified as stage IIB (40.7%) or IV (21.0%), and of moderate differentiation (grade II, 48.8%).

Comparative analysis of the unmatched cohorts revealed significant imbalances in key baseline characteristics between male and female patients (Figures S1A and S1B; Table S2). Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in the distribution of age, race, tumor grade, primary site, and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage, indicating a substantial potential for confounding in subsequent survival analyses.

To mitigate this confounding and create a comparable cohort for a robust assessment of sex-based differences in survival, we performed 1:1 PSM. This process successfully generated a well-balanced cohort of 9,464 matched pairs (n = 18,928). As conclusively demonstrated in Table 1, (after PSM) and its visual representation in Figures 1G–1L, the distributions of all measured covariates were effectively balanced between sexes after matching (Figures S1E–S1H). The standardized mean difference (SMD) for all variables was reduced to below the threshold of 0.1, confirming that any residual differences were negligible and that the matched cohorts were highly comparable. This rigorous adjustment established a solid foundation for the subsequent comparative survival analysis.

Age-dependent sexual dimorphism in survival outcomes

Leveraging the well-balanced cohort generated by PSM, we precisely assessed the impact of sex and age on survival outcomes. In the matched SEER cohort, a modest but statistically significant overall survival advantage was observed for female patients compared to males across the entire population (p = 0.042; Figure 2A and Table 2).

Upon stratification by age, this survival advantage was confined to younger female patients. Young females (≤55 years) had a statistically significant but modest survival advantage compared to young males, old males, and old females (p < 0.0001; Figure S2A). The median survival of young females was 15 months, versus 13 months in each of the other three groups.

This survival benefit specific to young females was further corroborated in the direct, age-stratified analysis. Among individuals aged ≤55 years, females exhibited a statistically significant but modest survival benefit compared to males (p = 0.001; Figures 2B, S1C, and Table 2). In contrast, for patients older than 55 years, the sex-based difference in prognosis was completely abolished, with nearly identical survival curves between males and females (p = 0.330; Figures 2C, S1D, and Table 2). This age-specific effect was consistent with the pre-matching analysis (Table S3).

Age-specific transcriptomic differences implicate bacterial response pathways in all but young female patients

To explore the potential molecular underpinnings of this disparity, we analyzed data from TCGA pancreatic cancer cohort (Table S4). The baseline characteristics of the TCGA cohort are summarized in Figures 2G–2I and Table S5. No significant differences were observed between males and females in the overall TCGA cohort (p = 0.365; Figure 2D) or in either age stratum (≤55 years: p = 0.462; >55 years: p = 0.531; Figures 2E and 2F), likely due to the limited sample size. Nevertheless, the consistent directional trend hazard ratios (HR < 1) in the overall and older cohorts aligns with the SEER findings, while the divergent result in the small young subgroup warrants further investigation in larger molecular datasets. To directly elucidate the molecular mechanisms, we next performed an unbiased transcriptomic analysis of TCGA samples, stratified by age and sex. This analysis revealed a profound transcriptomic divergence specific to young females. In patients ≤55 years, we observed clear segregation between males and females, whereas in older patients, the global gene expression profiles between sexes were markedly more homogeneous (Figures 3A–3D).

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis yielded a critical discovery that directly correlates with the survival patterns. Strikingly, we found that KEGG pathways related to host immune and inflammatory responses, including those annotated as Staphylococcus aureus infection, Pertussis, and Legionellosis, were a common feature in the TME of young male, old male, and old female patients, but were notably absent in young females. It is important to note that these pathway annotations represent host gene expression signatures commonly associated with bacterial recognition and response, rather than evidence of active, clinical infection. Their enrichment suggests a state of heightened immune activation within the TME that may be linked to host-microbe interactions. In the young male (≤55 years) cohort, the Staphylococcus aureus infection and Pertussis pathways were significantly enriched among upregulated genes (Figures 3E, 3G, S2B, and S2C). Key driver genes contributing to this enrichment included components of the innate immune response and opsonization, such as C4A/C4B (complement C4-A/C4-B), MASP1(Mannan-Binding Lectin Serine Peptidase 1), PLG (Plasminogen), and FGG (Fibrinogen Gamma Chain). While pathways related to host immune and inflammatory responses were a focus of our analysis due to their direct relevance to our subsequent microbiome investigations, many other pathways were also differentially enriched between subgroups, as shown in Figures 3E–3H. These included metabolic pathways, additional immune-related pathways, and various signaling cascades, providing a broader view of the transcriptomic differences across age-sex subgroups.

Similarly, in the old female (>55 years) cohort, the Staphylococcus aureus infection and Legionellosis pathways were significantly enriched among upregulated genes (Figures 3F, 3H, S2D, and S2E). The gene set defining this enrichment shifted toward antimicrobial peptides and leukocyte recognition receptors, including CAMP (Cathelicidin Antimicrobial Peptide), FPR2 (Formyl Peptide Receptor 2), FCGR2B/FCGR2C (Fc Gamma Receptor IIb/IIc), FCAR (Fc Alpha Receptor), and HLA class II genes (HLA-DQA2 and HLA-DOB). Furthermore, in the old male (>55 years) cohort, the Legionellosis and Toxoplasmosis pathways were significantly enriched, with key upregulated genes including HSPA1A/B (heat shock protein family A (Hsp70)) Member 1A/B) (Figures S2D and S2E).

This consistent enrichment of distinct host immune pathways, including those annotated as bacterial infection responses, in the three patient groups with poorer prognosis, and its absence in the prognostically favorable young female group, provides a compelling molecular correlate to the observed clinical outcomes. While these pathway annotations (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus infection) should not be misinterpreted as evidence of active infection, their enrichment suggests differential host immune activation states across age-sex subgroups, which may be influenced by interactions with the tumor-associated microbiota.

Validation of i.t. bacteria in pancreatic cancer patients

To directly validate the presence of i.t. bacteria suggested by our bioinformatic findings and to investigate potential sex-specific differences, we established an analytical pipeline (Figure 4A) to profile the tumor microbiome in a fresh pancreatic cancer cohort.

We collected tumor tissue samples under sterile conditions from 15 male and 15 female patients undergoing surgical resection, comprising 11 pairs older than 55 years (males, 67.73 ± 8.627 years; females, 68.91 ± 7.259 years) and 4 matched pairs aged ≤55 years (males, 47.00 ± 10.86 years; females, 48.50 ± 9.82 years). This design enabled us to evaluate sex-specific effects across both younger and older cohorts.

qPCR analysis confirmed the significant enrichment of bacterial DNA in both male and female pancreatic tumor tissues compared to sterile blank controls and environmental background control (EBC) (Figure 4B). However, the bacterial load between male and female tumor tissues was not significantly different. Furthermore, we successfully cultured bacteria from the homogenized tumor tissues of both sexes (Figure 4C), providing crucial cultivable evidence to support the molecular data and confirming the presence of viable bacteria within the pancreatic TME.

To spatially visualize and localize bacteria within the tumor architecture, we performed 16 S rRNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and lipoteichoic acid (LTA) immunohistochemistry, targeting a universal bacterial marker and a Gram-positive bacterial component, respectively. Representative staining from both assays confirmed the widespread presence of bacteria in situ. Positive controls using Staphylococcus aureus demonstrated assay specificity (Figures 5A and 6A). Background controls processed without EUB338 probe and without primary antibodies for cytokeratin 8/18 showed no significant fluorescence, confirming the specificity of the detected signals (Figure 5D). We subsequently detected abundant bacterial signals in both cancerous and paracancerous tissues from representative aged female and male patients using 16 S FISH (Figures 5B and 5C) and LTA staining (Figures 6B and 6C). The bacterial signals, visible as discrete aggregates, were consistently observed in close proximity to tumor cells, as visualized by co-staining with the epithelial cell markers Krt8/18.

The congruent results from these two independent histological techniques provide visual and spatial confirmation of intra-pancreatic bacteria, with a strong indication of Gram-positive constituents, thereby fully substantiating the molecular and cultivable evidence obtained from the same cohort.

The pancreatic tumor microbiome exhibits sex-specific taxonomic and functional profiles

Having confirmed the presence of viable bacteria within pancreatic tumors, we performed deep 16 S rRNA sequencing to comprehensively characterize the microbiome in our prospectively collected age-stratified cohort (11 pairs >55 years and 4 pairs ≤55 years) and compared it to environmental controls (EBC, n = 3).

Alpha diversity analysis, measured by the Shannon index, revealed no significant differences between male and female tumors in either the younger (≤55 years, Figure 7A) or older (>55 years, Figure 7B) cohorts, indicating comparable within-sample microbial diversity across age groups. However, beta diversity analysis constrained principal coordinates analysis (cPCoA) demonstrated significant separation of microbial community structures between sexes in both younger (Figure 7C) and older (Figure 7D) patients. Phylogenetic tree analysis further illustrated the evolutionary relationships of microbiota in both age groups (Figures S3A and S3B), providing additional evidence for the distinct microbial compositions between sexes regardless of age.

Taxonomic analysis across multiple levels (phylum, class, order, family; Figures 7E, 7F, and S4A–S4F) and specifically at the genus level (Figures 7G, 7H, and S4G) revealed a distinctive microbial signature uniquely associated with young female patients. Young female tumors exhibited the lowest relative abundance of Staphylococcus compared to other subgroups. Given the modest sample size of the young female subgroup (n = 4), these observations are descriptive in nature and will require validation in larger cohorts. Other subgroups showed distinct compositional profiles: Staphylococcus and Acinetobacter were detected in young male tumors; Staphylococcus, Acinetobacter, and Fusobacterium were detected in older female tumors; while Comamonas and Chryseobacterium were detected in older male tumors.

Notably, microbiomes enriched in older females—the subgroup that loses the survival advantage seen in young females—was predicted to harbor distinct metabolic potentials. Further analysis using the MetaCyc metabolic pathway database (MetaCyc) indicated that the older female-enriched microbiome was associated with specific microbial metabolic processes, including the degradation of chitin derivatives and myo-inositol, the reductive TCA cycle II, and nucleotide dephosphorylation (Figure S5A).

To further explore the functional basis of this observation, we analyzed the differential abundance of predicted microbial enzymes (Figure S5B). The microbiome enriched in older females exhibited an overrepresentation of enzymes involved in substrate degradation and modification, including chitinase and N,N′-diacetylchitobiose phosphorylase (involved in chitin derivative degradation); inositol 2-dehydrogenase and D-chiro-inositol-1-dehydrogenase (involved in myo-inositol degradation); arabinonate dehydratase and galactonate dehydratase (involved in sugar acid metabolism); ureidoglycolate amidohydrolase (involved in urea cycle metabolism); and phospholipase C (a bacterial enzyme that can disrupt host cell membranes). This enzymatic profile provides preliminary insight into the potential metabolic activities of the tumor-associated microbiota, although these predictions require validation by metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, or functional assays.

Staphylococcus promotes tumor growth in an estrogen-dependent manner

Given the observed trend of lower Staphylococcus abundance in young female tumors, we sought to functionally test whether Staphylococcus can promote tumor growth and whether this effect is modulated by estrogen status. We performed i.t. injection of Staphylococcus epidermidis (isolated from a human pancreatic cancer patient) into Panc02 tumor-bearing mice (Figure 8A). Strikingly, in ovariectomized females (estrogen-depleted), Staphylococcus significantly promoted tumor growth compared to PBS controls (0.96 ± 0.37 g vs. 0.46 ± 0.30 g, p = 0.0457; Figures 8B and 8C). In contrast, in sham-operated females with intact estrogen signaling, no significant effect was observed (0.56 ± 0.25 g vs. 0.43 ± 0.22 g, p = 0.410; Figures 8D and 8E). A non-significant trend toward increased tumor weight was observed in males (0.54 ± 0.38 g vs. 0.24 ± 0.14 g, p = 0.133; Figures 8F and 8G). These results demonstrate that estrogen status critically modulates the pro-tumorigenic effect of Staphylococcus.

Discussion

Although the causal role of the microbiome in pancreatic cancer initiation and progression remains uncertain and actively debated,14,15,16 a growing body of literature has established the presence and functional relevance of i.t. bacteria across multiple cancer types. For example, intracellular bacteria in breast tumors promote metastasis,41 microbial signals are detectable within brain tumors with immune-metabolic associations,44 and the spatial positioning of bacteria differentially modulates tumor immunity.45 These studies, along with others,4,8 demonstrate that tumor-associated bacteria are often intracellular, interact with host cells, and influence tumor progression and immune modulation. While we acknowledge that our current dataset does not definitively prove “stability” or “function” in the strictest sense, the convergence of our multi-method approach (qPCR, 16 S sequencing, culture, FISH, LTA staining) with this growing body of literature supports the interpretation that the bacterial signals we detect are likely to represent biologically relevant tumor-associated bacteria rather than passive contaminants.

Our integrated analysis reveals a previously unrecognized association between the i.t. microbiome and the distinct survival advantage of young female pancreatic cancer patients. By combining population-level epidemiology, transcriptomic profiling, and direct microbial validation, we find that a specific microbial feature—relatively low abundance of Staphylococcus in young female tumors—distinguishes this subgroup from other patients. This observation prompted us to functionally test whether Staphylococcus could directly influence tumor growth. Given the substantial functional heterogeneity within the Staphylococcus genus, which includes both commensal and pathogenic species, we opted to use a strain isolated and cultured from a human pancreatic cancer patient—Staphylococcus epidermidis—for our functional validation. However, i.t. injection of this isolate significantly promoted tumor growth in ovariectomized (estrogen-depleted) female mice, but not in sham-operated females with intact estrogen signaling. These results provide causal evidence that the pro-tumorigenic effect of Staphylococcus is critically dependent on estrogen status, suggesting that the favorable prognosis of young female patients may be linked to estrogen-mediated suppression of Staphylococcus pathogenicity. While we acknowledge that using a single isolate does not fully capture the diversity within the genus, this approach provides initial functional evidence supporting our clinical observations. Future studies employing shotgun metagenomic sequencing and a broader panel of isolates will be essential to resolve species- and strain-specific contributions to pancreatic cancer progression.

Analysis of the SEER database revealed a significant survival advantage for young female patients. While the absolute survival differences were modest (2 months), such effect sizes are comparable to those observed with approved therapies in pancreatic cancer46 and may reflect underlying biological distinctions rather than statistical artifacts. We therefore interpret these findings as a signal that young female patients represent a distinct biological subgroup, providing the rationale for our subsequent molecular and microbial investigations. However, it is important to acknowledge that PSM can only balance covariates captured in the SEER registry. Unmeasured confounders—including genetic alterations such as Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutation status, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) mutations, and gene fusions that are known to influence prognosis and treatment response, particularly in younger patients—could not be accounted for.47 Therefore, while our matching approach reduces confounding from measured clinicopathological variables, residual confounding from unmeasured biological factors may still exist.

Our transcriptomic analysis provided the first clue to a potential mechanism: while young male, old male, and old female patients all showed enrichment in host immune pathways annotated with bacterial infection terms (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus infection, Pertussis, Legionellosis), these pathways were conspicuously absent in young females.48,49 It is important to clarify that these pathway enrichments reflect host immune and inflammatory signatures rather than direct evidence of bacterial infection. Nevertheless, this pattern suggests that differential host immune activation states, potentially shaped by interactions with the tumor-associated microbiota, may contribute to the more aggressive tumor phenotype observed in all subgroups except young females.

The functional implications of these microbial differences were particularly evident in older females, who lose the survival advantage seen in young females. The microbiome enriched in older females was associated with specific microbial metabolic processes, including chitin and inositol derivative degradation that may influence the TME.50,51,52 The enrichment of enzymes such as phospholipase C further highlights the potential functional repertoire of these microbial communities. These findings align with emerging evidence that i.t. bacteria can influence cancer biology, as demonstrated in breast cancer models where intracellular microbiota promote metastasis by enhancing cellular resistance to mechanical stress.42

The sex-specific differences we observed are consistent with recent work in pancreatic cancer. He et al. showed that FPR2 drives immune exclusion in a sex-dependent manner,23 which may interact with the microbial differences we identified. Additionally, bacterial metabolites including inositol derivatives have been shown to exhibit sex-specific effects on immunity and cancer signaling.26,28 The age-dependent reversal of microbial patterns suggests that hormonal changes and immunosenescence may reshape the tumor microbiome over time,21,53 potentially explaining why the survival advantage of female patients is confined to younger age groups.

In conclusion, our study reveals an association between the i.t. microbiome and the distinct survival advantage observed in young female pancreatic cancer patients. We found that young female tumors exhibit relatively lower abundance of Staphylococcus, and functional experiments demonstrated that Staphylococcus promotes tumor growth in an estrogen-dependent manner. These findings suggest that interactions between the microbiome and host factors such as sex and age may influence cancer outcomes. While these results are exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts, they highlight the potential relevance of demographic-specific factors in cancer microbiology and may inform future microbiome-based therapeutic strategies.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although we employed PSM to address confounders, residual bias in retrospective data may persist.34,35 Our functional insights are primarily based on predictive bioinformatics, and future mechanistic studies using gnotobiotic models or targeted antimicrobial interventions will be needed to establish causality. The sample size in our prospective cohort, while sufficient for initial insights, warrants expansion in multi-center studies, and future work should include metastatic samples to assess the impact of i.t. bacteria on dissemination. Specifically, the young female subgroup (n = 4 pairs) limits statistical power and generalizability; we therefore frame these results as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Additionally, despite our rigorous contamination controls, we recognize that no study can completely eliminate the possibility of contamination in low-biomass samples. We will pursue quantitative spatial analysis in future work. Future studies employing shotgun metagenomic sequencing and validation in larger, multi-center cohorts will be essential to confirm and extend our findings.43

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants (SEER database cohort)

The study cohort was derived from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) 18 registries database (November 2020 Submission) maintained by the National Cancer Institute. Eligibility criteria included histologically confirmed pancreatic adenocarcinoma (ICD-O-3 site codes C25.0–C25.9; histology codes 8140, 8500), malignant behavior, active follow-up, and availability of key clinical data (age, sex, race, tumor grade, primary site, AJCC stage [6th/7th editions], survival months, vital status). Exclusion criteria were diagnosis via autopsy or death certificate only, missing survival data, and multiple primary tumors. The initial cohort comprised 19,645 patients with a median age of 68 years (range 27–95), 50.7% male, 85.2% older than 55 years, 80.1% White, 70.3% with pancreatic head tumors, and 48.8% with Grade II differentiation. After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), a balanced cohort of 9,464 matched pairs (n = 18,928) was generated, with standardized mean differences (SMD) < 0.1 for all covariates. De-identified data analysis was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval, and data access complied with SEER database regulations.

Human participants (TCGA PAAD cohort)

Clinical and transcriptomic data were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) cohort via the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Inclusion criteria required available RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and complete clinical annotation (age, sex, AJCC stage, survival information). The final cohort included 185 patients (102 males, 83 females), stratified into ≤55 years (n = 32) and >55 years (n = 153) subgroups, with a median age of 65 years (range 33–89) and AJCC stage distribution: I (9.7%), II (64.3%), III (14.6%), IV (11.4%). De-identified data analysis was exempt from IRB approval, and data access adhered to TCGA database regulations.

Human participants (prospective tissue cohort)

Fresh pancreatic tumor tissues were collected from 30 patients undergoing curative resection for pancreatic adenocarcinoma at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Eligibility criteria included histologically confirmed pancreatic adenocarcinoma, no prior neoadjuvant therapy, and ability to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were metastatic disease at diagnosis, concurrent infections, and incomplete clinical follow-up. The cohort included 15 males and 15 females, stratified by median age (≤55 years: 4 pairs, median age 47.5 years; >55 years: 11 pairs, median age 68.3 years). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, approval number: IIT-O-2025-276, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, with experiments conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animal experiments

Eight-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were divided into three groups: OVX-female (ovariectomized females, modeling post-menopausal estrogen-depleted status), SHAM-female (sham-operated females, modeling pre-menopausal status), and SHAM-male (sham-operated males). Due to the logistical constraints of surgical procedures and the need for age-matched controls, all groups were initiated concurrently but harvested at different experimental endpoints based on their respective tumor growth kinetics. All mice were subcutaneously implanted with 1.8×105 Panc02 murine pancreatic cancer cells (BDBIO, cat# C5137) in 100 μL PBS/Matrigel mixture (50 μL PBS +50 μL Matrigel, Corning #356234). When tumors reached 100–200 mm3, mice received doxycycline hyclate (0.4 mg/mL; Sangon Biotech #A600889-0100) in drinking water for one week to deplete endogenous microbiota. Each group was then subdivided to receive weekly intratumoral injection of either PBS or 5 × 106 CFU of Staphylococcus epidermidis (isolated from a human pancreatic cancer patient) in 25 μL PBS. At endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised and weighed. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2026–142).

Method details

Propensity score matching (PSM)

Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression with sex as the dependent variable and covariates including age, race, tumor grade, primary site, and AJCC stage. 1:1 nearest neighbor matching without replacement was performed with a caliper width of 0.02. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with absolute SMD <0.1 considered satisfactory. The matching process was conducted in R (v4.2.1, RRID:SCR_001905) using the MatchIt package (v4.5.0), resulting in a balanced cohort of 9,464 matched pairs for subsequent survival analysis.

Transcriptomic analysis (TCGA cohort)

Raw RNA-seq read counts from the TCGA PAAD cohort were normalized using the DESeq2 package (v1.30.1, RRID:SCR_000154) in R, followed by variance stabilization and log2 transformation. Sex-stratified differential gene expression (DGE) analysis was performed separately for ≤55 years and >55 years subgroups using DESeq2, with significance thresholds set at adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg method) and absolute log2 fold change >1. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes was conducted via KEGG pathway analysis using clusterProfiler (v4.2.2, RRID:SCR_016884), with FDR <0.05 considered statistically significant. No blinding was applied during computational data analysis.

Sample collection and contamination control (prospective cohort)

Fresh pancreatic tumor tissues were macro-dissected to remove necrotic areas and divided into three portions: 200 mg snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for DNA extraction, 100 mg placed in 1 mL sterile PBS on ice for same-day bacterial culture, and the remainder fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathology. All manipulations followed a low-biomass workflow to minimize contamination: instruments were sequentially treated with 10% bleach, 70% ethanol, RNase-/DNase-free water, and autoclaved; biosafety cabinets were UV-irradiated for ≥30 min before use. An environmental background control (EBC) consisting of 200 μL sterile PBS was processed in parallel with each batch of samples to monitor contaminant DNA. The successful culture of bacteria from homogenized tumor tissues (Figure 4C) provided critical evidence that the detected signals correspond to viable bacteria present within the tissue, while sterile PBS controls processed in parallel showed no growth, further supporting the authenticity of the bacterial signals. Sample processing was not blinded due to the exploratory nature of the initial investigation, but data analysis was blinded to sex and age until final validation.

DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 mg frozen tissue aliquots using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN #51804) with bead-beating (TissueLyser II, 30 Hz, 10 min, 4°C) and eluted in 100 μL C6 buffer at 42°C. DNA yields ranged from 400 to 800 ng with 260/280 ratios of 1.8–2.0. The V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515 F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3') with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA #KK8505). A biotin enrichment step (Dynabeads Streptavidin C1) was added to increase library concentration from low-biomass samples. After 8-cycle index PCR (TruePrep Index Kit V3) and 0.8× AMPure XP purification (Beckman Coulter #A63881), libraries (300–450 bp) were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 PE250 platform (Novogene) with technical triplicates for PCR reactions.

Bacterial culture and identification

Homogenized tumor tissues (100 mg) were plated on blood agar plates and incubated at 37°C under aerobic conditions for 48 h. Colony formation was visually inspected and documented as evidence of viable intratumoral bacteria. Sterile PBS was plated as a negative control to monitor environmental contamination. For identification of bacterial strains, colonies were picked and streaked on appropriate plates and cultured for 1–3 days to obtain single colonies. A single colony was then grown in liquid medium, and colony PCR was performed. Briefly, the 15 μL reaction mix contained 2X Es Taq MasterMix (Dye) (CW bio #CW0690) and 200 nM primers 27 F (5′-AGRGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492 R (5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTAYGACTT-3'). The PCR program consisted of 95°C for 10 min; 10 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, touchdown annealing (64°C decreasing by 1°C every second cycle) for 15 s, and 72°C for 60 s; followed by 30 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 55°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 60 s; with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were sequenced, and the resulting sequences were aligned against the 16 S rRNA sequences (Bacteria and Archaea) database using NCBI BLASTn. The Staphylococcus strain used for animal experiments was confirmed as Staphylococcus epidermidis.

Bioinformatic and contamination correction

Sequencing reads were merged, quality-filtered using vsearch (RRID:SCR_024533), denoised to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) via unoise3, and classified against the SILVA v123 database using the vsearch sintax algorithm with a conservative confidence threshold of 0.6 (RRID:SCR_006423). Of note, despite these parameters, species-level assignments were largely unattainable for most ASVs, with the majority of reads reliably classified only to the genus level. This is consistent with known limitations of short-read 16 S sequencing (V4 region) for discriminating closely related species within genera, where species-level annotations frequently fell below the confidence threshold. Sequences classified as non-bacterial (including chloroplasts, mitochondria, and Eukaryota) were removed using the otutab_filter_nonBac.R script from the EasyAmplicon pipeline.55 Contamination correction was performed using qPCR-adjusted absolute abundances and binomial tests to exclude taxa over-represented in EBC. Alpha diversity (Shannon index, observed ASVs) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis PCoA) were calculated using usearch and vegan packages in R. For group comparisons, statistical significance was assessed using the two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test for alpha diversity indices and MRPP (multi-response permutation procedure) for beta diversity, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Taxonomic composition is presented descriptively without statistical testing. No outliers were excluded in any statistical comparison.

Functional prediction

Functional profiles were inferred from the ASV table using PICRUSt2 (RRID:SCR_025965) and annotated to KEGG and MetaCyc (RRID:SCR_007778) databases via the ggpicrust2 package in R. It is important to note that PICRUSt2 predicts metagenomic functional potential based on 16 S rRNA data, rather than directly measuring gene expression or pathway activity. The presence of KEGG terms related to eukaryotic signaling pathways in the output indicates only that microbial genes homologous to some components of these pathways were detected, and does not imply that bacteria can execute these entire eukaryotic pathways. Differential functional analysis between groups was performed using multiple methods implemented in ggpicrust2, with FDR <0.05 considered statistically significant. This analysis included prediction of microbial enzymes and metabolic pathways to elucidate functional differences across age-sex subgroups.

Absolute bacterial quantification (qPCR)

Universal V9 primers (1369 F: 5′-CGGTGAATACGTTCYCGG-3'; 1492 R: 5′-GGWTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3') and probe (1389-probe: 5′-CTTGTACACACCGCCCGTC-3′) were used for absolute bacterial quantification on a Jena qTOWER3 system. A standard curve of serially diluted E. coli DNA (108–101 copies) was run in each plate to calibrate measurements. Results are expressed as log10 equivalent copies per gram tissue after subtracting the EBC background, with technical triplicates performed for each sample and negative controls included to ensure assay specificity.

16 S rRNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FFPE blocks were sectioned at 5 μm thickness, deparaffinized through sequential immersion in xylene and ethanol series, and rehydrated in 2× SSC buffer (Ambion #AM9765) for 10 min at room temperature. Samples underwent enzymatic pretreatment with 1 mg/mL lysozyme (Sigma #62970) for 10 min, 0.05 mg/mL lysostaphin (Sigma #L7386) for 10 min, and 10 mg/mL proteinase K (Ambion #AM2546) for 10 min at room temperature to enhance Gram-positive bacterial cell wall digestion. Cy5-labeled EUB338 universal bacterial probe (5′-GCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3′) and a nonspecific complementary control probe were diluted to 1 ng/μL in hybridization buffer (25% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 1 mg/mL E. coli tRNA, 0.02% BSA, 2× SSC) and incubated overnight at 37°C. Unbound probes were removed by washing in pre-warmed wash buffer for 30 min at 37°C, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma #D9564) for 5 min, and slides were mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium (Beyotime #P0126). Imaging was performed using an inverted epi-fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2). All images were acquired as Z-stacks with 0.5 μm optical sections. Genuine bacterial signals were identified based on two criteria: (1) they appeared as discrete, punctate structures rather than diffuse, sheet-like staining, and (2) they were present in only 1–2 consecutive optical sections, consistent with the size range of individual bacteria (0.5–2 μm). In contrast, nonspecific background or autofluorescence typically persists across multiple Z-planes and appears as diffuse staining. For every tissue section analyzed, parallel control sections were processed (1) without the EUB338 probe, and (2) without primary antibodies for cytokeratin 8/18. These controls were subjected to identical hybridization, washing, and imaging conditions as the experimental samples. No significant fluorescent signals were observed in these control sections under the same acquisition settings, confirming that the signals detected in experimental samples are not attributable to tissue autofluorescence or nonspecific antibody binding. Representative background control images are shown in Figure 5D. Positive controls (Staphylococcus aureus) were used to validate assay specificity.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h at 4°C, infiltrated in 30% sucrose overnight, and embedded in O.C.T. compound. FFPE tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, with antigen retrieval performed by heating sections in citrate buffer (10 mM Sodium Citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) for 15 min using microwave at low-to-medium power. Samples were blocked with TBS containing 2% BSA, 5% donkey serum, and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody staining was performed overnight at 4°C using Mouse Anti-Lipoteichoic Acid antibody (1:100; Abcam #ab20344) and Pig Anti-Cytokeratin 8 + 18 antibody (1:400; Abcam #ab194130). After washing, sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG-Cy5, Jackson ImmunoResearch #715-175-150; Donkey Anti-Pig IgG-488, Jackson ImmunoResearch #706-545-148) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL; Sigma) for 5 min, and slides were mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium. Imaging was performed on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope with blinding applied during image analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to compare overall survival between groups, with Log rank (Mantel–Cox) tests used to assess statistical significance and hazard ratios (HR) calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method. These analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.1) for both the SEER matched cohort and TCGA PAAD cohort, stratified by age and sex.

Group comparisons and statistical tests

Unpaired t-tests were used for comparisons of qPCR results and alpha diversity metrics between groups. Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables where applicable. Significance thresholds were set at two-sided p < 0.05 for general comparisons.

Sample size estimation and data reporting

The prospective cohort sample size (n = 30) was determined based on prior microbiome studies, sufficient to detect medium effect sizes with a power of 0.8 and alpha of 0.05. Statistical parameters including exact n values, what n represents (e.g., number of patients, technical replicates), measures of center (mean/median), and dispersion (SD/SEM) are reported in figures, legends, and tables. Detailed statistical values are provided in Supplementary Excel file.

Assumption testing and software

Normality of data was tested via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and variance homogeneity was assessed using Levene’s test. Outliers were identified via Cook’s distance and excluded if greater than 3× the interquartile range. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1, RRID:SCR_001905) and GraphPad Prism (v9.1), with specific packages including DESeq2, clusterProfiler, edgeR, usearch, and vegan used for transcriptomic and microbiome analyses.
