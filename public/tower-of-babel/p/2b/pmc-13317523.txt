Idiopathic spinal intramedullary epidermoid cyst arising in the thoracic region: illustrative case
Authors: Lemonick Michael B., Graham A. Branscom, Keshv Krishna Srinivasan, Evan M. Ren, Kevin V. Nguyen, Alexander J. Schupper, Matthew T. Carr, Bahie Ezzat, Siddharth Venigalla, Hanya M. Qureshi, Amish Doshi, Satomi Hiya, John Crary, Tanvir Choudhri

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSpinal epidermoid cysts are rare tumors typically found in the lumbosacral region in the intradural extramedullary space. These tumors are usually caused by defects in neural tube closure or acquired through iatrogenic or traumatic insult, with either mechanism leading to the inclusion of epidermal cells in cystic compartments in the spine. Although benign, these cysts can cause symptoms including back pain and weakness, and patients often undergo resection.OBSERVATIONSWe present a unique case of an intramedullary spinal epidermoid cyst appearing in the T6–7 region of a 69-year-old male. Furthermore, subcutaneous epidermoid cysts within the same region as the spinal tumor were observed. A review of the literature revealed that while intramedullary spinal epidermoid cysts are rare, their concurrent appearance with subcutaneous cysts has not been observed, demonstrating the novelty of this case.LESSONSThis case demonstrates the possible occurrence of epidermoid cysts in the intramedullary space, in superior spinal regions, and in the older population. Additionally, such cysts should remain on the differential in individuals with myelopathic symptoms and associated subcutaneous inclusion cysts on the back. Spinal epidermoid cysts, also referred to as spinal epidermoid tumors, are usually slow-growing, benign, intradural lesions that constitute less than 1% of all spinal neoplasms.1,2 They are most commonly caused by embryonic inclusion during neural tube closure.2,3 Other causes include iatrogenic insults, such as lumbar punctures or spinal anesthesia, or trauma, both of which may lead to the implantation of epidermal cells in the spine.4,5 Epidermoid cysts most commonly occur in the lumbosacral region, and they usually present in the intradural extramedullary space.6,7 Clinically, they may cause long-standing symptoms secondary to cord or nerve root compression, such as radiculopathy, motor weakness, incontinence, and nonspecific back pain.2,8

MRI studies are the gold standard for localizing and characterizing lesions of this type, building a differential diagnosis, and planning for resection.2,8 On imaging, these lesions usually appear cystic, often with iso- or hypointensity on T1, usually with peripheral rim-like enhancement, and hyperintensity on T2 MRI.2,9 Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) can also be used for the diagnosis of these lesions, which may show marked diffusion (high signal).9 Histologically, epidermoid cysts consist of a keratin-rich, white, cystic material with encapsulation of a thin layer of stratified squamous epithelium.5,8 Cellular debris and cholesterol clefts have also been noted as findings for such lesions.8,10 Treatment consists of resection, with gross-total resection being the goal.1,7

Here, we present a rare case of an intramedullary epidermoid inclusion cyst arising at the T6–7 region—an uncommon location along and also within the spine—with resultant lower extremity weakness and numbness. Subcutaneous epidermoid cysts were also found on the patient’s back in the same region. Resection of the intradural lesion was successfully performed, which alleviated the patient’s myelopathic symptoms. We also present a review of the literature and other similar reports, thereby providing relevant context for our findings. This report provides a useful example and explanation of how to approach and manage spinal lesions of this type and may therefore inform clinical decision-making surrounding such lesions.

Illustrative Case

The patient is a 69-year-old man with no surgical history who presented with progressive neurological deficits of severe right leg weakness, numbness, and resultant gait instability over the course of several weeks. He was referred to our neurosurgical service, and initial physical examination demonstrated 2/5 strength in the right lower extremity (RLE) with decreased sensation and 5/5 in the left lower extremity (LLE). In particular, the patient exhibited poor control of the RLE as he circumducted his right leg when ambulating. The patient denied any incontinence or saddle anesthesia. MRI showed a heterogeneous, centrally hypointense, T2/STIR expansile intramedullary mass at the level of T6–8 measuring approximately 3.9 cm in length, with axial images showing mixed central and eccentric components, most likely representing an intramedullary tumor. The lesion was causing significant cord expansion, especially on the right. There was no evidence of hypervascularity or infiltrative disease of the surrounding osseous and dural structures (Figs. 1 and 2). Two T1 hypointense lesions were also noted in the subcutaneous fat at the levels of T5 and T3–4. Given the patient’s clinical symptoms and radiographic findings, a T6–7 laminectomy was performed for intradural lesion biopsy, resection, and cord decompression. The patient’s subcutaneous back cysts were also biopsied.

Surgical Procedure and Pathological Analysis

The patient was brought to the operating room, intubated with general anesthesia, and carefully turned onto a standard prone setup. Subcutaneous nodular lesions corresponding with the cysts identified on imaging were seen in the left and right paraspinal regions just superior to the spinal level of interest. Neurological monitoring was used during the case. Using fluoroscopy and local anatomy, we made an incision of approximately 7 cm at the T6–7 space.

Self-retaining retractors were inserted in the wound, and a Bovie electrocautery device was used to dissect the spinous processes and elevate the left paraspinal muscles to the level of the facets. A high-speed drill and Kerrison rongeurs were used to perform T6–7 laminectomies. The ligamentum flavum was removed at the exposed levels. Intraoperative ultrasound was used to guide the dural opening, which was made in the midline around T6–7.

The dura was opened in standard fashion in the left paramedian region with a no. 11 blade and 4-0 silk and Neurolon sutures. The dural opening was then extended with microscissors. The operating microscope was brought into the surgical field, and microdissection was used for the intradural portions of the procedure. As expected, the T6–7 spinal cord was expanded with a yellowish exophytic component coming to the surface, predominantly on the right (Fig. 3A). A significant resection, estimated to be between 95% and 100%, was performed, thereby decompressing the spinal cord (Fig. 3C). The lesion was biopsied and sent for pathological analysis, and postoperative radiographic images confirmed a complete resection (Fig. 4). Pathological analysis showed anucleate keratin debris consistent with an epidermoid cyst (Fig. 5). In addition, pathological analysis of the subcutaneous cysts on the patient’s back showed cysts containing laminated keratin and lined by stratified squamous epithelium. After verifying hemostasis, the dura was closed with a running suture. After testing the dural closure with a Valsalva maneuver, the wound was copiously irrigated. DuraGen was applied to the dura, and then DuraSeal was used to seal any leaks. The wound was closed, a sterile dressing was applied, and the patient was returned to the supine position. The patient was extubated and taken to the recovery unit in stable medical and neurological condition; the patient tolerated the procedure well without complications and remained in stable neurological condition.

Immediate postoperative follow-up the next day showed no new neurological deficits with improved RLE strength of 3/5 with limited yet improved sensation, while LLE strength was 5/5 and sensation remained fully intact. Follow-up at 15 weeks showed 4/5 RLE strength with only slightly decreased sensation and unchanged findings on the left, reflecting continued improvement. The patient continues to be followed.

Discussion

A search of the PubMed database was conducted for English-language articles using the following search terms to identify reports or studies concerning spinal epidermoid tumors: (“Spin*”) AND (“Epidermoid Cyst” OR “Epidermoid Tumor”). Resultant articles were independently reviewed for relevance.

Observations

Several unique aspects of this case deserve fuller mention. First, the intramedullary location of this epidermoid cyst constitutes an atypical presentation. An analysis of a large case series (12 patients) of intraspinal epidermoid and dermoid cysts from Siller et al. found that the cysts in 11 of the 12 examined cases presented in the intradural extramedullary space.7 Indeed, a separate report of 3 intramedullary epidermoid cysts and review of the literature from Rahimizadeh and Sharifi found that, in addition to their reported cases, only 29 such cases have been reported between 1992 and the publication of their article in 2020.1 The authors also estimated that true intramedullary epidermoid cysts occurring without spinal dysraphism or prior surgery comprise only 0.8% of all spinal epidermoid cysts, which already comprise less than 1% of adult spinal tumors.1 In a complementary review of published cases of intramedullary epidermoid cysts prior to 1992, Roux et al. found 47 reports of such cases, with the first appearing in the literature in 1883.11 Sirbu et al. further corroborated the rarity of such a presentation, noting in their literature review that only 139 cases of epidermoid spinal cysts were reported between 1962 and 2019, with 39 being classified as intramedullary cysts.2 Our review of the literature complemented these findings, with the vast majority of reported cases concerning intradural extramedullary examples of spinal epidermoid cysts.

Other unique aspects of this case concern the age of the patient as well as the lack of surgical history. In their review, Sîrbu et al. note a bimodal age distribution of epidermoid spinal cysts, with a peak in childhood and another peak in the mid-30s.2 Other sources also support this; Beechar et al. noted that of 65 reported cases of epidermoid spinal cysts examined in their review, the median age was 23 years.10 In their review in the Journal of the Belgian Society of Radiology, Morbée et al. note that epidermoid spinal cysts are often first noticed between the 2nd and 4th decades of life, when they may first cause clinical symptoms.3 The reason for the normally young age at which patients present concerns the proposed mechanism for the development of this tumor type. In patients with no known instances of possible iatrogenic or traumatic introduction of skin cells into the cord, such as our patient, these tumors are presumed to be caused by problems during neural tube closure. Specifically, congenital spinal epidermoid cysts are related to the inclusion of ectoderm at the time of neural tube closure, which occurs between the 3rd and 4th weeks of life.9 Given the slow-growing nature of these congenital lesions, they tend not to present in infancy.12 However, presentation in childhood is more common, evidenced by the fact that epidermoid cysts constitute approximately 10% of pediatric intraspinal tumors.3

One curious aspect about this case is the existence of subcutaneous epidermoid cysts on the patient’s back. This dermatological finding, which is usually acquired as opposed to being congenital in nature, is not unusual for this patient’s age, and it is unclear if or how these lesions correlate with the intramedullary spinal lesion that is the focus of this report.13 Skin findings associated with spinal epidermoid tumors typically arise with spinal dysraphisms (e.g., hair tufts, hyperpigmentation, etc.), and thus investigating a possible connection between epidermoid cysts in the skin and in the spine provides an avenue for future research.14 To our knowledge, this is the first such report that notes subcutaneous epidermoid cysts on the back as well as epidermoid cysts of the spine within the same region.

The spinal region in which this cyst presented, specifically at the T6–7 space, also merits investigation. Several studies have noted that spinal epidermoid cysts more commonly arise in the lumbosacral region. This reflects the proposed mechanism of defects in neural tube closure, which most commonly occur in the cranial or caudal portions of the tube. Thus, congenital spinal cysts due to defects in tube closure would commonly occur in distal regions of the spine, as seen in the majority of cases. Congenital epidermoid cysts are also commonly associated with spinal dysraphisms, for similar reasons.15,16 In reviewing reports on the location of such lesions, Manno et al. found that most spinal epidermoid cysts form in the lumbar spine (92%), while Munshi et al. found that the lumbar levels and conus regions were the most common sites for the presentation of epidermoid cysts in their series of 15 patients.6,17 However, when specifically addressing common regions for intramedullary epidermoid cysts, Rahimizadeh and Sharifi found in their review that 54.8% of case reports of such tumors since 1992 occurred in the thoracic region.1 Roux et al., in their extensive review of intramedullary epidermoid cysts, found that a majority arose in the thoracic region (31/47 cases), especially between T5 and T8, as was the case in our patient.11 This unique contrast between locations of intra- versus extramedullary (yet still intradural) epidermoid cysts, with intramedullary cysts showing a higher prevalence in the thoracic region, is a unique finding in our review of the literature and is thus another element that is deserving of additional attention in future research.

Radiologically, spinal epidermoid cysts typically demonstrate hypointense to isointense signal on T1-weighted images and hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images.2,4,9 These trends are also observed across both intradural extramedullary and intramedullary spinal epidermoid cysts.2,4 Spinal epidermoid cysts can also be homogeneous or heterogeneous and have minimal gadolinium enhancement on MRI.8,9 There is also documentation of peripheral enhancement on MRI.5 The radiological findings in our case reflect this trend; however, T2-weighted preoperative images in our case showed elements of both hyperintensity and hypointensity, with the central part of the lesion displaying hypointensity, which is likely due to viscous contents within the cyst that were dually observed on operative dissection.18

Pathologically, spinal epidermoid cysts are usually identified as a lining of epithelial tissue supported by a network of collagen.8 Also, there is no clear difference in the histological findings of epidermoid cysts by spinal region or in relation to the dura. Funao et al., in a case report of an intradural extramedullary spinal epidermoid cyst at L4–5, and Dodson et al., in a report of an extramedullary tumor at T12–L1, both observed cyst walls lined with stratified squamous keratinizing epithelium on histological evaluation.4,5 Histological appearance is also similar in intramedullary epidermoid cysts.2 Our pathological results of the spinal cyst identified keratin debris and the presence of viscous cyst content, which were enough to diagnose this lesion as an epidermoid cyst (Fig 5). Pathological examination of the cysts from the patient’s back revealed cyst walls without adnexa, confirming that they were subcutaneous epidermoid cysts.

From a surgical standpoint, our operative goal was maximal resection, which is also the preferred treatment of these cysts.19 This may be complicated by adhesion of the cyst capsule to surrounding neural structures, including the arachnoid membrane, spinal cord, or nerve roots. In the event of strict adherence preventing gross-total resection, subtotal resection should be pursued. However, this increases the recurrence rate of such lesions, especially when basal germinal cells cannot be excised.20 In addition to preventing recurrence and achieving maximal decompression, gross-total resection should also be pursued to limit inflammatory reactions that may result from the release of cyst contents.4 Past reports detail the risk of acute, chemical meningitis in the event of rupture of the cyst capsule and spilling of contents, which may include keratin, fat, and cholesterol, into the subarachnoid space.4,21–23 For these reasons, and for providing symptomatic relief, timely surgical treatment is critical. We were able to achieve gross-total resection in our presented case, and the tumor capsule was observed to be intact. Markers of tumor rupture and spread of contents, including the presence of lipid droplets in the central canal and ventricles, were not observed.21

In terms of extrasurgical management of these spinal cysts, systemic chemotherapy has not been reported as a component of treatment. However, one case report from Li et al. noted successful use of intraoperative local chemotherapy and postoperative radiotherapy in a patient with an epidermoid cyst of the lumbar spine with repeated recurrence despite previous surgeries.24 In addition to chemotherapy, the use of radiotherapy has been reported in several studies.25–27 In particular, radiotherapy has been used in the event of recurrence or malignant transformation of spinal epidermoid cysts, although these instances remain exceedingly rare. Radiotherapy has been more widely reported in the treatment of intracranial epidermoid cysts that have also undergone malignant transformation, but resection is still the first-line treatment.28 In summary, the use of chemotherapy and radiotherapy is not standard of care for the initial treatment of spinal epidermoid cysts, but localized chemotherapy and radiotherapy may be useful adjuvant therapies in the event of cyst recurrence or transformation.

There are several limitations to this study. First, it is difficult to generalize the findings of a single-case study. Additionally, while we understand the patient’s medical history not to include cases of trauma or iatrogenic insult to the thoracic spine, it is possible that he is subject to patient recall bias. Finally, our limited understanding of the presentation of the patient’s dermatological findings in relation to his neurological symptoms poses another limitation to this study. However, this report discusses critical considerations in the diagnosis and treatment of intramedullary epidermoid cysts.

Lessons

Epidermoid inclusion cysts of the spine are rare in the adult population and are usually caused by defects in neural tube closure or the iatrogenic or traumatic introduction of epidermal cells into the spine. They usually occur in the intradural extramedullary space, although intramedullary cysts constitute a small percentage of all cases. Although benign in nature, these cysts may grow over time and result in myelopathic symptoms and back pain corresponding to the affected level. Advanced MRI is essential for lesion characterization and surgical planning, and definitive management typically requires resection. The goal of treatment is prompt gross-total resection to achieve spinal decompression, limit recurrence, and reduce the risk of eventual spillage of cyst contents that may result in inflammation or chemical meningitis. Although spinal epidermoid cysts most commonly arise in the lumbosacral region before the 5th decade of life, they have also been known to arise throughout the spine and may occur at any age. They may also present with subcutaneous epidermoid cysts on the back. As such, this condition should be considered when encountering patients with similar clinical symptoms and radiographic findings, which can appropriately guide treatment and improve long-term prognoses.
