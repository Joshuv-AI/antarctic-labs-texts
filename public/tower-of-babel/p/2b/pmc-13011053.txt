High genotoxicity of CRISPR/Cas9 versus limited efficacy of CRISPRi in chicken primordial germ cells
Authors: Chen Zhang, Mengqi Wang, Banghua He, Xianguang Yang, Xin Zhiguo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CRISPR/Cas9 technology has transformed genome editing across species; however, its application in avian germ cells remains constrained—not only by editing efficiency, but also by limited evaluation of potential genotoxic effects. In this study, we systematically assessed the performance and genomic safety of CRISPR/Cas9 and CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) in chicken primordial germ cells (PGCs). While CRISPR/Cas9 achieved high editing efficiency, it simultaneously induced substantial DNA damage, apoptosis, and sex-specific cell cycle arrest, revealing the pronounced genotoxic sensitivity of PGCs. In contrast, CRISPRi was well tolerated but failed to achieve effective gene repression in chicken cells. Comparative experiments showed that CRISPRi functioned efficiently in human 293T cells but not in chicken PGCs or somatic DF-1 cells, suggesting species-dependent limitations of mammalian-optimized repression systems. Together, these findings reveal a fundamental trade-off—“efficient but toxic” versus “safe but ineffective”—when applying CRISPR tools to avian germ cells. Our results highlight the need for species-adapted, low-toxicity genome-editing platforms in poultry and provide a framework for evaluating editing strategies in developmentally sensitive cell types.

Introduction

Poultry are economically important animals that serve as a primary source of meat and eggs for humans. However, compared to livestock, the development of breeding and reproductive technologies in poultry remains relatively underdeveloped. Livestock breeding has widely adopted techniques such as genomic selection, frozen semen, artificial insemination, and embryo transfer, enabling large-scale propagation of superior genetics and reducing resource wastage through sex-controlled semen (Hasler, 2003; Bonet et al., 2013; Brito et al., 2019). In contrast, poultry—due to their oviparous nature—face difficulties in directly applying these technologies. Current poultry breeding still primarily relies on genomic selection to improve single production traits such as egg-laying rate, growth rate, and feed conversion efficiency (Saxena and Kolluri, 2018). Although genomic selection has substantially accelerated genetic gain, it operates within the limits of existing allelic variation and recombination-based inheritance. As a result, the incorporation of novel resistance alleles from wild or exotic germplasm through conventional breeding remains time-consuming and frequently associated with linkage drag. Moreover, negative genetic correlations between productivity and robustness often complicate simultaneous multi-trait improvement. Therefore, establishing stable and efficient gene-editing technologies as a precise genetic intervention to complement traditional breeding holds promise for overcoming the limitations of oviparous reproduction. This approach enables the precise integration of multiple traits by bypassing the biological constraints of sexual recombination, achieving synergistic improvements in both productivity and disease resistance.

Primordial germ cells (PGCs) are germline precursor cells that can serve as carriers for germplasm preservation. Long-term in vitro culture and cryopreservation of PGCs have been successfully achieved (van de Lavoir et al., 2006; Whyte et al., 2015). By performing efficient gene editing on circulating PGCs in vitro and leveraging their ability to migrate to the gonads, the operational hurdles associated with avian oviparity can be circumvented, allowing the generation of edited offspring and enabling precise improvement of target traits. In recent years, the CRISPR/Cas9 system has been widely applied in PGC research. Starting in 2015, when Véron et al. (2015) first knocked out PAX7 in chicken somites via electroporation of CRISPR/Cas9, CRISPR/Cas9 gained prominence in poultry research, particularly in studies utilizing PGCs. Oishi et al. (2016) used CRISPR/Cas9 in PGCs to mutate ovomucoid and subsequently obtained OVM-mutant offspring through blood-vessel transplantation. To enhance disease resistance in a commercial chicken line, Hellmich et al. (2020) employed CRISPR/Cas9 in PGCs to mutate a susceptible tryptophan at position 38 (W38), generating lines resistant to ALV-J. Fallahshahroudi et al. (2025) generated miR-2954 knockout chickens using PGCs and uncovered a unique sex-chromosome dosage compensation mechanism in birds. Thus, utilizing PGCs as a model to produce gene-edited chickens has broad applications in both breed improvement and fundamental research (Dimitrov et al., 2016; Challagulla et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024). However, current research often prioritizes editing efficiency while overlooking the potential for population loss and declined cellular fitness. For instance, the low mutant frequency (15%) in G0 semen reported by Oishi et al. (2016) suggests a competitive disadvantage for edited cells. Given that the field currently prioritizes editing efficiency while often overlooking post-editing population loss and declined cellular fitness, it is imperative to conduct comprehensive assessments of cytotoxicity and genomic integrity to ensure the biological quality of edited PGCs.

The use of CRISPR/Cas9 for genome-wide, large-scale screening is now common across various fields (Shalem et al., 2014; Parnas et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2020). However, a major concern when employing CRISPR/Cas9 for functional screening is the p53-mediated DNA damage response triggered by DNA double-strand breaks, which can skew results by selectively eliminating cells with intact p53 function (Haapaniemi et al., 2018). Avian PGCs enforce stringent quality control to ensure extremely high genomic fidelity, often preferring apoptosis over repair. Upon DNA damage, PGCs upregulate pro-apoptotic factors (e.g., FAS and BAK1) and downregulate pluripotency markers like NANOG, effectively purging damaged cells to protect the germline (Rengaraj et al., 2022). It remains unclear whether this protective mechanism, while conferring stringent genomic quality control, also renders PGCs hypersensitive to CRISPR/Cas9-induced DNA damage. Meanwhile, CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), as an epigenetic regulatory tool that does not induce DNA cleavage, theoretically circumvents genotoxicity concerns; however, its gene knockdown efficiency and stability in avian PGCs have yet to be systematically evaluated.

Here, we systematically evaluated the performance and safety of different gene-editing tools in chicken PGCs, revealing several key findings. We observed that while CRISPR/Cas9 achieves efficient editing, it simultaneously triggers apoptosis and increases phosphorylated histone H2AX (γ-H2AX) signals. Additionally, under both chemically induced and radiation-induced conditions, PGCs exhibit higher sensitivity to DNA damage than somatic cells. Finally, CRISPRi-mediated gene knockdown is significantly less effective in PGCs compared to mammalian cells. These results uncover the unique DNA damage response mechanisms in avian PGCs, highlight the need for species-specific optimization of gene-editing technologies, and provide an empirical basis for developing efficient, low-toxicity editing tools tailored to avian germ cells. Collectively, this work offers important insights for advancing poultry germplasm innovation and reproductive biology research.

Materials and methods

Chicken PGC isolation and culture

Fertilized Jinhua chicken eggs (Gallus gallus) were obtained from Yukang Agricultural Development Co., Ltd. Eggs were incubated at 37 °C (humidified, no rocking) until Hamburger–Hamilton stage 16 (∼60 h of incubation), when primordial germ cells enter the embryonic circulation. Embryos were accessed at the blunt pole of the egg using sterile forceps under a dissection microscope. The dorsal aorta was visualized and gently pierced with a glass capillary pipette to draw ∼1–2 µL of embryonic blood, which was immediately transferred into 200 µL of pre-warmed PGC culture medium in a 96-well plate. In some preparations, red blood cells were selectively lysed by mixing the blood with 10× volume of ACK lysis buffer (Thermo A1049201) on ice for 15–30 min prior to seeding. Remaining cells were then pelleted (5 min at 200–300×g, 4 °C), washed with PBS, and resuspended in culture medium. The base culture medium was Avian KnockOut DMEM (a calcium-free modified DMEM; MeilunBio PWL037) supplemented with 1× B-27 (Gibco 17504-044), 1× non-essential amino acids (Gibco 11140-050), 1× GlutaMAX (Gibco 35050-061), 1× nucleosides (Millipore ES-008-D), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma M3148), 1.34 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco 11360-039), 0.15 mM CaCl₂ (Sigma 499609), 0.2% ovalbumin (Sigma A5503), and 100 µg/mL heparin sodium (MCE HY-17567A). Prior to use, this basal medium was further supplemented with 100 µg/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Life 15140122), a mycoplasma inhibitor (Lonza VZA-2022), 4 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF2; novoprotein C046), 25 ng/mL Activin A (novoprotein C687), 25 ng/mL insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1; novoprotein C032), 10 µg/mL chicken ovotransferrin (Sigma C7786), and 0.2% filtered chicken serum (VivaCell C2550-0100). This serum-free, low-calcium medium (sometimes termed "FAOT" medium for FGF2/Activin/Ovotransferrin) supports long-term expansion of chicken PGCs in semi-suspension culture (Whyte et al., 2015). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, with half of the medium changed every 2 days. PGCs remained mostly non-adherent, often forming small floating clumps; to prevent over-clumping, gentle pipetting was performed weekly after feeding. After about one week in culture, small PGC colonies became visible. By 2–3 weeks, cultures typically contained several ×104 PGCs, at which point they were expanded to larger wells or split to maintain a density below ∼3×105 cells per 0.5 mL (24-well) to ensure logarithmic growth. Cultures with poor growth (< 5×104 cells after 4 weeks) were considered non-established and discarded.

To cryopreserve PGC lines, cells in mid-log phase (approximately 1–2×105 cells in 0.5 mL) were harvested and mixed 1:1 with filter-sterilized freezing medium (final concentrations: 8% DMSO, 10% chicken serum in basal medium with 0.15 mM Ca²+). Aliquots were cooled in a controlled-rate freezing container at –80 °C overnight and then transferred to liquid nitrogen. For revival, vials were thawed quickly at room temperature, and cells were immediately diluted dropwise into 4× volume of room-temperature basal medium. After centrifugation (5 min, 200×g), the cell pellet was resuspended in fresh PGC culture medium and returned to 37 °C. Post-thaw viability typically exceeded 90% for healthy cultures.

Each PGC culture was confirmed to consist of primordial germ cells by morphology and marker expression. Live PGCs were identified by their large size (> 12 µm) and distinctive phase-bright cytoplasm with refractile granules. For further confirmation, an aliquot of cells was examined by immunofluorescence staining for stage-specific embryonic antigen 1 (SSEA-1) and chicken VASA homolog (CVH, also known as DDX4), which are specifically expressed by avian PGCs (see “Immunofluorescence Staining and Imaging” below). The sex of each PGC line was determined via PCR genotyping of the embryo’s genomic DNA: tissue from the same embryo was digested in alkaline lysis buffer, neutralized, and used as template for amplification of Z- and W-linked CHD1 gene fragments (580 bp Z allele; 434 bp W allele). No live animal procedures were performed in this study; all PGCs were obtained from early embryos (pre-hatching stages), and experiments were carried out entirely in vitro in compliance with institutional guidelines.

Plasmid transfection and genome editing in PGCs

To validate CRISPRi functionality, we first constructed a lentiviral CRISPRi vector. We modified pLV hU6-sgRNA hUbC-dCas9-KRAB-T2A-GFP (Addgene #71237) to generate a single-expression plasmid, pLV-PGK-dCas9-KRAB-T2A-GFP-BSD, for constitutive dCas9-KRAB expression in PGCs.

All DNA mixtures (CRISPRi plasmid + control/mock or sgRNAs, plus other plasmids as listed in Table S1) were transfected following the protocol below. For co-transfection of Sleeping Beauty transposon constructs, the vector pSB-CAG-EGFP (VectorBuilder VB240513-1797jga) was co-transfected with the transposase plasmid pRP-CMV>T7/SB100X at a 2:1 mass ratio. Solution A: 1 µg of total DNA was diluted in 50 µL Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher 31985070). Solution B: 3 µL of FuGENE® HD transfection reagent (Promega E2311) was diluted in 50 µL Opti-MEM. Solution A was gently mixed and added dropwise to Solution B. After a brief vortex, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, the transfection complex was added to ∼4×104 PGCs, mixed gently, and incubated at 37 °C for another 15 min. The cell–DNA mixture was then transferred to one well of a 24-well plate and cultured for 48 h.

To enrich for successfully transfected PGCs, antibiotic selection was applied corresponding to the resistance markers on the plasmids. After 48 h post-transfection, cells were pelleted and resuspended in PGC medium containing the appropriate antibiotic. For example, plasmids carrying a puromycin-resistance gene were selected with 3 µg/mL puromycin (Solarbio P8230) for 12 h. For blasticidin resistance, 40 µg/mL blasticidin S (Solarbio B9300) was used for 12 h. These concentrations were determined by prior kill curves on untransfected PGCs. After the selection period, surviving PGCs (presumably stably transfected) were expanded in antibiotic-free PGC medium for subsequent assays.

In some experiments, 48 h after transfection with pSB-CAG-EGFP, cells were subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate EGFP-positive PGCs, ensuring a pure population of transgenic cells.

To achieve higher transfection efficiency, we also employed electroporation using a Lonza 4D-Nucleofector system. PGCs were concentrated by gentle centrifugation (5 min, 300×g) and resuspended in Lonza P3 Primary Cell Nucleofector solution (20 µL per reaction; Lonza V4XP-3032) containing the plasmid DNA. For each electroporation, approximately 2×105 PGCs and 1 µg of plasmid DNA were placed in one well of a 16-well Nucleocuvette strip (Lonza). Electroporation was performed using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector™ X Unit with program code CM-137. Immediately after the pulse, 100 µL of pre-warmed PGC medium was added to the cuvette, and the cells were gently transferred to a 96-well plate with an additional 100 µL of medium. This electroporation protocol, based on recent optimizations for feeder-free chicken PGCs, typically achieves high DNA delivery efficiency (60–70% transfected cells) with good viability (Zhao et al., 2025). Following electroporation, PGCs were cultured for 48 h before downstream analyses or drug selection. For CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing, cells were electroporated with a mix of Cas9/sgRNA plasmid (1–3 µg Cas9 at equivalent molar amounts, using a 1:1.2 Cas9:sgRNA ratio). Co-transfection under these conditions has been shown to permit precise genomic integration in a substantial fraction of PGCs (Zhao et al., 2025). The gRNAs used in this study are listed in Table S1. For CRISPRi gRNA design, transcription start site (TSS) regions (−50 to +100) of target genes were first predicted using the EPD database (Dreos et al., 2015; Meylan et al., 2019). The sequences were then input into the CRISPOR tool (Concordet and Haeussler, 2018) to identify optimal sgRNAs. gRNAs with high predicted specificity and minimal off-target effects were selected and synthesized (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai), and the corresponding expression vectors were constructed by Suzhou Zuoxing Biology.

DNA damage treatment assays

To examine DNA damage responses in PGCs, we treated cells with either a DNA double-strand break (DSB)-inducing chemotherapeutic or ionizing radiation. For chemical induction of DSBs, PGC cultures were exposed to etoposide (a topoisomerase II inhibitor; MCE 33419-42-0) at various concentrations (0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 µM, and 1 mM) for 24 h. Control cells were treated with vehicle for the same durations. Etoposide creates DNA breaks and activates DNA damage signaling (Saleh et al., 2012), enabling assessment of DNA repair responses. For physical induction of DNA damage, PGCs were subjected to X-ray irradiation using an X-ray generator (Radsource RS2000Pro-225). Cells were irradiated at doses of 2, 4, 6, and 8 Gy (dose rate ∼1.5 Gy/min). Sham-irradiated controls were kept at room temperature for an equivalent duration. Following these treatments, cells were either immediately analyzed or returned to the incubator for 48 h of recovery before analysis. DNA damage was primarily quantified by immunofluorescent detection of γ-H2AX foci (see below). In addition, cell viability after DNA damage was measured using the CCK-8 assay; apoptosis was evaluated by flow cytometric analysis of Annexin V/PI staining; and cell cycle distribution was determined by flow cytometric analysis of PI staining (see below). All DNA damage experiments were performed with at least three independent PGC cultures.

Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining

PGCs were fixed in 70% ethanol for 10 min. A PAS staining kit (Solarbio G1360-5) was used as follows: 100 μL of periodic acid solution (room temperature) was added to each sample and incubated for 10 min in the dark. Cells were collected and washed 3× in PBS (5 min each). Next, 100 μL of Schiff’s reagent was added, and samples were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After removing the reagent, samples were washed in PBS for 5 min. Cells were counterstained with hematoxylin for 30 s and rinsed with PBS to remove excess dye. Stained cells were observed directly in a 24-well plate (or transferred to slides for microscopy).

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

Suspension PGCs (∼5×104 cells) were placed onto a glass slide (within a hydrophobic boundary drawn by a PAP pen) and allowed to settle on a 37 °C slide warmer for ∼10 min to promote attachment. Cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS for 10 min at room temperature and washed 3× with PBS. Permeabilization was performed with 0.1% Triton X-100 (plus 0.01% Tween-20) in PBS for 20 min, followed by three PBS washes. To block non-specific binding, cells were incubated in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in 3% BSA/PBS and applied to the cells overnight at 4 °C (or for 2–3 h at room temperature). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-SSEA-1 (mouse IgM, Santa Cruz sc-21702; 1:500 dilution), anti-VASA/DDX4 (rabbit IgG, Abcam ab13840; 1:100 dilution), and anti-γ-H2AX (phospho-H2AX Ser139, Millipore 07-164; 1:1000 dilution). After primary incubation, slides were washed 3× in PBS (5 min each). Appropriate secondary antibodies were then applied for 1–2 h at room temperature in the dark, including Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated antibodies against mouse IgM/IgG (for SSEA-1; Thermo A11029, 1:1000) and rabbit IgG (for γ-H2AX; Thermo A11008, 1:1000), and Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (for VASA; Thermo A11012, 1:1000). After three final PBS washes, slides were mounted with fluorescence medium containing DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; 1 µg/mL) to counterstain nuclei. Coverslips were sealed with nail polish, and slides were stored at 4 °C in the dark until imaging. The number of γ-H2AX foci per cell was quantified using ImageJ by counting total foci and total nuclei in captured images.

Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus (Evident) SpinSR spinning-disk confocal microscope with appropriate filter sets and a 60× objective. Exposure settings were kept constant between control and experimental samples. PGC identity was confirmed by co-staining: PGCs showed strong surface SSEA-1 and cytoplasmic VASA signals, distinguishing them from any somatic cell contaminants. Negative-control slides (omitting primary antibodies) showed negligible fluorescence. All image processing and analysis were performed with Olympus CellSens Dimension software under identical parameters for all groups.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

PGC proliferation and viability under various conditions were assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Yeasen 40203ES76) colorimetric assay. PGCs were plated in 96-well flat-bottom plates at ∼3×103 cells per well in 200 µL of PGC medium, with treatments (e.g., DNA-damaging agents or other compounds) applied as appropriate and vehicle controls included. At designated time points, CCK-8 solution was added directly to each well (20 µL, i.e. 10% of the culture volume) without removing the medium, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 0.5 h (for 293T, DF-1, and THP-1 cells), 4 h (for CEF cells), or 2 h (for PGCs). Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured with a microplate reader. Each condition was tested in 3–5 replicate wells, and background absorbance (medium plus CCK-8 in empty wells) was subtracted. Relative cell viability (%) was calculated by normalizing the absorbance of treated wells to that of control wells (after background correction): Viability = 100% × [(Atreated– Ablank) / (Acontrol– Ablank)]. Growth curves were generated by performing CCK-8 assays at multiple time points (e.g., daily) and plotting the increase in absorbance, which reflects cell proliferation. In addition, cell counts and viability were independently verified using a Trypan Blue exclusion assay on a DeNovix CellDrop automated cell counter. These assays confirmed that PGCs maintain high viability (>90%) in standard culture, and that cytotoxic treatments (such as high-dose etoposide) reduce viable cell numbers in a dose-dependent manner.

Cell cycle detection

PGCs were washed once with PBS and collected by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. The cell concentration was adjusted to 1×106/mL to prepare a single-cell suspension. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and cells were fixed in 500 μL of pre-cooled 70% ethanol for at least 2 h (up to overnight) at 4 °C. Prior to staining, fixed cells were washed with PBS to remove ethanol. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL RNase A solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 400 μL propidium iodide (PI) staining solution (Solarbio CA1510) was added, and samples were incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry, and red fluorescence (PI) was recorded with 488 nm excitation.

Cell apoptosis detection

Treated cells were collected and centrifuged at 100×g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed once with PBS. After another centrifugation, the supernatant was removed and cells were resuspended in 500 µL of 1× Annexin V Binding Buffer (from Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit, Elabscience E-CK-A211). Next, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC reagent and 5 µL of PI reagent (50 µg/mL) were added to the cell suspension. The mixture was gently vortexed and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15–20 min. Samples were then immediately analyzed by flow cytometry, with fluorescence detection in the FITC and PerCP/Cy5.5 channels.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Cowin CW2333S) and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min in SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime P0015F). Proteins were separated on a 4–20% precast gel (ACE ET15420Gel) in MOPS running buffer (ACE BR0001-02) at 160 V for 45 min. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore IPVH00010) using a transfer buffer (Tris and glycine, BioFroxx) at 200 mA for 1 h 40 min. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk (Lanjieke BS102) for 1 h at room temperature and washed three times with TBST (Solarbio T1082). Mouse Cas9 antibody (SAB4200701, sigma)/ rabbit anti–γ-H2AX antibody (Abcam ab81299) was diluted in TBST and incubated with the membrane overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime A0208/A0216) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using an ECL kit (Proteintech PK10002) and imaged with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc™ XRS+ system.

Analysis of editing sites

To assess the editing efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9, cells were collected 48 h after co-electroporation of Cas9 and sgRNA. Genomic DNA was extracted and the target locus was amplified by PCR. The amplicons were subjected to Sanger sequencing, and sequencing traces were analyzed using the Inference of CRISPR Edits (ICE) software tool (v3.0, Synthego). Additionally, PCR products were digested with T7 Endonuclease I (T7EI; NEB M0302S) and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to visualize indels.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells, and cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme R323-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was performed in triplicate using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Q712-02) on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System. Cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH mRNA was amplified as an endogenous control for normalization. Relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Data analysis and statistics

Unless noted otherwise, all quantitative experiments were performed with n ≥ 3 independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 software. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. For multi-group comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied, followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Numerical p-values are shown directly on the figure. Where relevant, outliers were identified using the Grubbs’ test and excluded from analysis.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9 achieves high editing efficiency in PGCs

Chicken PGCs isolated from embryonic blood (at ∼2.5 days of incubation) displayed the expected morphology of avian PGCs—large, refractile cells often in clumps (Fig. 1A) —similar to previous reports (Karagenç et al., 1996; Whyte et al., 2015). Growth curve analysis showed no significant sex-specific difference in proliferation rate between male and female PGCs (Supplementary Fig. 1A; p = 0.14). Immunofluorescence confirmed that PGCs express the germ cell markers DDX4 (VASA) and SSEA-1, with VASA localized to the cytoplasm and SSEA-1 on the cell surface (Fig. 1B). By contrast, DF-1 somatic cells were negative for these markers (Supplementary Fig. 1B). PAS staining revealed abundant magenta-stained cytoplasmic glycogen granules in PGCs, whereas control DF-1 fibroblast cells showed negligible PAS reactivity (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Together, these results demonstrate that we established stable male and female PGC lines in vitro that proliferate robustly and maintain key PGC features. To explore suitable gene-editing approaches in PGCs—especially for potential genome-wide screening—we next generated a stable EGFP-reporter PGC line by integrating a CAG promoter–driven EGFP cassette via the Sleeping Beauty transposon system and sorting EGFP-positive cells by FACS (Supplementary Fig. 1D). We then co-electroporated these EGFP+ PGCs with either Cas9 mRNA or Cas9 protein (various doses) along with targeting sgRNAs. Higher doses of Cas9 (3 µg vs. 1 µg) led to more pronounced cell loss by 5 days post-electroporation (Fig. 1C; left panels show Cas9 mRNA conditions, right panels Cas9 protein). Flow cytometry confirmed a marked reduction in EGFP fluorescence in the CRISPR-edited populations (Fig. 1D). Quantitatively, the percentage of EGFP+ cells significantly decreased in all Cas9-treated groups compared to the pSB-EGFP control (98.77±0.12%). Specifically, in the Cas9 mRNA groups, the percentage of EGFP+ cells dropped to 22.37±0.23%, 33.23±1.70%, and 6.59±0.27% for doses 1 µg, 2 µg, and 3 µg, respectively. In the Cas9 protein groups, EGFP+ cell percentages were 82.13±0.53%, 54.03±0.44%, and 38.47±0.58% for the three corresponding doses. This dosage-dependent decline was accompanied by a sharp decrease in live-cell recovery during flow cytometry (Fig. 1D, right panel), demonstrating that Cas9 treatment, particularly in mRNA form or at higher protein doses, leads to substantial population loss in PGCs. Furthermore, a T7EI assay of the EGFP target region confirmed Cas9-induced indels, evidenced by cleaved heteroduplex bands (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Sanger sequencing and ICE analysis revealed diverse indel alleles near the PAM site in edited cells (Supplementary Fig. 1F). Despite the high editing efficiency achieved by CRISPR/Cas9, the associated cellular damage cannot be ignored.

CRISPR/Cas9 induces DNA damage and apoptosis in PGCs

We next investigated the potential genotoxic effects of CRISPR/Cas9 in chicken PGCs. PGCs (and controls with dCas9) were electroporated with Cas9 or dCas9 plus various sgRNAs (targeting Ifitm3, Ifnar1, or miR-2954), then assessed for apoptosis 24 h later. These target genes were selected because they have been studied in avian cells (Samy et al., 2025; Fallahshahroudi et al., 2025; Jenkins et al., 2025). Because CRISPR/Cas9 creates site-specific DSBs, we also measured γ-H2AX foci as a marker of DNA damage 24 h after electroporation with Cas9 or dCas9 and the same set of sgRNAs (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. 2B). Cas9-mediated targeting led to a significant increase in γ-H2AX foci per cell (p<0.01) compared to both the Cas9-only (no sgRNA) group and the mock control (Fig. 2D). Crucially, the use of a catalytically inactive dCas9 with sgRNA did not elevate γ-H2AX signals. This crucial control proves that the observed DNA damage is specifically triggered by the DSBs generated by nuclease cleavage, rather than the electroporation process or the expression of exogenous proteins. Thus, CRISPR/Cas9 gene targeting—but not Cas9 expression alone—significantly increases γ-H2AX foci, confirming that on-target CRISPR cuts induce a DNA damage response in PGCs.

PGCs are more sensitive to DNA damage than somatic cells

The pronounced cell death following CRISPR editing prompted us to examine the general DNA damage sensitivity of PGCs relative to somatic cells. We compared PGC responses to those of several somatic cell types—chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEF), DF-1 chicken fibroblast cells, human HEK 293T cells, and human THP-1 monocytes—after treating with the DSB-inducing agent etoposide (ETP). Etoposide stabilizes DNA cleavage complexes (Saleh et al., 2012). To compare DNA damage sensitivity across cell types, we included chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEF), DF-1 cells, and human cell lines (293T and THP-1) as reference populations. We first calculated the IC50 of ETP for male and female PGCs. Both male and female PGCs were highly sensitive to ETP (IC50 ∼3 µM), whereas the IC50 for CEF cells was about 29 µM (Fig. 3A). In contrast to primary cells from chicken embryos, established cell lines (chicken or human, adherent or suspension) showed much higher tolerance to DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 3A). We next examined apoptosis in PGCs at a low dose of ETP. Even at 0.03 µM ETP, we observed a clear increase in late-apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+; p<0.01) (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. 3B). Western blotting also showed a strong increase in γ-H2AX protein levels following 3 µM ETP treatment (p<0.01, Fig. 3C). CRISPRi exhibits limited gene knockdown efficiency in PGCs

As a less genotoxic alternative for gene perturbation, we evaluated CRISPR interference in chicken cells. Using a dual-plasmid reporter system targeting a CAG-driven mCherry cassette, we compared CRISPRi-mediated transcriptional repression in chicken cells versus human cells (293T), which served as a positive control (Fig. 4A). When targeting the CAG promoter, flow cytometry showed a significant reduction in mCherry+ cells in 293T, whereas DF-1 cells showed no change and PGCs showed only a minor decrease (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. 4A). This result suggests that CRISPRi does not achieve the same high efficiency in chicken cells as in mammalian cells. To rule out insufficient dCas9 expression due to transient transfection, we established a stable CRISPRi PGC line by introducing the dCas9-KRAB construct via lentiviral transduction and selecting with blasticidin. qRT-PCR confirmed significantly higher dCas9 expression in CRISPRi cells than in control PGCs (p<0.05; Supplementary Fig. 4B). Western blot further confirmed the protein expression of dCas9-KRAB in the CRISPRi cell line (Fig. 4C). We then co-transfected three different sgRNAs per target gene into the CRISPRi cell line; however, qRT-PCR showed no significant knockdown of any target gene (Fig. 4D). In summary, although CRISPRi induces negligible DNA damage, its efficacy in silencing target genes in PGCs is substantially lower than in mammalian cells. Discussion

The CRISPR/Cas9 system enables targeted genome editing by introducing site-specific DSBs, which engage endogenous DNA repair pathways to create insertions or deletions (indels) (Ran et al., 2013). While capable of high-efficiency editing, this approach carries significant risks: Cas9-induced breaks can lead to large-scale chromosomal deletions (Cullot et al., 2019), chromosome fragmentation (Leibowitz et al., 2021), and other genomic rearrangements (Tao et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022). Multiplex editing further elevates cell mortality (Hart et al., 2015; Munoz et al., 2016), a phenomenon particularly severe in human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), where even a single cut can eliminate most cells (Ihry et al., 2018). In this study, we found that chicken PGCs exhibit similarly high sensitivity to DSBs, akin to hPSCs. Electroporation of Cas9 and sgRNA in PGCs induced significant apoptosis and elevated γ-H2AX foci, whereas delivery of catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9) had no such effect. Therefore, using CRISPR/Cas9 in stem cells—especially for large-scale or genome-wide screens—requires caution regarding DSB-induced apoptosis that may confound experimental outcomes.

Germ cells have a unique, evolutionarily conserved hypersensitivity to DNA damage, an adaptation thought to safeguard genomic integrity for offspring. In both mice and humans, PGCs are highly sensitive to genotoxic stress (Bloom and Schimenti, 2020). This extreme vulnerability is well documented across species, from insects to mammals (Arnon et al., 2001; Meistrich, 2013; Gunes et al., 2015; Lu and Yamashita, 2017). We observed a similar sensitivity in chicken PGCs. Upon chemical induction of DNA damage (etoposide treatment), PGCs showed much lower tolerance compared to mammalian somatic cells and chicken embryonic fibroblasts, with apoptosis triggered at even 0.03 µM ETP. Interestingly, we observed a sex-specific difference in cell-cycle arrest following damage: female (ZW) PGCs exhibited a pronounced G2/M arrest (potentially via ATM/CHK2), while male (ZZ) PGCs showed significant accumulation in S-phase (possibly via ATR/CHK1 activation). Although the molecular basis for this divergence remains unclear, it may reflect underlying differences in sex chromosome composition (ZW vs. ZZ) and avian-specific dosage compensation mechanisms that influence checkpoint regulation in germ cells. Moreover, unlike many somatic cells that attempt cell-cycle arrest and DNA repair, germ cells (and pluripotent stem cells) often preferentially undergo apoptosis to prevent the propagation of deleterious mutations (Bloom et al., 2019). From an evolutionary perspective, this strategy likely represents a stringent quality-control mechanism, whereby damaged germ cells are eliminated rather than repaired through potentially error-prone pathways. Although human cell lines were included as external reference populations to contextualize the magnitude of DNA damage sensitivity, inherent biological divergence between avian and mammalian systems—such as differences in DNA repair pathway utilization and chromatin architecture—limits direct quantitative equivalence. Accordingly, these comparisons are intended to provide qualitative context rather than strict functional equivalence.

By contrast, CRISPRi offers a way to modulate gene expression without permanently altering the genome or creating breaks, by fusing a repressive domain to dCas9. However, our implementation of CRISPRi in chicken PGCs yielded suboptimal outcomes, with only minimal reduction in target gene expression. This finding aligns with previous reports of partial knockdown in some systems (Chapman et al., 2023) and indicates that CRISPRi is less effective in chicken cells than in typical mammalian systems. Multiple factors may contribute to this limitation. At the target level, suboptimal sgRNA positioning relative to transcription start sites (TSS), high basal transcriptional activity, or regulatory redundancy could reduce repression efficiency. At the chromatin level, the developmental epigenetic landscape of PGCs may restrict dCas9 accessibility to specific promoters. More fundamentally, CRISPRi systems commonly rely on mammalian-optimized repressor domains, particularly KRAB. Although dCas9-KRAB can partially repress certain chicken genes, the addition of stronger repression modules (e.g., KRAB-MeCP2) has not substantially improved silencing in avian cells, highlighting potential species-specific differences in co-repressor recruitment (Duke et al., 2020; Chapman et al., 2023; Usluer et al., 2023). One plausible bottleneck may involve the endogenous availability or recruitment efficiency of avian co-repressors such as KAP1 (TRIM28). If avian KAP1 exhibits reduced affinity for human-derived KRAB domains, or if its expression levels are limiting in PGCs, the establishment of stable heterochromatin could be compromised. Additionally, pre-existing chromatin configurations—such as insufficient H3K9me3 enrichment or promoter contexts resistant to heterochromatin spreading—may further constrain repression efficiency. Taken together, these findings suggest that the limited CRISPRi activity observed in chicken PGCs likely reflects incomplete compatibility between mammalian-derived repression modules and the avian epigenetic environment. Future studies quantifying endogenous KAP1 expression, assessing H3K9me3 deposition at targeted loci, and developing avian-optimized repressor domains will be essential to overcome this mechanistic bottleneck. Accordingly, effective application of CRISPRi for single-gene or genome-wide knockdown in chickens will require careful adaptation to species-specific chromatin and co-repressor contexts.

Limitations of the Study: This study primarily assessed acute DNA damage responses and short-term population loss following genome editing. Long-term in vivo validation of germline transmission efficiency and genomic stability of the surviving edited PGCs was beyond the scope of this work. Additionally, CRISPRi efficiency was evaluated using a limited number of target genes and mammalian-optimized KRAB repressor domains, which may not fully reflect the potential of avian-adapted repression systems. Furthermore, although human and chicken somatic cell lines were included for contextual comparison, differences in species background, immortalization status, and cell lineage may influence DNA damage sensitivity and transcriptional repression outcomes. These considerations highlight the need for future in vivo studies and species-specific tool optimization.

In summary, this study provides the first systematic evaluation of the capabilities and limitations of two widely used CRISPR-based platforms in avian primordial germ cells. Our findings demonstrate that although CRISPR/Cas9 achieves efficient genome editing in PGCs, the double-strand breaks it introduces robustly activate DNA damage responses and apoptosis, thereby constraining its utility in functional genomics applications. In contrast, CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), while avoiding nuclease-induced genotoxicity, exhibits limited transcriptional repression efficiency in avian PGCs, likely reflecting incomplete compatibility between mammalian-derived repression modules and the avian epigenetic environment.

These observations reveal a fundamental trade-off between editing efficiency and genomic safety in avian germline engineering. Accordingly, future efforts should prioritize next-generation genome editing technologies that minimize or eliminate double-strand breaks. Base Editing (BE) and Prime Editing (PE) represent promising alternatives, as they enable precise nucleotide substitutions or small insertions/deletions without eliciting the extensive DNA damage–induced cell death associated with conventional Cas9 nucleases. Importantly, our results do not question the feasibility of avian genome engineering; rather, they underscore the necessity of adapting genome-editing strategies to the unique biological and epigenetic properties of germ cells. Together, this work establishes a conceptual and practical framework for developing species-optimized, low-toxicity genome-editing tools to advance poultry reproductive biology and precision breeding.
