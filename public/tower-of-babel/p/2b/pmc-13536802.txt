Cancer stem cell plasticity: mechanisms, immune microenvironment crosstalk, and therapeutic implications
Authors: Jingyu Tan, Tao Wen, Jian Liu, Xiaoli Ru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer stem cells (CSCs) represent functionally defined and phenotypically plastic tumor cell populations implicated in therapeutic resistance, relapse, and metastasis. CSC plasticity is regulated through coordinated stemness signaling and epigenetic mechanisms. Canonical stemness-associated pathways, including Wnt, Notch, and Hedgehog, interact with epigenetic programs to maintain dynamic stem-like states and facilitate cellular adaptation to environmental and therapeutic stress. These regulatory networks are also associated with metabolic reprogramming and may support CSC survival under therapeutic and immune pressure. This review summarizes recent advances in our understanding of CSC plasticity, with a particular focus on the bidirectional interplay between CSCs and the tumor immune microenvironment. From a translational perspective, it further summarizes emerging strategies for targeting CSC plasticity in combination with immunotherapy, and discusses the present limitations and challenges of combination strategies. These observations may provide a conceptual framework for the development of more rational combination strategies, although their clinical benefit remains to be validated.Keywords: Cancer stem cell, Cancer plasticity, Tumor immune microenvironment, Immune evasion, Immunotherapy

Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have provided clinically meaningful and, in some patients, durable benefits across multiple cancer types [1, 2]. However, primary and acquired resistance limit the clinical outcomes of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in many patients. In addition, a subset of patients receiving ICIs may even experience hyperprogressive disease (HPD), which refers to an atypical and detrimental response pattern after ICI therapy. HPD is typically characterized by accelerated tumor growth and rapid clinical deterioration compared with the pretreatment disease course [3, 4]. Under sustained immune selection, heterogeneous and plastic tumor cells may acquire immune-evasive states, contributing to adaptive immune resistance (AIR) and immunotherapy failure [5–8].

CSCs are generally defined by functional properties, including self-renewal capacity, tumor-initiating ability, and the potential to regenerate tumor heterogeneity [9, 10]. Functional assays, including limiting dilution transplantation [11], serial transplantation [12], and serial sphere formation [13], remain essential for evaluating tumor initiation and self-renewal. Although markers such as CD133 [14], CD44 [15], aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) [16, 17], and leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 5 (LGR5) [18] have been widely used to enrich for CSC-like tumor cell populations, their expression is tumor type-specific, state-dependent, and dynamically regulated [6, 19]. Therefore, marker positivity alone is insufficient to define CSC. CSC identification still requires integrated functional assays, molecular characterization, and advanced approaches such as lineage tracing and single-cell analyses [20, 21].

Within this framework, CSC plasticity refers not simply to stemness itself but to the capacity for reversible transition between CSC and non-CSC states under microenvironmental, therapeutic, and immune pressures. Accordingly, non-CSCs should not be viewed as a permanently fixed non-stem population [22]. Under therapeutic pressure [23], inflammatory cues [24, 25], hypoxia [26], or niche-derived signals [27], some differentiated tumor cells may enter drug-tolerant, quiescent, or epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like states and, in certain contexts, acquire self-renewing and tumor-initiating properties. However, these plastic states are not equivalent to functional CSC identity unless they demonstrate durable self-renewal, tumor propagation, and regeneration of tumor heterogeneity [28, 29]. Thus, CSCs are better understood as a dynamic subpopulation of cancer cells rather than a stable cell population defined solely by marker positivity, EMT traits, or drug resistance [30–32].

CSCs are not merely passive targets of immune attack but can both respond to and reshape the surrounding immune environment. The tumor microenvironment (TME) encompasses the broader ecosystem surrounding tumor cells, including immune, stromal, vascular, extracellular matrix, metabolic, and soluble components, whereas the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) specifically refers to the immune-cell composition, immune regulatory signals, and immune functional states within the TME [33, 34]. In this review, we examine current evidence that CSC plasticity facilitates tumor cell adaptation to therapeutic and immune stress, with particular emphasis on CSC–TIME crosstalk and emerging strategies that combine CSC-directed interventions with immunotherapy.

Molecular drivers of CSC plasticity

CSC plasticity generally refers to the capacity of tumor cells to undergo dynamic and potentially reversible transitions between stem-like and non-stem cell states [35, 36]. In response to microenvironmental cues or therapeutic stress, some non-CSCs may acquire or reacquire CSC-associated properties through dedifferentiation or phenotypic switching [37, 38]. This plasticity may help sustain a dynamic pool of CSC-like cells that contribute to intratumoral heterogeneity and adaptive resistance (Fig. 1).

Epigenetic and transcriptional regulation of CSC plasticity

CSC plasticity often reflects reversible changes in cell state that can occur without stable alterations in DNA sequence, although the genetic context may constrain or facilitate these transitions [39, 40]. Epigenetic and transcriptional mechanisms allow tumor cells to remodel chromatin states, gene-expression programs, and stress responses without altering the genomic sequence [41]. Through these mechanisms, non-CSCs can reacquire stem-like properties under therapeutic pressure or microenvironmental stimuli, thereby maintaining a flexible CSC pool and contributing to adaptive resistance [42, 43].

Chromatin remodeling

Chromatin remodeling mechanisms can influence CSC states by reshaping chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity, and differentiation-associated transcriptional programs in a context-dependent manner [44, 45]. Multiple complexes that regulate higher-order chromatin organization are involved in this process. For example, PU.1 recruits SWI/SNF complexes to enhancer regions, maintaining chromatin accessibility for core regulatory factors and sustaining MYC expression. SWI/SNF inhibition disrupts these enhancer programs, promotes leukemic differentiation, and reduces the bone marrow leukemia stem cell burden [46]. In another example, reduced expression of the linker histone H1.0 is associated with a chromatin state permissive for self-renewal, whereas H1.0 re-expression can repress oncogenic and stemness-associated transcriptional programs [47].

DNA methylation

DNA methylation modulates CSC maintenance and state transitions by regulating genes involved in differentiation, self-renewal, and responses to extrinsic cues . Ten-eleven translocation (TET) family dioxygenases promote DNA demethylation by sequentially oxidizing 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) and other oxidized derivatives, thereby contributing to the regulation of cell-fate programs [48]. In hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, TET2 loss increases self-renewal and promotes neoplastic transformation, indicating that TET2 helps maintain the balance between stem-cell renewal and differentiation [49].

Mutant isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) 1/2 produce R-2-hydroxyglutarate (R-2HG), which inhibits TET enzymes and other α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases. The resulting abnormalities in DNA and histone methylation can impair differentiation and favor the persistence of poorly differentiated cell states [50]. However, the effects of DNA methylation on CSCs are locus- and context-dependent. In hepatocellular carcinoma, UHRF1-dependent maintenance of DNA methylation supports CSC self-renewal, whereas UHRF1 depletion induces widespread DNA hypomethylation, promotes differentiation, and reduces tumor initiation [51]. Thus, DNA methylation may support or restrict CSC-associated states depending on the genes and regulatory regions affected.

Histone modification

Histone modifications provide a potentially reversible regulatory layer for CSC plasticity [52]. H3K4me3 is generally associated with active promoters, whereas polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2)-mediated H3K27me3 is linked to transcriptional repression [53]. In MLL-rearranged AML, H3K4me3 patterns are associated with leukemia stem cell (LSC) identity [54]. In patient-derived colorectal cancer-initiating cells, EZH2 inhibition derepressed Indian Hedgehog (IHH) and reduced self-renewal, whereas dual EZH1/2 inhibition promoted cell-cycle entry and differentiation of quiescent LSCs in AML models [55, 56]. Histone acetylation is usually linked to open chromatin and transcriptional activation [57]. Histone demethylases, such as lysine-specific demethylase 1 A (LSD1/KDM1A), can also regulate stem and progenitor cell states. In transgenic mice, overexpression of its shortest isoform enhanced hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) self-renewal and increased susceptibility to T-cell lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma after irradiation, although the study examined preleukemic HSCs rather than established CSCs [58].

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) and related deacetylases regulate CSC-associated properties through both histone and non-histone substrates, with effects that vary across tumor types and molecular contexts. In patient-derived glioblastoma stem cells, HDAC2 cooperates with SMAD3–SKI signaling to upregulate SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2) and oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG2), thereby promoting stemness and tumorigenesis [59]. In colorectal cancer cell models, IL-6/STAT3 signaling promotes HDAC6-dependent deacetylation of FRA1, thereby enhancing FRA1-mediated NANOG transcription and increasing the proportion of CD44⁺/CD133⁺ stem-like cells [60]. However, deacetylase activity is not uniformly stemness-promoting. In myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) stem and progenitor cells, the class III HDAC sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) activation deacetylated TET2 and enhanced its catalytic activity, thereby reducing self-renewal, colony formation, and xenograft engraftment [61]. In addition, bromodomain and extra-terminal domain (BET) proteins recognize acetylated lysine residues and sustain transcriptional programs driven by factors such as MYC, although these effects are not specific to CSCs [62]. Overall, these findings are largely preclinical and remain dependent on tumor type and experimental model.

RNA modification

RNA modifications add another reversible layer to the regulation of CSC plasticity. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a major RNA modification installed by the methyltransferase-like 3/14 (METTL3/METTL14) core complex [63, 64]. Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) is a context-dependent RNA demethylase rather than a universal stemness-promoting factor. FTO supports stem-like programs in AML [65], glioblastoma [66], hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [67], and gastric cancer [68] models, whereas it suppresses ovarian CSC self-renewal [69] and colorectal cancer stem-like properties [70]. These contrasting findings indicate that the role of FTO in CSC regulation depends on the cellular context and RNA substrate.

Core transcriptional networks

Epigenetic regulation is integrated with transcriptional networks. Core factors, including SOX2, OCT4/POU5F1, NANOG, MYC, KLF4, STAT3, and Yes-associated protein/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (YAP/TAZ), form self-reinforcing circuits that sustain self-renewal, an undifferentiated state, and stress adaptation [71]. These regulators interact with Wnt, Notch, Hedgehog, Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), Hippo, and TGF-β signaling to control transitions between stem-like and non-stem-like states [72]. Although the dominant circuits vary across cancer types, they commonly support adaptive state switching [73–75].

Therapy-induced state switching

Therapeutic pressure can promote chromatin remodeling, allowing some tumor cells to enter drug-tolerant, slow-cycling, or CSC-like states. Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells generally represent a reversible, non-genetic survival state associated with therapeutic resistance and relapse [28, 76]. In murine MCA205 fibrosarcoma cell cultures and syngeneic C57BL/6J tumor models, immunogenic chemotherapy enriched CSC-like tumor cells through IFN-I–KDM1B signaling [77]. In prostate cancer cell and patient-derived xenograft models, androgen receptor (AR) blockade can induce lineage plasticity, converting AR pathway-positive tumor cells into a mesenchymal and stem-like state associated with therapy tolerance and metastatic potential [78]. Taken together, therapeutic pressure can induce a switch toward stem-like states, enabling a subset of tumor cells to transiently evade treatment-induced killing.

Wnt signaling pathway

The Wnt signaling cascade is a well-established regulatory axis that governs CSC plasticity. It links β-catenin-dependent transcriptional programs with stemness-associated genes such as CD44, SOX2, MYC, and EZH2 [79–81]. Rather than acting only as a tumor cell-intrinsic pathway, Wnt signaling is reinforced by stromal and immune cues within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [82]. For example, hypoxic cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can secrete CLEC11A, which engages LGR5 on tumor cells and activates Wnt signaling to promote colorectal cancer lymphatic metastasis [83]. IFN-induced SLC14A1⁺ CAFs also promote bladder cancer stemness through paracrine WNT5A signaling [84]. In parallel, tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)-related Wnt5A/C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) signaling links Wnt activity with inflammatory macrophage programs [85]. Together, these findings suggest that Wnt signaling contributes to CSC-associated phenotypes and tumor progression through both tumor cell intrinsic mechanisms and context-dependent interactions with CAFs and TAMs.

Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway is a contact-dependent cell-fate regulatory cascade that is initiated by interactions between membrane-bound Delta-like or Jagged ligands on neighboring cells and Notch family receptors, including Notch1–4 [86]. Ligand engagement triggers sequential proteolytic cleavages by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) and the γ-secretase complex, resulting in the liberation of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD) [87]. Once translocated to the nucleus, NICD recruits coactivators, such as Mastermind-like transcriptional coactivators (MAML) 1–3 and p300, to the CBF1/Suppressor of Hairless/Lag-1 (CSL) complex, thereby shifting it from a transcriptional repressor to an activator of stemness-associated genes [88]. This transcriptional switch drives the expression of downstream effectors, including hairy and enhancer of split 1 (HES1) and hairy/enhancer-of-split related with YRPW motif 1 (HEY1). It further regulates MYC, NANOG and SOX2 transcription in specific tumor types [89].

Studies across various malignancies have demonstrated the context-specific role of Notch signaling in regulating CSC plasticity [90, 91]. Besides, this contact-dependent and transcription-coupled mode of signaling makes Notch particularly suited to integrate niche-derived signals with epigenetic regulation [88]. In breast cancer, Notch activation promotes ALDH1A1 deacetylation and expands ALDH⁺ CSC populations, linking Notch activity with acetylation-dependent regulation of stemness [92]. Because ALDH1A1 activity can also remodel the myeloid compartment and promote myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)-mediated immunosuppression [93], the Notch–ALDH1A1 axis may represent a potential bridge between CSC maintenance and immune suppression. Separately, pharmacological Notch inhibition enhances the efficacy of ICB in triple-negative breast cancer models by reducing TAM infiltration, suggesting that Notch downstream cytokines can limit ICB responsiveness [94].

Hedgehog signaling pathway

The Hedgehog (Hh) pathway is tightly regulated in adult tissues, but aberrant Hh signaling activity has been implicated in CSC-associated phenotypes in several cancers. In the absence of Hh ligands, Patched1 (PTCH1) constitutively inhibits Smoothened (SMO). Upon Hh ligand binding, this inhibition is relieved, allowing SMO-dependent activation of the GLI family of zinc-finger transcription factors [95]. Activated GLI factors subsequently regulate genes involved in cell survival and stem-like phenotypes, including BCL2, NANOG, SOX2, OCT4, and BMI1, in a context-dependent manner [96].

Hh signaling can contribute to tumor initiation in specific cellular contexts [97]. For example, Sonic hedgehog (SHH)-expressing basal cells have been identified as a cell of origin for bladder cancer [98]. Furthermore, the activation of Hh effectors is closely linked to treatment failure, as exemplified by GLI2-mediated sunitinib resistance in renal cell carcinoma [99, 100]. In established tumors, Hh signaling can also help maintain stem-like traits under microenvironmental stress. Under hypoxic conditions, SHH signaling promotes cancer stemness, EMT, and invasion in cholangiocarcinoma, indicating that Hh activity may connect hypoxic stress with stem-like state transition [101–104]. In addition, Hh signaling also contributes to an immunosuppressive niche. GLI1 activation can induce CCL2 expression in cholangiocarcinoma, whereas the GLI1–snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (SNAI1) axis promotes macrophage polarization in breast cancer [105, 106]. GLI signaling may also directly [107] or indirectly [108] induce programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on tumor cells, thereby suppressing cytotoxic T-cell activity, and protecting CSCs from immune elimination. The available evidence indicates that dysregulated Hh/GLI activity can support stem-like phenotypes and treatment resistance and shape immunosuppressive tumor–immune interactions, although these effects are highly dependent on the cellular context and mode of pathway activation.

Functional hallmarks of CSC plasticity

Metabolic reprogramming

Glycolysis–oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) switching

Metabolic reprogramming is a key adaptive feature that enables CSCs to survive under fluctuating nutrient, oxygen, and treatment conditions. The traditional Warburg model emphasizes that cancer cells frequently exhibit increased glycolysis and lactate production despite sufficient oxygen availability [109]. However, CSCs do not exhibit a uniformly glycolytic phenotype. In some tumor models, enhanced glycolysis supports stem-like properties by providing ATP and biosynthetic intermediates and by influencing redox and epigenetic states [110]. It has been linked to stemness, tumor initiation, and therapy resistance through regulators such as pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) [111, 112], 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase (PFKFB) family members [113], SOX2 and protein disulfide isomerase family A member 6 (PDIA6) [114], and lactate-dependent epigenetic remodeling [115]. In breast CSCs, ZFP64 directly activates glycolysis-related genes and promotes stem-like properties [116]. Separately, macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) secreted by hypoxic breast cancer cells and breast CSCs enhances glycolysis in neighboring tumor cells through Wnt/β-catenin–c-MYC signaling and contributes to immune evasion [117].

However, CSCs can shift between glycolysis and OXPHOS depending on tumor type, cellular state, and microenvironmental pressure [118–122]. Hypoxia [123] and nutrient limitation [124] can promote adaptive metabolic rewiring. In poorly perfused tumor regions, hypoxia commonly promotes a glycolytic program through hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-dependent adaptation, which can support survival under limited oxygen availability [125]. By contrast, when glucose becomes limiting, proliferation slows, or long-term survival is prioritized, some CSCs have been reported to increase mitochondrial respiration and adopt an OXPHOS-dependent state [126, 127]. Therapeutic pressure can further reinforce this shift by enriching for persister or stem-like populations with greater mitochondrial fitness and OXPHOS dependence [128]. Therefore, CSC metabolism is characterized less by uniform reliance on a single pathway than by context-dependent metabolic heterogeneity and adaptive remodeling under stress.

Mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondrial structural plasticity provides a potential structural basis for this metabolic switching. Mitochondria continuously undergo fission and fusion, and this reversible remodeling may help CSCs adjust the balance between glycolytic and oxidative states [129, 130]. Fragmented mitochondrial networks are often associated with reduced cristae organization, impaired electron transport chain (ETC) supercomplex formation, and lower basal respiratory efficiency [131], which may favor a glycolysis-biased state under glucose-abundant conditions [132, 133]. Conversely, mitochondrial fusion can generate more interconnected networks that support respiratory efficiency and OXPHOS when energy demand increases or nutrient availability changes [127]. Fission also facilitates mitochondrial quality control through mitophagy and the segregation of aged mitochondria, which may help preserve stem-cell properties [134, 135]. In some tumor models, dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1)-mediated mitochondrial remodeling has been linked to CSC maintenance, glutamine metabolism, and ferroptosis sensitivity [136], whereas altered mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 1 and 2 (MPC1/2)-dependent pyruvate import can influence stemness and metabolic reprogramming [137]. More recently, ALDH4A1 was identified as a component of the MPC complex that maintains mitochondrial pyruvate import and supports tumor-suppressive metabolism [138].

Glutamine addiction and redox homeostasis

When pyruvate-derived carbon entry into the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) is limited, some CSC populations may increase glutamine dependence to sustain mitochondrial metabolism and redox balance [139, 140]. Through glutathione (GSH)-dependent antioxidant defense and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)-related lipid peroxide detoxification, the glutamine–redox axis further protects CSCs from reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and ferroptotic vulnerability, although its contribution may vary across CSC contexts [141, 142].

In essence, CSC metabolism can be viewed as an interconnected adaptive network that integrates glycolysis–OXPHOS switching, mitochondrial fission–fusion dynamics, glutamine utilization, and redox homeostasis (Fig. 3). These features may help CSCs adapt to hypoxia, nutrient stress, acidosis, and therapy-induced pressure, and may also influence CSC–immune crosstalk through lactate accumulation, nutrient competition, redox buffering, and mitochondrial fitness.

Enhanced invasion and metastasis plasticity

EMT-associated plasticity provides a useful framework for explaining how selected CSC-like cells gain invasive and metastatic competence, without implying that all CSCs are intrinsically migratory. Rather than a binary switch, EMT is now viewed as a reversible continuum, in which partial or hybrid epithelial–mesenchymal states can preserve epithelial adhesion while acquiring motility, survival fitness, and state-switching capacity [143–146]. Therefore, EMT and CSC programs are related but not equivalent, and their functional overlap is most relevant when stemness is coupled with EMT-associated plasticity [145, 147].

This coupling is shaped by both intrinsic transcriptional circuits and niche-derived signals. ZEB1 can link EMT activation with stemness by repressing stemness-inhibiting microRNAs, while inflammatory cues from M1-like TAMs, TNF-α-stimulated cancer stem cells, and CCL2-mediated tumor–stromal crosstalk can further reinforce mesenchymal or stem-like phenotypes and support invasion or niche interactions [147–150]. During dissemination, tumor cells with stem-like or metastasis-initiating features may benefit from partial epithelial retention, circulating tumor cell (CTC) cluster formation, and adhesion- or endothelium-mediated support during vascular arrest and extravasation [151–153]. CD44 and integrins contribute to CTC arrest and stable adhesion under shear flow, while CTC clustering is associated with increased metastatic seeding and DNA methylation changes linked to stemness- and proliferation-associated programs [154, 155]. Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)-associated β1-integrin interactions may further support circulating tumor cell capture in inflammatory vascular niches [156]. At distant sites, metastatic outgrowth depends on niche adaptation rather than invasion alone. CSC-like cells can exploit tenascin C-containing metastatic niches and immune-niche signals, such as RANKL/RANK-mediated regulatory T cell (Treg) recruitment, to support stem-like properties and metastatic outgrowth [157–159].

To sum up, metastatic progression is better viewed not as a linear invasion program, but as a plastic process in which stemness, partial EMT, stromal remodeling, and immune-niche support cooperate to sustain CSC-like states across changing microenvironments (Fig. 4).

Therapy-tolerant residual states

Intrinsic survival programs under therapy

After therapy, an important CSC-related concern is the persistence or emergence of residual CSC-like states, which may not be adequately reflected by bulk tumor shrinkage alone. Such states can be sustained by quiescence, drug efflux, DNA damage repair, and increased resistance to apoptosis [14, 160–163]. CSCs in several tumor models have been reported to overexpress ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, including ABCG2 and ABCB1, which reduce intracellular drug accumulation and contribute to side-population and drug-resistant phenotypes [164–168]. Residual stem-like cells may also engage DNA damage responses and anti-apoptotic signaling, which can support their survival under treatment-induced stress [169–172]. Together, these mechanisms are better viewed as potentially interconnected components of therapy-tolerant residual states rather than as isolated or universally shared resistance traits.

Niche-supported residual plasticity

Residual CSC-like cells can be further stabilized by therapy-remodeled niches. They may remain slow-cycling while maintaining stemness-associated programs that support survival and subsequent re-expansion [173, 174]. Treatment-induced inflammation can activate IL-6- and IL-8-associated feedback loops, which may reinforce stem-like features, mesenchymal states, and treatment tolerance [175–177]. In addition, stromal and immune components can shape these residual states. TGF-β, hypoxia, and ubiquitin-specific peptidase 9, X-linked (USP9X)/HIF-2α signaling promote stemness-associated transcriptional programs and cisplatin tolerance [178], whereas interactions between cancer stem-like cells and neutrophils or macrophages can support tumor-promoting niches [177, 179].

Persister, dormancy, and relapse-prone CSC-like states

Therapy can eliminate proliferative drug-sensitive cells while selecting a reversible drug-tolerant persister pool [180–182]. Drug-tolerant persister cells and stem-like cells should therefore not be regarded as identical populations. Persister cells are defined as cells occupying a reversible, non-genetic survival state induced or selected by therapy [23], whereas stem-like cells refer to cells with functional self-renewal and tumor-initiating potential. However, these states can overlap, because some persister populations display quiescence, epigenetic remodeling, ALDH-associated stem-like features, and the capacity to regenerate proliferative tumor cells after drug withdrawal [23, 180, 181, 183].

Dormancy represents another closely related but distinct residual state [184]. Slow-cycling or dormant stem-like cells can retain tumor-initiating potential under unfavorable conditions, thereby contributing to minimal residual disease and later relapse [173, 174, 185, 186]. Mechanistically, NR2F1–SOX9/RARβ signaling integrates quiescence, survival, and stemness-linked plasticity in disseminated tumor cells, supporting long-term persistence before relapse [187]. During treatment or after drug withdrawal, residual cells may undergo reversible transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming, enabling subsequent proliferative outgrowth [181, 188, 189]. Quiescent CSC-like populations may also retain distinct transcriptional programs associated with treatment tolerance and relapse [189]. Therefore, the clinically relevant residual population after treatment is better understood as a continuum of therapy-tolerant persister, dormant, and CSC-like states rather than a single static CSC subset.

These residual states may link conventional treatment resistance to subsequent tumor regrowth and, in some contexts, altered interactions with the immune microenvironment. By integrating quiescence, stemness programs, stress-buffering mechanisms, and niche protection, therapy-tolerant CSC-like cells may contribute to relapse and establish a basis for the CSC–immune microenvironment crosstalk discussed in the following sections (Fig. 5).

Therapy-induced transition from sensitive tumor cells to CSC-enriched resistant states. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy preferentially eliminate proliferative and more differentiated non-CSCs through DNA damage, ROS accumulation, apoptosis, mitotic catastrophe, or senescence. Surviving cells enter a hybrid transitional state characterized by adaptive plasticity, including drug efflux, DNA damage response activation, survival priming, OXPHOS preference, ROS buffering, and chromatin remodeling. These residual cells also remodel the niche through IL-6/IL-8 signaling, CAF support, hypoxia–HIF activation, and immune suppression. Together, these changes promote enrichment of CSC-like resistant cells with quiescence, self-renewal, invasive capacity, niche-supported survival, and drug exclusion, thereby contributing to migratory phenotypes and tumor relapse. Immune regulation of CSC states

This section addresses one direction of the bidirectional interaction between CSC plasticity and the immune microenvironment: how immune pressure may select for, induce, or reinforce CSC-like states. Rather than functioning only as an external barrier to tumor growth, the immune microenvironment can eliminate immunogenic tumor cells while favoring the persistence of less immunogenic or more adaptable tumor cell states. Cytokines, immune checkpoint signaling, and suppressive immune niches can then stabilize these states, thereby contributing to adaptive immune resistance.

Immune editing selects CSCs

Reduced antigen visibility is one mechanism through which CSC-like populations may evade immune surveillance [190]. The cytotoxic activity of CD8⁺ T cells relies on T cell receptor (TCR)-mediated recognition of antigenic peptides presented by major histocompatibility complex class I/human leukocyte antigen class I (MHC-I/HLA-I) molecules on tumor cells [190, 191]. This recognition occurs either through direct antigen presentation by tumor cells or via dendritic cell (DC)-mediated cross-presentation after antigen uptake [192]. However, in several experimental models, CSC-enriched populations exhibit a pronounced capacity to evade this process. Studies have shown that CSCs commonly exhibit reduced MHC-I expression, and tumors containing CSC-like populations may also acquire functional alterations in HLA-I/β2-microglobulin (B2M) that further impair immune recognition [193, 194]. For instance, in colorectal cancer, tumor-initiating cells reactivate a SRY-box transcription factor 17 (SOX17)-driven fetal transcriptional program that suppresses interferon-γ (IFN-γ) sensing, limits MHC-I expression and T-cell recruitment, and generates immune-evasive LGR5⁻ tumor cells [195]. Collectively, impaired antigen presentation and reduced IFN-γ responsiveness can limit immune recognition in some CSC-related models (Fig. 6a). However, the underlying mechanisms vary across tumor types, and the extent to which each mechanism specifically operates in CSCs still requires stronger functional and clinical validation.

Checkpoint signals reinforce CSC states

CD8⁺ T cells are essential mediators of antitumor immunity. They eliminate malignant cells through cytotoxic granule release and secretion of effector cytokines, particularly IFN-γ. IFN-γ not only enhances antigen presentation but also exerts selective pressure on tumor cells. In response, CSC-enriched populations in some tumor models may reduce immune recognition or upregulate immune checkpoint ligands that attenuate T-cell activity [196–198].

PD-L1 is one of the best-characterized checkpoint molecules implicated in adaptive immune resistance in CSC-like populations. In bulk tumor cells, PD-L1 can be induced by inflammatory signals, especially IFN-γ [199]. Some CSC-enriched populations have also been reported to maintain relatively sustained or cytokine-independent PD-L1 expression [200, 201]. Such expression may arise from intrinsic alterations, such as CD274 amplification or 3′ untranslated region disruption [202], as well as activation of stemness-associated signaling pathways, including phosphoinositide 3-kinase–protein kinase B (PI3K–AKT), STAT3 [203] and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [204]. Moreover, stemness-associated factors, such as CD44 [205] and MYC [206], have been implicated in the regulation of PD-L1 expression. Functionally, PD-L1 has also been reported to promote β-catenin signaling and self-renewal, supporting a direct link between immune evasion and stemness maintenance [207].

Other checkpoint molecules may further reinforce immune-resistant states in selected CSC models. B7 family members, including B7 homolog 3 (B7-H3/CD276) and B7 homolog 4 (B7-H4/VTCN1), have been associated with CSC-enriched populations and may suppress CD8⁺ T-cell activation or infiltration [208, 209]. Besides PD-L1, CSCs may also upregulate inhibitory ligands such as galectin-9 [210], CD155 [211], as well as the antiphagocytic molecule CD47 [212]. These findings suggest that checkpoint compensation in CSCs is not restricted to PD-L1 but may represent a broader adaptive program that stabilizes a stem-like, immune-resistant phenotype.

CSC immune evasion also extends to innate immune surveillance. Natural killer (NK) cells are specialized to eliminate cells with reduced MHC-I expression through missing-self recognition. However, CSCs have evolved sophisticated synergistic strategies to circumvent this innate surveillance. One such mechanism involves ADAM10/17-mediated shedding of natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) ligands, including MHC class I chain-related proteins A and B (MICA/B). Soluble MICA/B can reduce NKG2D-dependent NK-cell activation and cytotoxicity [213, 214]. CSC-like cells may also express nonclassical MHC-I molecules, including HLA-E and HLA-G, as well as the nectin-like molecule CD155. These molecules engage inhibitory receptors such as natural killer group 2 member A (NKG2A) and T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domains (TIGIT) on NK cells, thereby suppressing NK-cell degranulation [215, 216]. Through these mechanisms, CSC-related populations may reduce their susceptibility to both CD8⁺ T-cell-mediated killing and NK-cell surveillance (Fig. 6b).

In summary, checkpoint regulation may connect immune evasion with the maintenance of CSC-like states, but its mechanisms and clinical relevance remain context-dependent. PD-L1 is the most frequently reported checkpoint associated with CSC-like states, whereas evidence for other inhibitory ligands and NK-evasion pathways is still largely preclinical. Therefore, whether CSC-intrinsic checkpoint programs can serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets independently of checkpoint expression in the broader tumor population remains unresolved.

IFN signaling bidirectionally regulates CSC states

Type I interferons (IFN-I) and IFN-γ have traditionally been regarded as antitumor cytokines that enhance antigen presentation, immune recognition, and tumor control [217]. However, in CSC conversion and plasticity, IFN signaling can exert context-dependent effects [218, 219]. The IFN-associated molecular mechanisms underlying this bidirectional regulation are summarized in Fig. 6c. IFN-I and IFN-γ activate the canonical JAK–STAT pathway through their respective receptors and induce the expression of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) [220]. Many canonical ISGs support antigen processing, apoptosis, and immune-mediated clearance [221]. Consistent with these functions, effective STAT1/ interferon-stimulated gene factor 3 (ISGF3) activation has been linked to reduced CSC-like properties, epithelial differentiation, and stemness suppression in some experimental models [222, 223]. By contrast, persistent or noncanonical IFN-response programs have been associated with EMT, anti-apoptotic signaling, adhesion remodeling, and immune escape [199, 218, 224]. In addition, unphosphorylated ISGF3 (u-ISGF3) and the IFN-related DNA damage resistance signature (IRDS) have been linked to DNA damage resistance, migration, and reduced treatment sensitivity [218, 225, 226]. IFN-induced PD-L1 expression [199, 201] and ISGylation-related programs [227] further indicate that IFN signaling may generate immune-evasive outputs in selected settings.

CSC-associated inflammatory signaling can also converge on noncanonical branches, including STAT3/STAT5 [228], p38 MAPK [229], the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mechanistic target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) axis [230], and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) [231]. STAT3 supports EMT- and stemness-associated programs, including OCT4, NANOG, and SOX2, and can increase PD-L1 expression [232–234]. In IFN-α-resistant melanoma models, STAT5 has been reported to counteract IFN-I/STAT1-mediated suppression and is linked to survival and therapy resistance [228]. The effects of p38 signaling are also context-dependent. p38γ can promote CSC expansion in triple-negative breast cancer [235, 236], whereas p38 inactivation has also been reported to support CSC-like properties in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [237]. IFN-α may further activate PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF-1α signaling to promote EMT-related survival programs in 769-P renal carcinoma cells [230].

The effects of IFN signaling on CSC-related states can depend on dose, duration, basal signaling state, and epigenetic context, in addition to cytokine class [77, 238, 239]. Acute IFN-γ exposure more often reflects classical antitumor activity [240]. By comparison, chronic low-dose IFN-γ can impose persistent immune pressure and select for adaptive stem-like states, as shown by increased sphere formation, CD133 or intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) expression, and maintenance of CSC traits [241, 242].

Overall, IFN-I and IFN-γ can exert either CSC-suppressive or CSC-adaptive effects under different experimental conditions. Current evidence linking chronic IFN signaling to stemness relies mainly on preclinical exposure models and molecular signatures. Further clinical validation is therefore required before chronic IFN signaling can be regarded as a clinically established driver of CSC adaptation.

Immune niche supports CSC states

Immune cells can protect pre-existing CSCs from immune elimination and, in certain contexts, support stem-like, slow-cycling, or therapy-tolerant tumor states [243, 244]. TAMs are among the most extensively studied immune-cell contributors to CSC maintenance [245, 246]. TAM-derived IL-6 [245], S100A9 [247], and TGF-β [248] have been reported to enrich CSC-like populations or promote EMT-associated stemness in specific tumor models. This interaction is often bidirectional. Stemness-associated tumor cells can also reprogram macrophages, forming feed-forward loops that reinforce both CSC traits and immunosuppression [246].

Regulatory T cells may contribute to CSC-associated niches in selected tumors. In gastric cancer, TNF⁺ Treg-derived IL-13 activates STAT3 signaling and promotes stem-like properties [243]. Treg-mediated TGF-β signaling may further support an immunosuppressive microenvironment, although its direct contribution to CSC maintenance remains incompletely defined and context-dependent [249].

B cells are less established in CSC–immune crosstalk, but several models suggest a niche-supportive role. In prostate cancer, B-cell-derived lymphotoxin activates IKKα and BMI1-related programs, supporting castration resistance and tumor recurrence [250, 251]. In melanoma, tumor-associated B-cell-derived IGF-1 induces CD20⁺, CD133⁺, and CD271⁺ stem-like subpopulations and drives resistance to BRAF/MEK inhibition [252]. In metastatic breast cancer lymph nodes, CD24hi CD27⁺ regulatory B cells enhance stem-like features and multidrug resistance of residual tumor cells through IL-6 and TNF-α [253]. Because these findings come from specific tumor models, B cells should be described as an emerging and context-dependent component of the CSC niche, rather than a broadly conserved CSC driver.

Neutrophil–CSC interactions are also less well defined. In melanoma, CSC-like cells can prime neutrophils toward a pro-tumor phenotype, and these educated neutrophils may in turn reinforce stemness-related traits [254]. This finding supports a bidirectional CSC–neutrophil loop, but current evidence is still limited to selected tumor models. NET-associated inflammation may also promote metastatic reactivation by awakening dormant cancer cells, although this mechanism is more closely linked to dormancy and relapse than to direct CSC expansion [255].

Beyond specific immune-cell subsets, the immune–stromal niche also provides soluble and metabolic cues that may support CSC maintenance. Signals such as TGF-β, HGF, IL-6, TNF-α, and Jagged-1 can influence EMT, Notch activation, and immune exclusion, thereby creating conditions favorable to stemness-associated plasticity [245, 256]. In parallel, hypoxia, lactate accumulation, and nutrient competition link microenvironmental stress to the persistence of stem-like tumor states [257–259].

Together, these observations indicate that the immune microenvironment can actively support CSC-like states, but the dominant cellular and molecular circuits vary across tumor types and experimental models. TAM-mediated support appears relatively recurrent across solid tumors, whereas Treg-, B-cell-, and neutrophil-related mechanisms are supported mainly by selected tumor contexts, including ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, melanoma, and metastatic breast cancer lymph-node models. Immune-niche support of CSC-like states should therefore be regarded as a recurrent but heterogeneous feature rather than a universally conserved program. Accordingly, AIR in CSCs may involve varying combinations of immune selection, reduced cytotoxic surveillance, context-dependent IFN responses, and niche-mediated reinforcement of stem-like states (Fig. 6d).

CSCs remodel the immune microenvironment

Immune pressure can select or induce CSC-like states with enhanced immune resistance. In turn, CSC-enriched populations may remodel the immune microenvironment and create feed-forward interactions that reinforce stemness and adaptive immune resistance. They can alter antigen presentation, suppress cytotoxic lymphocytes, recruit immunosuppressive immune and stromal cells, and establish metabolic or physical barriers. This section focuses on this reverse arm of the feedback loop: how CSCs reshape immune surveillance after immune selection or stabilization (Fig. 7).

CSCs impair antigen presentation

CSCs are not only enriched by immune editing but can also actively influence antigen presentation, thereby weakening the initiation of antitumor immunity. DCs, especially cross-presenting DC subsets, are required for tumor antigen uptake, processing, and CD8⁺ T-cell priming [260, 261]. Therefore, CSC-associated DC dysfunction may represent one mechanism linking stem-like tumor states to impaired antitumor immunity.

CSCs can impair DC differentiation, maturation, and T-cell stimulatory capacity through soluble factors and extracellular vesicles (EVs) in some experimental models [262]. Direct evidence has been reported in renal cancer, where CD105⁺ renal CSCs and their EVs suppress DC maturation markers and T-cell activation more strongly than non-tumor-initiating cells, partly through HLA-G [263]. In liver cancer, retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-deficient stemness-high tumor cells secrete more TGF-β1 and induce tolerogenic DC phenotypes through TGF-β–AKT–SMAD2 signaling [264]. These findings support a functional link between stemness-high tumor states and impaired DC priming, although they do not prove that all CSCs suppress DCs through the same mechanism. EV-mediated immune suppression provides another route. Glioblastoma-derived EVs carrying galectin-9 (LGALS9) have been shown to impair DC antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell immunity [265]. Head and neck cancer cell-derived EVs can also disrupt DC function and carry regulatory microRNAs [266], but this evidence is not CSC-specific and more broadly supports tumor EV-mediated immune regulation.

Stemness-associated tumor pathways may also contribute to reduced DC recruitment. β-catenin signaling is a well-characterized example, as melanoma-intrinsic β-catenin suppresses CCL4 production, limits DC recruitment, and prevents T-cell infiltration [267]. Similar β-catenin-associated immune exclusion has been observed in NSCLC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, although the extent to which these effects are specific to CSC states remains unclear [268, 269].

In summary, CSC-enriched or stemness-high tumor populations may impair antitumor antigen presentation by suppressing DC differentiation, converting DCs toward tolerogenic states, delivering inhibitory EV cargo, and limiting DC recruitment. The renal CSC model provides the strongest direct CSC-versus-non-CSC evidence [263], whereas liver cancer and EV-based studies support stemness-high or tumor-derived suppression [264–266]. Together, these mechanisms may reduce CD8⁺ T-cell priming and help CSC-enriched populations maintain an immune-protected niche, but their clinical relevance remains dependent on tumor type and mechanism.

CSCs suppress T-cell effector function

CSC-like states may emerge or become enriched under immune pressure and can, in turn, contribute to reduced T-cell effector activity. In addition to effects on antigen presentation and T-cell priming, CSC-enriched populations may limit T-cell persistence and cytotoxicity through cytokine programs and inhibitory checkpoint signals, forming feedback interactions between immune selection and reduced effector function [270–274].

Cytokine-mediated suppression appears to be highly context dependent. In colorectal cancer, CSC-associated IL-4 is more strongly supported as an autocrine survival and therapy-resistance signal than as a direct mediator of T-cell suppression [271, 275]. In NSCLC, metastasis-initiating cell-enriched spheroids expressing CD133 and C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) suppress CD8⁺ T-cell IFN-γ production and promote Treg induction through IL-10- and CD73/CD38-associated immunosuppressive programs, which can be partly reversed by CXCR4 inhibition [276]. Therefore, CSC-associated cytokine programs can influence T-cell activity through distinct mechanisms across tumor models, while also supporting the persistence of CSC-enriched populations.

Beyond this, CSC-linked inhibitory signaling may further reduce T-cell activity. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), CD44⁺ tumor-initiating cells display higher constitutive and IFN-γ-inducible PD-L1 expression than their CD44⁻ counterparts [200]. CD44-associated PD-L1 regulation has also been reported in triple-negative breast cancer, NSCLC, and bladder cancer models [277]. Similarly, SOX2 directly binds to and activates the PD-L1 promoter in hepatocellular carcinoma, and OCT4/SOX2 induce a BRD-dependent immunosuppressive transcriptome in glioblastoma stem-like cells [278]. In melanoma and NSCLC models, ALDH3A1-high stem-like cells increase PD-L1 output and other immunosuppressive mediators, leading to reduced lymphocyte proliferation [273].

To sum up, CSC-enriched populations can impose sustained local restraint on T-cell effector function rather than merely transient inhibition. These interactions may support survival under immune pressure and contribute to further remodeling of the immune niche. However, much of the current evidence comes from sorted CSC-like populations, spheroid cultures, co-culture systems, and mouse models, whereas patient data remain largely correlative. Whether these suppressive circuits can be therapeutically reversed to eliminate CSCs in patients remains insufficiently validated.

CSCs recruit and reprogram suppressive cell populations

CSC-enriched populations can influence the recruitment and functional states of suppressive immune and stromal cells, thereby contributing to reciprocal interactions within the tumor microenvironment. These populations release cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular vesicles that promote the recruitment or reprogramming of TAMs, Tregs, regulatory B cells (Bregs), MDSCs, and CAFs. In some settings, these cells can, in turn, support stemness-associated properties, invasion, immune exclusion, and treatment resistance.

Cytokines secreted by CSC-enriched populations can promote myeloid-cell recruitment or immunosuppressive polarization. In squamous cell carcinoma, tumor-initiating cells/CSC-derived IL-33 induces the formation of FcεRIα⁺ TAM subsets, which in turn produce TGF-β and support CSC invasiveness and treatment resistance, forming a reciprocal signaling circuit [244, 279]. Additional evidence is that glioblastoma stem cells secrete periostin to recruit M2-like TAMs and support malignant growth [280]. YAP activation in single tumor-initiating cells can also induce CCL2 and CSF-1, recruiting macrophages that protect early tumor-initiating cells from immune clearance [281]. Conversely, TAM-derived IL-6–STAT3 signaling expands CSC-like populations in hepatocellular carcinoma and breast cancer [280, 282]. These findings indicate that CSC–TAM interactions can become self-reinforcing in selected tumors, although the dominant chemokines and macrophage states vary by tumor type.

CSCs further reinforce immune suppression by recruiting and activating Tregs. Ovarian CSCs have been reported to increase IL-10 production by Tregs, which may reduce antitumor immune activity [283]. In colorectal cancer, Treg-derived RANKL activates RANK signaling in tumor cells, increases stemness and migration, and promotes further Treg recruitment through CCL20–CCR6 [159]. B-cell involvement is less established, but CD24hi CD27⁺ Bregs in metastatic breast cancer lymph nodes have been associated with increased stem-like features and multidrug resistance through IL-6 and TNF-α secretion, whereas breast cancer cells reciprocally enhance Breg activation through CD40L- and PD-L1-dependent signals [253].

Myeloid and stromal components may provide additional niche support. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, NEDD9 maintains stemness and recruits MDSCs through CXCL8 [284]. Model-dependent evidence also supports reciprocal interactions between CSCs and CAFs. In mammary tumors, CSC-derived SHH activates Hedgehog signaling in CAFs, which feed back to sustain CSC expansion and self-renewal [285]. Tumor cell-derived extracellular vesicles can also promote CAF-like phenotypes in Piwil2-induced CSC and colorectal tumor sphere models [286, 287]. More recently, primary triple-negative breast cancer stem cells were reported to promote IRF6-dependent stromagenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling in fibroblasts [288].

In summary, CSC-enriched populations may shape immunosuppressive cellular niches by recruiting and reprogramming TAMs, Tregs, Bregs, MDSCs, and CAFs, thereby reinforcing immune evasion, stemness, and therapy resistance. Evidence for CSC–TAM interactions is relatively well developed, whereas Treg-, B-cell- and fibroblast-related mechanisms remain more context dependent and require further validation.

CSCs rewire immune metabolism

CSC-enriched populations in several tumor models exhibit increased glycolytic activity and lactate production compared with more differentiated tumor cells [289, 290]. This metabolic state may support adaptation to hypoxia and nutrient stress, while also influencing the local immune microenvironment. In hypoxic niches, CSC-enriched cells can increase lactate transport and acid adaptation through monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) and carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX), thereby supporting intracellular pH control and extracellular acidification [291, 292]. In breast cancer, ETV4-driven glycolysis sustains stemness through CXCR4–Sonic Hedgehog signaling [290], and recent evidence further associates lactate-dependent regulation with CSC plasticity [293].

Lactate-rich and acidic niches can impair several components of antitumor immunity. Acidic pH impairs Kv1.3-dependent calcium signaling in CD8⁺ T cells, reducing TCR activation and cytotoxic mediator release [294, 295]. Similarly, the activation and function of NK cells are impaired under acidic conditions [296, 297]. Lactate can also redirect DC maturation toward tolerogenic programs through pathways such as SREBP2 and GPR81, thereby limiting antigen presentation and T-cell priming [298, 299]. Together, these effects suggest that lactate production and extracellular acidification associated with CSC-enriched states may contribute to reduced innate and adaptive immune activity.

Moreover, lactate can directly influence immune-cell states through metabolic signaling and epigenetic modification [300]. In TAMs, lactate can promote M2-like polarization and induce pro-tumorigenic gene expression, including Arg1, partly through lactate-linked epigenetic regulation such as histone lactylation [301, 302]. However, lactate does not affect all immune cells in the same way. Effector T cells are sensitive to lactate-associated acidic stress, whereas intratumoral Tregs can import lactate through MCT1 and convert it into pyruvate to support mitochondrial metabolism [303–305]. This flexibility may support Treg persistence in glucose-poor, lactate-rich tumor regions. Lactate can also favor MDSC development and suppressive programming, including STAT3-linked pathways and ROS-mediated T-cell inhibition [306, 307].

CSC-enriched populations may also influence immune responses through additional metabolic pathways. Elevated indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) expression can deplete tryptophan, which limits effector T-cell proliferation, whereas the accumulated byproduct kynurenine activates AhR signaling and promotes Treg differentiation [308, 309]. Similarly, CSC-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) has been reported to impair DC maturation [310] and blunt the cytotoxic activity of NK cells [311]. CSCs and CSC-derived extracellular vesicles may also express CD39 and CD73 to degrade extracellular ATP into adenosine, which subsequently binds to the adenosine A2A receptor on T cells [312, 313]. This signaling axis acts as a metabolic brake, restraining T-cell activation and effector function.

Collectively, metabolic features associated with CSC-enriched states may contribute to a niche characterized by nutrient limitation, lactate accumulation, extracellular acidification, and immunoregulatory metabolites. These conditions can differentially affect effector T cells, NK cells, DCs, Tregs, TAMs, and MDSCs, although the direction and magnitude of these effects vary across experimental models. Therefore, more than merely supporting tumor-cell survival, CSCs could reshape the selective pressures of the local niche so that stem-like tumor states and immunosuppressive immune populations become mutually compatible and potentially self-sustaining.

CSCs shape an immunosuppressive matrix niche

The extracellular matrix (ECM) associated with CSC-enriched tumor regions can display increased density, stiffness, or altered organization, thereby restricting immune-cell migration and access to tumor cells. This matrix niche is shaped not only by CSCs but also by CSC-associated stromal programs, especially CAF-driven remodeling [314–316].

Among ECM components, TNC has been directly associated with both stem-like tumor populations and impaired T-cell responses. Prostate cancer stem-like cells and brain tumor-initiating cells produce TNC, which suppresses T-cell activation by disrupting cytoskeletal remodeling and mTOR signaling through α5β1 and αvβ6 integrins [314, 317]. TNC can also bind CXCL12 and retain CD8⁺ T cells in the stromal matrix, thereby limiting their entry into tumor nests and weakening tumor-cell killing [318]. Thus, TNC may influence antitumor immunity through both spatial sequestration and direct inhibition of T-cell function.

Hyaluronan (HA)-rich matrices provide a related but less CSC-specific barrier. Hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2) is upregulated in metastatic breast cancer stem-like cells, while excessive HA production can induce CSC-like programs through Twist and TGF-β–SNAI1 signaling [319, 320]. In ovarian and lung cancer models, HA-metabolism genes are enriched in chemoresistant or tumor-initiating populations, and HA inhibition reduces spheroid formation and CSC marker expression [321–323]. These studies suggest that CSC-like states and HA-rich matrices may reinforce one another, but direct evidence that CSC-derived HA alone excludes T cells remains limited [324].

Overall, CSC-associated matrix remodeling is better understood as a cooperative niche program rather than a mechanism executed by CSCs alone. TNC provides relatively direct evidence that a matrix component can couple stem-like tumor states to impaired T-cell access and function, whereas collagen stiffening and HA accumulation more often reflect broader stromal remodeling. The matrix niche may therefore act as a physical barrier that favors stem-like tumor states while constraining effective immune surveillance. Therapeutic approaches should aim to disrupt specific immunosuppressive matrix signals without eliminating stromal functions that restrain tumor growth or preserve tissue integrity.

Targeting epigenetic regulators to restrain CSC plasticity and improve responses to immunotherapy

Epigenetic therapies are among the more clinically developed approaches discussed in this section because they can modulate stemness-associated programs while also affecting antigen presentation, interferon signaling, chemokine expression, myeloid suppression, and checkpoint responsiveness [467]. However, the available evidence more often supports immune priming and partial restraint of plasticity than the direct elimination of functional CSCs.

DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors, such as azacitidine and decitabine, can restore the expression of tumor antigens, antigen-processing genes, chemokines, and interferon-related genes, providing a rationale for epigenetic priming before ICB [467]. Their CSC relevance is supported by studies showing that DNMT inhibition or DNMT1 loss can reduce CSC-associated traits in colorectal and mammary tumor models [468, 469]. In immunotherapy models, decitabine priming expands progenitor exhausted CD8+ T cells and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy [470], whereas azacitidine-based priming has improved sensitivity to PD-1 blockade in relapsed or refractory natural killer/T-cell lymphoma [471]. Clinical experience with DNMT inhibitor–immunotherapy combinations remains concentrated in early-phase or relatively small studies, with efficacy varying across disease settings, dosing schedules, and treatment sequences.

The TET/IDH axis also links epigenetic state control with stem-like cell maintenance. TET2 restoration or vitamin C-enhanced TET activity can reverse aberrant self-renewal in hematopoietic stem/progenitor and leukemia models [472], whereas mutant IDH1 inhibition promotes differentiation and reduces leukemia stem cells in AML models [473]. Preclinical immune studies further suggest that TET2 can promote IFN-γ–JAK–STAT-dependent chemokines and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade sensitivity, whereas mutant IDH1 inhibition can reshape antitumor immunity in glioma models [474, 475]. By contrast, ivosidenib plus nivolumab showed limited clinical benefit in treatment-refractory IDH1-mutant solid tumors [474, 476].

Histone methylation regulators provide another epigenetic route. EZH2 inhibition can restore antigen presentation, chemokine expression and sensitize resistant HNSCC or melanoma models to immunotherapy [477, 478]. Lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) inhibition can activate endogenous retroviral or interferon programs, restore MHC-I antigen presentation, increase CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and improves PD-1/PD-L1 blockade response in several models, including small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [479–481]. A more direct CSC-related example comes from HNSCC, where LSD1 targeting suppresses stem-like properties and increases sensitivity to PD-1 blockade [482].

Histone deacetylase (HDAC) and BET inhibitors affect acetylation-dependent transcription. HDAC1 and HDAC7 have been reported to be enriched in breast and ovarian CSCs, and HDAC inhibition can reduce CSC-associated phenotypes [483]. BET inhibition or degradation has also reduced ALDH activity, CSC markers, stemness-related genes, and tumor sphere formation in ovarian cancer and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) models [484, 485]. Evidence for their combination with ICB is mostly immune-based rather than CSC-tracking based. Entinostat can reprogram MDSCs and sensitize breast and pancreatic tumor models to checkpoint blockade [486], whereas tucidinostat (chidamide) has enhanced PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in selected models [487, 488]. Clinical results have been heterogeneous, ranging from limited activity in some studies to early termination of the RO6870810–atezolizumab study because of substantial immune-related toxicity [489–491].

RNA-targeted epigenetic strategies remain less mature. Current RNA epigenetic studies mainly support immune remodeling rather than direct CSC suppression. Clinically, the METTL3 inhibitor STC-15 is being evaluated in early-phase studies, both as monotherapy in advanced malignancies and in combination with the anti-PD-1 antibody toripalimab. FTO, ALKBH5, ADAR1, and YTHDF1 remain largely preclinical in the ICB-combination context [492–497].

Epigenetic targeting is among the more clinically developed approaches for modulating CSC-related plasticity and immune resistance, although its effects vary with tumor epigenetic and immune states. Direct evidence that epigenetic–ICB combinations eliminate functional CSCs remains limited. These combinations may be more effective when selected according to specific epigenetic alterations and the associated immune defects, such as impaired antigen presentation, myeloid suppression, or persistent IFN-response programs.

Blocking stemness pathways to potentiate ICB

Wnt/β-catenin signaling is frequently associated with immune exclusion and ICB resistance. In HCC, it has been reported that pathway activation correlates with poor disease control after ICB [498]. Mechanistically, β-catenin activation impairs CCL5-dependent dendritic-cell and CD8+ T-cell recruitment, thereby promoting anti-PD-1 resistance in HCC preclinical models [499]. Furthermore, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pan-cancer analysis associates Wnt/β-catenin activation with non–T-cell-inflamed tumors [500]. Wnt/β-catenin inhibition can increase immune infiltration and improve PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in selected ovarian cancer, HCC, and melanoma models [501–503]. Because Wnt signaling also maintains CSC-like programs, as shown in colorectal cancer models [504], Wnt blockade is best viewed as a context-dependent immune-sensitizing strategy rather than a broadly validated CSC-depleting approach.

Notch signaling can connect stemness programs with cytokine-driven immune remodeling. In breast cancer models, tumor-derived Jagged1 activates Notch-dependent cytokine secretion, recruits macrophages, suppresses tumoricidal T-cell activity, and cooperates with PD-1 blockade when combined with Notch inhibition [505–507]. In melanoma, Notch1 promotes TGF-β1-dependent accumulation of Tregs and MDSCs while reducing CD8+ T-cell and NK-cell infiltration [508]. Consistently, γ-secretase inhibition reduces immunosuppressive cells and enhances antitumor immunity in HNSCC models [509]. These findings support Notch blockade as a preclinical ICB-sensitizing strategy, but its clinical translation remains constrained by pathway pleiotropy and the lack of robust CSC-specific biomarkers.

Clinical development of Hedgehog (Hh) pathway inhibitors in combination with ICB remains limited. Hh/GLI signaling is relevant because it can support CSC-associated programs and contribute to stromal and immune remodeling. Preclinical studies suggest that Hh/GLI activity can promote immunosuppressive niches through angiogenic, myeloid, Treg, PD-L1, or IDO1-related programs [510–512]. A phase II trial has been registered to evaluate vismodegib plus atezolizumab in platinum-resistant ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal cancer (NCT05538091), although efficacy results are not yet available. Hh inhibition should therefore be regarded as a potential approach for modifying stromal and CSC-associated niches rather than as an established ICB combination strategy.

In conclusion, inhibition of Wnt, Notch, and Hh signaling provides a preclinical basis for sensitizing tumors to ICB by disrupting CSC-supportive and immunosuppressive programs. Clinical evaluation of these combinations remains limited. Because these developmental pathways also regulate normal tissue homeostasis and immune-cell function, their therapeutic use will require sufficient pathway dependence in the tumor, an acceptable treatment window, and biomarkers that identify patients most likely to benefit.

Targeting CSC markers in combination with immunotherapy

CD133 is expressed in CSC-like populations across glioblastoma [513], colorectal [514], lung [515], and breast cancers [516]. CD133-associated signaling can support self-renewal and immune escape, including AKT activation, PD-L1 upregulation, and TGF-β-associated immunosuppression [517–519]. Clinically, CD133-directed CAR-T cells showed preliminary activity in advanced HCC [520]. Nevertheless, stable disease was the most frequent best response, while hyperbilirubinemia in patients with pre-existing biliary obstruction raised concerns about potential on-target, off-tumor toxicity [521]. Multi-antigen recognition may reduce antigen escape, whereas more precise CAR-T designs may improve tumor selectivity.

CD44 is both a CSC-associated marker and a regulator of tumor–stroma interactions. CD44 isoform switching helps define breast CSC states, whereas CD44v6 marks metastatic colorectal CSCs [522, 523]. The CD44–hyaluronan axis may also contribute to immune resistance by promoting PD-L1 expression and stromal barriers that restrict immune infiltration, and hyaluronan degradation can enhance PD-L1 blockade in preclinical models [277, 524]. However, single-marker CD44 targeting has faced clinical limitations, as anti-CD44 antibody therapy and CD44v6-directed antibody–drug conjugates have faced efficacy or safety constraints [525, 526]. Dual-target CAR-T strategies recognizing both CD44 and CD133 have shown improved selectivity and durable activity in glioblastoma models, suggesting that combinatorial antigen recognition may be safer than single-marker targeting [527].

ALDH activity can enrich for stem-like tumor populations, but ALDH-directed combinations with immunotherapy remain largely preclinical. Disulfiram can suppress CSC-like cells in preclinical models and may enhance T-cell antitumor immunity [528, 529]. However, available clinical studies of disulfiram-based cancer therapy remain exploratory and are not direct ICB-combination trials [530–532]. Its translation is complicated by chemical instability, copper dependence, and uncertain intratumoral exposure [533, 534]. Disulfiram is therefore better regarded as a potential CSC-sensitizing agent than as an established immunotherapy partner. Recent research outcome nanomedicine-based systems are being developed to enhance disulfiram stability, increase tumor-site accumulation, and enable more controlled co-delivery with copper [535, 536]. Future progress will depend on overcoming these pharmacokinetic barriers, clarifying how copper dependence can be managed safely, and incorporating biomarker-guided patient selection based on ALDH1A expression or related target dependency.

EpCAM functions as a CSC-associated epithelial marker and an immune-engaging target. EpCAM signaling has been reported to promote PD-L1 stability, supporting its relevance to adaptive immune escape [537]. EpCAM/CD3 bispecific or trispecific antibodies can recruit T cells and show antitumor activity in preclinical or early clinical settings [538–540]. In addition to promoting antitumor immunity, EpCAM-directed targeting may also deplete EpCAM+ CSCs, potentially reducing a reservoir of immune-resistant cells [541, 542]. Clinically, the EpCAM/CD3 bispecific antibody catumaxomab has been investigated for gastric cancer with peritoneal disease/peritoneal carcinomatosis, including randomized phase II evaluation in combination sequences with systemic therapy, demonstrating the feasibility of regional EpCAM-directed immunotherapy [543, 544]. However, evidence for combining EpCAM-directed therapy with systemic PD-1/PD-L1 blockade remains limited, and EpCAM expression in normal epithelial tissues raises concerns about tumor selectivity [545, 546].

Unlike EpCAM, CD24 is more prominently implicated in innate immune evasion, particularly through its interaction with Siglec-10 on myeloid cells [547]. CD24–Siglec-10 signaling can suppress macrophage phagocytosis and may impair antigen cross-presentation in some models, functioning as an innate immune checkpoint analogous to the CD47–SIRPα axis [548–550]. In high-grade serous ovarian cancer models, targeting the mesothelin–CD24 axis repolarizes macrophages and enhances the response to PD-1 blockade [551]. The anti-CD24 monoclonal antibody ATG-031 has entered phase I testing in advanced solid tumors or B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NCT06028373). A primary constraint is the tissue-specific heterogeneity of CD24 as a CSC marker. Although it identifies cancer stemness in various malignancies, breast CSCs characteristically exhibit a CD44high/CD24low phenotype [552]. Accordingly, CD24-targeted interventions should be regarded as a means of disrupting myeloid immune suppression rather than a pan-CSC depletion strategy. Future studies should define whether high Siglec-10 expression or impaired phagocytosis predicts response.

Taken together, marker-directed strategies have generated substantial preclinical evidence, but clinical studies have not yet established selective depletion of functional CSCs or consistent synergy with ICB. Among the stemness markers discussed above, only selected CD133- and EpCAM-directed approaches have entered clinical evaluation, while most other strategies remain at the preclinical or early translational stage. Considering the phenotypic heterogeneity and plasticity of CSCs, future therapeutic development will likely require dual- or multi-target CAR-T designs to improve the selectivity of CSC-like tumor cells.

Targeting metabolic plasticity to relieve immune suppression

Metabolic targeting is discussed only briefly here because most available evidence supports immune modulation or sensitization rather than direct CSC depletion. In HCC models, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of SRSF10 reduced MYB-dependent glycolysis and lactate production, decreased macrophage H3K18 lactylation and M2-like polarization, and enhanced the antitumor activity of PD-1 blockade [553]. Mitochondrial metabolism provides another potential target. Metformin reduced tumor hypoxia and enhanced responses to PD-1 blockade in preclinical models [554]. By contrast, nivolumab plus metformin produced no objective responses in treatment-refractory microsatellite-stable metastatic colorectal cancer (CRC), with stable disease observed in only 2 of 18 evaluable patients [555]. Among metabolic pathways, fatty acid oxidation (FAO) currently provides a more direct CSC–ICB connection. Targeting carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) increased ferroptosis in lung cancer stem-like cells and enhanced the antitumor activity of PD-1 blockade in mouse models [556]. Glutamine metabolism is also being explored, as GLS inhibition can enhance the effects of PD-L1 blockade in ovarian cancer models [557], whereas telaglenastat plus nivolumab was tolerable but showed heterogeneous and limited activity across melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and non-small cell lung cancer cohorts [558].

In conclusion, metabolic interventions should be selected according to the dominant dependencies of the targeted CSC or persister state and the surrounding immune compartment, rather than assigned broadly by tumor type. OXPHOS-directed approaches may be relevant to OXPHOS-dependent states reported in pancreatic precursor lesions, ovarian CSC models, and endocrine therapy-tolerant ER⁺ breast cancer [118, 126–128], whereas glycolysis/lactate targeting may be more suitable for glycolysis-driven or lactate-rich settings, including breast cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer, HCC, CRC, and glycolysis-adapted NSCLC [120–122, 553]. FAO- and glutamine-directed approaches likewise require evidence of pathway dependence [556–559]. Their therapeutic value will depend on whether pathway inhibition preferentially disrupts tumor-cell adaptation while preserving or enhancing antitumor immune function.

Current translational status and challenges

The clinical translation of CSC-related immunotherapy combinations remains heterogeneous (Tables 2 and 3). Epigenetic strategies are the most clinically advanced, whereas most developmental pathway inhibitors, CSC-marker-directed approaches, and metabolic interventions remain supported primarily by preclinical or early-phase evidence. Importantly, many of these combinations are not designed to eliminate CSCs directly, but rather to restrain plasticity, relieve immune suppression, or improve ICB responsiveness in selected tumor contexts. Therefore, future clinical interpretation should distinguish true CSC-targeted effects from broader immune-priming effects.More promising strategies may be biomarker-guided combinations that align stemness-associated plasticity, immune-resistance mechanisms, and tumor-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Discussion

CSCs are increasingly understood as reversible functional states rather than fixed tumor-cell subsets. Their plasticity is shaped by intrinsic regulatory programs, therapy pressure, immune selection, and niche signals. This allows tumor cells to switch between metabolic, proliferative, invasive, and immune states, thereby supporting residual disease, immune escape, and metastasis. The major challenge is therefore not only the presence of pre-existing CSCs but also the ability of tumor cells to repeatedly enter and re-establish stem-like states under selective pressure.

The evidence reviewed here supports a bidirectional relationship between CSC-related states and the tumor immune microenvironment. Immune editing may eliminate highly immunogenic tumor cells while allowing less visible and more adaptable CSC-like populations to persist. Chronic inflammation, hypoxia, cytokine signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and suppressive immune niches may further induce or stabilize these states. In turn, CSC-enriched populations can impair antigen presentation, reinforce immune checkpoint signaling, resist natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, alter local metabolic conditions, recruit suppressive immune and stromal cells, and contribute to matrix barriers that restrict immune-cell access. These reciprocal interactions link CSC plasticity to adaptive immune resistance and may contribute to both primary and acquired resistance to immunotherapy.

Combining immunotherapy with strategies targeting CSC-associated pathways has produced encouraging results, especially in preclinical models. These include approaches targeting epigenetic plasticity, Wnt/Notch/Hedgehog signaling, CSC-associated surface markers, and metabolic dependencies such as lactate production, mitochondrial respiration, fatty acid oxidation, and glutamine metabolism. Clinical translation, however, remains heterogeneous. Some epigenetic and metabolic agents have entered early-phase trials in combination with PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade, whereas many marker-directed, pathway-directed or niche-targeting strategies remain exploratory. Importantly, improved responsiveness to ICB does not by itself demonstrate direct suppression of CSCs. Clinical and preclinical studies should therefore distinguish general immune remodeling from changes in CSC self-renewal, tumor-initiating capacity, frequency, and state conversion.

Several biological and therapeutic challenges remain. CSCs lack a unified definition, and marker enrichment alone does not establish functional CSC identity. Stemness-related pathways also overlap with normal stem and progenitor programs, which limits the therapeutic window. In addition, the optimal dose, sequence, duration, and safety of CSC-targeted combinations remain unclear. Tumor cells may escape through compensatory signaling, metabolic switching, or transition into alternative stem-like states. Current models also do not fully reproduce the human immune ecosystem, stromal architecture, and spatial heterogeneity.

This review also has several limitations. Its broad scope required the integration of evidence from different tumor types, experimental systems, treatment settings, and definitions of CSCs, which limits direct comparison across studies. Moreover, many studies inferred CSC-related states from marker expression, tumor sphere formation, metabolic features, or transcriptional signatures without functional validation through serial transplantation, lineage tracing, or limiting-dilution assays. Some reported effects may therefore reflect broader changes in tumor-cell fitness or plasticity rather than selective effects on functional CSCs. In addition, much of the mechanistic evidence remains preclinical, whereas available clinical studies were generally designed to evaluate safety and antitumor activity rather than CSC-specific endpoints. Clinically, the assessment is further limited by ongoing trials, incomplete reporting of negative findings, and the rapid evolution of this field. Finally, this review provides a mechanistic synthesis rather than a formal quantitative comparison or evidence-grade assessment. Its conclusions should therefore be interpreted as a framework for understanding CSC–immune interactions rather than as a ranking of therapeutic efficacy.

Against these methodological and translational limitations, future strategies should move beyond targeting CSC-like populations at a single time point. More effective treatment may require simultaneous disruption of stemness maintenance, CSC-state conversion, niche protection, metabolic adaptation, and matrix-mediated immune exclusion. In some tumors, pushing CSC-like cells toward a more stable and treatment-sensitive differentiated state may be more feasible than direct eradication. Biomarker-guided patient selection, dynamic monitoring, humanized models, organoid–immune cocultures, spatial omics, single-cell analysis, and lineage tracing will be important for refining these approaches. The ultimate objective is to interrupt the reciprocal reinforcement between CSC plasticity and immune suppression, thereby limiting adaptive resistance and improving the durability of immunotherapy responses.
