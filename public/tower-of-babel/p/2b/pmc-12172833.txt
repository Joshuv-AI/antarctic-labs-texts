Hearing loss is associated with decreased default-mode network connectivity in individuals with mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Nicole Grant, Natalie Phillips
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and hearing loss (HL) have been separately associated with increased dementia risk. These highly co-occurring dementia risk factors are associated with aberrant functional brain connectivity. In individuals with HL aberrant functional connectivity has been associated with cognitive impairment. In individuals with MCI, aberrant brain connectivity has been associated with severity of cognitive impairment and conversion to dementia. Despite the high prevalence of HL in individuals with MCI, the relationship between the two is understudied, especially in the context of functional connectivity.Participantsinclude 94 older adults with MCI. Hearing measures include pure-tone hearing thresholds and speech-reception thresholds. In analyses with pure-tone hearing loss, participants were classified as having normal hearing (n = 60) or hearing loss (n = 34) based on a pure-tone screening protocol. We used independent component analyses on resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data to identify the default-mode network (DMN). Analysis of variance with covariate control was used to investigate DMN connectivity differences between the normally hearing individuals with MCI and individuals with MCI and pure-tone HL. A linear regression was used to investigate the relationship between speech-reception threshold and DMN connectivity in the whole sample.Compared to those with MCI and normal hearing, those with MCI and pure-tone HL had decreased connectivity between the DMN and the caudate and thalamus. There was no difference in DMN connectivity as a function of speech-reception threshold.Our findings indicate that in individuals with MCI, HL is associated with decreased connectivity between the DMN and regions important for sensory and cognitive processing. This suggests that the increased risk for cognitive decline and dementia in individuals with HL may be due to decreased DMN connectivity in individuals with HL.

Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is conceptualized as an intermediate stage of cognitive impairment that is often a transitional phase from the cognitive changes that are present in normal cognitive aging to those typically seen in individuals with dementia (Petersen, 2004). Amnestic MCI is considered a risk-state for Alzheimer's disease (AD) based on the presence of AD pathology and the increased development of AD in these individuals (Petersen, 2004). Individuals with amnestic MCI have a subjective memory complaint, objective evidence of memory impairment for their age and education level, yet intact activities of daily living (Petersen, 2004).

Hearing loss and dementia

Hearing loss was first identified as an independent risk factor for cognitive decline decades ago (Uhlmann et al., 1989). There has been increasing evidence for a strong link between hearing loss and the development of dementia in older age. Hearing loss has been identified as the leading potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia, with up to 8% of global dementia cases estimated as being attributable to hearing loss (Livingston et al., 2020). The Lancet Commission reported a pooled relative risk of 1.9 times greater risk of incident dementia in individuals aged 55 and older with hearing loss compared to those with normal hearing (Livingston et al., 2020). Hearing loss was conceptualized based on the World Health Organizations threshold for hearing loss, which is 25 dB (Livingston et al., 2020). Baseline hearing loss was also associated with a 30% increase in dementia incidence (Odds ratio = 1.3) per 10 dB of worsening hearing loss (Livingston et al., 2020).

Hearing loss currently impacts an estimated 20% of the global population, which is more than 1.5 billion people worldwide (World Health Organization, 2021). Over 65% of adults above 60 years of age experience some degree of hearing loss (World Health Organization, 2021). Age-related hearing loss, also called presbycusis, accounts of the largest percentage of hearing loss cases around the world and presents as a gradual decrease in hearing ability (World Health Organization, 2021). Age-related hearing loss is a progressive, bilateral, and symmetrical sensorineural hearing loss related to age-related degeneration of the inner ear structures (Fischer et al., 2016). More than 90% of hearing loss in older adults is accounted for by age-related hearing loss (Vos et al., 2017; Mick et al., 2021).

Hearing loss and MCI are two dementia risk factors that are highly co-occurring. Hearing loss is prevalent in and associated with MCI (Lau et al., 2022). Lau et al. (2022) recently conducted systematic review and meta-analysis of population-based studies on the relationship between hearing loss and MCI. They found that the pooled risk ratio across all studies of MCI prevalence in people with hearing loss was 1.44 (Lau et al., 2022). Significantly more people with MCI had peripheral hearing loss compared to those without (Lau et al., 2022). When the incidence was examined, significantly more people with peripheral hearing loss had MCI compared to those without (risk ratio = 2.06) (Lau et al., 2022).

There have been several studies investigating the timing of the relationship between hearing loss, cognitive decline, and dementia. Findings from these studies have indicated that hearing loss is prospectively associated with cognitive decline. Influential reports in 2017 and 2020 summarized the data on the relationship between hearing loss and cognitive decline and reported on three high quality studies that found an association between hearing loss and incident dementia (Livingston et al., 2017, 2020). In a study by Lin et al., 2011a of 639 dementia-free individuals who underwent audiometric testing between 1990 and 1994, hearing loss was independently associated with the incident all-cause dementia at a median follow-up period of 11.9 years. Gallacher et al. (2012) investigated the association between auditory thresholds, cognitive decline, and dementia in 1057 men and found that auditory thresholds at baseline were associated with cognitive decline and dementia over a 17-year period. Deal et al. (2017) investigated the associations between hearing loss, cognitive change, and incident dementia and found that hearing loss was associated with increased risk of incident dementia over 9 years. Finally, a recent study examined the relationship between pure-tone and speech audiometry and cognitive outcomes (Marinelli et al., 2022). Although there was no association between objectively measured hearing impairment and dementia, hearing impairment was associated with longitudinal cognitive decline and informant-based reports of hearing difficulties were associated with developing dementia (Marinelli et al., 2022).

Although the mechanisms underlying the relationship between hearing loss and cognitive decline remain unknown, predominant hypotheses have indicated the importance of changes in brain structure and function (Griffiths et al., 2020; Humes et al., 2013; Lindenberger and Baltes, 1994; Powell, 2022; Wayne and Johnsrude, 2015). Both hearing loss (Jafari et al., 2021) and MCI have been associated with alterations in functional brain connectivity (Eyler et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). Altered functional brain connectivity has been associated with cognitive impairment in both groups (Jafari et al., 2021; Wang et al. 2013, 2018; Li et al., 2017) and progression to AD in individuals with MCI (Petrella et al., 2011). Findings of brain altered connectivity and its association with cognitive impairment in individuals with hearing loss and in individuals with MCI are discussed in detail in sections 1, 1.3.4.

Functional connectivity

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a non-invasive technique for examining brain function. It utilizes changes in blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal to identify areas of increased or decreased neural activity. This temporal correlation is thought to reflect connectivity between brain regions (Biswal et al., 1995). Functional connectivity refers to the temporal correlations between the BOLD activation time series of different brain regions and it can be defined as the synchrony of neural activity among regions.

Functional MRI recorded in the absence of a task is called resting-state fMRI and it measures the functional connectivity of the brain at rest. Resting-state fMRI has revealed the presence of intrinsic resting-state brain networks that support core perceptual and cognitive processes (Biswal et al., 1995, 2010). Resting-state networks are spatially separate and functionally connected brain regions that are metabolically co-active when a person is at rest (i.e., not engaging in an externally oriented or attention demanding task). Resting-state networks are known to share and support cognitive functions.

One of the most robustly identified and extensively investigated resting-state networks involves a set of regions that decrease activity during tasks requiring focused attention, referred to as the default-mode network (DMN). The DMN is a resting-state network of widely distributed brain regions that includes the posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, inferior lateral parietal and medial prefrontal cortices (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014; Smallwood et al., 2021). The DMN shows increased activity during a passive state and is thought to support unconstrained cognitive processes (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014). Its activity has been associated with introspective mental processes, such as spontaneous and deliberate self-generated thought, self-referential processing, internally generated thoughts, and autobiographical memory (Smallwood et al., 2021; Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014). Other studies have shown that the DMN is also active under resting conditions when focused attention is directed away from external stimuli (Rosazza and Minati, 2011). According to some studies, the DMN also includes the hippocampi and is involved in the consolidation of episodic memory (Rosazza and Minati, 2011). In addition, it is suggested that DMN may facilitate or monitor performance during active tasks, rather than simply de-activation during task performance (Rosazza and Minati, 2011).

MCI and functional connectivity

There is extensive evidence for disrupted DMN connectivity in individuals with and at risk for AD. It is suggested that the posterior components of the default-mode network (i.e., the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus) are among the first to be affected by age, MCI, and dementia pathology (Cavanna and Trimble, 2006). This is supported by the unique metabolic and connective properties of the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus that are thought to make these structures vulnerable to the neurodegenerative processes associated with aging and dementia pathology (Matsuda, 2001). Indeed, the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus are known to be metabolically expensive and highly connected regions of the brain (Cavanna and Trimble, 2006). Neuronal activity stimulates aerobic glycolysis, which may increase amyloid beta production and neurodegeneration (Ruan et al., 2016; Sperling et al., 2011). This is notable as increased amyloid beta in the brain increases the risk of cognitive decline and developing dementia. Moreover, increased deposition of amyloid in the DMN has also been associated with impaired performance on measures of memory (Han et al., 2011; Sperling et al., 2011).

The presence of altered functional connectivity is well-established in individuals with MCI. Studies using resting-state fMRI have shown that alterations in brain network connectivity occur early in AD and are associated with AD pathophysiology (Badhwar et al., 2017; Jack et al., 2018). Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have investigated resting-state network connectivity across the spectrum of cognitive impairment that manifests in individuals with and at risk for AD (Badhwar et al., 2017; Eyler et al., 2019; Li et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018). Despite the methodological and clinical heterogeneity that exists across this spectrum, certain patterns of altered brain connectivity have emerged. In individuals with AD, neural networks generally show widespread disruption and a progressive loss of functional connectivity, particularly in the DMN (Ruan et al., 2016). Studies using resting-state fMRI have shown that alterations in brain network connectivity occur early in AD and are associated with the cognitive symptoms and AD pathophysiology (Han et al., 2011; Ruan et al., 2016). Changes in brain connectivity are thought to begin years before AD diagnosis and may be an important marker for early AD (Matthews and Hampshire, 2016; Sperling et al., 2011; Vemuri et al., 2012). Alterations in resting-state fMRI can be detected prior to extensive neurodegeneration and neuronal loss associated with AD pathology, suggesting that it is a biomarker for neuronal injury that holds promise for early diagnosis (Jack et al., 2018).

A recent meta-analysis showed that MCI is reliably associated with decreased DMN connectivity in the bilateral precuneus, posterior cingulate cortices, and medial temporal lobes (Wang et al., 2018). Interestingly, these regions are implicated in the episodic memory deficits that are associated with AD (Wang et al., 2018). In the same meta-analysis, greater severity of cognitive impairment was correlated with stronger functional connectivity in several regions (Wang et al., 2018). This was interpreted as reflecting a compensatory mechanism for maintaining cognitive efficiency (Wang et al., 2018). Another recent study that compared DMN connectivity in adults with MCI to healthy older adults found that MCI was associated with both regions of increased and regions of decreased DMN connectivity. In this study, these alterations were correlated with reduced memory function (Li et al., 2017).

Although there is extensive evidence of altered DMN connectivity in adults with MCI, there has been heterogeneity in the literature regarding the nature of these connectivity alterations (Eyler et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2013, 2018). Compared to healthy controls, individuals with MCI have shown either no differences in functional connectivity, increased connectivity, decreased connectivity, and regionally mixed directions of effect (Eyler et al., 2019). Despite this, the presence of altered DMN connectivity of some variety has been a consistent finding in studies of individuals with MCI (Eyler et al., 2019). The presence of and degree of altered DMN connectivity has been associated with severity of cognitive impairment in individuals with MCI and AD. In individuals with MCI and AD, greater alterations DMN connectivity have been correlated with more severe cognitive dysfunction (Binnewijzend et al., 2012). In another study of individuals with MCI, disrupted network connectivity was associated with global cognitive function and memory function (Dicks et al., 2018). In the same study, greater network disruption was associated with a steeper rate of cognitive decline (Dicks et al., 2018). In addition, altered DMN connectivity has shown utility in predicting further cognitive decline and conversion to AD in individuals with MCI (Petrella et al., 2011).

While recent meta-analyses have reported that methodological differences do not appear to fully explain the inconsistencies in the literature concerning DMN connectivity in individuals with MCI, the studies reviewed did not include hearing loss as a variable (Eyler et al., 2019). As there is evidence that hearing loss is also associated with altered brain connectivity and given that hearing loss is prevalent in adults with MCI, the failure to examine the occurrence of hearing loss in studies of individuals with MCI could have substantially influenced the heterogeneity reported in the literature on the relationship between MCI and DMN connectivity.

Hearing loss and brain connectivity

Hearing loss is associated with the neuroplastic reorganization of several brain regions and networks. This is not restricted to individuals with severe and profound hearing loss or deafness; plasticity occurs in mild-moderate hearing loss that is related to aging (Glick and Sharma, 2017). There is evidence that hearing loss is associated with altered brain function in cognitively healthy and cognitively impaired older adults (for a review, see Jafari et al., 2021). These alterations in brain function have been associated with impaired performance on cognitive tasks in individuals with hearing loss (Jafari et al., 2021). Moreover, it is thought that the cognitive impairment that is often present in adults with hearing loss is due to the disruption of the brain network connectivity (Cardin, 2016; Griffiths et al., 2020; Jafari et al., 2021; Powell, 2022; Pronk et al., 2019). It is also thought that cortical reorganization due to hearing loss utilizes areas typically involved in higher-level cognition, subsequently hindering the brain's ability to adapt to aging and AD pathology (Griffiths et al., 2020; Pronk et al., 2019). This suggests that the alterations in functional connectivity due to hearing loss may be a potential pathway or mechanism by which hearing loss is related to cognitive impairment (Griffiths et al., 2020; Jafari et al., 2021).

There is evidence that hearing loss is associated with altered DMN connectivity (Cardin, 2016; Jafari et al., 2021). Many of the studies that have explored the relationship between hearing loss and brain connectivity have focused on sensorineural hearing loss, which occurs due to inner ear damage or due to problems with the nerve pathways that extend from the inner ear to the brain (Powell et al., 2022). While sensorineural hearing loss can contribute to the development of age-related hearing loss, age-related hearing loss can be due to a variety of aetiologies. Therefore, although sensorineural hearing loss contributes to the development of age-related hearing loss, age-related hearing loss may not be solely attributed to sensorineural hearing loss. As a result, age-related and isolated sensorineural hearing loss may have differential effects on brain connectivity. Despite this, evidence for the relationship between sensorineural hearing loss and altered DMN connectivity is presented below due to the comparable lack of data on DMN connectivity in individuals with age-related hearing loss.

Several studies using fMRI have found altered DMN connectivity in individuals with sensorineural hearing loss (Li et al., 2015; Luan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2015, 2018). One study that compared DMN connectivity between normal hearing individuals and adults with long-term unilateral sensorineural hearing loss found that hearing loss was correlated with alterations in the DMN (Zhang et al., 2015). Another study that compared DMN connectivity between normal hearing adults and adults with sensorineural hearing loss found decreased between- and within network connectivity alterations in DMN as a function of hearing loss (Li et al., 2015). Similar results have been found using graph theory approaches to fMRI analysis, where between- and within-network connectivity was compared between a normal hearing group and a group with bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (Luan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2018). Sensorineural hearing loss has been associated with widespread brain network reorganization, including the decreased connectivity between DMN regions (Zhang et al., 2018). Similarly, another recent study found that sensorineural hearing loss was cross-sectionally correlated with widespread disruptions in functional connectivity, including regions of the DMN (Luan et al., 2020). In the same study altered DMN connectivity was associated with lower scores on tests of processing speed (Luan et al., 2020).

There is also evidence for altered DMN connectivity as a function of age-related hearing loss (Ren et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2020). A recent study that compared a group of normally hearing and a group of hearing-impaired older adults found that individuals pure-tone hearing loss had decreased DMN connectivity compared to a normal hearing control group (Xing et al., 2020). In this study, alterations in DMN connectivity were cross-sectionally correlated with poorer performance on cognitive tests (Xing et al., 2020). In addition, age-related hearing loss has been associated with decreased connectivity between key nodes of the DMN; these alterations were associated with cognitive impairment (Ren et al., 2021). There is also evidence that age-related hearing loss is associated with widespread disruptions of functional connectivity outside of the DMN (Chen et al., 2018; Yong et al., 2022). For instance, age-related hearing loss measured with pure-tone audiometry has been associated with decreased hippocampal connectivity (Chen et al., 2018). In this study, decreased connectivity between the hippocampus and the inferior parietal lobules was associated with impaired performance on a processing speed test (Chen et al., 2018). Alterations in brain connectivity have also been associated with cognitive performance and speech/language processing abilities in individuals with age-related hearing loss (Chen et al., 2018; Yong et al., 2022). Finally, alterations in brain connectivity are thought to contribute to the relationship between hearing loss and cognitive decline, indicating the importance of further investigating the relationship between hearing loss and brain network connectivity in adults with various levels of cognitive impairment (Griffiths et al., 2020; Pronk et al., 2019).

Research objectives and hypotheses

The relationships between hearing loss, functional connectivity, and MCI remain poorly understood. Moreover, studies investigating the relationship between hearing loss and resting-state connectivity have primarily used measures of pure-tone hearing. The present study has included measures of pure-tone hearing detection thresholds and suprathreshold speech-reception thresholds (SRTs) in noise, a measure of speech-perception in noise. Our research objective was to characterize the relationships between pure-tone detection thresholds, SRTs, and DMN connectivity in individuals with MCI. Based on the well-established relationship between MCI and altered DMN connectivity and the known association between hearing loss and altered brain connectivity, it was expected that both hearing measures would be associated with altered DMN connectivity. Specifically, as decreased DMN connectivity is associated with disease progression in individuals with AD, it was expected that poorer hearing would be associated with decreased DMN connectivity.

Methods

Data

All data are from the Comprehensive Assessment of Neurodegeneration and Dementia (COMPASS-ND) study of the Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging (CCNA). The CCNA was established by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) to coordinate and strengthen Canadian research on AD and other neurodegenerative diseases (Chertkow et al., 2019). The COMPASS-ND study was approved by relevant Research Ethics Boards. Further details of the COMPASS-ND study are available at http://ccna-ccnv.ca/compass-nd-study/). Data used in the present study are currently stored on the Longitudinal Online Research Imaging System database (see https://ccna.loris.ca; (Mohaddes et al., 2018).

COMPASS-ND is the signature study of the CCNA. It is a national initiative that includes cohorts of individuals with various types and degrees of cognitive concerns and/or neurodegenerative diseases (Chertkow et al., 2019). COMPASS-ND participants include cognitively healthy individuals and individuals with subjective cognitive decline, MCI, AD, and other types of dementia (Chertkow et al., 2019). Aims of the COMPASS-ND study include better understanding neurodegenerative disease, improving quality of life for individuals living with dementia, and working towards dementia prevention (Chertkow et al., 2019).

COMPASS-ND participants were recruited from 31 centres across Canada (i.e., memory, movement disorder, and stroke clinics). At an intake interview a nurse or physician assessed study participants to determine their demographics, medical history, and eligibility based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants then completed comprehensive evaluations that included clinical and neuropsychological assessment, bio sample collection, and MRI neuroimaging.

The present study includes a subset of COMPASS-ND participants from the second data release (December 2021) that met the criteria for MCI. Of the 104 COMPASS-ND participants meeting the criteria for MCI, one participant was excluded because they did not have MRI data, eight participants were excluded because their MRI data failed quality control with visual inspection, and one participant was excluded for missing SRT data. Hearing measures

Pure-tone audiometry

The most used measure of hearing loss is pure-tone audiometry (Katz et al., 2015). Typically, an auditory stimulus (pure tone) is presented at several frequencies [measured in Hertz (Hz)], commonly within the range of 250–8000 Hz. The presentation level of each pure tone begins at an audible level and is decreased to determine the lowest threshold level [i.e., the volume in decibels hearing level (dB HL)] at which the individual can detect the tone (Powell , 2022).

We used a hearing screening protocol assessed participants’ ability to detect at least one of two pure tones presented at three pre-selected selected frequencies at fixed dB HL levels. Each ear was tested separately. First, a 2-kHz pure tone was presented at 40 dB HL during two trials. If the participant was able to detect at least one of these tones, then two trials at 25 dB HL were presented at 2 kHz, 1 kHz, and 4 kHz. Participants who failed to hear a 2-kHz pure tone at 40 dB HL were provided with a Pocket Talker assistive listening device throughout the neuropsychological and clinical assessment if they did not have their own hearing aid. Participants were assigned to one of two hearing categories, normal hearing, or pure-tone hearing loss, based on their ability to detect at least one of the two 2-kHz tones presented at 25 dB HL. The COMPASS-ND hearing classification system has been validated against two large data sets that measured audiometric thresholds with full audiometric assessments in older adults (see Giroud et al., 2021 for details).

Canadian digit triplet test

The Canadian Digit Triplet Test (CDTT) was used to assess participants speech-reception threshold (SRT), which is a measure of speech perception ability in an adverse listening condition. Scores on the CDTT were used to compute SRTs for each participant, which correspond to the signal-to-noise ratio at which triplets of digits were recognized 80% of the time. The CDTT uses an adaptive 1-up-1-down procedure (speech level decreases after a correct response and increases after an incorrect response) and responses were considered correct if all three digits were repeated correctly at the triplet level (i.e., in the correct order; Ellaham et al., 2016; Giguère et al., 2020; Giroud et al., 2021). The CDTT application was run on a Dell XPS laptop using a USB audio card (Creative Sound Blaster X-Fi Go! Pro). The standard deviation of the responses by each participant and the number of their reversal of digits were used to identify erratic runs.

Vison measures

Reading acuity

The MNRCharts were used to obtain measures of reading acuity (the ability to read sentences at a given distance) by assessing participants reading performance using various font sizes (Mansfield et al., 1993). Participants were instructed to read aloud sentences on a hand-held chart viewed perpendicularly and at 40 cm away. They were instructed to use both eyes and to start reading at the top of the chart and to continue until they could not read any words in a sentence. If available, participants were permitted to wear their habitual corrective lenses.

Reading acuity was operationalized as the smallest print size that the participant could read the entire sentence without making significant errors. Reading acuity was measured as the logarithm to the base 10 of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR10) of the last sentence the participant was able to read. The calculation considered number of sentences correctly read, words missed, and errors made: [acuity = 1.4-(amount of all sentences read x 0.1) + (total amount of errors x 0.01)].

Contrast sensitivity

The MARS contrast sensitivity test was used to assess contrast sensitivity (the ability to distinguish an object from its background) by measuring the sensitivity of the eyes in processing letters across different levels of contrast (Mars Perceptrix, 2020). For this task participants read letters on a chart held perpendicularly by the participant, with the participants eyes at 50 cm from the chart. Participants were instructed to read the letters with both eyes from the left to right of each line, from the top to the bottom of the chart. When the participants made two consecutive errors, this task was discontinued. Participants were encouraged to wear their habitual corrective lenses if available. Contrast sensitivity was operationalized as the last letter correctly read by the participant. The log contrast sensitivity was calculated by identifying the value at the lowest contrast letter prior to two incorrectly identified letters and subtracting it by the number of errors prior to the final correct letter (Mars Perceptrix, 2020). See Rehan et al. (2021) for details on the measures of visual function in the COMPASS-ND study.

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Data acquisition

T1-weighted images and resting-state scans were obtained using 3 T scanners from different COMPASS-ND sites across Canada following the Canadian Dementia Imaging Protocol (CDIP; Duchesne et al., 2019). The CDIP is a validated, harmonized protocol for MRI data acquisition in individuals with neurodegeneration (Duchesne et al., 2019). It is available for GE, Phillips, and Siemens scanners. The sequences used are standardized across MRI machines throughout the country and were used to obtain 3D T1, PD/T2, FLAIR, gradient echo, resting state fMRI and Diffusion Tensor Imaging scans for all participants. The CDIP parameters for the resting-state scans and 3D-T1 weighted images can be found in Tables 4 and 5 of the supplementary document. The full parameters for the CDIP protocols can be found here https://www.cdip-pcid.ca/. Acquisition of resting-state scans used a T2-weighted BOLD-sensitive sequence, with a resolution of 3.5 × 3.5 × 3.5 mm3, TR 5 2110 msec (GE: 2500 msec), and 300 vol over 10 min (Duchesne et al., 2019). Parameters varied depending on the scanner type and version allowing for the images to be as comparable as possible (Duchesne et al., 2019).

MRI preprocessing

Results included in this manuscript come from analyses performed using CONN (RRID:SCR_009550; Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012) release 20. b (Nieto-Castanon and Whitfield-Gabrieli, 2020) and SPM release 12.7771 (RRID:SCR_007037; Penny et al., 2011).

Functional and anatomical data were preprocessed using a flexible preprocessing pipeline including realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, outlier detection, direct segmentation and MNI-space normalization, and smoothing (Nieto-Castanon, 2020). Functional data were realigned using SPM realign & unwarp procedure (Andersson et al., 2001), where all scans were coregistered to a reference image (first scan of the first session) using a least squares approach and a 6 parameter (rigid body) transformation (Friston et al., 1995) and resampled using b-spline interpolation to correct for motion and magnetic susceptibility interactions. Potential outlier scans were identified using CONN's Artefacts Detection Tool (ART; Whitfield-Gabrieli et al., 2011) as acquisitions with framewise displacement above 0.9 mm or global BOLD signal changes above 5 standard deviations (Power et al., 2014), and a reference BOLD image was computed for each subject by averaging all scans excluding outliers. No outlier scans were detected using ART. Functional and anatomical data were normalized into standard MNI space, segmented into grey matter, white matter, and CSF tissue classes, and resampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels following a direct normalization procedure (Calhoun et al., 2017) using SPM unified segmentation and normalization algorithm (Ashburner and Friston, 2005; Ashburner, 2007) with the default IXI-549 tissue probability map template. Last, functional data were smoothed using spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full width half maximum (FWHM).

Denoising

In addition, functional data were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline (Nieto-Castanon, 2020) including the regression of potential confounding effects characterized by white matter timeseries (5 CompCor noise components), CSF timeseries (5 CompCor noise components), motion parameters and their first order derivatives (12 factors; Friston et al., 1996), outlier scans (below 37 factors; Power et al., 2014), session effects and their first order derivatives (2 factors), and linear trends (2 factors) within each functional run, followed by bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD timeseries (Hallquist et al., 2013) between 0.008 Hz and 0.09 Hz. CompCor (Behzadi et al., 2007; Chai et al., 2012) noise components within white matter and CSF were estimated by computing the average BOLD signal as well as the largest principal components orthogonal to the BOLD average, motion parameters, and outlier scans within each subject's eroded segmentation masks. From the number of noise terms included in this denoising strategy, the effective degrees of freedom of the BOLD signal after denoising were estimated to range from 0 to 78.2 (average 0.8) across all subjects (Power et al., 2014).

First level analyses: MRI analyses

Three types of functional connectivity analysis were conducted: 1) seed-based connectivity 2) region-of-interest (ROI) analysis 3) and independent component analyses (ICA). Seed-based connectivity metrics are used to characterize the connectivity pattern of a pre-defined seed or region-of-interest (Nieto-Castanon, 2020). Seed-based connectivity maps represent the level of functional connectivity between a seed or ROI and every other voxel in the brain (Nieto-Castanon, 2020). In the present study seed-based connectivity maps were calculated using the following DMN seeds: medial prefrontal, left lateral parietal, right lateral parietal, and posterior cingulate cortex. The four connectivity maps calculated with these seeds represent the functional connectivity between each seed and every other voxel in the brain.

Region-of-interest (ROI) analyses characterize the connectivity between all pairs of selected ROIs among a predefined set of regions. In the present study, ROI-ROI connectivity matrices were calculated to represent the level of functional connectivity between four DMN ROIs (medial prefrontal, left lateral parietal, right lateral parietal, and posterior cingulate cortex). ROI-ROI analyses were conducted to characterize within-network DMN connectivity. Network measures such as independent component analyses (ICA) summarize the properties of the entire voxel-voxel connectome, or all of the functional connections between every pair of voxels in the brain (Nieto-Castanon, 2020). The maps created with ICA represent a network's level of connectivity at each voxel. In the present study, ICA was used to estimate the DMN.

Independent component analyses

Group-level independent component analyses (group-ICA; Calhoun et al., 2001) were performed to estimate 30 temporally coherent networks from the fMRI data combined across all subjects. The BOLD signal from every timepoint and voxel in the brain was concatenated across subjects and conditions along the temporal dimension. A singular value decomposition of the z-score normalized BOLD signal (subject-level SVD) with 64 components separately for each subject was used as a subject-specific dimensionality reduction step. The dimensionality of the concatenated data was further reduced using a singular value decomposition (group-level SVD) with 30 components, and a fast-ICA fixed-point algorithm (Hyvarinen, 1999) with hyperbolic tangent (G1) contrast function was used to identify spatially independent group-level networks from the resulting components. Last, GICA3 back-projection (Erhardt et al., 2011) was used to compute ICA maps associated with these same networks separately for each individual subject.

Seed-based and ROI-ROI analyses

Seed-based connectivity maps and ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrices) were estimated characterizing the patterns of functional connectivity with the following regions. The medial prefrontal cortex (MNI coordinates = +1, +55, −3), the left lateral parietal (MNI coordinates = −39, -+77, +33), and the right lateral parietal (MNI coordinates = +47, −67, +29) and the posterior cingulate cortex (MNI coordinates = +1, −61, +38) while controlling for all other ROIs. Functional connectivity strength was represented by Fisher-transformed semipartial correlation coefficients from a weighted general linear model (weighted-GLM; Nieto-Castanon, 2020), defined separately for each target area, modeling the association between all seeds simultaneously and each individual target area BOLD signal timeseries. In order to compensate for possible transient magnetization effects at the beginning of each run, individual scans were weighted by a step function convolved with an SPM canonical hemodynamic response function and rectified.

Second-level analyses

Group level analyses were performed using a General Linear Model (GLM; Nieto-Castanon, 2020). For each individual voxel a separate GLM was estimated, with first-level connectivity measures at this voxel as dependent variables (one independent sample per subject), and groups or other subject-level identifiers as independent variables. Voxel-level hypotheses were evaluated using multivariate parametric statistics with random-effects across subjects and sample covariance estimation across multiple measurements. Inferences were performed at the level of individual clusters (groups of contiguous voxels). Cluster-level inferences were based on parametric statistics from Gaussian Random Field theory (Nieto-Castanon, 2020; Worsley et al., 1996). Results were thresholded using a combination of a cluster-forming p < 0.001 voxel-level threshold, and a familywise corrected p-FDR <0.05 cluster-size threshold (Chumbley et al., 2010).

Independent component analyses

We performed one ANCOVA and one linear regression on the independent component from the ICA corresponding to the DMN. The ANCOVA was used to evaluate if the level of connectivity between the DMN and the rest of the brain differed between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups. The covariates included age, education, contrast sensitivity and reading acuity. Next, a linear regression was used to evaluate if there was a relationship between SRT and the level of connectivity between the DMN with the rest of the brain. In the linear regression age, education, contrast sensitivity and reading acuity were controlled for.

Seed-based analyses

We performed complementary and exploratory seed-based analyses to examine the connectivity of individual DMN regions with the rest of the brain. Four DMN regions were used to calculate seed-based connectivity maps: medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate cortex, the right lateral parietal cortex, and the left lateral parietal cortex. Four paired t-tests (one for each calculated seed-based connectivity map) were performed to investigate if the connectivity between each DMN region and the rest of the brain differed between normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss and groups.

Region-of-interest analyses

We performed complementary and exploratory ROI-ROI analyses to evaluate if within-network DMN connectivity differed between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups. The ROI-ROI analyses included the following four DMN regions as ROIs: medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate cortex, the right lateral parietal cortex, and the left lateral parietal cortex. One paired t-test was performed on the ROI-ROI connectivity matrix to evaluate if within-network DMN connectivity differed between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups. We then separately characterized within-network DMN connectivity in the normal hearing and hearing loss groups using the same four DMN ROIs. In each group, a multivariate voxel pattern analysis (MVPA) omnibus F test was used to characterize the level of connectivity at each ROI and all of the connections from each ROI. The results of the two MVPA omnibus F tests were visually compared.

Seed-based analyses

In exploratory analyses, four paired t-tests were performed to individually evaluate if the connectivity patterns of four DMN seeds (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, left lateral parietal cortex, and right lateral parietal cortex) differed between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups. Analyses of the seed-based connectivity maps found no statistically significant group connectivity differences between any of the four individual DMN seeds and the rest of the brain.

Region of interest analyses

In exploratory analyses, ROI-ROI connectivity matrices were calculated for the following four DMN ROIs: medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, left lateral parietal cortex, and right lateral parietal cortex. A paired t-test was then conducted on the ROI-ROI connectivity matrices to evaluate if within-network DMN connectivity differed between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups. Results revealed that there were no differences in overall within-network DMN connectivity between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups.

We also performed ROI-level P-FDR corrected MVPA Omnibus F tests to characterize the strength of each pairwise ROI-to-ROI connection within the DMN. This was done separately in the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups. When we independently characterized the overall within-network DMN connectivity in each group, clear qualitative differences between the groups emerged. In both groups, the right and left lateral parietal ROIs had the strongest connectivity with other DMN regions, while the posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex ROIs had the weakest connectivity. In the pure-tone hearing loss group, the ROIs with the greatest-to-least connectivity were the left lateral parietal > right lateral parietal > posterior cingulate cortex > medial prefrontal cortex. Discussion

This study had two objectives. The first objective was to evaluate if DMN connectivity differed between two groups of older adults with MCI, a group with normal hearing and a group with pure-tone hearing loss. Analyses controlled for age, education, contrast sensitivity and reading acuity. When compared to individuals with MCI and normal hearing, those with MCI and pure-tone hearing loss had decreased connectivity between the DMN and portions of the bilateral caudate and the right thalamus. We also observed non-significant but qualitatively different pattern of within-network DMN connectivity between the pure-tone hearing loss and normal hearing group, namely decreased overall connectivity of all four DMN regions, decreased pair-wise ROI-ROI connectivity, and the absence of connectivity between the medial prefrontal cortex and right lateral parietal cortex ROIs in the pure-tone hearing loss group. The second objective was to investigate the relationship between SRT and DMN connectivity in older adults with MCI. There was no relationship between SRT and DMN connectivity. These results are discussed in turn.

Within-network connectivity

Although not statistically significant, the observed trend of decreased within-network DMN connectivity as a function of pure-tone hearing loss in individuals with MCI is notable. While AD is characterized by a widespread loss of connectivity, individuals with at risk for AD often show regionally mixed patterns of effect, such as a pattern of increased frontal and decreased posterior DMN connectivity (Wang et al., 2018). This increased frontal connectivity in individuals at risk for AD or with MCI has often been interpreted as a compensatory mechanism due to AD-related neurodegeneration in the posterior DMN, which is a target of early AD pathology (Sperling et al., 2011). Thus, our finding of decreased connectivity between the DMN regions may suggest that adults at risk for AD with hearing loss show less compensatory increased connectivity of frontal regions of the DMN during early AD-related brain changes compared to their normal hearing peers, possibly due to negative effects of hearing loss on the brain (Gates, 2012; Jafari et al., 2021). Indeed, it is thought that cortical reorganization due to hearing loss subsequently hinders the brain's ability to adapt to aging and early AD related brain pathology (Gates, 2012). Given that altered DMN connectivity is an indicator of early AD pathology (Jack et al., 2018), our results suggest that pure-tone hearing loss may be associated with increased early AD-related brain pathology when compared to their MCI peers without HL. This would be consistent with recent findings that greater hearing loss is associated with increased amyloid burden (Golub et al., 2021). Moreover, this supports the hypothesis that altered brain connectivity is a potential mechanism underlying relationship between hearing loss and cognitive impairment/decline.

Between-network connectivity

We also observed that pure-tone hearing loss was associated with decreased connectivity between the DMN and portions of the bilateral caudate and right thalamus. The caudate nuclei are a set of bilateral subcortical grey matter structures in the basal ganglia important for motor, affective, and cognitive functioning (Schwab et al., 2014). The role of the caudate nucleus in higher-order cognitive processes is well established (for a review, see Middleton and Strick, 2000). The caudate nuclei are thought to be associated with directing attention, switching attention between processes, processing speed, and mental flexibility (Grahn et al., 2008). The thalamus is involved in several functions, including sensory processing, attention, decision making, and memory (Roy et al., 2022). The thalamus is thought to act as a relay center between the cerebral cortex and subcortical regions, particularly with sensory information. Interestingly, while the thalamus is not considered to be part of the DMN, research has shown that the thalamus is functionally connected to the DMN (Cunningham et al., 2017).

To the best of our knowledge, there is no report of an association between hearing loss and altered caudate connectivity. There are only a few reports of pure-tone hearing loss and altered thalamic connectivity, however, these alterations in thalamic connectivity have been associated with cognitive impairment (Xu et al., 2019a, 2019b). The caudate and thalamus are also known to be affected by AD pathology (Braak and Braak, 1990). Decreased connectivity between the thalamus and the DMN is present in individuals with MCI and has been associated with cognitive decline in individuals with MCI and AD (Wang et al., 2012). As decreased functional connectivity, particularly of the DMN, is a marker of disease progression in AD (Jack et al., 2018), our finding of decreased connectivity between the DMN and the caudate and thalamus suggests that in individuals with MCI, hearing loss may be associated with increased pathological burden of early AD on the brain.

When we further investigated our finding of decreased connectivity between the DMN and the caudate and thalamus with seed-based analyses to evaluate if the connectivity between any of the four DMN regions and the rest of the brain differed between the normal hearing and pure-tone hearing loss groups; no significant group differences were found. This suggests that the main findings from the ICA (i.e., decreased connectivity between the DMN and caudate and thalamus) may represent a distributed effect that can only be identified with network-level statistics. Indeed, there is evidence that seed-based analyses are preferred for findings with high anatomic consistency and that network-based approaches have better sensitivity for detecting differences in anatomically distinct networks (Badhwar et al., 2017).

Implications

We believe that our results imply that hearing loss increases the pathological burden of AD on the brain in individuals with MCI. Our rationale is as follows. It is well known that hearing loss is associated with the functional reorganization of the brain (Jafari et al., 2021; Griffiths et al., 2020). It is also well known that AD is characterized by a widespread loss of DMN connectivity (Badhwar et al., 2017; Jack et al., 2018). Moreover, decreased DMN connectivity has been associated with cognitive impairment in both groups and is thought to be a marker of disease progression in MCI and AD (Binnewijzend et al., 2012). Therefore, our finding of decreased DMN connectivity as a function of pure-tone hearing loss suggests that individuals with both MCI and hearing loss have greater early AD-related brain changes than those with MCI and normal hearing. We speculate that this could be due to the functional brain reorganization that is necessitated by hearing loss reducing neural capacity to compensate for early AD-related brain pathology in individuals with MCI. This view suggests an additive effect of hearing loss and AD pathology on functional connectivity changes in the brain in individuals with MCI.

The other possibility is that the altered connectivity between the DMN and the caudate and thalamus simply represents alterations in functional auditory networks. The caudate has increased functional connectivity with auditory cortex in individuals with tinnitus (Perez et al., 2020) and, of course, the thalamus plays a crucial role in ascending auditory input and the gating of auditory signals (Rikhye et al., 2018). Thus, another interpretation is that our findings may be driven only by the presence of HL in the MCI group and does not imply additional burden of AD pathology in individuals with MCI. Thus, the subcortical disruption in individuals with MCI reported in other studies may be in part driven by unexamined hearing loss in these groups. This is a reasonable interpretation due to the high prevalence of hearing loss in older adults and the known association between hearing loss and alterations in brain structure and function. This reasoning also suggests that the altered DMN connectivity consistently reported in older adults with or at risk for AD may be driven by unexamined hearing loss. However, as these connectivity alterations were observed with the DMN suggests that our findings are not simply driven by hearing loss. Our reasoning is based on the well-established relationship between DMN connectivity and early AD pathology and disease progression. Furthermore, as the DMN is not closely associated with sensory or speech processing it seems unlikely that the neuronal reorganization of this network could be fully attributed to hearing loss. Whether altered connectivity represents changes in functional connectivity due to the negative effect of hearing loss on the brain per se or whether hearing loss compounds brain pathology in individuals with MCI remains to be determined. Our future work will examine the relationship between hearing loss and DMN connectivity in individuals with normal cognition and subjective cognitive decline, an even earlier risk state for AD, to investigate these possibilities.

Strengths and limitations

There are several strengths of the present study. First, it makes a unique contribution by exploring the relationship between hearing loss and resting-state brain connectivity in individuals with MCI, which has been largely unexamined to date. We also used both pure-tone audiometry and SRTs in noise to measure hearing ability. Pure-tone hearing detection thresholds in quiet and supra-threshold SRTs in noise involve different levels of auditory processing and may reflect hearing processes with potentially different relationships with brain function. Therefore, we thoroughly investigated the relationship between hearing and brain connectivity by using different measures of hearing ability. Our study indicated that pure-tone hearing loss has a stronger relationship with brain connectivity than SRT. This suggests that measures of pure-tone hearing ability may have a larger or more reliable relationship with brain connectivity than measures of speech-reception threshold. Finally, the sample size (n = 94) is relatively large for a study using fMRI data.

Nevertheless, there are several limitations that must be noted. First, although we initially classified participants into one of six categories of increasing hearing loss severity based on performance on a pure-tone audiometry screening, the hearing loss group was collapsed due to small numbers in some categories. This resulted in a group of individuals with hearing loss that ranged from mild to moderate, restricting the detail with which we could explore this variable. Also due to small group sizes, we were unable to investigate the potential role of sex, which is known to have differential effects in both hearing loss and dementia prevalence (Delhez et al., 2020; Dubno et al., 2013; Reavis et al., 2023). This is an important avenue for future research.

Due to the way the hearing variables were operationalized in the COMPASS-ND study, different analysis types were used depending on the independent variable. When the independent variable was pure-tone hearing an ANCOVA was used to compare DMN connectivity between the normal hearing and hearing loss groups. When SRT was the independent variable linear regressions were used to examine the relationship between SRT and DMN connectivity in the whole sample. Although the use of different analysis types may limit the comparability of our findings, the way these variables were measured has informed decisions regarding data analysis. We also note that we used screening protocol for pure tone hearing that was restricted to 2000 Hz, which captures only a single mid-to-high range frequency (Alshuaib et al., 2015). This did not allow us to identify pure tone hearing loss across a range of frequencies. Given that different presbyacusis phenotypes are associated with different pathophysiologies and aetologies (Eckert et al., 2021), we are not able to distinguish between potentially different pathophysiological and/or functional mechanisms underlying the relationship between hearing and brain function in our participants. In addition, our normal hearing and hearing loss groups were unequal in terms of sample size. Finally, the participants were a predominately white and highly educated group of volunteers, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our results showed decreased connectivity within the DMN and between the DMN and the bilateral caudate and right thalamus between a group of older adults with normal hearing and a group of older adults with pure-tone hearing loss. Our results provide evidence that pure-tone hearing loss is associated with decreased functional connectivity in individuals with MCI compared to their peers without hearing loss. The functional disconnection of the DMN in individuals with MCI and hearing loss may reflect reduced functional compensation in the face of neurodegeneration and early AD pathology in individuals at risk for AD (Dicks et al., 2018). Based on this, the pathological burden and neurodegeneration may be greater in individuals with both MCI and pure-tone hearing loss compared to those with MCI alone. Therefore, loss of functional connectivity is a potential mechanism by which pure-tone hearing loss is associated with cognitive impairment, cognitive decline, and dementia risk.
