Exercise preconditioning reduces the electrostimulation-induced incidence of arrhythmias in exhausted rats by regulating the CaMKⅡ-RyR2 signaling pathway
Authors: Yibo Wang, Yang Wang, Peng Xu, Chenyan Wang, Heling Huang, Xiangnong Li, Huanhuan Huang, Xiaoli Zhang, Xuebin Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntense or exhaustive exercise is known to increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias. One important mechanism is the disruption of intracellular calcium homeostasis. Exercise preconditioning (EP) has been reported to exert cardioprotective effects. However, under exercise-induced stress, how EP reduces arrhythmia susceptibility through regulation of calcium-handling signaling pathways remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate whether EP reduces exercise-induced arrhythmias by modulating the CaMKII–RyR2–PLN signaling pathway.ResultsIn this study, a rat model of repeated exhaustive exercise (EE) was established. The effects of EE and EP on myocardial electrophysiological properties were compared. The results showed that EE significantly shortened exhaustion time. EE also increased the incidence of electrically induced ventricular arrhythmias. In addition, EE markedly altered the expression of proteins related to calcium homeostasis. Specifically, EE significantly increased CaMKII activation and enhanced RyR2 phosphorylation at the Ser2814 site. These changes were accompanied by reduced SERCA2a expression and elevated PP1 levels. These findings indicated impaired sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium reuptake and increased calcium leakage. EP significantly prolonged exhaustion time and preserved myocardial structural integrity. EP reduced the occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias. Moreover, EP suppressed CaMKII activity and prevented excessive RyR2 phosphorylation. SERCA2a expression was restored, and PP1 levels were partially normalized. The CaMKII inhibitor KN-93 acted synergistically with EP. This result further confirmed the central role of CaMKII overactivation in exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias.ConclusionEP effectively reduced susceptibility to exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias by regulating the CaMKII–RyR2–PLN signaling pathway and maintaining calcium homeostasis. These findings highlight CaMKII as a potential therapeutic target. Introduction

Exercise affects cardiovascular health [1]. Exercise preconditioning (EP) is a sustained or repeated interval of moderate intensity exercise in a short period of time, which causes relative ischemia and hypoxia in the myocardium, induces the heart to up-regulate the expression of protective effector substances, thereby improving myocardial tolerance and promoting the formation of protective effects in the myocardium [2]. EP has multiple roles in myocardial protection, for instance, EP can reduce the size of myocardial infarction, promote the recovery of myocardial diastolic systolic function, reduce the structure damage of cardiomyocytes, and improve cardiac conduction function [3, 4]. Exercise training improves cardiac function and electrophysiological function, and reduces susceptibility to arrhythmias in SD rats with heart failure caused by myocardial infarction [5]. But different levels of exercise have different effects. Serious cardiotoxic activities, such as strenuous physical activity and long-term endurance exercise will increase the potential risk of arrhythmia [6]. A meta-analysis reported that excessive endurance exercise was harmful for some athletes, compared to sedentary people, and middle-aged endurance athletes had a five-fold higher risk of atrial fibrillation [7]. Exhaustive exercise (EE) is a continuous strenuous exercise that exceeds the capacity of body. EE damages the morphological structure of myocardial tissue and leads to cardiac electrophysiological disorders, leading changes in myocardial enzymes and abnormal changes in myocardial mitochondrial energy metabolism [3].

The maintenance of the transmembrane potential and the cyclical process of myocardial contraction and relaxation are driven by changes in intracellular calcium concentration. Variations in calcium levels trigger electrical activity across the cell membrane. During excitation–contraction coupling (ECC), calcium ions act as the primary regulatory factor. Calcium promotes myocardial contraction mainly through binding to troponin. Sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) ions also play essential roles in maintaining membrane potential and regulating action potentials. Influx of Na⁺ and efflux of K⁺ form the basis of membrane depolarization and repolarization. Through their effects on membrane potential, these ions indirectly influence calcium flux and cellular excitability. Calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) also plays a critical role during ECC. Calcium binds to calmodulin and activates CaMKII. Activated CaMKII regulates calcium release and reuptake. This process helps maintain intracellular calcium homeostasis and supports effective myocardial contraction [8, 9]. After strenuous exercise, the content and activity of CaMKII increase, while RyR2 was hyperphosphorylated in cardiomyocytes. Excessive activation of RyR2 will increase calcium leakage, then reduce the concentration of Ca2+ in SR, and accumulate and trigger arrhythmic calcium waves further [10, 11]. Ser2814 site of RyR2 plays a relatively large role in Ca2+ associated arrhythmia [12]. Studies on patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) and mouse models of AF have suggested that the increased activity and content of CaMKⅡ can specifically phosphorylate S2814 site of RyR2, thereby the open probability of RyR2 increases, and the risk of arrhythmia increases finally [13, 14]. In heart failure (HF), the CaMKⅡ-RyR2 signaling pathway is usually excessive activated, and Ser2814 site of RyR2 in cardiomyocytes is hyperphosphorylated, which leads to the Ca2+ leakage of SR in cardiomyocytes to increase. Meanwhile, the concentration of Ca2+ in the cytoplasm of cardiomyocytes increases during diastole, and the increased Ca2+ activates sodium-calcium exchange (Na+-Ca2+ exchanger, NCX) [15, 16]. Thus, local calcium ion potential aggregates and leads to the arrhythmia [17].

Most of Ca2+ in the cytoplasm is pumped back into SR via the Ca2+-ATPase 2a (SERCA2a) of SR. The activity of SERCA2a is reversibly controlled by SR-related protein phosphorlamban (PLN) [18]. And the phosphorylated PLN will separate from SERCA2a that increases the activity of SERCA2a and increases the rate of Ca2+ pumping back to SR in cytoplasm. And the phosphorylation at Thr 17 of PLN is regulated by CaMKⅡ. In contrast to the effect of Thr17 on PLN, protein phosphatase1 (PP1) is the primary phosphatase in SR, and PLN can be specifically dephosphorized by it, thereby reducing the activity of SERCA2a [19]. The reduced activity of SERCA2a leads to the difficulty in recovering calcium that leaks into the cytoplasm, which results in the disturbed balance of Ca2+ in myocardial cells, and the incidence of arrhythmias increases [20].

At the same time, increased levels of protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) further inhibited calcium reuptake mediated by SERCA2a. Normal calcium homeostasis in cardiomyocytes was disrupted. As a result, the incidence of ventricular calcium waves increased markedly [21]. Although the CaMKII–RyR2 signaling pathway has been widely investigated in the mechanism of arrhythmogenesis, evidence remains limited regarding EP as a non-pharmacological intervention. It is still unclear whether EP can attenuate exhaustive exercise–induced arrhythmias through modulation of this pathway. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether EP exerts electrophysiological protective effects against arrhythmias induced by exhaustive exercise. In addition, the study sought to clarify whether these protective effects mechanistically overlap with CaMKII inhibition. In this context, KN-93 was used solely as a mechanistic validation tool rather than as a therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

1. Experimental Animals and Model Establishment

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks, 200 ± 20 g) were provided by Beijing Zhongkeda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License No.: SCXK(Beijing)−2016-0002). They were housed at 18–22 °C with 40–55% humidity, and had free access to standard rodent feed and water. Experiments were conducted following ethical guidelines (Approval No.: 2021004).

One hundred and twenty healthy male Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly divided into 8 groups (n = 15) for each group: control group (CON), exhaustive exercise group (EE), EP group, exhaustive exercise after exercise preconditioning group (EP + EE), control group injected with KN-93 (Ctrl + KRI), exhaustive exercise group injected with KN-93 (EE + KRI), EP group injected with KN-93 (EP + KRI), exhaustive exercise after EP group injected with KN-93 (EP + EE+KRI).

The CaMKII inhibitor KN-93 was introduced primarily to verify the involvement of CaMKII signaling in the cardioprotective effects of EP. Its role was defined as a mechanistic probe rather than an independent pharmacological intervention. No systematic evaluation of its specific or nonspecific effects was conducted. KN-93 is a commonly used CaMKII inhibitory tool in cardiovascular and cellular research. It has been applied to suppress CaMKII-dependent phosphorylation and to influence calcium handling in cardiomyocytes [22].

Based on previous studies by Domenech and Lennon [23, 24], animal models of EP and exhaustive exercise (EE) were established. Treadmill exercise intensity was adjusted according to the exercise tolerance of rats. Under zero-grade conditions, treadmill running at 25–32 m/min is generally considered moderate-intensity or submaximal workload. In contrast, running at 30–35 m/min until exhaustion represents high-intensity or near-maximal exercise load. Accordingly, the EP protocol in this study was designed to simulate moderate-intensity endurance stimulation. The EE protocol was used to model extreme exercise stress exceeding the physiological tolerance threshold. During implementation, all groups except CON, C + K, EE, and EE + K underwent one week of adaptive treadmill training. The speed was set at 10–15 m/min for 15 min per day without incline.

During implementation, all groups except CON, C + KRI, EE, and EE + KRI underwent one week of adaptive treadmill training. The speed was set at 10–15 m/min for 15 min per day without incline. The EP group then underwent three weeks of training: 15 min of exercise at 25–32 m/min (adjusted to individual constitution), followed by 5 min of rest, repeated for three sets. Training was conducted 6 days/week. Three weeks later, the EE, EP + EE, EE + KRI, and EP + EE+KRI groups performed exhaustive exercise at 30–35 m/min until exhaustion. KN-93 (3.327 × 10⁻³ mmol/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 30 min before exhaustion to the EE + KRI and EP + EE+KRI groups. For the EP + KRI and Ctrl + KRI groups, KN-93 was administered, and procedures were conducted 2 h post-injection. Exhaustion was defined as the inability to maintain running speed, significant postural changes, and weakened righting reflex. Exhaustion time was recorded, and sampling was completed within 60 min post-exhaustion.

2. Reagents and Instruments

Reagents: KN-93 (GLPBio, USA), Phospho-RYR2-S2814 Rabbit pAb and Phospholamban (Wuhan ibotec co., Ltd.), CaMKⅡα/δ (phospho-T286), CaMKⅡα/δ (M281), RyR2, PP1α (G315) (Nanjing Ba Aode Co., Ltd.), Actin (Shanghai Shenger Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), SERCA2a and phospho-PLN (Thr17) (Beijing Boaosen Co., Ltd.).

Instruments: Animal treadmill (Taimeng, China), PowerLab system (AD Instruments, Australia), Automatic embedding machine and microtome (LEICA, Germany), PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA), Deionized water meter (PALL, Purelab Plus, USA), Ice Machine (AF10 SCOTSMAN, USA), Electronic scales (Sartorius, Germany).

3. Electrocardiography

Adaptive electrocardiography training was performed in all experimental rats. The rats were placed in supine position, and their limbs were fixed on the thermostatic mat. The electrode needles were used to puncture the subcutaneous of right upper limb (positive electrode), the subcutaneous of right lower extremity (grounding electrode), and the subcutaneous of left lower extremity (negative electrode). PowerLab electrophysiological recording system was used to collect the electrocardiogram of rats, and record the related parameters such as HR, P-R interval, QRS interval, P wave and R wave.

4. Arrhythmias induced by transesophageal electrical stimulation

The rats were intubated through the oral tube to maintain breathing by connecting to an animal ventilator after the rats were anesthetized (3% pentobarbital sodium, intraperitoneal injection, 40 mg/kg). Then, the needle electrode was connected and the PowerLab electrophysiological recording system was used to record the Lead Ⅱ electrocardiogram of the rats. ECG was recorded for 5 min, and arrhythmia was determined according to literature [25]. The arrhythmias induced by exercise was observed. If there was no arrhythmia, we prepared to induce arrhythmia by transesophageal electrical. After disinfection, the 4 F electrode catheter was inserted into the esophagus and found a position that close to the atrium, and the depth of the insertion was about 7 cm from the teeth. Next, we observed the changes of esophageal electrocardiogram. When high positive A wave and low flat negative V wave appeared (P wave was greater than or equal to R wave, and S wave was smaller), it indicated that the electrode sheet at the front of the electrode catheter was close to the atrium. Afterwards, low-frequency direct current was applied to stimulate and pace atrium with a current intensity of 3–4 Ma and a frequency of 8 Hz. We used the electrophysiological stimulator of AD company, and fine-tuned the position of the electrode catheter. When the heart rate of the rats was paced by the current frequency, the position was accurate, and the power supply was suspended, the electrode catheter was fixed. Finally, the current frequency was adjusted to 50HZ for 30 s to induce arrhythmia. Electrical stimulation termination criteria: Arrhythmias were considered successfully induced if they lasted more than 2 s. Failure to induce atrial fibrillation after three attempts of burst pacing indicated the failure of induction.

5. Collection of myocardial specimens

The rats’ hearts were removed and cleaned with PBS after electrical stimulated. The tissue near the apex of the left ventricle was cut and used as a light microscopic specimen, and the tissue was fixed in 10% neutral formalin fixator and stored in a refrigerator at −4℃ for HE. The rest of the myocardial specimens were divided into EP tubes for Western blot detection, and stored in −80 ℃ refrigerator after labeled.

6. Observation of the myocardial structure under light microscope

The tissue was removed from the − 4℃ refrigerator, fixed and embedded in paraffin. Then dehydrated, embedded and sliced (4 μm), and the slices were dewaxed in an oven at 65℃ for 2 h. After cooling, the slices were dewaxed in xylene, soaked in gradient alcohol for dehydration, and rinsed with pure water for 3 times. Next step was staining, gradient alcohol for dehydration and tissue-transparency. Finally, we observed the tissue structure under a microscope.

7. Detection of protein contents of CaMKⅡ, p-CaMKⅡ, RyR2, p-RyR2-(S2814), PLN, p-PLN, PP1 and SERCA2a by Western blot

Myocardial tissues were homogenized and lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (1:100, Roche, Cat. No. 04693132001). All procedures were performed at 4 °C. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatants were collected as total protein samples. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method (BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 23225).

Equal amounts of total protein (20 µg) were mixed with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer. Samples were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and then subjected to electrophoretic separation. Proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels consisting of 5% stacking gel and 10% resolving gel at a constant voltage of 80 V. Proteins were subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system. Transfer conditions varied according to protein size: RyR2 and p-RyR2 (180 mA, 90 V, 180 min); PLN and p-PLN (80 mA, 30 V, 40 min); all other proteins (130 mA, 60 V, 60 min).

After transfer, PVDF membranes were blocked at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The antibodies and working dilutions were as follows: CaMKII (1:1000), p-CaMKII (1:1000), RyR2 (1:1000), p-RyR2 (Ser2814, 1:1000), PLN (1:1000), p-PLN (Thr17, 1:1000), PP1 (1:1000), SERCA2a (1:1000), and β-actin (1:5000) as the internal control.

On the following day, membranes were washed three times with TBST (10 min each). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000) were applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After final washing with 1× TBST, protein bands were visualized. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

8. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Before group comparisons, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Levene test. For data showing normal distribution and homogeneous variance, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was applied. When overall differences were significant, post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted. For data not meeting normality or homogeneity assumptions, appropriate non-parametric tests were used. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using the χ² test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To evaluate the effects of EP on exercise tolerance, exhaustion time was compared among different groups of rats. The results showed that exhaustion time was significantly prolonged in the EP + EE and EP + EE+KRI groups compared with the EE group (P < 0.05). In addition, exhaustion time in the EP + EE and EP + EE+KRI groups was also significantly longer than that in the EE + KRI group (P < 0.05). These findings indicated that EP significantly improved exercise tolerance in rats. (Table 1)

2. Effects of EP on myocardial structure in exhausted rats

To examine the effects of EP on myocardial structure after exhaustive exercise, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed for morphological analysis of myocardial tissues. In the Ctrl group and EP-related groups, myocardial fibers were regularly arranged and structural integrity was preserved. In the EE group, marked myocardial cell swelling, fiber disruption, necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration were observed. Compared with the EE group, myocardial structural damage was clearly attenuated in the EP + EE and EE + KRI groups. The most pronounced improvement was observed in the EP + EE+KRI group, in which only mild structural abnormalities remained. These findings suggested that EP and CaMKII inhibition alleviated myocardial injury associated with exhaustive exercise. (Fig. 1)

5. The comparison of protein contents of CaMKⅡ, p-CaMKⅡ, RyR2, p-RyR2-(S2814), PLN, p-PLN, PP1 and SERCA2a

To further clarify the molecular mechanisms by which EP reduced susceptibility to arrhythmias after exhaustive exercise, phosphorylation levels of proteins involved in the CaMKII–RyR2 signaling pathway (expressed as the ratio of phosphorylated protein to total protein) and the expression of calcium homeostasis–related proteins were examined in myocardial tissue (Fig. 4).

The results showed that exhaustive exercise markedly activated the CaMKII–RyR2 signaling pathway. Compared with the control group, the EE group exhibited significantly increased p-CaMKII/CaMKII and p-RyR2 (Ser2814)/RyR2 ratios (P < 0.01). At the same time, SERCA2a expression was significantly reduced and PP1 levels were significantly elevated (P < 0.01). These changes indicated impaired sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ reuptake and disruption of calcium homeostasis.

EP significantly attenuated these abnormal alterations. Compared with the EE group, the EP + EE group showed markedly reduced p-CaMKII/CaMKII and p-RyR2/RyR2 ratios (P < 0.01). In parallel, SERCA2a expression was significantly increased (P < 0.05), while PP1 levels were significantly decreased (P < 0.01). These findings suggested that EP alleviated exhaustive exercise–induced CaMKII overactivation and its downstream effects at the molecular level.

Under CaMKII inhibition, the EP + EE+KRI group demonstrated a further reduction in the phosphorylation ratios of CaMKII and RyR2. The magnitude of these changes was significantly greater than that observed in the EE group alone (P < 0.01). In addition, SERCA2a expression showed a more pronounced recovery, indicating a synergistic effect between CaMKII inhibition and EP in regulating calcium handling pathways.

Furthermore, exhaustive exercise induced significant changes in both PLN expression and its phosphorylation level (p-PLN/PLN) (P < 0.05). These alterations were partially reversed by EP and KN-93 treatment. Collectively, these results suggested that EP may maintain intracellular calcium homeostasis and reduce arrhythmia susceptibility through coordinated multi-target regulation of the CaMKII–RyR2–PLN–SERCA signaling pathway.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that exercise preconditioning (EP) significantly reduces electrostimulation-induced arrhythmias in exhausted rats by modulating the CaMKⅡ-RyR2 signaling pathway. EP prolonged exhaustion time, preserved myocardial structural integrity, and reduced arrhythmia incidence, aligning with its role in enhancing endogenous cardioprotection [1, 2]. Mechanistically, EP suppressed CaMKⅡ activity and RyR2 phosphorylation at Ser2814, thereby attenuating sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca²⁺ leakage and stabilizing intracellular Ca²⁺ homeostasis. These effects were synergistically enhanced by KN-93, a CaMKⅡ inhibitor, confirming the centrality of CaMKⅡ hyperactivity in exercise-induced arrhythmogenesis [26, 27].

CaMKⅡδ, the predominant cardiac isoform, features an N-terminal catalytic domain, a regulatory segment with autoinhibitory and calmodulin-binding regions, and a C-terminal association domain facilitating oligomerization [28]. Under physiological conditions, Ca²⁺/calmodulin binding relieves autoinhibition, enabling CaMKⅡ to phosphorylate targets like RyR2 and phospholamban (PLN). However, pathological hyperactivation of CaMKⅡ—observed here in exhaustive exercise (EE)—promotes RyR2-S2814 hyperphosphorylation, exacerbating diastolic SR Ca²⁺ leakage [12, 29]. Concurrently, reduced SERCA2a expression and elevated PP1 levels impair Ca²⁺ reuptake, creating a pro-arrhythmic milieu characterized by delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs) [20, 30]. Our data corroborate this: EE rats exhibited elevated p-CaMKⅡ and p-RyR2 levels alongside diminished SERCA2a, while EP reversed these trends (Fig. 4).

Previous studies have shown that under conditions of markedly increased cardiac load, such as high-intensity exercise, hypoxia, or metabolic stress, intracellular calcium homeostasis is often influenced by multiple factors. These factors include altered ion exchange activity and dysfunction of calcium release channels [31, 32]. Under such stress conditions, sustained activation of CaMKII is considered a key molecular hub linking metabolic stress to RyR2 dysfunction. This process amplifies diastolic calcium release and promotes the development of triggered arrhythmias [33–35]. Consistent with these findings, abnormal activation of the CaMKII–RyR2–PLN signaling pathway observed in the present study provides a plausible mechanistic framework to explain the increased susceptibility to arrhythmias under exhaustive exercise conditions. The arrhythmia-reducing effect of KN-93 observed in this study is in line with previous reports showing that CaMKII inhibition attenuates arrhythmia susceptibility under various forms of cardiac stress [36].

In heart failure (HF), CaMKII overexpression exacerbates SERCA/PLN imbalance, diminishing Ca²⁺ reuptake capacity despite enhanced PLN-Thr17 phosphorylation [37]. Similarly, EE-induced SERCA2a downregulation and PP1 upregulation render compensatory PLN phosphorylation insufficient, aggravating Ca²⁺ mishandling. EP’s ability to restore SERCA2a highlights its therapeutic potential in preserving Ca²⁺ homeostasis under stress (Fig. 4). Furthermore, combining EP with KN-93 synergistically reduced PP1 and PLN levels, suggesting dual targeting of CaMKⅡ and SERCA pathways may optimize antiarrhythmic outcomes.

Previous studies have also demonstrated that during intense exercise and other cardiac stress states, elevated levels of angiotensin II and aldosterone increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through activation of NADPH oxidase [38, 39]. ROS-mediated oxidative modification leads to sustained activation of CaMKII. This mechanism has been well documented. In this context, ROS-driven CaMKII activation is thought to enhance RyR2 phosphorylation and interfere with sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium reuptake. These effects further aggravate myocardial calcium imbalance and increase arrhythmia susceptibility. This regulatory axis provides an important framework for understanding persistent CaMKII activation and its downstream effects during exercise-related stress. In addition, previous studies using calcium imaging and electrophysiological techniques have shown that under high-intensity exercise or hypoxic stress, cardiomyocytes exhibit reduced calcium transient amplitude. At the same time, the frequency of diastolic calcium sparks and calcium waves increases markedly. Calcium reuptake is also delayed [40]. Together, these changes enhance calcium-induced calcium release and create favorable conditions for delayed afterdepolarizations and triggered arrhythmias. Taken together, the abnormal activation of the CaMKII–RyR2–PLN signaling pathway observed in this study provides a biologically reasonable explanation for the development of exercise-induced arrhythmias.

It should be noted that the mechanistic conclusions of this study were not based solely on Western blot expression data. In related preliminary studies, we used the IonOptix system for simultaneous measurement of cell shortening and calcium transients. These experiments showed that repeated exhaustive exercise significantly reduced the maximal contraction amplitude of rat cardiomyocytes. This effect was accompanied by an increased frequency of calcium sparks and a reduction in sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ content [11]. In addition, in an acute hypoxic exercise model, patch-clamp recordings and confocal calcium imaging revealed prolongation of action potential duration, increased L-type calcium channel current density, and a marked rise in diastolic calcium sparks and calcium wave events. These changes enhanced calcium-induced calcium release and delayed calcium reuptake. As a result, the risk of triggered arrhythmias increased [38]. Together, these findings indicate that calcium homeostasis imbalance plays a critical role in myocardial injury associated with high-intensity or exhaustive exercise. They also provide important theoretical support for interpreting exercise stress–induced calcium dysregulation from the perspective of the CaMKII–RyR2–PLN signaling pathway.

However, this study has several limitations. The present work primarily focused on the expression regulation of key proteins in the CaMKII–RyR2 pathway and their association with arrhythmia phenotypes. Direct functional measurements, such as calcium transients, calcium sparks, or sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ content, were not systematically presented in the main text. Therefore, although the current results provide strong molecular and phenotypic support for the proposed mechanism, the causal link between EP and improved Ca²⁺ dynamics requires further confirmation through direct functional experiments. Future studies should build upon the molecular evidence presented here. Integration of cardiomyocyte calcium imaging, electrophysiological recordings, and Ca²⁺ kinetic analyses will be necessary to fully elucidate the mechanisms by which EP regulates calcium homeostasis through the CaMKII–RyR2 pathway and reduces the risk of exhaustive exercise–induced arrhythmias.

Conclusion

EP attenuates EE-induced arrhythmias by suppressing CaMKⅡ-RyR2 signaling, reducing diastolic Ca²⁺ leakage, and restoring SERCA2a function. These findings underscore EP’s clinical relevance for populations engaged in high-intensity activities, offering a non-pharmacological strategy to mitigate exercise-associated cardiac risks [41].
