Association and mediating pathways between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms: findings from high- and middle-income countries
Authors: Yingchao Zeng, Simu Huang, Peiyi Lu, Liying Luo, Haidong Lu, Xuexin Yu, Chenjin Ma, Yi Huang, Lindsay C. Kobayashi, Luyang Guo, Chihua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEducation is an important determinant of health throughout the life course. However, less is understood about whether and how intergenerational educational mobility—comparing an individual’s own and their parents’ education—is associated with depressive symptoms among aging populations across diverse settings. This study examines this association and its mediating pathways.MethodsUsing data from six harmonized longitudinal aging studies: the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and its partner studies in England, European countries, South Korea, Mexico, and China, we conducted cross-sectional (n = 94,655) and longitudinal analyses (n = 56,817) to first establish the association across diverse populations, and then strengthen causal inference by examining whether educational mobility relates to depressive symptoms over time. Educational mobility was categorized as stably low, downwardly mobile, stably middle, upwardly mobile, and stably high group based on country- and cohort-specific percentiles. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Euro-D or CES-D. We also examined the moderating role of gender in these associations. Mediating pathways through household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity were also explored.ResultsLongitudinal analyses revealed consistent gradient associations across countries. Compared to the stably low group, downwardly mobile (OR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.76–0.93), stably middle (OR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.72–0.84), upwardly mobile (OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.67–0.83), and stably high (OR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.53–0.80) showed lower odds of depressive symptoms. Gender differences emerged in European countries and South Korea, where women had lower odds than men in downwardly mobile and stably high groups. Cross-sectional results showed similar but stronger patterns. Household wealth was the most consistent mediator, explaining 24–34% of the association in the USA and comparable proportions elsewhere except for South Korea. Social activeness and multimorbidity also showed important but varying mediating proportions across countries.ConclusionsAdvantaged educational mobility is associated with reduced depressive symptoms in mid- and later-life. Household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity contribute as important mediators. Future policies are needed to promote intergenerational educational equity and implement integrated strategies to reduce mental health disparities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04808-w.

Background

Depression affects one-third of older adults globally and carries heavy health and economic costs [1], including higher morbidity and mortality, reduced quality of life, and elevated healthcare spending [2–4]. It is therefore urgent to identify key risk factors for depressive symptoms and potential interventions [3]. Socioeconomic status—particularly education—has been recognized as a fundamental cause of health [5]. While socioeconomic position is multidimensional, education is a fundamental and early-life resource that shapes later material, psychosocial, and health advantages; in population health research, it is also measured more consistently across countries than income or occupation, enabling valid cross-national, intergenerational comparisons. Education’s policy relevance further supports its use in life-course analyses [6–8]. Higher educational attainment has been consistently related to better mental health outcomes, in part because education shapes employment and income, health behaviors, and psychosocial resources across the life course [5, 9–12]. These interconnected pathways explain how early-life educational advantage or disadvantage can have lasting implications on mental health.

Recent research has shifted from individual-level education to intergenerational educational mobility—the educational attainment of individuals compared to their parents [13–16]. This approach captures the transmission of educational advantage or disadvantage across generations and offers a fuller view of life-course socioeconomic position [15, 16]. Taking an intergenerational approach captures not only individual-level access to resources and opportunities but also the enduring influence of early-life contexts. For example, having stably high or upwardly mobile educational mobility across generations may promote psychosocial capital by enhancing access to social and economic resources, whereas consistently low or downwardly mobile educational mobility may increase vulnerability by limiting opportunity and disrupting expected life-course advantages [16–18]. This intergenerational, life-course perspective underscores how social factors like education shape health mobility over time [13, 14, 16]. Recent empirical studies have begun to map these pathways, though results remain inconsistent. Some research suggests that upward educational mobility acts as a critical resource substitution, effectively offsetting early-life disadvantages [17, 19–21]. Conversely, other studies indicate that the health returns to mobility may be attenuated by psychosocial stressors, such as relative deprivation, status inconsistency, or the challenge of adapting to new social hierarchies [22–24].

Important knowledge gaps remain in what we know about education and mental health. First, existing research fails to provide a comprehensive understanding of how intergenerational mobility shapes depression risk across diverse societal contexts. Most evidence is derived from high-income countries, failing to account for low- and middle-income countries where distinct structural factors and socioeconomic returns to education may fundamentally modify the expected health benefits of mobility [25, 26]. Second, few studies bring together cross-sectional and longitudinal perspectives or account for the secular increase in educational attainment across birth cohorts [18]. Third, the potential mediating pathways linking intergenerational educational mobility to mental health remain underexplored through mediation analyses [27]. Addressing these gaps can sharpen theory on the impact of education on health and guide targeted interventions to promote psychological well-being in aging populations.

Therefore, we used data from six longitudinal aging studies to examine the relationship between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms. We employed a cross-national comparative framework alongside a relative measure of educational mobility specifically adjusted for secular trends, while integrating cross-sectional and longitudinal perspectives on depressive symptoms. This approach distinguishes the effects of social mobility from widespread educational expansion, allowing for a more precise assessment of relative deprivation and status inconsistency. Furthermore, we conducted mediation analyses to explore whether household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity can explain these associations. Each represents a widely examined mediating pathway: household wealth corresponds to socioeconomic inequality, social activeness to social disconnection and cultural mismatch, and multimorbidity to biological embedding of disadvantage [13, 16, 28–30]. By integrating an intergenerational life-course approach with a cross-national comparative framework, this study provides new insights into how patterns of educational advantages and disadvantages across generations can shape mental health across diverse settings.

Methods

Study design and data

We used data from six large nationally representative and harmonized aging studies of both high- and middle-income countries or regions: the United States Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [31], the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) [32], the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) [33], the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) [34], the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) [35], the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) (Additional file 1: Table S1) [36]. Both original datasets and harmonized versions provided by the Gateway to Global Aging Data team were used [37]. These studies were chosen because they share harmonized sampling strategies and data collection protocols, collect measures of depressive symptoms over time and information on educational attainment for both participants and their parents, and include rich sociodemographic and health information across the life-course—features that enable cross-national comparison, confounder adjustment, and exploration of mediating pathways. We chose 2010–2020 as our study follow-up period to maximize comparability and ensure sufficient follow-up. While the years of data collection are not identical across studies, we selected waves that fall within this roughly similar time frame (Fig. 1A). Analyses were conducted at the cohort level, treating ELSA (England) and SHARE (continental Europe) as independent cohorts. SHARE was analyzed as a single harmonized multi-country cohort due to common instruments, sampling, and fieldwork, and to ensure sufficient sample sizes for the mobility categories.Fig. 1Study design and directed acyclic graph of the relationship between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms. Participants from each study were included if they were aged 50 or older at baseline, had information on both own and parental educational attainment levels, and had depressive symptoms assessed at baseline (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). For the cross-sectional analysis, we examined the association between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms at baseline using 94,655 participants: HRS (n = 17,172), ELSA (n = 7680), SHARE (n = 37,615), KLoSA (n = 7512), MHAS (n = 12,441), and CHARLS (n = 12,235). For the longitudinal analysis, we further restricted the sample to participants without depressive symptoms at baseline and with at least two follow-up waves of depressive symptom assessments, yielding 56,817 participants: HRS (n = 10,481), ELSA (n = 5329), SHARE (n = 22,392), KLoSA (n = 4336), MHAS (n = 7431), and CHARLS (n = 6848). The design adopted a life-course framework, aligning the temporal sequence of the exposure, mediators, and outcome, as well as the hypothesized causal pathways and confounding structures (Fig. 1B). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines in conducting and reporting this study (Additional file 1: Table S2) [38].

Measures

Exposure: intergenerational educational mobility

We prioritized education as the primary measure of socioeconomic position because it facilitates cross-national harmonization and serves as a well-defined target for policy interventions, whereas data on parental occupation and income were largely unavailable or inconsistently measured across datasets [6, 7]. Participants reported their own and their parents’ highest educational attainment using country-specific categories (Additional file 1: Table S3). For parental educational attainment, we used the highest education attained by either parent, reflecting the maximum educational resource available in the household [7, 18]. Because access, quality, and economic returns to schooling differ across countries and birth cohorts, we constructed a relative measure that situates each participant within their national educational and birth cohort context [39]. For robustness, we also used an absolute measure based on degree levels in sensitivity analyses (Additional file 1: Text and Table S4). This has a highly skewed distribution in some countries. For example, in China, only 1.0% and 2.6% were in the high and middle categories, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S5).

To construct the relative measure of intergenerational educational mobility, we first grouped participants into 10-year birth cohorts (≤ 1929, 1930–1939, 1940–1949, 1950–1959, ≥ 1960) to account for secular trends, combining the earliest and latest cohorts where sample size was small. This stratification was essential given that our sample spans 1910–1960, a period characterized by substantial shifts in educational systems and economic development trajectories within each country. By creating these 10-year windows, we assumed the distribution of educational attainment was relatively stable among peers in the same cohort. Because parental birth information was unavailable, we used participants’ birth cohort as a proxy for parents. Second, we determined relative educational positions separately within each country for each 10-year birth cohort. This ranking procedure normalized the exposure, effectively removing secular cohort effects by positioning each participant and their parents relative to their generational peers. Specifically, we calculated percentile rankings to assign participants and parents to percentile-based groups, low (bottom 25%), middle (25–75%), and high (top 25%), based on the educational distribution specific to their country and birth cohort (Additional file 1: Table S6) [40]. When educational attainment clustered at certain levels (e.g., primary school in China), we used cutoffs that approximated quartiles while preserving meaningful variation. Third, parental and participants’ education groups were cross-classified to define five intergenerational educational mobility: stably low (low–low), downwardly mobile (high–middle, high–low, middle–low), stably middle (middle–middle), upwardly mobile (low–middle, low–high, middle–high), and stably high (high–high) (Fig. 2) [16]. These mobilities are ordered to reflect cumulative advantage, with stably low mobility as the least-advantaged reference [41]. Health outcome: depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies of Depression scale (CES-D) in HRS, ELSA, KLoSA, MHAS, and CHARLS, or the Euro-Depression scale (Euro-D) in SHARE [42–45]. Items on both scales were coded dichotomously, with “yes” denoting 1 and “no” denoting 0, indicating the presence or absence of specific feelings or behaviors (e.g., loneliness, restless sleep). The total scores ranged from 0 to 8 in HRS and ELSA, 0 to 30 in KLoSA and CHARLS, 0 to 9 in MHAS, and 0 to 12 in SHARE. Consistent with prior studies, we defined depressive symptoms based on study-specific cutoffs: scores ≥ 3 in HRS and ELSA, ≥ 10 in KLoSA and CHARLS, ≥ 5 in MHAS, and ≥ 4 in SHARE [42, 44, 46].

Mediators: household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity

We examined three mediators as proxies for key theoretical constructs: household wealth (as an indicator of socioeconomic inequality) [16, 28], social activeness (as an indicator of social disconnection and cultural mismatch) [13, 29], and multimorbidity (as an indicator of biological embedding of disadvantage) [30]. All mediators were coded as binary variables to improve interpretability and cross-study comparability. Household wealth was calculated as the total value of household assets divided by the square root of the number of household members and was categorized into “low” (bottom 50%) and “high” (top 50%) household wealth groups. Social activeness was derived from self-reported participation in at least one social activity and coded as “yes” or “no”. For example, in the USA, the measurement of social activeness was based on one of the questions: “whether the respondent participates monthly in going to a sport, social, or other club?”. Multimorbidity was measured based on self-reported physician diagnoses of several chronic conditions available across studies: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric disorders, and arthritis. It was coded as “yes” if the participant had two or more conditions. Despite extensive efforts to harmonize these mediators across studies, some measurement differences remained; further details are provided in Additional file 1: Table S7.

Covariates

We adjusted for covariates that could potentially confound the association between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms, including common demographic characteristics, country-specific contextual variables, and childhood conditions. Common demographic characteristics included baseline age (in years) and gender (men, women). Gender was assessed as self-identified gender via questionnaires. Country-specific variables varied across settings and included race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and other) in the USA; race (White, non-White) in England; urban/rural residence in the USA, European countries, South Korea, Mexico, and China; nativity status (native-born, foreign-born) in European countries; and Hukou status (agricultural, non-agricultural) in China. Childhood conditions were added where available, though the availability of specific variables differed by study. These included height (in meters, two decimal places) as a proxy for nutritional status during childhood [39, 47], and four binary indicators (yes, no) of childhood adversity and well-being: experience of stressful events, good childhood health, good childhood financial status, and parental warmth (Additional file 1: Table S7).

Information on missing data for mediators and covariates by country is presented in Additional file 1: Table S8. Assuming data were missing at random, we conducted multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), applying predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression approaches for categorical variables [48, 49]. Interaction terms were prespecified to ensure unbiased estimates of associations between exposure and outcome within levels of the effect modifier.

Statistical analysis

We described the sample using medians (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. To examine the association between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms, we used generalized linear models (GLM) with a “logit” link for cross-sectional analyses and generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a “logit” link for longitudinal analyses. Given that gender is a key focus, we included an interaction term between educational mobility and gender to examine whether gender modifies the association with depressive symptoms. We also conducted gender subgroup analyses to examine potential gender-specific patterns.

GEE was selected as the longitudinal analytical approach because it accounts for within-subject correlations in repeated measures data, provides population-averaged estimates, and accommodates unbalanced follow-up across waves. The stably low mobility served as the reference group. Results were reported as the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). To synthesize findings across studies, we conducted meta-analyses using the “meta” package, presenting both common-effect (fixed-effect) and random-effects models [50]. GEE-based mediation analyses were performed to investigate potential mediating pathways separately, including household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity. The R “mediation” package was used for the analysis [51]. We generated the total effect (TE), natural direct effect (NDE), natural indirect effect (NIE), and mediation proportion “NIE/(NDE + NIE) × 100”. To evaluate the robustness of the probit-regression-based mediation analysis, we implemented bootstrap resampling [52]. This involved randomly selecting distinct proportions (50%, n = 100 times) from the cumulative sample. All models adjusted for covariates.

We conducted seven sensitivity analyses. First, we applied the inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) to account for potential attrition bias due to differential loss to follow-up, which re-weights the analytical sample inversely to the probability of retention to restore baseline representativeness [53]. Second, we repeated the main analyses using survey sampling weights to adjust for probabilities of selection and nonresponse. Third, we excluded childhood conditions to address potential role ambiguity (confounder vs mediator), given that they may overlap in terms of temporality. Fourth, we re-ran our analyses using absolute educational mobility. Fifth, we repeated the analysis using the “stably high” mobility as the reference group to benchmark disparities against the most advantaged group. Sixth, to examine the robustness of household wealth measurement, we repeated the analysis, treating it as a continuous standardized variable rather than a binary measure. Seventh, given methodological debates around mobility assessment models [54, 55], a causal inference framework was applied by conceptualizing destination categories as treatments to estimate the conditional average treatment effect, providing an alternative assessment of the mobility effect [56]. All analyses were conducted using R 4.4.3, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value less than 0.05.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This large cross-national study of over 90,000 middle-aged and older adults offers strong evidence relating intergenerational educational mobility to later-life depressive symptoms across diverse settings. We observed a clear and consistent gradient in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses: individuals with more advantaged educational mobility—whether stably high or upwardly mobile—had a significantly lower likelihood of depressive symptoms than those with a stably low intergenerational educational mobility. Educational mobility exhibits clear gender differences across countries. Gender modified the educational mobility–depressive symptoms associations in European countries and South Korea in longitudinal analysis, but not in other countries. Beyond assessing these associations, our study explored underlying mediating pathways. We identified household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity as key mediators, though the strength and significance of each varied across countries and educational mobility. Together, these findings underscore the long-term protective role of higher educational attainments across generations on mental health and point to targeted interventions for high-risk mobility of middle-aged and older adults.

Gradient and gender patterns of intergenerational educational mobility

Across countries, individuals in the upwardly mobile and stably high intergenerational educational mobility consistently demonstrated lower likelihood of later-life depressive symptoms compared to those in the stably low group. The downwardly mobile and stably middle mobility also showed lower likelihood, but the association was smaller. These findings shared similar patterns across both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, demonstrating a gradient, stepwise pattern. Notably, effect sizes were substantial: in cross-sectional analyses, the odds of depressive symptoms were reduced by approximately 50% among those in the upwardly mobile and stably high mobility; in longitudinal analyses with up to around 10-year follow-up, the reduction remained substantial at around 30%. We used the destination-as-treatment causal framework to estimate the mobility effect and the results also strengthen our main findings. In European countries and South Korea, the association between educational mobility and depressive symptoms is stronger among women than men. This gender-specific interaction was not observed in other countries. The institutional and cultural settings may modify gendered mobility experiences [57, 58]. Nevertheless, the estimates based on meta-analysis revealed comparable educational gradients between men and women. This suggests that, despite contextual variations that accentuate gender differences in some countries, the fundamental relationship between educational mobility and mental health operates similarly across gender at the population level.

Comparison with previous research

Our findings align with previous studies reporting protective mental health effects associated with upwardly mobile and stably high intergenerational educational mobility [17, 18]. Some studies have observed that the downwardly mobile group is associated with poorer mental health. However, such associations are inconsistent across studies, and these discrepancies can be attributed to both methodological differences and contextual heterogeneity. Methodologically, variations exist in the measurement of depressive symptoms (e.g., CES-D vs. Euro-D), the definition of mobility, the choice of reference group (e.g., stable high vs. stable low), and analytical methods [16, 17, 22, 59]. For instance, those studies do not account for secular trends in education across birth cohorts, meaning their "stably low" group may already reflect intergenerational decline, thereby diluting the estimated effect of measured downward mobility. Contextual heterogeneity across settings is also critical, including differences in study populations (age groups, cultural contexts) and study period (e.g., periods of economic recession vs. stability), which may buffer or exacerbate the mental health impacts of downward mobility [16, 59]. Furthermore, the relative deprivation and perceived failure associated with downward mobile status may, in certain contexts, lead to worse outcomes than remaining in a stable low status [22–24, 60]. It also should be noted that existing studies have largely been single-country or single-cohort [17, 18, 59]. Although the magnitude of effects varies by country in our study, the observed gradient across countries suggests that improvement in educational attainment across generations may help to maintain later-life mental health across diverse socioeconomic settings. It also affirms the importance of both parental and personal educational attainment as long-term determinants of mental health and supports the life-course perspective in explaining persistent disparities in mental health.

Theoretical frameworks for observed patterns

The observed gradient resonates with several theoretical frameworks. First, consistent with fundamental cause theory, education acts as an upstream, flexible resource, shaping access to material and psychosocial advantages across the life course [41]. Second, the cumulative (dis)advantage theory helps explain the social gradient of mental health: upward mobility offers partial protection, while stable advantage compounds across generations [61, 62]. Third, the pattern among the downwardly mobile group—whose depressive symptoms were lower than the stably low group, yet higher than the stably middle group—partly aligns with resource substitution theory, suggesting that individuals from an advantaged background can substitute one missing resource (e.g., education) with another (i.e., household wealth) [41, 63]. A more in-depth exploration requires nuanced comparison group classifications, such as high-to-middle, high-to-low, or middle-to-low, which are currently combined within the downwardly mobile group in this study. Fourth, the downwardly mobile group retained some protection from their higher parental educational attainment, likely through early resource accumulation (e.g., cognitive stimulation, health behaviors, or social capital), as posited by resource multiplication theory [64]. Together, these findings highlight the importance of education mobility across generations in influencing long-term mental health, above and beyond individuals’ own education.

Countries variation: the case of South Korea

In South Korea, the association between intergenerational educational mobility and depressive symptoms was relatively weaker than that in other countries, but it remained statistically significant for stably middle and upwardly mobile groups. This attenuated pattern aligns with the country’s distinct educational mobility pattern. South Korea exhibits the highest proportion of low parental educational attainment, the largest stably low group, and the smallest stably high group. This profile is rooted in its unique historical context: the parental generation came of age during the post-war reconstruction and rapid industrialization of the 1950s–1970s, a period when access to formal education was substantially limited [65]. In contrast, the offspring benefited from the dramatic educational expansion that followed. Against this backdrop of sharp intergenerational educational shifts, the mental health returns of educational mobility appear moderated. The intense competition, credential inflation, and shifting labor market dynamics characterizing South Korea’s education system may further dilute the psychological benefits typically associated with higher educational attainment [66, 67]. These pressures may diminish the mental health benefits typically conferred by higher educational attainment, as the stress of maintaining or improving social status through education becomes a pervasive experience across all socioeconomic backgrounds [68]. While intergenerational education remains an important determinant of mental health in South Korea, its impact may be moderated by context-specific cultural and structural factors.

Roles of household wealth and variation in different settings

We further explored three distinct yet complementary mediating pathways of household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity. These factors are well-documented in the literature as important determinants of mental health [13, 16, 28–30]. The mediation proportion of these factors varied substantially across countries, particularly for household wealth (e.g., for the upwardly mobile group, mediation proportion ranged from 5% in Mexico to 77% in England). This striking variation highlights the context-specific nature of the wealth–health gradient and reflects broader differences in economic opportunity and generational wealth transfer. The mediating role of household wealth was most pronounced in the upwardly mobile and stably high mobility, indicating that advantageous educational mobility fosters greater financial and material accumulation in later-life. This in turn, protects mental health through improved access to resources and autonomy.

Roles of social activeness and multimorbidity

Social activeness and multimorbidity also mediated the associations, though generally to a lesser extent than to household wealth. This aligns with mechanisms of social disconnection/cultural mismatch and the biological embodiment of disadvantage [13, 28, 30]. Social activeness likely operates by fostering broader and more supportive social networks, which can provide emotional support, reduce isolation, and promote purpose in older age [69]. These findings align with prior research showing social inactivity mediated a moderate proportion of the education–depressive symptoms relationship [70]. In parallel, multimorbidity reflects differences in health behaviors, healthcare access; fewer and better-managed chronic diseases are strongly associated with lower risk of depressive symptoms [69, 71]. Collectively, these mediators explain a substantial portion of the negative association between more advantaged educational mobility and depressive symptoms, reinforcing education across generations as a fundamental cause operating through multiple pathways.

Implications for healthy aging initiatives and future directions

Our findings have important implications for the healthy aging framework, which views healthy aging as maintaining functional ability through the interplay of intrinsic capacity and supportive environments across the life course [72]. First, in a life-course perspective, investments in equitable education tailored to country-specific contexts can reduce social stratification and foster healthier, more resilient aging, aligning with global aging agendas that prioritize lifelong learning and cognitive reserve [73–76]. Healthy aging initiatives should prioritize populations with stably low educational mobility, who face the highest risk of depressive symptoms in later life. An integrated approach is needed: upstream investment in equitable, high-quality education, such as free or subsidized secondary schooling for disadvantaged youth, can promote upward mobility and build long-term mental health resilience [16]. Second, to maintain intrinsic capacity in later life, targeted mental health services, such as depression screening and management integrated into primary elder care, should be provided to older adults in the stably low group to mitigate immediate risk. Moreover, it is possible that expanding access to lifelong learning may provide opportunities for mental health improvement even in older age [77]. Finally, shaping supportive environments is key, as our mediation analysis indicates that integrated policies ensuring economic security, fostering social participation, and managing chronic diseases can buffer the mental health impacts of disadvantaged mobility. Future healthy aging strategies should therefore adopt a multigenerational perspective, recognizing that educational mobility shapes later-life mental well-being through complex and interrelated pathways, thereby contributing to sustainable aging societies.

Limitations and future research prospects

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this is an observational study, residual confounding and period effects from pooling data across different years cannot be excluded. However, extensive adjustment for multiple sociodemographic, country-specific, and childhood conditions variables in our study makes it unlikely that these issues would overturn the main findings. Second, measurement limitations remain despite using both relative and absolute educational mobility. Participants’ and parental education were retrospectively reported, introducing potential recall bias, and for parents in Mexico, it was captured in only four broad categories, increasing misclassification. We encourage more detailed measurement of parental educational attainment in future studies in low- and middle-income settings. Third, the dichotomization of mediators improved interpretability but likely reduced variability; future work should use continuous measures to probe gradients and thresholds. Future work could explore these continuous measures to investigate more refined gradients. Finally, although six aging studies provide a harmonized depressive symptoms assessment framework, cross-national differences in language, cultural settings, and familiarity with testing procedures may influence depressive symptoms scores, even after statistical adjustment. Future work may consider applying advanced psychometric methods to improve measurement comparability [78, 79].

Conclusions

In this large cross-national study, we adopted a life-course approach and provided evidence that intergenerational educational mobility is associated with later-life depressive symptoms across diverse settings. Individuals with more advantaged educational mobility—especially those in stably high or upwardly mobile groups—had substantially lower likelihood of depressive symptoms than those with stably low education across generations. We further identified household wealth, social activeness, and multimorbidity as mediators, clarifying how educational mobility operates as a fundamental cause of health. These findings underscore the importance of promoting educational opportunities and implementing integrated strategies to reduce mental health burden and disparities globally.
