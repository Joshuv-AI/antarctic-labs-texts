The link between nutrient intake and anthropometric indices with COVID-19 infection in pregnant women
Authors: Masoomeh Alsadat Mirshafaei, Shiva Khodarahmi, Masoumeh Dorosti, Zahra Falahnejad, Alireza Khosravani, Mohammad Keshavarz Mohammadian, Newsha Faghihshojaei, Seyedeh Hajar Sharami, Niayesh Naghshi, Sara Samiei, Misa Naghdipour Mirsadeghi, Sara Khoshdooz, Saeid Doaei, Forozan Milani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe incidence and severity of COVID-19 complications in pregnant women may be significantly influenced by their nutritional status. So, the present study aimed to investigate the association between nutrient intake and anthropometric indices with COVID-19 infection in pregnant women.MethodsThis case‑control study was conducted on 117 pregnant women infected with COVID-19 as the case group, and 126 non-infected pregnant women as the control group. Information on demographic, social, anthropometric and biochemical indicators was collected. In both groups, food intake data were collected using a valid food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) covering the previous year.ResultsHigher vitamin D levels were associated with reduced odds of COVID-19 (OR = 0.93, CI95%:0.89–0.99, P = 0.02), even after adjusting for age, underlying diseases, caloric intake, and levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). No significant associations were observed between weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and other dietary nutrients with the risk of COVID-19.ConclusionPregnant women with insufficient dietary vitamin D intake are suggested to be at a significantly higher risk of COVID-19. Therefore, ensuring adequate vitamin D consumption is essential. It is recommended to conduct further longitudinal investigations to obtain more definitive results, particularly among pregnant women.

Introduction

The presence of COVID-19 during pregnancy places a substantial burden on clinical services and the healthcare system [1]. Multiple studies have shown thedetrimental impact of COVID-19 on both the mother and the infant [2–4]. Studies have shown that individuals with suppressed immune systems, including pregnant women who experience immunosuppression, are more susceptible to severe disease and complications from COVID-19 compared to those without such conditions [5, 6]. The estimated increase in the mortality rate of pregnant women in England during the COVID-19 pandemic was 9.6%, while in Iran it was reported to be 2.8% [7].

Pregnant women are more vulnerable to infectious diseases, especially respiratory ones, and severe pneumonia because their immune systems are weakened and their respiratory anatomy is altered by high levels of estrogen and progesterone, along with restricted lung surface area [8–10], particularly if they have congenital disorders or chronic diseases [11]. The risk of infection in pregnant women is elevated by changes in their cardiovascular and respiratory systems, including an increase in heart rate and cardiac output volume, decreased lung capacity, increased oxygen consumption, and increased immunological compatibility that enables pregnant women to tolerate the fetus as a different antigen [8, 11]. It is noteworthy to acknowledge that the potential risks associated with a cytokine storm resulting from infection in expectant women can lead to severe complications and even death [9].

Many factors affect anthropometric measurements, including genetics, nutrition, physical activity, lifestyle, and environmental conditions [12]. One of the essential principles of prenatal care for all women during pregnancy is the appropriate and sufficient ingestion of macronutrients and micronutrients [13]. The nutritional status of a woman during pregnancy impacts the health of the fetus and neonate, as well as pregnancy outcomes [14]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the ingestion of micronutrients in appropriate and sufficient quantities can prevent negative pregnancy outcomes. For instance, the impact of folic acid supplementation on neural tube defects has been well established [15]. The incidence and severity of COVID-19 complications in pregnant women may be significantly influenced by their nutritional status. Dysfunction of the immune system, which may exacerbate the progression of COVID-19, is a consequence of a deficiency of certain micronutrients, including vitamin D [15, 16]. According to a study that investigated the correlation between nutritional status, immunity, and COVID-19, the consumption of anti-inflammatory nutrients, including omega-3 fatty acids, beta-carotene, phytochemicals, and plant-based foods that are high in fiber, may serve as a potential strategy for reducing the risk of contracting the disease and its severity [17]. Additionally, the results indicate that plant-based diets and a Pescatarian diet (a vegetarian diet that incorporates seafood) may be employed as a protective measure against severe COVID-19 [18]. On the other hand, some recent studies have suggested that obesity and overweight may increase the likelihood of contracting COVID-19; however, the findings have been inconsistent [19]. So, this study aimed to explore the relationship between COVID-19 infection with anthropometric indicators (e.g., height, weight, and body mass index (BMI)) and the nutritional status of pregnant women. The central hypothesis of this study is that maternal nutrient intake is associated with the risk of COVID‑19 infection during pregnancy. We further hypothesized that anthropometric indices such as BMI may also influence susceptibility to COVID‑19 in pregnant women.

Methods

This case‑control study was conducted from March 2021 to March 2022 at the - Al‑Zahra Obstetric Hospital, Rasht, Iran. The study initially planned to include 112 participants in each of the case and control groups, with the sample size calculated using OpenEPI software according to an odds ratio (OR) of 5.5 reported in prior research. To account for a potential 20% dropout rate, the final sample included 135 pregnant women diagnosed with COVID-19 (via positive PCR test) as the case group, and 135 pregnant women without COVID-19 as the control group. Matching was performed based on gestational age and maternal age. The participants were categorized into specific age ranges (18 to 25 years, 26 to 33 years, and 34 to 40 years), with an equal number of participants selected from each age range in both groups to ensure comparability.

All participants were 18 to 40 years old, with a gestational age greater than 20 weeks, who were referred to Al‑Zahra Maternity Hospital. Exclusion criteria included inability to respond, ICU admission, kidney or metabolic diseases, unwillingness to continue participation, or inability to collect anthropometric data. In addition, those who were exclusively vegetarian and patients who died during the study were excluded. After applying these exclusion criteria and accounting for withdrawals, the final number of participants analyzed was 117 in the case group and 126 in the control group.

Data collection

Demographic and lifestyle data were collected using a 25‑item general information questionnaire, which included categorical (yes/no or multiple choice) and Likert‑scale questions. The questionnaire assessed age (years), occupation, and history of diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension (HTN, defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg). Laboratory parameters, including liver enzymes (AST: Aspartate aminotransferase; ALT: Alanine aminotransferase; ALP: Alkaline phosphatase), were obtained from the patients’ medical records at Al‑Zahra Hospital.

Dietary intake was assessed using a 168‑item Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), originally developed and validated in Persian for the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study [20]. Participants reported the frequency of food consumption over the previous year, and responses were converted into average daily nutrient intakes using Nutritionist IV software. Using the collected data and Nutritionist IV software, the intake of nutrients, including macronutrients and micronutrients, was calculated. The software matched each food item with its nutrient composition profile and calculated daily intakes of different types of macronutrients and micronutrients. For local foods not fully covered by the software database, nutrient estimates were supplemented using the Food Composition Tables of the National Nutrition and Food Technology Research Institute (NNFTRI). Individual nutrient intakes were obtained by summing across all reported food items. Anthropometric indicators, including weight, height, and BMI, were collected using a bio-impedance analyzer (BIA) device [21] in the special department for COVID-19 patients (for the case group) and the prenatal care department (for the control group) of Al-Zahra Hospital.

Statistical analyses

The two groups were compared in terms of demographic, anthropometric, biochemical, and food intake factors using independent t-tests (for quantitative data) and chi-square tests (for qualitative data). The normality of the research data was investigated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, kurtosis, and skewness indices. Subsequently, the homogeneity of variance among the studied groups was checked using Leven’s test. The relationship between COVID-19 infection with anthropometric indicators and food intake was evaluated using different logistic regression models: Model 1: Adjusted for age, occupation, AST, ALT, ALP, and calorie intake; Model 2: Further adjusted for history of DM and HTN, and BMI. All statistical data were processed using SPSS software version 21, with a significance level of 0.05.

Ethics considerations

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations Ethical Committee of the Guilan University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR.GUMS.REC.1401.013). All procedures of the studies involving human participants were by the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. All participants signed informed consent forms at the beginning of the study.

Results

The case group was partially older than the control group (33.23 ± 7.39 years vs. 30.54 ± 6.58 years, P = 0.05). The cases had higher occupation (35 (29.9%) vs. 12 (9.5%), P < 0.001) and diabetes (22 (17.5%) vs. 10 (8.5%), P = 0.03) compared to the controls. No significant differences were noted in terms of height (162.27 ± 5.46 cm vs. 160.74 ± 6.73 cm, P = 0.24), weight (73.06 ± 13.47 kg vs. 71.49 ± 13.80 kg, P = 0.59), BMI (27.47 ± 4.23 vs. 27.71 ± 5.22, P = 0.81), and history of hypertension (26.2% vs. 23.1%, P = 0.34). Additionally, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups in the serum levels of AST (31.16 ± 22.88 U/L vs. 37.40 ± 116.41 U/L, P = 0.67), ALT (27.84 ± 29.88 U/L vs. 32.65 ± 93.31 U/L, P = 0.70), and ALP (252.05 ± 105.56 U/L vs. 246.34 ± 102.74 U/L, P = 0.78) (Table 1).

Discussion

Our study showed a significant association between the risk of COVID-19 infection in pregnant women and vitamin D intake. Higher vitamin D intakes were significantly associated with a reduced risk of contracting COVID-19, even after adjusting for potential confounders (Fig. 1).

The link between nutrient intake and anthropometric indices in pregnant women with COVID-19 infection

In line with the findings of our study, research by Sinaci el showed pregnant women with COVID-19 had significantly lower vitamin D levels compared to healthy pregnant women, and those with mild COVID-19 had higher vitamin D levels than those with more severe cases. Although vitamin D supplementation did not show a statistically significant difference, all severe cases were observed among women who did not take supplements [22]. A meta-analysis revealed that obesity in pregnant women with COVID-19 is linked to more severe infection, higher maternal mortality, and increased hospital admissions, while lower levels of micronutrients, particularly vitamin D, are also observed but less conclusively [23]. Also, consistent with our study findings, other studies have reported a significant relationship between low vitamin D levels and increased incidence of COVID-19, as well as increased disease severity and mortality [24–28]. Angelidi et al. discovered a negative association between vitamin D levels and the need for invasive mechanical ventilation [27].

In contrast with our findings, Tekin et al. investigated the relationship between vitamin D levels and the clinical severity of COVID-19 in pregnant women and showed no significant association between vitamin D status and the seriousness or pulmonary involvement of COVID-19, with similar rates of vitamin D deficiency found in both infected and healthy pregnant women [29]. In addition, several studies have failed to establish a significant association between COVID-19, dietary intake, and serum levels of vitamin D [30, 31]. For instance, in research conducted by Lohia et al., no statistically significant correlation was seen between vitamin D levels and mortality [32]. Nutz et al. reported no association between 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels and changes in the clinical progression of COVID-19. However, they observed that low levels of 1- and 25-dehydroxyvitamin D were associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation and higher APACHE2 scores [33]. The disparity in findings may be attributed to variations in study design, population characteristics, and methods of vitamin D assessment. Differences in sample size, baseline health conditions of participants, and the timing of vitamin D measurement in relation to infection could also contribute to inconsistent results across studies.

According to the findings of the present study, there was no significant impact of weight, height, and body mass index on the probability of contracting COVID-19. In line with our study, some prior research has shown that there is no association between BMI and developing COVID-19 [34]. In contrast to our findings, a systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that an increase in BMI is associated with the hospitalization of COVID-19 patients [35]. Based on a comprehensive analysis of the research, it can be concluded that obesity diminishes the functionality of the immune system, rendering obese individuals more vulnerable to infections and a range of disorders, including COVID-19 [36].

The mechanisms connecting vitamin D and COVID-19 are still under investigation. Adami et al. found a direct correlation between a deficiency of vitamin D and heightened inflammatory reactions [37]. Also, it is suggested that vitamin D plays a protective role in pregnant women with COVID-19 by enhancing ACE2 expression, which is downregulated by SARS-CoV-2 infection [35]. ACE2 is critical for producing Angiotensin 1–7 (Ang-1–7), a vasodilator that helps modulate blood pressure and hemodynamics, especially during pregnancy when Ang-1–7 levels are naturally elevated [38]. In COVID-19, reduced ACE2 levels and increased inflammatory cytokines may contribute to hypertensive complications like pre-eclampsia, a condition marked by lower Ang-1–7 levels. By increasing ACE2 expression and Ang-1–7 levels, while simultaneously lowering Angiotensin II and reducing inflammation, Vitamin D supplementation may help prevent pre-eclampsia or hypertension in pregnant women with COVID-19, thus reducing maternal and perinatal mortality and morbidity risks [39, 40].

Our study has several strengths, including the careful calculation of sample size using OpenEPI software and the rigorous application of matching criteria based on gestational age and maternal age, which enhances the comparability between the case and control groups. The use of validated tools like the FFQ for dietary assessment and BIA for anthropometric measurements enhances data reliability and validity. Comprehensive statistical analyses, adjusting for key confounders such as age, education, and substance use, further strengthen the robustness of the findings. However, this study had some limitations. The reliance on self-reported dietary data via the FFQ introduces potential recall bias, which may affect accuracy. Moreover, vitamin D obtained from sunlight exposure was not adjusted for in the analysis. The relatively small sample size, especially after exclusions, limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, as an observational study, it cannot establish causal relationships between COVID-19 infection and the dietary or anthropometric factors examined. Future studies addressing these limitations may help improve the accuracy and applicability of the results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case–control study evaluated the association between anthropometric indices, dietary intake, and the risk of COVID-19 infection among pregnant women. The research results suggested that when taking possible confounding variables into consideration, weight, height, and body mass index did not influence the risk of infection by COVID-19. While a greater intake of some carbohydrates and sugars was noticed in the COVID-19 group, this association lost significance when considering total energy intake, indicating that the difference may be attributed to total caloric intake rather than to specific macronutrients.

Among the assessed micronutrients, vitamin D showed a significant inverse association with the odds of having COVID-19, regardless of demographic variables, biochemical variables, total calories, comorbidities, and BMI. This suggests a potential protective role of adequate vitamin D intake against COVID-19 in pregnant women. No other micronutrient showed any independent association after adjusting for energy. Overall, these results highlight the importance of vitamin D as a modifiable nutritional factor in pregnancy, while indicating that anthropometric characteristics and most dietary components may not independently influence COVID-19 susceptibility. Further prospective studies are recommended to confirm these findings and clarify causal relationships.
