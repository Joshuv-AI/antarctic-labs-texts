Investigating the relationship between e-health literacy and the tendencies and preferences of patients with type 2 diabetes in using self-management technologies
Authors: Hasan Siamian, Malihe Ram, Shahrbanoo Pahlevanynejad, Sara Afrazandeh, Zahra Karbasi Dahouji, Azam Sabahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Type 2 diabetes is known as the most common type of diabetes and requires continuous and comprehensive patient management and support. Diabetes self-management is a set of defined activities and behaviors the patient performs to achieve control and management of diabetes in their daily life. This study examines the relationship between e-health literacy and the tendencies plus preferences of patients with type 2 diabetes in using self-management technologies of their disease in Ferdows city in 2022.MATERIALS AND METHODS:In this descriptive-analytical study, the research population consisted of all clients of health centers and hospitals of Ferdows city, whereby 305 patients with type 2 diabetes were randomly selected and examined. The inclusion criteria were age over 18 years, reading and writing literacy, and being able to use a mobile phone. The collection tool was a three-part questionnaire including a checklist of individual characteristics, a standard 8-question Norman electronic health literacy questionnaire, and a researcher-made questionnaire on the preferences and tendencies of diabetic patients in the field of using mobile health in self-management of the disease. The data were described using descriptive statistics analysis including frequency tables, mean, variance, and standard deviation by SPSS software version 26, after which nonparametric tests such as Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, and Spearman’s correlation coefficient were used.RESULTS:A total of 305 people participated in this study, of whom 153 (50.2%) were men and the rest were women. The average age of the participants in the research was 50.17 ± 12.91 years. The average health literacy score of the participants in the study was 27.02 ± 5.21. The number of people who used methods based on companion health to manage their disease was 278 people; among the various methods, telephone call-based ones were the most frequent with 69.2%, followed by the use of SMS among patients with a relative frequency of 51.5% as the most used method. Out of 305 patients participating, 111 people had not used any of the health-related methods in managing their disease. Most of the people (55.9%) mentioned that they did not know that these methods exist for managing their disease.CONCLUSION:Given the low level of e-health literacy among patients, implementing national diabetes programs and providing self-management education to patients should be a priority. The use of disease management strategies along with the usage of technologies would enhance patient interaction with electronic tools, which would, in itself, improve e-health literacy.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is the most common metabolic, chronic, noncommunicable disease in the world, called “hidden epidemic” by the World Health Organization.[1] Persistent hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both leads to diabetes.[2] High blood sugar levels generally cause vascular damage in the heart, glaucoma and cataracts, urinary tract infections, skin infections, and foot and nerve problems, resulting in a variety of complications.[34]

The global diabetes prevalence is estimated to be about 422 million, and it has been estimated that more than 690 million will be affected by 2045.[5] According to a study from the World Health Organization from 2020, the overall prevalence of diabetes in Iran is 10.3%, which is estimated at 9.6% in men and 11.1% in women.[6] The global cost of diabetes in 2021 was estimated at 966 million dollars, reported to be about $1838.4 per person with diabetes.[7]

Studies have shown that one of the main ways to treat and prevent type 2 diabetes is to create a establish lifestyle.[89] As a result, improving eating habits and physical activity can improve health status of patients with type 2 diabetes and be effective on reducing the complications caused by this disease.[1011] Type 2 diabetes requires continuous and comprehensive patient management and support.[12]

Self-management refers to an individual’s ability to manage the signs and symptoms, treatment, physical and psychosocial consequences, and lifestyle changes associated with living with a chronic condition. Advances in digital technologies enable the modification of health behaviors and promotion of self-management.[13]

The growing use of mobile phones and smart phones can be harnessed to manage and control the disease.[14] Also, mobile applications have made it possible to capture, transmit, and receive feedback anytime and anywhere, which facilitates remote monitoring and timely health care recommendations, as all mobile applications can enhance self-management.[15]

M-health, a subset of digital health, refers to the use of mobile wireless technologies. M-health technology is an evolving communication system used in patient and health-professional interactions.[16] Health interventions along with providing comfort and care in a natural environment and minimizing the barriers of distance, time, and cost have overcome many traditional barriers. M-health interventions have enabled clinicians to evaluate prescribed interventions, monitor side effects, and identify areas for service improvement.[14]

To use mobile health in disease management, people require many skills, such as instructing users to track and recognize health information in the Internet environment, which is affected by e-health literacy.[17] E-health literacy is a degree of individual skills and competencies needed to provide, communicate, process, and understand basic health information and services required to make appropriate health decisions.[18] Therefore, e-health literacy can be an effective factor on the formation of patients’ preferences and inclinations to receive health interventions in the management of their disease. In recent decades, researchers have tried to use mobile health applications in the field of type 2 diabetes self-care. For example, according to Mao et al.,[19] self-efficacy has been found to be effective on patients with diabetes.

In a study conducted by Dincer and colleagues, with the aim of examining the effect of a mobile application on the foot care of individuals with type 2 diabetes, individuals in the experimental group who received animation-supported M-DFCE1 had significantly higher knowledge, self-efficacy, and foot care behavior levels than the control group.[20] Elsewhere, Mengna Guo and colleagues found that mHealth management contributed to significant reductions in BMI2, FBG3, 2hPG4, and HbA1c5 and improved quality of life and self-management among patients.[21] In the study by Xinghan Wu and colleagues, they mentioned that there is plenty of evidence about the effectiveness of mobile phone applications for lifestyle modification in type 2 diabetes.[22]

Overall, attention should be paid to the preferences and desires of patients since it has a motivating and participatory role in self-management of the disease, enhancing the satisfaction of patients as well as effectiveness of educational and therapeutic interventions with the use of health. Meanwhile, considering that it is necessary to participate in treatment decisions, health literacy is required for disease self-management.[23] This research aimed to determine the relationship between e-health literacy and the tendencies plus preferences of patients with type 2 diabetes in employing M-health in self-management of their disease in Ferdows city in 2022.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted through a descriptive-analytical method in 2022. The research population included all clients of health centers (Javadalaimah, Valiasr, Shahid Delkhah, Shahid Shakuri) and hospitals of Ferdows city (Shahid Dr. Chamran and Hazrat Rasool) who met the eligibility criteria to enter the study. Sampling was done on patients who visited the health centers of Ferdows city in the third quarter of 2022.

Study participants and sampling

According to the information available in the study by Fernández-Gutiérrez et al.,[24] in which the average self-care score of type 2 diabetic patients was 16 ± 5.33, and based on the size of the population of type 2 diabetic patients in Ferdows city, which was reported to be 284 people, plus the confidence level of 95% and the error of 0.1σ and the formula for determining the sample size of limited communities in the cross-sectional studies below, the minimum required sample size was obtained as 163 people. However, finally, in order to boost the validity of the study, the researchers used the census of all diabetic patients of Ferdows city who had met conditions to enter the study so that more reliable results could be obtained.

The eligibility criteria of this study were as follows: age over 18 years, reading and writing literacy, and being able to use a mobile phone. In case of incomplete filling of the questionnaires, such that more than 20% of the questions remain unanswered, they would be excluded from the study.

Data collection tool and technique

Data were collected using a three-part tool.

Part I: It included a checklist of personal characteristics (age, gender, level of education, employment status, marital status, duration of disease, suffering from other diseases).

Part II: Norman and Skinner[25] standard electronic health literacy questionnaire. This questionnaire included eight questions. The electronic health literacy questionnaire is a self-declaration based on the knowledge and understanding of what health information resources are available on the Internet, where a person can search for useful health resources, how to access these resources, and how to use the Internet in order to answer related issues. The ability to evaluate online health information is focused on distinguishing between low-quality sources and quality sources on the Internet. However, this questionnaire does not evaluate people’s real knowledge, rather their perceived knowledge. People can express their opinion regarding the components of the questionnaire based on a 5-point Likert scale from completely disagree (1 point) to completely agree (5 points). As a result, the final score of each participant varied from 8 to 40, where a score of 32 and above indicates high e-health literacy.[26] The reliability and validity of the translated Farsi version of the e-health literacy questionnaire were examined by Bazm et al.[27] They found the factor loadings of the items range from 0.723 to 0.862, which is an acceptable value. The open-test coefficient (P < 0.001, r = 0.96) and Cronbach’s alpha coefficient were reported as an acceptable value (Alpha = 0.88, P < 0.001). The results of this study revealed that the translated version of the questionnaire was equivalent to the original version and presented good validity as well as reliability.[28] In the current study, the validity and reliability of this questionnaire were measured with its Cronbach’s alpha coefficient being 0.81, which is acceptable.

Part III: The questionnaire was developed by researchers and based on the search plus review of articles in the field of M-health and its role in self-management of type 2 diabetes, in which 14 questions were asked separately. These items include two questions related to the use of practices based on mobile health, eight questions related to the experience of using diabetes applications, one question related to learning and using diabetes applications, and three questions linked to preferences in practices, all being based on mobile health. The validity and reliability of the questionnaire were calculated based on Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and using SPSS version 26 software. Based on this, the alpha coefficient of this questionnaire was found to be 0.876.

Data analysis

After collecting and entering the SPSS software version 26, the data were described using descriptive statistics methods including frequency tables, central and dispersion charts, such as mean, variance, and standard deviation, followed by correlation assessment and differential analysis. Considering the non-normality of the health literacy variable, nonparametric tests such as Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, and Spearman’s correlation coefficient were used among the variables.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Birjand University of Medical Sciences with the approval code IR.BUMS.REC.1401.279. The necessary coordination was made to publish the questionnaires. From among all people participating in this study, a written consent was obtained related to noninterventional plans and they were informed that they can refuse to answer the questions and terminate their participation in this plan at any time. Participation in the study was voluntary, and their personal information remained confidential.

Results

A total of 305 people participated in this research, of whom 153 (50.2%) were men and the rest were women. The average age of the participants in the research was 50.17 ± 12.91 years, where the youngest age among the participants was 22 years and the oldest age was 95 years. Specifically, 234 people (76.7%) were married and the rest were single. The highest frequency of people’s education level was at the bachelor’s level, with the number of 109 people (35.9%); 73 subjects (24%) were literate at the reading and writing levels, 63 (20.7%) were at the diploma level, 41 persons (13.5%) at the post-diploma level, and 18 (5.9%) at the master’s level. Most of the individuals (83.6%) participated in the urban arease. Also, 65 subjects (21.3%) were employees, 71 (23.4%) were housewives, 10 were unemployed (3.3%), 43 were farmers (14.1%), 39 (12.8%) were retired, and 75 (24.7%) were employed in other jobs.

Regarding the duration of the disease, 55 subjects (18%) reported that they had been suffering from the disease for less than a year, 101 (33.1%) reported 1 to 5 years, 74 (24.3%) reported 5 to 10 years, and 75 (24.6%) reported more than 10 years, where 52.8% of the subjects had no other comorbidities. Furthermore, 70 subjects (43.49%) had high blood pressure, 35 (21.74%) had fatty liver, 31 (19.25%) had heart disease, 12 (7.45%) had kidney disease, 6 (3.73%) had hypothyroidism, 3 (1.86%) had a history of fatty liver disease, 3 (1.86%) had asthma, and 1 (0.62%) suffered from polycystic ovary disease.

Discussion

Low health literacy can cause adverse health outcomes for many chronic diseases. Type 2 diabetes is one of the most important of these diseases. As Internet-based eHealth resources are being developed to facilitate disease management, understanding eHealth literacy and its associated factors is essential. This research was conducted with the aim of exploring the relationship between e-health literacy and the tendencies plus preferences of patients with type 2 diabetes in the use of mobile health in the self-management of their disease in Ferdows city in 2022. The results of this study revealed that the average e-health literacy score of the participants in the study fell within an average level (27.02 ± 5.21). The results of the study by Karbasi et al.[29] indicated that the participants’ e-health literacy level was low. In the study by Shiferaw et al.,[18] it was also found that the e-health literacy of most patients with chronic diseases participating in the study was at a low level. Given the low level of e-health literacy among participants, patient education and support from healthcare providers can be helpful in this regard.

In order to achieve the second goal, the results of the study revealed that among the different methods based on mobile health, telephone calls were the most frequent, followed by use of text messages. Also, about half of people stated that they searched about their illness on web pages. In the study by Knaitza et al.,[30] most patients also wanted to be contacted if they experienced any unusual symptoms regarding their health. Given that the increasing penetration of smart phones and the expansion of wireless network coverage, digital systems that have equipped health with new facilities, this has led to growing acceptance of mobile health facilities such as phone calls and SMS in the health of patients.

The results of the study demonstrated that a small number of patients (21.3%) used mobile health applications to manage their disease. Consistent with the results of this study, results from studies conducted in Taiwan[31] and China[32] illustrated that the use of diabetes apps among participants was very limited. Lack of awareness and unfamiliarity with diabetes applications could be one of the reasons for people’s limited use. Therefore, raising awareness across the diabetic population about these programs is a priority.[31]

The results of this study indicated that less than half of those who used these applications found the application somewhat useful and believed that learning the application was easy with the instructions given for use and did not require any effort. The results of the study by Knaitza et al.[30] also showed that more than two-thirds of patients believed that electronic medical applications were beneficial for their health; however, only 4.1% of patients currently use medical apps.

According to the present study results, the majority of people had declared that they would like to use telephone calls in the management of their disease in the future. This preference is due to the fact that the majority of patients with type 2 diabetes are elderly, and older people may lack the skills and knowledge to use online health resources. Also, the results of the present study indicated that most patients prefer to have their blood sugar control and medication adherence management done by mobile health devices. In a study by Belete et al.,[33] it was also found that mobile phone-based reminders improved medication adherence. In the study by Karbasi et al.,[29] it was found that study participants preferred to use telephone calls to manage their illness, which was due to a lack of sufficient knowledge about mobile health.

Less than half of the participants preferred to have their disease managed on a monthly basis by a mobile health tool. The results of the study by Knitza et al.,[30] which was conducted on the preferences of using mobile health in rheumatic patients, revealed that patients would prefer their disease to be managed weekly by the software, which contradicted the results of the present study. This discrepancy could be traced back to the target population.

The results of the study showed that there is a statistically significant difference between e-health literacy and marital status, people’s place of residence, suffering from other underlying diseases, education level, job, and duration of diabetes. There was an inverse significant relationship between patients’ health literacy and their age. According to the study of Altaş et al.,[34] there was a significant relationship between the e-health literacy score and the level of education of individuals, which is in line with the results of the present study. It seems that lower education level may have a negative impact on patients’ health. This is because patients with low education level use electronic resources significantly less to obtain information about their health. Thus, it is necessary to assess patients’ e-health literacy and identify their problems in accessing e-health resources, especially in patients with low education level. Elderly people cannot access health information using electronic resources due to their inability to use smart phones or similar electronic devices. Chronic diseases are more common in older age groups, so interventions for this population should be done with the aim of boosting their Internet usage skills.

The results of the study indicated that there was a statistically significant difference between the e-health literacy of people who used text messages, phone calls, applications, educational packages, and web pages in their disease self-management and those who did not. The results of Lin and Lou study[35] also demonstrated that mobile health programs effectively improve patients’ e-health literacy.

Planning to enhance the level of e-literacy, identify valuable resources, and make people aware of them in order to manage their disease are important priorities.[36] Improving e-health literacy and subsequently promoting the use of e-health tools is effective on improving the quality of life.[28] Therefore, the results of this study can be a basis for planning and policymaking in the health sector.

While our findings provide important insights into the relationship between e-health literacy and self-management preferences among patients with type 2 diabetes, several aspects should be considered when generalizing these results to other populations. First, participants in our study were mainly from a specific geographic region with unique cultural, social, and demographic characteristics that could influence e-health literacy and technology acceptance in populations with different diseases. Furthermore, our findings suggest that older adults may be less familiar with newer technologies such as mobile health applications, a limitation that is also expected in other populations. Finally, technology acceptance and use can vary significantly between different patient populations. Attitudes toward health management and technology can also affect how self-management works, which is not specific to the diabetic population and can be effective in other groups as well.

The findings of this study highlight moderate levels of e-health literacy observed in the participants. In order to enhance patient engagement and improve e-health literacy, it is essential to implement targeted educational programs and integrate them with traditional communication methods. It is recommended to create awareness campaigns to inform patients about digital health resources, which will enable patients with type 2 diabetes to effectively use self-management technologies, ultimately leading to better health management and quality of life. In this regard, policymakers would do well to support initiatives that increase e-health literacy as part of broader public health strategies.

The strengths of this study are as follows: The study included a sizeable sample of 305 patients with type 2 diabetes, which enhances the reliability of the findings. Another positive point was use of the standardized and comprehensive Norman questionnaire which can inform healthcare providers about effective communication methods with patients. Based on the findings of this study, which emphasizes the need to increase e-health literacy, the results of the study can be used for effective interventions. Given that this study examined various variables, it did not consider factors affecting these variables, such as previous experiences using digital health tools and the level of access to these tools, which could be a weak point of this study. Among the limitations of the study are the lack of cooperation of patients in completing the questionnaire and the fact that the study was limited to a specific geographical location.

Conclusion

Given the low level of e-health literacy among patients, implementing national diabetes programs and providing self-management education to patients should be a priority. The use of disease management strategies along with the usage of technologies would enhance patient interaction with electronic tools, which in itself improves e-health literacy. In order to improve these interactions, it is essential to have clear and standardized criteria to ensure the quality of health programs. Intervention policies should be geared toward supporting education and management technologies. Therefore, in order to boost e-health literacy, alignment with national diabetes program criteria and e-health interventions is emphasized. It is suggested that future studies use different mixed methods to identify e-health tools required by patients in different populations.
