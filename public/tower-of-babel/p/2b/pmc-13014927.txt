Mechanobiological research fueling the advancement of mechanotherapy and mechanohealth
Authors: Peixiang Ma, Zhifeng Yu, Ning Wang, X. Ed Guo, Guy M. Genin, Ling Qin, Dennis E. Discher, Zonglai Jiang, Jie Zhao, Yi-Xian Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrating mechanobiological principles into disease pathogenesis, therapeutic development, and tissue engineering is reshaping our understanding of biological systems and accelerating the advancement of mechanotherapy and mechanohealth. This field reveals how mechanical cues regulate cellular behavior, such as force transmission along integrin-nucleus pathways and collective cell migration, and tissue functions. In doing so, mechanobiology connects fundamental research with clinical applications, from limiting cancer metastasis and fibrosis to promoting bone regeneration and maintaining vascular homeostasis. Building on discussion from the Inaugural International Conference on Mechanobiology (ICM) 2025, integrating mechanobiological research with clinical strategies offers new opportunities to address unmet needs, including personalized anti-fibrotic interventions or precision bone regenerative therapies. At the same time, it supports the broader concept of mechanohealth -- a paradigm focused on preserving the physiological mechanical balance within tissues.

Introduction

Since its establishment in 2023, the Mechanobiology in Medicine (MBM), an Elsevier Journal, has been a driving force in this rapidly evolving field. The first International Conference on Mechanobiology (ICM) was successfully organized in July 2025, chaired by Jie Zhao (China) and Yi-Xian Qin (USA). The field is rapidly expanding, and the growing importance of mechanobiology has driven advances in medical science. This inaugural conference has highlighted cutting-edge research in the field and predicted its future directions, particularly in understanding the mechanisms and potential clinical impacts.

Drawing on this conference, the commentary explores advancements in mechanobiology—such as the role of intermediate filaments in glioblastoma progression, osteocyte mechanosensing in bone adaptation, and tension anisotropy in fibrotic remodeling—alongside potential challenges (e.g., unannotated gene function validation in murine models, translational gaps between in vitro mechanics and in vivo physiology). Practical implications abound: studies on tumor cell adhesion strength as a metastatic predictor offer new prognostic tools, while macrophage intrudopodia-mediated tumor elimination paves the way for mechanotherapy-based cancer immunotherapies. In clinical settings, mechanobiology-driven innovations (e.g., microtopography-engineered biomaterials, mechanical niche regulation for fibrosis) enhance therapeutic efficacy, reducing complications in tissue regeneration and implantable device applications (e.g., orthopedic implants, vascular stents).3,4

Combining mechanobiological research with traditional clinical approaches is proposed to address unmet needs—such as personalized anti-fibrotic interventions or precision bone regenerative therapies—while fostering mechanohealth, a paradigm centered on maintaining physiological mechanical balance in tissues. To maximize impact, rigorous validation of mechanical biomarkers (e.g., MMP activity, immune cell mechanical phenotypes), strategic integration of mechanogenetics into immunotherapeutic pipelines, and interdisciplinary collaboration (across cell biology, biomedical engineering, and clinical medicine) are recommended to accelerate the translation of mechanobiological insights into actionable mechanotherapies and sustained mechanohealth.

Deciphering cellular-level mechanical sensing and intracellular mechanotransduction

Future research will prioritize unraveling the mechanical behaviors of core cellular components (e.g., nucleus, membrane, cytoskeleton, ECM, and RNA) and their roles in force sensing, transmission, and the maintenance of multi-tissue function (e.g., brain, bone, heart, nerve system, and immune-system) (Fig. 2). Vimentin is a cytoskeletal intermediate filament protein that plays crucial roles in epithelial-mesenchymal transitions during tumor invasion, wound healing, and development. Recent advances have significantly enhanced our understanding of vimentin's multifaceted roles in cellular architecture and mechanical resilience. As a key intermediate filament protein, vimentin dynamically organizes intracellular structures and exhibits exceptional deformability, as revealed by cryo-electron microscopy studies of its high-resolution 3D structure.5 Vimentin organizes the motion and positioning of numerous organelles, including mitochondria and the nucleus. Furthermore, it synergizes with the actin cytoskeleton to protect cells from extreme mechanical deformations. Finally, vimentin expression in epithelial-mesenchymal transitions has a functional role in tumor invasion analogous to embryonic development and wound healing.5 Notably, vimentin forms liquid condensates that facilitate rapid filament assembly, with these phase-separated droplets exhibiting protective effects on actin stress fibers.6 Moreover, vimentin intermediate filaments (VIFs) and F-actin were traditionally viewed as independent networks, but recent super-resolution microscopy and cryo-ET studies demonstrate they form an interpenetrating network (IPN) that synergistically enhances cellular contractility and mechanical resilience.7 These findings collectively transform our understanding of cytoskeletal interactions, particularly highlighting vimentin's central role in integrating mechanical and structural functions across diverse biological contexts.

The mechanical landscape of the metastatic niche (matrix stiffness, topography, and physical stresses) actively regulates tumor growth and proliferation. A deeper understanding of how cells sense and respond to these mechanical cues could open new therapeutic avenues for multiple diseases, e.g., tumors, fibrosis, etc.8 The intracellular mechanosensing dictates the tissue development. Recent studies have revealed novel mechanisms by which extracellular matrix (ECM) components regulate cellular mechanosensing and mechanotransduction pathways.9 In breast epithelial cells, laminin-111 (via integrin β4) establishes a mechanical linkage between the substrate and the keratin cytoskeleton, which stiffens the network and shields the nucleus from actomyosin-mediated deformation, thereby impairing YAP nuclear localization and mechanoresponses in a rigidity-dependent manner. This laminin-mediated mechanoshielding effect influences chromatin methylation and 3D cell invasion, demonstrating how specific ECM components can modulate tissue sensitivity to mechanical signals.10 Complementary research on mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) shows that crosstalk between integrins and N-cadherins modulates the mechanosensing of substrate viscosity. When both integrin (RGD) and N-cadherin (HAVDI) ligands are present, these adhesion receptors compete for actin cytoskeleton engagement, leading to weakened integrin-mediated adhesion and altered mechanotransduction. This competition, modeled through a modified molecular clutch mechanism, ultimately controls MSC lineage commitment in response to viscous microenvironments.11 The mechanobiology can be applied to design intelligent wearable sensors empowered by smart materials and artificial intelligence for the mechanotherapy.12

Mechanobiology-driven innovation in disease pathogenesis and therapeutic development

A major translational focus will be harnessing mechanobiological insights to address unmet clinical demands, with particular emphasis on cancer, fibrosis, musculoskeletal disorders, and vascular pathologies.i)Cancer: In solid tumors, the abundance of macrophages is typically associated with a poor prognosis. Recent studies have elucidated that macrophage spatial organization and functional states critically regulate their tumoricidal activity. Dynamic macrophage clusters form preferentially in interferon/cytokine-induced "M1-like" states through upregulated cell-cell adhesion receptors and suppressed actomyosin contractility, which reduces cortical tension and promotes protrusive "intrudopodia" that cooperatively dismantle tumor cell nests for phagocytosis - a process associated with the survival marker ITGAL.13 Therapeutic enhancement of this phagocytic potency shows remarkable synergy when increasing macrophage numbers, tumor cell opsonization with monoclonal antibodies, and disrupting the CD47-SIRPα phagocytic checkpoint.14 This tripartite strategy not only induces immediate tumor regression but also triggers endogenous IgG production that confers lasting protection against recurrence and metastasis.14 These findings collectively demonstrate that optimized macrophage clustering and phagocytic coordination can overcome solid tumor defenses through both mechanical disruption of tumor architecture (via intrudopodia) and immunological priming, offering promising combinatorial therapeutic avenues against aggressive malignancies.The cytoskeleton's three major networks (actin, microtubules, and intermediate filaments) demonstrate intricate functional coordination that extends beyond their individual roles, particularly in regulating cell motility and structural plasticity. Current research reveals that intermediate filaments actively participate in cross-network communication through molecular linkers, motor proteins, and signaling regulation, forming an integrated mechanical signaling hub.15 This coordination is particularly critical in neural cells, where connexin-30 (Cx30) modulates astrocyte migration by altering actin dynamics and cortical stiffness, effectively serving as a mechanical brake that reduces migration persistence and directionality.16 The system's complexity is further exemplified by CDC42 isoform specialization in neural cells; while CDC42u localizes to leading edges to establish polarity through Par6-PKCζ recruitment, CDC42b regulates endocytosis via N-WASP at intracellular membranes - together enabling precise control of neural precursor chemotaxis.17 These findings collectively highlight how cytoskeletal components achieve functional specialization through: direct physical interactions between networks, mechanical modulation of membrane properties, and isoform-specific localization of regulatory proteins - mechanisms that are particularly crucial in the central nervous system, where both structural stability and cellular motility must be precisely balanced. Cellular mechanotype — encompassing stiffness, contractility, and adhesion directly influences metastatic cell navigation. By sorting metastatic cancer cells into strongly adherent (SA) and weakly adherent (WA) phenotypes, recent studies have demonstrated that SA cells exhibit durotaxis (migration toward stiffness gradients), whereas WA cells exhibit adurotaxis due to reduced actomyosin activity.13 The distribution of adhesion strengths, if measured at appropriate concentrations of cations, may have prognostic value for metastasis.18, 19

ii)Fibrosis: Mechanobiology regulate fibrosis through extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and cellular mechanosensing. The pericellular matrix (PCM) attenuates long-range force transmission by reducing collagen fiber alignment, thereby shielding cells from mechanical stress. However, cell elongation or anisotropic contraction can override this attenuation, enabling mechanical signaling over extended distances—a process critical in fibrosis, wound healing, and development.20 Complementing these findings, further studies identified a bistable mechanosensitive feedback loop involving integrin β1 and Piezo1, where matrix stiffening amplifies Ca2+/YAP signaling, further promoting ECM stiffening by fibroblasts. Disrupting this loop reversed fibrotic phenotypes, suggesting novel therapeutic strategies.21 Liver fibrosis progression involves complex mechanobiological interactions between hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and an increasingly crosslinked extracellular matrix (ECM). The advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) mediate pathological collagen crosslinking in cirrhotic livers, forming stiff, macrophage-resistant fibril bundles that impair tissue remodeling and promote type II macrophage polarization.22 The rosmarinic acid can serve as a potential therapeutic agent by inhibiting AGE-mediated crosslinking. Mechanical reprogramming and mechanogenetics: Emerging paradigms for immunotherapy

The application of mechanical principles to immunotherapy has achieved significant progress.30,31 Recent advances in focused ultrasound (FUS)-controlled therapies demonstrate its potential for precise spatiotemporal regulation of cancer treatments. The noninvasive FUS-inducible CRISPR toolbox (including CRISPRa and CRISPRee) capable of deep-tissue genomic/epigenomic reprogramming, showing that FUS-CRISPR-mediated telomere disruption enhances CAR-T cell therapy efficacy by creating tumor cell "training centers" for synNotch CAR-T activation.32 Complementing this, sonogenetic EchoBack-CAR T cells were developed featuring an ultrasensitive heat-shock promoter that enables sustained CAR expression upon FUS stimulation.33 These cells exhibited superior persistence and cytotoxicity in glioblastoma and prostate cancer models while minimizing off-target effects, as revealed by single-cell RNA sequencing. Together, these studies establish FUS as a powerful modality for controlling both genome-editing tools (CRISPR systems) and engineered immune cells (CAR-T), offering synergistic strategies for precise treatment of solid tumors.

Besides the ultrasound, the matrix stiffness could also serve as a force application tool. Recent research has revealed that matrix stiffness serves as a biphasic regulator of epigenetic state and fibroblast-to-neuron conversion efficiency, with optimal reprogramming occurring at an intermediate stiffness of 20 kPa.34 In addition, the viscoelastic properties of extracellular matrices drive epigenetic remodeling to potentiate cellular plasticity.35 Synthetic viscoelastic antigen-presenting cells enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.36

Establishing overarching theoretical frameworks for living cell mechanics

Physiological and pathological processes such as embryonic development and tumor progression involve complicated interplay of mechanical, chemical, and biological factors cross a wealth of spatial and temporal scales. The mechano-chemo-biological coupling theories have been developed to elucidate the mechanism in biological tissues and cells, and applied in cancer, immunological, and other diseases.37 The mechano-chemo-biological growth theory interrogates the mechanical, chemical, and biological coupling mechanisms underpinning the growth, remodeling and degradation of tissues such as tumors. The mechano-chemo-biological instabilities of cells and tissues can be systematically analyzed, with particular attention to those induced by coupled mechano-chemo-biological mechanisms. The phenomenon of mechanomemory enables cells to retain biochemical and mechanical adaptations long after mechanical stimuli cease.38 For instance, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) develop a fibrotic mechanomemory when transitioning from stiff to soft substrates, mediated by sustained upregulation of miR-21 through a myocardin-related transcription factor-A (MRTF-A)-dependent pathway.39 Mechanical force directly stretches chromatin, upregulating transcription.40 The stem cells generate mechanical memory, using YAP/TAZ acting as an intracellular mechanical rheostat storing information from past physical environments and influences the cells' fate.41 The mechano-chemo-biological multiscale computational framework can investigate dynamic processes in cells and tissues, such as the migration and metastasis of cancer cells.

Conclusion

In summary, the future of mechanobiology will be defined by strong interdisciplinary integration – bringing together cell biology, mechanics, biomedical engineering, and clinical medicine – along with a clear translational focus that converts fundamental mechanistic mechanobiological insights into clinically actionable therapies. At the same time, advances in precision approaches will enable interventions that specifically target distinct mechanical niches, cell types and populations, or molecular mediators. Together, these developments will position mechanobiology as a key foundation for personalized medicine and regenerative therapies.
