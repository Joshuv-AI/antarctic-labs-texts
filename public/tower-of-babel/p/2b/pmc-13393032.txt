2‑Mercaptobenzimidazole-Modified Copper Foil for Regulating Lithium Deposition in Anode-Free Lithium Batteries
Authors: Shuangshuang Huang, Chenji Hu, Lingfei Tang, Qi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anode-free lithium batteries are limited by irreversible lithium loss and unstable lithium deposition on Cu current collectors. Herein, Cu foil was modified by a simple wet-chemical treatment using 2-mercaptobenzimidazole (MBI) to regulate the Cu/electrolyte interface. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy suggests that MBI is anchored on the Cu surface through Cu–S and Cu–N interactions, while atomic force microscopy and Kelvin probe force microscopy show that the modification changes the surface roughness and surface potential of Cu foil. Electrochemical measurements show that MBI Cu exhibits a lower lithium nucleation overpotential (26.6 mV), a higher exchange current density (1.64 mA cm–2), and reduced interfacial kinetic barriers compared with Bare Cu. In Li∥Cu half cells, MBI Cu also shows improved plating/stripping reversibility, slower impedance growth, and more compact lithium deposits. In anode-free Cu∥LiFePO4 full cells, MBI Cu delivers an initial discharge capacity of 119.03 mAh g–1 and retains 49.15% of its capacity after 100 cycles, whereas the Bare Cu counterpart retains 20.71% under the same conditions. These results suggest that MBI modification is a simple molecular approach for improving interfacial stability and regulating lithium deposition on Cu current collectors in anode-free lithium batteries.

Introduction

Lithium metal has long been regarded as one of the most attractive anode materials for next-generation rechargeable batteries because of its ultrahigh theoretical specific capacity of 3860 mAh g–1 and its low electrochemical potential. , In recent years, anode-free lithium batteries have received increasing attention because they eliminate excess lithium on the anode side and therefore offer the possibility of higher cell-level energy density and a simplified cell configuration. In such systems, however, all active lithium is supplied by the cathode, which makes the electrochemical performance highly sensitive to irreversible lithium loss during cycling. Even a small amount of inactive lithium formation or continuous parasitic interfacial reactions can rapidly deplete the limited lithium inventory, resulting in severe capacity decay and poor cycling stability. , Therefore, improving the reversibility of lithium deposition and stripping at the current collector interface is essential for the development of anode-free lithium batteries.

Among the different components in anode-free cells, the Cu current collector plays a particularly important role because lithium is initially plated directly onto its surface, and its surface properties directly affect lithium nucleation and growth. , Commercial Cu foil is also widely used as a practical current collector and can be readily integrated with existing battery manufacturing procedures. However, the Cu surface does not inherently provide an ideal interface for reversible lithium deposition. Recent work has shown that poor control of lithium nucleation directly on Cu, together with lithium diffusion into the Cu substrate, can make it difficult to form a large number of uniformly distributed nuclei and thereby promote inhomogeneous electrodeposition in anode-free cells. More broadly, Cu current collectors with insufficient lithiophilicity or nonuniform surface characteristics are prone to heterogeneous deposition, dead lithium accumulation, electrolyte consumption, and rapid interfacial deterioration during cycling. Therefore, regulating the Cu/electrolyte interface is a rational approach to improving the electrochemical stability of anode-free lithium batteries.

To address these issues, a variety of strategies have been explored to regulate lithium deposition on Cu current collectors. Tuning the surface electronic structure of current collectors has been shown to affect the early irreversible capacity and capacity-fade behavior in anode-free cells. Three-dimensional current collectors provide another route to homogenize lithium deposition by redistributing the local current density. , In parallel, molecular modifiers such as benzotriazole and triazole derivatives have been introduced onto Cu foil to improve lithiophilicity and guide more uniform lithium plating. , Recent studies on self-assembled molecular layers on Cu have further shown that surface-bound organic layers can regulate lithium nucleation and early growth. , Although these approaches and other polymer-based current-collector modifications have led to encouraging progress, some require relatively complex architectures or additional fabrication steps. For small-molecule surface treatments in particular, the discussion has often remained centered on lithiophilicity improvement and dendrite suppression, whereas the relationship between molecular anchoring on Cu, surface potential redistribution, interfacial kinetic barriers, and the resulting anode-free full-cell behavior remains less clearly described. A clearer understanding of these interfacial factors would be useful for developing simple and effective Cu surface treatments for anode-free lithium batteries.

In this work, 2-mercaptobenzimidazole (MBI) was introduced as a molecular modifier for Cu foil through a simple wet-chemical treatment. Owing to the presence of sulfur- and nitrogen-containing functional groups, MBI can interact with Cu surfaces and form a surface-bound interfacial layer. On this basis, the influence of MBI modification on the surface chemistry, surface morphology, surface potential, interfacial kinetic barriers, lithium plating/stripping behavior, and anode-free full-cell performance was examined. Compared with Bare Cu, the MBI-treated Cu foil exhibited a lower lithium nucleation overpotential, improved interfacial reversibility, slower impedance growth during cycling, and enhanced stability in anode-free Cu∥LiFePO4 full cells under the tested conditions. Overall, this study examines how a simple molecular treatment of Cu foil affects lithium deposition and interfacial stability in anode-free lithium batteries.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Bare Cu and MBI Cu

Commercial Cu foil was punched into 15 mm-diameter discs. The Cu discs were immersed in 12 M HCl for 3 h to remove surface impurities, rinsed three times with deionized water, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The resulting acid-cleaned Cu foil is referred to as Bare Cu throughout this work.

For surface modification, Bare Cu was immersed in a 0.1 wt % ethanolic solution of 2-mercaptobenzimidazole (MBI) at 50 °C for 5 min. After treatment, the Cu foil was rinsed once with anhydrous ethanol to remove residual solute and then dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 2 h. The modified sample is referred to as MBI Cu.

Preparation of LiFePO4 Cathodes and Cell Assembly

The LiFePO4 (LFP) cathode slurry was prepared by mixing LFP, acetylene black, and poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) in a mass ratio of 8:1:1 using N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as the solvent. The slurry was cast onto Al foil and dried sequentially at 60 °C for 3 h, 80 °C for 3 h, and 100 °C under vacuum for 10 h. The dried electrode sheet was roll-pressed and punched into circular cathodes. The LFP cathodes used for full-cell tests had a diameter of 12 mm and an areal mass loading of 4.7–5.1 mg cm–2.

CR2025 coin cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox, where both H2O and O2 levels were maintained below 0.1 ppm. A polypropylene separator with a diameter of 19 mm was used in all cells. The electrolyte consisted of 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME (1:1 by volume) with 2% LiNO3, and 60 μL of electrolyte was used for each cell. The same electrolyte was used for both Li∥Cu half cells and anode-free Cu∥LiFePO4 full cells. However, further optimization of the electrolyte formulation is highly important to explore the feasibility of extended full-cell operation with improved cycling performance.

For Li∥Cu half cells, a Cu foil disc (15 mm in diameter) was used as the working electrode and a Li metal disc (15.8 mm in diameter, 200 μm in thickness) was used as the counter electrode. For anode-free full cells, the LFP cathode was paired with a Cu foil current collector (Bare Cu or MBI Cu) without excess lithium on the anode side, giving an N/P ratio of 0. The cells were sealed under a press setting of 450 kg for 5 s.

Materials Characterization

The surface chemical states of the Cu foils were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Fisher Scientific). All binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV as the reference. Surface morphology and roughness were characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM, NX10, Park Systems) using a scan area of 5 μm × 5 μm and a scan rate of 0.5 Hz. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements were conducted on the same instrument to evaluate the contact potential difference (CPD) distribution of the Cu foils. The morphologies of the Cu foils and the deposited lithium were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Regulus 8230, Hitachi) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.

Electrochemical Measurements

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on a BioLogic VMP-300 electrochemical workstation with an AC amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency range from 7 MHz to 100 mHz. Temperature-dependent EIS measurements were carried out from 25 to 55 °C at intervals of 5 °C. Before each measurement, the cells were allowed to equilibrate at the target temperature for 0.5–1 h. The apparent activation energies of interfacial processes were obtained by Arrhenius fitting of the EIS results from Li∥Cu half cells. The EIS spectra were fitted using the equivalent circuit Rs-(R SEI∥CPESEI)-(Rct∥CPEct) to separate the solution resistance, SEI-related interfacial process, and charge-transfer process. In this circuit, Rs represents the solution resistance, R SEI/CPESEI describes the SEI-related interfacial contribution, and Rct/CPEct corresponds to the charge-transfer process. The constant phase elements were used instead of ideal capacitors to account for the nonideal capacitive behavior of the heterogeneous electrode/electrolyte interface. The fitting focused on the selected interfacial semicircle region to extract R SEI and Rct for comparing the SEI-related and charge-transfer barriers among different Cu electrodes. Representative raw Nyquist plots, fitted curves, and the equivalent circuit are provided in Figure S1.

Tafel measurements were conducted on Li∥Cu half cells by linear sweep voltammetry in the potential range from −0.2 to 0.2 V at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s–1. The exchange current density was determined from Tafel fitting. The lithium nucleation overpotential was obtained from the initial galvanostatic plating profiles of Li∥Cu half cells at 1 mA cm–2.

The Coulombic efficiency of lithium plating/stripping was evaluated in Li∥Cu half cells at 0.5 mA cm–2 and 0.5 mAh cm–2. For the Aurbach test, 5 mAh cm–2 of lithium was first plated onto the Cu electrode, followed by 10 plating/stripping cycles at 0.5 mA cm–2 and 0.5 mAh cm–2. The remaining lithium was then stripped to 1 V, from which the average Coulombic efficiency was calculated.

Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests of anode-free Cu∥LiFePO4 full cells were performed on a Neware CT-4008T battery testing system at 30 °C within a voltage range of 2.5–4.0 V. The cells were first activated at 0.1 C for one cycle and then cycled at 0.5 C. Rate capability tests were performed at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and then 0.2 C, with five cycles at each rate. All specific capacities were calculated based on the mass of active LiFePO4 material, and 1 C was defined as 170 mAh g–1.

Results and Discussion

Surface Chemistry, Morphology, and Surface Potential of MBI-Modified Cu

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was used to examine whether MBI was bound to the Cu surface after the wet-chemical treatment.

The molecular structure of MBI and the modification process are illustrated in Figure a,b. In the Cu 2p spectra (Figure c), no obvious satellite features were observed after modification, suggesting that no obvious Cu­(II) species were formed under the present treatment conditions.

High-resolution XPS spectra of Pristine Cu, Bare Cu, and MBI Cu: (c) Cu 2p, (d) S 2p, and (e) N 1s. AFM topography images (upper panels) and the corresponding KPFM contact potential difference (CPD) maps (lower panels) of (f) Pristine Cu, (g) Bare Cu, and (h) MBI Cu.

In the S 2p spectrum of MBI Cu (Figure d), the peak centered at 162.1 eV can be assigned to Cu–S bonding, suggesting chemisorption of MBI on the Cu surface. In addition, the N 1s spectrum of MBI Cu (Figure e) can be deconvoluted into two components at 399.2 and 400.6 eV, which are attributed to coordinated Cu–N species and pyrrolic N, respectively. Together, the S 2p and N 1s spectra support the formation of a surface-bound MBI layer involving sulfur- and nitrogen-containing groups. In addition, the high-resolution C 1s spectra of Pristine Cu, Bare Cu, and MBI Cu are provided in Figure S2. Pristine Cu and Bare Cu mainly show C–C/CC and C–O components, whereas MBI Cu exhibits an additional C–N/CN contribution associated with the benzimidazole structure of MBI. This change further supports the presence of MBI-derived organic species on the Cu surface.

The influence of MBI treatment on the surface morphology of Cu foil was further investigated by AFM. As shown in Figure f–h, the surface roughness of MBI Cu was 222.6 nm, which was lower than those of Pristine Cu (231.9 nm) and Bare Cu (253.2 nm). The decreased roughness after MBI modification suggests a surface-leveling effect, likely because the chemisorbed MBI layer partially smooths the local surface features created by acid etching, such as microvalleys, protrusions, and defect sites.

KPFM measurements were further performed to evaluate changes in the surface potential distribution after modification. As shown in Figure f–h, the average CPD value changed from −88.2 mV for Pristine Cu and −144.9 mV for Bare Cu to 75.9 mV for MBI Cu. The positive CPD shift indicates that MBI adsorption alters the surface electronic state and potential distribution of the Cu current collector. The more uniform potential distribution on MBI Cu may help reduce spatial variations in the local electric field and interfacial ion flux, thereby providing a more homogeneous environment for Li nucleation. This more uniform potential distribution is expected to lower local current concentration and reduce the kinetic barrier for Li nucleation, which is consistent with the observed lower Li nucleation overpotential, higher exchange current density, and more compact Li deposition on MBI Cu.

Interfacial Kinetics and Lithium Nucleation Behavior

The effect of MBI modification on interfacial kinetics was evaluated using temperature-dependent EIS, Tafel analysis, and lithium nucleation tests. As shown in Figure a,b, MBI Cu exhibited the lowest apparent activation energies among the three samples, with values of 21.76 kJ mol–1 for the SEI resistance (R SEI) and 39.94 kJ mol–1 for the charge-transfer resistance (R ct). In comparison, the corresponding values for Bare Cu were 24.08 and 46.50 kJ mol–1, while those for Pristine Cu were 63.25 and 61.84 kJ mol–1. The lower activation energies point to facilitated ion transport and charge transfer at the Cu/electrolyte interface after MBI modification. Such an effect is expected to lower the kinetic barrier for the initial reduction of Li+ on the Cu surface.

Interfacial kinetics and lithium nucleation behavior of different Cu foils. Arrhenius plots and the derived activation energies based on (a) R SEI and (b) R ct. (c) Tafel plots of Li∥Cu half cells. (d) Lithium nucleation overpotentials obtained from galvanostatic plating profiles measured at 1 mA cm–2.

The interfacial reaction kinetics were further compared by Tafel measurements. As shown in Figure c, the exchange current density of MBI Cu reached 1.64 mA cm–2, which was higher than those of Bare Cu (1.31 mA cm–2) and Pristine Cu (0.80 mA cm–2). The higher exchange current density points to faster interfacial charge-transfer kinetics on MBI Cu. In the initial plating profiles (Figure d), MBI Cu also showed the lowest lithium nucleation overpotential of 26.6 mV, compared with 34.4 mV for Bare Cu and 38.5 mV for Pristine Cu. Although deliberately engineered Cu2O boundaries have been reported to promote Li nucleation by providing lithiophilic sites, the native oxide/contamination layer on air-exposed Pristine Cu is not equivalent to such a designed oxide layer. Native passivation layers on current collectors can influence interfacial reactions, charge-transfer resistance, and Li nucleation/deposition behavior. Therefore, the higher nucleation overpotential of Pristine Cu is mainly attributed to its nonuniform native oxide/contamination layer, which increases interfacial heterogeneity and hinders initial Li+ reduction. Acid cleaning removes part of this layer and exposes a cleaner Cu surface, explaining the slightly lower nucleation overpotential of Bare Cu. MBI modification further introduces N/S-containing interfacial sites, providing a more favorable surface for Li nucleation and resulting in the lowest nucleation overpotential among the three samples.

Taken together, the Arrhenius, Tafel, and nucleation results show that MBI modification is associated with a Cu/electrolyte interface that has lower kinetic barriers for early stage lithium deposition. These kinetic features provide a basis for the improved plating/stripping reversibility and full-cell behavior discussed below. The simultaneous decrease in the apparent activation energies associated with R SEI and Rct can be attributed to the combined effects of improved interfacial chemistry and more stable Li deposition. The N/S-containing MBI layer anchored by Cu–S and Cu–N interactions provides a more favorable interface for Li nucleation and charge transfer, thereby lowering the charge-transfer barrier. In addition, the more uniform Li nucleation and compact Li deposition on MBI Cu can reduce porous Li formation, local current concentration, and repeated SEI fracture/regeneration, leading to a lower SEI-related barrier.

Lithium Plating/Stripping Behavior on Bare Cu and MBI Cu

The effect of MBI modification on lithium plating/stripping behavior was evaluated using Coulombic efficiency measurements in Li∥Cu half cells. As shown in Figure a, MBI Cu maintained a higher and more stable Coulombic efficiency during cycling at 0.5 mA cm–2 and 0.5 mAh cm–2, whereas Bare Cu showed a marked efficiency decay after extended cycling. Additional Coulombic efficiency curves obtained under different current-density/areal-capacity conditions are provided in Figure S3. The higher stability observed in Figure a suggests that MBI modification helps improve the reversibility of lithium plating/stripping on Cu. The improved reversibility observed for MBI Cu is consistent with the lower nucleation overpotential and more favorable interfacial kinetics discussed in the previous section.

Reversibility and interfacial evolution of lithium plating/stripping on Bare Cu and MBI Cu. (a) Coulombic efficiency of Li∥Cu half cells tested at 0.5 mA cm–2 and 0.5 mAh cm–2. (b) EIS spectra before cycling. (c) EIS spectra after 100 cycles. (d) Average Coulombic efficiency determined by the Aurbach method.

The interfacial evolution during cycling was further examined by EIS. Before cycling, Bare Cu and MBI Cu showed comparable impedance responses, although the MBI-modified electrode already exhibited a slightly lower interfacial resistance (Figure b). After cycling, Bare Cu showed a much larger impedance increase than MBI Cu (Figure c). In contrast, the impedance change for MBI Cu was relatively limited, with the interfacial resistance increasing only from 25.38 to 30.91 Ω. This trend suggests that the MBI-derived interfacial layer mitigates impedance growth during repeated lithium plating/stripping. In combination with the kinetic results in Figure , these observations support the interpretation that the modified Cu surface provides a more stable interfacial environment for lithium deposition.

The smaller impedance increase after cycling also suggests that the MBI layer affects SEI formation and evolution. The surface-bound MBI molecules may passivate part of the highly reactive Cu sites and reduce direct exposure of bare Cu to the electrolyte, thereby suppressing initial interfacial side reactions. In addition, the more compact Li deposits observed on MBI Cu after cycling (Figure S6) reduce the exposure of high-surface-area fresh Li during repeated plating/stripping. These effects can mitigate continuous electrolyte decomposition and repeated SEI reconstruction, resulting in slower impedance growth and improved interfacial stability.

The Aurbach test further supports the improved lithium reversibility on MBI Cu. As shown in Figure d, MBI Cu delivered an average Coulombic efficiency of 99.03%, which was slightly higher than that of Bare Cu (98.58%) under 0.5 mA cm–2 and 0.5 mAh cm–2. A similar tendency was also observed under the more demanding condition of 1 mA cm–2 and 1 mAh cm–2 (Figure S4), although the difference between the two electrodes became smaller. The smaller difference at higher current density and areal capacity suggests that the improvement is more pronounced under the milder testing condition.

Ex-situ SEM images at low Li deposition capacities further reveal the difference in early-stage Li nucleation and growth on Bare Cu and MBI Cu (Figure S5). At 0.05 mAh cm–2, Li nuclei on Bare Cu are unevenly distributed, whereas MBI Cu shows more uniformly distributed small Li deposits. With increasing deposition capacity to 0.20 and 0.50 mAh cm–2, Bare Cu develops more localized and filamentous/stripe-like deposits, while MBI Cu maintains a more continuous and relatively compact deposition morphology. These observations indicate that MBI modification helps homogenize early Li nucleation and suppress localized Li growth.

Anode-Free Full-Cell Performance

The cell-level influence of MBI modification was further evaluated in anode-free Cu∥LiFePO4 full cells. As shown in Figure a, the MBI Cu∥LFP cell delivered an initial discharge capacity of 119.03 mAh g–1 and retained 49.15% of its capacity after 100 cycles at 0.5 C, whereas the Bare Cu∥LFP cell showed an initial discharge capacity of 78.98 mAh g–1 and a capacity retention of only 20.71% under the same conditions. Because no excess lithium reservoir is present on the anode side, this difference is consistent with reduced cumulative lithium loss on the MBI-modified current collector during cycling.

Electrochemical performance of anode-free LFP full cells based on Bare Cu and MBI Cu current collectors. (a) Cycling performance at 0.5 C. Representative charge–discharge profiles of (b) Bare Cu∥LFP and (c) MBI Cu∥LFP cells at selected cycles. (d) Rate capability from 0.1 to 1 C.

The corresponding charge/discharge profiles further illustrate the different cycling behavior of the two cells. As shown in Figure b,c, the Bare Cu∥LFP cell experienced rapid capacity fading with cycling, whereas the MBI Cu∥LFP cell maintained higher discharge capacity and more stable voltage profiles over the tested cycle range. The MBI Cu∥LFP cell also showed better rate capability than the Bare Cu∥LFP cell (Figure d), delivering average discharge capacities of 134.16 mAh g–1 at 0.1 C and 111.42 mAh g–1 at 1 C. To further evaluate the rate capability and polarization behavior, the corresponding GCD profiles and voltage hysteresis analysis are provided in Figure S7. Compared with LFP∥Bare Cu, LFP∥MBI Cu delivers higher charge/discharge capacities at the tested C-rates and shows a more stable voltage plateau, whereas Bare Cu deviates earlier from the plateau and exhibits broader sloping regions, especially at high C-rates. The voltage hysteresis was calculated from the charge/discharge voltage gap at 47.6 mAh g–1, corresponding to 28% of the theoretical capacity of LFP used in this work. As shown in Figure S7c, MBI Cu exhibits slightly lower voltage hysteresis at all tested C-rates, indicating reduced polarization and improved rate capability after MBI modification.

Postcycling SEM observations of the Cu electrodes harvested from full cells further support this interpretation. As shown in Figure S8, the electrode from the Bare Cu-based full cell exhibited a rougher and more heterogeneous residual surface after 100 cycles, whereas the MBI Cu electrode retained a flatter and more compact surface morphology. Together with the results in Sections –, these observations link MBI chemisorption and CPD redistribution to lower nucleation overpotential, faster interfacial kinetics, and more compact lithium deposition. The improved anode-free full-cell performance of MBI Cu is therefore consistent with the mitigation of interfacial instability and irreversible lithium consumption during cycling.

Overall, the full-cell results show that simple molecular modification of Cu foil improves the electrochemical stability of anode-free lithium batteries under the present testing conditions. Although capacity fading was still observed during prolonged cycling, the MBI-treated current collector outperformed Bare Cu in both cycling stability and rate capability, highlighting the usefulness of molecular interfacial regulation for current-collector design.

Conclusions

In this work, Cu foil was modified with 2-mercaptobenzimidazole (MBI) through a simple wet-chemical treatment to regulate the current-collector interface in anode-free lithium batteries. XPS results indicate that MBI was anchored on the Cu surface through sulfur- and nitrogen-containing functional groups, while AFM and KPFM measurements show changes in the surface topography and CPD distribution of the Cu foil. These interfacial changes were associated with lower lithium nucleation overpotential (26.6 mV), higher exchange current density (1.64 mA cm–2), and lower apparent activation energies for interfacial processes compared with Bare Cu. In Li∥Cu half cells, MBI Cu showed improved plating/stripping reversibility, slower impedance growth, and more compact lithium deposits, together with an average Coulombic efficiency of 99.03% in the Aurbach test. In anode-free Cu∥LiFePO4 full cells, the MBI-modified current collector delivered an initial discharge capacity of 119.03 mAh g–1 and retained 49.15% of its capacity after 100 cycles, compared with 20.71% for the Bare Cu counterpart under the same conditions. Overall, these results show that simple molecular modification of Cu foil is a useful approach for tuning the Cu/electrolyte interface, regulating lithium deposition, and improving the electrochemical stability of anode-free lithium batteries under the present testing conditions.
